## 16. The Route That Killed Them

The gorge did not want them in it. That was the first thing Ines understood about it, standing at the lip where the river dropped away beneath them into a slot of grey stone, and he said so out loud, because the others deserved honesty more than they needed courage.

"It's a throat," he said. "And we're going to be swallowed."

"Cheerful," Mara said.

"I'm a guide. Cheerful is extra."

Emil Voss laughed from his litter — a dry, cracked sound, like ice settling on a lake — and then stopped laughing because the movement hurt him. His shattered leg was splinted with an ice axe shaft and two tent poles, and every jolt of the journey would remind him of it. He'd insisted on coming anyway. "I spent six weeks crawling toward that survey," he'd said when Brandt suggested leaving him at the high camp with a radio. "You think I'll let you carry my life's work down a gorge without me? I'll weigh it. I'm mostly skin and grievance."

So they went down.

* * *

The route Rennick had falsified ran along the true left bank of the Sombra for eleven kilometers through the gorge, according to the official map — the map that had sent thirty-one miners and two rescuers to their deaths in 1961, and later, by way of a burned report and a scapegoat, had ended Ines Halloran's career. The official map showed a bench of solid ground, a gentle shelf above the waterline, easy walking for laden men.

Rennick's corrections told a different story. The bench didn't exist. It never had. The surveyor who drew it had traced it from a sketch made by a foreman who wanted the haul road approved, and Rennick — young, ambitious, already owned — had inked over the truth instead of reporting it. The real route hugged the wall itself, a scramble of ledges and chimneys, sometimes no wider than a coffin laid sideways.

Ines had memorized those corrections years ago without knowing why. He'd studied the old maps the way a man studies the face of someone who betrayed him, looking for the flaw, the tell, the place where the lie lived. Now the lie was under his boots, and it was holding.

"Here." He crouched at a seam in the rock, barely visible under a skin of ice. "Chimney system, forty meters, then a ledge traverse. The map says open bench. If anyone's following the official route—" he nodded downstream, toward the pursuers they could not yet hear but all of them could feel, "—they'll walk off the edge of a shelf that isn't there."

"That seems fitting," Brandt said.

"It's murder, is what it is," Tomás said from the second litter.

Tomás was worse than Emil. The bullet had gone through his side, high, missing everything vital by what the doctor-less arithmetic of Brandt's field training called *probably*. He was grey around the mouth and joking anyway, which was how Tomás apologized. Ines hadn't forgiven him yet. He wasn't sure forgiveness was the right shape for what he felt — something closer to the grim tenderness you feel for a rope that held when it should have snapped, and you know you'll cut it out of your kit eventually, but not today. Not while it's still bearing weight.

They rigged the litters and lowered them through the first chimney one at a time, Emil swearing in three languages with genuine scholarly range, Tomás silent in a way that frightened Ines more than any scream.

* * *

By midday the river had begun to rise.

Ines noticed it first as a change in the sound — the Sombra's voice dropping half an octave, gathering bass — and then as a smell: meltwater, mineral-cold, the breath of glaciers waking up. Upstream, somewhere above the source, the sun was doing its work on the snowfields, and all of it was coming down this throat after them.

"How fast?" Mara asked. She'd stopped asking questions like *are we going to be all right* sometime around the third day inside the valley. Her questions were all engineering now. Ines found he respected her enormously for it.

"Fast enough that we don't stop for lunch," he said. "The gorge narrows twice below us. When it hits the first pinch, the level jumps. We need to be past the second narrows before—"

A crack rolled down the canyon, deeper than thunder, and the far wall answered it. Rockfall. Somewhere above them a shelf the size of a chapel floor had let go, and the dust came drifting down through the light like the gorge exhaling fifty years of held breath.

Nobody said anything about the pursuers. Nobody had to. Rockfall meant vibration, and vibration meant people, and people meant the second team had entered the gorge behind them.

"Move," Ines said.

* * *

They moved.

It became, in memory afterward, less a march than a single long held breath. Ines led with the corrected survey pages folded into a waterproof case strapped across his chest, reading stone the way other men read print. *Ledge, two meters, exposed, trust it.* *Chimney, loose at the top, keep left.* *Traverse above the waterline — the waterline of 1961, which is not the waterline of today.* Every line of Rennick's handwriting was a confession written in advance, and Ines walked it backward, undoing the lie step by step, and there was something in it that felt almost holy and almost funny at once: his whole ruined career had been built on these false lines, and now the same lines, corrected, were carrying four broken people to safety.

"You're smiling," Mara said, hauling on a rope beside him. "Why are you smiling?"

"He taught me to read terrain," Ines said. "Every trick I know, he put there. And now the tricks are saving us from his own lie." He shook his head. "It's like being robbed by your own reflection."

"Family reunions are complicated," Mara said.

Behind them, Emil called out corrections from the litter — he'd spent weeks with the survey, knew it nearly as well as Ines now — and between the two of them, mentor's ghost and mentor's victim, they threaded the gorge.

* * *

The supplies went at the second narrows.

There was no dramatic moment, no rope parting, no cry. There was just a ledge that Brandt tested three times and trusted once too often, and the food barrel sliding with a terrible gentleness, almost politely, out of its lashings and down into the white water, where it vanished as if the river had been waiting for it specifically. Twenty minutes later, crossing a flake of rock slick with spray, Tomás's medical kit followed — Ines saw it go, saw Tomás watch it go, and saw Tomás close his eyes with an expression that was not fear but arithmetic.

"What do we have left?" Mara asked.

Ines took inventory aloud, because inventories spoken are promises. "One stove. Half a kilo of rice. A tin of condensed milk Tomás has been hoarding like a dragon. Six meters of cord, two headlamps, one flare, and the survey." He paused. "And each other, before anyone makes me say it."

"You were going to say it," Brandt said.

"I was absolutely going to say it."

Emil, from the litter, with the gravity of a man delivering a verdict: "Then we eat the milk tonight. All of it. If I'm going to die in a gorge, I refuse to die having saved condensed milk for an occasion that never comes."

They ate the milk that night in a hollow above the flood line, passing one tin and four spoons, and it was, Ines thought, the finest meal of his life, though he'd eaten tasting menus in cities whose names he no longer mentioned. Tomás got the last spoonful. He looked at it for a long time.

"Ines," he said. "About the money they paid me—"

"Eat your milk, Tomás."

"I reported our position twice before the camp. They know exactly where we entered the gorge. They'll have the official map, and the official map says—"

"The bench," Ines said. "Yes."

"They'll be walking it right now. Four armed men on a shelf that doesn't exist, in the dark, with the river rising." Tomás turned the spoon over. "I keep trying to feel bad about it and I can't get past feeling like it's fair."

Ines considered that. The firelight moved on the wet walls of the gorge. Somewhere above, another distant grumble of rock letting go.

"It's not fair," he said finally. "Fair would be them drowning and us getting our fuel back. But it's honest, which is more than either of us managed last week." He lay back against the pack. "Sleep. Tomorrow we beat the water to the lower pinch, or we learn to breathe underwater."

* * *

They beat the water by ninety minutes, and it cost them everything they had left.

The lower pinch was a slot where the whole Sombra compressed to the width of a truck, and the corrected survey showed a bypass — a high ledge system on the true right, gained by a ramp of talus that Rennick had annotated in a hand so cramped it looked like the writing of a man ashamed to be seen making it: *NOT PASSABLE WITH LOADS IN SPRING. USE LEFT BENCH ONLY IF YOU WANT TO DIE.* Even in his falsification, apparently, Rennick had left warnings — little buried truths, guilt leaking through the ink. Ines had always wondered if the old man had wanted to be caught.

The talus ramp was moving. Not dramatically — not yet — but under their feet it shifted with every placement, a slope of frost-loosened blocks deciding, block by block, whether to stay a mountain or become a landslide. They ferried the litters across it in relay, three carries each, and on the final carry, with Emil halfway across and Ines spotting below, the slope made up its mind about a section the size of a tennis court.

There was no decision to make. Ines was simply moving before the sound reached him, the way Rennick had trained into him twenty years ago — *the mountain tells you first, boy, it always tells you first, the question is whether you've been listening* — and he had Emil's litter straps in his hands and was throwing his weight downslope against the slide, both of them surfing a moving country of stone, Mara screaming his name in a voice he'd remember longer than the pain in his shoulder, until the slide met the anchor of a house-sized block and stopped, and stopped them, and the gorge went quiet except for the river, which had risen to within four meters of their ledge and was still climbing.

They got everyone across. Ines sat down hard on the far side and discovered his hands were shaking and found, to his surprise, that he was laughing.

"What," said Brandt, who did not laugh, ever, as far as Ines could tell.

"He told me once the mountain always warns you." Ines wiped his eyes. "First useful thing he ever said to me. Took me twenty years and a felony to hear it."

* * *

They lost the stove at dusk, to a wave, in a stretch where the corrected route dipped briefly to the waterline — the one place Rennick's annotations admitted defeat: *SPRAY ZONE. RUN IT.* They ran it. The wave that took the stove also soaked everything, including the spare matches, including, briefly, Mara, whom Ines caught by the collar of her jacket as the river tried a casual abduction. After that they walked in the dark by touch and by Ines's memory, which was the only instrument left that worked.

And here was the thing Ines could not have explained to anyone, and did not try: in the dark, cold, starving, pursued, responsible for three damaged lives and one bag of documents, he was happy. Not giddy — nothing so cheap. It was the happiness of a lock turning. For seven years he had been a man defined by a route he supposedly botched, a lie he supposedly told, a disaster he supposedly caused. Now the lie was under his feet and he was walking it home, line by line, and the map — Rennick's map, the true one, the buried one — was telling the truth at last, and the truth was passable. Barely. Dangerously. But passable. Everything Rennick had hidden, Ines was carrying into daylight, and every meter of the gorge that yielded to his reading of it was a sentence in his own defense written in granite.

"You've gone quiet," Mara said, near midnight, on the last ledge before the gorge opened. She walked beside him now without being asked; she'd been doing it for hours. "Good quiet or bad quiet?"

"Map quiet," he said. "For the first time since Puerto Ceniza, the map and I agree about everything."

She bumped his shoulder with hers, gently, on the side that wasn't wrenched. "Then we're almost out."

"We're almost *somewhere*," Ines said. Because he had been studying the survey's final pages all day, in the snatched moments between crises, and there was a thing in them he hadn't yet said aloud. The corrected lines didn't end at the gorge mouth. They continued upstream — upstream, into the high country, to a set of coordinates marked in red, in Rennick's hand, with a note that Ines had read four times and still felt in his sternum:

*TRUE SOURCE. NOT WHERE THE MAPS SAY. WHERE THE FLOOD BEGAN.*

Below them, the gorge opened its mouth and gave the river back to the sky, and ahead, under the first grey hint of dawn, the valley climbed away toward a wall of ice that no map had ever drawn correctly.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable, carried on the cold air down the throat of the gorge, came the sound of voices. Human ones. Angry ones. Four men, Ines guessed, standing at the edge of a bench that did not exist, looking for a route that had never been there — and finding, instead, fresh tracks heading up.

Toward the source.
