## 17. The Source

The route ended where routes are supposed to end: in a wall of ice.

Ines had known it was coming for the last hour—had felt it in the way the gorge narrowed, in the sound of the water changing from a conversation to an argument, in Tomás's silence behind him, which had a particular quality when Tomás was doing arithmetic he didn't like. The corrected survey lines ran straight up the throat of the gorge on paper. The gorge itself had other ideas. Somewhere above them, unseen, a serac had calved and filled the passage with debris and meltwater, and the route that had killed men in 1961 now simply refused to exist.

"Options," Emil said from the litter they'd rigged between two packs poles. His shattered leg was strapped and splinted with a tent stake and more optimism than medicine. He'd been remarkably cheerful about it, in the way of men who have already decided not to die of anything so ordinary as a broken leg.

"One," Ines said. "Up."

He pointed at the left wall of the gorge, where a shelf of old moraine climbed toward a notch in the rock. It wasn't on any map. It wasn't on Rennick's falsified map or the corrected survey or the treaty documents or, as far as Ines knew, anyone's imagination. It was simply the line his eye kept returning to, the way water returns to a channel.

"That's not a route," Brandt said. "That's a suggestion."

"I've been paid worse for less."

Brandt didn't laugh. Brandt hadn't laughed since Puerto Ceniza, possibly since 1961, but he shouldered his end of the litter without further argument, which from Silas Brandt was the same thing as applause.

They climbed for three hours through fog that came off the glacier in slow white breaths. Mara went first, finding holds with a sureness that surprised no one who'd watched her haggle with debt collectors, and called back the good footing in a voice gone flat and professional—the voice of a woman who had decided that if she let herself feel anything before the top, she might not make it there. Twice the fog opened like a curtain and showed them the valley below: the terraced ruins of San Ildefonso, the drowned town, its church tower standing out of the new river like a man wading to meet them. Then the curtain closed again, and there was only rock and breath and the small sounds of five people agreeing, without discussion, to keep going.

At the notch, the wind hit them, and then—

Then the world stopped.

* * *

The basin beyond the notch was a bowl of old snow perhaps four hundred meters across, ringed by rock walls stained rust and green. And at its center, welling up out of the earth with a gentleness that seemed almost apologetic, was the source of the Rio Sombra.

It did not look like much. That was the first thing Ines thought, and the second thing, and the thing he kept thinking as they crossed the snowfield toward it. A spring the size of a dinner table, ringed by stones some patient hand had arranged long ago. The water rose through it pale blue-green, impossibly clear, and slid away south in a stream you could step over—and that stream became the river that carved the gorges, turned the turbines, drowned the town, carried their boat, ruined his life, and killed forty-one people in the winter of 1961.

All of it, from a pool you could empty with two buckets.

"It's cold," Mara said unnecessarily. Her breath came out in ribbons. "Why is it warm-smelling? Like—sulfur?"

"Deep water," Ines said. "It comes up from far down." He crouched at the ring of stones. Beneath the surface, the gravel shifted and breathed, and he understood suddenly what he was looking at: not a spring but a wound. The mountain's plumbing, exposed. The water rising here had traveled through the mine.

Through the shaft Rennick had erased from the map.

"The flood started here," he said quietly. Nobody needed him to say it, but he said it anyway, because some things want witnesses. "November '61. The meltwater found the workings through this fracture. Filled the lower levels in under an hour. Then it blew out the adit and took the town." He looked up at Brandt. "This is where your river is born. Out of the mine."

Brandt stood at the edge of the pool with his hands hanging at his sides, and for a moment he looked less like a man guarding a secret than like a man standing at a grave he'd dug himself and never once visited.

"Yes," he said.

Tomás sat down heavily on a stone, which was Tomás's way of announcing that a situation had exceeded his professional interest. "So this is it," he said. "The top of everything. Very nice. Very quiet. I would like to note for the record that we are standing inside a closed valley in violation of a treaty, next to a haunted swimming hole, carrying a fifty-year-old document that half the country wants burned and the other half wants stolen, and my only weapon is a fish knife."

"Noted," Ines said.

"For the record," Tomás added, "the fish knife is very sharp."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the southern wall, because the light was failing and because none of them could bear to leave yet. There was a strange domesticity to it—Mara melting snow for tea, Emil propped against his pack dictating corrections to his field notes while she held the pen for him, Tomás rigging the tarp with the exaggerated competence of a man avoiding conversation. The spring murmured at the center of it all, indifferent, eternal, smelling faintly of struck matches.

It was after dark, with the stove hissing and the fog sealed outside the basin like a lid, that Brandt took the survey case from where it had ridden against his chest for eleven days and set it on a flat stone between them all.

"Burn it," he said. "Here. Now. While there's still a choice."

Nobody moved.

"You've seen what's up here." Brandt's voice was low and even, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this speech for twenty years in a hut with no one to hear it. "You've seen what the valley does when people decide the mountain owes them something. Forty-one dead in '61 because Aldous Rennick drew a map that hid the truth, and every year since, someone has come sniffing after what's buried here—not the bodies, nobody wants the bodies—they want the *ore*. The survey proves the lower shafts were never worked out. It proves the town was built on ground that was hollow before it ever flooded. You carry this out, and within five years there will be drills in this valley. Within ten, a company town. And one day the meltwater will find the workings again, and there will be another San Ildefonso, and there will be no one left alive who remembers enough to seal the adit." He put his hand flat on the case. "I have spent twenty years making sure nothing leaves this valley. I am asking you—one last time—to help me finish."

The fire cracked. The spring murmured. Ines found himself looking at the water instead of the man, because the water was easier.

"You're wrong about one thing," Ines said finally. "Maybe the most important thing."

"Enlighten me."

"You think silence has been protecting this valley. It hasn't. Silence is what killed it." He turned to face Brandt fully. "Rennick chose silence in '61. He drew the safe map, buried the real one, told forty families the ground under them was solid. That silence didn't protect anyone—it just scheduled the disaster for a date convenient to the owners. And twenty years later he chose silence again, when he needed someone to blame, and that silence landed on me." He heard his own voice stay level and was grateful for it. "Every bad thing that happened in this story happened because a man who knew the truth decided the truth was too dangerous to say out loud. You're not guarding the valley, Silas. You're keeping Rennick's last promise. And look what his promises were worth."

Brandt's jaw worked. "You don't know what I—"

"No," Ines said. "I don't. And I'm asking you to tell me. But not tonight."

"Why not tonight?"

"Because tonight I have to say my piece, and I can't do it while I'm waiting for yours."

He reached out and laid his hand on the case, next to Brandt's. The leather was cold and scarred and heavier than paper had any right to be.

"My name has been dirt in two countries for six years. Every guide I trained won't return my letters. I have shoveled fish guts in Puerto Ceniza and been glad of it, because it was work nobody had to trust me to do." He said it plainly, without performance; the shame had been handled so many times it had gone smooth as river stone. "This document clears me. Do you understand? Not metaphorically. There is a page in here with Rennick's own hand admitting the falsification, dated before the disaster, and a second page where he names me as the guide who 'questioned the route'—his words—which is the entire basis of my blacklisting. I could burn this case right now and spend the rest of my life clean in every way except the one that matters."

"And still you want to carry it out."

"Because it isn't mine." He looked at Mara. "And it isn't hers either. That's the whole point. It belongs to the forty-one."

* * *

Everyone looked at Mara then, and Ines hated it a little—that the decision would land on her, that her father's discovery and her grandfather's sin had conspired to make a nineteen-year-old woman the hinge of history in a frozen bowl at the top of the world. But she met their eyes one at a time, and she did not flinch, and Ines thought: *Emil's daughter.*

She knelt by the case. She did not touch it.

"When I was small," she said, "my grandfather used to take me walking along the Sombra in Puerto Ceniza, and he'd tell me the river was our family's river. That we'd given the valley its roads and the town its wages. He said the flood was an act of God." Her voice caught, steadied. "Two weeks ago I learned he owned the mine. Last week I learned he helped bury the report. Everything I was going to inherit—the house, the accounts, the name—all of it was bought with this valley's silence." She looked up at Brandt, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "You think burning this protects the dead. Maybe it does. But it protects *him* too. It lets him keep being the generous old man who walked me along the river. And I am done letting dead men be kinder than they were."

"Mara," Emil said softly, from his place against the pack. "You don't owe the dead anything. Least of all your future."

"I'm not giving up my future, Papa. I'm giving up a lie I was going to inherit instead of one." She turned back to Brandt. "Carry it out. Testify. Let the treaty be amended so the valley stays closed forever—but closed *honestly*, with the reason written down where no one can pretend it was God. That's the only grave marker those forty-one will ever get. Don't take it away from them."

The silence afterward was long. The stove hissed. Somewhere above, wind worried at the rim of the basin like a dog at a door.

Brandt looked at each of them in turn—Emil, Tomás, Mara, and last Ines, longest of all. And Ines watched him and understood that whatever was breaking open in the man's face had been breaking slowly for twenty years, and that tonight was merely the day it finished.

"There were three of us who knew," Brandt said at last, very quietly. "Rennick. Voss senior. And the surveyor who drew the true lines and watched them get erased." His hand came off the case. "You want to know why I stayed in this valley? It wasn't to guard it."

"Silas—" Emil began.

"No." Brandt's voice cracked like ice underfoot. "You've had your testimony. All of you. Tomorrow, on the descent, you'll have mine. All of it. What I lost, and what I did, and why the adit is bolted shut." He looked at the spring, at the pale water breathing up out of the mountain's oldest wound. "But the survey goes down whole. Every page. And I carry it myself."

He picked up the case and held it against his chest again, and it seemed to Ines that the man holding it was lighter than the man who had set it down.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Ines walked to the spring alone.

The stars were out in force, hard and close in the thin air, doubled perfectly in the still water until he couldn't tell which sky was real. He crouched at the ring of stones and dipped his fingers in, and the cold went through him like a bell being struck.

Somewhere below his feet, in the dark of the drowned workings, the river was remembering everything. Every timber, every tool, every name. It had carried the truth up out of the mountain for sixty years, drop by drop, and delivered it faithfully to the sea, and no one had thought to ask it.

"All right," Ines said aloud, to the water, to the dead, to the mentor whose maps had brought him here backward and true. "We'll tell them."

Behind him, at the edge of the firelight, a shape detached itself from the rocks—too deliberate to be Tomás sleepwalking, too armed to be a dream. Brandt stood watching him, and in the starlight his face was unreadable, and over the eastern wall of the basin, faint and rhythmic and utterly impossible at this altitude, came the sound of rotors.

Ines did not turn around.

"They're early," Brandt said.
