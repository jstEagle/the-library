## 19. Running the Drowned Town

The helicopter came at dawn, and it did not bother to be quiet about it.

Ines heard it first as a change in the birdsong—or rather, the absence of it. The valley had spent three days teaching him its silences: the drip of meltwater off the terraces, the tick of frost letting go of stone in the early light, Brandt's cough from the corner of the survey hut where he slept like a dog who expected to be kicked. When all of that stopped at once, Ines was already on his feet.

"Helicopter," he said.

Mara sat up in her bag, hair wild, eyes instantly awake. She had learned that much in three weeks of river and rock—how to wake like a guide instead of a banker's daughter. "Theirs?"

"Theirs." He crossed to the hut's one window, a square of glass fogged with his own breath, and wiped it with his sleeve. Far down the valley, above the drowned town's terraces, a machine hung against the pale sky like a wasp deciding where to land. It didn't land. It circled once, twice, and then dropped something small and dark onto the flats below the old headframe, and rose again, and went away north.

"They're not leaving men here," Mara said. "They're dropping them."

"Yes."

"And coming back for them later. With the survey."

"With us, if we're lucky enough to still be attached to it." Ines turned from the window. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He had spent six years believing he was a man whose hands shook when things got serious, because that was what Rennick's lie had made him—a coward on paper, a guide who'd walked clients into a flood. It turned out the paper had been wrong too. "Wake Emil. Wake Brandt. We're moving now, before they get organized."

* * *

They moved badly, because Emil Voss could not be moved well.

His leg had set crooked over the long weeks, splinted with ski poles and survey staves, and every step downhill cost him ten minutes and a vocabulary that would have embarrassed a dockworker. Brandt carried most of Emil's weight without complaint, the old surveyor bent under the injured man's arm like a bridge piling taking a load it was built for. Mara carried the survey itself—the 1961 original, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped flat against her back beneath her pack. Ines had argued about that. She had won, on grounds he couldn't refute: *If they take me, they take everything anyway. At least this way I know where it is.*

Tomás would have laughed at the logic. Tomás was two days behind them, alive, broken, and honest for the first time in Ines's memory, having taken a bullet meant for Ines and then insisted—in the hospital tent of words they'd built around him—that they go on without him. They hadn't gone far without him. He was propped in the high camp's supply cave with a radio, a rifle he refused to touch, and instructions to raise any voice on any frequency that answered.

It was Tomás's voice, crackling and furious, that told them how bad it was.

"Ines. Ines, answer. Second team came up the lower gorge—eight men, maybe nine, boats and climbing gear. They passed my cave. They saw me." A pause full of static shaped almost like an apology. "I told them I was a prospector. I don't think they believed me. Ines—they have the falsified route map. The old one. Somebody gave it to them."

Ines stood on the terrace above the drowned town and felt the morning tilt.

Of course they had it. The debt collectors had bought everything else—his anonymity in Puerto Ceniza, Tomás's reports, the authorities' patience. Why not the map? The famous Halloran route, the one that had killed four climbers and ended a career, photocopied and folded into some hired man's pocket like a menu.

"They'll come up through the town," Tomás said. "It's the fast line. Ines, are you hearing me? They have your route."

"I'm hearing you." Ines looked down at the terraces spreading below him, gray-green in the dawn, and something moved in his chest that was not fear. He examined the feeling carefully, the way you examine a hold before you commit your weight to it. It took him a moment to name it, because he had not been allowed to feel it in six years.

It was anger. Clean, specific, useful anger. Not the sour shame he'd carried out of the inquiry, not the flinching dread of being recognized in markets and ferry lines. This was the anger of a man watching someone else pick up his tools and use them wrong.

"Tomás," he said. "Get out of there. Go downstream, hide, don't be brave. And listen—" He closed his eyes and let the old map unroll in his memory, the falsified one, the lie he had memorized so thoroughly that he could walk it blindfolded, because for six years it had been the only version of himself anyone would show him. "Listen carefully. I'm going to need you to do one thing, and it has to be exactly right."

* * *

The drowned town of San Sebastián de la Sombra had been a mining settlement of perhaps four hundred souls until June of 1961, when the upper shaft flooded and the river, finding a new path through broken ground, took the valley floor with it. What remained when the drought years dropped the waterline was a skeleton: foundations laid out in streets, doorsteps leading to rooms of sky, a church bell tower standing waist-deep in the shallows of the new channel. The mine's adit—the main entrance, sealed with modern bolts by whoever had guarded this secret—faced the town from the eastern slope like a closed eye.

Ines had studied it for two days from the terraces above, the way he'd once studied rivers for clients: where the water ran, where the ground fell away, where a body could move fast and where it couldn't.

Now he crouched behind a foundation wall with Mara beside him and watched nine men come up the falsified route.

They were good. That was the worst of it. They moved the way paid professionals move—spaced, quiet, weapons slung but hands ready—and they followed the old Halloran map with the confidence of men who believed the paper under their fingers. Ines watched them take the line he had once taken with Rennick's hand on his shoulder, guiding him along the traverse above the church, and he understood suddenly, completely, why the map was a lie worth killing for.

Because the falsified route didn't just avoid the unstable shaft. It avoided everything true. It curved wide of the real dangers and hugged the false ones—it sent travelers along the base of the eastern slope, directly beneath the adit, across ground that the 1961 flood had undermined and never rebuilt. Ground that held, in dry weather. Ground that did not hold, Ines had noticed yesterday, when the meltwater running off the glacier above began to find its way down through the old workings, as it did every afternoon when the sun hit the ice.

The river was rising. He could hear it, a low gathering note under the town's silence, the sound of a place remembering what it was built to swallow.

"Mara." He kept his voice below the water-sound. "When they reach the adit, they'll stop. It's bolted, it's obvious, and their map will tell them the route continues through the workings behind it. They'll want in. It's the only way the map offers."

"You want them in the mine?"

"I want them *at* the mine. Behind the bolts, in the cut, under the overhang where the adit mouth shelters them from above." He traced it in the dirt between them: the slope, the adit, the shelf of undermined ground above it, and above that the old drainage channel—a mining ditch, dry for sixty years except for afternoons like this one, when the glacier's melt came down the mountain looking for the shortest way to the river. "Rennick's lie put them exactly where the truth can trap them. All we have to do is open the ditch."

Mara stared at him. Then, slowly, she started to laugh—not the bright laugh he'd heard on the boat in the first week, but something lower, half horror, half delight. "You're going to drown them."

"No." He said it flatly, and she stopped laughing. "I'm going to make them very wet, very cold, and very trapped, in a mine entrance that floods to knee depth and no deeper—I've measured the grade, Mara, I've spent two days measuring it while you thought I was napping. The water goes in, fills the low chamber, and stops. They'll be stuck behind a wall of meltwater until it drains tonight, which gives us the whole day and a twelve-hour lead. Nobody drowns. Nobody freezes. But nobody follows us either." He met her eyes. "I've buried enough people with other people's maps. I'm not adding to the number today."

She looked at him for a long moment. "My father said you were the only guide who might understand Rennick's lie," she said finally. "He undersold you."

"Everyone does. It's been good for business." He shouldered his pack. "Come on. We have a ditch to dig."

* * *

The drainage channel was exactly where the corrected survey said it would be, which was one more small miracle Ines declined to think about too hard. Sixty years of silt and slide had half-filled it, but the miners who dug it in 1953 had built it to last, cut into bedrock with a gradient that still ran true. He and Mara worked upstream along it for an hour, breaking the worst dams of gravel with the ice axe and a length of survey stake, until they reached the point where the channel bent around a shoulder of rock and the glacier's meltwater came down off the moraine in a braided, busy stream, cold as a confession.

There was a plug point—a place where a slab of slide rock sat against the channel wall like a cork. Ines judged it, tested it, and found it would take both of them and everything they had.

"On three," he said. "And Mara—if it moves wrong, you let go. You let go before you think about it. Promise me."

"I promise."

"One. Two—"

They threw themselves against it together. For one horrible second nothing happened, and Ines felt the old fear rise, the fear that his strength was the kind that failed people. Then the slab groaned, shifted, and went, and the water came through the gap like something released from a long argument with itself—not a torrent, not a flood, but a determined, muscular flow, filling the channel to its banks and running down toward the town with sixty years of intention.

Ines lay on the rocks, gasping, soaked to the ribs, and started to laugh. It surprised him. It surprised Mara more; she stared at him as if he'd begun speaking in tongues.

"What," she demanded, "is funny?"

"He built the ditch," Ines managed. "Rennick. Or his generation did—the survey crew, '53. They cut this channel to keep the workings dry, and then twenty years later Rennick drew his false route straight under it." He pushed himself upright, water streaming off him, grinning like a fool and unable to stop. "He lied with such care, Mara. Such craftsmanship. He even used the truth as the trap. Come on—we have a chase to finish."

* * *

They came down through the town fast and low, using the foundations for cover, and reached the eastern slope in time to see the last three men of the second team arrive at the adit.

The first six had already gone in. Ines could see their lights moving behind the bolted gate—two points of white bobbing in the black throat of the mine, muffled voices echoing, someone trying the bolts and finding them stubborn. The last three waited outside under the overhang, checking watches, conferring. One of them held the falsified map open against his knee, and even at this distance Ines could read the posture: a man trusting his paper.

Above them, forty meters up the slope, the old drainage channel emptied into a stone chute the miners had cut to carry runoff clear of the adit mouth. The chute was choked with slide rock. It had been choked for sixty years.

It was not choked anymore. Ines had spent his nap time yesterday making sure of that, working alone in the hour before dawn while Mara slept, prying loose the stones with a patience he usually reserved for frightened clients. He had told himself he was preparing options. He had not let himself say aloud what option.

Now he put two fingers in his mouth and whistled—the long falling whistle guides used to pull climbers off bad ground—and the three men below snapped around, weapons up, staring up the slope at nothing, at movement, at a flicker of oilcloth and a shout in Spanish that was mostly taunt.

"There! On the scree!"

They came up after him. Of course they did. They were professionals, and professionals do not let a witness run uphill out of sight. Ines led them a merry fifty meters of it—enough to draw all three clear of the overhang, enough to fix their eyes on the scree and their boots on the slope—and then he cut sideways off the route, down through a gully he'd already walked twice, and flattened himself behind a boulder as the world above the adit began, quietly, to pour.

The chute took the channel's water and delivered it exactly as designed, sixty years late. It came down the slope in a brown roaring sheet, caught the three men mid-climb, and swept their feet out from under them with the cheerful indifference of gravity. Ines watched them go—watched, heart in his throat, counting—one, two, three, all three tumbling and sliding and fetching up against the adit's retaining wall in a tangle of limbs and cursing, alive, visibly alive, one of them already dragging another toward the overhang as the water sheeted past them and poured into the mine's outer cut.

Inside, the lights swung wildly. Shouting. The outer chamber, as Ines had measured, took the flow to knee depth and no deeper, a churning pool held by the graded floor, and the six men within discovered that the exit they'd come through was now a waterfall and the bolts on the inner gate were, thanks to a wrench and twenty minutes of Ines's night work, rusted shut in appearance and fact.

The three outside huddled under the overhang with the pool lapping at their boots, their radio out, their map sodden, their route—the famous Halloran route, the lie that had ruined him—drowned in front of their eyes.

Ines stood up on the boulder where they could see him. It was possibly the stupidest thing he had ever done, and he had once guided a honeymoon couple up a ridge in a lightning storm because the groom insisted the weather was romantic.

"Gentlemen!" he called down, over the water. "You're standing in a falsified route! I'd recommend the east ledge—it drains clean—but your map won't show it, because your map is a lie sold to you by the same people who sold you the idea that this valley could be quietly reopened!" He let that land. He watched the man with the radio look down at the ruined paper in his hands, and watched something shift in the man's face—not defeat yet, but the beginning of arithmetic. "The water drops by midnight. Your friends inside are fine—knee-deep, like I said. Nobody has to die today. That was always optional. Ask the men who wrote your orders whether it stays optional."

Then he turned and ran, because standing on a boulder lecturing armed men is only heroic until one of them remembers he's armed.

* * *

They caught up with Brandt and Emil at the rendezvous—the church bell tower, standing in the shallows where the new channel bent, its bell green with sixty years of waterline. Emil sat propped against the tower's base, gray-faced with the effort of the descent, and Brandt stood over him with the rifle he'd finally allowed himself, though his finger had never once touched the trigger guard's near side.

"Well?" Emil said.

"They're sealed," Ines said. He was breathing hard, soaked, scraped, grinning again—he couldn't seem to stop. "Nine men, one flooded adit, zero casualties. The water drops by midnight. By then we'll be through the gorge."

Emil looked at him for a long moment. The old man's face, carved by altitude and grief and three weeks of pain, did something complicated—pride and sorrow fighting for the same ground.

"Rennick taught you that?" he asked. "How to use a lie?"

"No." Ines looked back down the valley, at the drowned town lying quiet in the sun, at the thin thread of smoke where the trapped men had managed a fire under the overhang. Somewhere behind them, the river was still rising with the afternoon melt, patient as paperwork. "He taught me how to read one. The rest I learned walking it backward."

Brandt shouldered his pack and nodded up-valley, toward the gorge, toward the descent, toward whatever waited below the treaty line. "Then walk," the old surveyor said. "The river's coming down to check our work—and so, I think, is everyone else's."

Ines turned his face toward the gorge and the long drop to the world, and led them into it.
