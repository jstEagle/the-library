## 21. Burials

The hospital in Puerto Ceniza smelled of iodine and old paint, and Tomás had already charmed two nurses and a visiting bishop by the time Ines found his room.

"Look who it is," Tomás said from the bed. His left arm was in a sling, his ribs were wrapped like a parcel someone had given up on halfway, and there was a bandage above his ear that made him look like a man who had argued with a propeller and lost on points. "The famous guide. They let you in the country again? Or are we smuggling you?"

"They un-blacklisted me," Ines said. "Officially. There was paperwork."

"Paperwork." Tomás tried to laugh and thought better of it, pressing a hand to his ribs. "The state forgives you with paperwork. I shoot my mouth off for twenty years and I get a bullet through the shoulder. There's no justice in this world, only accounting."

Ines pulled the plastic chair up beside the bed and sat, because standing felt too much like a visit and sitting felt like staying. "The doctor says the shoulder will heal crooked."

"The doctor says that like it's a tragedy. I've been crooked my whole life. Now at least people can see it coming." He looked at Ines then, really looked, and the humor drained out of him the way water leaves a cracked pot—slowly, all at once. "I owe you more than a bullet, Halloran."

"You paid it back."

"I took their money. I told them where you were. Twice." He held up his good hand before Ines could speak. "Don't do me the courtesy of making it small. I did it because they came to me when I was drunk and broke and they made it sound like a favor to you—*he needs watching, he's not well.* And I believed them because believing them was easier than believing anyone would want me along anyway." His voice caught on something. "You took me upstream knowing what I'd done?"

"I didn't know yet. You told me at the falls."

"And you still untied the rope."

"You'd have drowned."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Ines leaned back in the chair until it creaked. "Tomás. Twenty years ago you pulled me out of the Quebrada Honda with a rope you'd tied yourself, badly, and it held anyway. I've been repaying a bad knot ever since. We're even. We've probably been even since the falls, and everything after that was just two stubborn men refusing to say so."

Tomás turned his face toward the window. The light came in yellow and dusty, full of moths. "Your mentor," he said. "Rennick. The maps. Is it true, what they're saying in the papers?"

"It's true."

"He taught you everything."

"He taught me everything except how to tell the truth with a straight face." Ines rubbed his jaw. "That part I had to learn downstream of him."

Tomás nodded slowly, as if this confirmed some long-held theory about teachers in general. "Then you'll know what to do with him. The dead don't apologize. Somebody has to do it on their behalf or the living start doing it for themselves, and the living do it badly."

It was, Ines thought later, the wisest thing Tomás ever said, and he said it while picking at hospital gelatin.

* * *

They buried the maps on a Tuesday, on the riverbank below town, where the Rio Sombra ran wide and brown and unhurried, as if it had forgotten everything it carried down out of the mountains.

Emil Voss came down from the clinic on crutches, his shattered leg pinned and plated, refusing the wheelchair with a ferocity that suggested the wheelchair had insulted his mother. Mara walked beside him carrying the oilcloth packet. Brandt came too—he had insisted on being discharged early, saying that hospitals made him nervous, which everyone understood to mean that walls made him nervous now—and he stood a little apart, hat in his hands, a thin man weathered down to essentials.

There was no priest. Emil had asked if they wanted one and Ines had said no, and nobody had argued, though the bishop Tomás had charmed sent a blessing by way of a nurse, which Ines accepted on Rennick's behalf because Aldous would have found it hilarious.

Aldous Rennick had been dead four years. There was no body here either—just as there had been no body under the ice axe on the riverbank, only the shape a man leaves behind when he goes. What they buried instead was the packet: the falsified route maps, the ones Rennick had drawn with a steady hand and a lie in the margins, the ones that had damned a mine, drowned a town, ended forty-one lives in 1961, and then, decades later, quietly ended a young guide's career when he'd signed his name beneath his teacher's lines without looking closely enough—or perhaps without wanting to look closely enough. That question Ines had stopped asking. It had no answer that helped.

Mara knelt at the edge of the hole they'd dug—it wasn't deep; the ground was river silt and old gravel—and unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of someone handling a sleeping animal.

"I never met him," she said. "My grandfather knew him, I think. Everyone knew him. That was the whole problem with Aldous Rennick—everyone knew him, and nobody knew him." She glanced up at Ines. "Is that fair?"

"It's the fairest sentence about him I've ever heard."

She held the maps out to him. "You should do it."

Ines took them. They were lighter than he remembered, or heavier—the way a thing is always both, once you finally put it down. He had studied these sheets for three days in his room above the chandlery before leaving, tracing the route lines, finding the alteration he should have found years ago: the survey line that bent around the unstable shaft, drawn as solid ground. *WRONG*, Emil had circled, twice, in pencil, in a hut above a drowned town. And underneath, in Rennick's own hand, an older correction, erased so thoroughly that only the ghost of the graphite remained—a man who had known, and hidden it, and spent whatever remained of his life not talking about it.

"Aldous," Ines said. His voice came out rougher than he intended, so he cleared his throat and started over, and the river kept moving behind him, patient, indifferent, keeping its own records. "You taught me to read water. You taught me that a map is a promise you make to whoever comes after you. You broke that promise, and I carried it, and I hated you for four years, and I missed you every single day of those four years, which is the kind of arithmetic that doesn't come out." He paused. "I'm not going to forgive you today. Maybe not ever. But I'm putting this down, and I'd like you to know—I'd like whoever's listening on your end to know—that the truth got out anyway. Not because of you. Despite you. But it got out, and the valley's closed for good now, and nobody else is going up that river because of your lines."

He set the maps into the earth. The paper made almost no sound at all.

Brandt stepped forward then, and dropped something small onto the packet: a brass survey marker, one of the old 1961 pins, polished bright. "So he knows somebody finished the job," Brandt said simply.

Emil lowered himself onto a folding stool Mara had brought—she had learned, Ines noticed, to anticipate her father's refusals and pre-empt them—and said a few words in a language Ines didn't catch, low and formal, addressed less to God than to the record. Then they filled the hole, and Mara planted a wooden stake at the head of it, and on the stake she had carved, sometime in the night, judging by the fresh smell of the cuts:

**A. RENNICK — SURVEYOR. THE LINE ENDS HERE.**

"Not *rests*," she said, catching Ines reading it. "Ends. Rests implies he meant to pick it back up."

"Gentle humor," Tomás observed from his stretcher, "is wasted on this family."

* * *

Afterward they ate together at the long table outside the Fonda del Muelle, because Tomás refused to be buried anywhere but within reach of grilled fish, and because grief, Ines had learned, is a thing you do in company or not at all.

The fonda's owner brought out the good rum without being asked. Brandt drank half a glass, set it down, and looked at it for a long moment as if it had said something surprising. "Twenty years I guarded that valley," he said. "Kept people out. Kept the secret in. Told myself it was for the dead." He shook his head. "Turns out the dead wanted exactly what the living wanted. To be counted properly. All that silence, and it bought nobody anything."

"You bought time," Emil said. "Time enough for the treaty to hold until someone came with proof. If the survey had surfaced in '75, or '80, there were men who'd have used it to argue the mine was safe after all—*see, the shaft was mapped, the danger was known and contained.* Your silence kept the lie cold. Cold things keep."

Brandt considered this. "You always talk like a ledger, Voss."

"My family owned a mine. It's hereditary."

Mara laughed—a short, startled laugh, the first Ines had heard from her since the gorge—and then pressed her hand briefly to her father's shoulder, and Emil covered it with his own, and neither of them said anything else, because between them there was now a new country whose borders they were still learning, and both of them seemed willing to learn slowly.

Her debt was dissolved. That had happened in the chaos of the testimony week—the debt holders, exposed as the same interests who'd hired the helicopter men, had folded rather than face fraud charges, and the paper that had shadowed Mara from Puerto Ceniza to the source of the Sombra had burned, literally, in a courthouse incinerator, which she described with such relish that Tomás demanded a reenactment twice.

"I keep thinking," she said to Ines, later, when the table had gone quiet and the others had drifted toward the rail to watch the fishing boats come in, "that I hired you to find my father. That was the job. Find Emil Voss, bring him home." She turned her glass. "And instead you found—" she gestured vaguely, taking in the river, the town, the grave on the bank, the whole tangled year, "—everything else."

"The everything else was already there. I just walked upstream of it."

"That's not humility, that's cartography."

"I'm a guide. It's what I have."

She smiled, and then the smile settled into something quieter, and for a while they watched the boats. The evening light came off the water in long copper strokes. Somewhere behind them Tomás was telling the story of the falls for the third time, improving it with each telling—in the latest version he had held Ines underwater for a full minute out of pure spite—and Brandt was correcting the details with the grim precision of a man who had waited twenty years to correct details professionally.

"What happens to you now?" Mara asked.

"I don't know." Ines said it plainly, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere above the drowned town, and he found he didn't miss it. "There's a commission. A scientific team wants to document the source, now that the valley's open. Small group. Legal. Maps that get published." He shrugged. "I haven't said yes."

"Why not?"

"Because every time I've planned the next thing, the next thing has turned out to be a lie someone drew for me." He looked at her. "I'd like to try not planning. Just once. See what grows."

Mara nodded slowly. "And if nothing grows?"

"Then I'll have learned something about fallow ground."

She laughed again, and this time it lasted, and Ines thought: *there it is. Held loosely. Don't grip it.*

* * *

The dock, on her last morning, was all gull-cries and rope-creak and the smell of diesel and salt, which is to say it was every dock that has ever seen anyone leave.

Mara's boat was a slow coastal freighter, bound south, unsentimental as a schedule. Her father had gone ahead by air two days earlier, still arguing with airline staff about his crutches. She stood at the gangway with her bag over her shoulder and looked, for a long moment, not at the boat but back up the street, toward the riverbank, where a wooden stake stood in the morning light with a line carved into it.

"He'd have liked the epitaph," Ines said. "Or hated it. With Aldous it was usually both."

"Both is honest." She turned to him. "Ines Halloran. I came to this town looking for a disgraced guide, and everyone told me there was no such man here. And they were right, mostly. There was just a guy fixing outboard motors who flinched whenever anyone said the word *mountain*." She stuck out her hand, formally, absurdly, and then abandoned the handshake halfway and hugged him instead, quick and fierce, her chin against his shoulder. "Thank you for bringing my father down the mountain. Both of them. The one with the broken leg and the one with the broken maps."

"Take care of the leg. The other one's buried."

She pulled back, eyes bright, and pointed at him with mock severity. "If you ever go north—if you ever need work, or a witness, or someone to burn documents in a courthouse incinerator—"

"You'll be unreachable. You're a Voss. It's traditional."

"It is traditional," she agreed gravely, and then spoiled it by grinning. She climbed the gangway. At the top she turned and raised one hand—not waving, exactly. More like marking a bearing.

The freighter pulled out on the tide. Ines stayed on the dock until it was small, and then until it was gone, and Tomás came and stood beside him in his crooked-shouldered sling and said nothing for a while, which for Tomás was practically a eulogy.

"So," Tomás said eventually. "The commission. The scientists. The source."

"I haven't said yes."

"You've packed your gear."

"I maintain my gear. It's a discipline."

Tomás snorted. "Halloran. I've watched you run from your name for six years. I watched you walk back up a river to meet it. A man doesn't do all that and then stand still." He clapped Ines gently on the good shoulder with his good hand. "Say yes to the scientists. Take the compass. Go see what the spring looks like when nobody's lying about it."

Ines looked out at the empty water, at the space where the boat had been, at the wide brown patience of the Sombra going about its business, carrying its records to the sea.

"Yeah," he said. "All right."

And it surprised him how little the word cost—as if he'd been holding it behind his teeth for years, waiting for the river to give it back.
