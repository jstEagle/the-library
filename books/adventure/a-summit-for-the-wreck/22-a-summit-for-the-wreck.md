## 22. A Summit for the Wreck

The commission came in on a Tuesday, folded inside an envelope with a real return address and a stamp that hadn't been licked by someone in a hurry.

Ines read it twice at the kitchen table of the rented room he still kept above the chandlery in Puerto Ceniza, mostly out of superstition. *The Universidad del Norte requests your services as guide and route consultant for a three-week hydrological survey of the upper Rio Sombra basin.* There was a signature, a letterhead, a per diem listed to the centavo. At the bottom, in smaller print, a line that made him laugh out loud alone in his room: *References available upon request.*

He had references now. That was the strange part. Six months ago his professional history had been a rumor and a blacklist; now there were men in government offices who said his name in the tone people reserved for weather they'd survived. The treaty amendment had gone through in the spring. The valley was open — legally, formally, with permits and boundary markers and a small brass plaque at the trailhead that Tomás had insisted on polishing himself every time he passed it, which was often, because Tomás had nothing else to do while his leg finished deciding whether it belonged to him.

Ines wrote back that evening. He kept the letter short. *I know the way.*

* * *

The scientific team arrived in early October, when the rains had loosened their grip and the river ran clear instead of brown. There were four of them: Dr. Amaral, a hydrologist with the posture of a heron and the patience of one too; two graduate students named Pilar and Dev who bickered about sediment samples the way other couples bickered about dishes; and a quiet cartographer named Okafor who said perhaps forty words a day and drew everything.

They were, Ines decided within the first hour, the easiest clients of his life. They wanted to go slowly. They wanted to measure things. When he told them a stretch of bank was unstable, they believed him without testing the claim against his reputation first. It was such an unfamiliar sensation that he kept waiting for the catch, the way a man who has lived under a bad roof keeps flinching at good weather.

"You keep looking over your shoulder," Pilar observed on the second morning, as they broke camp above the lower gorge. "Expecting someone?"

"Old habit."

"Does it ever pay off?"

"Once," Ines said. "It cost me everything, and it paid off once." He shouldered his pack. "We'll make the narrows by noon if the fog holds off."

The river was kind to them, or as kind as the Sombra ever got. Where it had tried to kill him in the spring — the falls, the portage, the water rising through the gorge like something with an opinion — it now ran low and glassy, and he found himself pointing out the line he'd taken, the ledge where Tomás had nearly drowned, the seam in the rockfall where he'd walked Rennick's lie backward until it told the truth. The students took notes. Dr. Amaral asked questions that were actually good ones. And Ines discovered, somewhere around the third day, that telling the story no longer felt like carrying a stone. It felt like setting one down carefully, in front of people who would build something with it.

Okafor walked beside him most of that afternoon, sketching.

"You're drawing my face," Ines said.

"I'm drawing the gorge." Okafor didn't look up. "Your face happens to be in it. Maps need scale figures."

"I'm not a scale figure."

"No," Okafor agreed mildly. "You're the only reason anyone knows what's down here. I thought the record ought to show that."

Ines had no answer for that, so he gave the man silence, which Okafor seemed to consider fair payment.

* * *

They reached the drowned town on the sixth day.

The drought had ended months ago, and the river had taken some of the ruins back — the lowest terraces stood ankle-deep in green water now, the church roof a dark island. But the upper streets still rose out of the forest, walls without roofs, doorways full of ferns, and the mine adit with its modern bolts gleamed dully in the shade like a scar that had finally stopped arguing.

Dr. Amaral stood before it a long time, hat in hand. She was not a sentimental woman — Ines had watched her log a landslide with the same tone she used for lunch — but she stood there anyway.

"How many?" she asked quietly.

"The flood took the town in eleven minutes. Forty-one dead, official count. Probably more." He nodded at the adit. "The shaft behind that wall is why. My mentor mapped the route around it wrong on purpose. Somebody paid him to."

"And you carried that map for years."

"I carried it like it was scripture." He touched the bolted steel once, lightly, the way you'd touch a headstone. "Scripture's just paper, though. Took me thirty years and one very stubborn woman to learn it."

Pilar, behind him, snorted — she'd met Mara Voss at the hearings, everyone had, the woman who'd renounced a fortune on the courthouse steps in a dress she'd clearly bought for a funeral — and Ines let himself smile. Thinking of Mara had stopped hurting sometime over the summer. It had become something closer to ballast. He wrote to her from every town with a post office, which was fewer towns than you'd think, and she wrote back on graph paper, in handwriting like seismograph readings, always ending with some variation of *the debt is settled, stop keeping score.* He was learning. Slowly. He was learning.

They camped that night on the terrace above the old assay office, and if the valley was quieter than a valley had any right to be, nobody complained about it. Even the static in the radios had gentled — the engineers had traced the loop transmission to a derelict repeater on the ridge and shut it down in July, though Ines privately missed it a little. A valley that talked to you was less lonely than one that merely listened.

* * *

On the ninth day they climbed out of the forest into the high bowl, and there it was: the source.

The glacial spring came up out of the rock like breath through parted lips — a pool no wider than a kitchen table, impossibly blue, feeding a stream that would grow teeth and a name and a body count before it reached the sea. Above it, the icefield. Below it, everything: the gorge, the town, the valley, the river running all the way down to Puerto Ceniza and past it, to the coast, to the world.

Dr. Amaral's team went to work with the happy efficiency of people doing exactly what they were made for — flow gauges, isotope vials, Okafor sitting cross-legged with his board on his knees, drawing the pool as if it might be shy. Ines did what guides do, which was nothing, magnificently. He checked the tent lines. He melted snow. He sat on a rock in the sun and let the altitude do its clean work on his head.

That was when he took out the compass.

It was a brass thing, older than he was, with a hairline crack across the crystal and a needle that wandered a degree or two west of true and had always done so, and Aldous Rennick had pressed it into his hand twenty-nine years ago on a dock much like the one at Puerto Ceniza, saying, *A man who knows where north is can't be entirely lost, boy, whatever else happens to him.* It had been, Ines could admit now, both a gift and a leash. He had followed that needle through two countries and one disgrace, and it had never once pointed anywhere but wherever Rennick wanted him standing.

At the hearings, when they'd asked him how a falsified map had gone unchallenged for thirty years, he'd said: *Because we trusted the man more than the ground.* It was the truest sentence he'd ever spoken in public, and it had cost him nothing, which was how he knew he'd changed.

He turned the compass over in his palm. The needle swung, settled, pointed home — south, downriver, toward the chandlery and the rented room and the letters on graph paper and whatever came next, which for the first time in his adult life was not a place he was hiding in.

"You're going to leave it," Okafor said. Not a question. He'd stopped drawing. The whole team, Ines realized, had gone quiet without being asked to, the way people do at gravesides and finish lines.

"It doesn't point true," Ines said. "Never has. Rennick adjusted it — or bought it broken, knowing him. Either way, I spent half my life navigating by a man's error and calling it north." He weighed it once more. It was lighter than he remembered, or his hands had gotten stronger. "Seems right to leave it here. At the top of the river. Pointing home."

"To whom?" Pilar asked softly. "Him? Or you?"

Ines considered that. Downstream, the spring water went over its first small lip of rock and began the long fall toward the sea, patient as anything honest.

"Yes," he said finally, which was the only accurate answer, and set the compass on the flat stone at the edge of the pool, needle south, crystal catching the sun. Then he stood up, brushed off his knees, and turned to his clients with the expression that had once been famous in two countries and was now, at last, simply his own.

"All right," he said. "You've seen the source. Now let me show you the way down — and this time, I promise you, the map's correct."

The team laughed, and broke camp, and began the long walk home, and the compass stayed where he left it, pointing steadily south through every season, keeping watch over the water — which, having finally been allowed to tell the truth about where it came from, ran clearer than it had in sixty years.
