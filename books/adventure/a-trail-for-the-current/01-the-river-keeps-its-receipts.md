## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The river was lying about its depth, and Sable Nystrom knew it the way she knew her own pulse.

From the wheelhouse of the *Magpie*, the water ahead looked like hammered pewter, smooth and confident, promising six feet where there were four. The storm two nights back had rearranged everything—shifted the shoal off Gull's Tooth by a boat-length, maybe two, and dumped a season's worth of silt into the channel like a drunk emptying his pockets. The soundings on her chart were a week old and worthless. So she read the water instead: the way it wrinkled over the bar, the brown seam where current met slack, the single gray heron standing hip-deep exactly where no heron should be stupid enough to stand.

"Mark," she called down through the engine-room voice tube.

"Four and a half under the keel, give or take a lie," Doss's voice came back, gravel wrapped in oil-smoke. A pause, then the clank of something heavy being set down with exaggerated care. "Give or take half a foot of lie."

"Then we're rich." She eased the throttle back a notch. The *Magpie* answered the way she always did—reluctantly, grumbling through her timbers, an old woman asked to hurry who intended to make her opinion known about it. Forty years on the water and every plank of her had been replaced at least twice, which meant, as Doss liked to say, she wasn't the same boat at all, just the same argument.

Sable stood with one hand on the wheel and one on the brass rail worn to a shine by her father's hand before hers, and she watched the shoal come on. Low water season. Six weeks of this, maybe less if the monsoon came early, and after that the upper river would close like a fist. Everything they earned between now and landfall would come from running cargo nobody else would carry, into channels nobody else would try.

That suited her fine. She'd never trusted easy water.

"Pip!" she shouted aft. "Get your skinny self up here. I want eyes on the starboard mark."

Pip appeared at the rail below, sixteen years of elbows and appetite, squinting against the morning glare. "Starboard mark's gone, Captain."

"Gone."

"Washed away or stolen or walked off. Gone." He leaned out over the water, shading his eyes. "There's the stump of it. Storm took the rest."

Of course it had. She adjusted course by feel, threading toward where the mark used to mean safety rather than where it now meant nothing, and thought—not for the first time—that the river kept its receipts for everything. Every stick you drove into its mud, every name carved into a lock wall, every body it swallowed. It gave them back when it pleased, altered, waterlogged, unrecognizable. You just had to be patient. And mostly you didn't want to see what came back.

The *Magpie* crossed the bar with eight inches to spare and a shudder Sable felt in her teeth.

* * *

Marisol fed them mid-river, which was its own kind of navigation. She'd come up from the galley with a tray of fried plantains and salt fish and strong coffee, distributing plates on the aft deck with the brisk authority of a woman who had decided feeding this crew was the one thing holding civilization together, and she would not be argued with.

"Eat," she told Pip, who was already reaching. "You eat like the food owes you money."

"It does. I ran a tab."

"You're fourteen."

"Sixteen."

"You run a tab like you're fourteen." But she put an extra plantain on his plate, because that was Marisol all over—insult and kindness delivered on the same tray, and God help you if you thanked her for either.

Sable ate standing at the rail, watching the banks slide past. This stretch of the Corben Reach was working country: timber landings, a fuel depot with its tanks rusting orange, nets strung along poles like the ribs of drowned animals. Ordinary. The ordinary world, doing what it did. She had built her whole life inside that ordinariness since the inquiry, since the capsizing, since the four names she did not say aloud even in prayer—cargo runs, salvage jobs, small honest thefts of opportunity, a crew of three misfits who ate her food and complained about it and were, though she'd have died before saying so, the closest thing to family the river had left her.

"Doss says we've got work," Pip announced, mouth full. "Down past the bend. Something snagged."

"I heard him say he *heard* something snagged."

"He says there's a barge hung up on the willow bar. Half-sunk. He could hear it groaning over the engine."

Sable looked downstream. The bend was coming, and beyond it the willow bar showed as a green smear against the brown water, and rising above the smear—

She set down her plate.

Rising above the smear was the blunt black prow of a barge, canted hard to port, its bow driven deep into the willows like a nail half-hammered home. Storm-tossed. Abandoned. No flag flying, no crew visible, no distress pennant—and no sign anyone had ever tried to leave it.

"That's not from around here," Pip said quietly.

No. It wasn't. Sable had run this reach for eleven years and knew every hull that worked it, and that barge belonged to none of them. It was big—bigger than anything that should have come up this far in low water—and its lines were wrong somehow, too fine, too deliberate, the kind of craft designed by people who expected to be taken seriously.

Salvage law was simple on this river: finders keepers, minus whatever the true owner could prove and afford to litigate, which was usually nothing. Salvage was also how she made half her living. There was no honest reason to hesitate.

She hesitated anyway, for exactly three seconds, looking at that black prow.

"Bring us alongside," she said. "Gently. If she's groaning, she's dying, and I don't want to be underneath when she lets go."

* * *

They came at it from downstream, lines across, Doss in the engine room holding revolutions like a surgeon holds a pulse while Sable walked the *Magpie* in close enough to step across. The wrecked barge loomed over them, barnacled to the waterline, its name painted over—deliberately painted over, she noted, with a roughness that fooled no one—with only the ghost of letters showing through: a V, and part of an L.

"Vessel's dead," Doss reported, head out of the engine-room hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last year. "No heat in the boiler, no lights. Been sitting here since the storm at least. Maybe longer—she drifted down from upstream, way upstream." He said *way upstream* the way other men said *the far side of the moon*.

"Anything aboard?"

"Cargo hold's flooded. Whatever she was carrying is either gone or underwater." He paused. "There's a hold forward that's not flooded. Sealed. Watertight door, dogged down from outside."

Sable looked at him. "Dogged from outside?"

"Like somebody wanted it kept shut even if the rest of the ship went to the bottom."

Wind moved through the willows with a dry whispering sound, and somewhere in the flooded hold below their feet, something shifted—a slow groan of settling timber, or the river rearranging its receipts.

"Well," Sable said. "We're salvagers."

They went aboard with gaff hooks and lanterns, Pip first because he was young and light and foolish, Sable behind him because someone had to be responsible for the foolish. The deck was slick with river slime. Everything smelled of mud and diesel and something else beneath it, older, a smell she couldn't place—wax, maybe, or lamp oil, or paper long soaked and dried and soaked again.

The sealed hold's door was steel, and the dogs holding it were new brass, bright as coins, absurd against the rust everywhere else. Pip worked them loose with a wrench and a grunt, and the door swung open on darkness and cold air that flowed out over them like breath from a cellar.

Inside, lashed to cleats that had been installed with more care than any cargo deserved, sat a chest.

It was the size of a sea chest, iron-banded, and when they got lanterns close Sable saw why it had needed sealing: it was lined in lead. She could see the dull gray metal at the corners where the wood had split. Lead-lined chests were for keeping things safe from water, or from instruments, or from prying eyes of certain kinds. None of those reasons were reasons honest cargo required.

And stamped into the lid, pressed deep in red wax under a protective tin cover that had kept it perfect through the drowning, was a seal.

Sable had grown up on seals. Customs seals, ministry seals, shipping company seals, the seal of the River Authority her father had answered to. She knew heraldry the way she knew channel markers. This was none of them.

It showed a tower rising out of water, and around the tower, arranged in a circle like a wreath or a chain, a ring of small marks that might have been stars or might have been letters in an alphabet she didn't know. Beneath, in the same unknown script, a single word.

"Captain," Pip said.

His voice was wrong. She turned. The boy had gone pale—not seasick pale, not scared-of-the-dark pale, but a white, bloodless pallor, the color of a man reading his own name on a death warrant. He was staring at the wax seal the way you'd stare at a snake you'd nearly stepped on.

"You know it?"

"No." Too fast. Much too fast. "No, ma'am. Never seen it. It's nothing. Old thing, probably. Probably nothing at all."

He wouldn't look at her. He busied himself with the lashings, untying knots with fingers that weren't quite steady, and Sable let him, because she had learned long ago that you catch more truth by waiting than by pulling. But she marked it. She marked the pallor and the lie sitting on top of it like oil on water, plain to anyone who cared to look, and she wondered what a sixteen-year-old deckhand from nowhere in particular knew about a seal that made him look like that.

Below them, in the flooded hold, the wreck groaned again, longer this time.

"We'll want the chest off before she goes down," Doss called from the deck. "And Captain—we should think about whether we *want* it off. Salvage's one thing. That—" he nodded at the seal "—that's another kind of thing entirely."

"It's cargo, Doss."

"It's trouble is what it is. I can smell it. Trouble keeps better than most cargo, travels farther, and never rots."

"Everything's trouble if you look at it sideways." She put her shoulder to the chest with Pip. It was heavier than it had any right to be—lead, and whatever lay within, packed tight. Together they wrestled it across the ruined deck, down onto the *Magpie*'s aft well-deck, and lashed it down beside the spare line locker, where it sat squat and patient as a sleeping animal.

Behind them, the barge settled a final inch and sighed, and the willows closed a little further over its bow, the river taking its property back into inventory.

* * *

They made four miles before dusk, tying up at a rotation point Sable had used a hundred times—a cutbank with a mooring post some forgotten keeper had driven decades ago, high enough to be safe from wake, low enough to be lazy about. Supper was rice and the last of the smoked fish, eaten in the galley because the evening had turned cool and because Marisol had opinions about eating outdoors in storm-season air.

The chest sat on the aft deck. Nobody had suggested bringing it inside. Nobody had suggested leaving it out there alone, either, and so it sat in everyone's thoughts like an uninvited guest at table.

Sable stepped out after supper with a lantern and her father's pry bar, and found Pip already there.

He was standing over the chest with his hands jammed in his pockets, looking at the tin-covered seal, and when the lantern light reached him he flinched like he'd been caught at something.

"Shouldn't you be peeling potatoes?" Sable asked.

"Marisol threw me out. Said I peel like I'm digging a grave." He didn't smile at his own joke. He was watching her set the pry bar's edge under the lid. "Captain—you're not going to open it."

"Salvage law says I inventory what I recover."

"Salvage law doesn't know what that is."

"And you do?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the bank, a night bird called once and thought better of it. Pip looked at the seal, then at her, and she watched him decide—watched the truth rise in him and get shoved back down, twice, like a cork in rough water.

"I don't know what it is," he said finally. "I told you. I never seen it before."

"You're a bad liar, Pip. It's one of your best qualities. But right now it's costing us both."

"I gotta go peel—" He was moving already, backing toward the galley, and Sable let him go, because chasing a boy through a lie in front of witnesses would only teach him to lie better.

She stood alone on the aft deck with the lantern and the chest and the river sliding dark and endless past the hull.

Doss had said trouble keeps well and travels far. Her father had said something else, once, standing at this same rail in another life: *the river keeps its receipts, Sable-girl. Everything it takes, it means to give back someday, and it don't care whether you're ready.* She'd been nine years old and hadn't understood him. She understood him now, standing over a lead-lined chest stamped with a dead nation's seal—the words arriving in her mind unbidden, *a dead nation's seal*, and she didn't know why she thought *dead*, only that she was suddenly, absolutely certain of it, the way you're certain of weather coming.

Vhaloria. The word surfaced from somewhere deep—her grandmother's kitchen, maybe, or a schoolbook with pages torn out. A name from maps that no longer carried it. They'd erased it forty years ago, before she was born; whole cities renamed, records burned, families scattered or worse, until the country existed only in the shape of a border that now belonged to three other nations and in the memories of people who'd learned to stop speaking of it.

Carrying that seal, she remembered suddenly, hearing her grandmother's voice across forty years—carrying that seal was a crime. She couldn't remember the name of the law. She remembered only that it was a hanging offense, and that her grandmother had said it in a whisper, in her own kitchen, with the windows shut.

Sable looked at the chest for a long time.

Then she set the pry bar down, untended, unopened, and doused the lantern, and stood in the dark listening to the water go by. Not tonight. Tonight she'd sleep on it, and tomorrow she'd decide like a captain decides—coldly, with the crew's necks counted alongside her own.

But as she turned to go below, she caught movement at the galley window. Pip, watching her through the glass, lamplight behind him. Watching to see what she'd do with the chest. Watching the way you watch a fuse you lit yourself.

And far downstream, faint through the night air, came a sound she almost missed—a motor, running without lights, coming upriver against the current at a speed no lawful boat traveled at night.

The river kept its receipts. It seemed hers were already on the way.
