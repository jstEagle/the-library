## 2. A Dead Nation's Wax

The Magpie's hold smelled of bilgewater, creosote, and forty years of other people's cargo—the ghost-smells of grain and gunpowder and once, memorably, a shipment of caged songbirds that had left their terror in the wood for a full season after. Sable Nystrom knelt beside the chest in the lamplight with a pry bar in both hands and the particular silence of her crew pressing against her back like weather.

Doss stood at the foot of the ladder with his arms crossed over his forearms, which were crossed anyway over his chest, so that he looked like a man wrapped in his own disapproval. "I'm telling you one more time, Captain. Salvage law says anything sealed goes to the harbormaster unopened. You crack that lid, whatever's inside stops being found property and starts being *yours*. There's a difference between finding a thing and stealing it, and the difference is a lid."

"I know what the law says."

"You wrote half of it down yourself when you took your master's ticket."

"That's why I know it also says a captain may open sealed salvage to assess hazard." She set the flat of the bar against the hasp. The lead lining of the chest had made it absurdly heavy—four hundred pounds if it was an ounce—and the storm-swollen barge they'd winched it from had been riding low enough to suggest there'd been more like it, all of them gone to the mud now except this one stubborn survivor. "And I'm assessing. This thing could be full of black powder for all we know. Could be plague rags. Could be somebody's tax receipts."

"Could be," Doss said, in the tone of a man who did not believe a word of it and knew she didn't either.

Marisol had come down without being asked and stood by the lantern, flour still dusting her wrists to the elbow, watching the chest the way she watched a pot about to boil over. Pip was on the ladder. Not climbing up, not climbing down—just on it, one hand on a rung, his face in the shadow above the lamp's reach.

Sable worked the bar. The hasp had been soldered shut—not locked, *soldered*, which meant whoever closed it had never intended it to be opened again by ordinary hands—and the metal gave reluctantly, with a sound like a tooth coming out. A bead of gray solder popped free and rolled across the deckboards. Then another. Then the whole seam sighed, and the lid came up an inch, breathing out air that had been shut away from the world for longer than Sable had been alive.

It smelled of cedar and cold stone and something faintly sweet, like wax left too long near a candle.

She lifted the lid the rest of the way.

* * *

The first thing she saw was paper. Ledgers, stacked in careful ranks, bound in leather gone the color of tea, their spines stamped with characters she didn't recognize—a looping, angular script that looked less written than *grown*, like vines trained along a trellis. Beneath them, cradled in a bed of oiled wool, lay a brass instrument the size of a dinner plate: rings nested within rings, etched so finely that the engraving caught the lamplight and threw it back in threads. An astrolabe, or something shaped like one, but no astrolabe she'd ever seen had this many moving parts, or moved like this—when she tilted it, experimentally, the inner rings turned of their own accord, whispering against each other, settling, as if the instrument were *finding* something.

"It's weighted wrong," Doss said quietly. He'd come closer despite himself. "Look at the gimbal. That's not decorative. That's compensation—for river current, maybe. Tidal pull. I've never seen machining like that. Nobody's seen machining like that. That's not workshop work, that's—" He stopped.

"Say it," Sable said.

He didn't say it. He looked at the seal instead, pressed into the wax that still clung to the broken hasp: a circle bisected by three wavy lines, with a starburst at the center, stamped deep in a resin so dark it was nearly black.

At the top of the ladder, Pip made a small sound. Not quite a word. Sable heard it the way you hear ice cracking upstream—a warning that travels through the water before you see the break.

On top of the ledgers lay a letter, sealed with the same dark wax, addressed in that vine-script—and beneath it, smaller, in trade-common so heavily accented the letters leaned like tired men: *To the keeper at the source.*

"The source," Marisol said. "The source of what?"

"The river," Sable said. Her own voice sounded far away to her. "The headwaters. The upper gorge." Everyone on the river knew the upper gorge; nobody went there. The charts ended at the last cataract with a cartographer's shrug—*unsurveyed, impassable*—and the government had closed even the approach locks years before Sable was born. Officially because of flood danger. Officially.

She broke the letter's outer seal with her thumb. Inside were pages in the vine-script she couldn't read—but folded among them, a single sheet in trade-common, written fast, the ink blotched as though the hand that held the pen had been shaking:

*If you are reading this, the current chose you, and the current does not choose badly. What is in this chest must reach the keeper at the source before the last lock closes. They are burning the outposts. They have already burned Corben. Do not trust the ministry. Do not trust the river police. Trust the water signs. — V.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third time was when she noticed the brown stains at the bottom edge of the page, and understood they weren't ink.

Nobody spoke. The lantern hissed. Somewhere overhead, rain had started again on the deck planks, soft as fingers.

"Pip," Sable said, without turning around. "Come down here."

* * *

He came down slowly, boy-fashion, sideways, as if the ladder might change its mind about holding him. Seventeen years old, all elbows and appetite six months ago when Marisol had fished him off a dock in Corben Landing with a stolen apple in each fist; now he stood in front of the open chest with his hands behind his back like a man at a funeral, and his face had gone the color of the bilgewater.

"You've seen that seal before," Sable said. It wasn't a question. On the barge, when they'd hauled the chest dripping into the light, she'd caught it—one flicker, gone as fast as it came, a boy deciding very quickly to be bored. She'd let it go then. She wasn't letting it go now.

"No, ma'am."

"Pip."

"I haven't."

Marisol said nothing, but she moved—just slightly, just enough to stand where Pip would have to look past her to reach the ladder. Doss shifted too, closing the other side. None of it was threatening. All of it was. Sable had learned long ago that a crew corners people without ever raising a hand; that was what a crew was *for*, some days.

"Sit down," Sable said, gentler. "You're not in trouble. But you're going to tell me why a wax stamp on a drowned box made you look like you'd seen your own ghost."

Pip sat on a coil of mooring line. He looked at the chest. He looked at his knees. When he finally spoke, his voice came out low and strange, stripped of its usual bounce, and Sable realized with a small cold shock that she was hearing his grandmother's accent in it—the old woman who sold pickled fish two streets back from the Corben docks, whom Pip visited every rest-day, whom Sable had waved at a hundred times without ever really seeing.

"It's Vhalorian," he said. "The seal. Three rivers under a star. My gran has a spoon with it on the handle. She keeps it wrapped in a cloth at the bottom of her trunk and she only takes it out once a year, on a night in autumn, and she puts honey on the spoon and eats it standing up facing east, and she won't say why, and if you ask her she'll slap you." He swallowed. "Vhaloria isn't real anymore. They erased it. Forty years ago. It's not just gone off the maps—they passed laws. You can't print the name. You can't teach it. My gran's whole country, everything, the cities and the songs and everybody's names, they just—" His hands made a gesture that wasn't a gesture, opening on nothing. "They call it the Erasure. Like it never happened. And carrying the seal—" He finally looked up at Sable, and his eyes were wet and terrified and furious all at once, seventeen years old and suddenly much older. "Captain, carrying the seal is a hanging offense. It's in the statutes. Article Nine. *Trafficking in erased insignia.* They don't even pretend it's about treason anymore. It's about memory. Remembering is the crime."

The word hung in the hold. Sable found she was still holding the astrolabe, its inner rings turning with a sound like distant water, and she set it down carefully, the way you set down something sleeping.

"Doss," she said. "You served your conscription year in the ministry archives. Is he right?"

Doss's jaw worked. "Article Nine's real. Penalty's real. They hanged a bookbinder in Harrow's Ford eight years back. Old man. The charge was selling a map with the old borders on it. A *map*." He spat over the rail into the bilge, which was as close as Doss came to swearing. "I told you not to open it."

"You told me not to open it," Sable agreed. She looked down into the chest—at the ledgers, dozens of them, each one dense with names in that vine-script; at the letter stained with a dead man's blood; at the brass instrument that measured currents no living nation charted. And she felt the two duties pulling in her chest like lines rigged to opposite winches, the way they always pulled, the way they'd pulled her whole life: the duty to the law that kept the river safe and kept her ticket valid and kept men like Brant-of-the-world employed and fed; and the older duty, the one with no statute behind it, the one that said *the dead handed you this, and the dead do not take returns.*

"We put it back," Doss said. "We report the find. Harbormaster at Corben, honest declaration, salvage fee paid, done. Whatever this is, it's bigger than us, Captain. It's bigger than any of us. Let the ministry choke on it."

"And if the ministry burned Corben?" Marisol said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She nodded at the letter in Sable's hand. "That's what it says. *They are burning the outposts.* Corben Landing is the biggest outpost on this stretch of the river. We're tied up four miles downstream of it." She paused. "My brothers came from a river town that got renamed after the Erasure. New name, new history, new everything. My mother used to say the ministry didn't erase Vhaloria because it was dangerous." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, flour-white, had curled into fists. "She said they erased it because it was *true*."

The rain thickened overhead. Through the hull Sable could feel the river working at the anchor chain, patient, immense, indifferent to parliaments and statutes and the whole brief theater of human law. The river had been running before Vhaloria existed and it would run after every name in those ledgers was forgotten or remembered, whichever way she chose. The river did not care.

But she cared. That was the trouble with her. Always had been.

"Sable," Doss said, and it cost him something to use her name instead of her title, she could hear it. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking that a man bled on this letter getting it onto a sinking barge," she said. "I'm thinking he sealed it in lead so it would survive the water, and soldered it shut so it would survive the finders, and he did all of it knowing what Article Nine says, knowing exactly what it would cost him if he was caught, and he did it anyway. And then the river brought it to me. Out of every boat on this water, to mine." She looked around the hold at her crew—her ragged, stubborn, borrowed family—and saw the fear in all their faces and the something-else underneath it, the same something-else that was underneath her own fear, and she hated herself a little for what she was about to do to them. "I'm not deciding anything tonight. Nobody decides anything tonight. We sleep on it. But Pip—"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Tomorrow you're going to take me to your grandmother. And she's going to tell me what these books say." She held up one of the ledgers, its tea-colored spine cool in her palm, heavy as a heart. "Because I can't read a word of it. And I don't think anyone else on this river can, either."

Pip wiped his face with his sleeve. "Captain," he said, "my gran can't read it either. She only ever learned the prayers by ear." He hesitated, and Sable watched him decide—watched the moment land on him, the way the chest had landed on all of them, the weight of a thing that couldn't be given back. "But she'll know who can. There's supposed to be a keeper. Gran says the keepers were the ones the Erasure could never finish killing, because they kept the names in their heads." His voice dropped. "She says the last keeper went upriver. Toward the source. Before she was born."

Above them, through the deck planks, footsteps crossed the Magpie's roof—light, quick, wrong. Not Doss's tread, not Marisol's. Sable's hand went to the pry bar before her mind caught up, and the whole crew went still as the footsteps stopped directly overhead, and a voice drifted down through the hatch, pleasant and unhurried, a voice that belonged to a man standing in the rain with his hands clasped behind his back:

"Evening, Captain Nystrom. Ministry of Rivers and Reclamation. Might I come aboard? I have some questions about your salvage."
