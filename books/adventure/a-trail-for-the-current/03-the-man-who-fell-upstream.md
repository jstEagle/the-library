## 3. The Man Who Fell Upstream

The voice through the hatch was patient as rain.

Sable climbed the ladder slowly, pry bar still in her fist, and pushed the hatch open onto a man standing on her roof in a gray coat that had never once been rained on honestly. He had the kind of face you forgot while looking at it—middle-aged, mild, forgettable as a form to fill out—and he smiled down at her like they were neighbors discussing fence lines.

"Ministry of Rivers and Reclamation," he repeated. "A routine salvage audit. Storm debris came through this reach two nights ago. We're cataloguing claims."

"Salvage rights are filed at Corben Landing," Sable said. "Office hours are dawn to dusk. It's dusk."

"So it is." He didn't move. His eyes went past her shoulder, down into the dark of the hold, and for one long breath the pleasantness thinned to something with edges. Then it returned, smooth as river glass. "You'll be at the Landing, then. Perhaps we'll speak there. Captain Nystrom, isn't it? The *Magpie*." He said the boat's name the way a man reads an item off an inventory. "Safe travels upstream."

He descended the ladder bolted to the pilings without another word, and they watched from the rail until his lantern light merged with the lights of the village and vanished. Nobody spoke for a full minute. Rain ticked against the deck.

"That," Doss said finally, "was a man who already knows what we pulled up."

"He doesn't know anything," Sable said. "He suspects. There's a difference."

"There's a difference between drowning and being underwater, too," Marisol muttered, and went below to put a padlock on the chest herself.

They ran the *Magpie* downriver before first light, past the shoals and the drowned willows, and tied up at Corben Landing two days later with the chest hidden under tarpaulin and fish crates in the forward hold, and Sable told herself the knot in her stomach was just the current, just the season, just the ordinary weight of running a boat with more debts than planking. She told herself that all the way to the market.

* * *

Corben Landing on market day smelled of tar and frying onions and the green rot of the riverbank, and it roared. Stalls leaned together under patched awnings; bargemen shouted prices over each other's heads; children wove through the legs of the crowd stealing apples with the practiced grace of gulls. The *Magpie* lay at the public dock with her gangplank down, and Marisol had set up a folding table selling smoked eel and pickled mussels to fund their fuel, and Pip was supposed to be watching the plank and instead was watching a juggler, and Sable was haggling over engine oil with a supplier who lied the way other men breathed.

That was when the stranger came down the dock road.

She saw him from thirty yards and knew something was wrong before she knew what. He walked like a man carrying water across ice—one careful step, then another, his coat buttoned wrong, his left hand pressed flat against his side as if holding himself shut. He was young, maybe younger than Pip, though the gray in his face made age impossible to guess. The crowd parted around him without knowing why, the way crowds do around bleeding things.

He reached the gangplank. He looked at the boat—at the *Magpie*, at her name painted on the bow, at Sable standing at the rail—and something in his expression broke open with relief so naked it hurt to witness, the look of a drowning man who has decided a piece of driftwood is a ship.

"You're—" he started, and his knees went.

He fell onto the gangplank rather than off it, which was the only reason he didn't go into the river. The plank rang under him. The juggler stopped juggling. A hundred conversations died mid-word, and then the crowd did what crowds do: it formed a ring and watched.

Sable was down the plank before she'd decided to move. Up close he was worse—young, definitely young, with black stubble on a bloodless face and a stain spreading low on his left side that wasn't mud. His hand was clenched around something pressed to his chest, knuckles white, and when she knelt and reached for him he flinched and clutched it harder.

"Don't," he rasped. "Don't take it. Please. I carried it—I carried it from—"

"Marisol!" Sable shouted over her shoulder. "Blankets, my kit, now! And find a doctor—there'll be one by the chandler's—" She turned back, got an arm under him. He weighed nothing. That frightened her more than the blood did. "Easy. You're aboard. You're safe."

"No," he said, and almost laughed, wet and terrible. "No, I'm not. But you could be. If you're quick."

They got him onto the aft deck, into the lee of the wheelhouse, out of the eyes of the crowd—or most of them. Rings close slowly when there's blood involved. Doss came up the plank last and turned at the bottom, arms crossed, a wall of a man making himself a wall. Marisol knelt with the kit and cut the coat away, and sucked air through her teeth.

It was a knife wound, low on the ribs, ugly and deep and days old, dressed once with field bandaging gone brown and stiff, and the flesh around it was hot and streaked. Fever had been working on him for a while. Whatever had killed him was going to finish the job whether the knife had missed anything vital or not.

"Fever's in it," Marisol said quietly. "Captain—he's burning up."

"I know." Sable took his hand. It was cold despite the fever, cold as the chest had been cold, and that coincidence sat in her stomach like a stone. "Hey. Look at me. What's your name?"

"Tomas." His eyes wandered, found hers, held with effort. "Tomas Reyle. Listen—listen to me. There isn't time for the rest of it. They're behind me. Two men, gray coats, they were at Vell's Ferry yesterday, they'll be here by—"

"I know about the gray coats," Sable said.

Something flickered in his face. Hope, or its corpse. "Then you know why." His grip tightened on whatever he held. Slowly, wincing, he brought it up between them: oilcloth, wrapped tight and sealed with pitch, the size of a prayer book. "The route. Half of it. The other half—the other half went into the river at Vell's Ferry when they caught Ferro. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I couldn't—" He coughed, and the cough bent him double, and Marisol steadied him, and when he straightened there was blood on his lips and he spoke faster, spending words like a man spending his last coins. "The source. You have to take it to the keeper at the source. Upstream. Past the locks, past the Judgment, into the gorge. The archive is real. Everyone says it's a story—it's not a story, it's *real*, and it holds—"

His voice cracked. He swallowed. The crowd noise seemed very far away suddenly, the river loud beneath the hull.

"It holds the names," Tomas said. "All of them. Every name the Erasure deleted. Forty years of ledgers—births, deaths, marriages, villages, ships, songs—who lived, who they loved, what they were called before the maps were burned. My grandmother's name is in there. She was born in a town that officially never existed, and when she died there was nowhere to bury her because the town had been unwritten, do you understand? Do you understand what it is to come from a place that the world has agreed never happened?" Tears cut clean tracks through the grime on his face. "I'm nobody. I'm a courier. But those names—they're the only bones some of us will ever get. And the Ministry would burn them rather than let them be read. They've spent forty years teaching everyone to be ashamed of remembering."

Pip had come down from the rail without anyone noticing. He stood at the edge of the little circle, and his face was the face he'd worn in the hold when the seal came off the chest—pale, stricken, caught. Sable saw him look at the oilcloth packet and then away, jaw tight, and she thought: *he knows. He's known since the night on the roof.*

"The chest," Sable said carefully. "The salvage. Two nights ago, the snagged barge—"

"Was ours," Tomas whispered. "Ferro's and mine. We were running the route downstream to a safe house when the storm hit. Ferro went down with the barge. Or—" his eyes closed, opened, "—or he swam, and I hope to every god he swam. The chest was everything. Ledgers, instruments, the master letter. If it's in your hold, then the river chose you, Captain. The river keeps what it keeps and gives it where it's needed. That's what my grandmother believed. That's what—" Another cough, longer, wracking. When it passed he sagged against the wheelhouse wall, and Marisol looked up at Sable and gave a small, terrible shake of her head.

"How far to the source?" Sable asked, because someone had to ask practical questions while the world came apart. "In weeks. In this season."

"Five. Six if the monsoon comes early." His mouth twisted. "The locks are closed. Officially. But the old keepers remember the old signals. Ask the stones. The stones remember." His hand found hers again, gripped with the last of his strength, and his eyes fixed on hers with a ferocity that had nothing left behind it but itself. "I begged at three docks before yours. Three captains turned me away. One of them was probably already reporting me. You won't turn me away. I can see it. You've already lost people—the way you hold yourself, the way you don't let anyone stand behind you. So you know. You know what it is to carry names that can't be set down anywhere." His breath hitched. "Finish it. Finish my delivery. Say it. Say you'll finish it."

The crowd pressed closer. Somewhere back along the dock road, Sable heard a change in its texture—a ripple, voices dropping, heads turning toward two men in gray walking side by side, unhurried, hands clasped behind their backs, moving through the market the way a blade moves through dough.

There was no time to decide. That was the mercy of it, or the cruelty: the decision was already made, had been made the night she pried open a dead nation's wax, and everything since was just the river carrying the news to her.

"I'll finish it," Sable said. "I swear it on the hull. On my father's hull. On the *Magpie*."

Tomas smiled. It transformed him, briefly, into whoever he'd been before the knife. "Good," he said. "Good. Then give me a minute of quiet, and let them think I told you nothing. Let them think you're just a smuggler. Smugglers they can buy. Keepers they have to kill." His grip loosened. "The astrolabe—don't sell it. Don't trade it. It's not treasure. It's a *key*. The keeper at the source will know. Ask the stones. Ask the—"

He didn't finish. His head tipped back against the wheelhouse, and Marisol caught him, and for a moment Sable thought it was sleep, and then she saw Marisol's hands, and it wasn't sleep.

* * *

They laid him on the deck boards and covered his face with canvas, and Sable stood over him for the length of ten heartbeats, doing arithmetic she hated. A boy—barely a man—with a grandmother's name in a burned country, dead on her deck with his delivery half-finished, and two gray coats fifty yards away and closing.

"Pip," she said quietly. "Take the packet. Flour barrel, third from the port side, under the top layer. Move."

Pip moved. He was fast when it mattered; she'd say that for him. The oilcloth vanished into his jacket and Pip vanished into the hold, and Sable turned to Doss. "Get the plank up. We're not taking passengers today and we're not answering questions. Marisol—" but Marisol was already kneeling by the body, and had drawn the canvas back from the dead man's hand, and was staring at his palm.

"Captain," she said. "Look."

Pressed into the skin of his palm, ink transferred from something he'd held too long, was a partial impression: a route-map, folded and refolded a hundred times, and along its visible edge, in a fine antique hand, a line of script in no alphabet Sable recognized—except that some deep-buried part of her did recognize it, the way you recognize a lullaby sung in a language you were never taught. Below it, in trade-tongue, small and neat:

*To the keeper at the source, from the last of the order. The river remembers what the maps forget.*

And beneath that, a single word in the old script, and Sable's throat closed around it before her mind translated, because her father used to say it at the wheel in bad water, said it like a prayer, and she'd never once asked him what it meant or where he'd learned it, and now she never could, because the word was *Vhalor* and her father had been dead eleven years and had apparently spoken the language of a nation that had never existed.

"Sable." Marisol's voice, careful. "You've gone white as sailcloth."

"My father," Sable heard herself say, from a great distance, "used to sing river-chants in a tongue nobody else on this water ever used. I thought it was just—old family stuff. River families have their own words." She looked down at the dead courier, at the word in his palm, at the gray coats now twenty yards out and parting the crowd like a wound parting skin. "It wasn't family stuff. It was the same stuff."

The gray coats reached the foot of the gangplank, where Doss stood with his arms crossed, immovable as a piling. The taller one produced papers. "Ministry of Rivers and Reclamation," he said, in the same pleasant, unhurried voice Sable had heard through her own roof two nights ago. Inspector Calloway Brant looked up at her over the body of the man who had died delivering a nation's memory to her deck, and smiled his neighborly smile.

"Captain Nystrom," he said. "We really must stop meeting like this. Now—about that salvage audit."
