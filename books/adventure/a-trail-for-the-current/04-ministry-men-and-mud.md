## 4. Ministry Men and Mud

The launch came upriver at half past nine, white hull, grey smoke, moving with the unhurried authority of something that had never once had to worry about fuel costs. Sable saw it first from the wheelhouse, a smudge against the morning haze where the Corben Landing channel bent north, and she stood very still for a long moment with her coffee going cold in her hand.

Government boats didn't come to the salvage grounds. There was nothing here worth an audit—half-sunk barges, storm wrack, mud. Government boats came when someone wanted to look at you, and wanted you to know they were looking.

"Pip," she called down the companionway. "Company."

She heard him clatter up two steps at a time, then stop dead behind her shoulder. Through the salt-flecked glass they watched the launch grow definition: the twin pennants at the stern, the matte-grey gun mount under its tarpaulin, the man standing at the bow in a coat too good for the river.

"That's a ministry hull," Pip said quietly.

"I can see that."

"Salvage audit's a Revenue Office thing. Ministry doesn't do audits." He swallowed. "Ministry does *inspections*."

Sable set down her cup. Below her feet, through the deck plates, she could feel the Magpie's old heart beating—the diesel's idle throb, Doss keeping her warm in case the day turned ugly. The chest sat forty feet aft of them, under a false floor in the hold, wrapped in oilcloth, with four hundred pounds of chain scrap piled on top of it because Marisol had insisted that no inspector on earth would volunteer to move chain scrap.

"Marisol," Sable said into the voice tube. "Flour barrel. Now."

There was a pause, then Marisol's voice, tinny and unbothered: "Which flour barrel?"

"The one you know I mean."

* * *

The launch came alongside with a bump so gentle it was almost courteous, and a gangway went down before Sable had even finished tying off. The man who crossed was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a charcoal coat brushed clean of every speck of river. He moved like a man who had never once hurried and never once been late. Behind him came two assistants in matching grey, carrying document cases, and neither of them looked at anything except the deck directly in front of their shoes.

"Captain Nystrom." He extended a hand. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to exactly the pressure a handshake required and not a grain more. "Inspector Calloway Brant, Ministry of Records and Reclamation. This is my associate, Mr. Vell, and Mr. Ostrer."

"Sable Nystrom. You're a long way from any records I know about."

"The river is everyone's business, Captain. That's rather the point of it." He produced a leather folder and opened it with one finger. "I'm conducting a salvage audit. Storm debris season generates a great deal of... material claims. We've found discrepancies in the Corben Landing registry. Barges reported lost that were never properly accounted for. I'm boarding all vessels working the stretch between the Landing and the shoals."

"All vessels," Sable repeated. "How many is that?"

"Three. Yours is the second." He smiled. It was a well-made smile, machined like everything else about him, and it reached his mouth without troubling his eyes. "You'll forgive the intrusion. I understand the Magpie has been... industrious this week."

"We pulled a snagged barge off the shallows below Miller's Bend. Standard salvage, salvage rights filed, share posted with the harbormaster."

"Yes. The *Corvane*." Brant said the name the way a man turns over a stone to see what's underneath. "Interesting vessel. Interesting cargo manifest—or rather, the interesting absence of one. A barge running the upper channel with no manifest, no registered owner, no port of origin. And then it sinks, and a licensed salvor hauls up—" he paused delicately, "—whatever there was to haul up. You'll understand my curiosity."

"There wasn't much to haul up. Mud, mostly. Some rotted timber. A few barrels of spoiled grain we couldn't even give away."

"Mud," Brant said pleasantly. "Mostly."

Behind him, one of his associates—Vell, the taller one—had drifted two steps toward the hold hatch, and Sable watched him do it with the flat patience of a woman who had spent thirty years reading water. Men always thought looking away meant not seeing. She'd learned the opposite on her first voyage: the river shows you what matters by what it keeps doing when you stop watching.

"You're welcome to inspect the boat, Inspector. Mind the third stair, it's loose. Doss hasn't gotten to it."

"How is your engineer? Doss, isn't it? Ferren Doss." Brant consulted his folder again, though Sable would have bet her boat he knew the page by heart. "Long service record. Steady man. Family in Corben Landing?"

"He has a daughter."

"A daughter. Yes." Something flickered across Brant's face—not quite warmth, not quite threat, just the acknowledgment of a fact placed carefully on a table between them. "It's good work, Captain. Salvage. Honest, necessary. The ministry values men and women who do honest, necessary work, and we try to make sure they're left alone to do it." He closed the folder. "Provided the paperwork stays honest too."

"Is there some reason it wouldn't be?"

"No reason at all." He gestured graciously toward the main deck. "Lead on."

* * *

The inspection took two hours, and Sable hated every minute of it with a hatred so controlled it might have been mistaken for calm.

Brant did not rush. That was the thing she couldn't shake, walking behind him through her own boat: he did not rush anywhere, did not reach for anything, did not ask a single question whose answer he seemed to need. He examined the engine room while Doss stood rigid as a fence post beside his beloved machinery, and complimented the boiler work in a tone that made Doss's jaw tighten. He toured the galley, where Marisol stirred a pot of stew that smelled like heaven and tasted, Sable knew, like a warning—Marisol cooked angry when she was scared, and the stew had enough pepper in it to strip paint. He walked the cargo deck, nudged the chain scrap pile once with his shoe, and stood looking at it for eleven seconds by Sable's count.

Eleven seconds was a long time for a man who didn't rush.

"Chain," he said. "A great deal of chain."

"Ballast. Old mooring chain we strip off wrecks and resell by weight. It's listed on our salvage declaration."

"Of course it is." He crouched, picked up a link, weighed it in his palm, set it back with the care of a man returning a borrowed book. "Heavy stuff. Difficult to move."

"Very."

"And yet you managed to load it aboard in a single afternoon, according to your own log."

"Doss has a winch."

"Doss has a winch," Brant agreed mildly, and rose, and dusted his hands, and smiled at her again with those untroubled grey eyes. "Captain Nystrom, may I be candid with you? Professionally candid."

"You've been candid since you stepped aboard."

"I should hope so. Candor saves everyone time." He strolled to the rail and looked out over the brown water, hands clasped behind his back, coat stirring faintly in the breeze. "Forty years ago, this country did a difficult thing. An unpleasant thing, but a necessary one. A nation that could not govern itself, that exported nothing but trouble and treason—a wound on the map. We closed that wound. It hurt. Healing hurts." He turned his head just enough to catch her profile. "And like any healing wound, it must occasionally be checked. For infection. For things buried beneath it that fester."

"I'm a riverboat captain, Inspector."

"You are a riverboat captain who pulled a sealed chest out of a dead barge three days ago." He said it lightly, conversationally, watching the water. "Or so a fisherman downstream believes he saw. Fishermen see such strange things. Lead-lined chests. Wax seals. Of course, fishermen also see mermaids, so one learns to discount the testimony."

Sable kept her breathing even and her face bored. Somewhere below them, under chain scrap and oilcloth, the chest sat with its impossible seal, and she willed herself to think about nothing at all—about bilge water and fuel gauges and the price of rope.

"If you find a chest," she said, "you're welcome to it. Mud and all."

"Mm." Brant straightened his cuffs. "Well. I won't take more of your morning. The audit will show no irregularities—I'm sure of it, and I'll say so in my report." He extended his hand once more, and this time the grip held a half-second longer than calibration allowed. "One piece of professional advice, freely given. The monsoon closes the upper gorge in six weeks. After that, nobody goes upstream, and nobody comes down. A wise captain spends that window on safe, profitable, *documented* work. The river rewards patience, Captain. It punishes curiosity. I'm told you of all people know what the river does to the curious."

The words landed like a hook set deep. Sable let them sit there.

"Safe travels, Inspector."

"Oh, I don't travel, Captain. I wait." He descended the gangway, paused at the bottom, and looked back up at her with something almost like kindness. "Give my regards to your crew. All of them. Even the young one—Pip, is it? Grandmother from the old country, I hear. How remarkable, how these little threads survive." He touched his hat brim. "Until next time."

The launch pulled away, white and grey and unhurried, and Sable stood at the rail until it was a smudge again, and then until it was nothing at all.

* * *

They found the beacon at dusk.

Doss found it, actually—crawled under the hull plates in the lazarette chasing a slow leak in the stuffing box, swearing in three languages, and came out holding a small brass-cased cylinder wired neatly into the electrical system with a splice so clean it could have been textbook work.

"It's drawing off our battery," he said, turning it in his thick fingers. "Transmits on a government band. Range, I'd guess, fifty miles if the weather's kind. Somebody wanted to know where we went without having to follow us."

"When?" Sable asked.

"Splice is fresh. Days, not weeks." Doss's face was grim in the lamplight. "Could've been the launch this morning. Could've been anyone at Corben Landing. Half the dockhands would plant one for a bottle of gin."

Pip had gone the color of dishwater. "They know about my grandmother. He said—he said *give my regards*, he knew, how did he—"

"Because knowing is his job," Sable said. "Knowing is the whole job."

Marisol wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the beacon the way she'd look at a rat in the flour. "So what now? We smash it, they know we smashed it. We leave it, they follow us everywhere we go."

"We leave it," Sable said.

Four faces turned to her.

"We leave it running, and we use it." She took the cylinder from Doss, felt its small dumb weight, its patient little pulse of signal singing their position out into the dark to a man who waited instead of traveled. "He told us exactly who he is, Pip. He doesn't chase. He waits. Which means right now, somewhere south of us, Calloway Brant is sitting in a comfortable chair watching a dot on a map—and believing every word I fed him about mud and spoiled grain."

"And when the dot stops being boring?" Doss asked.

"Then he comes, and we'll know it, because we'll know what he knows." She handed the beacon back. "Wire it back in exactly how you found it. Cleaner, even. And Doss—" she met his eyes, and held them, and saw the fear in them and the thing underneath the fear, "—when the time comes, I want you able to kill that signal in under ten seconds. Can you build me that?"

Doss looked at the beacon a long moment. Then at his hands. Then, briefly, somewhere far away that Sable understood was a daughter in Corben Landing.

"Yeah," he said finally. "Yeah, I can build you that."

Marisol snorted and went back to her galley, muttering about inspectors and their pepper tolerance. Pip lingered, turning his cap in his hands.

"Captain. What he said about the wound on the map. About it being necessary." His voice cracked and steadied itself. "My gran used to say the Erasure wasn't a healing. It was an amputation. And amputations don't heal anything. They just teach the body to pretend the leg's still there."

Sable looked at the boy—at the dead nation living in his face—and out past him at the dark river sliding past the hull, carrying everything it carried, keeping every receipt.

"Get some sleep," she said. "We cast off before dawn."

"Where to?"

She didn't answer that. Not yet. But in the wheelhouse, alone, she took out the letter addressed to *the keeper at the source*, and read it again by lamplight while the beacon pulsed its faithful lie into the night, telling a patient man in a comfortable chair that the Magpie was exactly where a boring, law-abiding boat ought to be.

For now.

In the morning, she'd feed the dot a detour worth watching—and then lose it in the fog banks above Miller's Bend, where even ministries went blind.
