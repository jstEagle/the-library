## 5. Ivo Kestrel Buys a Drink

The Saltcrust Tavern stood where Corben Landing's boardwalk gave up pretending to be respectable and surrendered to the mud. Its sign had once depicted a fish; decades of river damp had eaten it down to a gray suggestion, a shape that might have been a fish or a drowned man or nothing at all. Inside, the air was thick with lamp oil, spilled beer, and the particular melancholy of a town that existed only because the river paused there long enough to be taxed.

Sable sat with her back to the wall, as she always did, a glass of plum brandy untouched in front of her. Across the room, Pip was losing money at dice to a pair of dredge workers who smelled of tar and cruelty, and Marisol was arguing with the cook about the proper way to treat a river eel, which was a conversation that could last all night and had before. Doss was back aboard the Magpie, which was where Doss preferred to be, among his engines, where things behaved according to laws he understood.

The chest was hidden. The documents were hidden inside the chest. The astrolabe was hidden inside a flour barrel that Marisol had declared off-limits to all hands with a look that could have curdled cream. And still Sable could not stop seeing it—that impossible brass instrument with its rings within rings, its engraving fine as spider silk, its weight in her hands like a held breath. She had held a lot of things in her life: mooring lines under strain, drowning men, her father's cold hand. The astrolabe had felt like all of them at once.

"You're drinking that brandy like it owes you an apology," said a voice at her elbow.

She hadn't heard him sit down. That alone put her teeth on edge. Corben Landing's taverns were loud, but the space around this man seemed to have gone quiet, as if the noise itself had decided not to bother him.

He was maybe forty, lean in the way of men who had spent years being careful with money and food, with dark hair going gray at the temples and a face that would have been handsome if it had ever once relaxed. He wore a river pilot's oilskin two sizes too large and a traveler's boots, and on the table between his hands lay a passport—open, face up, as casual as a man showing off a winning hand.

The passport said his name was Tam Voss, of Harrow Bend. It was a very good forgery. Sable knew because she'd seen a dozen bad ones, and because the ink of the official stamp was the wrong shade of violet, and because no man from Harrow Bend sat like that—spine straight, hands still, watching the room the way a scholar watches a text he is translating.

"Captain Nystrom," he said.

"I don't know you."

"No. But you know what I'm going to say, or you wouldn't have gone still as a heron the moment I sat down." He closed the passport with one finger and slid it away into his coat. "My name is Ivo Kestrel. I'd like to buy you a drink, but you haven't touched the one you have, so instead I'd like to buy you six weeks of your time."

Sable turned her glass a quarter-turn on the scarred wood. "You've got the wrong boat."

"I've got the only boat." He said it without heat, the way a man states the weather. "The Magpie draws four feet loaded. She's got a shallow-draft hull with a reinforced skeg—Corben shipyard, thirty years ago, the last of that run. She can pass the shoals above the second lock at half-water, which nothing else on this river can do right now. And her captain has a reputation for running water other captains call suicide."

"My reputation is mostly dead men."

"I know that too." Something moved behind his eyes—not pity; she'd have thrown her brandy at him for pity—but recognition, the look of a man who had carried his own dead a long way. "Four of them. Six years ago. The inquiry blamed you. The river didn't."

Sable set the glass down with a click that was louder than she intended. "You've been reading old newspapers."

"I've been reading everything I could find for forty years. It's a habit of my profession." He leaned forward, and now his voice dropped to something meant only for her, a thread of sound beneath the tavern's din. "I am the last trained keeper of the Vhalorian archive, Captain. My grandfather held the office before me, and his mother before him, back eleven generations to the founding of the collection. I was nine years old when the Erasure came. I left Vhaloria in a grain barge with my mother's hand over my mouth so I wouldn't cry out at the checkpoint. I have spent my whole life in exile learning to be nobody, and I am very, very tired of it."

The word *Vhaloria* landed in Sable's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Around them the tavern went on—dice rattling, eels being slandered, a fiddler tuning in the corner—but it seemed to her that the room had tilted, that everything in it was sliding slowly toward this table.

"You should be careful," she said quietly. "Saying that word in public. There are men who hang people for it."

"I know. I've spent the last three days watching one of them watch you."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the tavern's river damp. "Inspector Brant."

"He has a name for the paperwork. He has other names among his own people. He boarded your boat under a salvage pretext, and he left something behind on your hull when he did it—a beacon, if his men are competent, and they are. You should check your stern, below the waterline, next time you careen. But that's not why I'm here." He reached into his coat again, and Sable's hand went to the knife at her belt before she could stop it, and he paused, and very deliberately, slowly, drew out not a weapon but a folded square of oilcloth, which he unwrapped on the table.

Gold. Four coins of it, heavy, old, stamped with the twin-river crest of a nation that had not existed for forty years. They caught the lamplight and threw it back warm, the way gold does, the way it has always tricked people into thinking it means safety.

"There's more," Kestrel said. "A great deal more. Recovered from the old treasury vaults before the ministry sealed them—scattered, hidden, carried out by people like me, a coin at a time, for forty years. Enough to buy you a new boat. Enough to buy you ten new boats, and a crew that never has to smuggle anything, and a life where no one asks you to run the Judgment rapids for pennies. I'm offering you a fortune, Captain Nystrom, to do one thing: take me upstream. Past the three closed locks. To the source of the river. Before the monsoon makes landfall—six weeks, perhaps less."

Sable looked at the coins for a long moment. Then she looked at him, and what she saw was a man holding himself together with posture and forged paper, and underneath it something that had been waiting forty years to be said aloud.

"No," she said.

He didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. "May I ask why?"

"You may ask." She stood, dropping a coin of her own—Corben currency, dull and honest—beside the brandy she hadn't drunk. "I run cargo. I don't run revolutions. And I don't run whatever it is you're actually asking for, because a man doesn't offer a fortune for a boat ride. He offers a fortune to make someone else carry the risk of a thing he won't carry himself."

"I would carry it," Kestrel said, and there was the first crack in him, a hairline fracture in the careful voice. "I would carry it gladly. I simply can't. I don't know the river. I don't know the locks. I don't know how to keep a boat alive in white water, and the archive doesn't need a dead keeper, it needs a living one, and it needs—" He stopped himself. Drew a breath. When he spoke again the scholar's calm was back, but thinner, like ice over moving water. "Captain. There are names in that archive. Thousands of them. Everyone the Erasure deleted—every village renamed, every family scattered, every person struck from the registries so thoroughly that their own grandchildren don't know what they were. I am the only person alive who knows how to read the catalog. If I die, the names die with me. If the archive is found by the ministry first, the names are burned, and this time there will be no one left to remember there was ever anything to burn."

It was a good speech. Sable had heard good speeches before—from salvage brokers, from press-gang recruiters, from her father, once, explaining why her mother had to be left behind in the flooded village. Good speeches were what men used when the truth wouldn't fit through the door.

"The answer's still no," she said. "Find another boat."

"There is no other boat. There is only the Magpie, and six weeks, and you." He rose too, and for a moment they stood facing each other across the little table with its abandoned gold, and the tavern noise seemed very far away, as though they were standing in the eye of something. "You opened the chest," he said. "They told me a river captain wouldn't. They told me you'd sell it back to the salvagers or sink it out of fear. But you opened it. I know you did, because the lock was a Vhalorian tumbler-seal and it can only be opened from the left, and no one who didn't already wonder would ever have tried."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because I built that seal," Ivo Kestrel said. "Or my grandfather did, before I was born, and I've restored six of them since. Captain—I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to keep wondering. That's all any of us can do now."

He left a coin on the table anyway—buying the drink he'd promised, buying nothing—and walked out into the rain, and Sable stood there a long time, watching the door swing, feeling like a woman who had just refused a fortune and lost something worth more.

* * *

She was still awake at two in the morning, sitting in the Magpie's wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, when the light over the harbor changed.

It started as a glow, orange and wrong, bleeding up from behind the warehouse row—the long timber sheds where Corben Landing stored resin, rope, and the bales of raw cotton that kept the town's ledgers honest. Then came the sound, arriving late the way sound does across water: a low roar, and beneath it, voices. Shouting. Running feet on the boardwalk.

Sable was out the wheelhouse door before she'd decided to move.

The third warehouse down was burning. Not smoldering—*burning*, flames already through the roof in three places, the tarred planking catching with a hunger that said lamp oil, said intent, said *this was not an accident*. Figures milled on the dock in their nightclothes, hauling a hand-pump that was too far away to matter. And at the edge of the firelight, unhurried, stood two men in the gray coats of the Ministry of Records and Reclamation, watching the fire the way inspectors watch a scale settle.

"Pip." The name came out of her like a cough. Pip slept aboard. Pip had gone to the tavern and then—

She found him on the gangplank, barefoot, frozen, his face lit orange and his eyes fixed on the burning warehouse with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it herself, once, watching the river take four people she loved. He was holding something against his chest. A satchel. Papers.

"They were asking about me," he said. His voice was very small. "The gray coats. At the tavern. Asking who'd been asking about Vhalorian marks. And then the warehouse—I was in the warehouse, Captain. I was sleeping in the loft. Marisol told me not to, she said it wasn't safe, and I—" He was shaking. "They weren't trying to burn it down. They were trying to burn *me* out."

Sable took his shoulder and turned him away from the fire, away from the gray coats, toward the Magpie, toward home. "Marisol," she called, and Marisol was already coming down the deck with a lantern, her face grim, Doss behind her in his shirtsleeves with a wrench in his hand as though a wrench had ever stopped anything.

"We cast off," Sable said. "Now. Tonight. Before they come back with more than fire."

"Upstream?" Doss said. Not a question, really. Doss's questions were just statements he hadn't accepted yet.

"Upstream."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment, and then at Pip, shivering and soot-streaked and clutching his stolen copies of a dead nation's map. Something in Marisol's face closed like a hand around a decision. "I'll get the boiler warm," she said.

"Captain." The voice came from the shadows at the end of the boardwalk, and every one of them turned, and there stood Ivo Kestrel with rain running off the too-large oilskin and firelight on his face. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder and the look of a man who had been running for forty years and had just decided, finally, to run in a direction. "You'll need someone who can read the lock-signals above the second gate. No one alive knows them but me. And you'll need to leave *now*, because in about four minutes those two men are going to remember that the Magpie's stern is where they wired their beacon, and they're going to come down here to cut it loose—or to cut loose something else."

Sable looked at him. At the fire climbing into the wet night sky. At her crew—her ragged, stubborn, impossible crew—waiting on the deck of her father's boat for her word.

"The stern," she said to Doss. "Cut the beacon. Then cast off."

Doss went. Kestrel came up the gangplank, and Sable didn't stop him, and didn't welcome him either; she simply stood aside, which in the grammar of the river meant the same thing.

"I refused you," she said, as he passed.

"I know," said Ivo Kestrel. "I'm hoping you'll reconsider. I can be very patient. I've had forty years of practice."

The Magpie's boiler woke with a shudder and a groan, and the lines came in, and the current took her bow and turned it, gently, irresistibly, upstream—into the dark, into the closing season, toward three locked gates and a source that had been waiting a long time to be found.
