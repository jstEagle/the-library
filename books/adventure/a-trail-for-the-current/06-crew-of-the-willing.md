## 6. Crew of the Willing

Sable laid it out on the galley table the way she'd lay out charts before a bad crossing: everything visible, nothing softened.

The chest sat open between them, its lead lining dull as old pewter. The ledgers were stacked in a brick beside the astrolabe, and the letter—*to the keeper at the source*—lay on top where everyone could see it. Marisol had wiped down the table first. That was Marisol: even for a mutiny or a hanging offense, you cleared away the crumbs.

"Here's the shape of it," Sable said. "A man died on our gangplank asking me to carry this upriver. Ministry men burned a warehouse last night looking for Pip. There's an inspector who smiles like a knife going in, and he'll be back. We can burn the papers, sink the chest in Corben mud, and pretend we never saw any of it. Or—" she tapped the letter once, "—we run the river to the source. Three closed locks between here and there. Monsoon lands in six weeks. And every government from here to the headwaters will want us stopped."

Nobody spoke. The lamp swung gently with the Magpie's breathing against her lines, throwing their shadows long across the bulkhead.

"So I'm not deciding," Sable finished. "I've decided enough for this crew in my time. This one we vote on. All in, or all out. No half-measures—if anyone stays behind, they stay clean of it."

Doss spoke first, because Doss always did when silence got heavy. He was turning his wedding ring around his finger, that old habit, the brass knuckles of it catching lamplight.

"No," he said.

Marisol's chin came up. Pip looked stricken.

"I vote no," Doss repeated, and his voice was steady as a bearing. "It's a fool's run. Closed locks, ministry gunboats, monsoon water in a gorge that eats boats twice our size. And for what? Papers. Dead men's names." He shook his head slowly. "I've got a daughter in Corben who writes me every week. I'm not making her an orphan for ink."

The word *orphan* landed hard on Pip; Sable saw him flinch. She nodded. "Noted. Anyone else?"

"I'm in," Marisol said, and there was no hesitation in it at all, which was somehow worse than hesitation would have been. She folded her dish towel with terrible precision. "You all know why. The Erasure took my brothers. Tomás and Rafael, gone off the rolls like they'd never drawn breath. My mother went to the records office in Vhal City—what was Vhal City—and they told her there was no such person as Rafael Marisol-Ondi. Not *dead*. *No such person.*" Her hands stilled on the towel. "I spent thirty years thinking grief was the worst thing the ministry could do to me. Then I learned they could do worse than kill a man. They can unmake him." She looked at the ledgers. "If those books hold his name—his real name, written down somewhere the fire couldn't reach—then I sail. I'd sail alone if I had to."

"You won't," Pip said quickly, and then, when they all turned to him, he stood up straighter than Sable had ever seen him stand. He was nineteen and still growing into his own shoulders, but just then he seemed taller than the deckhead. "I claim the chest."

"Claim it," Sable said carefully.

"It's mine. By blood." His voice cracked and he pushed through it. "My grandmother was Vhalorian. Amelie Kestrel-Voss, of the village of Halden's Reach—which isn't on any map anymore because it was never allowed to be. She taught me letters from a book she kept wrapped in oilcloth under her floorboards. She made me swear never to say the word *Vhaloria* out loud, and I've kept that oath since I was eight years old, and keeping it has felt like swallowing glass every day of my life." He put his hand flat on the chest lid, over the seal stamped into the lead. "She died two winters ago. If this is what she hid from, if this is why—then it's inheritance. And I say we take it home."

The lamp swung. Somewhere above them, rain started on the deck planks, soft as fingers.

"Doss?" Sable asked quietly.

Doss looked at his ring. Looked at Pip's hand on the chest. Looked at Marisol, whose face had gone wet without her seeming to know it.

"I voted no," he said gruffly. "Vote stands. It was a free vote and I'm free to be wrong about how it goes." He pushed back his chair. "Somebody has to keep the boiler alive through the Judgment rapids, and it sure as hell isn't going to be any of you three." He paused at the galley door. "We cast off tonight. Low water's coming up the tide tables by morning, and if we're running a closed lock I want moonlight on the gates, not sun."

He left. Marisol laughed—a broken, astonished sound—and pressed the towel to her eyes. Pip sat back down like his strings had been cut.

Sable looked at the chest, and at the letter, and felt the ordinary world she'd been clinging to since the storm finally let go of her altogether.

"All in," she said. "Then we've work to do. Marisol—flour barrels, double bottoms. Pip—get below and start copying that route-map onto plain paper, three copies, hide them separate. I'll see about fuel and fresh line." She blew out a breath. "And somebody find me the man with the forged passport. I have a feeling he hasn't left the dock."

* * *

Ivo Kestrel heard the Magpie's crew moving before dawn broke properly, and he counted the sounds the way he'd been trained to count catalog entries: one set of boots on the gangplank, heavy, unhurried—Doss. Two voices low in the wheelhouse, Nystrom and the boy. The smell of frying onions from the galley, which meant the cook was feeding people for a voyage, not a morning.

He'd watched taverns long enough to know how crews voted. They voted with their feet, and the Magpie's feet were moving toward the fuel barge.

Still, when Captain Nystrom found him at the end of the pier—coat collar up against the drizzle, pretending to study the shipping notices—he made himself wait for her to speak first. Pride was a luxury he'd mostly trained out of himself, but not entirely.

"You're still here," she said.

"You haven't given me a reason to leave."

"I'm not giving you one now. I'm telling you the terms." She came closer, and in the gray light he could see the sleeplessness carved under her eyes, and something else underneath that—the look of a woman who had already grieved one crew and was daring the river to make her do it again. "We cast off tonight. We run the first lock in the dark. If the ministry takes us, everyone aboard hangs or worse, and that includes a man traveling on a passport that says he was born in a town that burned down. You'll pull your weight. You'll take orders at the wheel. And if it comes to choosing between the chest and the crew, the crew wins. Every time. Say it back to me."

"The crew wins," Ivo said. "Every time."

Something in her shoulders loosened a fraction—not trust, he understood, but the settling of a weight into a known position. Trust would take longer. Trust would take white water, maybe. Trust might take more than he had to give.

"Six weeks to landfall," she said. "Three closed locks. And a man named Brant who will follow us the whole way like a debt."

"I know Brant's kind," Ivo said. "They closed my father's office with men like him standing in the doorway, very polite, holding the inventory list." He met her eyes. "Captain—I've waited twenty years to go home. I'll be no trouble at all until someone gives me a reason."

"That's what worries me," Sable Nystrom said, but the corner of her mouth moved, almost a smile, and she turned back toward her boat. "Be aboard by dusk. Bring whatever you're carrying in that satchel and don't lie to me about what it is."

"It's the master key to the archive," Ivo said, because she'd told him not to lie. "Brass. Older than either of us. It doesn't leave my body."

She stopped. For a moment she looked at him the way she must look at a stretch of river she hadn't run before—measuring, respectful, a little hungry.

"Aboard by dusk," she said again, softer, and walked the plank home.

* * *

They cast off at half past eleven, riding a tide Sable had timed to the minute, with the moon three days shy of full and generous about it. No running lights. No horn. Just the Magpie's engine at a murmur Doss had coaxed out of her—*she'll whisper for you, Captain, but she can't whisper forever*—and the black water sliding past the hull like a held breath.

Pip stood his first proper watch at the bow with a boathook he barely knew how to hold, and Sable let him, because a crew is built out of small trusts the same way a boat is built out of small nails. Marisol had the wheel instructions memorized and opinions about everything else. Ivo Kestrel stood at Sable's shoulder in the wheelhouse, silent as ballast, watching the channel markers slide by with an expression she couldn't read and didn't ask about yet.

The first lock was called Verren's Gate, and it had been closed since the Erasure—forty years of rust on forty years of machinery, a stone throat with a chain across it and a ministry notice board gone gray with weather: *CLOSED BY ORDER. NO PASSAGE. NO EXCEPTIONS.*

The lock-keeper's cottage had a lamp burning in it. That was the part nobody had planned for.

"Somebody lives here," Pip whispered down the deck. "Somebody's *home*."

"Keepers don't always get relieved," Ivo said quietly. "Sometimes they just get forgotten."

Sable brought the Magpie in slow, port side to the near wall, and killed the engine ten meters off the chain. The silence after was enormous. Water lapped the stones. Up on the lock wall, a figure emerged from the cottage—a woman, old, wrapped in a blanket against the night chill, carrying a lantern she raised high so the light fell across the boat.

Sable opened her mouth to talk her way through whatever came next, and never got the chance.

The old woman saw Ivo Kestrel's face in the lantern light and made a sound Sable had only ever heard at funerals.

"*Kestrel,*" the keeper breathed. "Little Ivo. Little Ivo Kestrel, home off the water." She was crying without any shame at all, lantern trembling in both hands. "You have his face. You have your mother's stubbornness and your father's face, and I looked after you both at the second gate when you were small enough to carry, and I thought—I thought everyone with that name was ash—"

"Margit," Ivo said, and his voice came apart on the name. "Margit Osten. You used to give me honey-bread on the gate steps."

"And you always ate it too fast and choked yourself." The old woman laughed and sobbed in the same breath. Then her face changed, quick as weather, and she leaned far out over the wall, dropping her voice though there was no one for miles. "Listen to me, boy. Listen. A boat went upstream two days ago. Black hull, no name painted, running dark like you are. Men aboard. One of them asked after the source by its *old name*, and no honest man knows the old name anymore." Her knuckles whitened on the lantern handle. "I gave them passage because they showed ministry paper and I'm too old to argue with paper. But I've regretted it every hour since, and now the river's sent me you, and I think the river knows its business better than I do."

She unshipped the chain herself, hand over hand, ancient arms shaking, refusing help.

"Go," Margit Osten said, opening the lock gates by lamplight while the moon stood witness. "Go fast, and go careful, and when you come back down—come back *down*, do you hear me, all of you—there'll be honey-bread on the step."

The Magpie rose in the lock chamber, stone walls closing around them slick and black, and Sable took the wheel for the lift with Ivo beside her and four hearts hammering in the dark.

Above the gates, the upper river waited—wide, cold, and running north toward country no map admitted to.

Two days' head start, Sable thought, easing throttle forward into the current. Somebody ahead of us wants the past buried deeper than it's buried already.

She opened the engine up, and the Magpie answered like she'd been waiting all her life to run this water.
