## 7. What the Locks Remember

The second lock announced itself before they saw it—a long gray seam of masonry stitched across the gorge, half-swallowed by ivy and forty years of indifference. Sable brought the Magpie in against the current with the engine muttering, Doss below decks keeping the boiler at a sullen simmer, and studied the structure through her glass.

She'd locked through this stretch a hundred times in her life. She'd never once looked at it.

Now, with Ivo Kestrel standing beside her at the rail, she couldn't stop looking.

"You see it?" he asked. He wasn't looking at the lock itself but at the stonework flanking the approach channel, where the blocks were dressed smooth and fitted so tightly a knife blade wouldn't find purchase. Cut into the keystone above the upstream gate was a symbol: a circle bisected by a wavy line, worn shallow by weather but unmistakable once you knew to see it.

"A maker's mark," Sable said. "Every lock on this river's got one. Masons were proud of their work back then."

"Masons, no." Ivo smiled, and there was something in it that made her chest tighten—not triumph, exactly. More like grief that had learned good manners. "That's not a mark. That's a word. *Vhal.* It means *the river remembers*. Every structure my people built carried it—locks, weirs, bridges, culverts. Not for pride. For instruction."

"Instruction for who?"

"For keepers." He turned from the rail. "Come below. There are things you should know before we reach the gate, and I'd rather say them to you than shout them across open water to strangers."

* * *

In the wheelhouse, with the door latched against Pip's curious ears, Ivo unrolled a sheet of oilcloth across the chart table. Beneath it were rubbings—charcoal on paper, dozens of them—of carvings taken from lock walls up and down the watershed. Sable recognized the style immediately because she'd been staring at its cousin all morning: circles, waves, chevrons, spirals, each one precise as mathematics.

"Lock-signals," Ivo said. "Before the Erasure, these rivers were worked by keeper-families. Each lock had one. They read the water, they maintained the gates, and when a vessel came through carrying something that mattered—something the archive needed moved—they signaled it downstream in code, carved or chalked or hung in flags, so the next keeper would know what was coming and how to receive it." His finger traced a spiral. "This one means *bearer under protection*. This—" a chevron over two waves "—means *pass by night, eyes closed*."

Sable went very still.

Because she knew that one. Not from books. From childhood. From standing on her father's shoulders in the wheelhouse of his own boat while he pointed at the old lock at Tarn's Bend and told her, *See that mark, little fish? That's an old mason's joke. Means the beer cellar's flooded.*

Her father, who had never drunk beer in his life. Her father, who had taught her to read water before she could read words, who had known every shoal and eddy of the upper river like the lines of his own palm, who had gone quiet and strange whenever anyone mentioned the years before the Erasure, and had died with a brass key on a cord around his neck that no lock in the province would open—or so Sable had assumed, all her life, without ever once asking herself why a man kept a key he could use nowhere.

"Ivo," she said. Her voice came out flat and careful, the way it did when the deck was shifting under her. "Show me the one for *keeper*."

He drew it without hesitation: a circle containing three vertical strokes, like a fence inside a full moon.

Sable walked to the bulkhead behind her wheel—the same bulkhead her father's boat had carried, salvaged plank by plank when she'd built the Magpie—and pried loose the trim board she'd never had reason to remove. Behind it, recessed into the wood, was a small brass plate, green with age, stamped with a circle and three strokes.

Below it, scratched smaller, almost shyly: *N.*

"Nystrom," she said. The name sat in her mouth like a stone. "My father's name."

Ivo didn't gloat. He didn't even look surprised, which somehow was worse. He only reached out and touched the plate with two fingers, gently, the way you'd touch a grave marker.

"The Nystroms held the third lock and the ferry crossing below it," he said quietly. "For four generations. Your father would have trained at the source, same as any keeper. Same as me." He looked up. "You've been steering a keeper's boat your whole life, Captain. You just weren't told."

The wheelhouse seemed suddenly too small, too warm. Sable gripped the wheel—not steering, just holding, the way you hold a railing in an earthquake. Forty years the nation had been scraped off the maps, its name forbidden, its records burned, its people scattered or worse. And all that time her father had stood at this wheel with erased blood in his veins and a secret bolted behind a trim board, saying nothing, teaching her everything anyway—the water-signs, the reading of currents, the exact angle to take a flood channel—all of it, she understood now, a curriculum. A keeper's training passed hand to hand in the only language left after the burning: the river itself.

"He never said," she managed. "Not once. Not even to my mother."

"They never did. My parents didn't either, until the end." Ivo's voice roughened. "It wasn't cowardice, whatever you're thinking. It was arithmetic. A child who doesn't know can't be made to betray. They kept us ignorant the way you'd keep cargo dry."

Sable thought of her father's hands on the wheel—her hands now, the same broad knuckles, the same scar across the left thumb—and felt the strange vertigo of discovering that a thing you'd built yourself had been built on foundations someone else poured in the dark.

"The key," she said. "The brass key he was buried with. Circle and three strokes?"

Ivo exhaled slowly. "That's a source-key, Captain. There are eleven. We have one." He tapped his own chest, where something hung beneath his shirt. "Yours makes twelve. And the archive needs—"

Above them, Pip's boots hit the deck hard, twice, their signal for *boat ahead, moving fast*, and the moment broke like ice underfoot.

* * *

The second lock's keeper came down to the gatehouse leaning on a stick older than she was, and Sable saw at once that the ministry's replacement-keepers—bored men in gray who checked papers and collected fees—had never gotten this far upstream. This was an old woman in a black shawl with a face like a riverbed: everything soft long since washed away, what remained held together by habit and stone.

She took Sable's papers without reading them. She was looking past Sable entirely, at Ivo, who stood at the gangplank with his hat in his hands like a boy called before a schoolmistress.

The stick clattered against the planks.

"*Kestrel,*" the old woman said. "*Little Ivo.*"

And then she was weeping—not delicately, but the way the very old weep, with their whole bodies, ugly and unashamed—and Ivo caught her as her knees went, and Sable turned her back to give them privacy and found she couldn't move anyway, because the old woman was saying his name over and over in Vhalorian, a language Sable had never heard spoken aloud and yet understood the shape of, the way you understand a song from before you were born.

Her name was Maren. She had known Ivo's mother, she said, when they were girls at the source. She had held the second lock alone for forty years, signing the ministry's false ledgers with one hand and keeping the true count in her head the whole time—every vessel, every cargo, every name that came through her gate, memorized, because paper burned but memory, memory they hadn't figured out how to drown.

She fed them stew in the gatehouse kitchen—real meat, which nearly caused a riot among the crew—and refused payment with such ferocity that Marisol wisely tucked the coins away. And it was over the stew, with Pip asleep sitting up and Doss smoking by the door, that Maren's tears dried and her face did the thing old faces do, folding shut into warning.

"You're not the first boat this month," she said. "There was one through two days ahead of you. Paddle-steamer, black hull, running under Corben registry papers that smelled wrong to me—too new, too clean, the ink not faded right." She fixed Sable with eyes pale as frost. "A man aboard asked me about the source. Asked polite, the way a knife is polite. I told him nothing, but I'll tell you what I already knew: he had the look of a man who'd sold something once and been paid in shame ever since. There's a name for that kind, from the old days."

"Vessarin," Ivo said quietly.

Maren nodded once. "And he wasn't alone. There were others aboard who didn't ask questions at all. Men who stand like soldiers pretending to be sailors." She reached across the table and gripped Sable's wrist with surprising strength. "Girl. Your father piloted my gate twenty times and never once told me his business, and I never asked, and that was the covenant. So hear me break it now, because the times have broken it first: whatever you carry, whoever hunts you—they are ahead of you, and the third lock has been closed by ministry order since spring, and the only way through is the old way, and the old way will cost you something you don't want to pay."

"What old way?" Sable asked.

Maren looked at Ivo. "Tell her about the flood-tunnel."

"I will," Ivo said. "At the third lock. Where she can see it."

Maren released Sable's wrist and stood, joints cracking like green wood, and went to a cabinet older than the ministry itself. From it she took a lantern of pierced tin, Vhalorian work, throwing light in patterns of stars and waves, and pressed it into Pip's sleeping hands, closing his fingers around the handle.

"For the boy," she said. "His grandmother lit hers every year on Remembrance Night, in a village that isn't there. Tell him the light remembers even when the map forgets."

Then she turned to Sable last, and took both her hands, and the frost in her eyes had thawed into something fierce and sorrowing.

"Your father came through my gate the week before they came for the keepers," she said. "Last time I ever saw him. He stood where you're standing and he said, *Maren, if anything happens to me, my daughter will run this river someday, and she won't know why the water feels like home to her—but she'll feel it.*" The old woman's grip tightened. "Do you feel it?"

Sable thought of the brass plate behind the trim board. Of the key buried with a man who'd spent his whole life pretending to be only a boatman. Of the current pulling north toward the source with the patience of something that had waited forty years and could wait forty more.

"Yes," she said.

"Then go," Maren said. "And go fast. Because the man in the black hull knows the tunnel too—and he's got two days on you, and no love in him for what waits at the top of this river."

They cast off at first light, the Magpie's bow swinging upstream into the narrowing gorge, brown water rising, the sky to the south gone the color of a bruise coming up. At the wheel, Sable passed the second lock's gate and, without thinking, raised two fingers to it—an old salute she'd invented as a girl, or thought she'd invented.

Behind the trim board, the brass plate caught the early sun.

Two days behind, and gaining.
