## 8. Rapids Called the Judgment

The gorge narrowed all morning, walls closing in like a book being shut around them, and by noon Sable could hear the Judgment before she saw it—a sound like the whole river clearing its throat, deep and continuous, the way a storm sounds from three ridges away.

She'd run rapids her whole life. She'd run the Widow's Teeth at Corben Landing with her father's hand over hers on the wheel, and she'd run the Splits alone at nineteen, and she'd come through the capsizing four years ago that had taken four good people into the water and never given them back. She knew what white water said when it meant to kill you. The Judgment was saying it now, saying it plainly, without any interest in whether anyone listened.

"Two miles," Ivo called down from the bow, where he'd been standing for an hour with his coat buttoned to the throat and his eyes on the water like a man reading scripture. "Maybe less."

"You've seen it?" Doss asked from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had long ago surrendered to grease.

"I've read about it." Ivo didn't turn around. "Every keeper's manual ends at the Judgment. It's the last thing they teach you before the source itself. The water-signs are carved into the approach stones—my instructors made me memorize them when I was nine years old, and I have spent thirty years hoping I'd never need them." He paused. "And needing them anyway. That's the whole of an archivist's life, if you think about it."

"That's comforting," Marisol said dryly, lashing down the last of the galley stores. "A man running our lives off a book he read as a child."

"It's not the book I'm reading." Ivo lifted one arm and pointed left, downstream, where the river bent against a wall of black granite. "There. See how the current folds? Where it goes smooth and green just before the drop—that's not calm water. That's the river holding its breath. Everything smooth above white water is a lie. You steer for the lies, Captain. They're the only honest signposts there are."

Sable looked where he pointed. He was right. There was a seam in the current, a tongue of glassy water threading between two boils, and it ran exactly where her own eye would never have trusted it—straight toward what looked like certain ruin.

"Pip," she said. "Get forward. I want your eyes on the starboard channel—if anything floats out of that mess, I want to know before it knows us."

Pip came up the deck steps slowly. He'd been quiet since the second lock, since the old keeper had wept at the sight of Ivo's face, and quieter still since the warning about the boat ahead. He took his place at the starboard rail and gripped it with both hands, and Sable noticed—filed away without comment—that his knuckles were white before they'd even reached the roar.

* * *

The Judgment announced itself properly half a mile on: a staircase of water dropping forty feet in three successive ledges, churning white from bank to bank except for that single green thread, no wider than the Magpie herself, winding through it like a path through fire.

Sable brought the boat down to quarter throttle and felt the current take hold of the hull—not pushing anymore but gripping, the way a hand grips, with intent. Behind her, Doss slammed the engine hatch and shouted something about steam pressure that she chose to interpret as encouragement.

"First ledge," Ivo called back, arm extended, fingers spread. "Steer for the smooth water. Left of the standing wave. Do not fight the boil on the right—it will win, and it will not be gracious about it."

"Left of the wave," Sable repeated, and put the wheel over.

The Magpie swung her head toward the green seam, and for three heartbeats nothing happened—the current held the bow the way a wrestler holds an opponent's wrist—and then the hull broke free and slid into the smooth water, and the world went silent in the strangest way, because the roar was still there but the boat was suddenly part of it, moving with the river instead of against it, and silence like that is only ever found inside danger.

"Good," Ivo said, very quietly. "Now trust me. Second ledge comes fast after the first. When I say hard right, you give me everything."

The first ledge took them. It wasn't violent so much as enormous—the whole river tilting beneath the keel, the banks leaping upward, spray coming over the foredeck in cold sheets—and then they were through it and dropping toward the second, and Ivo's voice cut through the roar:

"HARD RIGHT!"

Sable gave it everything. The wheel fought her like a living thing, spokes kicking against her palms, and the Magpie heeled into the turn with her rail nearly kissing the flood, and for one suspended moment Sable saw straight down into the trough below—a churning gray chaos with a boulder showing through it like a skull through skin—and then the bow came around and caught the seam and they shot through the gap between two waves tall enough to have names.

"Third ledge!" Ivo's voice again, ragged now, alive. "Straight down the middle! Whatever you see, whatever the water does—straight down the middle and DO NOT STOP TURNING!"

"The middle's a hole!"

"The middle is the ONLY THING THAT ISN'T!"

She drove her at it. The third ledge was the worst because it asked for faith instead of skill—the boat went weightless at the crest, hung there a moment like a held breath, and then dropped, and the impact came up through the keel and into Sable's spine and teeth, and the whole world became white water and noise, and somewhere in it she heard a cry that might have been Pip or might have been the river itself.

Then they were through. Through, and out, and the gorge opened its hands and the water went brown and heavy and comparatively gentle, and the Magpie wallowed in the calmer pool below the falls like a horse that has cleared a fence and can't quite believe it either.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Doss's whoop came up through the deck plates, wordless and enormous, and Marisol was laughing on the aft deck, and Sable realized her hands were shaking so badly she had to lock her elbows to keep the wheel steady.

Ivo turned from the bow. His hair was plastered flat, his collar soaked through, and he was grinning—actually grinning, wide and unguarded, a boy's grin on a man who had spent thirty years being careful. He raised one fist in the old Vhalorian salute she'd seen him give the lock-keeper, and Sable, helpless, returned it.

Then the cry came again, and this time there was no mistaking it.

* * *

Pip was in the water.

He'd frozen at the rail during the third drop—frozen the way people do when the body decides, wrongly, that stillness is safety—and when the boat had wallowed in the landing pool, the recoil of the wake had simply lifted him over the low rail like a leaf off a table. No splash she'd heard, no shout at the time; the river had taken him quietly, the way it took everything, and now he was twenty feet astern and going backward, arms windmilling, his head barely keeping above the brown churn of the eddy line.

Sable didn't remember giving the order. Later she'd reconstruct it from the evidence: her voice shouting *man overboard, hard astern*, her hand slamming the telegraph to full ahead while spinning the wheel, Doss already dragging the spare line from the stern locker because some orders get to the engine room before the engine room hears them. The Magpie came hard about in the pool, throwing a curling wake, and Sable steered with her whole body, putting the bow across the current upstream of the boy the way you're supposed to, the way her father taught her, the way she'd failed to do once before with other names attached—

No. Not today. Today the arithmetic was going to come out different.

Ivo was already at the stern rail with the line coiled, and when the bow swung level he threw—a clean, beautiful throw, the coil opening in the air like a bird—and the rope landed a foot short of Pip's flailing hand.

"He can't swim for it!" Marisol screamed. "He's got his boots on—he's sinking—"

Pip went under. Came up. Went under again, longer this time, and when he surfaced his movements had lost their coordination entirely, and Sable knew with a cold professional clarity that she was watching the last ninety seconds of a person unless something changed.

"Again!" she shouted, and Ivo threw again, and this time the rope's end slapped the water directly across Pip's chest, and the boy's hand came up out of the river and closed on it with the desperate strength of the drowning.

They hauled him in over the stern. It took four of them—Doss on the line, Marisol with her arms hooked under his shoulders, Ivo pulling, Sable steering one-handed and hauling one-handed until Doss pushed her aside and took the wheel without asking. Pip came over the coaming like a landed fish, vomited half the river onto the deck, and lay there shuddering while Marisol wrapped him in every blanket the boat owned.

His hands were cut. In his death-grip on the rope he'd torn the skin off both palms, and the blood mixed with the river water and dripped pink onto the deck boards.

"I froze," he said. His teeth were chattering so hard the words barely assembled. "I heard you call me forward and I—I couldn't move. I just stood there. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Captain, I froze—"

"Stop." Sable crouched beside him. She took his bleeding hands in hers, gently, turning them over. "You listen to me. Freezing isn't cowardice. It's the body voting for survival with bad information. Every person on this boat has frozen at something. I froze at my own inquiry—sat there like a stump while they read the findings." She squeezed his hands. "You're alive. The rope was thrown. That's the whole story, and it's a good one."

Pip looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and not only from the river.

"My grandmother crossed this river," he whispered. "Right here, maybe. Right here at the Judgment. And I almost let it have me."

"But it didn't." Sable stood. "Because we don't give the river what it wants. Not ever again. Get below. Marisol, those hands cleaned and wrapped. Doss—" she turned to the wheelhouse, "—check the stern locker. Tell me what we lost."

Doss checked. The answer came back grim: the spare anchor chain, thirty fathoms of good forged links, had been swept from the unsecured locker in the third-drop impact. Gone into the Judgment, along with the spare anchor itself.

"We'll manage," Sable said, though her stomach knotted. A riverboat without a spare anchor, heading into monsoon country, was a gambler down to her last chip. "We'll manage," she said again, softer, mostly to herself.

From the bow, Ivo watched her with an expression she couldn't read, and did not try to.

* * *

Night came down into the gorge like a lid. They tied off in a side slough behind a gravel bar, engines cold, a single lantern burning in the wheelhouse because Sable had insisted on taking first watch alone and everyone else had been too tired to argue.

Except Ivo.

He came up the wheelhouse stairs an hour into the watch carrying two tin mugs, and handed her one without asking, and stood beside her at the window looking out at the black water sliding past the hull.

"Marisol's asleep sitting up," he said. "Doss is sleeping in the engine room, which I'm told is normal. Pip finally stopped shaking about an hour ago."

"He'll freeze again someday," Sable said. "Everyone does. The trick is being on a boat where someone throws the rope."

Ivo was quiet a moment. "That's the truest thing anyone's said to me in years."

"Archivists must hear a lot of untrue things."

"Archivists hear a lot of *careful* things." He sipped his tea. "Careful things are how you survive exile. You learn to say nothing that could be weighed, measured, filed. Thirty years of careful things." He turned and looked at her, and in the lantern light his eyes were tired and young at once. "You didn't say a single careful thing tonight. To Pip. On the wheel. In the rapids. You just—said what was true, at volume, and steered into it."

"Somebody had to hold the wheel."

"Somebody always has to hold the wheel." He set his mug down on the chart table. "My parents held it too, in their way. They stayed in Vhaloria when everyone with sense was leaving. My father said somebody had to keep the books honest until the end. The end came. It came for them, and it spared me because I was small enough to hide in a crate of maps." His voice stayed level, but his hand on the table had curled slightly, knuckles pale. "I've spent my whole life thinking that if I'd been braver, older, louder—I could have been one more name in the ledger instead of the one person left to read it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm running rapids with a woman who talks to rivers like they owe her money, and I find I'm not afraid of the ending anymore. Only of arriving too late." He hesitated, and then, quieter: "When you threw the wheel into that last turn—you didn't hesitate. Not for a breath. You trusted me, a stranger you've known two weeks, with your boat and your crew and your life, on the strength of a water-sign you'd never seen."

"You read the river like you'd lived on it."

"I read it like I'd *mourned* it." Something moved through his face, quick and unguarded. "That's the same thing, maybe. Maybe that's the only kind of knowing there is."

Sable looked out the window at the dark water. The lantern flame leaned in a draft. She was aware of him beside her the way you're aware of a change in current—not seeing it, feeling it, in the hull of yourself.

"There were four of them," she said, before she could stop it. "My crew. Before. I've never told anyone on this boat the whole of it, and I'm not going to tonight, either. But I'll tell you this much: after the inquiry, I stopped trusting my own hands. Four years of checking every knot twice, of lying awake listening to the bilge pump, of taking crews because a captain alone is just a hermit with a boat." She turned to look at him fully. "Today I put my wheel where you pointed, blind, at full speed, and it was the first time in four years my hands didn't shake because of fear. They shook because I was *awake*. All the way awake. I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it," Ivo said softly. "Not tonight. Tonight it's just true, and it can sit here between us like the tea getting cold, and neither of us has to pick it up."

The lantern flickered. Down in the gorge the river ran on, patient and enormous, carrying their lost anchor chain toward the sea along with everything else it had ever taken.

Sable reached out—not far, just far enough—and set her hand over his where it rested on the chart table. His hand was warm. Neither of them spoke. Nothing needed picking up; nothing got put down. It simply stayed there, in the lamplight, while the watch burned down around them, and when his fingers finally turned and laced through hers, it felt less like a beginning than like a recognition—as if some current beneath both of them had been converging the whole voyage and had only now, in the dark, become visible on the surface.

They kept the watch together until dawn, shoulder to shoulder, saying almost nothing, and it was the best conversation Sable Nystrom had had in four years.

* * *

Gray light found them still at the window. Sable woke from a doze she didn't remember allowing, her neck stiff, Ivo's coat draped over her shoulders that she definitely hadn't accepted. Below, the smell of coffee and fried something was climbing the stairwell, and Marisol's voice was already going, aimed at Doss, about the state of the flour barrel.

Sable stood, stretched, and took the wheel out of habit, though the boat was tied fast and going nowhere.

That was when she saw it.

Smoke. Not cookfire smoke—too black, too much of it—rising from beyond the next bend upstream, hanging in the still morning air of the gorge in a column that could only mean one thing: a boat, burning.

And riding down the current toward them, turning lazily, came a piece of charred debris that bumped against the Magpie's hull and spun slowly in the eddy. Sable leaned over the rail and fished it out.

It was a fragment of nameboard, scorched at the edges, gilt lettering half gone.

What remained of the letters read: *—ESSARIN.*
