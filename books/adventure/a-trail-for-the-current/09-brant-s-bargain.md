## 9. Brant's Bargain

The fueling depot at Harrow Bend was the last honest diesel between the second lock and the gorge country, and Sable Nystrom hated it on sight. It wasn't the depot itself—a squat tin-roofed warehouse, a gravity tank on legs, a fuel barge moored alongside with its hoses coiled like sleeping snakes. It was the flagpole out front, and the ministry pennant snapping from it, and the black government launch tied up in the shade of the fuel barge as if it had every right to be there.

Which, she supposed, it did.

"Company," Pip said from the bow, where he'd been coiling line with the exaggerated calm of a boy pretending not to be afraid.

"I see it."

"Could run past. Top off at Corben—"

"Corben's forty miles back the way we came." Sable eased the Magpie's throttle down and let the current carry them toward the dock. "We take on fuel here or we don't make the Judgment before the water drops. And if we don't make the Judgment before the water drops, we don't make anything at all." She glanced at him. "Besides. If Brant wanted us dead, he'd have shot at us by now. He wants something else."

"What could he possibly want?"

"The same thing everybody wants, Pip." She rang for slow ahead. "What we're carrying."

The launch sat low in the water, twin engines under canvas, a radio mast bristling with more antennae than any river patrol needed. Two crewmen in gray watched them come in without expression. Inspector Calloway Brant stood on the depot's plank dock with his hands clasped behind his back, hat brim level, boots clean despite the mud everywhere else in the world. He looked like a man who had been waiting exactly long enough to make waiting seem like his idea.

Sable brought the Magpie alongside soft, kissed the fenders against the pilings, and killed the engine. The silence after was enormous.

"Captain Nystrom." Brant touched his hat brim. "You're burning diesel faster than I'd have guessed. Heavy boat."

"Heavy cargo," she said. "Scrap iron. Salvage rights are posted at Corben Landing if you'd like to check my paperwork."

"I've read your paperwork." He smiled the way a ledger balances—precisely, without warmth. "Twice. It's very good paperwork. Almost too good. You have a gift for documents, Captain. May I come aboard?"

"You may not."

He considered that. Then he gestured, and one of the gray men handed him a leather folder, and he held it up so she could see the seal. "Then let's do this on the dock, in the open air, like honest people. Though I'll note for the record that your engineer is watching me through the engine-room port with what appears to be a pipe wrench, and your cook has positioned herself at the rail with a very large ladle. I admire a close-knit crew. They're rarer than you'd think."

"They're mine," Sable said. "That's all the note-taking anyone needs."

"Indeed." He opened the folder but didn't look at it. "Here is what I know, and please stop me if I'm wrong. Four nights ago a man named Aldous Renick fell onto your gangplank at Corben Landing with half a route-map in his fist and a bullet hole in his back. He died on your deck. Before he died, he gave you something—or asked you to carry something—and you said yes, because you are, whatever else you are, a woman who keeps her word. Since then you've been seen running closed locks by moonlight with an unregistered passenger who answers to the name Kestrel. Shall I go on?"

"You can go on all morning," Sable said. "Doesn't mean any of it's true."

"No," Brant agreed pleasantly. "But here's the part that is true whether or not you like it: carrying Vhalorian state materials is a capital offense under the Erasure Statutes. Section nine. Harboring a Vhalorian archivist, section eleven. Running a closed lock, section four, which is merely a fine, but I mention it because it's the least of your troubles." He turned a page he hadn't read. "Your deckhand, Philip Ansel Marsh. His grandmother was Vessaline Ansel of the Lower Reaches, deported in the third year of the Erasure, died in transit. That makes Pip a person of erased descent, which means everything else applies to him doubly. Your cook, Marisol Vega—the Erasure took her brothers, as I'm sure she's told you; they were on the lists. Your engineer, Doss Merrick, has a daughter in Corben Landing who attends a school the ministry funds."

The dock planks seemed to tilt. Sable kept her face still, but inside her chest something went cold and heavy, like ballast shifting.

"You've done your homework."

"It's my job," Brant said. "And here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so listen carefully. Give me the chest. Everything in it—papers, instruments, maps, all of it. In exchange, I bury every charge. Not just yours. All of them. Your crew walks away clean. The boy's grandmother stays a footnote instead of a case file. Miss Vega's brothers stay dead, which is sad, but dead is legal. Mr. Merrick's daughter keeps her school. And you, Captain, keep your boat, your license, and your life." He closed the folder. "Refuse, and I don't arrest you. Arrests are noisy. I simply wait. I'm very good at waiting. The river will do most of my work for me—it always does."

* * *

She didn't answer him right away. She made herself look past his shoulder, downriver, where the water ran brown-green and patient toward the gorges, and she thought about the letter in the flour barrel addressed to *the keeper at the source*, and about a dying man's hand closing around her wrist with the strength of a drowning man and the certainty of a priest.

"Give me until dark," she said. "I don't sell my crew's lives standing up. I sit down first."

Brant studied her for a long moment, and something flickered behind his eyes—not respect exactly, but recognition. The look of a man meeting another creature built from the same hard timber.

"Until dark," he agreed. "I'll be at the depot office. My men will not board your vessel. You have my word, and unlike most people on this river, Captain, my word is load-bearing." He tipped his hat again and walked away, unhurried, his boots leaving almost no prints in the mud.

Sable watched him go, then climbed aboard and called her crew into the saloon.

* * *

The saloon of the Magpie was twelve feet by ten and smelled of diesel, coffee, and Marisol's pepper stew, and it had never felt smaller than it did with all five of them crammed inside and the truth laid out on the table like a gutted fish.

"Bury the charges," Doss said. He sat with his huge hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking, knuckles scarred from forty years of machinery. "All of them. Clean papers. We fuel up, we turn around, we go home, and nobody hangs."

"That's the offer," Sable said.

"And you're actually thinking about it."

"I'm actually listening to it. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Marisol said quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. She stood by the galley door with her arms folded, and Sable had never seen her stand that still. "You want to hear the difference? The difference is my brothers' names are in that chest. Somewhere in those ledgers, in some clerk's handwriting forty years old, there's a line that says Tomás Vega and Rafael Vega, deported, and maybe—if the archive is what the dying man said—maybe there's a page after it that says where they went. What happened to them. Whether they—" Her voice caught and she killed it, strangled it, and went on flatter. "You give that chest to Brant, and my brothers die twice. First the Erasure, then the forgetting. I will not be on a boat that does that. I'll swim ashore at Harrow Bend and walk, but I won't be on it."

"Nobody's giving anything to anybody yet," Sable said.

"Then say it plain, Captain. Say you're not taking the deal. Because if you take it, I'm telling you now, in front of God and the river and this whole sorry family—we mutiny. I'll put you off your own boat if I have to."

"Marisol." Doss set the mug down. "Be reasonable. It's paper. Old paper. Dead people's names don't eat, don't breathe, don't keep a girl in shoes. Livvy's eleven years old. You think she cares whose name is carved in a canyon when the ministry closes her school and puts her father in a work camp? Brant doesn't need to hang us. He just needs to squeeze, and we're squeezed."

"So that's it." Marisol looked at him with something close to pity, which was crueler than anger. "Forty years on this river and you still think they'll keep their word. You think a man like that buries charges. He files them. He keeps them in a drawer, and every year for the rest of our lives he opens the drawer and asks for one more small thing, and we pay, and pay, and pay, because we already sold the only thing we had that was worth anything."

Doss's jaw worked. "You don't know my life."

"I know your daughter deserves better than being a hostage. Which is what she is the moment you take this deal. Brant told you himself—he knows where she goes to school. Taking the deal doesn't free her, Doss. It brands her."

Pip had said nothing through all of it. He sat on the bench under the porthole, knees drawn up, and when Sable finally looked at him he was staring at his own hands like they belonged to someone else.

"Pip?"

"My gran used to sing," he said, to his hands. "Vhalorian songs. I thought they were just lullabies. I didn't even know the words meant anything—I thought they were nonsense sounds, the way kids think. After the Erasure she never said the word Vhaloria out loud, not once, not in the house, not with the curtains closed. But she sang." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious at once. "They took her language and her country and her name off the maps, and then they took her, and the only thing left of her in the whole world is what she hummed to me when I was small. And you're telling me there's a box full of everything they tried to erase, and we should hand it over so we can keep smuggling scrap iron in peace?" He laughed, and it was an awful sound from a sixteen-year-old. "That chest is the only inheritance I've got. I'm not selling it for a clean record. A clean record of what? Being nobody from nowhere?"

The saloon went silent. Somewhere outside, the river slapped against the hull, patient as ever.

Sable let the silence sit until it had done its work. Then she spoke, carefully, because she knew every word would be remembered.

"Here's where I stand. I promised a dying man a delivery. I don't break promises to living people, and I'll be damned before I start breaking them to dead ones. So we're not turning around. But—" she raised a hand before Marisol could speak, "—I'm not going to pretend the cost is nothing. Doss is right that Brant doesn't bluff, and he's right that Livvy is leverage, and I'd be lying if I said I hadn't thought about it since he walked away. So here's what happens next. Doss, you fuel us up, top to full, and while you're doing it, you think hard about whether you're with us or whether you want off at Harrow Bend, and either answer is honorable. Marisol, you keep cooking, because hungry crews fight worse. Pip—you're below decks until we clear the bend, and that's not a punishment, it's arithmetic; you're the one they want most. And Ivo—"

Everyone looked at the archivist, who had been leaning against the bulkhead by the door, silent as furniture, watching the argument the way a man watches weather.

"—you knew this was coming. You've been running from the ministry since you were a child. Tell me something true about Brant. Is he a man who keeps bargains?"

Ivo Kestrel was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his accent thickened the way it always did when something mattered. "Inspector Brant is the most dangerous kind of servant. He believes in the ministry the way priests believe in God—which means he will keep his word to the letter and betray you with every syllable he leaves out. Yes, he would bury the charges. And yes, the moment it became inconvenient, he would find new ones. You cannot bargain with a man whose conscience belongs to an institution." He pushed off the bulkhead. "But Captain—there is something else. Something I learned this morning, before he arrived, from the lock-keeper's cousin at the bend. The boat that went upstream two days ahead of us. The one the old woman warned us about."

"What about it?" Doss asked.

"It isn't a ministry boat." Ivo's voice dropped, and the temperature of the room dropped with it. "It's registered to a shipping concern called the Meridian Line. But the man who owns it—" he paused, and Sable saw his hands curl slowly into fists at his sides, the first uncontrolled thing she had ever seen him do, "—the man who owns it is named Aurel Vessarin."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Sable didn't recognize it. But Marisol went white, and Doss frowned, and Pip whispered, "Who's Vessarin?"

"A Vhalorian," Ivo said. Each word came out flat and polished, like stones laid over a grave. "A keeper's son, like me. His father kept the third lock. And in the third year of the Erasure, when the ministry came looking for the archive, Aurel Vessarin gave them names. Lists of keepers. Lists of families. My parents' names were on his lists." He looked at Sable, and behind the calm of his face she saw something ancient and banked, a fire that had been burning underground for forty years. "He sold his own nation to save his own skin, and the ministry paid him for it, and he has been their creature ever since. If Vessarin is running upstream ahead of us, toward the source, then this was never just about the chest. Brant doesn't know where the archive is. He needs us—or rather, he needs what we carry—to find it for him. And Vessarin is already showing him the way."

Sable stood at the head of the table and felt the middle-dark settle over the journey like weather coming down the gorge. Ahead of them, a traitor with a head start. Behind them, an inspector with infinite patience. Beside her, a fractured crew, and below the floorboards, a dead nation riding in a flour barrel.

"Doss," she said quietly. "Fuel us up."

He looked at her for a long three seconds. Then he drained his cold coffee, picked up his wrench, and went out to the hoses without another word—which was, from Doss, as good as a signed oath.

Marisol exhaled. Pip sagged against the bulkhead. And Sable turned to the porthole, where across the brown water the ministry launch sat at anchor, and beyond it the river ran north toward the gorges, carrying somebody else's boat and somebody else's sins toward the source.

"We cast off at first light," she said. "And from here on, nobody sleeps alone."

* * *

They took on diesel through the long gold afternoon, and Sable spent it on the roof of the wheelhouse with a spyglass, learning the shape of the enemy. The Meridian Line boat had passed through two days ahead—that was all the lock-keeper's cousin knew—but Brant's gray crewmen talked, the way bored men talk, and Pip, forbidden from the dock, was not forbidden from the radio, and Pip could mimic a customs official's voice well enough to raise a laugh and loosen a tongue. By dusk they had a name confirmed, a description of the vessel—a long, fast, shallow-draught packet boat with a reinforced bow, built for gorges—and one detail that turned Sable's blood to river-water: Vessarin's boat had taken on a passenger at the second lock. A ministry man. Quiet. Carrying a strongbox.

Brant and Vessarin, rowing in harness.

At sunset, Brant himself appeared at the foot of the gangplank, alone, hat in hand against the dying light. Sable met him halfway, on the planks, where the deal had been offered and would now be refused.

"Well, Captain?" he asked. No folder this time. No performance. Just the question, bare.

"You can tell the drawer where you keep buried charges," Sable said, "that it's going to stay empty. We're running upstream, Inspector. All of us. Together."

Brant looked at her for a long moment, and then past her—at the Magpie, at the lamplight in the saloon windows, at the silhouettes of her ragged, stubborn, impossible family moving behind the glass. Something crossed his face too quickly to name.

"The water above the Judgment won't forgive you," he said. "Neither will I."

"Then we'll both have to learn," Sable said, and walked back up her gangplank, and did not look back.

By full dark the lines were cast off, the engines were warm, and the Magpie slid out from Harrow Bend into the current, running black water under a sky bruising over with storm-light, chasing a collaborator's wake toward the heart of a drowned country—with the monsoon nine days out, and every mile of river between here and the source narrowing like a throat.
