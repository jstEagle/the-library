## 10. The Collaborator's Wake

Ivo Kestrel had not slept in a proper bed in eleven days, and the third lock's mooring wall offered him the next best thing: a stretch of old stone, sun-warmed, where the Magpie lay tied for the night like a tired animal at a trough. He sat against her hull with his back to the rivets, a cold cup of tea going colder in his hands, and watched the lock-keeper's lamp burn yellow in its window across the water.

The lock itself was a cathedral of the old country. That was the thing no one outside the river country understood—the Vhalorians had not merely built locks, they had *inscribed* them, and even now, forty years after the Erasure scraped the name from every map and register, the masonry still carried its testimony. Ivo could read it from where he sat. The water-line marks cut in graduated notches, each one a prayer for a safe descent. The keystone carved with the twin-current sigil that meant *what goes down will rise again*. His mother had taught him the alphabet of it when he was six years old, tracing his finger along a garden wall in Vessarin's own district, of all the bitter ironies, before the walls themselves were torn down and the district renamed and the people in it scattered like seed on wind.

He had told Sable some of this. Not all of it. Not yet.

Above him, on the wheelhouse roof, Pip was supposed to be on watch. Ivo could hear the boy's breathing, slow and even, tipped back against the pilothouse glass with his cap over his eyes. He let him sleep. There was no threat worth waking a child for—not tonight, not here, at a lock so minor the ministry hadn't bothered to automate it, staffed by one old man who charged passage in firewood and gossip.

The firewood was stacked on the foredeck. The gossip was already spent: the lock-keeper had come down at dusk, taken their coins, and mentioned—casually, the way country men mention things that have been eating at them all day—that another boat had locked through two days back, headed upstream, flying no colors at all. A steel-hulled packet boat, twin screws, running dark. The old man had shrugged. Boats ran dark all the time on this stretch. Smugglers, mostly.

Ivo had felt the name settle into him before the lock-keeper even finished: *Vessarin*.

He had known it the way you know a scar in the dark—by touch, by texture, without needing light.

* * *

The explosion of sound came at half past three in the morning, and it was not an explosion at all. It was worse. It was the *absence* of sound—the groan and slap of the Magpie's hull suddenly swinging free of her lines, the mooring ropes parting not with the snap of overstrained fiber but with the soft, deliberate whisper of steel through hemp.

Ivo was on his feet before his mind caught up. He had slept in archives for twenty years, and archives had taught him this: catastrophe rarely announces itself. It arrives as a small wrongness. A silence where there should have been creaking. A current where there should have been stillness.

The Magpie was drifting.

Not downstream—the lock basin held her, a pool of slack water between the gates—but sideways, her stern swinging with the slow patience of a thing that had all night to kill itself against the masonry. And below decks, already, there was a new sound: water finding its level, that intimate, industrious gurgling that no riverman ever wants to hear beneath his feet.

"PIp!" Ivo's voice cracked across the basin. The boy came up out of his watch-chair like a startled bird, cap flying, and in the same instant the pilothouse lamp snapped on and Sable Nystrom was there, barefoot, already dressed in everything that mattered—boots half-laced, knife in her belt, her mind visibly assembling the situation faster than Ivo could describe it.

"Lines are cut," she said. Not a question. She had read the stern's swing in one glance. "Doss—engine room—"

"I'm going." Doss was already through the deckhouse door, wrench in hand, moving with the unhurried urgency of a man who had spent thirty years being the person who went below when everyone else went up. Marisol came behind him with the lantern. Pip stood frozen at the rail, and Ivo put a hand on his shoulder and steered him to the winch. "Not you. Crank. When I say."

The stern quarter struck the lock wall with a sound like a door closing on a very large room. Not fatal. Not yet. But the old boat groaned from keel to rail, and from below came Doss's voice, muffled and terrible in its calm: "We're taking water. Aft compartment. Someone's opened her up like a tin of sardines."

* * *

What they found, when the lanterns went below, was craftsmanship. That was the detail Ivo could not shake, standing hip-deep in the rising bilge while Marisol passed buckets and Sable held the light, watching Doss fight the intake valve. The cut mooring lines had been sawed, not chopped—sawed with a fine blade and then frayed at the break so the parting would look like wear. The hull breach was not a hole but a *seam*: a section of planking near the stern tube, worked loose with a pry bar, the caulking raked out with surgical patience, so that the river would be invited in slowly, politely, in the dark, while the whole crew slept ten feet above.

"This isn't rage-work," Sable said quietly. "This is a bill. Someone's presenting us a bill."

"Vessarin," Ivo said.

The name came out of him flat and hard, and he heard how it sounded—how Sable's head turned, how the lantern light caught the question in her face. He had been holding the name for two days like a stone under his tongue. Now it was out, and the water was at his knees, and there was no putting it back.

"You know him," she said.

"I know *of* him." Ivo climbed out of the bilge, water sheeting off his trousers, and found he could not look at her while he said the rest. He looked at the lantern instead. At the flame. "Emory Vessarin. He was the registrar of the Ninth District. Civil records, census rolls, property titles. The most boring man in Vhaloria, my mother used to say, and thank the currents for boring men, because they keep the world stitched together." His voice did something strange on the last words, thinned and roughened, and he made himself go on before it could close entirely. "When the ministry came for the Erasure, they didn't start with soldiers. They started with the registrars. Because a nation isn't its army. A nation is its *lists*—who lived where, who owned what, who was born, who married, who died. Delete the lists and you've deleted the people. The soldiers were just there to make sure no one rewrote them."

The engine room was filling around them, the bilge pump clanking in its dying rhythm, and Doss looked up from the valve and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"Vessarin kept the Ninth District's master rolls," Ivo said. "Every family. Every name. And when the ministry's commissioners arrived, he handed them over. Not just the rolls. He *annotated* them. He marked who was likely to resist. Who had hidden documents. Who should be taken first." He heard his own voice go distant, the way it did in the archive stacks, the professional calm that was really just a lid screwed down over a boiler. "My parents were on his list. Third page. My father because he was a keeper of the source archive and had moved records out of the capital in the last year. My mother because she was married to him. That was the entire charge. *Married to.*"

Sable reached for him. He stepped back—not from her, he wanted her to know, never from her—but because if he was touched just then he was going to come apart, and the boat was still taking water, and there would be time, later, for coming apart.

"They were taken in the spring. I was seven. I was already gone—my father's last act was to put me on a grain barge with a forged name and a letter to a cousin in the delta. Ivo Kestrel drowned that spring, officially. Some other boy arrived at my cousin's house." He finally looked at her. "I have spent my whole life being the boy who didn't exist, Sable. Vessarin is the man who made that necessary. And he's two days upstream of us, on a ministry's payroll, running dark toward the only thing left in the world that I love."

* * *

They saved the boat. It took until dawn, and it cost them, and the cost had a name.

The intake valve was jammed half-open by the pry work, and the only way to close it was to get a man into the flooded compartment beside the boiler, where the steam line, stressed by the listing and the cold water, had begun to weep at a joint no wider than a knife's edge. Invisible steam. The kind that cuts. Doss went in with a wrapping of wet canvas over his arm and his wrench in his good hand, and for six minutes the engine room was nothing but his breathing and the pump and the hiss of the leak, and then the valve seat ground home, and the pump began to win, and Doss walked out under his own power with his left forearm scalded from wrist to elbow, skin gone the color of old candle wax, and sat down on the coal scuttle and said, "Somebody put a bandage on this before Marisol sees it and makes a fuss," and Marisol was already there, making a fuss, her hands steady and her eyes wet, and Ivo stood in the doorway and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blow, that he had done this.

Not Vessarin. Not the ministry. *He* had done this. He had brought his war aboard a working boat, a family boat, and the family was paying the freight.

He said as much to Sable, later, on the foredeck, with the sun coming up the color of brass and the lock-keeper's gates groaning open ahead of them. She was coiling the ruined mooring lines—the sawed ones, the evidence—and she didn't stop working while he talked, which was one of the thousand things he had come to love about her without ever having said the word.

"If I left," he said. "Took the chest, went overland from here, alone. Vessarin's business is with me. The boat could turn around."

"Could it." She coiled another fathom of line, unhurried.

"Sable—"

"Doss is below decks right now with a arm that's going to scar, and he'd throw you over the rail himself if you suggested leaving again. Marisol signed on for names—her brothers are *in* that archive, Ivo, do you understand? You're not carrying your war. We're all carrying the same one. It just landed on different decks at different times." She stood, and put her hand flat against his chest, brief and hard, the way you'd steady a swaying load. "You want to make it up to the boat? Get us to the source. That's the only penance anyone's taking on this crew."

* * *

It was Pip, of all people, who assembled the last of the picture—Pip, who had gone quiet and watchful since the chest came aboard, Pip with his grandmother's blood and his grandmother's silence, who came to the wheelhouse that afternoon with a scrap of paper and an expression Ivo had last seen on the faces of witnesses in old tribunal photographs.

"I checked the lock-keeper's ledger," Pip said. "The one he keeps for tolls. The dark boat signed in two days ago. They have to sign—it's the law, even out here." He put the paper on the chart table. "Name's not Vessarin. But look at the cargo line."

The handwriting was a clerk's handwriting, neat and bloodless. *Cargo: survey instruments. Chartering ministry, Office of Rivers and Reclamation.* And beneath the signature, the agent's name: *E. Vessarin, consultant.*

"Consultant," Ivo said. The word tasted like river mud.

"Here's the thing," Pip said, and his voice was too steady, the way voices go when they're holding something down. "The Office of Rivers and Reclamation doesn't charter consultants. I looked. Marisol's cousin works in the Corben Landing customs house; I asked her to check the registry codes. That office was folded into the Ministry of Interior eleven years ago. The code on that signature line—" he tapped the paper— "is the old code. The one from before the reorganization. Somebody's using dead paperwork, because dead paperwork doesn't get audited."

Sable was silent at the wheel. Ivo watched her work it through, her eyes on the brown water ahead, and he watched the moment the pieces locked together, because her hands went still on the spokes.

"Brant," she said.

"Brant," Ivo agreed. The inspector's face rose in his memory, courteous and cold, sitting in the Magpie's saloon with his tea untouched. *A salvage audit.* The tracking beacon wired to the hull. The warehouse fire in Corben Landing, meant to flush Pip out. And now this: a steel packet boat running dark, crewed by a ministry consultant traveling under forty-year-old credentials, two days ahead of them on the only route to the source. "He's not hunting us, Sable. He doesn't need to. Vessarin knows the way—Vessarin *was* the registrar, he'd have handled the source archive's supply contracts, he knows the locks, the passes, the old flood-tunnels. Brant let us run because we're the hound. We're finding the archive *for* him, and Vessarin is riding ahead to be standing on the porch when we arrive."

"Trading on the location," Sable said. "Brant delivers the archive's recovery to the ministry, Vessarin delivers us, and they split whatever comes of it. The chest. The ledgers. The—" She stopped.

"The names," Ivo said. "Forty years ago Vessarin sold the ministry a nation's people one page at a time. Now he's selling them the last page. The one with everyone on it." He felt the rage come up in him then, finally, the full weight of it, and this time he didn't screw the lid down. He let himself look at it. "When we catch him—"

"*If*," Sable said. "And when we do, we do it my way. Nobody hangs off my boat for revenge, Ivo. Not even for this." But her eyes when she said it were not soft, and her hands on the wheel were not gentle, and Ivo understood that she was not forbidding the fight. She was only insisting on choosing its ground.

* * *

They locked through at dusk, the third gate swinging open on the upper river, and the water above was a different water—faster, colder, stained amber with the season's first mountain rains. The monsoon was coming. You could smell it on the wind now, a green metallic edge under the evening's warmth, and every soul aboard knew what it meant: the calendar had stopped being their enemy's weapon and become everyone's.

Ivo stood at the stern rail as the Magpie climbed the lock's lift, the walls rising around them, the carved notches of the old water-marks sliding past his shoulder one by one. *What goes down will rise again.* His mother's finger, his small finger, tracing the groove. Somewhere upstream, a man who had written his parents into a death ledger was sleeping soundly on a steel boat, believing the past was a country that stayed drowned.

Ivo had come four hundred miles to tell him otherwise.

Behind them, far downstream, a light showed briefly on the water—there and gone, a boat's lamp, or a trick of the last light. Sable saw it too. Neither of them said anything. But at dawn, when Doss came up on deck with his bandaged arm and his tools, he checked the fuel line twice, and Pip took his watch standing up, and the Magpie went into the upper river like a blade going into a sheath—slow, careful, and all the way.
