## 11. Monsoon Sky

Pip woke to the smell of burnt solder and the sound of Doss swearing at the boiler in three languages, only one of which was any language at all.

He lay in his bunk in the fo'c'sle and listened to the *Magpie* breathe around him. That was how he'd come to think of her, over the years—not as a boat but as an old animal, groaning in her sleep, her bones settling, her heart the thump-thump-thump of the auxiliary pump that had been running since the third lock and hadn't stopped since. The engine room had taken four feet of brown water before they'd plugged the breach, and the boiler had scalded Doss's forearm to the elbow, and the *Magpie* had been limping upriver ever since like a dog with a stone in its paw.

Pip sat up and the world tilted sideways and came back wrong.

He'd been ignoring that. He'd been ignoring it for two days, which was two days longer than Marisol would have tolerated if she'd known, which was exactly why he hadn't told her. The rope burn across his palm and up his forearm—from the mooring line that had nearly taken him over the side at the third lock—had gone from angry red to a deep, sullen purple, and the skin around it was hot and tight and shining, and when he pressed it with his thumb the pain came back at him like something alive, something that had moved in and wasn't paying rent.

*Septic,* said a small, honest voice in his head, the voice of his grandmother, who had been a riverwoman before she was a ghost before she was a name on a list that no longer existed. *That's septic, boy. You don't fool a wound. A wound knows what it is even when you don't.*

"Shut up, Gran," he whispered, and swung his feet to the deck plates.

The fever made the morning feel like it was happening to someone else, at a distance, through smoked glass. He pulled his sleeve down over the arm—Marisol was in the galley, and Marisol had eyes like a fishhawk—and went up on deck to see how bad the sky was.

The sky was very bad.

It had been building for days, he realized, the way you realize you've been listening to a sound for hours before you notice it. The clouds had come down from the north in great gray shelves, stacked like slate, and the air had the thick, sweet, metallic taste that meant rain the way a knife's edge means cutting. Downriver, where the gorge walls pinched the channel, the water had already come up a foot overnight, and it was the wrong color—not the green-brown of a living river but the chocolate brown of a river carrying whole hillsides in its teeth.

Ivo Kestrel was standing at the bow rail with the wireless handset pressed to his ear, and Pip watched his shoulders as he listened, and watched them go lower.

* * *

"—repeat, landfall projected within nine days, possibly seven. Outer bands reaching the middle gorge within seventy-two hours. All river traffic advised to make for sheltered anchorages below the Kessel Narrows. The upper gorge—" the voice on the other end crackled, dissolved, came back, "—closed. I say again, the upper gorge is closed to all traffic until further notice."

Ivo set the handset down on the wireless cabinet with the care of a man putting down something that had bitten him. His face, when he turned, was the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Nine days," Sable said. She'd come up from the wheelhouse without Pip hearing her, which was unusual; Sable Nystrom moved like a cat even when she was tired, and she was always tired now. There was a new line between her eyebrows that hadn't been there at Corben Landing, and the graze on her ribs from the narrows ambush had stiffened her left side so that she turned like a ship coming about. "Maybe seven."

"Maybe seven," Ivo agreed.

"And the portage above the cataract takes how long, in your expert estimation?"

Ivo's jaw worked. "Three days. Four, if the winch anchors won't bite in wet rock."

"And if the water's already up?"

Ivo didn't answer. He looked upriver instead, at the gorge walls going gray and soft in the haze, at the river running fast and brown and full of itself, and Pip, watching from the shadow of the deckhouse, understood that the silence was the answer. *If the water's already up, we don't portage. If the water's already up, the river takes the Magpie and everyone on her, and it keeps her, the way it kept the four who went down with the last crew, the way it keeps everything, eventually.*

"Then we don't stop," Sable said. "We don't stop for weather, we don't stop for the boiler, we don't stop to sleep. Nine days. We make the cataract in six."

"Captain," Doss said from the engine room hatch, his scalded arm wrapped in greasy gauze, "the boiler won't take six days at pressure. It'll take four, maybe five, and then she'll let go and she'll take the engine room and half the stern with her."

"Then you've got four days to make it take six."

"Captain—"

"Doss." Sable's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the thing about her that had frightened Pip the first month aboard and steadied him ever after: she didn't raise her voice, she lowered the temperature. "I know what you're telling me. I'm telling you what I need. Find the space between."

Doss looked at her for a long moment, and Pip saw the argument die in him, saw it go out like a lamp. He wiped his good hand down his face, leaving a smear of grease from brow to chin like a man marking himself for war.

"Four days to make it take six," he repeated. "Right. Pip—get below. I need hands on the condenser, and yours are the only ones small enough to reach the aft bracket."

Pip's stomach dropped through the deck plates. "Aye," he said, and made himself walk toward the hatch at a normal speed, sleeve down, right hand curled out of sight.

Marisol caught him at the hatch.

She didn't grab his arm. She never grabbed; she'd grown up with brothers who grabbed, she said once, and she'd learned that the hand you don't see coming is the one that lands. She just stood in the doorway of the galley with flour to her elbows and looked at him, and her eyes went from his face to his sleeve and back to his face, and Pip watched her do the arithmetic—the two days of him taking his meals alone in the fo'c'sle, the way he'd stopped hauling lines, the sheen of sweat on him that the morning heat didn't explain.

"Sit down," she said.

"I've got the condenser—"

"*Sit down,* Pip."

He sat down. It was that or fall down, and the galley bench was right there. Marisol washed her hands, unhurried, the water running pink where the flour came off, and then she took his wrist—gently, gently, the way you handle a bird—and turned the arm over and pushed the sleeve up, and for a moment she didn't say anything at all, and that silence was worse than any swearing.

The rope burn ran from the base of his palm to the crook of his elbow, a broad stripe where the line had run through his grip as the current took it and he'd held on because letting go meant going into the water and the water meant the rocks. The center of it had broken down into a wet, yellowish ulcer the size of a coin, and the skin around it was purple-black and hot and streaked—faint red lines running up toward his shoulder like rivers on a map, like the route-map he'd been copying every night by lamplight, and the fever made him think of that, of red lines running up a map toward a place that wasn't there, and he laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, and Marisol's head snapped up.

"Red streaks," she said. "How long."

"Two days. Maybe—"

"Two *days?*"

"It didn't seem that bad."

"It didn't seem—" She closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet and furious, which Pip had learned was the shape love took on Marisol: fury with water in it. "You listen to me. Your grandmother, wherever she is, is looking down at you right now, and she is *ashamed.*"

That landed. That landed harder than any blow could have, and Marisol knew it, and Pip hated her a little for it and loved her completely, and the fever blurred the two together until he couldn't tell which was which.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be sorry. Be still." She was already moving, pulling the galley's medical kit from under the sink—the real one, not the one they showed inspectors—laying out the scalpel and the iodine and the boiled cloth and the jar of honey, because Marisol's mother had been a healer in a village that also didn't exist anymore, and Marisol held that honey was worth more than any ministry medicine, and Pip had never once seen her proven wrong. "This is going to hurt. I want you to hold on to the bench and I want you to think about something else. Tell me about the map."

Pip went cold all over, colder than the fever could account for.

"What map?"

Marisol looked at him. Just looked. The fishhawk look.

"You've been burning lamp oil in the fo'c'sle three hours a night for a week," she said. "You think I don't count the oil? I count everything on this boat. It's how I keep us fed. You've got paper under your bunk that you think I don't know about, and you've got ink on your left hand that you're right-handed, and you've been copying something, and there's only one something on this boat worth copying." She picked up the scalpel. "The route-map. Ivo's route-map. Or half of it, the half the dead man carried."

The engine room thumped beneath them. The river ran. Somewhere above, the first outer band of the monsoon was sliding over the gorge walls like a lid.

"I'm not stealing it," Pip said quickly. "It's not—I'm not taking it anywhere. I'm copying it. For myself. Because—" and here the thing he'd been carrying for weeks, the thing that had gone septic in him just like the arm, came up and out before he could stop it, "—because it goes past my grandmother's village. The route. The old route. It's on the map, Marisol, it's *right there*, the bend where the village was, and nobody knows it's there because it's not on any map that exists anymore, it's only on this one, and if I don't copy it while it exists then when the map's gone—when *we're* done with it, when it's delivered or burned or whatever happens to it—it's gone again. She's gone again. The whole village, gone twice." His voice cracked and he hated that too. "I just want to find it. I want to stand where she stood. Is that—is that so much? Everyone else gets their names read at the end of this. I just want to stand in the place."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. The scalpel rested in her hand, and the honey jar sat open, and the river ran brown and fast beneath the hull.

"No," she said finally, and her voice had gone soft, the fury drained out of it and something older left behind. "No, boy. It's not so much. It's the least there is." She took his arm firmly. "Now hold still. This is going to hurt like the devil, and you're going to think about that village the whole time, and you're going to tell me about it while I work. The orchard. The bell. Everything you know. Go."

It hurt like the devil. He told her about the orchard anyway.

* * *

The fever broke near midnight, and it broke the way a fever does—all at once, sweat flooding out of him, the world coming back into focus like a lens being wiped clean. Pip lay in his bunk in the wet dark and felt, for the first time in days, the *Magpie's* motion under him as motion instead of as nausea: the long slow roll, the shudder of the boiler working at pressure it had no business working at, Doss down there feeding her coal and lies in equal measure.

He should sleep. He knew he should sleep. Marisol had left a cup of willow-bark tea on the crate beside his bunk and a threat that she'd check it was drunk, and she would, she counted everything.

But the lamp was there. And the paper was there. And under the bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, the copy was there—three sheets now, done in his careful surveyor's hand, the hand his grandmother had taught him before her hands shook too badly to hold a pen: the river from the third lock to the cataract, with the old Vhalorian soundings in the margins, the depth-marks in a script that hadn't been written in forty years and that Pip had learned from her, letter by letter, at a kitchen table in a country that had deleted itself.

He unrolled the newest sheet and held it to the lamp.

There. The bend. *Village of Ash-Kettle Bend, population 61,* in the old script, with the orchard marked and the schoolhouse and the ferry landing, and beside it, in the margin, in his grandmother's hand, in ink forty years faded: *where the water goes home.* He had copied even that. He had copied her handwriting the way you'd copy a face from memory, desperate to get it right, knowing you wouldn't.

The fo'c'sle door opened without a knock.

Pip's heart slammed. He swept the sheet off the crate and under the blanket in one motion, too slow, way too slow, and looked up into Sable Nystrom's face in the lamplight.

She looked terrible, which was to say she looked like all of them now: gray-faced, hollow-eyed, running on tea and stubbornness. She stood in the doorway with one hand braced on the frame—her bad side—and looked at him, and at the crate, and at the corner of oilcloth sticking out from under his blanket, and Pip felt the whole confession rise in his throat like floodwater.

"Captain, I—"

"I know," Sable said.

Pip stopped.

"I've known for a week," she said. "You count lamp oil around Marisol. You don't count it around me. I own the oil." She came in—slowly, favoring the ribs—and sat down on the crate, which put her eye to eye with him, which was worse than standing over him. "I wanted to see how long you'd go before you told me yourself. Then you went septic, and Marisol nearly took my head off, and I figured the deadline had passed."

"I wasn't stealing it."

"I know that too." She was quiet a moment. The boiler thumped. Rain had started somewhere above them—he could hear it now, the first fat drops hitting the deckhouse roof, the outer bands arriving, the sky beginning to come down. "Pip. Tell me why."

So he told her. The village, the bend, the orchard gone wild, the bell. His grandmother's hands teaching him letters that officially had never existed. *Where the water goes home.* He told her about wanting to stand in the place, and about being afraid—this was the part that came out only because it was midnight and he was fever-hollowed and her eyes didn't waver—that the village would be like her, like all of them: a name on a list, a story with no ground under it. That if he didn't stand there, she'd never have been anywhere at all.

Sable listened the way she did everything: completely, without interrupting, like a woman taking a sounding.

When he finished she was silent for a while, and the rain thickened on the roof, and then she reached over and pulled the oilcloth copy out from under his blanket, and unrolled it, and studied it in the lamplight with a navigator's eye—the soundings, the margins, the faded ink.

"Where the water goes home," she read aloud. Her voice did something strange on the last word. "My father used to say that."

Pip's breath stopped. "Captain—"

"Vhalorian lock-sign," she said. "Carved over every keeper's station on the upper river. I grew up hearing it and never knew what it was until a man with a forged passport taught me last week." She rolled the copy up, careful, careful, and handed it back, and looked at him, and Pip saw something in her face he'd never seen there before—something tired and fierce and cracked open, like stone split by frost. "Here's what happens now. You keep copying. You copy everything, both halves, every margin, and when we make the source you copy the ledgers too if they'll let us. But you do it *openly*, and you eat, and you sleep, and you let Marisol dress that arm twice a day, because I have buried one crew already and I am not burying a boy who wanted to find his grandmother's village. Are we understood?"

"Yes, Captain."

"And Pip." She stood, favoring the ribs, and paused in the doorway with the lamplight behind her and the rain loud now on the roof, coming down like the sky meant it. "When we're past the cataract—if we're past the cataract—we'll find the bend. All of us. I give you my word on it."

She left. Pip lay back in the wet dark with the copy against his chest and the fever gone and the rain coming harder, and above him, through the deckhouse roof, he heard the wireless crackle to life, and Ivo's voice, low and urgent, calling Sable to the wheelhouse, and a word that came through the timbers clear as a bell even to a boy who didn't speak the language of ministries:

*Seven days.*

Not nine. Seven.

Pip pressed the map to his chest and listened to the river rise.
