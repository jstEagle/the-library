## 12. The Village That Isn't There

Marisol smelled it before anyone saw it—orange blossom, faint and impossible, drifting off the water like a rumor.

She was at the galley stove when the scent came through the porthole, sweet and green and wrong for this stretch of river, where the banks were all shale and gray willow and the air should have tasted of mud and diesel. She set down her knife, crossed the small room in two steps, and leaned out into the morning. The Magpie was sliding through a side channel so narrow the branches brushed both rails, and somewhere ahead, upwind, something was blooming.

"Pip," she called. "Come smell this."

He came at a run, because Pip always came at a run, and stuck his head out beside hers. She watched his face do something complicated—the hope arriving before he could stop it, then the fear of the hope, then the stubbornness clamping down over both like a hatch cover.

"That's them," he said quietly. "The trees. My gran talked about them. Said you could smell the village before you could see it, and that's how you knew you were almost home." He swallowed. "I thought she was making it pretty. The way old people do."

"Old people don't make things pretty," Marisol said. "They make them true. It's just that nobody writes it down."

Sable brought the boat around the last bend with the engine barely breathing, and there it was.

Not a village, not anymore. Not on any map—Marisol had checked every chart they carried, and Ivo had checked the ones he'd memorized, and between them they held more of this river than the Ministry itself. Officially, this channel led to nothing. Officially, no one had ever lived here. But below the bluff, where the water slowed and spread into a wide brown pool, there were foundations. Rows of them, moss-eaten, half-swallowed by grass gone tall and gold. Streets that were now just suggestions of streets. And rising above it all, an orchard—dozens of citrus trees, untended for forty years, grown wild and enormous and heavy with fruit, their branches tangling together into a single green roof that the morning light came through in pieces.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. The engine idled. Water lapped the hull. Somewhere in the orchard, birds were arguing about something.

"Well," Doss said finally, from the engine room hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before Marisol met him. "That's not supposed to be here."

"No," Sable said. "It isn't."

* * *

They went ashore in the skiff, all six of them, though none of them said why they all needed to go. Pip stood in the bow the whole way across, gripping the gunwale with white knuckles, and Marisol put her hand on his shoulder and left it there. He didn't shrug it off. His fever had broken three days ago but he was still thin, still moving carefully, like a man who'd learned recently that bodies could fail.

The moment her boots hit the bank, Marisol understood something she hadn't expected to understand: this place had been loved. You could tell. The foundations weren't scattered randomly—they were arranged, ordered, facing each other across lanes that someone had once swept. There had been a well with a stone lip worn smooth by generations of rope. There had been gardens, and the herbs had escaped and gone feral, rosemary and sage running riot along what used to be walls, so that walking through the ruin meant walking through a kitchen that had caught fire with living things.

And everywhere, the oranges. Some had fallen and rotted into the grass, and the smell of that—sweet going sour, abundance going back to earth—was so thick it was almost a presence. Pip picked one without thinking, the way you pick fruit in a place you belong, and then stopped, holding it, looking at his own hand as if it had acted without permission.

"It's all right," Marisol told him. "Take it. They've been waiting forty years for somebody to take one."

Ivo walked the village like a man reading a document. He moved from foundation to foundation, and once or twice he crouched and touched the stones, and Marisol saw his lips move—counting, or naming, or praying; with an archivist it might have been all three. At the largest foundation, near the center, he stopped and stood very still.

"This was the meeting house," he said. "Or the school. The records call these places *assembly halls*, but everyone who wrote about them was writing after the fact, from memory, and memory says school." He looked up at the bluff, and Marisol followed his gaze, and her breath caught.

Hanging from a bracket under the eave of the only structure still standing—a stone shell, roofless, its walls thick enough to survive anything except being forgotten—was a bell.

Bronze, green with age, maybe two feet across, swinging gently in the river wind with a sound too soft to hear at this distance. A schoolhouse bell. Pip's grandmother had told him about a bell. Marisol remembered him saying it on the second night out, feverish, copying his route-map by lamplight: *she said there was a bell, and when it rang everybody knew to come eat, and the whole valley could hear it.*

"It's still hanging," Pip said. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "Forty years, and nobody took it. Nobody stole it. It's just—" He couldn't finish.

"People don't loot grief," Ivo said softly. "Even the men who burned the records wouldn't come up here. The Erasure was done with ink and fire in the cities. Out here, they just... let go. Let the river have it." He turned. "But the river doesn't erase. It only moves things."

Marisol had hung back, letting the others drift through the ruins, doing what she did best: watching people instead of places. She watched Sable stand apart, hat brim low, one hand resting on the rail of the well like a woman greeting another widow. She watched Doss kick absently at a rusted hinge and then, thinking no one saw, pocket it. She watched Ivo touch the doorframe of the schoolhouse the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head.

And then she wandered, alone, toward the row of foundations at the eastern edge, where the houses would have been smallest. Workers' homes. Her family had been workers. Wherever her brothers had died—and she still didn't know where, still didn't know how, only that the Erasure took Vhalorians in the cities first and asked questions never—it would have been in houses like these.

She wasn't looking for anything. That's what she'd have said. She was just walking.

The third foundation had a doorway still standing—one wall of it, anyway, brick and plaster gone soft, with the frame intact enough to show where a door had swung on iron hinges for years and years. Someone had lived behind that door. Someone had cooked behind it, argued behind it, hung washing behind it, marked their children's heights against it—

Marisol stopped.

There were marks in the wood. Scratched, not carved—made in haste, or secrecy, or both. Letters. Names. Dozens of names, overlapping, crowded, some cut deep and some barely legible, climbing up the frame the way height-marks climb, the way families grow.

She stepped closer. The wind moved the long grass. Somewhere behind her, Pip laughed at something—short, startled, the laugh of a boy finding an orange tree—and the sound seemed to come from another world.

She read the names.

Most were Vhalorian—she knew the letterforms now, had been learning them from Ivo on slow evenings, the beautiful looping script of a language officially extinct. But at the bottom of the frame, cut shallow and crooked, in plain trade-tongue letters any dockworker would use, were three words she had known her whole life without ever seeing written:

*M. AREVALO. R. AREVALO. T. AREVALO.*

Her brothers. Mateo. Rafael. Tomás.

The world tilted. She put her hand flat against the wood to steady herself, and her fingers fit into the grooves of the R as if the groove had been waiting for them, and she made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from her own throat.

They had been here. Her brothers—her loud, stupid, brave brothers who'd signed onto a river crew at fifteen and sixteen and seventeen to send money home, who'd written her letters from towns she couldn't find on maps, whose last letter had said *we're going upstream, Mari, there's work at the source, real money*—they had stood at this doorframe. They had scratched their names into this wood with a nail or a knife, the way rivermen did, the way you mark a place so the place remembers you. And then they had gone on, upriver, toward whatever took them, and the village had emptied, and the maps had burned, and for forty years the only proof that Mateo and Rafael and Tomás Arevalo had ever drawn breath was three lines cut into a doorframe in a town that did not exist.

She was on her knees in the grass and didn't remember getting there.

* * *

They found her—or heard her, rather. Marisol wasn't quiet about grief; she'd never learned to be, and she'd decided long ago that she wasn't going to start learning now, at fifty-three, in a dead village full of orange trees. When Sable reached her, Marisol was still touching the names, saying them out loud over and over, the way you'd read a ledger, the way—Ivo would say later, his voice rough—the way keepers read.

Sable didn't say anything. She just knelt down in the grass next to her, in the dirt and the fallen oranges, and put her arm around Marisol's shoulders, and stayed. Doss stood a few feet off with his cap in his hands, which was the most reverent thing Marisol had ever seen him do. Pip cried openly, without embarrassment, the way children cry and grown men are taught not to. Ivo knelt on her other side and read each name aloud once, slowly, in proper Vhalorian pronunciation, giving the letters their true sounds, and it undid something in her and rebuilt it at the same time.

"They were here," she managed, when she could manage words at all. "Forty years, I thought—they just vanished. Paperwork. A clerical thing. Numbers on a ministry list." She laughed, wet and terrible. "They scratched their names in a door, Sable. Like boys. Like idiots. Like my brothers."

"Like people," Ivo said quietly. "That's what the Erasure was, in the end. Not politics. Not strategy. It was the decision that certain people were never people at all." He touched the frame above her brothers' names, where the Vhalorian script climbed in loops. "And this is what the archive is. Not a weapon. Not a treasure. Every name in those ledgers is a scratch on a doorframe. Somebody saying: *I was here. Remember me.*"

Marisol looked at him, and then at Sable, and something settled in her chest—not peace, nothing so gentle, but resolve, hard and hot as a coal raked from a fire.

"Then we get there," she said. "Whatever it costs. Whatever's chasing us. We get to that source, and we read every name in those books, and we say them out loud until the whole river knows them." She stood, brushing off her knees, and her voice didn't shake anymore. "My brothers fed crews on boats like this one for years. They kept people alive with bad coffee and worse jokes. And the Ministry decided they were a rounding error." She picked up her knife from where it had fallen in the grass, and closed her hand around it. "I'm the cook. Feeding people is what I do. Well. I'm going to feed a nation back into existence."

Nobody laughed. Nobody needed to. Sable squeezed her shoulder once and stood, and the look that passed between them needed no translation.

They spent the rest of the day in the village, and it felt less like salvage than like a vigil. Doss found the old cistern and got it working again, muttering, and they drank cold clear water that tasted of stone and time. Pip climbed the bluff and rang the schoolhouse bell—once, hesitantly, and then again with his whole body, and the sound rolled down the valley and across the water and came back off the far hills, and Marisol saw Sable close her eyes at the sound like it was a prayer she'd forgotten she knew. They picked oranges until the skiff was piled with them, and that night Marisol made a cake in the Magpie's little oven, the first cake she'd baked since before the Erasure took her family, and they ate it in silence that was somehow the loudest meal they'd ever shared.

Late that night, Marisol stood a final watch at the rail, watching the village fade into the dark behind them. The bell had gone quiet. The orchard went on blooming in the dark, scenting the water for whoever came next.

Behind her, on the bench by the wheel, Ivo was showing Sable the route ahead by lamplight—and his voice, when Marisol caught it, had changed. Gone tight. Careful.

"The channel forks past the Narrows," he was saying, tapping the chart. "We'll want the western branch. But Sable—the Narrows themselves. The walls come within thirty feet of each other, and there's no landing, no way to turn a boat this size around once you're committed." He paused. "And if someone wanted to trap us, that's where they'd do it."

Marisol watched Sable study the chart for a long moment, lamplight moving on her face.

"Then we run it fast," Sable said, "and we pray the current's on our side."

Far off downstream, so faint it might have been imagination, Marisol thought she heard an engine that wasn't theirs.
