## 13. Ambush at the Narrows

Ivo Kestrel had not been on the water since he was nine years old, and he had never been on water like this.

The gorge swallowed the Magpie whole. The walls rose sheer and rust-streaked on either side, close enough that Pip could have thrown a stone from the rail to either bank, and above them the sky narrowed to a ribbon of bruised gray. The river ran fast here—faster than anything downstream—and it made a sound Ivo had no word for in trade-tongue, only in Vhalorian, which was *khessavarat*, the voice of stone being argued with. His grandmother had sung it to him as a lullaby, absurdly, a song about a river that talked back.

He stood at the bow with the route-map flattened against his chest by the wind, calling depths to Sable at the wheel. "Dark water to starboard—hole, maybe eight feet. Swing left after the bend. Left, then hard right where the foam fans out."

"You're sure?" Sable's voice carried over the engine, level as always, but her knuckles were white on the wheel spokes.

"I'm sure." He wasn't, entirely. He was sure of a map drawn from memory by a dying man, sure of signals carved into lock walls, sure of a childhood spent listening to exiles talk about a river they'd been forbidden to name. Whether that added up to knowledge was another question. But Sable had looked at him an hour ago and said, *You're the only one aboard who's ever been this way*, which was true even if his way had been secondhand, and so he stood at the bow and read the water and tried to make his certainty louder than his doubt.

Behind them, Doss had the boiler singing at a pressure Ivo suspected was technically criminal. Marisol crouched low near the cargo hatch with Pip, both of them keeping their heads below the gunwale—not because anyone had ordered it, but because Marisol had looked up at the gorge walls twenty minutes ago and said, very quietly, "If I were going to shoot at a boat, that's where I'd stand," and nobody had argued.

The Narrows came on them the way bad news comes: gradually, then all at once. The walls drew in. The current quickened until the Magpie seemed less to be moving through the river than to be carried by it, a leaf in a throat. Ivo could see the far end now, half a mile of white-flecked channel, and beyond it the gorge opened into the wider water past the fork.

"Clear ahead," he called back. "Clear for—"

The first shot cracked off the rock wall six feet above his head and spattered stone chips across the deck like thrown gravel.

* * *

Later—Ivo would think about this many times in the years after—he would try to reconstruct those minutes and find that memory had refused to arrange them in order. There was only a sequence of impressions, each burned clean and separate:

Marisol shouting *"Starboard bank!"* and the muzzle-flash winking from behind a fallen slab of rock like a signal mirror.

Doss hauling the whistle-cord, and the Magpie's old steam horn bellowing into the gorge, a sound so enormous it seemed to shove the walls apart.

Sable's voice, calm in a way that Ivo understood now was not calm at all but its opposite—a calm forged under pressure, diamond-hard—"Pip, below. *Below.*" And Pip going, because everyone went when Sable used that voice.

And the boat.

The boat was there all along, and Ivo hadn't seen it, because it had been painted the color of shadow and moored in a fold of the gorge where the rock overhung the water. It slid out now, upstream of them, angled across the channel—a black-hulled river steamer, smaller than the Magpie but newer, faster, with a mounted swivel-gun on its foredeck and men along its rail. Painted on its bow, in letters that had once been official and had been crudely painted over but not well enough: *VIGILANT*.

Vessarin.

Ivo felt something move through him that was older than fear. He had imagined this meeting for forty years—in nightmares as a child in the exile quarter, in cold daydreams as an adult, always at a distance, always abstract. Here it was not abstract. Here was the man who had carried his parents' names to the ministry in exchange for a pension and a pardon, standing at the rail of a stolen government boat with a pistol in his hand, and he was older than Ivo had pictured, softer, with a face like wet paper, and he was *real*, and he was between them and the open water.

"Sable!" Ivo shouted. "He's blocking the channel—we can't go around—"

"I see him."

"He'll sink us in the current. If we take water here—"

"I *see* him, Ivo."

There was a moment—Ivo would keep this one whole, because it was the truest thing he ever saw—when Sable Nystrom stood at her wheel in the throat of the Narrows with a traitor's guns ahead and an inspector's trap behind, and she did not look afraid. She looked *furious*. Not hot fury; something glacial, patient, forty years deep. Her eyes moved across the water the way they'd moved across charts, measuring, weighing, and Ivo watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had: they could not stop, could not turn, could not surrender. The current would not permit any of it.

The Vigilant fired its swivel-gun. The shot went wide—Vessarin's gunner was compensating for the current badly—but the near miss threw a column of spray over the bow and the concussion slapped against the gorge walls and came back doubled, tripled, an echo-war that made the whole river seem to shout.

"They'll bracket us on the next one," Doss yelled from the engine hatch, soot-faced, alive in a way Ivo had never seen him. "Two shots to walk it in. That's how it's done."

"Then we don't give them two," Sable said.

Ivo saw it a half-second before she said it. He saw her hands shift on the wheel, saw the Magpie's bow begin to swing—not away from the Vigilant, not toward the sheltered water, but *directly at it*, head-on, down the full weight of the gorge current.

"No," he said. "Sable—"

"She's heavier than they are." Sable's eyes never left the other boat. "Older, uglier, and twice the displacement. Doss—everything you've got. Now."

Doss gave her everything. The Magpie shuddered down its whole length and surged, and Ivo grabbed the rail as the deck tilted and the gorge walls began to run past like water itself, and the Vigilant grew in front of them, and Vessarin's men stopped shooting because suddenly there was nothing to shoot at except the inevitable.

They hit at the bow, port quarter to starboard quarter, a glancing blow that was still the hardest thing Ivo had ever felt through his boots. Wood screamed. The Vigilant's rail folded. Men went down into a tangle of rigging, and the swivel-gun swung wild and discharged into the cliff face, and somewhere in the chaos of it Ivo heard Vessarin's voice, high and cracking, screaming orders that no one obeyed.

But the Vigilant was newer and its hull was steel-sheathed, and the Magpie was forty years of patched oak and stubbornness. Something below the waterline gave—a long grinding crunch along the port side—and the Magpie slewed sideways, and the current took her broadside and began to roll her toward the rocks.

"Hard to starboard!" Ivo screamed, though she knew, she must know—

She knew. She was already turning, fighting the wheel with her whole body, and for three glorious seconds the Magpie answered, straightening, clawing free—

The second shot from the shore party found her.

It was a rifle ball, not the swivel-gun, one clean shot from the rocks, and it took Sable high in the ribs as she stood exposed at the wheel. Ivo saw her jerk. Saw her hands stay on the spokes through pure will while her knees unhinged beneath her. Saw the wheel spin free as she went down against the deck plates.

The Magpie, unsteered, swung her bow into the current, and the current, which owes nothing to anyone, drove her at the rock wall.

* * *

Ivo did not decide to move. That was the thing he would remember most clearly afterward—that there was no decision anywhere in it. One moment he was at the bow rail; the next he was running aft through the chaos, over Marisol who was dragging Pip below, past Doss hauling the whistle-cord again for no reason anyone would ever understand, and he went over the side without checking the water, which every sailor knows is the first way to die.

The river took him like a debt collector. It was colder than the season allowed and stronger than the maps admitted, and it rolled him twice against the hull before spitting him clear, and when he surfaced, gasping, the Magpie was grinding along the rock wall thirty feet away with a sound like the world's largest door being forced, and Sable was in the water.

She had gone over the starboard side—thrown by the impact or by her own failing grip, he never learned which—and she was not swimming. She floated on her back, unnaturally still, her dark hair fanned out around her, blood unraveling from her ribs into the brown water in a slow red thread, and the current was carrying her toward the gap between the Magpie's stern and the rocks where the water funneled through with a sound like tearing cloth.

Ivo swam. He had swum in rivers as a boy—the exile settlement had sat on a tributary, and the elders had pretended it was the same river, and the children had all known better—and his body remembered what his mind had spent forty years forgetting. He caught her collar with one hand and got an arm around her chest and turned his back to the current, and the river slammed them both against his own grip, trying to take her, trying to take them both.

"Sable." His mouth was full of river. "Sable, look at me. Look at me."

Her eyes opened. They were unfocused, then focused, and the last thing in the world they should have held—annoyance—came into them like a lamp being lit.

"The wheel," she rasped. "Nobody's—"

"Doss has the wheel. Marisol has the wheel. The whole crew has the wheel. You're done steering for today."

"My boat—"

"Is being sailed by people who love her almost as much as you do." He hauled his arm tighter around her and kicked for the shallows below the point where the Magpie had wedged herself, bow-first, into a shelf of submerged rock—wedged, and holding, which was more than Ivo deserved to hope. "Hold on to me. That's your only job. Hold on to me and breathe."

She held on. Weakly, then less weakly, her fingers finding his shoulder with the same deliberate strength she brought to everything, and together they washed into the eddy behind the rock shelf, and Doss's arms came down out of the tangle of the stern rail and pulled them up over the wood like hauling a net.

* * *

The bullet had gone through the flesh above her hip, high enough to miss everything that mattered, low enough to bleed impressively. Marisol cleaned it with river-boiled water and grain spirit while Sable lay on the deck plates in the engine room's warmth, jaw set, refusing with her entire posture to make a sound. Pip held the lamp. His hands shook; his voice, when he finally spoke, was steady.

"It missed everything. I've seen worse on the docks. She'll be lame for a week and furious for a month."

"Furious longer than that," Sable said through her teeth, and even managed something that was almost a smile.

Ivo crouched at the edge of the lamplight and let Marisol work, because there was no room for him closer and because looking at the wound made something happen in his chest that he didn't have time for. Behind them, muffled by the hull, the sounds of the gorge continued: the Vigilant's engine working back and forth, men shouting on the rocks, the swivel-gun firing twice more at nothing. Brant's gunboat had appeared at the downstream mouth an hour ago—a gray shape settling into the open water past the fork like a lid closing on a jar—and between the two of them, the Narrows had become a bottle with the Magpie wedged in its neck.

"We can't run it," Doss said, coming down the ladder, wiping his hands. "Bow's driven onto the shelf. I can get us off, maybe, but the planking's sprung along twelve feet of port side, and if we take the current broadside again we founder inside ten minutes. And they know it. That's why nobody's pressing. We're caught. They just have to wait for us to rot."

"Then we don't wait to rot." Sable pushed herself up on one elbow, pale as paper, absolutely immovable. "Ivo. You said the map showed something in this stretch. A tunnel. Tell me you weren't dreaming it."

Ivo had almost forgotten. He dug the route-map from his oilskin, unrolled it with hands that were still shaking from the river, and there it was—the annotation in the dead man's careful hand, in Vhalorian script with a trade-tongue gloss beneath: *Flood-tunnel, old survey cut, west wall, 300 paces above the Narrows foot. Sealed since the Erasure. Wide enough for a shallow boat. Only a keeper-pilot may run it.*

"Only a keeper-pilot," Marisol read aloud, skeptical.

"That's me," Ivo said. "Or nearly. My grandfather ran this tunnel. He described it to me when I was small—over and over, because he was afraid the knowledge would die with him. The entrance is hidden behind a false fall—water comes out of the rock face and you steer *into* it. Inside, it runs dry except in flood season, straight through the spur of the mountain, and comes out above the fork, past the Narrows, past the gunboat." He looked up. "It comes out exactly where we need to be."

"And if your grandfather's memory is wrong," Doss said, "we die in the dark inside a mountain."

"If my grandfather's memory is wrong," Ivo said, "then Vhaloria truly is gone, and none of this was ever worth doing." He heard his own voice and was surprised by it—steady, certain, the voice of a man who had decided something. "But it isn't wrong. I'd stake everything on it. I am staking everything on it."

Sable looked at him across the lamplit engine room—wounded, soaked, surrounded by the wreckage of her ship's pride—and Ivo watched her weigh it, the way she weighed everything, honestly and without flinching.

"Can you pilot it?" she asked. "In the dark. From a child's memory."

"Yes."

"Then you'll take the bow, and I'll take the wheel, and we'll do it together." She started to rise; Marisol pushed her back down with a flour-dusted hand. "*Together,*" Sable repeated, and there was something in the word that was not about piloting at all, and Ivo felt it land in him like a struck bell. "Marisol, bandage me tight enough to hurt. Doss, get us off this shelf as gentle as a lullaby. Pip—"

"Aye, Captain?"

"Get the lanterns. All of them. We're going into a mountain."

* * *

They came off the shelf at dusk with the boiler at a whisper, drifting stern-first until the current caught them, and Ivo stood at the bow with three lanterns hung around him and the map in his head rather than in his hands, counting paces against the rock face sliding past. Two hundred. Two hundred and fifty. His grandfather's voice in his ears, forty years old and dead these twenty: *You'll know it by the sound, boy. The false fall doesn't roar like real water. It hums.*

At two hundred and eighty paces, Ivo heard it.

A low, resonant note in the rock, almost beneath hearing, like a finger run round the rim of a glass. He raised the lantern. Ahead, in the west wall, a curtain of thin water spilled from a crack sixty feet above the waterline, falling so fine it seemed to hang in the air like a veil—and behind the veil, where no entrance had any right to be, darkness.

"Left," Ivo called, and his voice cracked and he didn't care. "Hard left. Into the fall. *Into it.*"

Sable brought the Magpie around with the tenderness of a woman dressing a wound, and the bow crossed the eddy line, and the veil of water broke over the foredeck in a cold silver sheet—and then they were through, and the roar of the Narrows cut off behind them as if a door had closed, and the lantern light fell on smooth-cut stone walls receding into a straight, dry, man-made corridor that ran into the heart of the mountain.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Pip laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—and Marisol said something in her mother's tongue that needed no translation, and Doss stood in the engine hatch with soot on his face and tears cutting clean lines through it, though he'd have denied them under oath.

Ivo turned aft. Sable stood at the wheel, propped against the spokes, gray-faced, bandaged, alive, and the lantern light moved on her face, and the tunnel carried them forward through the dark like a secret being kept.

"You knew," she said quietly. "All this time, you knew you could do that."

"I hoped," Ivo said. "There's a difference. Hope is just faith with wet feet."

The tunnel ran on ahead of them, straight as a hymn, toward whatever waited at the far end—and behind them, faint through forty yards of living rock, Ivo thought he could hear the Vigilant's engines still searching the Narrows for a boat that was no longer there.

But far ahead, where the tunnel should bend toward daylight, the lamplight found something else instead: a wooden barrier, old and tarred and studded with iron, stretched wall to wall across the channel—and nailed to its center plank, fresh within days, a strip of white cloth bearing a single mark in red.

The mark of the Ministry.
