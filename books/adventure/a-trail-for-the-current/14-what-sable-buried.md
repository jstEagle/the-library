## 14. What Sable Buried

The fever came on her like weather.

She'd felt it building all day—the graze across her ribs gone hot and tight beneath the bandage Marisol changed twice, the skin around it flushed the color of storm-light. By evening she was shivering under two blankets in the Magpie's aft cabin while the boat lay moored in a slough of dead water, tied off to a drowned cottonwood, waiting out the last of Vessarin's wake and the first of the rising wind.

"You should be at the hospital in Corben," Marisol said, pressing the back of her hand to Sable's brow with the tenderness of a woman who had raised brothers and buried them. "You should be anywhere but this boat."

"The river's my hospital." Sable's voice came out cracked. "Always has been."

"Then the river's a bad doctor." Marisol tucked the blanket to her chin like she was a child, which Sable supposed, feverish and shaking, she was. "Ivo's got the watch. Pip's asleep. Doss—" She stopped. The name sat between them like a stone dropped in still water, and neither of them reached for it. "I'll bring you broth."

She went out, and the cabin door swung, and through the gap Sable saw the lamplight in the wheelhouse and the silhouette of a man sitting very straight at her chart table, as if he'd been invited there, as if he belonged.

* * *

He came down an hour later, when the shivering had turned to burning and the burning had turned to something stranger—a loosening, a warmth that felt less like sickness than like the river itself rising in her, carrying things up from the bottom that had been sunk there a long time.

Ivo Kestrel moved quietly for a man who'd spent his life among stone vaults. He set the lamp on the shelf, checked her bandage without asking, and frowned at what he found.

"It's angry," he said.

"It's entitled to be. I've been asking a lot of it."

"You've been asking a lot of everything." He sat on the stool Marisol had left, and for a while he just looked at her, and she let him, because fever had stripped away whatever it was that usually stood between her and being looked at. "In the tunnel," he said. "When I pulled you out of the water. You said a name."

"I don't remember what I said."

"Corwin." He said it carefully, the way a man sets down something breakable. "You said *Corwin, I've got you, I've got you.* And then you said it again, later, when you were sleeping. Only the second time it sounded like an apology."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the door seam. Sable watched it, because watching it was easier than watching Ivo, and because the flame was the same kind of flame that had been burning on the night everything ended.

"His name was Corwin Ashe," she said. "He was my mate. My first mate, on the old Magpie—her before this one. This boat is named after her, but she isn't her. Nothing's ever its second version." She heard her own voice going somewhere flat and far away, the voice of testimony, and understood distantly that the fever had pried the hatch open and the water was coming in and she was too tired to pump. "There were five of us. Me and Corwin, and the Voss brothers—Tam and Ellery, they ran the engine together and finished each other's sentences and cheated each other at cards—and a deckhand named Wren Oduya, who was nineteen and laughed at everything, even the storm."

Ivo didn't move. He had the stillness of archivists, she was learning—the patience of a man whose whole trade was waiting for things to finish speaking.

"It was the spring flood, six years back. We were running cargo down from the timber camps, nothing worth dying over, and the river came up faster than the reports said it would. There's a bend below Harrow Cut where the current sets hard against the bluff and then rebounds off a shoal, and in high water it makes a standing wave you can ride if you know it, or it makes a grave if you don't." Her hands had found the edge of the blanket and were gripping it. "I knew it. I'd ridden it eleven times. But the water was higher than I'd ever seen it, and I misjudged by—I don't know. A boat-length. Half a boat-length. The wave took us broadside and rolled us like a barrel."

"You survived."

"I survived because I was in the wheelhouse and the wheelhouse broke free and floated. That's the whole of it. That's the entire difference between me and the bottom of the river. A door came open and I was inside it." The fever made the memory bright and unbearable, the way fever does, every detail lit like a stage: the wheel spinning loose in her hands, the black water coming through the windows, the sound—she had never told anyone about the sound—the sound of the hull filling, which was not a roar but a hush, a terrible polite hush, like a crowd settling before an execution. "Corwin was on deck. He'd gone forward to cut the cargo lashings, to give us a chance, and he didn't come back inside. Tam and Ellery were in the engine room. Wren—" Her voice failed and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "Wren was in the galley. She'd made coffee. There was a pot of coffee on the stove when we went over."

Ivo closed his eyes for a moment. When he opened them they were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise.

"The inquiry blamed me," Sable said. "Not in words. In words they said *conditions exceeded the safe operating envelope of the vessel,* which is what a committee says when it wants to say *the captain was wrong* without saying it. They took my cargo license for a year. They let me have it back because there was a shortage of licensed pilots and the river doesn't care about grief, it only cares about hands. And every man on that board shook my hand afterward and every one of them looked at me the same way, which was the way you look at a place where something bad happened." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I kept the boat. Everyone told me to sell her, to run a hotel, to do anything, and I kept her because—"

"Because the river forgives nothing," Ivo said quietly. "And neither do you."

She looked at him then, really looked, and the fever swam in her eyes and made him shimmer at the edges like a figure seen through heat off the water. "You've been talking to Marisol."

"Marisol talks about flour and weather. You just talked about the rest."

"I don't talk about the rest."

"I know." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and the lamp threw his shadow long across the cabin boards. "That's why I'm not going to say what people say. I'm not going to tell you it wasn't your fault. I wasn't there. I don't get to absolve you, and neither does any inquiry, and neither—" his voice gentled, "—neither do you, maybe. Some things you just carry. My people believed that. We believed the river keeps its receipts, and grief is a receipt."

"Your people," she said. "You say it like that. *My people.* Past tense."

"Forty years past tense." He was quiet a moment, and then, as if her fever had been contagious in some way that had nothing to do with blood, he said: "You've told me your door. Let me tell you mine."

* * *

The rain had started outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon's outer bands ticking against the cabin roof, and Sable pulled herself half upright against the bulkhead to listen, because she wanted to be awake for this, fever or no fever.

"I was four when the Erasure came," Ivo said. "Which means I remember it, which is its own cruelty. Children remember everything and understand none of it, and then they spend their lives assembling the understanding from scraps." He turned his hands over, palms up, an archivist's gesture, presenting the record. "We lived in Kellin's Reach, a river town—there, I've said it, a river town, you'll appreciate that—upstream of the third lock. My mother was a keeper. Not of locks. Of names. She kept the parish ledgers, the birth records, the marriage lines, the death rolls. She used to let me hold the sealing wax when it was warm. I can still smell it. Beeswax and resin and something like cinnamon. The whole world, when I was four years old, smelled like sealing wax."

The rain thickened. Sable didn't dare move.

"When the ministry came through, they didn't burn the town. That's the thing nobody understands about an erasure. It's not fire. Fire leaves ash, and ash can be read. They took the ledgers. They took the signs off the buildings. They took the names off the graves—my father's grave, he'd died the winter before, and I watched a man with a chisel take my father's name off his own stone, and I didn't cry, because I was four and I thought it must be a mistake that would be fixed." His jaw worked. "It was never fixed. That's the whole of the Erasure. It's a mistake that is never fixed, on purpose, by policy."

"And your mother?"

"She got me out. There was a network—keepers, ferrymen, people who understood that if the records died, the people died twice. She put me on a barge with a woman I didn't know and told me she'd follow on the next boat." The lamplight caught the scar of an old expression crossing his face, something that had frozen there decades ago. "The next boat was stopped at the second lock. She was sent to a processing camp in the east. I was raised by the woman on the barge, and then by an uncle in exile, and I trained in a foreign archive, and I became a keeper of a country that no longer existed, which is either a madness or a vocation, and I've never decided which." He looked at his hands. "And here is the thing I have never said to anyone, Sable, so you'll understand what it costs me to say it to you while you're feverish and can't be held to remembering it."

"I'll remember it."

"I know you will. That's why I'm saying it to you." He breathed. "I never went back. Forty years. I've had the routes memorized since I was a teenager. I could draw the locks from memory, the channels, the villages—I *did* draw them, that map in the chest, half of it is mine. And I never once went home, because home was a door, and on the other side of the door was my mother's empty chair and my father's blank gravestone, and I could not—" His voice cracked, the first time she had heard it crack, the careful archivist's cadence breaking like ice under a boot. "I could not stand in a town that had been taught to forget me and ask it to remember. Because what if it had forgotten? What if I stood in Kellin's Reach and said my name and the name meant nothing to anyone? Better to keep it sealed. Better to keep the archive in a cavern and the grief in a box and the door shut. Some doors, Sable, you're afraid to open. Not because of what's behind them. Because of what opening them says about you—that you needed what was behind them more than you needed the safety of never knowing."

The rain was a steady drumming now, and the river in the slough had begun to murmur against the hull, restless, rising.

Sable found that her eyes were wet too, and let them be. "You came upstream with me," she said.

"I did."

"Why now? After forty years of shut doors?"

He was silent long enough that the lamp flame steadied and the rain filled the pause. "Because of you," he said finally, and held up a hand before she could speak. "Not—not the way you think, or not only the way you think. I watched you take a boat through the Judgment rapids with a dead man's name in your mouth and your hands steady on the wheel. I watched you refuse Brant's bargain with four people's freedom on the table. And I understood that you had opened your door—the worst door, the one with your own name on the other side—and you'd walked through it every single day since, onto that wheel, onto that river. And I thought: if she can do it daily, I can do it once." He laughed, low and unsteady. "The archivist's logic. Very unromantic. I'll work on it."

"It's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Sable said, "and I once had a deckhand propose in a hailstorm."

That startled a real laugh out of him, and the laugh broke something open in the cabin, some pressure that had been building since the tunnel, since the narrows, since the first night he'd bought a drink and she'd said no. He moved from the stool to sit on the edge of the bunk—not touching her, just near, his shoulder a hand's width from hers, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through the fever's own heat.

"Corwin," he said. "Tell me about him. Not the drowning. Him."

And she did, because the fever had burned away the last of the sealant and there was nothing left to do but let the archive open. She told him about a man who chewed tobacco and hid it from her and thought she didn't know, about a man who sang off-key in the fog to keep the crew's spirits up and had one song, only one, about a girl from Vhaloria who waited by a lock that no longer had a name—and Ivo went very still at that, and said, softly, *that song is older than the Erasure, my mother sang it*—and the two griefs, his and hers, sat down at the same table like strangers discovering kinship.

"He'd have liked you," she said at the end. "He'd have called you *professor* and meant it as an insult and a compliment in the same breath."

"I'd have liked him." Ivo reached out and took her hand—her fever-hot hand in his cool one—and didn't let go. "Sable. What happened to your crew was the river. What happened to mine was men. But we've both been piloting wrecks ever since, and I think—" he stopped, started again, the archivist choosing his words with care because the words were going to be on the permanent record, "I think maybe two wrecks can float each other."

The fever peaked and broke sometime in the small hours, sweat soaking through her shirt, Marisol coming down with broth and finding them still sitting shoulder to shoulder, talking in low voices about locks and songs and the dead, and saying nothing about it, just leaving the broth and a look that was its own kind of ledger entry.

* * *

Sable slept until the grey of dawn and woke clear for the first time in two days. The fever had left her hollowed out and light, the way a flooded house is light after the water goes down and takes the furniture with it.

She climbed to the wheelhouse wrapped in a blanket, ignoring Marisol's protest, and found Ivo at the chart table with Pip's copied route-map spread out and weighted with brass fittings, and Pip himself standing beside him, feverish-eyed and eager despite Marisol's strict orders, both of them bent over the same stretch of river.

"Show her," Pip said, grinning.

Ivo tapped a place on the chart above the last cataract, where the gorge narrowed to a thread. "The old charts call it the Pilgrim's Stair. The Vhalorians walked it every year, up to the source. It's not on any ministry map." He looked up at her, and the morning light was on his face, and she saw that the man who had bought her a drink with a forged passport was gone, and someone with an open door behind him stood in his place. "There's something up there, Sable. Something the old keepers wrote about in code. Carvings. Thousands of them."

"Carvings of what?"

Ivo held her eyes, and outside the wheelhouse glass the brown water rose and rose, and the first true rain of the monsoon came walking up the gorge toward them like something with a purpose.

"Names," he said. "It's a wall of names."
