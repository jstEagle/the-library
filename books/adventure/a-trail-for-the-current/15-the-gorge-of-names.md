## 15. The Gorge of Names

The rain reached them before the names did.

It came in a gray curtain up the gorge, and Ivo stood at the Magpie's bow with his collar turned up, watching the first drops strike the river like small dark birds diving. Behind him he could hear Sable's voice through the wheelhouse glass—steady, clipped, giving Doss engine orders—and Pip's lighter answer from the winch deck. The boat groaned beneath him, her hull still carrying the scars of the Narrows, but she ran true. She always ran true when it mattered.

The canyon narrowed as they climbed. That was the thing about this river that no chart admitted: it did not simply flow downhill, it *ascended* in spirit, each mile upstream a mile deeper into something older than any of the nations that had fought over its banks. The walls closed in, red stone streaked black with forty centuries of rain, and the water between them went from brown to a strange bruised green, deep and slow, as if the river itself were holding its breath.

Then Sable cut the engine, and the silence that followed was so complete Ivo could hear the rain on the stone.

"There," she said quietly, beside him now. She had come out without him noticing. "There it is."

He had told her what to expect. He had described it in the flat archival language he used for things too large to feel: *incised inscriptions, pilgrimage-era, likely pre-Erasure by several generations.* He had prepared her for it the way you prepare someone for a grave.

Nothing had prepared him.

The names began at the waterline. They climbed the canyon walls in columns, in ribbons, in spirals, tens of thousands of them, cut into the living rock by hands working from rope ladders and wooden scaffolds long since rotted away. Some were shallow, worn nearly smooth by time. Others were fresh-cut even after four decades, sharp-edged, defiant. They rose two hundred feet above the river and vanished into the mist where the walls bent inward, and they covered every surface—the vertical faces, the ledges, the overhangs, the great fallen slabs along the shore that had been carved on both sides before they fell.

Names. Thousands upon thousands of names.

"They walked here," Ivo said. His voice came out strange, thin, as though it belonged to someone else. "Before the Erasure. It was—it was the last pilgrimage. When Vhaloria knew what was coming, in those final years, people walked up the river to the source. And when they passed through this gorge, they cut their names into the stone. So that the river would carry them." He swallowed. "So that *something* would carry them."

Pip had come forward silently, and Marisol, and even Doss stood in the engine room doorway with grease still on his knuckles, staring. The rain fell harder, beading on the carved letters, running down the channels of the grooves so that the names seemed to weep, seemed to fill and shine, seemed almost to move.

"Which one's yours?" Pip asked softly.

Ivo didn't answer. He couldn't yet. He stepped off the gangplank onto the narrow shingle beach where the boat lay against the current, and he walked toward the wall, and the crew let him go alone, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in twenty years.

* * *

His father had told him about the gorge once, in the last year before the exile, when Ivo was seven and the world still had edges. *Every keeper walks the gorge,* his father had said, sitting by a fire in a house that would be confiscated within the season. *You read the names going up. All of them. It takes three days. You don't skip any, because every name is a door, and behind every door is a person, and the archive isn't paper, boy—the archive is what the paper points at.* His father had smiled then, one of the few times Ivo could remember it. *When you find our name, you'll know why we do what we do.*

Ivo had never made the walk. He had been carried out of Vhaloria in a fish crate at eight years old, and everything after that was forged passports and borrowed languages and a profession spent cataloguing the possessions of the dead in foreign archives, keeping other people's memories because he was not permitted to keep his own.

Now he stood before the wall with the rain running down his face, and he read.

He started at the waterline because that was where the pilgrims had started, the oldest names lowest, and he worked upward letter by letter, syllable by syllable, in the old script that lived in his mouth like a mother tongue he'd been forbidden to speak. *Selu an-Korath. Miren of the Third Terrace. The brothers Ovane, who came together. A child's name, cut small and high, where someone must have lifted the child up to do it.* He read them all. He did not skip any. His father's voice was in his ears, and the rain was in his eyes, or perhaps not only the rain.

He found the name an hour past noon, on a ledge above his head, half-sheltered by an overhang so the cutting was still crisp.

KESTREL. And beneath it, in the family formula: *of the Archive, who keeps.*

His father's hand. He knew it instantly—the particular deep stroke of the K, the way the terminal flourish hooked left, a habit his father had never corrected in thirty years of ledger work. His father had stood on this same ledge, on some gray morning forty-five years ago, with a chisel and a mallet and the knowledge of what was coming pressing down on him like the whole gorge, and he had cut his family's name into the stone of his own country because it was the last place left to put it.

And below his father's name, added later, shallower, in a different hand—a woman's hand, quicker, less sure of the chisel:

*Iva Kestrel. Who kept anyway.*

His mother. Cut after the fall, after the Erasure, when carving the name alone was a hanging offense. She had come back. She had climbed the scaffolds or the ropes or whatever remained of them, in secret, in danger, and she had added herself beneath him. *Who kept anyway.*

Ivo put his hand flat against the stone. The rock was cold and wet and utterly indifferent to him, and it was the most alive thing he had ever touched.

He meant to say something. He had imagined this moment for twenty years and in every imagining he said something—made a vow, spoke the family formula aloud, completed the rite his father had described. But when he opened his mouth nothing came out, and what came instead was a sound he had not made since he was a child in a fish crate crossing a border in the dark, and he pressed his forehead against the carved name of his father and wept like the rain, without dignity, without end, while the river below went on carrying everything it had ever been given.

* * *

They found him there—or Sable did; the others had the grace to stay with the boat. She climbed the ledge and stood beside him without touching him, looking up at the two names, reading them in silence. She had learned enough of the old script in six weeks of nights at the wheelhouse table to make out the family line.

"Who kept anyway," she said.

"That's my mother." His voice was wrecked and he didn't try to repair it. "She came back after. After they took everything. To add herself."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then: "My father's name is on a lock gate two hundred miles downstream. Just initials, scratched with a marlinspike, no ceremony at all. I sailed past it my whole life and never once looked up." She touched the stone below his mother's name—not the letters, just the rock, the way you touch a shoulder. "We're both late readers, Ivo."

"We have to copy them." He heard himself shift into keeper's cadence, the register that held him together when nothing else would. "All of them. Rubbings, photographs, anything. If the ministry finds this gorge—"

"They won't get the chance to burn stone," Sable said. "But you're right. We copy what we can. Pip's got the camera dry-bagged. We'll work the lower walls tonight and tomorrow while we run the portage." She looked upriver then, and her face changed, and Ivo followed her gaze and felt the awe in his chest curdle into arithmetic.

The cataract filled the gorge ahead like a wall thrown across it. Even from here they could hear it, a continuous white thunder, and above it the brown water was rising—he could see it, actually see it, new marks on the stones appearing and vanishing as the monsoon's first surge came down from the high country. In three days, maybe four, this beach would be gone. This ledge would be underwater. The whole lower gallery of names, the oldest ones, the ones closest to the source of everything, would be drowned until the dry season—if the dry season came gently, which the old records said it sometimes did not.

"We portage tomorrow at first light," Sable said. "Whatever we don't finish copying, the river keeps. Same as always."

* * *

The portage route was marked on Ivo's route-map in the old script: a track that left the river on the left bank, climbed through a fold in the canyon wall, crossed a saddle of pine forest, and descended to the water above the cataract—two miles of ground, most of it steep, none of it built for moving a sixty-foot riverboat.

But the Vhalorians had moved boats here. That was the thing Ivo kept returning to as he walked the track that evening with a lantern, checking it against the map. The map showed stone ramps. Stone ramps meant cradles, and cradles meant rollers, and rollers meant that somewhere along this ridge there had once been capstans, and where there were capstans—

He found the first one at dusk, half-swallowed by a century of moss: a great wooden drum mounted on a stone axle-block, its timbers black and hard as iron. Then a second. Then, at the top of the saddle, the ruin of a winch house with its roof beams fallen inward, and inside it, hanging from a peg on the wall where someone had left it forty years ago expecting to return, a block and tackle with its rope still coiled.

"They planned for this," Doss said behind him. The engineer had come up the track uninvited and uncomplaining, which from Doss was a kind of benediction. He laid his palm on the capstan drum and knocked it, listened, knocked again. "Heartwood. Somebody built this to outlast their grandchildren." He was quiet a moment. "Outlasted mine, anyway."

"The Erasure took the nation," Ivo said. "It never got the engineering."

Doss grunted—the sound he made instead of laughing—and began pulling moss off the drum with his bare hands.

They worked through the night by lantern light, all five of them, and it was the strangest labor Ivo had ever shared: hauling forty-year-old tarps off roller beds, digging out stone ramp channels, greasing axles with tallow Marisol rendered from the galley stores, while below them the cataract thundered and the first outer bands of the true storm came through in gusts that flattened the pines and sent the lanterns swinging. Pip ran lines. Marisol cooked on a wood fire and fed them in shifts and said nothing about her brothers, whose names she had found scratched in a doorframe a week ago and who had, Ivo now understood, walked this same track or one like it in another life entirely. Sable directed everything, everywhere at once, a lantern in one hand and a winch handle in the other, refusing help climbing and refusing rest, and Ivo watched her and thought: *this is what a keeper looks like. My father had it wrong. It was never the ones who could read the script.*

By midnight the track was ready. By two, the Magpie sat in a timber cradle on the lower rollers, jacked, braced, and lashed, looking less like a boat than like a wounded animal being carried. At three, Sable called the first haul.

* * *

They moved her at dawn, under a sky the color of wet slate.

Four capstans, five people, one boat. The system was simple and brutal: bars shipped into the drum heads, the whole crew walking the bars in a circle, taking up slack through the block and tackle while Doss chocked the rollers behind them. Ten feet at a pull. Then reset, rechock, re-lash, again. Ten feet. Again. The rain came and went in waves, sometimes soft, sometimes with a violence that drove them flat against the boat's flanks, and between the waves the heat rose off the forest like breath.

Ivo took his turn at the bars beside Sable, shoulder to shoulder, and they learned each other's rhythm within the first hour—the small hitch in her stride on the push, the way she exhaled on the turn—and after that they moved as one mechanism, and he thought absurdly, sweat-blind, arms burning: *this is a courtship, this is how my people courted, under load, in the rain, moving something too heavy to move.* He did not say it. There would be time, or there would not, and either way the bar had to come around again.

Pip sang. That was the surprise of the day—the boy, fever-scarred and rail-thin, set the pace with an old hauling chantey in the Vhalorian style, words half-garbled, tune perfect, learned from a grandmother who had sung it in a kitchen in a country that officially had never existed. Marisol picked it up. Then Doss, humming. Then Ivo himself, helpless, joining on the response line in the true words, and Pip's face when he heard the correct syllables come back to him was worth the whole journey, was worth everything, was the archive entire in a boy's cracked voice on a mountainside.

Ten feet. Again. Ten feet.

At the saddle, near midday, they paused to re-rig around a bend in the track, and Ivo climbed high enough on the rocks to look back down the gorge.

The wall of names stretched away below him, miles of it, disappearing into the rain-haze, and the brown water had climbed another six feet up the stone. The lowest carvings were already underwater. Somewhere down there, right now, the river was flowing over the name of some pilgrim who had cut it into the rock four hundred years ago believing the water would carry it home, and the water was doing exactly that, and there was no one to see it, and it did not matter, because being seen had never been the point.

"Sable," he called down. "Come look."

She climbed up beside him, breathing hard, a rope burn across one palm that she was pretending wasn't there. Together they looked down at the drowning gallery.

"He'd have wanted it that way," Ivo said. "My father. The names going into the current. He always said the river was the first archive and it would be the last one standing."

Sable leaned her shoulder against his, briefly, deliberately, the way she did everything—once, and meaning it. "Then let's get your boat over this hill," she said, "before the second archive closes too."

Below them, Doss bellowed for bars. Pip's chantey started up again, thin and clear through the rain. And far upstream, past the cataract, past the last miles of white water, Ivo felt it rather than saw it—that pull at the base of his skull, the keeper's compass his father had sworn was superstition and Ivo had never once known to lie.

The source was close. And they were not the only ones racing for it: on the wind, faint under the thunder of the falls, came a sound that did not belong to any river.

An engine. Running hard, upstream, in the flood.
