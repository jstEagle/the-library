## 16. Betrayal at the Weir

Doss had been awake for three days, and in that time he had learned something about himself he did not want to know: a man can carry a betrayal the way he carries a tool, close against the body, warm from use, until he forgets it isn't part of his hand.

He sat on the portage trail above the weir with the astrolabe wrapped in an oilcloth against his chest, and he watched the Magpie's crew—*his* crew, he corrected himself, and then stopped correcting himself because it was a lie now—haul the last of the winch cable up through the drizzle. The gorge walls rose on either side of them like the pages of a closed book, and carved into those pages were names. Thousands of them. He'd run his thumb over a few that morning while the others slept: *Maren Vosk, 1901. The child Ilse, who walked here with her mother.* Names cut by hands forty years dead and more, pilgrims walking to a spring that fed a nation the maps no longer admitted to.

He should have left the astrolabe under the floorboards where it had lived since Corben Landing. He should have left it and let the whole rotten arrangement die quietly. But Brant's letter had been waiting at the fueling depot, tucked into the fuel invoice like a knife into a sheath, and Doss had read it twice and burned it, and the burning hadn't helped at all.

*Your daughter starts secondary school in Harrowgate in September,* the letter had said, in Brant's neat ministerial hand. *The school has a generous scholarship fund. It also has a long memory for the debts of parents.*

That was all. That was everything. No threat spelled out, none needed. Brant had never once raised his voice to Doss, not in four years of reports passed over fuel invoices and dockside handshakes that lasted half a second too long. He didn't have to. He'd found the pressure point early—everyone had one, Brant said once, almost kindly, and Doss had thought then that he was talking about engines—and he pressed it the way you press a valve: just enough, just steady, just often enough that the machine kept running.

It had started small. Hull numbers. Cargo manifests. Whether the Magpie ran lights after curfew. Things Doss told himself weren't really betrayals, because everyone smuggled a little, because the ministry was a faraway thing that mostly wanted paperwork, because Lissa needed new boots and the school fees and a father who wasn't on a list somewhere. Then the chest came up out of the river with a dead nation's wax seal on it, and Doss had stood in the hold with Sable prying at the lid and felt the small warm tool in his hand turn into something else entirely.

He'd wired the beacon to the hull himself. That was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain: four people upstream versus one girl in Harrowgate. Four people who could swim, fight, run, choose. One girl who couldn't do anything but be eight years old and be his.

Except the arithmetic had stopped working somewhere around the Judgment rapids, when Pip went white-knuckled at the rail and Doss had grabbed the back of the boy's shirt without thinking, holding him to the boat the way you'd hold anything you loved. And it had stopped working again in Marisol's galley, when she'd set down four bowls of stew and talked about her brothers' names scratched into a doorframe in a village that officially did not exist, and her voice hadn't broken, which was worse than breaking. And it had stopped working completely this morning, watching Ivo Kestrel stand in front of the carved wall with his family name under his fingers, unable to speak, and understanding—Ivo knew, Ivo had to know, some archivist instinct in him had been circling Doss for weeks like a gull over a fish shoal—that what Doss was about to do would carve *his* name into a different kind of stone entirely.

The rain thickened. The first outer band of the monsoon, Sable had called it, squinting at the sky like she could read the weather's handwriting. She could, mostly. She read water the way Brant read men, and both of them always found the soft place.

Sable climbed up the switchback toward him, hauling a coil of line, and dropped it at his feet. "You're slow today," she said. Not accusing. Observing. That was the cruelty of Sable Nystrom—she observed everything and accused almost nothing, and so you convicted yourself in the silence she left you.

"Old man," Doss said. "Boiler takes more out of me every year."

"You're fifty-one."

"Fifty-three."

She looked at him. Rain ran off the brim of her cap and dripped from her chin. Behind her, the gorge roared with the rising brown water, and above the cataract the name-wall glittered wet, ten thousand chiseled lives catching what little light the sky allowed.

"Doss." She said it quietly. "Whatever it is you're carrying, put it down before we go into the last gorge. It'll drown you otherwise."

And God help him, he almost did. He opened his mouth to say it—to say *Brant, the beacon, the letters, Lissa, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I've been sorry since Corben Landing*—and at that exact moment, downstream, Pip shouted something about the winch anchor slipping, and Sable turned, and the moment went past Doss like a log in flood current, and he watched it go, and he let it go, because letting things go was the one skill his whole life had taught him.

"I'm fine," he said.

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the coil of line and went back down the switchback, and Doss sat alone in the rain with a stolen nation's instrument against his chest, and understood that he had just made his final choice, and that it had felt exactly like nothing at all.

* * *

He left two hours before dawn, in the black pause between rainbands, when even the river seemed to hold its breath.

The plan had been arranged weeks ago, in the sterile language of Brant's letters: take the astrolabe—it contained, Brant believed, the navigational key to the source vault, though Doss suspected the inspector wanted it less as a key than as a trophy, proof the file could be closed—mark the crew's route on the depot map, and walk down the maintenance track to the helicopter pad at the old survey station, where a ministry boat would be waiting. In exchange: the file on Daniel Doss closed, expunged, forgotten. Lissa's name never spoken in a ministry office again. A life handed back across the table like change from a purchase.

He wrote the note at the weir, by lamplight, sheltered under the overturned skiff. He wrote it eleven times. Every version sounded either like a coward or a saint, and he was neither, so finally he settled for the truth, which sounded like both:

*I took the astrolabe. Don't come after me. There's a gunboat below the last cataract—you'd be running into it blind. I'm sorry. I was always going to be sorry. Some debts you pay in money and some you pay in people and mine was always going to be paid in people. —D.*

He weighted it under a stone on Sable's bedroll, where she'd find it at first light, and then he stood there in the dark looking down at her sleeping shape—the woman who'd pulled him off a barge fire nine years ago, who'd voted to keep him when half the river said he drank too much, who'd taken him back after every failure because she believed, insanely, faithfully, that a man was the sum of what he fixed rather than what he broke—and he touched two fingers to his cap, the way deckhands used to salute a departing captain, and walked away before he could hear himself argue.

The maintenance track clung to the gorge wall like a scar. He climbed it in the dark with the astrolabe heavy against his chest and the rain starting again, softer now, a fine gray veiling rain that got inside everything. Twice he stopped and nearly turned around. Both times he made himself think of Lissa's face on the dock at Harrowgate last spring, gap-toothed, holding up a drawing of the Magpie she'd done from his descriptions—a lopsided boat with a smokestack like a pencil—and he made himself understand, fully, without anesthesia, that he was trading these people for that drawing, and he went on anyway, because that was the terrible secret of coercion that no one who hadn't lived it ever understood: knowing you're doing wrong does not stop you. Knowing is just another weight you carry.

At the old survey station, dawn came up gray behind the gorge walls, and the helicopter pad sat empty in the rain, and Doss stood on it with his arms wrapped around himself and waited to feel relieved.

He felt nothing. That was the payment. He'd expected guilt, shame, self-loathing—all coins he was familiar with, all denominations he'd handled before. Instead there was just a great flat nothing, like the calm stretch of water below a waterfall, smooth and deep and moving very fast toward something he couldn't see.

The helicopter came at midmorning, beating up the gorge through the rain, and Brant stepped out of it with his coat buttoned and his face arranged into its usual courtesy, and Doss handed over the oilcloth bundle without a word.

Brant unwrapped it right there on the pad, rain spotting the brass, and turned the astrolabe over in his hands with something that was almost reverence. "Extraordinary," he said. "Forty years and it still keeps perfect time. They built things to last, the Vhalorians." He glanced up. "You look unwell, Mr. Doss."

"I need your word. About Lissa. Written."

"You have my word."

"Written."

Something moved behind Brant's eyes—not anger; Doss had never seen anger in him—something more like disappointment, the way a teacher is disappointed when a student asks whether the exam will really be graded fairly. "You're in no position to demand paper," Brant said mildly. "But yes. I'll write it. I keep my arrangements, Doss. Ask anyone I've ever ruined." He folded the oilcloth. "Now. Their route. Where are they, and how fast?"

Doss told him. All of it. The portage line above the weir, the plan to reach the source cavern on foot by tomorrow night, the flooded approach tunnel Ivo had described, the old flood-gates. He gave it away piece by piece and each piece cost him something he couldn't name, and when it was done he stood in the rain feeling lighter and filthier than he had ever felt in his life.

"One more thing," Brant said, turning toward the helicopter. "Vessarin's survivors are already in the upper gorge. If they reach your friends first—" He shrugged, a small elegant motion. "Well. The ministry prefers its problems solve themselves."

Doss grabbed his sleeve before he could stop himself. "Call them off. You've got what you want."

Brant looked down at the hand on his sleeve, then at Doss, and for a moment—just a moment—the courtesy dropped and Doss saw the actual man underneath, and the actual man was tired, and old, and carrying something of his own that bent him slightly forward, like a man walking into wind.

"Mr. Doss," Brant said gently, "do you imagine you're the first person who ever sold something to buy a child's safety? You won't be the last. Go home to Harrowgate. Be a father. It's the only clean work any of us ever get to do." He pulled his sleeve free, unhurt, unhurried, and boarded the helicopter, and the rotor wash flattened the rain in a great silver circle, and Doss stood alone on the pad in the middle of a drowned kingdom with his daughter's future bought and paid for, and discovered that the flat calm inside him had begun, at last, to move.

Toward something he couldn't see yet. But moving.

* * *

They found the note at first light, and Sable read it standing in the rain with the paper going soft in her hands, and then she read it aloud, because Marisol demanded it, and because Sable had learned long ago that secrets rot faster than grief.

Pip took it worst. "I'll kill him," the boy said, voice cracking, fists shaking at his sides. "After everything—he wired the beacon, he's been reporting since Corben Landing, he—"

"He wired the beacon because a man in a good coat told him he'd ruin his daughter if he didn't." Sable folded the note along its worn creases. "Read it again, Pip. All of it. Especially the end."

Marisol read it again. Her jaw worked. "He still did it."

"Yes."

"And you're just going to—"

"I'm going to keep walking." Sable tucked the note inside her coat, against her ribs, where she kept the things that hurt. "Because there's a gunboat below the cataract, and Vessarin's men in the gorge, and nine hours of light left, and I intend for every one of us to be alive at the end of them. Hate Doss on your own time."

Ivo had said nothing through all of it. Now he came to stand beside her at the head of the trail, looking down the long gray throat of the final gorge, where the river ran brown and swollen with the monsoon's first fury.

"You knew," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"I suspected. Since the depot." Ivo's voice was quiet. "A man who flinches every time a ministry boat passes isn't afraid of boats."

"Why didn't you say?"

"Because I watched you vote him into this crew." Ivo turned to her, rain streaming down his face. "And because I have spent forty years being afraid of doors, Sable, and I know what fear makes people do. It doesn't make them villains. It makes them small. Doss was never small until he was cornered."

Sable looked down the trail where their engineer's footprints were already filling with rain. She thought of hands on a boiler, of a shirt collar gripped at the rail, of nine years of a man showing up, fixing things, staying.

"He chose his kid," she said. "I'd have done the same. God help me, I'd have done it faster."

Then she shouldered her pack, and the crew of the Magpie—four of them now, where six had set out, counting a boat they'd never see again—started down into the Gorge of Names as the sky split open above them and the monsoon came to collect its toll.

Behind them, far downstream, a gunboat began to move.
