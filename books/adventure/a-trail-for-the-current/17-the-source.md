## 17. The Source

The rain came down the gorge in ropes.

Sable had seen monsoon rain her whole life—rain that flattened rivers, rain that drowned roads—but this was different. This fell as if the sky had been holding its breath for forty years and had finally exhaled. It sheeted off the canyon walls in silver curtains, pooled in the carved names beneath their feet until every letter of every dead Vhalorian shone like it had been cut that morning, and turned the trail ahead into a stairway of waterfalls.

Ivo led. He had not stopped leading since they'd found his family's name two days ago—*KESTREL* chiseled shoulder-high beside a woman's name worn nearly smooth, his mother, he'd said, or someone who'd loved her enough to add her to the stone—and he climbed now with a certainty that had nothing to do with maps. He'd been born knowing this route, Sable realized. Some part of him had been climbing toward it his whole exile.

"Half a mile," he called back over the roar. "The trail drops through the notch. After that you'll hear it before you see it."

She heard it before the notch. A sound beneath the rain—not the river's chaos but something older and more patient. Water moving through stone. The sound of a spring that had been running since before anyone thought to write anything down.

They came through the notch single file—Sable, then Marisol with Pip leaning on her shoulder, his fever finally broken but his strength still three days behind him—and the gorge opened like a held secret.

The source cavern was not a cave. That was her first thought, standing on the wet lip of it with rain streaming off her chin. It was a wound in the mountain, a half-moon mouth of black stone forty feet high, and out of its floor rose the spring itself—a pool of impossible clarity, fed from below by veins of water that glimmered green-white in the grey light. The river began here. Not dramatically, not with a cataract. It simply *began*, welling up out of the dark the way memory wells up out of grief, and slid away downstream toward everything they'd left behind.

And above the pool, built into the rock like something the mountain had grown rather than man had made: shelves. Thousands of them. Tier upon tier rising into the shadowed heights, and on every shelf, rows upon rows of ledgers—oilcloth-wrapped, cord-bound, stacked with a precision that spoke of hands that had believed the work would matter forever.

Marisol made a sound Sable had never heard from her. The cook, who'd sworn at boilers and bureaucrats with equal fluency, who'd scratched her brothers' names into a doorframe in a village that officially did not exist and wept without shame—Marisol put both hands over her mouth.

"It's all here," Ivo said quietly. He'd stopped at the threshold, rain pouring off him, and for a moment he looked like a boy at the door of a house he'd been told was haunted. "Every district register. Every census. Every birth, every marriage, every death the Erasure tried to unmake." His voice caught. "Forty years I thought it was a story my father told to make me sleep."

Pip pushed past them all. Fever-thin, unsteady, he walked straight to the nearest shelf and pulled down a ledger with both hands as if lifting something holy, and stood there holding it, shaking, saying nothing at all.

Sable stayed at the entrance. Old habit—the captain checks the approaches first—and old instinct, which had kept her alive through an inquiry, a capsizing, and six weeks of being hunted by her own government. She looked up.

The rain hid most of the gorge walls. But above the cavern mouth, cut into the living rock where no trail should have been able to reach, there was a flat shelf of stone. Man-made. Wide enough—

Her stomach dropped.

"That's a landing pad," she said.

Ivo turned. "What?"

"Up there. Cut ledge, level, drainage channels—" She'd serviced enough river stations to know a helipad when she saw one, even one built by a nation that had died before helicopters carried ministry men. The Vhalorians had built it for something else, maybe; it didn't matter. What mattered was who could use it now. "Brant doesn't need the river anymore."

* * *

They had perhaps twenty minutes of grace, and they spent them the only way people like them knew how: working.

Ivo moved through the shelves naming things under his breath like a priest at liturgy—*the Corben district rolls, the harbor registries, the school censuses*—while Marisol and Sable dragged the dryest ledgers they could find toward the pool-side wall, out of the spray-line, stacking them against the worst. Pip copied. Of course he copied; the boy had been copying since the flour barrel, and now he sat cross-legged on the stone with paper spread across his knees, transcribing names in a hand that shook and did not stop.

Sable climbed to the cavern mouth and looked downstream.

The gorge below was white noise. But far off—so far it might have been imagination, except her instincts hadn't been wrong yet on this river—she saw movement in the rain. Boats. Two of them, small, coming up the flooded channel with the arrogance of men who knew the water would carry them.

And behind them, upstream of everything, around the last bend of the Gorge of Names, a shape she knew too well: the gunboat. Brant's gunboat, riding high and mean, its searchlight cutting useless cones through the downpour.

"They're coming," she said. "All of them. From both directions."

Marisol straightened up from the ledgers, and Sable watched something settle over her face—the look of a woman who had already buried brothers and had decided, somewhere back in a burning warehouse, that she was done being buried herself.

"How many guns we got?"

"Your rifle. My pistol. Ivo's flare gun." Sable almost smiled. "Against whatever Vessarin has left and a ministry gunboat."

"So we do what we do best," Marisol said. "We stall. We're very good at stalling. We stalled Calloway Brant himself with a flour barrel."

It wasn't funny, but they all laughed anyway, the way people laugh at the edge of a thing—because the alternative was to be quiet, and quiet in that place, among those names, would have been unbearable.

* * *

Vessarin's men reached the cavern first.

There were five of them, coming up the trail from downstream with their weapons wrapped in oilcloth against the rain, and Sable saw them before they saw the cavern—saw the way the lead man hesitated at the notch, the way they bunched together like men entering a church they didn't believe in. Collaborators' nerves. She'd read about what happened to Vhalorians who'd sold their neighbors to the ministry; some of them had been given new names and new lives, and none of them, the archive's histories said, had ever slept well again.

"Inside," she said. "Behind the shelves. Nobody fires unless I fire."

They took positions among the ledger stacks—Ivo at the pool's edge with the flare gun looking absurd and resolute, Marisol's rifle braced on a shelf of birth registers, Sable at the cavern mouth where the light was worst and the sightlines best. Pip they pushed deep into the vault, and he went, furious, clutching his half-copied pages.

The five men came around the spring pool and stopped.

For a long moment nobody moved. Rain hammered the stone outside. Water whispered up from the deep places. And five armed men stood in the doorway of the complete record of everyone their master had betrayed, and seemed unable to take another step—as if the room itself were weighing them.

Then the lead man raised his weapon, and the moment broke.

"Hold!" Sable stepped out of the shadows, hands visible, pistol low. "You're standing in the middle of forty years of murder, friend. Every name your boss sold is on these shelves. You want to add yours?"

The man's rifle wavered. Behind him, one of the others had gone pale and was staring at the shelves with an expression Sable recognized from mirrors—recognition. These weren't all hired muscle. Some of them were descendants too, men who'd taken Vessarin's coin because the world had left them nothing else, and they were standing in the proof of what had been taken.

That was when the helicopter came.

It came out of the rain like a judgment—rotors hammering the gorge, searchlight swinging down the canyon walls and setting every carved name ablaze with white light—and settled onto the cut ledge above the cavern with a precision that meant Brant had flown it before, or wanted very much to seem like he had.

It did not shut down. It crouched there, engines screaming, throwing rotor-wash and rain down the rock face in sheets, while a rope dropped and a figure descended hand over hand with the unhurried competence of a man who had done everything in his life unhurriedly.

Inspector Calloway Brant dropped the last ten feet, landed in a puddle, and walked into the cavern mouth with his coat plastered to him and his hands empty and open.

"Captain Nystrom." Even through the rotor thunder, his voice carried—that courtroom calm, that terrible courtesy. "You've made me chase you across half a country. I do hope it's been worth your while."

He looked past her. Sable watched his eyes move across the shelves, the ledgers, the spring, and for one unguarded instant something crossed his face that was not bureaucratic at all—something old and hungry and afraid. Then it was gone, filed away, and he was the Ministry again.

"You're surrounded," he said. "Vessarin's men at the entrance. My gunboat closing the gorge. And Captain—" his gaze flicked upward, to the still-turning helicopter, "—the weather window is closing faster than you are. The upper gorge floods within the hour. Whatever happens next, no one leaves by river."

"Then why are you talking?" Sable said.

"Because I am offering you the same bargain I have offered from the beginning, and because this time, Captain, I am authorized to make it generous." He took a step forward. Marisol's rifle tracked him; he didn't appear to notice. "Hand over the documents you carry. Withdraw any claim to this site. In exchange: every charge against every member of your crew—smuggling, obstruction, the warehouse, the locks, all of it—disappears. Sealed. You go home clean. Your engineer's daughter keeps her father. Your boy keeps his record clear." His eyes found Sable's and held them. "Everyone walks, Captain. Everyone. That is the offer, and it expires when the water does."

Silence, except for the rotors and the rain and the spring whispering up from the dark.

Sable became aware that her crew had gathered behind her—Marisol with the rifle, Ivo dripping and silent, Pip emerging from the depths with his copied pages crushed in his fist, fever-bright-eyed and defiant. Her found family, assembled in a dead nation's heart, waiting on her word.

Every debt come due at once. Doss's betrayal, bought with a child's safety. Marisol's brothers, waiting on these shelves. Pip's grandmother. Ivo's parents. Four names from her own past that she still couldn't say aloud, whose ghosts had steered this boat longer than she had.

"Here's my counteroffer," Sable said.

"Sable." Ivo's voice, low, at her shoulder. Careful. "Whatever you're going to do—"

"These ledgers belong to the people they name." She said it loudly, to the cavern, to the collaborators at the door, to the helicopter, to the rain. "Not to a ministry that erased them. Not to a traitor who sold them. Not even to us. We're just the current that carried them home." She looked at Brant, and let him see that she meant every word. "So no. I'm not handing over anything. And I'm not leaving."

Brant sighed—the sigh of a patient man reaching the end of his patience—and nodded once, almost sadly, to the men at the entrance.

"Take them," he said. "Gently, if they allow it."

And that was when the mountain spoke.

It began as a tremor underfoot, a deep groan in the rock, and Sable felt it in her teeth before she understood it—the spring surging, the pool at the cavern's heart rising visibly, brown-tinged now, the monsoon's floodwater finding the old channels at last. Above the entrance, ancient and corroded and built by engineers who had known this day would come, iron gears began—impossibly, magnificently—to turn.

Ivo was staring at the mechanism with his mouth open.

"The flood-gates," he breathed. "My grandfather's drawings—they were real. They were *real*—"

The water in the pool rose another foot. Outside, down the gorge, the river's roar changed pitch, deepening, gathering, becoming the sound of a decision already made.

Brant looked up at the turning gears, and for the first time since Corben Landing, Inspector Calloway Brant looked afraid.

"Captain Nystrom," he said quietly, "what have you done?"
