## 18. High Water

The rain came like a verdict.

Ivo Kestrel had read about monsoons in exile—had studied the hydrology of the Vhalorian gorge in smuggled textbooks, tracing contour lines with a child's finger, imagining the river his parents had died beside. Nothing in those books prepared him for the sound. It was not rain falling on stone. It was the sky emptying itself of forty years of withheld grief, all at once, onto the one place in the world that could not afford it.

He stood at the mouth of the source cavern and watched the approach gorge turn from river to torrent in the space of an hour. The brown water climbed the carved steps of the old pilgrimage stair—each step bearing names worn shallow by centuries of feet—and swallowed them one by one. Somewhere above, through the drumming rain, he could hear the helicopter pad Brant's men had established at the rim, a flat gray slab of modernity bolted onto sacred ground.

Inside the vault behind him, lamplight moved across ten thousand ledgers. Marisol's voice drifted out, hoarse now, still reading. She had been reading for hours.

*Vessarin is still out there,* Ivo thought, and the thought sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

The collaborator's surviving men held the outer gallery—the antechamber between the vault proper and the flooded approach—a crescent of dry stone that was getting smaller by the hour as the river rose. Three men, maybe four, with rifles they'd barely used since the standoff began. Brant's soldiers kept to their side of the no-man's-land of rising water, shouting offers through cupped hands that the rain tore apart before they reached anyone's ears.

Sable found him at the entrance, rain streaming off her jaw, the graze across her ribs wrapped in a strip of Marisol's tablecloth. She looked exhausted and utterly awake, the way she always did when the river was trying to kill her.

"Water's up another meter," she said. "Doss would know how long we have. Doss isn't here." She said it without bitterness, which was its own kind of heartbreak. "The flood-gates. You said your grandfather helped build them."

"My grandmother." Ivo wiped rain from his eyes. "She was a hydraulics keeper. The last of her line to hold the post." He gestured at the wall beside the entrance, where the rain had revealed what dry weather concealed: a relief carving, geometric, abstract to any eye trained only on the present century. "The Vhalorians didn't build this archive to be found, Sable. They built it to survive. The gates were the last argument—if the nation fell, if the Erasure came upstream, the keepers could drown the approach and seal the vault behind thirty meters of living rock and moving water. No army gets through that. No ministry. Nothing."

"And us?"

"The gates work both ways." He made himself say it plainly. "Once they close, nothing gets out either. Not until the engineers' mechanism releases—which was designed to be found again, but the finding requires knowledge that mostly died with the Erasure." He met her eyes. "I know enough. I think I know enough. But once I pull that lever, we are inside with the dead, and the river decides everything after that."

Sable looked past him into the vault, where Pip sat among the ledgers with a fever-bright gaze and a copied chart spread over his knees, and where Marisol read names in a voice gone raw, and where the lamplight made the whole cavern glow like the inside of a lantern.

"We didn't come this far to stand in the doorway," she said.

That was when the smoke started.

* * *

It began as a smell—sharp, chemical, wrong beneath the clean mineral breath of the cavern. Ivo turned toward the outer gallery and saw the orange bloom of flame against the wet dark, and understood in a single sickening instant what he was seeing.

Vessarin stood at the edge of the rising water with a jerrican in each hand, flinging fuel across the ledgers stacked in the antechamber—the overflow crates, the copies the first keepers had begun preparing for dispersal forty years ago and never finished. His men had fallen back from him. Even they, Ivo realized, had not known what their master intended.

"You don't understand what you're doing!" Ivo was already running, boots skidding on wet stone, Sable's shout behind him swallowed by the rain. "Those are names! Those are *people*—"

"They're evidence!" Vessarin spun to face him, and Ivo saw that forty years had finally caught the man entirely; he looked less like a traitor than like a drowning man who had decided everyone else should go under with him. "You think history judges, boy? History doesn't judge. History *convicts*. Every name in this hole is a rope around my neck. I sold them so I could live, and I lived, and now you want to dig them all up and hang me with paper—" He hurled the second jerrican into the flames and the fire leapt, greedy, climbing a stack of ledgers whose spines bore gilded letters Ivo had dreamed about since childhood. *Registry of the Eastern Parishes. Names of the Weavers' Guild.*

Ivo did not decide to move. His body decided for him.

He went through the fire-line low, the way you cross a deck in heavy weather, and got his hands on the burning stack and shoved it sideways into the standing water at the gallery's edge. Steam screamed up around him. Heat seared his forearm through his sleeve. And then Vessarin was on him—not with the rifle his men still carried, but with a boat knife, the kind of blade a collaborator keeps for practical work, and Ivo felt it open his side below the ribs with a bright cold line of pain that seemed almost administrative, almost orderly, before the blood came hot and sudden down his hip.

He heard Sable roar—a sound he would remember the rest of his life, whatever length that turned out to be—and then she hit Vessarin like the Magpie hitting a shoal, shoulder-first, and the two of them went down into the shallows. The knife skittered away across wet stone. Vessarin fought like a man with nothing left to lose, because he wasn't, and Sable fought like a woman who had already lost everything once and refused to do it twice, and there was never really a question of which of those things wins.

It was Pip who ended it—feverish, half-collapsed against the doorway, swinging a boat hook with both hands and catching Vessarin across the temple. The collaborator dropped into the rising water and did not get up. Two of his men waded in and dragged him back toward their own lines, and the third simply threw down his rifle and raised his hands, and no one had the attention to spare for him.

"Let me see." Sable was kneeling beside Ivo, pressing her palm hard against his flank, her face very close and very pale. "It's a slice. It's a slice, Ivo, look at me—it's not deep—"

"It's fine." His voice came out strange and thin. "The ledgers—"

"Marisol's got them. They're wet but they're saved." She laughed, or sobbed, or something between. "You idiot. You beautiful idiot. You went through *fire*."

"I've been walking toward this archive my entire life," Ivo said. "A little fire wasn't going to make me detour."

Above them, through the rain, a voice called down from the helicopter pad—Brant's voice, distorted by weather and distance, offering terms one final time. Sable looked up at the sound, then at the flood-gate mechanism in its alcove, then at Ivo, bleeding in her arms in the doorway of the nation that had raised him.

"No more terms," she said.

* * *

The mechanism was exactly as his grandmother's letters had described it, and exactly as maddening: a bronze assembly recessed into the living rock, gears gone green with a century of patience, a lever shaped like a river reed. Around it, carved into the stone in the old script, ran the words every Vhalorian schoolchild had once memorized and every Vhalorian child born after the Erasure had never been taught:

*THE RIVER GIVES. THE RIVER KEEPS. THE RIVER GIVES BACK.*

Ivo stood before it with blood soaking through Sable's improvised bandage and his hand hovering over the lever, and for one long moment he let himself feel the full weight of what the lever meant. Behind him: the crew. The ledgers. The lamplight and the reading voices and the feverish boy copying charts by hand because some things deserved more than one copy. Ahead of him: water, and stone, and time.

His grandmother had pulled this same lever in a drill, once, when he was small enough to ride her shoulders. He remembered her saying: *We built the doors so that we could choose. That's all a door ever is, Ivo. A choice made solid.*

"Sable," he said. "Last chance. Once this closes—"

"I know what it closes." She put her hand over his on the lever. Her palm was rain-cold and steady. "And I know what it keeps. Pull it."

Together, they pulled.

The sound was enormous—not mechanical but geological, a groan rolling up through the mountain's bones as counterweights older than any living memory engaged and released. Water thundered somewhere below them, diverted, commanded. And along the length of the approach gorge, Ivo watched the miracle his grandmother's generation had engineered: sluice channels opening in the canyon walls, admitting the monsoon flood into the lower approach, raising the water not gradually but *decisively*, a brown wall climbing the pilgrimage stairs two steps at a time, swallowing the gallery where Vessarin's men had stood, sealing the passage between the vault and the world outside.

On the far side of the closing water, tiny with distance, Inspector Calloway Brant stood at the rim of the rising flood and did not fire his weapon. Ivo saw him take off his hat. Whether it was surrender or salute, the rain took the answer downstream.

Then the last channel closed, and the roar settled into a deep continuous thrum, thirty meters of churning water and living rock between the archive and the ministry, and Ivo Kestrel leaned against the green bronze of his grandmother's machine and understood that he had just done the one thing he had spent his whole exile afraid to do.

He had shut the door. From the inside. With everyone he loved on his side of it.

Sable's arm came around him, careful of the wound. "Committed," she said quietly.

"Committed," he agreed.

From inside the vault, unhurried, unafraid, Marisol's voice rose above the thrum of the sealed water—reading on, as if floods and knives and ministries were merely weather:

"*Kestrel, Alenna. Kestrel, Doran. Kestrel, Ivo, age six.*"

His parents' names. His own name, entered in the ledger of a country that had officially deleted him, recorded in a child's careful hand by some keeper who had believed, even at the end, that writing it down might matter.

Ivo slid down the wall until he was sitting, and Sable sat with him, and the water they had summoned held the world at bay while the lamp burned on among the names.

Somewhere in the dark of the vault, Pip's fevered voice asked the question none of them wanted to say aloud, the question the sealed gates pressed against them like a hand:

"If the river gives back—" a cough, a shiver, "—how do we get out?"
