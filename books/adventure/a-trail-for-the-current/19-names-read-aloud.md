## 19. Names Read Aloud

The water came for them the way grief comes for a house—slowly at first, a dark seeping under the doors, and then all at once, a roar that swallowed the gorge and everything in it.

Marisol stood at the archive's inner threshold with a lamp in her hand and watched the flood-gates do what their dead builders had made them to do. The approach tunnel—the one Vessarin's men had held, the one Brant's gunboat had anchored above—vanished under brown water in less time than it took her to say a prayer she hadn't said since she was a girl. Stone boomed. The river found its new bed and settled into it with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

Then, silence. Or near enough. Just water, everywhere, pressing at the old Vhalorian masonry, and the nine of them—Sable, Ivo, Pip, Doss, herself, and the four Vhalorian descendants who had come up the gorge a day behind and been caught inside with the rest—sealed in a vault of lamplight and paper while the monsoon did its work outside.

"Well," Doss said. His voice cracked on it. He was still gray from the steam, still moving like a man made of scar tissue, and he had not looked at Sable since the gates closed. "Nobody's leaving."

"Nobody's leaving," Sable agreed. She said it the way she said everything—flat, certain, a captain's word laid down like a keel. But Marisol had cooked for that woman for three years, and she saw the tremor in her hands as she set the lamp down among the ledgers. Sable Nystrom had just drowned the only door out, on purpose, with all of them on the wrong side of it. She'd done it because the alternative was letting Brant's men and Vessarin's knives into this place, and Marisol understood that. Understanding it and being at peace with it were different animals.

Pip was shaking. Not from fever anymore—the rope burn had finally turned, thanks to Ivo's kit and Marisol's willow-bark and three days of being ordered to rest he'd ignored—but from something else. He stood in the middle of the archive with his mouth open, turning in a slow circle, and Marisol realized the boy was crying without making any sound at all.

Shelves. Shelf after shelf of them, carved into the living rock of the cavern, rising up into darkness beyond the reach of any lamp. And on the shelves, ledgers. Thousands of them. Leather-bound, cloth-bound, some just oilcloth bundles tied with tarred string, all of them labeled in a hand so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. Names. The whole dead nation of Vhaloria, filed and shelved and waiting forty years in the dark for somebody to come and say it all out loud.

*Ay, mis hermanos,* Marisol thought. *Are you in here?*

She didn't say it. She was the cook, the one who kept everyone fed and mostly sane, the one who didn't break down, and so she rolled up her sleeves and said, "Somebody find me a table and more lamps, because I am not reading by one candle like a woman at a wake."

It was a wake. She knew it even as she said it. But there were ways to do a wake and she'd sat through enough of them to know that the worst ones were the quiet ones.

* * *

They built a circle of lamps around the great reading table in the center of the vault—the one Ivo said had been the keepers' table, the one with a brass rail worn smooth by a century of hands—and they opened the first ledger at random, because Ivo's hands were shaking too badly to find the index, and because Sable said, "It doesn't matter where we start. It's all of them or none of them."

The first name was a child's. *Aldous Venn, aged six, of the Lower Terraces.* There was a note beside it in the old keeper's hand: *deported north, records end.* Ivo read it aloud and his voice broke on the word *six*, and Marisol watched a grown man, an archivist who had trained his whole life for this room, put his palm flat on the table and breathe until he could go on.

So it went. They read for hours.

They read the way you bail a boat—not because you'll win, but because stopping means sinking. Ivo read most, his voice going hoarse and rough, and when it gave out entirely Sable took the ledger from him and read in her flat captain's voice, which turned out to be its own kind of mercy, because nothing in her tone pretended this was anything but what it was: a list of the dead, read over their graves. The four descendants from the gorge took turns. Doss read, when Marisol pushed a ledger under his nose and refused to take no, and his engineer's careful diction gave every name the weight of a part number for a machine the world had lost the plans to.

Marisol read too. She had said she would and she did, though her throat closed on the first dozen and she had to pretend she was checking the lamps.

She was looking for two names. She had been looking for them since Corben Landing, since the night she'd signed on, since before that if she was honest—since the morning twenty-two years ago when the ministry men came to the barrio with their lists and their stamps and her brothers went out the door and did not come back, and her mother burned every photograph rather than let them be found with Vhalorian faces in the house. Rafael. Tomás. Two boys who'd swum in the irrigation ditches and stolen mangoes and fought over the last of everything, and then were nobody, legally, officially, a rounding error in a ministry ledger somewhere in the capital.

She found them in the ninth hour. Or maybe the ledger found her—she'd stopped choosing volumes by then, just pulling them down wherever her hand landed, trusting it the way her grandmother had trusted cards.

*Rafael Osa Marín. Tomás Osa Marín.* Both of them, on the same line, same page, same hand. *Detained at Corben Landing. Transferred to the Northern Works. No further record.*

No further record. Four words to hold two whole men, two whole lives, two boys who used to push her in a wheelbarrow and charge her a mango for the ride. Marisol read the line silently, twice, three times, her thumb pressed under the names like she could warm them, and then she heard her own voice, small and steady, saying them out loud into the lamplight.

"Rafael Osa Marín. Tomás Osa Marín. My brothers. They liked mangoes and they cheated at cards and my mother never stopped setting a third plate."

The vault was quiet. The river roared beyond the stone, indifferent and enormous. And then Pip—feverish, hollow-eyed Pip, who hadn't stopped shaking since the gates closed—reached across the table and put his hand over hers, and said, "I'm glad they had a sister like you," and Marisol discovered that a woman could keep a kitchen running through a monsoon, a mutiny, and a gunfight, and still come apart completely over a boy of nineteen with a burned hand.

She let herself, for exactly as long as it took to drink the cup of tea Sable pressed into her hands without a word. Then she wiped her face, and said, "Next name," because that was the whole of it, wasn't it. That was all any of them could do, and it was not nothing. It had never been nothing.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight—though in a cavern under a flooded gorge, midnight was a matter of faith—Ivo found his parents.

Marisol saw it happen from across the table. He had been reading steadily for an hour, voice worn down to a rasp, turning pages with the mechanical care of a man who had taught himself not to feel what he was reading. Then he stopped. His finger stayed on a line and did not move, and his shoulders did something Marisol had only seen once before, on a man at his wife's graveside who could not make his body understand she wasn't coming back.

"Emeline Kestrel," he said. Barely a sound. "Aldric Kestrel." He swallowed. "They—there's a note. My mother's handwriting. She was a keeper too. I didn't—" His voice failed. He tried again. "She wrote the entry herself. Before they took her. She knew what was coming and she came here and she wrote *my son is Ivo and he is seven years old and he is being taken south and I ask the record to remember that he exists.*"

Nobody moved. The lamps hissed. Sable put her hand on the back of his neck, the way you'd steady a spar in a crosswind, and Ivo bent over the ledger and wept like the seven-year-old his mother had written into history, and the record held him, all ten thousand shelves of it, the way it had held every name, every note, every small stubborn *we were here* that a dead nation had left behind.

Marisol looked away to give him his privacy and found Sable looking at her instead. Something passed between them across the table—the cook's look and the captain's look, the one that says *I see you holding us together, put it down for a minute.* Marisol nodded. She didn't put it down. Somebody had to keep the lamps filled.

It was Pip who broke the rhythm, near what Marisol decided to call morning. The boy had been quiet too long, following along in a second ledger Ivo had given him, hunting his grandmother the way Marisol had hunted her brothers. When he found her he didn't read the entry. He read something else.

"She's not—there's more than a name. There's a letter. In the back. The keepers let people leave letters, I think—" His voice cracked. "Can I? Is it—"

"Read it," Sable said. "All of it. That's what it's for."

So Pip stood up, because he was Pip and he was nineteen and his grandmother had died in the Erasure before he was born, and he read a dead woman's letter to a room of strangers by lamplight while a river tried to drown the world outside. It was short. It was about a garden—about an orchard she'd planted with her husband, about the blossoms coming in spring, about how she hoped whoever read this would stand under a flowering tree sometime and think of Vhaloria, because the trees would outlive the maps, she was sure of it, trees were patient that way.

Marisol thought of the wild orchard in the village that wasn't there. She caught Sable's eye across the table and saw that she was thinking of it too.

When Pip finished, he was smiling through the tears, and Marisol thought: *there it is.* That was the thing the ministry had never understood, the thing Brant and his stamps and his forty years of erasure had never touched. You could delete a nation from the maps. You could burn the photographs and rename the streets and make the very word contraband. But you could not stop a boy from reading his grandmother's letter and smiling. Memory didn't live in the archives. It lived in people, and people kept being born.

* * *

It was Ivo, hoarse and pale, who noticed the light.

"Someone's in the tunnel," he said, and the room went still as a held breath.

Beyond the flooded gates, far down the approach passage where the water had sealed them in, there was a glow. Faint. Moving. A lantern, held by someone standing in water up to their waist on the far side of the grate, where the flood had found its level and stopped. Marisol's hand found the nearest heavy thing—which happened to be a ledger, and she set it back down, because some things you don't throw—and Sable was already up, moving low along the wall, and Doss was beside her with a wrench, and the four gorge-descendants had knives out and faces like flint.

The lantern came closer. A shape behind it. A man's shape, alone, hands visible, empty.

"Brant," Sable said.

"Inspector Calloway Brant," the voice came through the grate, roughened by water and something else. "I'm alone. Vessarin's men are gone—what's left of them scattered when the gates went. I'm not here for the chest. I—" A pause. The lantern light wavered on the wet stone. "I've been standing here for two hours listening to you."

Marisol's skin prickled. Two hours. All those names, read aloud into the dark, and the whole time the man who'd hunted them up the river had been standing in the floodwater on the other side of the stone, listening.

"I have no right to ask anything," Brant said. His voice was not the cold courteous instrument from the Magpie's deck, not the audit-and-arrest voice. It was just a tired man's voice, coming apart at the seams. "But my grandfather's name is in there. I heard it an hour ago. *Calloway Brant the elder, of the River District, keeper's assistant, third class.* I heard a stranger read my grandfather's name, and I—" The light shook. "I have spent my whole life enforcing the thing that erased him. I didn't know. I *told* myself I didn't know. And then I heard his name said out loud like he was a man who had existed, and God forgive me, I couldn't hold the gun up anymore."

Silence. The river thundered. Marisol looked at Sable, and Sable looked at Ivo, and Ivo—whose parents this man's ministry had taken, who had every reason in the world to want the water to take Brant too—walked slowly to the grate.

"What do you want, Inspector?" he asked.

"I want to hear the rest," Brant said. "That's all. I want to stand here and listen until you're done, and then I want to help you get them out. All of them. Copies, originals, whatever you'll give me. I'll file whatever report I have to file. I just—" His voice went to pieces and he let it. "Nobody should have to hear their grandfather's name for the first time through a grate in the dark. Nobody should have to hear it *once*."

Marisol found she was crying again and didn't care. She picked up the ledger she'd set down—Rafael and Tomás inside, mangoes and wheelbarrows and a third plate—and carried it to the grate, and held it up to the lantern light so the man on the other side could see the names.

"Then come listen properly," she said. "There's room. It's a big family. It got that way the hard way."

For a long moment Brant just looked at the ledger through the grate, at the names of two brothers he had never erased personally and had erased completely, all the same. Then he set his lantern down on a dry ledge, and Marisol heard the unmistakable sound of a sidearm being unbuckled and laid on the stone, kicked away into the water.

"I'll take the next shift of reading," he said, unsteady. "If you'll have me. I have a clear voice. I'm told I'm thorough."

Sable looked at the water, at the drowned gates, at the man who had chased them five hundred miles up a river and was now standing disarmed in the flood asking to be let inside the circle of lamplight. Marisol watched her captain weigh it—watched her think about the beacon on the hull, the warehouse fire, the audit, the gunboat at the Narrows—and then watched her decide, in that flat certain way of hers, that the ledger was bigger than the grudge.

"Pull up a lamp," Sable said. "There's ten thousand names left, and the water isn't going anywhere, and neither are we."

They read until the lamps burned low and were refilled and burned low again. They read Rafael and Tomás, Emeline and Aldric, Pip's grandmother and her orchard, and the child Aldous Venn, aged six, whose record ended and who now, at last, was heard. And when the last lamp of that long night guttered and the river still roared and the vault still held, Sable stood up, stretched her back like a woman who'd been at a wheel for days, and said the thing that turned Marisol's blood cold and warm at once:

"Water's dropping. I can hear it dropping. Which means in a few hours, somebody's going to have to figure out how we get out of here—and what's waiting for us on the other side of the flood."
