## 20. Inspector Brant's Report

The rain stopped on the third morning, the way a long argument stops—not with agreement, but with exhaustion.

Callum Brant stood at the lip of the gorge where the helicopter had set him down and looked at what the river had done. The approach to the source cavern was gone. Not damaged—*gone*. The flood-gates the old Vhalorians had built into the rock, gates his own ministry's engineers had dismissed as myth when they surveyed this stretch forty years ago, had opened and swallowed the switchback trail, the landing shelf, the lower weir, and a good hundred meters of gorge wall besides. What remained was a brown lake lying quiet as a held breath between cliffs, and somewhere beneath it, sealed behind stone that had outlasted an empire's spite, the archive.

His report was due in six hours. He had been composing it since the second day of the flood, standing in the lee of the gunboat's wheelhouse with his notebook sheltered under his coat, writing in the small precise hand that had made three commissioners trust him with their dirtiest files.

*Site assessment, upper gorge, following monsoon event.* He crossed it out. Too clinical. The truth required a different register entirely—the register of men who had spent careers learning how to bury things, because only such a man could write a burial convincing enough to hold.

He tried again. *The source cavern, subject of File 9-V (Erasure Records), has been rendered permanently inaccessible by structural collapse of the gorge approach during the flooding of [date]. Subsequent survey confirms total inundation. No recovery operation is feasible at any cost tier. Recommend the file be closed and the site stricken from all future surveys.*

It was, he reflected, the finest lie of his career, and every word of it was true.

That was the joke he would never be able to tell anyone. The cavern *was* collapsed, as far as the ministry would ever need to know. It *was* inundated. No recovery operation *was* feasible—because the people inside it were going to walk out copies through a tunnel a dead archivist's memory had found, and by the time any survey team arrived, there would be nothing left but water and names already scattered downstream like seed.

Brant closed the notebook. His grandfather's name still sat behind his teeth where Marisol's voice had put it, spoken aloud in lamplight through a flooded doorway while he stood in the rain with a service pistol growing heavy in his hand. *Aldous Venn, aged six.* Six years old, and the record ended, and for forty years the file had ended there too, one line in a ledger Brant had signed off on without reading, the way you sign off on everything, the way duty asks you to.

He had lowered the weapon. He had told Vessarin's surviving men to stand down, and two of them had, and the third had looked at Brant the way a dog looks at a master who has suddenly begun speaking another language—and then the water had risen past argument anyway, and the question had been taken out of all their hands.

Now the river was giving it back.

* * *

They came out of the cliff at midday, blinking like creatures from a burrow.

Brant watched from the shingle below the old helicopter pad, hands clasped behind his back, cap brim low, four of his own men arrayed behind him because a man in his position could not be seen arriving alone. Sable Nystrom came first, moving stiffly, one arm pressed to her ribs where the bullet graze had gone angry under the damp. Behind her, Ivo Kestrel carried a canvas satchel with the careful hunch of a man transporting something holier than explosives. Then Marisol, and the boy Pip, gray-faced and fever-bright but upright, and last of all Doss.

Doss. Brant felt the engineer's gaze find him and slide away, the look of a man who had already rehearsed his own arrest on the walk down and found no version of it survivable.

"Inspector." Sable stopped ten paces off. River-mud to the knees, hair plastered flat, eyes like a woman who had read all night and intended to read again tomorrow. "You're blocking our road."

"I'm standing on it," Brant said. "There's a difference. It matters in my line of work."

"You have a warrant?"

"I have a report." He took the folded pages from inside his coat and held them out—not to her, but open, so she could see the heading, the commissioner's routing stamp, the final line: *Recommend file closed; site stricken from record.* "As of this morning, the source cavern of the Vhalorian archive does not exist. It collapsed. Tragically. Unrecoverable. I have surveyed it personally and I grieve."

For a long moment Sable didn't move. Then something shifted in her face—not softening exactly; he doubted this woman softened—but a recalculation, the look of a captain re-reading a chart and finding the shoal marked in the wrong place.

"And if I tell you it didn't collapse," she said, "that we walked out of it not two hours ago carrying—"

"Then I'd say you were a grieving woman prone to confusion after a traumatic flood," Brant said, "and I'd note it in my log as shock." He let his voice drop, just enough that only she and Kestrel could hear. "I listened to every name, Captain. All night. My grandfather was six years old. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am not your problem anymore. I am the last person in this country who will ever come looking. That is worth more than a warrant."

Kestrel spoke for the first time, hoarse from reading, from the knife wound bound under his shirt. "Why?"

It was not an accusation. It was worse—it was genuine curiosity, an archivist's question, and Brant found he wanted to answer it honestly, which frightened him more than the pistol had.

"Because erasure doesn't work," he said. "Forty years, and the whole apparatus of the state, and all it produced was a woman on a riverboat who reads names to the dark until the dark gives up. You can't delete people. You can only make yourself deaf. And I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I spent my career being deaf. I'm filing my hearing back."

* * *

The copies came out over the next two days, and Brant helped carry them.

Not officially. Officially he conducted a three-day site survey, photographing flood damage, taking depth soundings of the new lake, and noting for the record that the terrain was impassable to all further inquiry. Unofficially, he discovered that a government launch has considerable cargo capacity and that no manifest inspector in the district looks twice at crates leaving a senior ministry man's boat.

Kestrel had spent the flooded days well. While the others read names aloud to keep despair at bay, the archivist had been copying—by lamplight, by hand, page after page, prioritizing family ledgers, village rolls, the indexes that let a scattered people find itself again. Three crates. Fourteen hundred pages. Brant carried two of them himself down the goat track to the launch, and if his men wondered why the Inspector insisted on the heaviest loads, none of them asked. They had heard the names too, through the doorway. Some of them had grandparents from villages that weren't on maps.

On the second evening, Doss appeared at the edge of the campfire light.

Brant saw him coming and did not pretend otherwise. The engineer walked like a man approaching his own sentencing—shoulders up, eyes down, tool roll slung across his back because Doss without tools was apparently a concept the universe refused to permit.

"Inspector." He stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to be struck. "I came to give myself up. Whatever happens. I just—" His jaw worked. "My daughter. If there's a way the file on me could—"

"There isn't," Brant said.

Doss flinched.

"There isn't a file on you," Brant continued. "There never was. I threatened you with one, in a room in Corben Landing, because coercion requires a lever and you handed me your daughter the moment you mentioned her school fees. But I never wrote anything down. Men like me know better than to write things down." He looked into the fire. "You gave me the astrolabe because I had your child. Sable Nystrom kept you aboard after she learned it because you have a child. Everyone in this story chose the kid. I'm not going to be the one man who punishes them for it."

"She knows? About me coming here?"

"She'll know what I choose to tell her. Go back to your boat, Engineer. Your daughter needs her school fees more than my ministry needs a confession."

Doss stood there a moment longer, and Brant understood—he had stood in enough courtrooms to understand—that absolution was the hardest thing some men could be made to accept. Then the engineer nodded once, the smallest motion, and walked back toward the Magpie's lights, and his shoulders came down half an inch with every step.

* * *

On the third morning, with the launch loaded and the survey formally concluded, Sable Nystrom came to him at the rail.

She was carrying the astrolabe.

Brant knew it on sight—brass, dense as sin, rings within rings, the kind of instrument that made his ministry's own surveyors weep with envy. Evidence item one of File 9-V. He had photographed it in a warehouse in Corben Landing and dreamed about it since.

"Doss says it belonged to his grandmother's trade guild," Sable said. "Before the Erasure. Says it's the only thing of hers that survived." She turned it in her hands, and the rings caught the morning sun and threw small coins of light across both their faces. "He also says you're the one who pulled his daughter's school file and flagged it for a full scholarship. Ministry education fund. Anonymous benefactor."

"The fund has broad discretion," Brant said.

"It has you," Sable said. "And now it has this." She held out the astrolabe. "For the girl. Tell her it's from her father. Let her grow up thinking her old man salvaged treasure off a river, because he did. Better story than the truth, and truer, if you ask me."

Brant took it. It weighed what a conscience weighs.

"You understand," he said, "that if any of this surfaces—if one crate, one page, one whispered name reaches a newspaper—I will have perjured myself in a federal report, and they will take everything I have."

"Yes," Sable said. "I do understand that." And she smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman who had run the Judgment rapids and meant to run a few more. "Welcome to the crew, Inspector."

* * *

He filed the report at 0900 the next day. By noon it was stamped, countersigned, and archived—File 9-V, *closed, unrecoverable*, the last official sentence ever to be written about the nation of Vhaloria, and a lie so complete it had become a kind of truth: the state would never touch the archive again. It was theirs now. All of theirs.

Brant stood at his office window a long time, watching the river move freight and rain and rumor toward the sea, his grandfather's name quiet in his chest like a coal that would not quite cool.

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper, and in his small precise hand—the hand that had buried a thousand things—began the only document he would ever write for himself:

*Aldous Venn was born in the spring. He liked the orchard.*

Downstream, faint on the wind off the water, he thought he could almost hear a riverboat's whistle—a battered old Magpie, riding the swollen current home, carrying fourteen hundred pages of the living dead back to the land of the living.

The current always returned what it took. He was counting on it.
