## 21. Downstream

The river had changed its mind about everything.

Sable felt it through the wheel before she saw it—the way the current no longer argued with the Magpie but carried her, a broad brown muscle of water swollen fat with monsoon rain, running fast and deep where six weeks ago it had scraped gravel and muttered. The channel markers were drowned. The shoals that had bloodied her keel on the way up were buried under ten feet of churning water. For the first time since Corben Landing, the river was not something she fought. It was something she rode.

She stood at the wheel with the glass cracked beside her ear—starboard pane, spiderwebbed from Vessarin's gunfire at the Narrows—and let the rain come down in long silver sheets beyond the pilothouse roof. The wiper on the port side still worked, dragging its arc across the window like a slow metronome. *Thk. Thk. Thk.* She'd been listening to it for three days.

Behind her, stacked in oilcloth and lashed to the deck below with Doss's best rigging, sat forty-one copies of the ledgers. Forty-one bundles of names. Marisol had counted them twice a day since they'd left the source cavern, touching each bundle like a rosary, and Sable understood why. You didn't carry that much grief downstream without checking that it was still there—that it hadn't dissolved into the rain like everything else the ministry had ever tried to drown.

The gorge fell away behind them by noon of the third day. By evening they were through the last of the flood-tunnels, and the valley opened wide and green and ordinary, terraced fields running down to the water, smoke rising from chimneys of houses that existed on maps, that paid taxes, that had never been erased. Sable watched a woman on the bank pause in her washing to watch them pass—a battered boat riding high and light, running with the flood—and thought: *she'll remember us for an hour and then forget*. That was the way of things now. They had spent six weeks learning what it cost when forgetting became law.

The engine note changed beneath her feet—Doss had rebuilt it twice since the third lock, cannibalizing parts from the flooded engine room, and it ran rough but honest, like an old man with a new hip—and she knew without looking that he'd shifted them down for the bend above Harrow's Reach. Good man. He hadn't said more than forty words to her since he came walking back into camp at the weir, gray-faced, empty-handed, expecting nothing. He'd stood in the rain with his hat in his hands and said, "I don't expect you to take me back."

And Sable had looked at him—at this man who had chosen his daughter over all of them, who had handed Brant the astrolabe with shaking hands because some men in a ministry office had shown him photographs of a schoolyard in Corben Landing—and she'd said the only true thing: "He chose his kid. I'd have done the same." Then she'd put him back on the boiler. What else was there? The river forgave nothing, but she was learning, slowly, at fifty-one years of age, that forgiveness wasn't the river's job. It was hers.

* * *

They made camp that night in a flooded willow stand, tied off to trees whose trunks stood waist-deep in moving water, and Marisol cooked the last of the good rice with river fish and too much pepper because Pip liked it that way and the boy was finally eating again, color back in his face, the septic rope burn on his forearm healing to a pink seam. He sat cross-legged on the cargo hatch with his copied charts spread around him, annotating them from memory—Ivo's memory, mostly, fed to him over six weeks of evenings—and every so often he'd look up at the darkening sky with an expression Sable recognized from mirrors.

He was already planning the next run. So was she. Neither of them had said it yet.

Ivo came up to the pilothouse as the light died. She heard him on the ladder—she always heard him now, had learned the particular weight of his step the way she'd learned the engine's voice—and she kept her eyes on the water while he settled against the chart table beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers. Rain drummed the roof. The wiper went *thk. Thk. Thk.*

"You're smiling," he said.

"I'm not."

"The corner of your mouth. It's been doing that since the gorge."

"It's a muscle spasm. From gripping the wheel."

"Of course." She could hear the smile in his voice, dry and warm, the archivist's voice that had read names aloud for eleven hours in a flooded vault while the world outside tried to drown them. "For the record, Captain, I've catalogued muscle spasms. This one has a distinct etiology."

"And what's that?"

"You're going home lighter than you left." He turned to look out the dark glass. "That's rare. Most people go the other direction."

Sable let the silence sit. Below them she could hear Marisol scolding Pip about pepper quantities, and Doss laughing—one short bark, rusty, like a gate opening for the first time in years—and the sound of it moved through her chest like current through a hull. Six weeks ago that laughter would have meant nothing to her. Six weeks ago she'd have called it noise.

"Ivo." She said his name and he went quiet, the way he did when he understood that what came next mattered. "What happens when we make Corben Landing? When the ledgers are distributed and the chest is empty and you're standing on the dock with your forged passport and your bullion money. What do you do then?"

"That," he said slowly, "is a question I've been avoiding with great discipline."

"Archivists avoid questions?"

"We defer them. We file them under headings that promise future attention." A breath. "There are descendants scattered from here to the coast. Someone has to find them. Someone has to carry—" He stopped. Started again. "I spent my whole life being the last keeper of a country that didn't exist. I told myself that when the delivery was done, I'd be finished. Free." His voice dropped. "But a keeper isn't finished. A keeper is only ever between deliveries."

The rain thickened. Out on the black water, something large turned over—a log, a snag, the river rearranging its furniture—and Sable watched the wake of it catch the last gray light.

"So you'll keep going upstream," she said. "Alone. With a satchel full of names."

"With a satchel full of names," he agreed quietly. "It's what I know how to do."

She turned from the wheel then, finally, and looked at him. In the lamplight of the pilothouse his face was all planes and shadows, the knife scar on his jaw from Vessarin's man still angry-red, and behind his eyes was that thing she'd seen at the Gorge of Names when he'd found his family carved into the canyon wall—the look of a man standing in front of a door he'd been afraid to open his whole life, holding the handle.

"Ivo Kestrel," she said. "You are the worst navigator I have ever met. You can't tie a bowline. You froze at the sight of open water our second day out and I had to steer around a deadfall while you apologized to the river in three languages."

"All true."

"But you read water-signs better than any pilot I've ever steered with, and you held a knife wound closed with your bare hand for two hours so we could live, and you read my father's name off a lock wall like you'd known him." Her voice caught, just slightly, and she let it. "You're not going anywhere alone. Not if I have anything to say about it."

His eyes searched her face. "Sable."

"The delivery is done. You asked me once what I'd do when it was done, and I didn't answer, because I didn't know." She stepped closer, close enough that she could smell the rain on him, the lamp oil, the faint mineral smell of the source cavern that still clung to his coat. "Here's the answer. The river took four people from me and I swore I'd never crew with anyone again, because everything I love goes into the water eventually. And then this ragged, impossible, half-drowned expedition happened, and somewhere between the Judgment rapids and the flood-gates, the river gave me a crew back. Doss. Marisol. Pip. You." She shook her head. "I don't intend to lose another one. Not to the water. Not to the ministry. Not to some fool idea that carrying names is a job for one person."

The wiper dragged its arc. *Thk.*

"Sable Nystrom," Ivo said, and his voice was not steady anymore, "are you offering me a position?"

"I'm offering you a berth. Permanent. Deckhand, navigator, keeper—whatever the day needs." She lifted her chin. "Pay is terrible. Food is Marisol's. The captain is difficult."

"The captain," he said, "is the finest pilot on this river, and I would follow her through any gorge she chose, including ones that aren't on maps." His hand rose, hesitated at the level of her shoulder—an archivist asking permission to touch the document—and she closed the distance herself, took his hand, set it there.

"Then it's settled," she said. "You're hired."

"I haven't signed anything."

"You'll sign. You're an archivist. You can't resist paperwork."

He laughed then—really laughed, the sound startled out of him, bright in the small wet room—and the laugh broke whatever careful distance they'd been keeping since the Narrows, since the tunnel, since the moment he'd pulled her bleeding from the white water and held her face in both hands and said nothing at all because there hadn't been time. There was time now. The whole swollen river of it, running slow and deep toward morning.

Sable reached past him and turned the lamp down to a coal. Rain ran the glass in silver threads. She put her hand flat on his chest, over the heartbeat, over the scar tissue and the stubbornness underneath, and felt him breathe.

"For the record," she murmured, "I've been avoiding this question too. With great discipline."

"Noted for the archive," he said, and kissed her.

It was not gentle, exactly—it was six weeks of white water and held breath and almost-said things arriving all at once, like a lock gate swinging open—but it was certain, the way she steered was certain, reading the current and committing to it, and when they broke apart her forehead rested against his and the wiper went *thk* and neither of them laughed at it, though both of them wanted to.

Below, faintly, Marisol's voice floated up the ladder well: "If either of you is steering, now would be the time to mention it."

"I'm steering," Sable called back, not moving.

"You're not steering," Marisol said, fond and unfooled. "Doss has the wheel. Has for an hour. Supper's on."

Ivo pressed his lips to her hairline, and Sable closed her eyes and let the boat carry them both.

* * *

Corben Landing came up out of the rain two days later, gulls wheeling over the fuel docks, the town smelling of tar and frying bread and the particular green rot of a river in flood. Word had traveled ahead of them somehow—it always did on the river, faster than any boat—and there were people on the dock. Not many. A dozen, maybe. Old men with caps in their hands. Women with the cheekbones of a country that no longer appeared on any map. One girl, perhaps fifteen, standing very straight, watching the Magpie come in like she was watching a ship return from a war.

Marisol stood at the rail with the first bundle of ledgers held against her chest, and Sable saw her take one breath, then another, and then walk down the gangplank into the crowd and hold the bundle out to the old man at the front, and say, in a voice that carried over the rain:

"These are yours. They were always yours. We just carried them."

Sable stayed at the wheel while the names changed hands below. Some of the people were crying. Some were laughing. The girl with the straight back received her family's ledger from Pip's hands and opened it right there on the wet planks, found the page, and read the name aloud to no one, to everyone, to the river—and the river took the sound and carried it downstream, the way it carried everything, the way it returned everything, given time.

Ivo came to stand beside her in the pilothouse. Together they watched the crowd thin, watched the ledgers disperse into a dozen pockets and arms and lives, watched Marisol embrace a woman she'd never met because the woman's mother had grown up two doors from Marisol's brothers.

"Well, Captain," Ivo said softly. "Delivery complete."

Sable looked at the wheel under her hands, at the cracked glass, at the old brass fitting where she intended—come tomorrow, come next week—to mount the Vhalorian seal. She looked at her crew on the dock, soaked and laughing and whole. She looked at the river, high and brown and endless, running south toward places none of them had ever been.

"No," she said, and smiled, and this time she didn't blame the muscle. "Just between deliveries."

She rang the engine bell twice, and the Magpie leaned into the current, and the rain came on, and the river remembered.
