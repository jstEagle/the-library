## 22. A Trail for the Current

The morning fog came off the river the way it always did in late autumn, rising in slow ribbons like the water was breathing out everything it had carried all summer. Sable Nystrom stood at the wheel of the *Magpie* and watched it burn away under a pale sun, and she thought—not for the first time—that the river looked different when you weren't running from something.

The boat had changed. That was the thing nobody warned you about: boats remember what you do to them, and they wear it. The hull still bore the scar where Vessarin's men had cut her mooring lines at the third lock; Doss had patched the engine room with steel that didn't quite match, a bright seam of newer metal running through the old plating like a healed wound. The rail where Pip had frozen during the Judgment rapids had been replaced entirely—new wood, paler than the rest, already darkening at the edges as the weather worked on it. Sable ran her thumb along the wheel's rim, worn smooth now in new places where her hands had gripped through monsoon water and gunfire and grief.

But there were other changes too. The flour barrel Marisol had used to hide the documents stood in the galley still, repainted a cheerful blue, its lid carved with a small Vhalorian knot by Pip's unsteady hand. And above the wheel, waiting on a hook of bent iron, hung the seal—the lead chest's stamp, the double-river crest of a nation that no longer appeared on any map made by any government that mattered.

She hadn't nailed it up yet. She'd been carrying it around for three months the way you carry a letter you haven't decided to send.

* * *

The new route wasn't on any chart either. That was rather the point.

They called it nothing, officially. Unofficially, word traveled the way word always traveled on the river—in taverns, in market whispers, in the careful language of people who had learned forty years ago not to say certain names aloud. A woman in Corben Landing would mention to another woman that the *Magpie* was making an unusual run on Thursday, if anyone happened to be going anywhere. A fisherman would note that Captain Nystrom charged no fare for passengers who could prove a grandmother born upstream of the Gorge of Names. And so they came: old men with folded photographs, young women clutching their children's hands, families who arrived at the gangplank after dark and left before dawn, visiting places that officially did not exist.

Pip ran the manifests now. He'd grown two inches since the monsoon and filled out across the shoulders from hauling line, but he still had the same look when someone spoke a Vhalorian name in his presence—that quick, hungry attention, like a man hearing his own language spoken in a foreign country. He kept his copied charts in an oilcloth roll under his bunk, annotated in three colors of ink: Ivo's archive knowledge in black, the old lock-signals in red, and in green, in his own hand, every village site they'd confirmed. There were more of them than anyone had guessed. Eleven so far. Foundations and orchards gone wild and schoolhouse bells still hanging in their frames, waiting for someone to come and ring them.

Today they were carrying the Ostrava family up to the second village—the one with the wild orchard. Old Tomas Ostrava, ninety-one years old and built like a bundle of dry sticks, sat wrapped in blankets near the boiler with his granddaughter, and he hadn't stopped talking since Corben Landing. He'd been seven when the Erasure came. Seven years old, put on a barge with his mother, told they were being relocated, and then the barge turned downstream instead of up, and the village was behind him forever.

"They burned the records first," he was telling Pip, who was pretending to check a mooring cleat but actually just listening. "Did you know that? Before anything else. My father was a notary—he kept the parish books, births and deaths and marriages, forty years of them—and the ministry men took the whole cabinet out into the square and burned it while we watched. My father never said a word after that. Not one word, for nine years, until he died." The old man's hands trembled on the blanket. "And now your captain tells me there's a book somewhere with my name in it. Written down before the burning. Is that true, boy?"

"It's true," Pip said. "I've seen it. Volume six of the source ledgers. *Ostrava, Tomas, b. Vhalor's Rest.* Your mother's there too. And your father's signature is on the page—it looks like he wrote it fast, but it's there."

Tomas Ostrava put his face in his hands and wept without any sound at all, the way very old men weep, and Pip stood awkwardly beside him until Marisol appeared with tea and a look that sent the boy forward to his work.

Marisol herself had changed less visibly, but Sable saw it in small things. She cooked bigger portions now. She'd taken to setting four places at the crew table out of habit and leaving them set, and no one ever asked why, and no one ever cleared them. Her brothers' names were scratched into a doorframe in a village eleven miles off the main channel, and once a month, weather permitting, the *Magpie* carried her there and waited while she sat in the ruined schoolhouse and talked to them.

Doss came up from the engine room wiping his hands on a rag, gray-streaked and heavier than he'd been before the journey, moving with the careful deliberation of a man whose body had learned what scalding steam could do. His daughter Lissa lived in Corben Landing now, enrolled in a proper school, and he carried her drawing—a crayon riverboat with too many windows—taped inside the cover of his tool chest. He'd come back in the spring, walking up the gangplank with his kit bag and his eyes on the deck, expecting nothing, and Sable had looked at him for a long moment and said only, "Boiler's been missing you." That was all either of them ever needed to say about the weir, about the astrolabe, about Inspector Brant's threats against a child. *He chose his kid. I'd have done the same.* She meant it still.

"Fuel's topped, wheel bearing's greased, and I re-seated the starboard intake strainer," Doss reported. "We're sound."

"We're always sound."

"That's your fault. You keep insisting on it." He squinted up at the hook above the wheel, at the seal hanging there. "You going to put that up today or carry it around another season?"

Sable followed his gaze. "Why does everyone keep asking me that?"

"Because everyone can see you're waiting for something." He shrugged. "I don't pretend to know what. But a thing like that isn't cargo, Captain. It's either part of the boat or it isn't."

He went back below, and Sable stood alone at the wheel with the fog burning off the water and the question sitting in her chest like a stone she'd gotten comfortable holding.

* * *

Ivo found her there at midmorning, as he usually did. He moved differently now than he had that first night in the dockside tavern with his forged passport and his fortune in bullion and his careful, rehearsed charm. The exile had gone out of him by degrees, the way dye goes out of cloth washed over and over—each trip upstream, each village found, each name read aloud lightening him a little. He still wore his archivist's precision like armor sometimes, cataloguing and cross-referencing, but more and more often she caught him simply looking at things. Orchards. Bells. Old women weeping at gates. Learning how to be a man from a country that existed again, if only in the memories of the people who carried it.

He set two cups of coffee on the ledge beside the wheel and leaned against the rail.

"Tomas Ostrava is asking whether we can ring the schoolhouse bell when we arrive," he said. "His granddaughter wants to photograph him standing under it. I told him I'd ask the captain."

"The captain says the bell's been waiting eighty years for somebody with a reason to ring it."

"I'll tell him." He drank his coffee and watched the water. Then, quietly: "There's a package at the Corben Landing office. Marisol picked it up this morning. It's addressed to the *Magpie*, care of the captain."

"From who?"

"There's no name. No return address." Ivo reached into his coat and produced a small parcel wrapped in brown paper and tied with twine, handling it with the deliberate gentleness he reserved for documents four centuries old. "It was posted from Harrow's Bend. That's the nearest post office to—" He stopped. "To the orchard village. To Pip's grandmother's place."

Sable dried her hands slowly on her trousers before she took it. The paper was soft with handling, as though it had been carried a long way before it reached the post. She untied the twine with her thumbnail and folded back the wrapping.

Blossoms. Fresh-cut orchard blossoms, white and faintly pink at the throat, still firm, still fragrant—impossible blossoms, in late autumn, from trees that should have been bare for a month. Someone had cut them recently and recently enough, which meant someone had found a way to keep them, or someone had found a tree that bloomed out of season, or—and this was the thought that raised the hair on Sable's arms—someone was tending that orchard. Pruning it. Keeping it alive the way you keep a grave, or a promise.

Beneath the blossoms lay a strip of paper, written in a careful, old-fashioned hand:

*For the keeper of the route. The trees bloom because someone remembers where they were planted. Come in spring — the whole village will be in flower, and there are those who wish to thank you properly. We have waited a long time to be found.*

No name. Just a small mark pressed into the corner of the paper in faded ink: the double-river crest.

Sable read it twice. Then she held it out, and Ivo read it, and when he looked up his eyes were bright and wet and he laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, like a man hearing a knock at a door he'd sealed shut decades ago.

"Hands unknown," he murmured. "Do you understand what this means? It means there are others. Descendants who stayed near, who kept the ground, who never left even after the maps changed. All these months we thought we were discovering these places, and some of them—we were being *watched*. Welcomed. They've known about us longer than we've known about them."

"Spring," Sable said. "The whole village in flower."

"You'll go?"

"I'll go." She looked down at the blossoms in her palm, delicate as paper, stubborn as anything alive. "Ivo. When I salvaged that chest, I thought I was hauling up salvage. Dead weight. Somebody's drowned paperwork." She shook her head slowly. "It was seeds. The whole time, it was seeds, and I've been carrying them upriver in a flour barrel."

Ivo covered her hand with his own, careful of the blossoms between them. Neither of them said anything else for a while. They'd gotten good, over the months, at letting silence hold what words would only shrink.

* * *

They cast off for the orchard village at noon, with the Ostravas settled aboard and the fog long gone and the river running clear and cold beneath a sky scrubbed blue. Pip stood at the bow with his charts, calling depths out of habit even in water he knew now the way he knew his own heartbeat. Marisol sang in the galley, low and tuneless, a song in a language she'd only learned the words to this year. Doss's engine turned over steady and sure beneath the deck plates, that familiar heartbeat, that forgiveness-with-a-flywheel that Sable had trusted her whole life.

At the wheel, Sable took the seal down from its hook one last time.

She'd brought nails in her coat pocket for a week now. She'd known, probably, since the day Doss first asked her about it. Some things you don't nail up because you're ready; you nail up because the not-nailing has started to cost more than the nailing would. Because a thing that stays in your pocket stays a debt, and a thing on the wall becomes a promise you've made in front of witnesses.

She pressed the lead stamp flat against the bulkhead above the wheel, just high enough to catch the light from the forward windows, and drove the first nail home with the heel of her knife handle, and then the second, and then stepped back.

The double-river crest gleamed dully there, above her head, where it would ride with every voyage from now on. Two rivers crossing. A nation erased from paper but not from water. The current always returns what it takes—she'd learned that the hard way, at the cost of four graves and a scar across her ribs and a year of her life—but standing here, with the blossom-scent still faint on her fingers and her crew around her and a village full of living descendants waiting upstream, she finally understood the other half of it, the half she'd missed:

The current also returns what you give it. Names given to the water came back as people. Grief given to the river came back as purpose. A delivery made in faith came back as a family, gathered at her table, setting four places and leaving them set.

"Captain?" Pip called from the bow. "Channel's clear to the bend. You want to take her up?"

Sable Nystrom put her hands on the wheel—on the old wood and the new, on every mile it had carried her—and swung the *Magpie*'s bow into the current, heading for a village that did not exist, carrying passengers bound for places that were nowhere, under a seal that was nowhere and everywhere, and she felt the river take the hull the way it always did, gently at first, then wholly, the way it takes everything eventually and gives most of it back.

"Ahead slow," she said, grinning. "Let's go see what blooms."

Behind her, nailed fast above the wheel, the seal of Vhaloria rode into the current—and far upriver, past the gorge, past the cataract, past the flooded vault where ten thousand names slept safe in the dark, the river ran on toward the sea, remembering everything.
