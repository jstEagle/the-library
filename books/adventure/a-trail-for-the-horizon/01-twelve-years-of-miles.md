## 1. Twelve Years of Miles

The Serapine at low water was a brown ribbon stitched through green country, and Yara Brightwater knew every stitch of it the way a seamstress knows her thread—by tension, by pull, by the places it frayed.

She stood at the sweep oar in the stern of the *Kestrel* and read the morning river. A mile downstream, where the current bent hard around the drowned fig at Cutter's Point, the surface lay smooth as poured wax. That smoothness was a lie. Beneath it, the water piled against the sunken root ball and rolled back on itself in a trough deep enough to ship a ton of gray water over any gunwale foolish enough to cut the corner close. Twelve years ago that lie had nearly ended her. Now she gave the point a wide, lazy berth, swinging the loaded barge out toward the far channel as if she had all the time the river had ever made.

She did have time. That was the whole trick of it.

"Point's clean if you hug the near bank," called Tam from the bow, where he crouched among the cargo with a boathook across his knees. He was seventeen, all elbows and opinions, and he'd been saying variations of that sentence since Merrow.

"The near bank is where the snags live," Yara said. "And I don't hug anything."

He grinned and let it go. He always let it go. She paid him three silver marks a run plus his food, and he carried his pay sewn into his shirt because he trusted her more than he trusted banks, which she considered the only sensible arrangement between two people who were not family. They were not family. They were not friends, exactly. He worked for her; she paid him; when they reached Salt Landing he would take his wages and vanish into the market noise until the next run, and neither of them would pretend otherwise. It was a clean way to share a boat. Clean things didn't rot.

The *Kestrel*, of course, was rotting anyway.

Yara could feel it through the deck boards—a softness under her left boot, just aft of the midship hatch, where the planking had begun its long argument with the water and was slowly losing. The hull groaned in a register she'd learned to sort: the good groans of a working boat flexing, and the bad ones, the wet whispers along the seams below the waterline that said *pump me, pump me, pump me.* She pumped twice an hour now, where once a day had done. The bilge smelled of rust and old fish and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. Boats died the way people did, she thought—not all at once but by increments, each one small enough to ignore.

She ignored them. She patched what could be patched, caulked what could be caulked, and told herself the same thing she'd told herself for twelve years: one more season. One more season, and then she'd have enough put by to re-bottom her properly, or buy a new hull outright, or—

Or nothing. There was no *or*. There never had been. The saving was the point, not the spending. A woman with money put by owed nobody, needed nobody, could tie up at any dock in the world and be a stranger with coin instead of a stranger without. Coin was a kind of distance you could carry in your pocket, and Yara Brightwater had spent twelve years buying distance by the mark.

The low Serapine unspooled ahead of them, wide and slow and brown-green, fringed with reed beds that whispered when the wind came off the water. Egrets stood in the shallows like clerks waiting for a counter to open. On the left bank, terraced gardens climbed away from the flood line—yams, cassava, the blue-flowered beans the river folk planted where the silt ran richest—and smoke rose thin and domestic from cooking fires behind screens of woven palm. Somewhere upstream a drum talked to another drum, a conversation too old and too quick for Yara to follow anymore, though once—she pushed the thought down before it could grow hands—once she had spoken that language fluently.

"Fish traps," Tam said, pointing. "Somebody's been busy."

"Left side's the Ondu family's water," Yara said. "Right side belongs to the people past the bend. Don't touch either."

"I wasn't going to touch anything."

"You were looking at it like you might."

He laughed. She didn't. Not because the joke failed, but because laughing loosened things, and she had spent a decade and a half keeping everything cinched tight as lashing on a storm load.

They passed a ferry crossing where children swam naked in the brown water, shrieking, diving off a moored dugout. One of them raced the *Kestrel* for thirty yards, arms windmilling, before the current took him sideways and he gave up, treading water, waving. Tam waved back. Yara kept her eyes on the channel and her hands on the sweep, and felt the wave pass through her like weather she refused to acknowledge.

Twelve years of miles. She had counted them once, early on, the way a prisoner counts days. After that she'd stopped counting and simply accumulated them, mile laid on mile like silt, until the number was too large to mean anything and the counting itself became the life. Down with the dry-season cargo—dried fish, palm oil, woven mats, the occasional crate of something fragile she charged extra to carry. Up with salt, iron, lamp oil, cloth. Down again. Up again. The river went nowhere and so, in every way that mattered, did she.

It was a good life. She had built it plank by plank, and it floated, and it was hers, and if there were nights at anchor when the fireflies came up off the water in their thousands and the silence got so complete she could hear her own heart working, and something in her chest turned over like a boat taking water—well. Every hull leaked. You pumped and you went on.

* * *

Salt Landing announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, drying fish, tar, and under it all the green-rot sweetness of the marsh at the town's back door. Then the docks swung into view—three long piers on pilings, warehouses with their shutters propped, the masts and awnings of a dozen smaller craft, and above everything the flagstaff where the colonial colors hung limp in the dead air.

Yara brought the *Kestrel* in against the current, feathering her way into the gap between a grain barge and a leaning ferry, and set her alongside the middle pier with a kiss so soft the fenders barely sighed. Tam made fast fore and aft while she stepped onto planking gone silver-gray with age.

"Manifest," said the dock clerk, appearing as dock clerks did, from wherever they gestated. This one was new—young, sweating through his collar, with the ink-stained fingers of a man who wrote all day and lifted nothing heavier than a stamp. He took her manifest, read it upside down out of habit, and stamped it.

"Rice flour and lamp oil, forty crates mats, six dried fish bundles," Yara recited. "Fees."

"Fees." He consulted a slate. "Berthage, wharfage, cargo duty—" he named a figure that had grown by a tenth since the last run. Yara paid it without comment, in exact coin, counted onto the scarred wood of his desk. She always paid exactly. No credit, no debt, no favor asked or given. The clerk counted it again anyway, which she also expected.

"There's a notice," he said, as she turned to go. "Posted this morning. You'll want to see it, probably. Being a licensed trader."

"I don't want to see most things."

"It's about the upper river."

That stopped her, though she'd have denied it under oath. The upper river was none of her business. She ran the low water and the middle reaches, and above the Gorge of Nine Bends the Serapine turned into something with teeth—water that ate boats and kept them. Nothing up there concerned her.

The notice board stood at the pier head, a plank frame under a little slanted roof, layered with tariffs and tide tables and warnings about counterfeit currency. The new notice was pinned dead center, fresh paper, crisp type:

**BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSIONER OF LANDS AND SURVEYS**

*Government survey parties are hereby proceeding upriver to conduct hydrographic and boundary surveys of the Serapine headwaters and adjoining territories. All licensed traders and carriers may be requisitioned for transport and logistical support upon presentation of proper authority. Obstruction of survey operations constitutes an offense against the Crown.*

*By command, etc.*

Yara read it twice. The words sat wrong in her stomach, though she couldn't have said why at first. *Boundary surveys.* Boundaries were lines drawn on paper by men who had never been rained on. *Adjoining territories.* Territories belonged to whoever lived on them; everyone on the river knew that, even if the paper said otherwise.

"Survey teams," the young clerk said, coming up beside her with the eager air of a man delivering news he found important. "Three of them. Went up past Fort Merrow last month. There's some talk the first party's had trouble." He lowered his voice, pleased with himself. "Trouble being, nobody's heard from them."

"People go missing on the upper river," Yara said. "It's what the upper river does."

"Not whole parties, though. Not with their gear." He glanced around, enjoying his role. "My cousin's mate works the Merrow telegraph. Says the instruments came back down. Carried down by porters, all neat, packed in their cases. But the men didn't come with them."

Yara looked at the notice again. *Requisitioned.* The word had teeth of its own. Her permit—the laminated thing in the oilcloth wallet inside her shirt, renewed every year, paid for every year, the single thread that tied her legally to the only trade she knew—her permit was issued by the same office whose name headed this notice. And notices like this one always ended the same way: with the government needing boats, and boat owners discovering what *may be requisitioned* meant when refusal was priced in revocation.

"They'll want the big barges first," she said. "Not the likes of mine."

"Probably." The clerk shrugged. "But they're posting at every licensed berth from here to the falls. Figured you'd want to know."

She wanted, very specifically, not to know. She wanted to load her return cargo, pay her fees, take on water, and slide back down the river she understood, into the season she had planned, inside the life she had built plank by careful plank.

Instead she stood at the pier head in the flat heat and looked upriver—north, where the water came from, where the country rose into hills she hadn't seen in twelve years and had sworn, in the specific and private way of oaths that are never spoken aloud, never to see again.

Smoke still rose from the gardens on the far bank. The drums still talked their quick old talk. Somewhere above the Nine Bends, men with instruments in neat rows were drawing lines on paper, and those lines, when they were finished, would come down the river like floodwater, and everything they covered would belong to someone else.

"Tam," she called. "We're loading by noon. I want to be at Merrow inside four days."

His head came around, surprised. "Merrow? That's up past the middle reach. Your permit doesn't cover—"

"My permit covers whatever they decide it covers." She folded the oilcloth wallet deeper into her shirt, against her skin, where it rode like a second heartbeat. "Get the mats stowed. We're going up."

The clerk watched her go with the expression of a man who has just seen the first leaf of a season turn. Above the pier, the colonial colors stirred once in a breath of wind that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of rain from the highlands—and then hung dead again, and the river ran on brown and patient toward the sea, carrying everything down and giving nothing back.
