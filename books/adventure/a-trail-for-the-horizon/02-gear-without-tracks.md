## 2. Gear Without Tracks

Fort Merrow announced itself the way all government places did: by smell before sight. Woodsmoke and wet lime, tar and tinned meat, the particular staleness of paper that had been filed too long in river humidity. Yara caught it a full bend downstream, riding the morning breeze off the water, and her shoulders set themselves against it without asking her permission.

She brought the *Kestrel* in slow, reading the current off the fort's stone quay the way she'd read it twelve years running. The Serapine ran wide and brown-green here, fat with highland silt, and the pilings of the official dock stood in a rank of green-furred posts like bad teeth. A customs shed. A flagpole with a flag gone gray as dishwater. Two soldiers leaning on rifles, doing their best impression of men who were not watching every boat that came down from the hills.

Yara tied off at the far end of the quay, among the trading skiffs, where the mud was honest and the smell was fish instead of ink.

"Permit," said the clerk inside the customs shed, before she'd finished ducking through the door. He was a thin man with ink to his elbows and a mosquito coil burning itself out on the sill, and he didn't look up from his ledger.

She put her trading permit on the counter. It was soft at the corners now, the stamp faded to the color of weak tea. Twelve years of renewals. Twelve years of paying her fees on time, hauling nothing contraband, owing no one anything.

The clerk examined it for longer than it deserved, then looked up, and something in his face made her stomach drop before he said a word.

"Brightwater. The cargo boat." He turned the ledger around so she could see an entry fresh enough to smear. "You run upstream, don't you. Above the gorge."

"I run wherever there's freight."

"You're going to run above the gorge." He said it flatly, the way a man reads a sentence someone else has written. "Requisition order came down yesterday. All licensed vessels are subject to conscription for government service." He tapped the ledger. "That includes you."

Yara kept her voice level. "Conscription for what?"

"The Merrow Survey Party. Six men, one guide, went up past the Gorge of Nine Bends three weeks ago to fix benchmarks for the new concession boundaries." He paused, and his eyes flicked toward the soldiers outside, then back. "They haven't come down."

"Rivers eat people. That's not news."

"These didn't drown." The clerk lowered his voice despite himself. "A patrol found their camp. Instruments stacked in perfect rows. Theodolite, levels, chain measures, all of it cleaned and arranged like a shop display. Cook fire cold but tidy. Boots by the tents." He swallowed. "No tracks leaving camp. None. Not theirs, not anyone's. Ground soft enough to hold a print after rain, and nothing."

Yara said nothing. She'd heard stories like this before, usually secondhand and usually drunk, and they always ended with the teller looking over his shoulder. But the clerk wasn't drunk, and he wasn't enjoying himself. He was frightened, and more than that, he was embarrassed—frightened the way a man is frightened when the thing scaring him doesn't fit anywhere he can file it.

"So find another boat," she said.

"Every other licensed vessel is already taken. Grain run, mail run, troop barge." He slid the permit back across the counter, then laid his hand flat on top of it before she could pick it up. "Refusal means revocation. No permit, no legal cargo, no dock rights at any colonial station on the Serapine. You'd be running smuggler's water for the rest of your life."

The words sat between them like a stone dropped in still water. Yara looked at his hand, pale and soft, pressing down on twelve years of her careful, paid-up, nobody-owes-nobody life.

"And if I go?"

"Standard requisition rates. Fuel allowance. And your permit renewed ahead of schedule when you bring them back or bring back proof of what happened to them." He finally lifted his hand. "Inspector Crane will want to speak with you. He's running the search. He's in the map room."

* * *

The map room was the largest room in Fort Merrow, which told Yara everything about what the government thought the river was for. A long table dominated it, scarred oak buried under charts, and bent over those charts was a man built like a folded umbrella—tall, narrow, joints sharp, dressed in a linen uniform too clean for the climate and sweating through it anyway.

He looked up when she entered, and his eyes did a quick inventory: hands, boots, the salt-stiff line of her shoulders, the knife at her belt. Cataloguing. She'd seen buyers do it with cattle.

"Yara Brightwater. Master of the *Kestrel*." Inspector Crane had a voice like dry paper tearing slowly. "Your clerk tells me you know the water above Nine Bends better than anyone licensed."

"I know it."

"Then you'll take us up."

"Us?"

"Myself, two soldiers, and my guide." He straightened. "Ede. He was with the survey party. The only one who came back."

The name meant nothing to her, but the word *guide* snagged like a burr under her skin. Every pilot knew guides who came back alone. Sometimes it meant luck. Sometimes it meant the guide had been walking at the front when whatever happened, happened. And sometimes—

"Where is he?"

"In the yard. He doesn't care for rooms." Crane gathered a folded chart and tucked it under his arm. "Master Brightwater, I'll be plain with you because I'm told you appreciate plainness. Three weeks ago six trained surveyors vanished from a secured camp without leaving a footprint. The monsoon front is building in the highlands—in ten days, perhaps twelve, the river will be unfittable above the gorge until September. The concession permits expire at the end of the month regardless. If we don't locate that party or their instruments before the water rises, the entire survey season is lost, and the company behind it loses a very great deal of money." He smiled without warmth. "Money, as I'm sure you know, does not forgive."

"And me?"

"You get paid. Your permit gets renewed. And you get the satisfaction of having been useful to civilization." He gestured toward the door. "Come and meet your other passenger."

* * *

The man in the yard was sitting on an ammunition crate in the shade of the powder magazine, eating nothing and watching everything. Gaunt wasn't strong enough a word—he was the kind of thin that comes from walking rather than starving, all tendon and weather, with a face burned dark and creased deep around the eyes. River-people features, hill-country coloring. His gear was government issue but worn like it belonged to him, and his rifle lay across his knees with the casualness of a man who'd carried it so long it had grown into his hands.

"Ede," Crane said. "This is Brightwater. She pilots."

Ede looked at Yara, and she felt the look go through her like a sounding line—down, down, touching bottom somewhere. Then he nodded once, and that was all.

"You guided the survey party," she said.

"Yes."

"Six men. You came back alone."

"Yes."

"That's a short story. I don't like short stories."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the ghost of where a smile might have lived once. "It's not short. It's just mostly silence." He stood, unhurried, and shouldered his pack. "I can show you the camp. I can show you where the trail ends. What I can't do is explain it, and I won't pretend otherwise."

"Honest," Yara said. "Or rehearsed."

"Both, probably." He looked at her again, and this time there was something almost like appeal in it, quickly shuttered. "Captain, when do we leave?"

"Not captain. Master. And we leave when I say." She turned to Crane. "Fuel, provisions, and pay in advance. Half now. And I want it understood—" she looked from Crane to Ede and back, "—on my boat, my rules. We run the water my way or not at all. The first man who argues with me about the river gets put ashore, inspector or no."

Crane's jaw tightened, but he inclined his head a degree. "Agreed."

Yara walked back down to the quay to ready the *Kestrel*, and only when her back was to them did she let her face do what it wanted. Resentment sat in her chest like ballast—old, familiar, load-bearing. Twelve years she'd kept the world at oar's length, paid everyone, owed no one, belonged nowhere, and in one morning the government had hooked its fingers into all of it: her permit, her boat, her river. Now she was to carry a frightened clerk's fears, a dry-paper inspector's ambitions, and a gaunt stranger whose story was *mostly silence* up into country where six men had left their boots behind and walked out of the world.

She checked the fuel lines twice. Checked the bilge. Coiled her lines the way she liked them, each thing in its place, everything accounted for—the way she kept her whole life, everything stowed, nothing loose, nothing that could be taken from her without her knowing exactly what was gone.

By late afternoon the *Kestrel* was loaded, the soldiers were aboard looking seasick before they'd left the dock, and Crane stood at the bow rail with his chart already open. Ede settled himself amidships, pack between his feet, rifle across his knees, and watched the fort shrink behind them with an expression Yara couldn't read and didn't try to.

She took her place at the sweep oar, felt the current take the hull, and pointed the bow upstream, toward the gorge, toward the rising water and the neat rows of instruments waiting in the empty green.

Behind them, the first thunder of the monsoon season muttered somewhere over the highlands, low and patient, like something clearing its throat.

And Ede, watching the water slide past, said quietly, almost to himself: "You'll want to see the camp before you see anything else."

Yara didn't answer. But she marked the way he said *see*, and she marked the way he wouldn't say *explain*, and she added it to the growing weight in the bottom of her boat, alongside the fuel drums and the fear of a thin clerk and the memory of a permit with soft corners.

Twelve years of miles, and now this. She pulled for the horizon and let the river decide how much truth it would give up today.
