## 3. The Stranger's Silence

Ede had lied to better men than Yara Brightwater, and he had never once enjoyed it.

He sat in the stern of the *Kestrel* with a coil of rope across his knees, pretending to check the splice, and watched her work the boat upriver through water that was trying to kill them. The channel had narrowed since dawn. What had been a broad brown road was now a throat of rock and current, the Serapine pinched between limestone shoulders, running fast and green-black in the shadows. Every few minutes Yara would stand at the tiller, tilt her head as if listening to something no one else could hear, and then move—two degrees to port, a hard shove of throttle, a drift that looked like surrender until it wasn't.

She read the river like scripture. Not like a book—like scripture. Something memorized so deep it had stopped being words.

*Voss didn't vanish,* Ede thought, for the hundredth time since Fort Merrow. *He walked into the hills on his own two feet, and I watched him go.*

The lie he'd told was a careful one, built from true pieces. He *had* guided the survey team above the gorge. He *had* been the last to see them. He *had* fled downriver alone, half-mad with exhaustion, and been picked up by a patrol skiff three days later, babbling about instruments left in rows and no tracks anywhere. All of it true. He had simply let the officials assemble the wrong story out of honest parts—the way you could build a dam out of river stones, if you were patient enough and didn't mind what got drowned.

The penalty for lying to a colonial magistrate was prison. The penalty for telling the truth, he suspected, was worse.

"Rope," Yara said, without turning her head.

He handed it forward. She took it one-handed, never taking her eyes off the water ahead, where a fallen fig tree leaned out from the bank like a man testing ice. The current piled against the trunk and curled back on itself, and beneath the boil there was a seam of slower water that only she seemed able to see. She threaded the *Kestrel* through it so close to the branches that leaves brushed the deckhouse roof, and then they were through, and the river widened a handspan, and she exhaled through her nose the way other people shrugged.

"You've done this before," Ede said.

"Twelve years."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get free." But there was no edge in it. She glanced back at him, and in the flat morning light he saw the lines around her eyes, the salt-bleached streak in her dark hair he hadn't noticed at the fort. "You guided Voss's team up here. You must know these channels."

"I know them walking. Not driving." He kept his voice level. "Different river when you're carrying weight."

Something moved behind her eyes—not suspicion exactly, but the place where suspicion would live if it ever came looking. He made himself hold her gaze. Lying well, he had learned long ago, was mostly the discipline of not filling silences.

She turned back to the tiller.

They ran upstream all morning, past banks where the jungle pressed close enough to touch, past fishing platforms abandoned for the season, past a shrine of stacked stones at a bend where every passing boatman, he knew, added a pebble. Yara did not add one. He noticed that. He noticed that she looked at the shrine too long, the way you look at a door you've locked yourself out of.

* * *

The first sign of trouble came at noon, and it came as a smell.

Ede caught it before the engine did—a thin sweetness under the diesel reek, like solvent where solvent shouldn't be. He was forward checking the fuel gauge because Yara had ordered him to, in four words, and he had obeyed in silence, because that was the economy they'd established: she commanded, he complied, and neither of them acknowledged that he was paying off a debt neither had named.

"Brightwater." His voice came out wrong. Too tight.

She was beside him in three strides. He pointed at the deck seam below the auxiliary tank, where a slow black thread was crawling aft along the planking, finding every low spot, writing its signature in diesel.

"Line's leaking," he said. "Not weeping. Leaking."

Yara dropped to her knees without a word. She pulled the inspection panel, reached into the hot dark of the engine bay, and came out with fingers slicked black. Then she went still in a way that frightened him more than the fuel had.

"Cut," she said finally. "Look."

She held up the line. There was a slit in the rubber, clean and deliberate, angled so the leak would start small and grow—so it would show itself hours after whoever made it was gone. Not wear. Wear frays. This was a wound.

"When?" Ede asked, though his stomach already knew.

"Fort Merrow." She said it flatly. "Or the dock before it. Someone wanted us dead in the gorge, or stranded above it. Either serves."

Ede crouched beside her and looked at the cut and felt the guilt in his chest sharpen into something with edges. Because he knew who moved through the survey camp freely. He knew which men had come and gone in the night while the instruments sat in their perfect rows. He had assumed the sabotage was aimed at the search party—aimed at *him*, maybe, at the one witness who'd walked away. But watching Yara's face, grim and unsurprised, he understood she had lived twelve years expecting the world to cut her fuel lines, and that made it worse, not better.

"We patch or we turn back," she said. "Turning back ends your permit and mine both."

"Then we patch."

"It'll take hours. Splicing rubber line with the river rising—" She stopped. Looked at him. "You know anything about engines?"

"Enough to be dangerous."

"Good. Be dangerous quietly."

* * *

They tied off at a gravel bar in the lee of a bluff, and worked through the afternoon while the sky over the highlands went the color of a bruise coming up. Yara stripped and spliced; Ede held, pumped bailing water, and handed tools at the right moment, which was apparently the whole art of being useful to her. Neither spoke more than necessary. The river talked enough for both of them—it had changed its voice since morning, deeper now, with a new undertone, the sound of a thing gathering itself.

It was during the third splice, his hands black to the wrist, that Ede almost told her.

She was bent over the fitting, hair tied back, swearing softly at a clamp, and the words rose in him like floodwater: *Voss is alive. Voss walked into the hills above the ninth bend. I carried his pack to the treeline and he told me to say nothing, and I have been saying nothing ever since, and it is eating me alive.*

He even opened his mouth.

And then he thought of Inspector Crane's ledger, the neat columns of names, the way Crane had looked at him at the fort—not suspicious yet, but *cataloguing*, filing him under things to be examined later. He thought of the concession men in their white shirts at Merrow, and the price on inconvenient witnesses, and the fact that whatever protected Voss was the same thing that would destroy anyone who helped him. And he thought, most shamefully, most honestly: *I want to live.*

He closed his mouth. He handed her the clamp.

But she had seen the opening of it. She straightened slowly, wiping her hands on a rag, and looked at him with those river-colored eyes that missed nothing that floated past.

"You were going to say something."

"The clamp's loose."

"No." Quietly. "It isn't."

The current pushed against the hull. Somewhere upstream thunder muttered, or the gorge talking to itself, hard to tell. Ede held her gaze and felt the lie sit between them like a third passenger.

"Later," he said. "When I can tell it right."

She studied him a long moment. He braced for her to push—she struck him as a woman who pushed—but instead she nodded once, turned back to the engine, and said, "Hand me the knife, then. If we're doing this, we're doing it before dark."

That frightened him more than anger would have. Anger he could answer. Patience, from a woman like her, was a debt accruing interest.

* * *

They made another six miles before full dark forced them into an eddy behind a rock spine, and it was there, banking the fire low, that Ede understood how bad the arithmetic had become.

Yara stood at the rail looking upstream, toward the gorge, toward the ninth bend, toward the hills where a man she believed drowned or taken was in fact alive and hiding. Behind them, downstream, the monsoon front had swallowed the horizon entire, a wall of purple-black with lightning stitching inside it like something struggling in a net. Between the two, on a leaking boat with a patched fuel line and a crew of two liars, they were expected to find seven missing men before the river rose and erased every track in the world.

"Your line will hold?" he asked.

"My line will hold." She didn't look at him. "Question is whether you will."

He kept his face still. Twelve years of miles had taught her to read water; the last three days were teaching him that she read people the same way, current by current, seam by seam, everything visible to her that he tried to hide.

"Everyone holds," he said, "until they don't."

"Hm." Almost a laugh. "Honest, at least."

She went below to sleep, or to pretend to, and Ede stayed at the rail with the fire dying and the rain beginning, fat warm drops that would become the deluge by morning. Upstream, faint through the rain-hiss, he heard the gorge—the real sound of it now, not the postcard version, a grinding boom like a door being opened very slowly by something enormous.

Tomorrow they'd run it. Tomorrow she'd thread the *Kestrel* through Nine Bends with the river rising, hunting ghosts, and he'd sit in the stern holding his silence like cargo that grew heavier every mile.

He looked at his hands, still stained black with her diesel, and thought: *the cut in that line was meant to strand us above the gorge. Someone wanted the search party stuck up there, in the rising water, with whatever Voss left behind.*

Which meant someone knew Voss had left something.

Which meant, by tomorrow night, someone might decide Ede knew it too.

The rain thickened. Downstream, lightning lit the wall of the storm from within, and for one white instant Ede saw the whole river laid bare—every bend, every bar, every mile between him and the truth—and then the dark came back down like a lid, and the *Kestrel* rocked on the swelling water, and he went below to lie awake beside the man-sized hole in everything he hadn't said.
