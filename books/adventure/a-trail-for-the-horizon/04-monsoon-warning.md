## 4. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came up out of the highlands like a debt being called in.

Yara had watched the sky over the Serapine for twelve years, and she knew its moods the way a wife knows a husband's silences. This was not weather. This was intention. The cumulus stacked themselves above the western ridgeline, blue-black and bruised at their bases, taller every hour, and beneath them the river had already begun to change—running a finger's width higher that morning, running *darker*, carrying down from the headwaters the first brown suggestion of what was coming.

"Front'll break by tomorrow night," she said to Ede, who stood at the bow rail with his hands folded behind him like a man at a funeral. "Maybe sooner. When it does, this channel becomes a millrace with debris in it. Whole trees. We don't want to be on open water when that arrives."

Ede said nothing. He'd said very little for two days, which suited her fine; she hadn't taken this job for conversation. She took it because Inspector Crane's men held her trading permit in one hand and a pen in the other, and she'd learned long ago that the only way to keep something was to never let anyone see how much it mattered.

The *Kestrel* groaned beneath her feet as if she disagreed.

Yara went below to check the bilge, and her stomach dropped before her eyes confirmed it. Water sloshed around the stringers, six inches of it, maybe more—gray water, river water, coming in faster than the old hand pump could answer. She knelt and worked the handle anyway, muscle memory from a hundred small emergencies, watching the level fall an inch and then hold, fall an inch and then hold. The pump was losing. The pump had been losing for years, honestly, but the river had been patient about it until now.

She pumped for twenty minutes, arms burning, and gained nothing. Above her she heard Ede's boots cross the deck, pause, cross again. Then his voice through the deck boards, flat as slate: "It's rising."

"I know."

"The water's coming in faster than you're taking it out."

"I know that too." She climbed the ladder into the gray light. "There's a village four bends upstream. Tallai. Stilt houses, deep anchorage behind the point. We shelter there, I patch what needs patching, and we run the gorge after the front passes."

"You know people there?"

The question landed harder than he could have known. Yara turned to check the fuel gauge rather than answer, and Ede, whatever else he was, had the sense not to ask twice.

* * *

They made Tallai as the first fat drops came down, heavy and warm as coins spat from a rich man's hand. The village sat on its stilts above the flood line like a flock of grey herons, thirty houses on ironwood legs, connected by walkways worn smooth by generations of bare feet. Behind the point, the anchorage lay calm and dark, and Yara brought the *Kestrel* in on the last of her steering before the rudder began to feel like it was dragging through porridge.

Villagers gathered along the main walkway as she tied off—not hostile, not welcoming either. Watchful. An old man with a face like cracked river clay studied her boat, then her, then her boat again, and something flickered across his features.

Then a child screamed.

Not in fear. A boy of perhaps eight came pelting down the walkway, barefoot, past the elders, past everyone, and skidded to a stop at the rail above her deck with his mouth wide open.

"It's the Heron," he shouted back up the walkway. "It's the Grey Heron! The Grey Heron's come back!"

The name went through the village like wind through cane. Yara stood with a mooring line still wrapped around her fist and felt twelve years of careful distance collapse all at once, like a wall she'd built stone by stone discovering it had been standing on the riverbank all along.

*Grey Heron.* Because she'd stood at the bow of her father's boat, skinny and patient, one leg tucked up, waiting for fish to move before striking—her uncle's joke, told so many times it stopped being a joke and became simply who she was. She hadn't heard it since she was nineteen years old. She had made sure of it.

An old woman pushed through the crowd, small and straight-backed, silver hair cut blunt at the jaw. Ama Sefi. Older now, her hands knotted like roots, but the eyes were the same eyes that had fed Yara cassava cakes on the dock when her father drank too much, the same eyes that had taught her which plants healed and which only looked like they did.

"So," Ama Sefi said. "Twelve years, and the river finally gives you back."

"Not the river," Yara said. "Weather. I'm sheltering. I'll be gone before it clears."

"Mm." Ama Sefi looked at the bilge water seeping through the deck seams, at the rust streaks down the hull, at the patched sail folded like a wounded wing. "Gone in that? The Heron always did lie about her nest."

Laughter moved along the walkway, gentle, unkind in the way only family can be unkind. Yara felt heat climb her neck. She was forty-one years old and being teased by children and grandmothers on a stilt dock, and some traitor part of her chest ached with how much she had missed it.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the rain settled in earnest, drumming on the canopy Yara rigged over the open hold. Two of Ama Sefi's grandsons helped her re-caulk the worst seams with resin and oakum the villagers traded upriver for; Ede, saying almost nothing, proved unexpectedly good with a caulking mallet, and she filed that away among the other things she didn't understand about him. The pumps got a rest. The *Kestrel* settled on her lines like a tired animal allowed finally to lie down.

At dusk the whole village crowded under the great house's roof—Ama Sefi's house, the largest, raised highest—and ate river fish and bitter greens while the storm tested the stilts and found them good. Children stared at Yara openly. One little girl crept close enough to touch her sleeve and then fled giggling, as though the nickname were a spell and touching the woman might confirm it true.

"They tell stories about you," Ama Sefi said, settling beside her with a clay cup of rice spirit. "That you went downriver and got rich. That you forgot your own name."

"I didn't get rich."

"No. But did you forget?"

Yara turned the cup in her hands. Below them the *Kestrel* rode her lines in the rain-dark anchorage, and beyond her the Serapine ran high and brown and getting browner, and somewhere up that river, above the Gorge of Nine Bends, a survey team's instruments sat in perfect rows in the mud, waiting to be picked up again.

"Why do you think we're here?" she asked quietly. "The officials. The survey."

"We know why." Ama Sefi's voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "A man came through last month. Measured our fields with instruments on tripods. Asked whose land this was, whose land that was. My grandson asked him why a river survey needed to measure gardens that have never flooded in living memory." She sipped her spirit. "He wrote things in a book and would not answer."

"They say it's for navigation charts."

"And you believe that?"

Yara thought of the instruments arranged in neat rows, no footprints, no tracks—a scene that had bothered her for days without her knowing why. Neat rows meant order. Order meant paperwork. Paperwork meant someone downstream wanted those numbers in a particular form.

"No," she admitted. "I don't."

"The concession men came to the lower villages first," Ama Sefi said. "Timber, rubber, whatever the papers call it. They showed documents with seals. The documents said the land belonged to the company, because the land was 'unregistered.' Unregistered." She laughed once, without any humor in it. "We have fished this bend since before Fort Merrow had a name. But we did not write it down in their language, so it is empty. So we are ghosts. And you can move ghosts anywhere you like."

Around them the great house murmured—elders speaking low, a baby fussing, rain on thatch. Yara looked at these faces and understood, with a slow cold turning in her gut, that she wasn't guiding a rescue anymore. She was ferrying the thing that would erase them. Every mile she put behind them upstream was a mile closer between these people and the eviction notices. Her permit, her boat, her twelve years of paying everyone and owing no one—all of it bought with distance from exactly this.

She had run from this village at nineteen because staying meant choosing, and choosing meant someone could take the choice away from her. She had spent her whole life since making sure she belonged nowhere, because nowhere couldn't be burned down.

And here she was, drinking rice spirit under the roof of the woman who had half-raised her, while the government's mapmakers waited upstream for her boat.

"The monsoon will swell the river three days from now," Ama Sefi said, watching her face. "After that, the gorge runs too fast for anything but a madwoman. Whatever you're carrying up there, Heron—you should decide what it is before the water decides for you."

* * *

Near midnight, unable to sleep in the guest house they'd been given, Yara walked out onto the wet planks. The rain had thinned to a drifting mist. Down in the anchorage the *Kestrel* swung gently on her line, lantern lit in the cabin where Ede sat awake—he was always awake, she was starting to notice, always awake and always watching the hills.

She stood at the rail and let herself remember, just for a moment, what it had been to be the Grey Heron: a girl with river mud to her knees who belonged to every house on this walkway, who owed everyone and was owed by everyone, and had never once been lonely.

Footsteps behind her. Ede, moving quiet as driftwood.

"You knew them," he said. Not a question.

"I grew up here."

"You told the officials you had no ties above Merrow."

"I told the officials a lot of things." She didn't look at him. "Why are you awake?"

For a long moment he didn't answer, and when he did, his voice was lower than she'd ever heard it, aimed more at the dark hills than at her.

"Because tomorrow we pass the place where the survey team stopped," he said. "And there are things about that stop I haven't told you."

The mist drifted between them. Upstream, faint beneath the rain-smell, the highlands rumbled—the monsoon front, arriving early, stacking its black weight against the sky.

Yara looked at him at last. "Then you'll tell me at first light," she said. "All of it. Or I turn this boat around, permit or no permit."

Ede held her gaze, and for the first time since Fort Merrow, the gaunt stranger looked afraid.
