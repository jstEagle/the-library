## 5. What the Instruments Measure

The gear was still where the survey team had left it, stacked on the gravel shelf above the drowned channel like a merchant's display no customer had come to buy.

Ede crouched over the crates in the gray light before dawn, and told himself he was only checking for damage. That was the story he'd give if anyone asked. The river had risen a hand's width overnight; the lowest crate sat in a skin of brown water, and it was only sensible to move it. Sensible men checked things. Sensible men did not kneel in the mud at first light with their heart going like a drum because they already knew what they would find.

He pried the lid off the first case anyway.

Inside, nested in wool, lay a theodolite of brass and black lacquer, its lens cap still fitted, its tripod straps still buckled. Beside it, a leather folio stamped with the colonial crest and a number: *Concession Survey 7 — Serapine Headwaters.* He had seen that number before, three weeks ago, inked on the manifest Voss had burned page by page in the cookfire while Ede stood watch and said nothing.

A river survey measured depth. It measured current, channel width, shoals, the fall of the water through the gorge — everything a pilot needed and nothing more. Ede had run enough government boats to know what a hydrographic kit looked like, and this was not one. There was no sounding lead in any of these cases. No flow meter. No log line coiled in its tub.

Instead there were staves for measuring ground. Plane tables for plotting parcels. A box of boundary markers, iron pins banded in red paint, each one numbered in a hand too neat to belong to anyone who had ever carried a load on his back.

They weren't mapping the river. They were mapping everything the river touched.

Ede sat back on his heels and let that settle into him the way cold water settles into a boot — slowly, then all at once. Concession boundaries. The word from Fort Merrow had been *survey*, soft as a pillow, and everyone aboard had heard *river*, because that was the only kind of survey the Serapine had ever known. But concessions were not about water. Concessions were about land, and land meant timber, and timber meant the villages on the stilts, the gardens on the terraces, the graves cut into the hillsides above the flood line. Land meant people who would have to be somewhere else when the axes came.

He thought of the girl in the stilt village two days downstream, the one who had called Yara by a name that made the whole boat go quiet — *Palm-of-the-Current* — and how Yara had looked away as if the name were a splinter working toward her heart. Those children were on these maps. Every one of them, reduced to an iron pin with a number.

"You're up early."

Ede closed the lid too fast. The latch caught his thumb and he bit down on the curse.

Yara stood at the edge of the gravel shelf with two tin mugs, steam rising off them, her hair tied back wet from washing. She looked at the crates, then at him, and her eyes did the thing they always did — read him the way she read the water, noting every eddy, every submerged rock, every place where the surface lied.

"Checking the gear," Ede said. "River's coming up."

"Mm." She handed him a mug anyway and crouched beside the cases, not opening them, just looking. "Funny thing about a river survey," she said. "I've been running cargo twelve years, and I've never once seen a river crew pack boundary stakes."

The word landed between them like a stone dropped in still water. Ede drank his tea so he wouldn't have to answer immediately. Above them, on the Kestrel, someone moved — Crane, up at dawn as always, walking the deck with his little notebook, cataloguing. Ede had watched him do it for three days now: the hours each crew member slept, who spoke to whom, how many times Ede glanced upstream. The inspector wrote small and never crossed anything out.

"They're not measuring the channel," Ede said finally. It cost less than he expected, saying part of it aloud. "There's no sounding equipment in any of these cases. It's parcel work. Ground survey." He turned the mug in his hands. "Concession boundaries."

Yara was quiet a long moment. Downstream the mist was lifting off the water in rags, and somewhere a fish hawk screamed once and gave up.

"I guessed," she said. "Day before yesterday, when I saw the plane tables. I wanted to be wrong." She looked at him sideways. "You didn't guess. You knew."

It wasn't a question, and lying to her suddenly seemed not only wrong but useless — she had spent twelve years learning what the river hid under its surface, and Ede suspected she could hear a half-truth the way she heard a change in the current, some deep drag against the keel that said *rock ahead.*

"Voss didn't vanish," Ede said.

The words came out flat, and once they were out there was no taking them back, so he kept going, giving her the shape of it without the weight. "He was the senior surveyor. Good man. Careful. When the orders came through — the real orders, the ones under the river charts — he argued. Wrote letters. Got nowhere. Then one night he took his pack and walked into the hills, and we woke to find the camp arranged like a shop window and no tracks going anywhere, because he was smart enough to walk in the stream for a mile."

"And you?"

"I ran." He made himself say it plainly. "I was his guide. When they sent word back to Fort Merrow that the team was lost, I let them believe it. Let them believe all of it." He looked at the river rather than at her. "I told myself it was self-preservation. A man alone in the hills can't argue with a government. But the truth is I didn't want to be the one who found him, either. Finding him meant choosing."

Yara set her mug down on a crate with a small deliberate click. "Choosing what?"

"Whether the map gets finished." He met her eyes at last. "Whoever signs those boundaries owns nine days from now. After the monsoon, after the permits expire, the concession company moves on whatever's been signed. Voss has the original field sheets — the true ones, showing where the villages actually stand. The company has copies with the lines drawn differently." He paused. "Forged differently."

Something moved behind Yara's face, quick as a fish under ice. Not surprise. Recognition. Ede understood, with a small cold shock, that she had already been doing this arithmetic herself — that she had looked at these maps of village land and thought of stilt houses and children who remembered her by another name, and hated herself for thinking it.

"You should have told me at Fort Merrow," she said quietly.

"Yes."

"You let me sign on to a lie."

"I let you sign on to the same lie everyone signed. It was safer for you not knowing." He heard how it sounded even as he said it, and winced. "That's not — I'm not proud of it."

"No," Yara said. "You wouldn't be." She picked up her tea again, and for a while the only sound was the river worrying at the gravel, patient as a debt collector. "Half-truths are worse than lies, you know. A lie you can catch and throw back. A half-truth you carry around inside you, and every day it weighs a little more, and every day you have to decide again whether to put it down." She looked at the crates. "How much more is there?"

And there it was — the rest of it, sitting in his chest like ballast. Voss alive. Voss starving somewhere above the gorge with the field sheets strapped to his back. Ede himself, guiding this expedition straight toward the man he'd sworn, in the coward's way, by silence, never to betray.

"There's more," Ede said. "But it isn't mine to give. Not yet."

Yara studied him for a long moment, and he braced for anger, for the cold withdrawal he'd seen her use on officials and merchants alike. Instead she nodded once, slow, as if he'd passed some test he hadn't known he was taking.

"Not yet," she agreed. "But soon, guide. Because Inspector Crane is counting us."

* * *

Crane counted them at breakfast.

Ede noticed it the way you notice weather changing — not one thing but a pressure, a shift in the air. The inspector sat at the stern rail with his tea untouched and his notebook open, and he asked questions that sounded idle and were not.

"Fourteen years on the Serapine, Mr. Ede?" Crane said. "Remarkable. And yet your name appears in none of the Fort Merrow ledgers before last spring. Almost as though you arrived from nowhere."

"Guides come and go," Ede said. "The river keeps the records."

"The river." Crane smiled thinly and wrote something. His pencil moved in short strokes, precise as insect legs. "And you, Ms. Brightwater — your permit lists cargo runs between Merrow and the lower bends exclusively. Yet here you are, twelve years of local knowledge, volunteering for a headwater expedition the moment your permit was threatened. Loyalty or fear?"

"Arithmetic," Yara said, unbothered, mending a net with quick fingers. "Same as yours, Inspector."

Crane's smile thinned. He turned the page. "Your junior officer, Lieutenant Sarel, tells me he found a slashed fuel line two nights ago. Cut clean, professional. Curious, in country where the nearest saboteur would have to walk four days through leech country to reach us." He let that sit. "Curiouser still that whoever cut it knew exactly which boat in the convoy to choose."

The Kestrel. Their boat. Ede kept his face still and his tea level and felt the half-truths in his chest shift and grind against each other like stones in a flood. Someone aboard was cutting lines. Someone aboard was writing in a little book. And somewhere above the ninth bend, a starving man walked with the fate of nine villages on his back, waiting to learn whether the people coming up the river were rescuers or hunters.

Crane snapped the notebook shut. "We make the gorge tomorrow," he said pleasantly. "I do hope everyone sleeps well tonight. We'll want clear heads." He paused at the companionway, and his eyes went from Ede to Yara to the crates of instruments lashed on deck, and lingered there a moment too long.

"Oh — and Ms. Brightwater. Do keep your crew away from the survey gear. Some of those documents are sensitive." Another thin smile. "Boundary matters. You understand."

He went below. The net-mending stopped. Yara stared at the closed hatch, and Ede saw her knuckles whiten around the shuttle, and understood that whatever she had decided about half-truths, the time for carrying them was running out faster than the river was rising.

Above the gorge, far off, thunder rolled down out of the highlands — long, low, and getting closer.
