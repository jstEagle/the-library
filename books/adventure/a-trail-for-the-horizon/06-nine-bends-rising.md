## 6. Nine Bends Rising

The gorge announced itself before they saw it—a long gray seam in the highlands where the Serapine forgot how to be gentle. Yara stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* and watched the walls close in, limestone rising on either side like the ribs of something drowned, and felt the old arithmetic begin in her blood: current against hull, bend against bend, the monsoon front stacking black behind them like a debt come due.

"Nine bends," Ede said beside her. He'd said it twice already that morning, as if repetition could make the river kinder.

"It's eight if you cheat the third one right." She didn't look at him. "You guided a survey team through here. You should know."

"I guided them to the mouth of it. They went up with their instruments and their certainty." His jaw worked. "I waited at the lower camp. When they didn't come down—"

"You ran."

"When I saw what was coming down instead." He said nothing more, and she let him keep his silence. She'd learned on this river that people told you everything eventually, if you gave them enough water between questions.

Behind her, Inspector Crane stood at the rail with his ledger open against the wind, cataloguing. He'd been cataloguing for two days—the crew's names, the fuel gauge, the way Ede flinched at certain bends. Junior Officer Pell hovered at his shoulder, young and eager and too clean for this river, his boots still shining in a place where boots should have died of shame days ago.

"How far to the last known position?" Crane called over the engine noise.

"Half a day," Yara lied, because it was a quarter day, and she wanted the extra hours. "If the water lets us."

The water did not intend to let them anything. The Serapine had risen a hand's width overnight, brown now instead of green, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks. The *Kestrel* took the first bend wallowing, her patched seams weeping at the seams' own private pace, and Yara drove her anyway, because the permit deadline was nine days out and the monsoon would arrive in three, and between those numbers lay every choice she had left.

* * *

They found the trail at the fifth bend, in a slot of gravel beach where the cliff stepped back just wide enough for a camp.

Yara brought the boat in against the eddy line and stood a long time before she stepped off onto the stones.

Instruments. Arranged in rows.

Tripods nested by size. Theodolites boxed and squared, lids closed, latches fastened. A stack of survey notebooks bound with cord, weighted under a stone against the wind. And around it all, a swept circle in the gravel—deliberate, almost ceremonial—as though someone had tidied the evidence of their own vanishing the way a housekeeper straightens a room before leaving forever.

"No tracks going out," Pell said, circling the perimeter. "I've looked twice."

"There are tracks." Ede crouched at the edge of the swept zone, and his voice had gone strange. "One set. Going up." He pointed at the talus slope where the cliff began its climb into cloud forest. "Worn boots, left foot dragging. That's Voss. He walked up there eleven days ago."

"You knew his gait?" Crane's voice came down soft and cold as river fog.

"I carried his packs for six weeks." Ede rose slowly, and Yara watched him decide something—watched the scales tip behind his eyes. "Inspector, those aren't channel surveys." He crossed to the instrument crates, pried one lid, and turned it so the wind could take the papers inside. Sheets fanned out, and Crane caught one, and Yara watched the inspector's face do something complicated.

She caught a sheet herself as it skidded past. Contour lines. But not of the river—of the valley floor above the floodline. Village land. Stilt-village land, drawn in careful ink with boundary stakes marked at intervals, and across the top, in a clerk's precise hand: *Concession Block Seven — Provisional.*

"They weren't mapping the river," she said. The words tasted like copper. "They were mapping who lives on it."

Crane read the sheet twice. His thumb pressed white against the paper's edge. "This is provisional documentation," he said at last, in the voice of a man reading scripture he'd been taught all his life and only now hearing aloud. "It requires a senior surveyor's signature to be actionable."

"And Voss wouldn't sign it," Ede said. "That's why he's up that slope instead of in a report."

The wind shifted. Somewhere above the clouds, thunder cleared its throat.

* * *

They climbed.

Yara left Pell and the deckhand with the *Kestrel*, orders simple: pump every hour, watch the gauge, don't argue with the river. Then she led Crane and Ede up the talus on Voss's dragging track, through fern wet enough to wring out, the air thickening with rain that hadn't decided to fall yet.

They found him at midafternoon, in a hollow beneath an overhang where a spring trickled out of the rock.

He was worse than a ghost. Ghosts kept their weight. This man had burned himself down to sinew and purpose, cheekbones like keels, hands cracked around a oilskin packet he held the way a drowning man holds driftwood. He'd built a fire ring from stones and never lit it. Around the hollow, wedged into crevices, were more papers—duplicates, Yara realized. Copies. He'd copied everything before he ran.

"Voss." Ede went to his knees beside him. "You stubborn, magnificent fool."

"Ede." The surveyor's voice was a dry reed. "Did they send you, or did you come?"

"Neither. They sent her." Ede tilted his head toward Yara. "And him." A nod at Crane.

Voss looked up at Yara then, and there was no fear in it, only assessment, the look of a man measuring a hull for seaworthiness. "Brightwater. The cargo runner. They conscripted you."

"They threatened my permit."

"Mm." Almost a smile. "And what is a permit, when you've seen what it permits?" He lifted the oilskin packet with both hands, arms trembling. "Forged boundaries. Block Seven doesn't follow the watershed—it follows nothing. It was drawn in an office in the capital to maximize concession acreage, and my signature was the only thing standing between these villages and eviction. So I removed my signature and myself." He pushed the packet toward Crane. "Arrest me if you must. But the map goes to a magistrate, not a filing cabinet."

Crane didn't take it. He stood very still, rain beginning now in earnest, fat drops ticking off the stone. "Elias Voss, you are detained in the name of the Colonial Survey Authority—"

The mountain answered before anyone else could.

The sound came up the valley like a freight train derailing somewhere underground—a deep, grinding roar that Yara felt in her back teeth before her ears understood it. She'd heard it twice in twelve years. Both times, people downstream hadn't heard it at all.

"Flash flood." She was already moving. "The gorge. *Now.*"

They crashed downslope through streaming fern, Voss half-carried between Yara and Ede, Crane stumbling behind with the oilskin packet clutched to his chest because even now—even now—he would not let go of the paper. The rain had gone from drops to ropes. Below them, through the trees, Yara saw the gravel beach where the instruments sat in their tidy rows, and beyond it the *Kestrel*, small and brown and hers, and beyond *that*, the river coming down.

Not rising. *Arriving.* A brown wall of it, rolling through the fifth bend, swallowing the eddy line, the beach, the tripods, the swept circle, everything, in a single indifferent gulp.

"Pell!" she screamed, though the roar ate the name.

The *Kestrel* was moving. Someone aboard had cut the mooring—or the flood had done it for them—and the boat swung broadside into the current, wallowing, taking water, driven sideways down the gorge toward the strainer at the seventh bend where a fallen ironwood tree lay across half the channel, branches combing the flood like the fingers of something patient.

"We'll never—" Crane started.

"Get to the bank. High ground. *Go.*"

But Crane didn't go. That was the thing Yara would turn over for nights afterward, the fact she couldn't make sit flat: the inspector, who had catalogued her crew and threatened her livelihood and represented everything she'd spent twelve years keeping at oar's length, turned instead and ran along the bank, parallel to his own boat, shouting orders at it as if volume could steer.

The *Kestrel* hit the strainer's upstream reach and the ironwood's branches took her like a hand takes a moth.

Yara didn't remember deciding. Her body simply stopped obeying the part of her that had spent twelve years paying everyone and owing no one. She went down the bank on her heels and palms, hit the flood shallows, and swam—not for the boat, the boat was already dying—but for the figure clinging to the rigging where the stern had swung against the branches. Pell. The junior officer, the too-clean boots, the boy bought by the concession company or merely terrified, hanging on while the river tried to fold the *Kestrel* around the ironwood like a letter.

She got an arm through the shrouds. Got her other arm around Pell's chest. The boat groaned, rolled, and gave them both to the water.

The flood did what floods do. It was not malicious; it was merely enormous. It tumbled them, spat them, dragged them along the strainer's downstream face where the eddy finally, grudgingly, let go. Yara surfaced coughing brown water, one fist locked in Pell's collar, and hauled them both onto a gravel spit below the seventh bend where Crane stood ankle-deep, having waded in to his own ruin without hesitation.

For a moment nobody spoke. The river roared past with the *Kestrel*'s mast visible for one last second, snagged, tilting, then gone under or gone downstream—it hardly mattered which.

Pell vomited water and wept. Crane knelt in the gravel, soaked through, the oilskin packet somehow still clutched against his ribs like a heartbeat, staring at the empty channel where his authority and her boat had both just been swallowed.

Yara stood over him, shaking—with cold, with rage, with something she refused to name—and discovered that her hands had already pulled him partway up before her mind issued any order.

"I had every reason," she said quietly, "to let the river have you."

Crane looked up at her. Water ran off his chin. Whatever arithmetic lived behind his eyes was running new numbers now; she could see the columns shifting.

"Yes," he said. "I know." He rose, and pressed the oilskin packet—not to his own chest, but into her hands. "Then hold this. Because I find I no longer trust myself with it."

Above them, through the rain, Voss's voice drifted thin from the high ground: "The magistrate sits at the confluence. Nine days."

Yara closed her fingers around the map that owned the fate of everyone she'd ever pretended not to love, and looked downriver at the brown water where twelve years of miles had just gone under—and understood that the river had finally asked her the question she'd been fleeing since the betrayal she refused to name.

Whose side was she on?

Her hands, at least, had already answered.
