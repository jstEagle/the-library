## 7. The Price Named Aloud

The fire was small because small fires could be denied.

Voss crouched beside it on the shelf of granite above the gorge, feeding it twigs one at a time, the way he'd learned in his first month on the Serapine—before he'd learned that everything on this river could be counted, measured, and taken. Below him the flood water ran brown and enormous, hauling whole trees in its current like a man dragging furniture out of a house he'd lost. Somewhere down that churning throat, the Kestrel was wedged against a gravel bar, half-swamped, her crew scattered along the high water line like seeds thrown from a split pod.

He had watched the flash flood take the gorge apart. Had heard it first, actually—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat—and had time to climb forty feet before the wall of water came through the narrows and erased the river he'd known for six weeks. The survey team's instruments were somewhere under that. Good. Let the river have them. Let it keep what it wanted.

The woman came up the goat track with the stranger behind her, both of them soaked to the ribs, and Voss saw at once that she was the one he'd been waiting for. Not Crane, not the crew, not the magistrate's men who would come when the water dropped. Her. The boatwoman. He'd heard about her even in the survey camps—Yara Brightwater, who ran cargo where the company pilots wouldn't, who paid cash and asked no names. He'd heard she'd once lived upriver, in the stilt villages, before whatever it was that made her keep the world at oar's length.

She stopped at the edge of the firelight and looked at him the way she'd probably looked at the gorge: measuring, unimpressed, deciding whether he was worth the water it would take to drown him.

"You're Voss," she said.

"I'm Voss."

"The man who vanished."

"The man who left," he said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole reason you're standing here."

* * *

They got a tarp strung between two leaning slabs of granite, and the stranger—Ede, silent as a heron—brought Voss's pack down from the crack in the rock where he'd cached it six days ago, when the flood first threatened. Voss watched the woman's eyes track the pack the way a cargo master's eyes track weight. She knew what a pack like that meant. Documents. The whole reason he'd starved himself thin on this shelf of stone, eating river mussels and stolen yams, guarding a leather satchel like a dragon on a hoard of paper.

Crane came last, climbing with the careful dignity of a man whose authority had been washed downstream along with his hat. The inspector was gray-faced, wrung out, and something had changed in him since the water. Voss had watched from above as Yara Brightwater hauled him out of the current by his collar, against every instinct she owned—you could see the argument happening in her body even at a distance, the arm that wanted to let go, the arm that didn't. A man didn't forget being saved by someone who had every reason to hate him.

"Sit," Voss said, and to Voss's mild surprise, Crane sat.

The rain came back, a fine persistent drizzle that hissed on the coals. Voss opened the satchel.

"I was the boundary surveyor," he said. "Not the channel surveyor. That's what the team thought they were doing—mapping depths, marking shoals, the honest work of making the river navigable. I was sent separately, with a separate set of instructions, and I was told to keep them separate." He drew out a rolled map, oilcloth-wrapped, and set it on the granite between them without unrolling it. "These are the concession boundaries. The land grants. Every stilt village from the Gorge of Nine Bends to the delta falls inside them. The river communities aren't being surveyed. They're being inventoried."

Yara had gone very still. It was the stillness of a woman who had already suspected and was now being handed the proof, and Voss watched her decide not to look surprised.

"Eviction," she said.

"Displacement," Crane said automatically, and then, hearing himself, closed his eyes. "Eviction," he corrected. "Yes."

"The permits expire in nine days," Voss said. "That's the deadline that matters—not the monsoon, not the convoy. Nine days from the first of the month, every concession signature becomes void unless the boundary map is filed with the land office at Fort Merrow. Whoever files it owns the future of forty villages. Whoever destroys it owns nothing, and neither does the company." He looked around the fire, at each of them in turn. "Right now the only signed copy in existence is under this tarp. I am the only man alive who knows where every line on it was forged."

The rain ticked on the tarp. Below them the flood kept up its long brown roar.

"You forged the lines," Yara said. Not a question.

"I drew them where I was told to draw them," Voss said. "Through the village commons at Seru Bend. Through the burial ground at Tolo. I drew them in a government office in the capital, from a desk chair, and then I came here to walk the ground and make the forgery look like fieldwork." His voice didn't shake, though he felt the old shame rise in him like water in a bilge. "And then I walked the ground. I sat in the stilt houses. I ate from the cooking fires I was measuring for eviction. And I found I couldn't sign it. So I walked into the hills and let them think the river took me."

"You let a rescue team come looking," Crane said. "You let us nearly die."

"I let the clock run," Voss said. "That's all I could do. Every day the team searched was a day the map wasn't filed." He turned the oilcloth package over in his hands, once, twice, like a man testing the weight of a coin he intended to spend. "But the clock runs out either way. Nine days. And I'm half-starved, and the company will send better men than Crane here, and I can't outrun them on these legs."

He held the package out.

Not to Crane. Not to Ede.

To Yara Brightwater.

* * *

She didn't take it.

That was the first thing Voss understood about her: she didn't take things. She paid, she carried, she delivered, but she didn't take—because taking created a debt, and debts were chains, and Yara Brightwater had spent twelve years burning every chain she could find.

"What is this," she said quietly. "Exactly."

"Power," Voss said. "That's all it is. That's all it's ever been—a piece of paper that decides who lives where. And I'm telling you it can be handed off. That's the part they never say out loud. Power isn't a throne. It's a parcel. It has a weight and a shape and it can be carried by whoever's willing to carry it." His arm was trembling with the effort of holding it out, and he didn't lower it. "I can't carry it anymore. I'm done. I've been done since the day I sat in a cooking fire's light and wrote the word 'vacant' next to a village with forty children in it."

"Give it to the magistrate," Yara said. "Give it to Crane. That's the lawful—"

"The lawful path files it at Fort Merrow," Voss said, "where the company's solicitor has an office, and where documents have been known to go into a drawer and come out amended. You know this. You've run cargo past that fort for twelve years. You know exactly how paper behaves in that building."

Crane flinched. Voss watched him flinch, and watched the inspector look down at the fire, and understood that the flinch was not denial. It was arithmetic. Voss had seen that look on a hundred officials—the silent tallying of career against conscience, promotion against the faces of people they'd never have to see again. Usually the arithmetic came out one way. But Crane had been pulled from a flood by a woman he'd conscripted, and men who'd been saved against someone's instinct carried that like a stone in the boot.

"The company bought one of my own junior officers," Crane said at last, to the fire. "Sabotage on my own boat. My own crew. That's how deep this goes." He looked up. "If I take that map, it goes into an evidence lockbox at Fort Merrow, and I would be lying to you if I said I was certain what came out of that lockbox."

"There," Voss said softly. "You see. Even honesty has a chain of custody."

Ede spoke for the first time, from the edge of the firelight, his voice like gravel shifted by water. "The villages trust her."

All three men looked at him. He shrugged, elaborately indifferent, and went back to watching the dark.

"They don't trust me," Yara said. "I left."

"They remember you," Ede said. "The children at the stilt village had a name for you. That's not nothing, on this river."

Voss watched her face in the coal-light and saw the thing she'd been hiding from all of them, the thing she'd been rowing away from for twelve years, rise up like a fish breaking the surface. She wanted to belong. She had made an art form of not belonging, and underneath the art was the want, whole and unhealed.

"I'm a cargo runner," she said finally. "I move things. I don't decide what things mean."

"Then decide this one thing," Voss said. "The map is not cargo. Cargo is what other people want moved. This—" he shook the package gently, "—this is a choice with a weight. And I am telling you, woman of the Serapine, that I would rather put it in your hands than any office in the colony. Because you're the only person in this whole expedition who has nothing to gain from it. And that makes you the only person it's safe with."

* * *

She took it.

Not quickly. She looked at it for a long time, the rain dripping off her jaw, the flood roaring its one note below them, and Voss had the sense that she was reading the package the way she read water—looking for the hidden rock, the reversal current, the place where the surface lied. Then she reached out and took it, and Voss felt something leave him that he'd been carrying so long he'd mistaken it for his own spine.

"What do I do with it," she said. It wasn't a question either. It was a woman accepting a cargo and asking for the manifest.

"Nine days," Voss said. "The convoy will come up from the delta when the water drops, with a replacement surveyor and a replacement map—there's a blank copy in the company's Fort Merrow office, and they'll fill in the lines themselves if they have to. They'll run it upriver and file it before the deadline. The only way the villages survive is if the true map reaches the magistrate first—or if the convoy never reaches the filing office at all." He paused. "The Kestrel is the fastest thing on this river. Was. Is she still afloat?"

Yara's jaw tightened. "She's afloat."

"Then you have a boat, a deadline, and the only honest lines ever drawn on this river." Voss leaned back against the granite, suddenly, hugely tired, the hunger and the six weeks of vigil landing on him all at once. "Do with it what you'd want done if it were your village."

"It was my village," Yara said, very quietly, and Voss pretended not to hear, and the rain came down, and the fire burned small and deniable, and nine days began.

Below them, far down the drowned gorge, a lantern light moved against the current—slow, patient, climbing.

The convoy wasn't waiting for the water to drop after all.
