## 8. Crane's Arithmetic

Inspector Crane had kept ledgers since he was nineteen years old, and he trusted them the way other men trusted scripture. Every debt balanced. Every entry accounted for. He had built a career on the conviction that if you wrote things down honestly enough—hours worked, miles surveyed, men fed and paid and disciplined—the world would resolve itself into figures that could be defended before any tribunal in the capital.

The Kestrel's aft cabin smelled of bilgewater and lamp oil, and the ledger open on his knee refused to balance.

He sat with his back against the hull, which still wept through its patched seam at a rate he had measured twice: one inch of water per hour against the pumps' capacity to remove three. The boat would hold. That much arithmetic was sound. It was everything else that had gone crooked since the gorge.

Through the bulkhead he could hear Yara Brightwater talking to her engine in a low voice, half curse, half lullaby, as though it were a mule she'd owned longer than most marriages. Voss slept in the forward hold under a tarpaulin, wrists unbound because Crane had run out of justifications for binding them. And Ede sat on the cargo hatch outside, sharpening a knife with the patience of a man who had nowhere else in the world to be.

Crane dipped his pen and wrote:

*Assets recovered: one surveyor (Voss, R.), one field satchel containing boundary maps, one instrument case (damaged).*

Then he stopped.

Because the next line—the line he had been avoiding for two days, through the flash flood, through the rescue he still did not fully understand himself performing, through the long cold night on high ground while Voss talked and talked until the words got inside him like river water into a hull—was this:

*Liability: the truth.*

He had read the map. That was the trouble. A man could plead ignorance up to a certain point, and Crane had lived his whole career carefully below that point, signing what he was told to sign, surveying what he was told to survey, telling himself that the paperwork was clean even when the intentions behind it were not. But he had held Voss's map by lamplight in the flooded gorge camp, and he had seen the boundary lines drawn where no survey team had ever planted a stake. Lines that ran through the stilt village's fishing grounds. Lines that ran through ground where children had waved at their boat from the reeds. Lines backdated, initialed, certified—by his own office's stamp, or a forgery so good it might as well have been.

The concession company wasn't mapping the river. They were manufacturing a legal wilderness, emptying it of people on paper so they could empty it in fact.

And in nine days, if that map reached the magistrate's desk with the permit signatures intact, it would become true.

Crane closed the ledger.

* * *

The junior officer's name was Pell. Twenty-three years old, seconded from the Merrow garrison, competent with instruments and eager in the way that young men are eager when they believe the empire is a ladder rather than a machine. Crane had recommended him personally. That detail sat in Crane's chest like a swallowed stone.

It was Ede who found it—not the sabotage itself, but the thread leading back from it. The slashed fuel line had been patched on the third day out; the cut seacock handle had been discovered wedged under a coil of rope where someone had hidden it rather than thrown it overboard, which was either carelessness or conscience. But on the morning after the flood, while the others hauled the Kestrel off the gravel bar, Ede crouched at the stern and said nothing at all, which from Ede was a shout.

He showed Crane the spare fuel canister. Someone had drilled it. Slow leak, positioned to drain into the bilge where the rising waterline would mask it—a failure timed not to strand them but to delay them past the monsoon, past the permit deadline, past any chance of reaching the confluence with evidence intact.

"Who handled the canister last?" Crane asked, though he already knew.

Ede looked at him with those flat grey eyes and said, "You know."

Pell had volunteered for fuel duty every single day. Pell had insisted on taking the night watch during the gorge transit, alone at the stern, "to let the guide rest." Pell had written letters home from Fort Merrow that Crane had seen him seal with wax he did not recognize—expensive wax, dark red, stamped with a crest Crane now understood belonged not to the Survey Office but to the Serapine Concession Company.

They confronted him at dusk, on the bank, with the fire between them. Or rather, Crane confronted him; Ede simply stood at the edge of the firelight with his knife sheathed and his arms crossed, which proved sufficient.

Pell didn't deny it. That was the worst part. He stood up straight, chin lifted, and said, "They offered me four hundred crowns and a posting in the capital. Four hundred, sir. You've been an inspector eleven years and you don't make four hundred in *three years*. I've seen your ledger. I've seen what honesty pays."

The words went in like a splinter driven deep. Crane thought of the small house in Merrow he'd never bought. The promotion he'd been promised twice and given once, and then only after the man above him died. Eleven years of entries in columns that always balanced, and here was a boy of twenty-three reading the sum aloud: *honesty pays nothing.*

"You delayed us," Crane said quietly. "If the flood had caught us mid-gorge instead of at the bar, we'd all be dead. Did they tell you that? Did they price our lives into your four hundred crowns?"

Pell's face flickered—the first crack. "They said the river would decide. They said—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They said everyone on this boat was expendable except the map, and the map was already copied anyway."

That last part was a lie. Crane knew it was a lie because he had watched Voss burn the duplicate charts in the gorge camp, page by page, feeding the concession's future into the flames while the flood roared past. There was exactly one map now, and it was wrapped in oilcloth inside Yara Brightwater's cabin, entrusted—God help him—to a smuggler's leaking boat.

But he saw what the lie was for. Pell believed it, or needed to believe it, because if the map was already copied then none of this mattered, and if none of this mattered then he was not a man who had sold his colleagues' lives for four hundred crowns.

Crane disarmed him without ceremony. Took his sidearm, his dispatch case, and the red-wax seal from his kit. Then he sat the boy down by the fire and did not tie him, because tying him would make it official, and some part of Crane was not yet ready for anything aboard this boat to be official.

"I should arrest you," Crane said.

"Yes, sir."

"If I arrest you, the concession company knows by week's end. They'll have a new man on the convoy before we reach the confluence, and the map will be worthless, and the villages will go." He heard himself say it plainly, without rhetoric, and was surprised at how easily the truth came once he stopped dressing it as duty. "So instead you're going to sit on this bank and think about what four hundred crowns buys. And when we reach the confluence, you're going to stand where I can see you."

Pell looked at him with something worse than hatred—gratitude, half-formed and shameful—and Crane turned away before it could settle on him.

* * *

That night he climbed to the bow, where Yara stood her watch, one hand on the rail, the river sliding black beneath them. The storm front had passed but its aftermath hung in the air, a wet heat, the stars smeared behind thin cloud.

"The boy," she said, without turning. "What happens to him?"

"He comes with us. Under my eye."

"And the map?"

"That," Crane said, "is the question I've been trying not to ask myself for two days."

She turned then. In the dark her face was mostly shadow, but her eyes caught the lamplight from the cabin, and there was nothing soft in them. She had spent the whole voyage keeping him at oar's length, and he understood why now—or thought he did. People who had been betrayed learned to count everything, same as he did. Same as Pell did, in his way. Everyone aboard this boat was doing arithmetic.

"Let me save you the trouble," Yara said. "Deliver Voss and the map, get your promotion, take your share of the fees. That's the honest version, isn't it? The one that balances."

"It's the version I'm paid for."

"You're paid to inspect. Not to drown villages." She said it without heat, which made it worse. "I've read your file, Inspector. It came through this river with the mail boats for twelve years. Clean hands. Clean columns. You want to know what I learned running cargo? The water doesn't care about your ledger. It takes whoever's standing in the wrong place when the flood comes. Right now, those villages are standing in the wrong place, and your signature is the flood."

He should have been angry. Instead he felt the strange lightness of a man whose books have finally been opened by someone else and found, at last, honestly kept.

"There's another entry," Crane said. "One I haven't written down yet."

"Which is?"

"That I pulled you out of the flood's path because it was right, not because it was ordered. That I've known since the gorge what that map is. And that somewhere between the strainer and this rail, I decided which column I'd rather be buried in." He looked down at the black water. "I'm forty-six years old, Brightwater. I don't have time left to be on the wrong side slowly."

Yara studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the smallest motion, and it felt like being weighed and found worth carrying.

"The convoy will be at the confluence," she said. "Waiting for the deadline. When do they expect you?"

"At the expiry. Nine days minus five. Four days from now."

"Then we have four days to decide how a leaking cargo boat arrives at a armed convoy carrying a dead man's evidence and a defected inspector." Her mouth curved, dry as driftwood. "I've smuggled worse cargoes than justice."

She went below, and Crane stayed at the rail, and after a while he took out his ledger and opened it to the fresh page and wrote, in his careful clerk's hand, the first honest entry of his career:

*Concession boundaries: forged. Villages: innocent. Course of action: mine.*

He looked at the words a long time. Then, beneath them, smaller:

*Whatever it costs.*

Ahead, far down the darkening channel, a lantern blinked twice from a point of land—the confluence signal, early, impatient. The convoy was already gathering. And in the hold of the Kestrel, wrapped in oilcloth, the fate of nine villages waited on what a corrupt inspector, a smuggler, a defector, and a silent stranger would dare to do in four days.

Crane closed the book and began, quietly, to plan.
