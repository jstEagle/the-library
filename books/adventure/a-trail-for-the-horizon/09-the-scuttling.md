## 9. The Scuttling

The confluence announced itself before Yara saw it: a widening of the brown water, a slackening of the current where the Serapine met the Ossuary Fork, and there—anchored in the broad water like a fist of iron—was the convoy.

Three flat-bottomed steam barges, gunwales stacked with chain and timber and the gray crates of a company that intended to own the river's edges. A fourth vessel rode higher at the rear, white-canopied, flying the concession colors: a black heron on gold. Survey crews moved along the decks with the unhurried confidence of men who had already been paid. On the far bank, a tent city had bloomed overnight—stakes, flags, a surveyor's tripod already planted in mud that belonged to the Ferrow people.

Yara cut the Kestrel's engine and let her drift.

Behind her in the cockpit, Voss sat wrapped in Ede's spare coat, his hands still shaking from three days in the hills. The boundary map lay between them in its oilcloth sleeve, and Crane stood at the rail with his hands clasped behind his back like a man rehearsing a speech he hadn't decided to give yet.

"Nine days," Voss said quietly. "That's what I told you. Today is day nine."

"I can count," Yara said.

She could count other things too. She could count the years of miles—the Kestrel had carried them all, every leaking league of them, from the salt mouth to the high shallows where even the traders wouldn't follow. Twelve years of diesel and monsoon and the particular loneliness of a woman who paid everyone and owed no one. The boat was dying; she'd known it since the gorge, when the pumps ran longer than the engines. But knowing a thing and doing it were two different rivers.

"Crane." She didn't turn around. "How long until the magistrate's launch comes up from Merrow?"

"Tomorrow morning. Maybe midday if the fog holds." Crane's voice was careful. "The permit expires at sundown tonight. After that, the concession company doesn't need a legal boundary—they'll simply *have* one. The convoy moves upstream at first light regardless."

"And the evidence?"

"The magistrate will want it authenticated. Signatures, seals, my testimony as an inspector of record." He paused. "And yours."

"Mine?"

"You're the master of the vessel that found them. Your logbook is admissible. Your word carries weight on this river—more than mine does, frankly. Everyone from Fort Merrow to the salt mouth knows Yara Brightwater doesn't lie about cargo or currents."

She almost laughed. Twelve years of keeping the world at oar's length, and now the world wanted her name on paper.

* * *

They came alongside under the pretense of surrender.

It had been Crane's plan, argued out in whispers while the Kestrel drifted in the reeds downstream: deliver Voss and the map openly, in daylight, so that no one could later claim the inspector had conspired with fugitives. Let the convoy believe they'd come to bargain. Concession men understood bargaining. It was the only language they trusted.

So Yara steered the wounded Kestrel into the confluence with her flag up and her hands steady, and the lead barge's captain—a thick-necked man named Harrow whose shirt bore the black heron—leaned over his rail and grinned down at them like they were fish coming to the hook.

"Brightwater." He said her name the way officials always did, tasting it for leverage. "We heard your boat was conscripted. We heard you lost half your crew in the gorge." His eyes went to Voss, huddled and hollow-eyed, and something satisfied moved through his face. "And we heard you found what went missing. That's good news for everyone. Hand him up, hand over whatever he's carrying, and there'll be a fee in it for you. The company pays its debts."

"The company pays its debts," Yara repeated.

"It does."

"Then it owes this river eleven villages it never mentioned in any filing I've seen."

Harrow's grin thinned by a degree. "Ma'am, the concession is a matter of public record. Boundaries, easements, compensation schedules—all of it lawful."

"Show me the map."

He hesitated. Just a heartbeat, but she'd spent twelve years reading hesitation the way she read water, and she saw the snag beneath the surface. "The survey maps are proprietary."

"Then here's mine." She nodded to Ede, who passed the oilcloth sleeve up to the deck of the barge. "Voss's original field sheets. Compare them against whatever your proprietary version says about the Ferrow lands above the gorge."

Harrow took the sleeve without opening it. He didn't need to open it. Yara watched the knowledge move behind his eyes like a shadow under a bridge—the knowledge that the forgery was real, that the man who'd made it was standing right there, and that none of it mattered because the permit died at sundown and paper was only as strong as the men willing to enforce it.

"Appreciated," Harrow said, tucking the sleeve inside his coat. "You're free to go, Brightwater. Fee'll be wired to Merrow."

"We'll wait for the magistrate," Crane said loudly, from the rail. "As inspector of record, I have testimony to lodge, and I prefer to lodge it in front of the authority rather than the interested party."

Something changed on the barge then—not dramatically, nothing so honest as a drawn weapon, just a rearrangement. Crews shifted. Two men who'd been coiling rope stopped coiling rope. A boy near the stern crane walked away from it quickly, the way boys walk when they've been given an instruction they don't like.

Ede appeared at Yara's shoulder. He spoke without moving his lips, the way he said everything important. "They're not going to wait for the magistrate."

"No," Yara agreed. "They're going to weigh anchor at dusk and be forty miles upstream before anyone official wakes up. And the map in Harrow's coat is going to fall overboard somewhere convenient."

"So what do we do?"

Yara looked at the Kestrel's deck beneath her boots. At the scarred planking, the patched rail, the pump handle worn smooth by her own grip across twelve seasons of taking on water. The boat was listing already, two degrees to port, holding her secrets in her bilge the way she held everything else.

*Held everything else.*

An idea arrived the way floodwater arrives—not gradually, but all at once, having traveled a long way underground.

"Ede," she said. "How much chain do we still have in the hold?"

* * *

Dusk came down purple and slow, and the convoy began to stir.

Yara heard it before she saw it: the cough of the lead barge's boiler, the rattle of anchor chain being shortened, Harrow's voice carrying flat across the water with orders in it. The Kestrel lay moored thirty yards off the barge's stern, riding light, riding wrong, riding like a boat that had given up caring which way she leaned.

Inside her hull, Yara knelt at the seacock.

Every boat has them—the valves below the waterline, the small brass throats through which the river would pour if ever invited. She'd replaced them herself six years ago, on a beach above the delta, swearing at the threads and paying a boy in cigarettes to hold the lantern. Good valves. Honest valves. They had kept twelve years of miles dry.

Her hands were not shaking. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake.

"You don't have to," Ede said from the companionway. He'd insisted on staying aboard. Crane and Voss were already gone, slipped over the side into the skiff an hour ago, rowing hard for the far channel where the magistrate's launch would make its smoke by morning. This part—the last part—had never been theirs to share.

"Yes, I do." She found the first valve wheel. Cold brass. "Listen to me, Ede. When she goes down, she goes down across the channel. I've marked the shoal line—if she settles where I mean her to settle, nothing bigger than a canoe passes until the company brings salvage gear, and they won't bring salvage gear, because salvage gear means explaining to the magistrate why their convoy needed it. By the time they clear her, the permits are ash and the map is in a courtroom."

"And you?" Ede's voice was very quiet. "What happens to you?"

"I swim." She looked up at him—at the gaunt stranger who'd lied to her for a hundred miles and then, in the gorge, pulled Voss's half-drowned weight through floodwater without being asked. "I've been swimming away from things for twelve years. I'm due one swim toward something."

Above them, the lead barge's boiler built pressure. Somewhere on the white-canopied stern-wheeler, a bell rang twice—getting underway.

Yara turned the valve.

The sound was smaller than she expected. A whisper of water finding its level, a cold kiss against her boot soles, the river beginning its patient work. She moved to the second seacock, then the third, opening each one like a door she'd kept locked her whole adult life. The Kestrel shuddered once, settled deeper, and began—gently, almost gratefully—to drink.

"Take the skiff," Yara said. "Now. She'll go fast at the end."

Ede hesitated one moment longer. Then he gripped her forearm, hard, the way river people say goodbye instead of words, and dropped over the side into the bobbing skiff.

Yara stayed.

She stayed while the water climbed past her ankles, past her knees. She walked the deck one last time, touching things without naming them—the pump handle, the rail scar, the spot by the wheel where she'd slept ten thousand nights listening to the river breathe. In the cabin, her logbooks. She took only the current one. The rest could keep the boat company on the bottom; some records belonged to the water.

On the barge, someone had finally noticed. A shout went up—thin, confused, angry. A lantern swung toward her. Harrow's voice, cracking across the darkening water: *"Brightwater! What are you—she's scuttling! Stop her!"*

But there was nothing to stop. You cannot arrest a sinking. You cannot bribe a hull.

Yara put the Kestrel's wheel over hard and gave the dying engines their last order, and the old boat answered the way she'd always answered—imperfectly, loyally, all the way to the end. She drove herself across the channel with the river pouring through her own opened veins, and the shoal she'd chosen rose up to meet her keel, and the Kestrel grounded with a groan that Yara felt in her teeth and her chest and whatever lived beneath both.

Then she stepped off into the brown water and swam.

Behind her, the boat settled by degrees, listing across the channel like a felled tree, her mast dipping, her deck going under plank by plank. The convoy's searchlight found her and swept uselessly across the rising flood of her. Twelve years of miles went down into the Serapine without ceremony—no bell, no prayer, just the river closing over the place where a woman had kept herself safe and alone and afloat.

Yara reached the far bank and stood in the shallows, soaked to the ribs, watching the bubbles rise where her life used to be.

She was crying. She was laughing. Both were true, and neither was the whole truth, and somewhere in the middle of them was a feeling she had no name for except *free*.

Across the water, Harrow was screaming orders that no longer mattered. Upstream, invisible in the dark, the Ferrow villages slept in their stilt houses, unaware that the river had just grown a bone in its throat. And downstream, small and steady, a single lantern burned on the water—the magistrate's launch, early, coming up through the fog with Crane aboard and the truth in his coat pocket.

Yara wrung out her sleeves and started walking along the bank toward the light.

The river, for the first time in twelve years, was at her back—and it felt less like leaving than like arriving.
