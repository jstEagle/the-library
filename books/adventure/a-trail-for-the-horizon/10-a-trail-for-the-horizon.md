## 10. A Trail for the Horizon

The pilings went in on a morning so clear the river looked like hammered tin.

Yara stood waist-deep in the shallows with a maul in her hands, driving the last post while Old Sefa held it plumb and the children shouted advice from the bank that no one asked for. The wood was ironbark, cut from the ridge above the village, and each strike sent a shudder up her arms that felt almost like an apology. Twelve years she had driven oars through this water without once stopping to build anything in it. Now her shoulders burned with the good ache of making something that would stay.

"Again," Sefa said.

She swung. The post sank another hand's width.

"That's her," one of the children called — little Ami, who had appointed herself foreman of the entire operation at age seven. "That's the Brightwater stroke. My gran says you could hear her boat coming round the bend before you could see it."

*The Brightwater stroke.* Yara wiped sweat from her brow and let the name land where it landed. At Fort Merrow they had called her *the contractor*. In the cargo ledgers she was a permit number. Here, in this village of forty stilt houses leaning over brown water like herons over a fishhole, she was a nickname she'd buried so long ago she'd forgotten how much she missed the sound of it.

"One more," Sefa said.

She gave it everything. The post rang home, solid as bedrock, and the dock frame stood complete above the shallows — six planks wide, thirty long, strong enough to hold the market boats when the monsoon trade came back around. The first thing anyone had built on this stretch of river since the survey teams arrived with their tripods and their boundary stakes.

Since the concessions were voided, nothing had been torn down here. Everything was going up.

* * *

By midmorning the heat had weight to it, and Yara climbed onto the new planks to rest her arms and watch Voss fail to teach arithmetic.

He sat under the big tamarind at the village center with a slate balanced on his knee and six students arranged in a half-moon around him, ranging from Ami down to old Tam, who claimed he was learning letters so he could read the government notices himself instead of waiting for someone to lie about them. Voss had the patience of a man doing penance, which he was, though he never said so outright. He just kept drawing his careful columns of numbers and explaining them again, and again, in the flat precise voice of a surveyor who had spent his life measuring things and was now trying to measure something that wouldn't hold still.

"No," he said gently, for perhaps the ninth time. "If the field is four chains by three, the area is—"

"Twelve!" Ami shouted.

"Twelve what?"

"Twelve... fields?"

Voss closed his eyes briefly, the way he did when the river tested him, and Yara laughed out loud from the dock. He turned, found her watching, and something passed between them that needed no translation. She had delivered him to the magistrate herself, walking him down off the high ground with the forged maps wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and the magistrate had weighed those maps against the concession company's lawyers and found them heavier. Voss had walked out of that hearing a free man and a ruined one, and he had come straight back upriver, past Fort Merrow, past the gorge, all the way to the stilt village whose coordinates he had refused to sign away.

He'd told her once, on the walk down, why he'd staged the vanishing. *I stacked the instruments neatly because I wanted whoever came looking to understand that we left on purpose. Order means choice. A man who flees in panic leaves a mess.*

Now he taught children their letters in the shade of a tree that would have been inside Concession Block Seven if the map had held.

Tam raised a hand. "Surveyor. If a man owns a boat, and the boat sinks, does he still own the boat?"

Voss considered this with complete seriousness. "Legally, salvage rights are complicated."

"Emotionally," Tam said.

The children roared. Voss looked over at Yara again, helpless, and she shrugged with a grin: *welcome to the river, you get used to it.* But the question settled into her like silt into still water, because everyone in the village knew exactly whose boat had sunk, and why, and none of them had ever once asked her to tell the story. They had simply made room for her in it.

* * *

Ede was working on the Marrow's hull when she wandered down to the mudflats after the midday meal.

He had the old ferryboat hauled up on rollers, stripped to the ribs below the waterline, and he moved around her with the unhurried economy of a man who had finally stopped running. His shirt hung open in the heat; the scar along his ribs — the one he'd never explained, the one from whatever country he'd crossed to get here — caught the light. He didn't look up when her shadow fell across him.

"She'll float," he said. "Barely."

"The Marrow always barely floated. That's her personality."

A grunt that might have been agreement. Ede had maybe two hundred words a day in him, and he spent most of them on caulking. But in the weeks since the scuttling he had drifted to the village the way driftwood finds shore, and no one had asked him for papers or stories, and slowly the taciturn stranger had become the man who fixed things. He'd rehung three doors, rebuilt Sefa's smokehouse, and now he was bringing the Marrow back from the dead because the village needed a ferry and because, Yara suspected, hands that couldn't stop moving needed somewhere honest to go.

"You could do this," he said suddenly, not looking up. "Boat work. You'd be good at it."

"I did boat work for twelve years."

"You moved cargo." He drove a chisel under a rotten plank and levered it free with a crack. "This is different. This is keeping people on the water instead of carrying things past them."

Yara crouched at the edge of the hull and ran her thumb along the exposed rib, smooth and pale as bone. Below them the Serapine slid by, high and slow with the monsoon's leavings, carrying a raft of broken bamboo, a dead branch, a fishing net some upstream village had lost and mourned. It carried everything and kept nothing. For twelve years she had thought that was freedom — pay everyone, owe no one, belong nowhere, let the current take what it takes.

Then she had opened her own seacocks and watched the Kestrel go down across the channel with the survey convoy grinding to a halt behind her, and she had learned the difference between being carried and being held.

"Ede," she said. "When you guided Voss's team up the gorge. Before any of it. Did you know what the survey was for?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "I knew it wasn't a channel survey. The instruments were wrong for it. I didn't know what they were right for until it was too late to matter." He set the chisel down. "You're asking if I'd have told you sooner."

"I'm asking if you'd tell me now, if it happened again."

He looked at her then, direct, gaunt face unreadable as ever but the eyes steady. "There won't be an again. That's what the sinking bought." A pause. "But yes. Sooner. You read water better than anyone I've seen. People who read water that well shouldn't be lied to about where it goes."

It was the longest speech she'd ever gotten out of him. She let it stand, and they worked side by side the rest of the afternoon without another word, which between the two of them was its own kind of conversation.

* * *

Evening came up gold and loud. The village ate together on the new dock because of course they did — the first meal on the first new timber, rice and river fish and the sour green mangoes Ami stole from Tam's tree with Tam's full knowledge and public outrage. Someone brought out a battered concertina. Someone else brought out palm wine, and the palm wine brought out singing, and the singing was terrible and unending and Yara found herself laughing until her ribs hurt at a song about a crocodile who married a governor's daughter.

Sefa lowered himself onto the planks beside her, joints cracking like green firewood, and handed her a cup.

"My granddaughter says you're staying," the old woman said. Not a question. On the Serapine, nothing important was ever a question.

Yara watched the firelight on the water. "Your granddaughter is seven and runs this village already. Who am I to argue."

"There were permits," Sefa said. "Before. You had a boat and a permit and you went up and down and never stopped longer than a tide. We used to watch you pass. Twenty years, my mother watched you pass, and her mother before that." She sipped her wine. "We never called out to you. Do you know why?"

Because I paid my mooring fees and kept my distance and made it plain I wanted nothing, Yara thought. Because I built walls out of transactions.

"Because people who wave from a moving boat want to be waved at," Sefa said. "People who don't look up don't want to be seen. We let you have your not-looking." She patted Yara's knee with a hand like woven root. "Then you sank your own boat in front of the whole colony to keep our ground ours. That's a different kind of looking. That's looking like family."

Yara's throat tightened. She drank instead of answering, and Sefa, mercifully, let her.

Twelve years of miles. That was what the Kestrel had been — not a vessel but a measurement, a way of keeping score of her life in leagues and cargo tons and days without stopping. She missed the boat the way you miss a limb, a phantom ache at the end of every evening when the light went soft and some old habit whispered *cast off, cast off.* But habits quieted. And what filled the space they left was not emptiness. It was this: a dock she had built with her own hands, holding the weight of a whole village's supper. A nickname spoken without irony. A slate and a crooked school under a tamarind tree. A silent man passing her tools without being asked.

She measured in seasons now. Planting season, when the whole village turned out to the terraces and even Voss got a hoe put in his hands. High-water season, when Ede taught the young ones to read flood signs and Yara pretended not to hover. Dry season, when the sandbars emerged like secrets and the market boats came heavy with salt and cloth and news. The river kept its moods without her. It hadn't needed her to run its length to remain itself. Neither, it turned out, had she.

* * *

Late, when the fires had burned to coals and the concertina player had surrendered to sleep mid-verse, Yara walked the length of the new dock alone.

The night was enormous. Stars in their multitudes, the Milky Way spilled across the dark like flour from a torn sack, and beneath it the Serapine moved with its ancient patience, brown and broad and utterly indifferent to magistrates and concessions and the small stubborn creatures who loved it. Downstream, far downstream, Fort Merrow's lamps would be burning, and clerks would be filing the paperwork of a scandal, and somewhere a concession company's directors would be reading telegrams that ended careers. Upstream, the gorge of nine bends waited with its drowned instruments and its clean geometry, already becoming a story the boatmen told each other in the dry season, growing taller every telling.

Here, though. Here there was a dock, and a village, and morning coming.

Ami's voice floated down from the stilt houses, shrill with bedtime resistance: "—but Gran, the surveyor said the stars ARE numbers—"

"They are NOT numbers, child, they are lanterns, and if you don't sleep the lanterns will tell me—"

Yara smiled and leaned on the rail post she had driven that morning, feeling the ironbark solid under her palms, rooted in the riverbed, going nowhere.

That was when she saw the lights.

Far down the channel, where the black water met the blacker sky, a lantern swung — then two, then a cluster of them, low to the waterline, moving upstream against the current with the deliberate spacing of boats traveling in formation. Not market boats. Market boats traveled by day and sang while they rowed. These came silent, riding deep, and as her eyes adjusted she made out the shape of the lead vessel: long, low, government-built, with the unmistakable silhouette of a mounted swivel gun at the bow.

Behind it, more. Four. Five. Six hulls, strung out down the dark like beads on a wire, all of them headed for the village.

Yara stood very still on her own dock, in the place she had finally learned to call hers, and listened to the water carry the faint, rhythmic knock of many oars shipped in unison.

Then she turned and ran for the fires.
