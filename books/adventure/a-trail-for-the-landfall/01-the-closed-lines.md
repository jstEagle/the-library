## 1. The Closed Lines

The wilderness forgives nothing and forgets less. I have made my living off that fact for eleven years, and I have never once been able to decide whether that makes me a scavenger or a priest.

I was thinking about it that particular morning, walking the old Kestrel Spur at first light, because the frost had come early and hard, and frost is the wilderness's way of settling accounts. Everything that was going to fail had failed overnight. I could hear it in the way the trestle at Mile Three groaned when a crow landed on it—a sound like an old man clearing his throat before saying something he'd rather not say.

Let me explain my trade, since no one else has ever managed to explain it for me. When a railroad dies—and they die more often than the companies admit—the rails don't vanish. The ties don't vanish. The whole corpse of the thing stays where it fell, out past the last station, past the last fence post, into country where nobody with a salary goes. Somebody has to walk those corpses. Somebody has to say, with a straight face and a stamped document, *this line is dead, this bridge will not hold a handcar, and if you send men out here they will die in a manner that will cost you more in lawsuits than the salvage is worth.* That somebody is me. Edmund Joubert, surveyor of closed lines. There are perhaps six men in the territory who do it, and I am the only one who does it entirely off the books, which is a polite way of saying I take payment in ways a ledger can't follow.

It wasn't always the arrangement. But that's a story about my father's homestead and a bank with an appetite, and I find it goes down easier with coffee, so we'll come back to it.

The frost had done good work. At Mile One the ballast had heaved into little gray teeth along the old right-of-way, and the wire poles leaned at the angle of men listening to a joke they didn't quite catch. I catalogued as I went, because cataloguing is what I do instead of praying: two poles down, wire on the ground, salvageable copper if anyone cared—and no one had cared in nine years, which told you everything about the company that built this spur. It had been laid to serve a lumber camp, and when the lumber gave out, the company did what companies do. It looked away. The wilderness did not look away. The wilderness took notes.

Alder had come up through the roadbed in places, soft green whips no thicker than my thumb, and I made a note of that too, because alder is honest. Give it three more years and it would have the rails in its fist. Give it ten and a man walking this line would need a compass and a forgiving God.

The trestle at Mile Three was the reason I'd come, really. The rest was routine. But the Kestrel Creek crossing had been built in a hurry during the boom year—green timber, spike-lagged, no engineering to speak of—and I wanted to see what two hard freezes had done to it before the spring melt finished the argument. I stood on the abutment and looked at it the way you'd look at a sick animal you were deciding about.

It was dying. That wasn't the interesting part. The interesting part was *how* it was dying. The creek had shifted its channel—fifteen feet east, maybe more, sometime in the last few seasons—and it was now eating the east abutment from below, quietly, the way water does everything. The pilings on the west side stood in dry ground, holding up nothing, a bridge to a bridge. Somebody upstream had felled timber across the creek and the water, patient as arithmetic, had simply gone around.

I wrote it down. *Channel migrated east approx. 15 ft. West span stranded. Structure unsalvageable. Recommend no further inspection on foot.*

That last line was for the benefit of whatever clerk eventually read my reports, which is to say it was for nobody. I had already inspected it on foot. I always do. There is no other way to know a thing, and I have never trusted a conclusion I reached from the safe side of a creek.

I crossed it, as it happens. Carefully, testing each tie, keeping to the stringers where the frost hadn't gotten into the checks. Halfway over I stopped, because that's what the trestle asked of me, and I looked down at the water running brown and quick over its new bed, and I thought—this is the thought I was telling you about—*the wilderness forgives nothing and forgets less.* The company that built this bridge had forgotten it the day the last log train rolled off it. The creek had not forgotten. The creek remembered every spike they'd driven into its banks, every sawdust load they'd dumped, every year of neglect, and it was collecting, at its own rate, in its own currency, which is timber and gravel and eventually, if you let the account run long enough, men.

The railroad barons think they own the country because they drew lines across it. I've spent my life walking those lines, and I can tell you what actually owns the country: water, frost, and time, in that order, and none of them take drafts.

I finished the crossing, walked the last two miles to the spur's dead end—a rusted bumper, a snowed-in wye, a signpost with the station name rotted off it so that the place was now officially nowhere—and turned back. The sun was up by then, doing its best, laying long gold light down the right-of-way so the rails caught fire for a hundred yards at a stretch. It's the one honest beauty left in a dead line. The rails remember being important. For a few minutes every morning, the light reminds them.

I was a mile back along the grade, eating the heel of a loaf I'd baked myself—badly, but pride is a kind of seasoning—when I heard the horse.

That was the first wrong thing. Nobody rides the Kestrel Spur. The nearest ranch was six miles south, the nearest town eleven, and the only people who came out here were the occasional deer hunter, and they walked. A horse meant purpose, and purpose out here, on my lines, was generally aimed at me.

He came up the old roadbed at an easy trot: a dun mare, mud to the hocks, and on her a boy of maybe sixteen with a canvas mail satchel and the particular misery of a telegraph runner who has been told the address is *the man walking the dead line, you'll know him when you see him.* He saw me, and his shoulders did something complicated—relief and dread sharing the same small space.

"You Joubert?"

"Depends who's asking."

He didn't like that. To be fair to him, he was cold, and I was a stranger standing in the middle of a dead railroad eating bread. He fished a telegram out of the satchel and held it out at arm's length, the way you'd offer meat to a dog you weren't sure about.

"Answer's prepaid," he said. "There's a reply wire at the Junction office. They want it by four."

I took the envelope. It was good paper. That was the second wrong thing—telegrams to me came on the cheap yellow stock, from county engineers or salvage brokers, and they were spelled like the sender had been in a fight with the alphabet. This envelope was heavy cream stock, and my name on it was typed, and typed *correctly,* which almost never happens. *E. JOUBERT, SURVEYOR.* No first name. Somebody had done their homework on how I did my business, which meant somebody had asked around, which meant somebody had spent money before spending the money in the envelope.

The boy waited, because boys on horseback in the cold wait for nothing except being dismissed, and I dismissed him with a nod and he turned the mare and went back down the grade at a trot, and then it was just me and the frost and the rails and the telegram, and the rails catching fire in the morning light, and me standing there like a man who's found a wallet on a trail and knows better than to pick it up until he's looked at the country around it.

I opened it anyway. You knew I would. Curiosity is the one debt I've never managed to pay off.

*E. JOUBERT STOP COASTAL SURVEY REQUIRED STOP ROUTE ASSESSMENT ABANDONED RIGHT OF WAY NORTH OF GREYWATER STOP TRIPLE STANDARD RATE STOP EXPENSES ADVANCED STOP REPLY BY WIRE TO BOX 411 PORT HALLOWEN STOP NO FURTHER PARTICULARS BY WIRE STOP V.*

I read it twice. Three times. The mare's hoofprints were already frosting over behind me, filling in, the country erasing the boy's visit the way it erased everything, and I stood in the middle of my dead railroad and did arithmetic.

Triple rate. For a coastal survey. For a route *I never advertised for*—and that's the phrase that kept turning over, because here is the thing about my trade: I don't advertise. I have never advertised. The men who hire me hear about me the way you hear about a good horse thief, which is to say quietly, from someone who needed one once. So the advertisement wasn't the issue. The issue was that whoever sent this wire knew precisely what I was, knew how to reach me in country where I am very good at not being reached, and wanted a survey of abandoned coastal right-of-way north of Greywater—which is to say, drowned country, tide country, the kind of coast where the sea has been taking back right-of-ways since before there were right-of-ways to take.

Nobody surveys drowned coast for salvage. There's nothing to salvage that the sea hasn't already priced. You survey drowned coast for one of two reasons: you want to build something the sea will have to fight, or you want to know what the sea has uncovered.

And the signature. *V.* One letter, like a man so important his name is a formality. Important men don't send their own telegrams, which meant the *V* was a lawyer or a secretary or a shadow, and the actual *V* was somewhere warm, holding his hands over a fire I couldn't imagine, thinking about the coast north of Greywater in the exact week the autumn tides were running their lowest.

I want to be clear about my state of mind, because I've been accused—by Ruth, mostly, though she comes later—of pretending to be more careful than I am. I was not fooled. I want that on the record. A man in my position learns to feel a trap the way you feel weather coming: not through any single sign, but through a pressure, a flatness, a wrongness in the light. This telegram had that. Triple rate for work nobody wants. Cream stock. A single initial. *No further particulars by wire*—which is the sentence that should have ended it, because a legitimate client is eager to tell you what the job is, and a client who won't put particulars on a wire is a client whose particulars would look bad on a wire.

I knew all that standing there with the paper in my mittened hand, frost coming up out of the ground around my boots like the country itself raising its hand to object.

And I also knew what triple rate was. Triple rate was four hundred dollars for a season's work. Triple rate was my father's homestead—the quarter section on the Alder Fork that the bank had seized six years ago, sold at auction to a man who'd never seen it, who ran cattle on it badly and called it improvement—bought back with money left over. I had spent six years walking other people's dead railroads, sleeping in section houses and culvert mouths, keeping my name off every document that mattered, all of it toward the arithmetic of getting that land back. Not to live on. I don't know if I'd live on it. But it was the one piece of ground in the world with my name in its history, and a man can be erased from every ledger in the territory and still, at three in the morning, want one field with his name in it.

Triple rate was that field. Triple rate was maybe two seasons of that field, if I was careful, and I am always careful, except, as it turns out, in the ways that count.

I walked the rest of the spur back to my camp—a tent, a stove, a crate of instruments that had belonged to a better surveyor than me, or at least a better-documented one—and I made coffee, and I sat with the telegram propped against the coffee pot and let the steam fog it and clear it, fog it and clear it, while I thought about the shape of the thing.

Here's what I kept coming back to. Not the money. Not even the coast. It was the *timing.* The autumn tides north of Greywater run lower than any treads in the territory—the spring tides of October pull the sea back past every charted mark, and the old men on that coast will tell you, if you buy them enough whiskey, that there are years when the sea gives things back. Wrecks. Foundations. A door in a cliff that isn't a door in any other month. And here was a *V* with cream-stock paper wanting a survey of exactly that country, in exactly that season, before the storms came and closed the account.

Somebody knew what the tide was going to show. Or somebody suspected, and was spending triple rate to find out, and wanted the finding done by a man with no name on his documents, so that whatever came up out of that water would come up belonging to nobody—and therefore, by the alchemy that governs such things, to *V.*

The trap hadn't sprung. I'll say that for my own defense, and I'll say it now because later I won't be given the courtesy. The trap hadn't sprung. But I could feel its shape the way you feel a snare wire across a game trail with your boot before your weight comes down—a thinness in the world, a place where the country had been arranged instead of grown. Every instinct I had, every year of walking dead lines and reading what the frost and water wrote, said: *walk away, Edmund. There are other spur lines. There is other salvage. The homestead will keep. It has kept six years; it will keep seven.*

But there's a second instinct, the one I trust less and obey more, and it said: *a man who only walks lines other people have already given up on will one day be a line other people have given up on. Go see what the sea is giving back. Go see what a V wants with it. And keep your hand on your knife while you're about it.*

I finished the coffee. I got out my reply form, and my pencil, because I do my business in pencil and let other men commit to ink.

The boy had said four o'clock at the Junction office. It was a two-hour walk. I had time to be sensible, and I used all of it, and then, at half past one, I wrote the reply that I would think about for a long time afterward, in culverts and trestles and one flooded cave I will tell you about when I'm able:

*ACCEPT TERMS STOP SURVEYOR EN ROUTE STOP J.*

I signed it *J.* One letter, to match his. It felt, at the time, like a joke between professionals.

It was, in fact, the first payment on a debt I didn't know I owed, and the *V* at the other end of the wire—old man Vane, though I didn't know it yet, and his grandfather's graves, though I didn't know those either—collected on it for a long time.

The wilderness forgives nothing and forgets less. I walked down to the Junction in the late light with the frost coming on behind me, and I told myself I was the surveyor here, the one doing the examining, the one who'd know a trap when he stepped in it.

I had it exactly backward, of course. I was the thing being surveyed. And somewhere south, in a warm office, somebody was reading my one-letter reply and writing me down in a ledger of his own.
