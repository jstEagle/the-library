## 2. A Client Without a Face

The telegram had given me a room number, a hotel, and a time, in that order of importance. The hotel was the Meridian, which sits on the harbor front at Port Casterly like a fat cat on a warm windowsill—four stories of yellow brick, brass fittings, and the particular smell of cigar smoke that has been given time to think about what it's doing to the wallpaper. I had not stayed in a hotel in six years. I had not *been offered* a hotel in longer than that.

I arrived early, because a man who lives off abandoned railroads learns that the only reliable schedule is the one you set yourself. The desk clerk looked at my coat the way a man looks at weather he's decided not to comment on, and gave me a key without asking my business. That was the first odd thing. The second was that the room had been paid through the week.

"Through the week," I said.

"Through the week, sir." He slid a small envelope across the counter with the key. "Message came for you this morning."

The envelope held a note in a hand so even it might have been printed: *The gentleman will receive you at four o'clock. Room 41. Please come alone.* Underneath, in smaller letters, as though the writer had anticipated my sense of humor: *There is no gentleman. You will understand.*

There is a particular kind of dread that arrives not as fear but as recognition. I had felt it before, standing on a trestle at dawn, watching frost climb the rails, and understanding that the line had been abandoned not because the railroad failed but because the railroad *wanted* it to fail—for tax purposes, for insurance, for reasons written in ledgers I would never see. The wilderness forgives nothing and forgets less, but men are the opposite. Men forgive everything and remember only what profits them.

I went up to room 41 at four o'clock precisely, because if a trap is going to spring, I prefer it to spring on time.

* * *

The man waiting for me was not Mr. Aldous Vane. I knew this the way you know a drawing of a horse is not a horse—everything about him was correct and nothing about him was alive. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, dressed in a gray suit that had been cut by someone who charged by the hour and earned it. He stood when I entered, offered a hand dry as autumn paper, and said, "Mr. Joubert. Thank you for your punctuality. I am Mr. Quill."

"Quill," I said. "Like the pen."

"Like the pen," he agreed, with the small satisfaction of a man who has made this joke before and found it useful. "Please, sit."

The room was the best in the hotel—I knew because I'd asked the clerk, casually, the way you ask about weather—and it had been converted into an office. A table where the bed should have been. Papers squared in stacks. A lamp turned so its light fell on the guest's chair and left the lawyer's face in a soft penumbra, which was either an accident of furniture or the most honest thing about him.

"Before we begin," Quill said, "I want to assure you that my client is entirely legitimate."

"No one has ever had to tell me that before," I said. "It's usually the opposite. Men who are legitimate assume you can smell it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more like the place where a smile had been amputated. "You come well recommended, Mr. Joubert. Your work on the Ashford cut. The survey of the old Meridian spur. The—forgive me—the *unofficial* character of much of your recent employment."

"Unofficial," I said. "That's a kind word for it."

"It is a precise word for it. My client requires precision." He drew a folder from the nearest stack and opened it with two fingers, like a man dealing cards he'd already counted. "You will survey a coastal route. North of Casterly, along the drowned shore, as far as the Kestrel Point headlands. My client is considering an investment in rail infrastructure there. He requires an independent assessment of the terrain, the trestles, the harbor depths. Standard work."

"Standard work," I said, "doesn't pay triple."

"Triple," Quill said, without blinking, "is the market rate for discretion."

I let that sit. In my experience, when a man says *discretion*, he means he intends to do something a reasonable person would want witnessed. I looked at the folder. A map of the coast, recent, better than anything I'd seen in a government office. Soundings marked in a neat hand. And along one stretch of shoreline, a small annotation I would think about for weeks afterward: a stretch of coast left blank, with a symbol like a fishhook drawn in the margin.

"What's this?" I asked, tapping the blank.

"An error in the source chart," Quill said, too quickly and too smoothly. "You'll survey it like any other stretch."

I turned pages. Wages, per diem, a bonus clause for completion before the autumn gales. The figures were generous the way a snare is generous—it gives you all the room you need in exactly one direction.

"And the condition," I said.

Quill folded his hands. "Ah. You've done this before."

"I've been offered things before. Usually the condition comes last, after the money has softened me up."

"The condition is this." He slid a single sheet across the table. "No names in the field notes. Not yours, not your crew's, not my client's. The survey will be signed with a survey number, which my office will assign. All correspondence goes through me. You will not contact my client directly. You will not, at any point, represent yourself as working for him or describe the nature of your commission to any party. If asked, you are surveying for a land speculation syndicate. There are a dozen such syndicates; it is an unfalsifiable answer."

I read the sheet twice. It was drafted in the kind of legalese that sounds like politeness and functions like a padlock. I had signed contracts before—real ones, with railroads, back when I still signed my name to things and expected the name to mean something afterward. I knew what a clause like this was for. It wasn't to protect a client's privacy. Privacy is a coat you wear. This was a coat he intended to hang on me and then deny ever having seen me wear.

"And if I'm asked under oath?" I said.

Quill's expression did not change, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts under ice. "Then you will have the comfort of knowing you were never employed by anyone at all. My client's arrangements are structured so that no such employment can be demonstrated. For your protection, Mr. Joubert. Truly."

"For my protection," I repeated.

"Men in your line of work value invisibility. We are simply offering to extend it." He leaned back, and the lamp caught the edge of his jaw. "Consider it, if you like, a professional courtesy. You have spent years making yourself unfindable. My client is offering to make you *unaccountable*. Most men find that liberating."

Most men, I thought, find it liberating right up until the moment they need someone to account for them. But I didn't say it, because I was already doing the arithmetic I'd been doing since the telegram arrived, and the arithmetic was ugly and simple.

* * *

Here is what you should know about me, since I'm the one telling this: my father homesteaded eighty acres in the Calloway valley, and when the railroad came through—or rather, when the railroad *decided* to come through, and then changed its mind and took the land anyway under an easement clause that had been written by lawyers considerably better paid than Quill—the homestead was seized for a spur that was never built. My father fought it for nine years with petitions and lawyers and a stubbornness that outlived him. The land sits now under a tax lien held by a bank that has never once been troubled by the question of why it owns a field nobody uses. My father is buried on a hill above it, in sight of the fence line, which is the only part of the property the family was permitted to keep.

I could buy it back. That was the whole of it. Twenty-two months of survey wages at the ordinary rate, or four months at this one. I had done the sum on the train north, in my head, over and over, as though the numbers might change if I checked them enough times. They didn't. Four months. One season. And then a fence of my own, and a gate with my name on it, and the right to stand on my father's hill without being a visitor.

I tell you this so you understand I wasn't bought. I was *traded with*. There's a difference, and the difference mattered to me a great deal at the time, and almost nothing at all in the end, which is the way of most differences.

"Mr. Joubert," Quill said, into my silence. "You are hesitating."

"I'm reading," I said. "There's a difference."

"Of course." He waited. He was very good at waiting. It occurred to me that a man this patient had probably been hired for it.

"One question," I said. "Why coastal? A rail line along the drowned shore is engineering folly. The trestles rot, the foundations drown twice a day, and every storm season takes its toll. Any competent surveyor will tell your client the same. Why pay triple for an answer everyone can give for free?"

For the first time, Quill paused. It was a small pause, a hairline crack in the plaster, but I had spent six years reading hairline cracks in things—rails, trestles, faces—and I saw it.

"My client," he said, "is interested in the *whole* of the route. Not merely its viability." He gathered the folder, squared its edges against the table, and resumed his smoothness as though nothing had shown. "You will find, when you examine the coast, that there are features of interest to a man of his... historical enthusiasms."

"Historical enthusiasms," I said.

"The Vane family has been in this country a long time, Mr. Joubert. Long-lived families accumulate curiosities." He smiled then, fully, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all day, because it was warm. "You needn't concern yourself with them. Survey the route. Chart the trestles. Take your soundings. Leave the curiosities to their owner."

I looked down at the contract again. No names. Not mine, not my crew's. And there it was, the word I'd skipped past the first two readings: *crew*.

"I'll need to hire," I said. "A survey of that length wants at least three besides myself. A cartographer. Someone who can read wire and signal. A boat—the drowned shore can't be walked."

"Naturally. Hire whom you like." He said it lightly, but I caught the second hairline crack, because he said it *too* lightly, the way a card player says *your turn* when he already knows your hand. "Though I might observe that men who work off the books tend to know one another. My client trusts your judgment in assembling... compatible personnel."

Compatible. I would turn that word over many times in the months to come, and every time I turned it, it would look less like a compliment and more like a specification.

* * *

I walked down to the harbor while it got dark, because I wanted to see the water before I signed anything. This is a habit, not a superstition. A man who surveys coasts learns that the sea is the only honest party in any negotiation. It tells you exactly what it will do to you, twice a day, in writing, in salt.

The Kestrel was tied at the third berth, and I looked at her longer than I meant to—there's a chapter coming about her captain, and I won't steal its thunder here, except to say that even then, moored and dark, she had the look of a vessel that had been asked to do dishonest work and refused. I bought a bowl of chowder from a stand on the pier and ate it standing up, watching the lights of the Meridian across the water, and I asked myself the question a sensible man asks: *What does a man like that want with a blank stretch of coast and no names?*

The sensible answers were all innocent. Tax shelters. Land speculation. A rich man's hobby of buying up dead railroads the way other men buy up dead painters. The coast north of Casterly was full of ruins—trestles, spurs, a whole abandoned century of somebody's ambition—and a man with money could want any of it for any reason, and none of it needed to concern a surveyor paid to measure and not to ask.

But there was the fishhook symbol on the chart. And there was the blank where the coast should have been. And there was the way Quill had said *historical enthusiasms* the way a doctor says a symptom he's decided not to treat.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest: nobody pays triple for folly. They pay triple for silence about something that isn't folly.

I finished the chowder. I walked back to the Meridian. Room 41's lamp was still burning; I could see it from the pier, a yellow square hung on the dark like a lure.

Here is the thing I have come to understand about compromise, and I offer it free of charge, since I paid enough: it never feels like surrender at the time. It feels like prudence. It feels like a man being reasonable about the world as it is, rather than the world as it ought to be. You sign away a little of yourself—a name here, a clause there—and you tell yourself it's a coat you can take off later. You do not feel the hook set. You feel only the line going pleasantly taut, and you call it opportunity.

I went up. Quill had the pen ready, a good one, black lacquer, and the contract turned to the signature page with the practiced ease of a man who has done this many times and never once watched the pen hesitate.

"Survey number," he said, "will be Seven-Adam-Nine. You'll memorize it and destroy the card."

I signed *Seven-Adam-Nine* where my name should have gone. It looked strange on the line, those three characters standing in for thirty years of a man's life. It looked, I thought, like a blank stretch of coast.

Quill counted out the advance into an envelope—cash, of course—and slid it across with both hands, a small ceremony, almost respectful. "My client wishes you a productive season, Mr.—" and he stopped himself, deliberately, and smiled. "Forgive me. *Seven-Adam-Nine.* I'm sure I'll make that mistake for weeks."

He saw me to the door. As I left, he said one more thing, and I have replayed it often enough since to know he said it *on purpose*, that it was bait dressed as an afterthought:

"There is one further matter. Purely practical. The spring tides this season are the lowest in nine years. If you find yourself on that coast in the week of the equinox—" he adjusted his cuff, not looking at me— "you may find the sea has withdrawn further than any chart accounts for. My client would consider it a professional courtesy if you surveyed *everything* the tide gives back."

I stood in the hallway a moment. Down the corridor, the hotel went on being a hotel—laughter behind a door, a maid's cart, the sea-smell coming under the far window. Ordinary sounds. But I felt then what I had felt on the telegram, only closer now, with the shape of it filled in: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The feeling of standing on sound ground and knowing the water is coming for it.

"The week of the equinox," I said.

"Bring a good line," Quill said, and closed the door.

I took the stairs down, past the clerk, out into the street where the harbor lights lay doubled in the black water, and I stood a while with the envelope of money in my coat, heavier than paper has any right to be. Four months to my father's fence line. One signature to a man with no face. Somewhere north of here, the sea was already beginning to think about withdrawing.

I hired my crew the next morning. I should have known then, from the faces that said yes, exactly what kind of story I'd signed us all into.
