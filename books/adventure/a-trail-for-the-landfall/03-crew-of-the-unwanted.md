## 3. Crew of the Unwanted

The first thing you learn about hiring is that the good ones are already employed and the available ones are available for a reason. The second thing you learn, if you're me, is that the reason is often the best part of them.

I had Quill's money in my coat and a list in my head of every surveyor, runner, and boat-hand between here and the capital who would take work nobody else offered. It was a short list. I'd been on it myself for six years.

* * *

Ida Marsh kept an office above a chandler's shop that smelled of tar and old rope, which meant she paid her rent in maps drawn for men who'd never look at them twice. Her name was still stenciled on the door—MARSH, CARTOGRAPHIC SERVICES—but someone had tried to scratch it out with a knife and she'd let the marks stay, the way you keep a scar to remind yourself what the world thinks of honesty.

She was thirty, maybe, with ink on three fingers and spectacles pushed up into hair that had clearly lost an argument with the sea wind that morning. She didn't stand when I came in. She was correcting a chart of a harbor I happened to know intimately, and she was right to correct it, and whoever had drawn it originally should have been ashamed.

"You're Edmund Joubert," she said, without looking up.

"News travels."

"The news travels when the news is interesting. A man who maps dead rail lines taking money from a lawyer named Quill—that's interesting." She set down her pen at last and looked at me over the spectacles, which she did not put on, which told me she wanted to see me plainly, without even glass in the way. "You're not here about the harbor."

"I'm here about a coastal survey. North. Four months, maybe less. Triple rates."

"Whose coast?"

"That's the first condition. No names in the field notes. Not mine, not yours, not the client's. Locations by bearing and chain only."

Something moved behind her eyes—not greed, not fear. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a song she already knew all the words to.

"And the second condition?" she said.

"There isn't one yet. There will be. There always is, with money like this."

She leaned back. "Do you know why I'm off the rolls, Mr. Joubert?"

"I heard you falsified a survey."

"I did no such thing." She said it evenly, the way people say things they've said so many times the anger has been worn off like a coin's face. "I surveyed a bridge approach for the Corvane & Western and found the abutment sitting on fill instead of bedrock. I wrote that down. The company asked me to write down something else. I declined. They struck me off for falsification—theirs, not mine—and the record says Marsh, I., unreliable." She smiled, thin as wire. "I've been drawing harbors for chandlers ever since. Accurate ones. Nobody checks those."

"Why say yes now?"

"Because you asked me knowing." She stood, finally, and shook my hand with ink-stained fingers. "Everyone else who's come through that door in two years wanted a map that said what they'd already decided. You want a map that says what's there. That's worth triple rates on its own."

I almost told her then. Almost said: the client is Vane money, or close enough to smell it. But I'd signed a paper, and besides, I hadn't confirmed it myself. I hired her on the spot and walked out feeling lighter and dirtier in equal measure, which I was learning was simply what Quill's money felt like.

* * *

Pip Okonkwo found me before I found them, which should have been my first warning.

I'd posted nothing. I'd told no one but Ida. Yet there was a knock at my hotel door at seven in the evening, and standing in the hall was a kid of maybe sixteen in a telegraph company coat two sizes too big, sleeves rolled four times, holding a canvas satchel like it might be repossessed at any moment.

"You're hiring for the north run," Pip said. Not a question.

"How do you know that?"

"I read everything that goes through this office. Every telegram, every freight order, every private wire." The kid shrugged, unrepentant. "It's not hard. Half the operators don't even change their keys. You sent a wire yesterday asking after coastal boats under sixty tons. So either you're smuggling something small or surveying something wet."

I stared. "You read other people's telegrams."

"I read the wires," Pip corrected. "The wires belong to everybody. That's the whole point of them. Somebody just forgot to tell the companies." A pause, and then, quieter, with the studied carelessness teenagers use to hide the things that matter most: "You need a signal man. Old lines, derelict trestles, coast stations nobody's logged since the nineties. I can read a dead line faster than a live one. I can tell you from the hum whether a wire's grounded, cut, or just sulking."

"Where's your apprenticeship?"

"Gone."

"Pip. Where is your apprenticeship?"

The kid's jaw set. "Mr. Harrow's office, third floor, and he can have it back. He docks wages for 'attitude' and locks the practice key in his desk so we can't learn anything he can't charge us for later, and last month he took credit for my fault-tracing on the harbor loop because a boy's work doesn't count and a man's name does." The words came out fast and flat, rehearsed against a wall somewhere, many times. "So. Do you need a signal man or not?"

Here is what I should have considered: that hiring a runaway makes you party to a runaway. That Quill's people had chosen their moment, and maybe their crew, and a kid who reads other people's mail is a kid who could read mine. Here is what I actually considered: that I had been invisible for six years precisely because nobody hires the unwanted, and that Pip Okonkwo looked at me the way I used to look at passing trains—with the specific hunger of someone calculating exactly how far the next one goes.

"Room and board, forty a month, and you touch no wire that isn't ours," I said. "And if Harrow comes looking, I never saw you."

"You saw me," Pip said, grinning for the first time, and it changed the whole face. "You just won't remember where."

* * *

Captain Ruth Ellery was the hardest yes, and I nearly made it the hardest no.

The Kestrel sat at the coal dock looking like what she was—forty years old, ferry-built, broad-beamed, honest as a mule and about as fast. Ruth Ellery sat on her rail with a mug of coffee, watching me come down the gangway with the expression of a woman timing how long it takes a fool to reach her.

"Joubert," she said. "The line-mapper. You wired about charter terms."

"I did. Coastal survey, north of Greywater Sound, six weeks weather window, your boat, your rules on the water."

"My rules." She drank her coffee. "First rule: I say when we run and when we don't. Second rule: nobody touches the wheel. Third rule—" she stopped, and looked past me at the water, and for a moment I watched her decide whether to say the third rule aloud. "Third rule. We don't go anywhere near the Landfall."

The name went through me like cold water through a boot. The locals had said it too, down at the fish market, when I'd asked casually about the spring tides. The Landfall. Said quietly, the way you say the name of a churchyard.

"What is it?"

"A cave. Mouth opens at the low springs, closes again, takes somebody every generation or so. My husband—" She set the mug down with great precision, as if it might otherwise do something. "My husband was a fisherman with more courage than sense, and the sea respected neither, and eleven years ago the Kestrel came home with her gear full and him not in it. So. Third rule stands."

"Then I'll be plain with you, Captain, because you'll find out anyway and I'd rather you hear it from me." I told her about the tide charts, the anomaly, the cave mouth that appeared below any mark on any chart. I did not tell her about Vane, or the graves I half-suspected, or the shape of the trap I could feel but not yet see. I told her the truth I had, which was incomplete, and I watched her weigh it.

"You'll go in," she said. It wasn't a question either. This woman dealt entirely in certainties.

"If the survey requires it."

"It'll require it. Men like you always find that the survey requires the exact thing that will kill them." She looked at her boat a long moment—the patched deck, the engine housing she'd rebuilt herself, the name on the stern repainted so many times the letters stood proud of the wood. Then she looked at me, and whatever she saw there, it was enough, or it wasn't enough and she was tired of waiting for enough.

"Charter's double rates, payable weekly, in advance," she said. "Because if you drown, I'm not chasing your estate for wages. And Joubert—if I say run, we run. If I say anchor, we anchor. Swear it."

"I swear it."

"Then welcome aboard the Kestrel. Mind the third rail of the companionway, it bites."

* * *

We provisioned through three days of gray drizzle, and it was during those days, without anyone deciding it, that the crew became a crew.

I knew it had happened on the second evening, when I came down to the dock and found Ida spread across the Kestrel's cabin table with her charts weighted down with coffee mugs, arguing projection methods with me before I'd even got my boots off—she favored a transverse system for the coastal stretch, I argued the old chain-and-bearing method would hold better against the fog, and we went at it hammer and tongs for an hour while Ruth sat in the corner mending a net and making small contemptuous noises whenever either of us used the word "obviously." Pip sat cross-legged on the bunk copying out signal codes onto scraps of paper, occasionally interjecting facts about wire gauges that were, infuriatingly, always correct.

Nobody agreed. Everybody listened. That was the difference, and I'd been on enough crews to know how rare it was. On a normal job, argument is insubordination wearing a polite coat. On this one, argument turned out to be how we said we trusted each other—because you only fight over the truth with people you expect to still be beside you tomorrow.

Later, Ruth cooked. Fish stew, terrible, too much pepper, served in tin plates on a table that rocked with the swell. Pip ate three servings and lied about a fourth. Ida produced, from nowhere, a bottle of plum brandy that she claimed was payment from a satisfied chandler and that Ruth claimed, correctly, was stolen from the chandler by the chandler's own brother. We drank to the survey. We drank, though none of us said it, to being four people whom every respectable enterprise in the territory had declined to employ.

"To the unwanted," Pip toasted, raising the bottle, and Ida said "to the unreliable," and Ruth said, "to the widowed," and it should have been grim and instead it was the warmest room I'd stood in since my father's kitchen, and I understood that I was in trouble of a new kind. Money you can walk away from. A debt to a client you can survive. But these three—I was already doing arithmetic on what it would cost to lose them, and the numbers frightened me worse than the tide charts did.

Near midnight, Pip went up on deck to check the mooring lines, and I followed, and found the kid standing at the rail with a pocket telegraph key in hand, tapping soundlessly against the brass rail—practice, pure habit, sending something into the dark with no wire attached.

"What are you sending?" I asked.

Pip didn't stop tapping. "Nothing. Nobody's listening."

"Somebody's always listening. You taught me that."

The tapping paused. In the harbor lights I saw the kid grin, quick and crooked. "Then I guess it doesn't matter what I send."

But I noticed, before we turned in, that the rhythm had changed—slower, deliberate, the cadence of a message rather than practice. And I noticed, two days later when we cleared the breakwater and stood north along the drowned coast, that Pip kept tapping it at odd hours, the same pattern over and over, like a prayer, or a warning, or a name.

I never learned what it said. By the time I thought to ask, we were past Greywater Sound, and there was a sail on the horizon that had no business being there, and after that, none of us sent anything we wouldn't have minded the whole world reading.
