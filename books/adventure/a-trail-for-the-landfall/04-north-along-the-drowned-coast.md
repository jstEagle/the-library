## 4. North Along the Drowned Coast

I have run the Kestrel through weather that would make a church deacon swear, and I will tell you plainly: fog is worse than any gale. A storm at least has the decency to announce itself. Fog simply arrives and takes everything away—the headlands, the buoys, the horizon, your own bow—and leaves you alone with the sound of water and whatever doubts you brought aboard.

We cleared the harbor an hour before dawn with a tide that felt like an apology, and by mid-morning the fog had us. I stood at the wheel with one hand on the spokes and the other on the brass rail my husband had polished so often it shone like a promise, and I steered by memory and dead reckoning and the small stubborn voice in my chest that said *left here, Ruthie, there's rock under that calm water.*

The Kestrel is forty-two feet of oak and stubbornness, built by men who expected her to outlive them, and she has. She carries cargo mostly—freight, mail, the occasional passenger too poor or too proud for the steamers—but she has never once carried a crew like this one.

Edmund Joubert stood at the rail with his chart board strapped to his chest like a breastplate, mapping as we went. That was the arrangement: I ran the boat, he drew the coast. He'd rigged a plank across the starboard rail and worked standing up, pencil moving in short economical strokes, pausing every few minutes to sight along some headland or squint at a line of breakers I'd have given a week's pay to avoid. He had the look of a man taking dictation from something that spoke slowly.

"Derelict trestle," he called back to me, not turning around. "Pilings at eleven o'clock, maybe two hundred yards. There'll be more of it."

"There always is," I said. "That whole stretch was the Meridian Extension before the money died. They built forty miles of track into nowhere and then couldn't afford the nowhere."

"Someone spent real money getting the nowhere wrong," he said. "Look at the cut they blasted through that point. That's not a surveyor's mistake. That's pride."

I looked. The cut was a raw scar in the headland, granite faces still showing drill marks after thirty years, and the trestle running out from it on rotting legs like a creature that had lain down in the sea and been too tired to finish dying. It was, I suppose, impressive, in the way that ruins are impressive—impressive and faintly accusatory, as if to say *this is what certainty costs.*

Edmund mapped it all. Every piling, every cut, every drowned cribwork where a bridge had been started and abandoned. He worked without hurry and without complaint, which I have learned is rarer than skill. Plenty of men have skill. Very few of them can stand at a rail in cold fog for six hours and keep their handwriting legible.

* * *

Pip Okonkwo found the first wire around noon.

I'd put the boy below with the coffee because their hands were blue—they insist on being called Pip, and I've stopped asking questions I can see they don't want answered—and instead of drinking it, Pip went poking through the old signal cabinet aft of the engine room, the one that came with the boat when I bought her and that I'd assumed was full of nothing but spiders and regret.

"Captain." Pip's head appeared through the companionway, eyes enormous above the mug. "There's a line running off this boat. An actual telegraph wire. It goes into the masthead fitting and runs down inside and comes out through the hull near the waterline. Somebody wired this ferry once."

"Somebody wired half the coast once," I said. "It doesn't mean anything's listening."

"Can I check?"

"Check what? With what?"

Pip produced, from somewhere in that endless coat, a pair of earpieces and a little brass instrument I recognized as a sounder, the kind telegraph offices use, except this one had been modified—soldered, spliced, wrapped in tape with the patient madness of someone who loves a machine.

"I fixed it," Pip said. "Mostly. Can I?"

I should have said no. A captain says no; that's most of the job. But the boy's hands were steady now, and there was a light in their face I hadn't seen since we left harbor, and I remembered being fifteen and being told *no* about things I loved until the word wore a groove in me.

"One hour," I said. "And if you burn out my masthead fitting, you're swimming home."

What Pip found, over the next hour, hunched in the lee of the deckhouse with the sounder clicking against the silence, was this: the old coastal signal line—the one strung along the derelict trestles and abandoned stations of the Meridian Extension, the one every chart marked as DEAD—was not entirely dead. Sections of it still carried current, fed from who knew what forgotten battery house or leaky junction, and at least two of those sections passed within earshot of our route.

"There's a station code repeating," Pip told us at supper, talking with their hands as much as their mouth. "Just a station identifier, over and over. K-Q-7. And further north there's another one, weaker, K-Q-9. Those are section houses. Somebody's keeping current on those lines. Somebody's paying for batteries for a railroad that doesn't exist."

The table went quiet. Edmund set down his fork.

"You're certain it's not leakage? Induction off a live line somewhere inland?"

"I'm certain it's a station call sign," Pip said, with the particular firmness of the young defending their one territory. "Leakage hums. This talks."

Ida Marsh, who had spent the meal silently correcting Edmund's charts in her head—I could see her doing it, the way she'd glance at his work and press her lips together like a woman biting back edits—said, "Then somebody's using the old line. For what?"

Nobody had an answer. But I noticed Edmund looking at Pip afterward with an expression I'd describe as a man revising an estimate sharply upward.

* * *

The argument started the next morning, and it was, I maintain, the finest entertainment aboard since the rat incident of '09.

It began, as these things do, over something small. Edmund had drawn the coastline freehand on his board, working in what cartographers call a plane projection—flat paper, straight lines, distances true enough for a man in a rowboat. Ida looked at it for perhaps four seconds before saying, "You're going to want a conic projection if you're covering this much latitude. Your meridians are converging whether you like it or not."

"They're converging within the width of my pencil," Edmund said.

"And your scale drifts a quarter mile by the time you reach the head of the bay."

"A quarter mile in fog is the difference between rock and water either way."

"A quarter mile," Ida said, "is how charts get people killed. I've seen it done."

The deckhouse went very quiet. I kept my hands on the wheel and my ears wide open, because I am only human.

Edmund turned around slowly. "You were struck off the rolls," he said. Not cruelly. Just laying the fact on the table between them like a card.

"I was," Ida said, not flinching. "For telling the truth about a chart that lied. Ask me about projection methods sometime and I'll show you exactly how a lie gets drawn."

Something passed between them then—not warmth exactly, but recognition. Two people discovering they'd been fighting the same war from different trenches. Edmund looked at his board, then at her, and said, "Show me."

And Lord help me, she did. For three hours. She took his pencil and redrew his graticule, explaining convergence and standard parallels and why the old coastal surveys used one convention and the inland surveys another and why mixing them was how harbors acquired fictional islands. Edmund argued every third point—not to win, I realized, but to test, the way a man kicks a rail to see if it'll hold—and Ida parried each one with the weary precision of someone who had defended this ground before, in rooms with less honest company.

By afternoon they were finishing each other's sentences about tidal datum planes, which is either friendship or a fever, and I made them both tea because someone had to be the adult.

Here is what I learned watching them: neither one wanted to be right. They wanted the map to be right, and they were willing to be wrong in public to get there. In my experience that is the rarest quality a professional can have, and I have ferried engineers, politicians, and one opera singer, so my sample size is considerable.

* * *

We made the drowned coast proper on the third day, and I will say this for it: the charts are not wrong to fear it.

The water there changes color without warning—green to brown to a brown so dark it might be bottomless—and the soundings I remember from my husband's day have shifted, shoals wandering like livestock. Old trestles march out of the fog at intervals, some standing, some collapsed into pick-up-sticks, all of them hung with kelp that makes them look at a distance like drowned men waiting in rows. The air smells of creosote and salt rot.

Edmund mapped it all without blinking. Ida corrected him without mercy. Pip sat on the deckhouse roof with the sounder clamped to the old signal wire we'd rigged temporarily to the masthead—running a temporary lead up to intercept the coastal line whenever we passed close beneath it—and called out station codes like a birdwatcher naming warblers.

"K-Q-7 again, stronger. And—" Pip went rigid. "Captain. There's traffic. Actual traffic. Someone's sending a message on the nine line."

"What does it say?" Edmund asked, coming forward.

Pip's hands moved, copying, lips shaping the letters. Then stopped. Then started again, slower.

"It's... coordinates. Or something like them. Soundings, maybe. And a word repeated twice at the end." Pip looked up, and the color had gone out of their face. "The word is *Landfall.*"

The fog chose that moment to thicken, as fog does when it wants to hear the rest of a conversation, and we all stood there in the gray with the sounder's ghost-clicks fading behind us, nobody saying anything.

"Somebody's surveying ahead of us," Ida said finally.

"Or behind us," Edmund said quietly. "Depending on how you count."

I put the Kestrel's nose back into the fog and gave her more speed than I liked, because I have been a working captain long enough to know that the difference between curiosity and trouble is usually measured in hours, and I intended to spend ours well.

Behind us, faint through the murk, the old wire clicked on, patient as a pulse, carrying somebody's business toward the cave the locals say charges admission in lives.

I only hoped, steering into the darkening water, that we weren't the fare.
