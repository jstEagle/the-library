## 5. What the Tide Gave Back

The tide began going out at half past three in the morning, and I was awake to see it because I had stopped pretending I intended to sleep.

I sat on the Kestrel's rail with my boots hooked through the stanchions and watched the sea perform the only trick it knows that still surprises people: leaving. It went out the way a debtor leaves a room—slowly at first, then all at once, taking its dignity with it. By four o'clock the water had dropped below the lowest line on Ida's chart. By five it had dropped below the line on *my* chart, and I had drawn that one myself from three separate surveys, so I knew it was a line I had invented to make the coast behave.

The sea ignored it. The water kept withdrawing, peeling back from the headland in long brown sheets, uncovering ledges no surveyor had bothered to name because no surveyor had ever been fool enough to expect them to exist twice.

"That's wrong," Ida said behind me. She had come up without a lantern, which told me she'd been awake too, and she said it the way a doctor says a symptom—not alarmed, just filing it.

"It's a spring tide," I said.

"It's a spring tide *plus forty feet* of coastline that isn't on any chart I've ever seen, including ones I wasn't supposed to have seen." She leaned on the rail beside me. Her hands were wrapped around a tin mug of something she called coffee the way a missionary wraps around a Bible. "The ocean is showing us something, Joubert. The ocean never shows anybody anything for free."

Down the beach, in the grey hour before the grey hour, the locals had begun to gather. I'd noticed this over the past three days—the way the village emptied toward the water on the ebb, the way nobody fished, nobody gathered kelp, nobody did anything useful at all. They just stood. Old men with their hands in their coats. Women who pointed their children at the headland the way other people pointed children at church.

"They're waiting for it," Pip said, scrambling up from below with the ship's glass under one arm and a biscuit in his fist. He was sixteen and ran on a fuel no one had yet identified. "The cave. The Landfall. The fisherman's boy told me about it—said it only opens on the biggest tides of the year, and that it—"

"Charges admission in lives," I said. "Yes. I've heard the sermon."

"You don't believe it."

"I don't believe in anything that doesn't have a gradient I can measure." I took the glass from him and put it to my eye. "But I believe in tides, and that one is going to come back whether I believe in it or not, and I intend to be standing on dry ground when it does. Which means if there's a cave down there, I want to see it while the sea is away from home."

The glass found the headland's base, where the water had been all week. And there it was.

I'd like to tell you I was prepared. I'd like to tell you that a man who has spent eleven years walking abandoned rail lines—tunnels with the roofs down, culverts with the water up, grades that ended in mid-air where the company had run out of money or nerve—I'd like to tell you that such a man looks at a hole in the earth and feels nothing. But there are holes and there are holes, and this one had a *presence*. It was a black arch maybe thirty feet across, cut into the living rock at the base of the headland, and the rock around it was worn smooth in a way that water doesn't manage in a hundred years or a thousand. The stone looked polished. It looked *handled*.

The arch was tall enough and deep enough that my lantern-light, when I imagined it, simply stopped.

"There it is," Ida said quietly. "The Landfall."

"Where the land falls," Pip said. "That's what the boy said it meant. The place where the land gives up and lets the sea in."

"Cheerful child," said Ruth Ellery from the wheelhouse door. I hadn't heard her come up, which was becoming a habit of hers I resented on principle. She stood with her arms folded and her jaw set at the angle I had already learned meant the conversation was over before it started. "I've run this coast eleven years. I've heard the stories from men who don't scare, and every one of them tells it the same. The cave opens twice a year. It takes somebody. Every year it opens, somebody goes in who shouldn't, and the sea takes its fee on the way back in."

"The sea doesn't take fees," I said. "The sea takes *tolls*, and only from people who haven't read the water."

Ruth looked at me for a long moment. She had her husband's eyes, I would learn later—not the color of them, but the way they held on to a thing after the thing was gone. "And you've read it, have you, engineer?"

"I've read enough to know the tide table doesn't lie. Low water at 5:40. The turn begins within the hour. I want two hours inside, and I want my line anchored to something that was here before the ocean and will be here after."

"Two hours," Ruth said. "And if you're not back on this deck when the water starts up the shingle, I am leaving. I'll take your ghost a letter if you leave an address."

"Fair terms."

"They're not terms, they're a mercy." But something in her face shifted—something that might have been respect, or might have been the recognition of one stubborn animal by another. "Ida goes with you to the mouth. No further. If the roof so much as sneezes, you both come out. Pip stays aboard."

"Captain—" Pip began.

"You stay aboard because you're the one who can work the wireless if this goes wrong, and because you're sixteen, and because I said so." She turned to go, then stopped. "Joubert. The men who tell that story honestly—the ones who've seen the cave open—they don't look at it while they talk. Did you notice that? Watch them this morning. They'll point at the headland with their chins and look at their boots."

I watched. She was right. Every man and woman on that shingle stood with their backs half-turned to the greatest thing any of them would ever see, and talked to each other about weather.

* * *

We went down at 5:15, Ida and I, across a beach that did not exist an hour before.

That's the part I can't make a person feel who hasn't stood on a tide-flat: the wrongness of the ground under your boots. This was seabed. An hour ago it had been the property of cod and kelp and things with too many legs, and now I was walking on it, and it was giving up its water reluctantly, in pools that steamed faintly in the cold air, in streams that ran backwards toward the retreating sea as if ashamed. The smell was tremendous—iodine and rot and stone, the smell of a room that has been locked for a very long time and just had its door opened.

Ida walked with her chart case under her arm and her eyes moving constantly, left, right, left, cataloguing. She stopped once and crouched over a shelf of rock and said, "Look at this," and it was a line of old iron, a ring-bolt, green with a century of salt, set into the stone with no structure near it and no reason for it. Someone had anchored something here once. Something big enough to need a ring-bolt.

"Whaling?" I asked.

"Whalers don't anchor to bedrock at the mouth of a cave their own charts say doesn't exist." She marked the position anyway, because Ida Marsh marked everything, because marking things was how she had survived being struck off the rolls—by keeping a private atlas of everything the official maps got wrong.

The arch grew as we approached, the way a mountain grows, by refusing to be the size you'd assigned it. By the time we stood beneath it I had to tip my head all the way back to find its crown, and the crown was lost in a dark that my lantern couldn't argue with. The air coming out of it was cold and steady, a breathing. Forty feet wide, I'd have sworn. Maybe fifty. And the sound—the sound was the worst part. Not silence. A low, vast hollowness, like standing inside a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing.

There were marks on the stone. Ida found them first, because Ida finds everything: carved into the rock beside the entrance, low, at the height of a kneeling man. Seven of them. Tallies. Crude, old, weathered to the point of suggestion rather than statement.

"Graves?" she asked.

"Or a count," I said. "Or a warning." I put my hand on them. The stone was cold in a way that felt administrative, official. "Somebody wanted whoever came next to know a number."

"And the number is seven," Ida said. She didn't say anything else, but I saw her copy them into her notebook, all seven strokes, and I saw her hand be very steady about it, steadier than it needed to be, the way a hand gets when it's holding something down.

The tide table in my pocket said the turn came at 5:40. It was 5:33. I had, by my own arithmetic, a working margin of two hours and some change, and I have never in my life trusted arithmetic more than I trusted it standing at that mouth with the sea four hundred yards out and falling.

"I go in to the end of the line," I said. "Two hundred feet of hemp. If the line goes slack or goes tight wrong, you pull it twice and I come back. If it comes back cut—"

"Nobody's in there to cut it."

"Something is in there to trip over it. That's what I mean."

Ida looked at me. In the grey light her face was all planes and honesty, the face of a woman who had lost her career to the principle that maps should tell the truth and had come out the other side with her principles intact and nothing else. "Joubert. The locals charge this place a toll of one life per opening. Ruth believes it. Half of me believes it, and I'm the one who believes in nothing."

"Then believe in the tide table. It's the one document on this coast that's never been caught lying."

I paid out the line myself, knotting it to the ring-bolt—the old green ring, the one somebody had put there for exactly this purpose, or for something so like this purpose that the difference didn't matter—and I stepped through the arch.

* * *

The first hundred feet, the lantern showed me a tunnel of the kind I knew: sea-cut, sloping up, the floor littered with rounded stone that shifted underfoot like bad ballast. The breathing sound got louder. The air got colder. And then the tunnel turned, once to the left, once to the right, in that S-shape that sea caves cut to keep their interiors dark even from the memory of daylight, and when I came around the second bend and raised the lantern, I stopped being a surveyor for a while.

I don't have the words. I've had eleven years to collect words for holes in the ground and I did not have these.

The passage opened into a chamber the size of a church, and the lantern-light went up and up into a roof I couldn't find, and the walls ran back into darkness on every side, and the floor—the floor was not natural. It was cut. Levelled. There were walls inside the cave, low walls, the footings of structures, and a line of iron stanchions down the middle of the space with the rotted ghosts of planking between them. A tramway. A *railroad*, or the skeleton of one, running into the dark of a cave that appeared on no chart, driven by men who had meant to take something out of this rock and had gotten as far as the rails before they stopped. Before they were stopped.

My lantern found a cart first. It sat on the rails thirty feet in, upright, its wheels still on the iron, as if it had been parked rather than abandoned. Then a coil of rope on a peg. Then a shovel, standing against the wall where someone had leaned it, meaning to come back. Then a boot. Just the one. Standing upright, laced, with the trouser leg still in it, stuffed with a hundred years of salt wind, keeping the shape of the man who had stepped out of it or been stepped out of.

I stood there with my light shaking slightly, and I will be honest with you: I talked to him. To the boot, to the shovel, to the room. "All right," I said, out loud, because the silence needed breaking and I was the only one there to break it. "All right. I see you. I see what you were doing. I'm not here to take your claim. I'm here to take your *story*, and stories are the one cargo that doesn't weigh down a cart."

The line at my waist had gone out to its full two hundred feet. I was at its end, and the end of the line was the middle of a room where men had died, and the turn of the tide was coming, and every instinct I had spent eleven years sharpening was telling me to go. I knelt and put my hand on the rail. The iron was cold and sound. Sound, after all this time, in salt air that eats steel. Which meant the air in here was strange. Which meant the air in here had been *kept*.

And that was when the lantern showed me the marks on the wall.

Carved, like the tallies at the entrance, but these were letters. A hand that had worked by poor light, cutting into stone with a spike or a knife, and the hand had been steady, and the letters said:

*WE CAME IN SEVEN*

And below that, lower, smaller, as if the carver had been running out of light or time or both:

*AND THE SEA KEEPS THE*

The sentence stopped. Mid-word, maybe. Mid-thought. The stone after it was blank.

I stood in front of that sentence for a long time. Long enough that I heard, or thought I heard, far off in the dark behind me, water moving where water had not been moving before. The turn was close. The arithmetic was thinning. And the line on my waist suddenly felt like the only true thing in the world—a thread running back through the S-bend to a green ring-bolt and a woman with a notebook and a boat and a morning, and I put my hand on it the way you'd hold a man's arm.

I should have turned around then. I want that on the record, whoever's keeping one: I knew, and I stayed. Because the letters had stopped mid-sentence, and I am a man who has spent his whole life reading other people's abandoned lines, and I could not leave a sentence unfinished. Not that one. Not down there.

I raised the lantern and looked past the tramway, into the dark at the back of the chamber, and the dark looked back the way dark does when it has been waiting a long time to be looked at, and behind me, far off, faint but unmistakable, the sea began to come home.
