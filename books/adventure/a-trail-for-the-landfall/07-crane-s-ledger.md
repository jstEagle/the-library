## 7. Crane's Ledger

I learned cipher the way most kids learn to skip stones—out of boredom, and to show off. Back at the apprenticeship, before I ran, Mr. Hollis had me copying telegrams in a code book so old it predated the war, and after a month of that I could read a message through a keyhole of noise. Static, interference, half a line drowned out by weather—you learn to guess at what a sentence wants to be. You learn that people repeat themselves when it matters. Fear repeats itself.

So when Ida spread Silas Crane's ledger open on the Kestrel's chart table and said, "It's numbers, mostly, and none of them add up," I didn't say anything clever. I just put my elbows on the table and looked at it until it started talking.

* * *

The ledger was a surveyor's field book, canvas-bound, swollen with salt air so the pages waved like they were still breathing. The first third was ordinary enough—chainage figures, bearing readings, elevations, all in a cramped right-slanted hand that leaned into its work like a man walking uphill. Crane wrote small. He wrote like paper cost money, which in 1893 it did, and which told me something about him before I'd decoded a single line: this was a man who accounted for everything, because he'd been made to feel the cost of every inch.

The middle third was where the ordinary ended.

"Here." Ida tapped a page dense with columns. "Ore assays. He's recording samples—iron content, some copper, traces of other metals. But look at the locations."

She'd already penciled a rough plan of the cave on butcher paper, and she'd marked the sample sites as best she could from Crane's descriptions. When she connected them, they didn't scatter across the rock face the way a natural seam would. They ran in a line. A straight one, angling down and inland, deeper than any of us had gone.

"It's not a pocket," Ida said. "It's a vein. And it doesn't pinch out. Every sample is richer than the last."

"How rich?" Ruth asked from the galley doorway, where she stood with a tin cup she wasn't drinking from.

Ida did the arithmetic aloud, and I watched her do it, because watching Ida Marsh do arithmetic was like watching someone else breathe—it happened whether you attended to it or not. She gave the figure twice, once in tons and once in what it would be worth smelted and shipped, and the second time her voice went flat on purpose, the way you flatten your voice when the number is too big to say with feeling.

Ruth looked at the number the way you look at a dog that's bitten somebody. "That's not a mine," she said. "That's a reason."

"A reason for what?" Pip asked, and then remembered I was Pip, and that everyone was looking at me, and that the question was mine to answer. "For killing seven men?"

Nobody said no.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about ciphers: the good ones aren't complicated. Complicated codes break under field conditions—rain, hurry, a man with cold fingers working by lantern light. What survives is simple. A shifted alphabet. Numbers standing in for letters. A key word both parties can remember without writing it down.

Crane's key word took me two days, and I'm not going to pretend that was humility or patience. It was stubbornness with a grudge. I sat in the fo'c'sle with the journal open beside me and the ledger open in front of me and I read his ordinary entries over and over, hunting for the word he'd have chosen, the one he'd never forget even if he forgot his own name.

His daughter's name was in the journal. Marguerite. He wrote it in the margins sometimes, little notes to himself that weren't notes to himself at all—*ask M. if the fever broke*, *M.'s birthday, send the ribbon*. A man who writes his daughter's name in the margins of a death-soaked survey camp is a man who'd use it for a cipher key, because using it means saying it, and saying it is the closest he can get to home.

MARGUERITE. Nine letters, no repeats except the R. I laid it against the coded columns and the columns opened up like a lock that had only ever been waiting for the right hand.

The first thing I decoded was an inventory. Rails, spikes, blasting powder—quantities, dates, signatures. Ordinary. The second thing was a set of instructions, and my hands went cold on the second thing, and I want to be honest about that, because later, when people asked how I felt, I always wanted to say I'd been calm and scientific, and I wasn't. I was fifteen years old in a damp cabin reading a dead man's handwriting, and my hands shook.

*Vane has ordered the adit sealed. Not surveyed and closed—sealed. Powder charges at the throat of the tunnel, set for the fourteenth. The men are to be told it is a routine blast to test the seam's extent.*

Then, further down, in the same neat hand but pressed harder, the pen digging:

*I have checked the charge placements myself. They are not placed to bring down rock. They are placed to bring down ALL the rock.*

* * *

I brought it to the others that evening, and I'll give them this—they didn't waste time doubting me. Maybe because Ida had already worked out the vein, maybe because Ruth had spent forty years learning that the sea keeps receipts, maybe because there's a kind of horror that arrives pre-verified, needing no corroboration. You know it the way you know a storm front. You don't ask it for references.

"Read it again," Edmund said. So I read it again. My voice cracked on *all the rock*, and I hated that, and I kept reading anyway.

"The fourteenth of what?" Ruth asked.

"September." I turned the page. "Eighteen ninety-three."

Edmund got out the almanac without being asked. Tide tables, moon phases, weather records if the station had kept them. His finger moved down the column and stopped.

"Spring tide," he said. "Highest of the season. The same tide that opens the Landfall now would have been running that week." He looked up. "They sealed the tunnel at spring tide. If anything went wrong—if anyone got out past the blast—the sea was the second lock."

"And the graves?" Ida asked quietly. "Seven graves, seven spikes. If the blast killed them all inside, who buried them?"

Nobody answered, because the answer was worse than the question. Somebody had gone back in. Somebody had counted the bodies—or most of them—and dragged them out, or as many as they cared to account for, and put them in the ground with railroad spikes for headstones because proper markers cost money and the dead couldn't complain about the economy. Seven graves outside a cave that was supposed to hold them forever meant the math hadn't come out clean. Meant the powder hadn't done tidy work. Meant some of those men had died trying to dig their way toward daylight while their employer's foreman waited outside with a watch and a shovel.

"That's where the ledger gets worse," I said. Because it did. Because I'd kept decoding after my hands stopped shaking, because stopping felt like letting him win, and I don't mean Vane—I mean the part of me that wanted to put the book down and go up on deck and look at the horizon until the world was just a world again.

The last section of the ledger wasn't engineering. It was correspondence—copies, in Crane's cipher, of telegrams between himself and the Vane company offices. And threaded through the technical talk, like wire through cable, was the shape of a bargain being struck and then broken.

Augustus Vane had known about the seam from the first assay. Had ordered the expedition expanded, then hurried, then hurried again—*the syndicate meets in October, I must have figures in hand*—and when the figures came in richer than even he'd projected, the arithmetic changed. A claim that size, properly filed, would make the Vane name immortal. But a claim that size, improperly handled, could also be taken. Could be contested. Could be tied up in courts for a decade by any rival who smelled blood in the paperwork.

Unless the paperwork showed nothing at all. Unless the seam stayed a rumor, a legend, a cave the tide charged admission to—and Augustus Vane held the only map, unfiled, waiting for a year when courts and rivals and memory had all moved on.

"He wasn't hiding a disaster," Ida said slowly. "He was hiding an asset. The deaths were—" She stopped.

"The deaths were the price of keeping it off the books," Edmund finished. His voice was very level, the way it goes when he's holding something down. "Same as his grandson's field notes. No names. Nothing on the record."

And there it was. The condition the lawyer had put on our contract, sitting in the lamplight between us like a third person at the table. No names in the field notes. I'd thought it was eccentric rich-man paranoia when we signed. Rich men are eccentric; that's practically their job description. But it wasn't paranoia. It was housekeeping. It was the family doing what the family had always done—sweeping the coast clean behind them, leaving no trail, charging admission in lives and paying in silence.

"The money paying us," Ruth said. She said it to her cup, not to any of us. "Is his money. Has been since the day we signed."

"Blood money twice over," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it sounded like something off a broadsheet, and then immediately was glad I had, because nobody contradicted me, because it was true.

* * *

We didn't sleep much, any of us. I went back to the ledger around midnight because sleep was a country I couldn't get passage to, and I found the last entry. Or near-last. The coded columns stop mid-page, mid-calculation—a bearing reading begun and abandoned, the numbers trailing off like breath. Below that, uncoded, in plain hand for the first time in thirty pages, three lines:

*If anyone reads this, know the men died well and were murdered better. Their names are in the margin of every page. Do not let the company write them out.*

I sat there a long time with the lamp turned low. Then I went back through the ledger, page by page, and looked at the margins I'd been skipping for two days—the little technical annotations, sample numbers, chain corrections. And there they were. Names. Tiny, tucked into corners, one per page, in the same cramped uphill hand: *Hollis.* No—that one was *Holt.* *Amos Holt.* *T. Reyes.* *J. Whitcombe.* On and on, seven of them scattered through sixty pages like seeds. He'd hidden them where only someone reading closely would ever find them, because a margin looks like nothing. A margin is the one place a company man never checks.

I copied all seven names onto a scrap of paper and carried it up on deck, because suddenly it mattered to me physically, holding them, that they be somewhere above ground and under sky.

Ruth was at the wheel even though we were anchored, because Ruth is always at the wheel. She took the scrap when I held it out, read it in the moonlight, and handed it back without a word. But she touched my shoulder on her way past, once, brief, the way you'd steady something that might tip.

Out past the point, far off, there was a light where no light had been two nights ago. Small. Steady. Sitting on the water like it had all the time in the world.

I told myself it was a fishing boat. Fishing boats work at night; that's the whole business. I told myself the coast was full of lights and there was no reason this one should feel like an eye.

But I'd spent two days learning how a careful man hides things in plain sight, and I knew what I was looking at. That wasn't a fisherman's light. That was patience with a hull around it.

I went below and woke Edmund, and put the scrap of names in his hand, and said the only thing that seemed to matter:

"We need to decide what we're worth—and fast. Because I don't think we were hired to find this. I think we were hired to lose it."
