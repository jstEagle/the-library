## 8. Smoke on the Water

I have been reading weather off that horizon for thirty years, and I want it understood that what I saw that morning was not a cloud. Clouds sit. Clouds drift. What I saw was *standing*—a smudge of gray holding its position against a wind that should have carried it away, the way a man holds his ground when he's waiting for you to come to him.

I was on deck with coffee I'd already regretted twice, because the Kestrel's galley stove has opinions about altitude and we were anchored in a cove that had no business being as pretty as it was. Edmund was below with his charts spread out like a man playing solitaire with the coastline. Pip was aloft in the rigging, which I'd stopped forbidding around day four on the grounds that the boy could fall out of a telegraph shed faster than out of my mast and had done so, allegedly, twice. Ida sat at the stern rail sketching the headland, because that woman cannot look at a piece of geography without wanting to argue with it.

"Smoke," I said.

Pip's head came out of the shrouds like a gull off a fish cart. "Where?"

"There." I nodded at the smudge standing off the southern horizon, just shy of where the headland would swallow it within the hour. "Steam, not wood smoke. And she's not moving with the wind, which means she's moving against it, which means somebody's paying for coal."

Edmund came up the companionway with a chart still rolled in one fist, which is how he does everything—half-finished, held like evidence. He looked where I pointed for a long moment. Then he did the thing he does, which is go very quiet and very still, like a man listening to a house settle.

"How fast?" he asked.

"If she's a steamer and she's pushing? Twelve knots, maybe fourteen if her engineer hates his boiler." I took another sip of the bad coffee. "We're doing six if I'm kind to her, and I am always kind to her."

"That cove we passed yesterday," Edmund said. "The one with the shoals."

"The one I told you never to mention again?"

"Yes."

"What about it?"

"I think we're going to need it."

* * *

Now, before I take you into those shoals, you deserve to know something about me and water, because everyone aboard assumed they knew it and every one of them was wrong.

My husband Thomas drowned eleven years ago this spring, off the point at Harrow Neck, in water I had sailed since I was nine years old. The papers called it a squall that came up sudden. That part was true. What the papers didn't print was that I'd told him the barometer was falling and he'd gone anyway, because the ferry schedule was the ferry schedule, and a man who keeps a schedule on the sea is a man arguing with something older than himself. The sea won. It usually does. It let me keep the boat, though, which I've never decided whether to read as mercy or mockery.

After that, I learned the coast the way a widow learns a house at night—by touch, by memory, by the things that catch you when you stop paying attention. Every unmarked rock between here and the capital lives in my head with a name I gave it myself, because the charts don't bother and the government certainly doesn't. There's the Widow's Tooth. There's the Sleeping Ox, which wakes twice a day and drowns people who trust low tide. There's a shelf off Gannet Rock that doesn't show at any tide but will open your hull like a tin of sardines if you cross it at speed, and there is exactly one channel through the shoal field past Miller's Cove, and it is forty feet wide, and it turns twice on itself like a snake digesting.

I have never taken the Kestrel through it at full steam.

I have thought about it. There's a difference.

* * *

By noon the smudge had become a ship—a black-hulled steamer with too much freeboard for her length and a wake that said she wasn't pretending to be a fishing boat anymore. Edmund stood at my elbow with a spyglass he held wrong, the way landmen always do, and I didn't correct him because watching him hold it wrong was the only entertainment left to me.

"She's flying no colors," he said.

"Ships without colors are either smugglers or lawyers," I said. "Same cargo, different paperwork."

"Vane's."

"You don't know that."

"I know the lawyer in the port hotel told me the survey window was three weeks." Edmund lowered the spyglass and looked at me, and I saw something in his face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but arithmetic. "It's been eight days, Captain. Nobody's ahead of schedule unless somebody changed the schedule."

Pip came down from the mast with the particular energy of a boy carrying news too big for his pockets. "Signal station on the headland's alive," he said. "Old wire, nobody's supposed to be using it, but somebody's tapping on it right now. Short bursts. Professional hand."

"Can you read it?" Ida asked, coming aft with her sketchbook still open, charcoal on her fingers like she'd been fighting the landscape barehanded.

"Some. They're using a routing code, not plain language. But—" Pip hesitated, and I watched the kid decide whether to say the next thing, and watched them lose. "But the address prefix. It's a private line code. Railroad private line. My master used to handle those. They're reporting our position."

The wind came around the headland and touched the back of my neck, cold even in summer, the way it always is when it comes off deep water. I looked at the steamer. I looked at my boat—forty years old, patched twice, honest as a dog. And I did the sum everybody on that deck was doing and nobody wanted to say aloud.

A financier hires an engineer nobody's heard of. A cartographer struck off the rolls. A telegraph runner who ran. A ferry captain with nothing left to lose and, more to the point, nothing left anybody would miss. Four people whose disappearances would raise fewer questions than a lost dog.

"We were never meant to come back," Ida said quietly.

There it was. Said aloud at last, hanging in the salt air like something the tide brought in.

Edmund didn't flinch, which I'll give him credit for. "The lawyer said Vane wanted the route surveyed discreetly."

"Discreetly," I repeated. "That's one word for it."

"Captain." He turned to me fully, and I saw the other thing in his face now, under the arithmetic: shame. A man realizing the money in his pocket might be priced in someone else's blood. "If you want to put me ashore at the next harbor and take the Kestrel home, I'll not—"

"Mr. Joubert." I said it the way I used to say it to deckhands who apologized instead of bailing. "You are standing on my boat, which means you are my problem, and I do not abandon my problems. Also," and here I confess I smiled, because some part of me had been waiting eleven years for a reason, "you are all standing between me and the men who sent that steamer, and I find I mind that more than I expected to."

Ida looked at me over her sketchbook. Pip looked at their shoes, then at me, then stood a little straighter, the way kids do when an adult says something they can carry.

"So what do we do?" Pip asked.

I looked south at the steamer, closing the water between us with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry because it had never once needed to be. Then I looked north, at the white water standing over the shoals past Miller's Cove, singing its little song that only I knew the words to.

"We buy a day," I said. "Get below, all of you. Strap anything you love to anything heavy."

* * *

I'll tell you about the run, because I earned it, and because if I don't tell it while my hands have stopped shaking you'll get the clean version, and the clean version is a lie.

The channel past Miller's Cove runs like this: enter on the falling tide, hard against the northern marker rock—there is no marker rock, I named it that because it marks where a man named Reed went down in '81—then turn south the moment your keel stops singing, which it will sing, briefly, over gravel no chart admits exists. Then north again around the Sleeping Ox before she wakes, which gives you perhaps ninety seconds depending on the moon, and the moon that night was being no help whatsoever.

I came in at eleven knots. The Kestrel is not built for eleven knots. She told me so continuously, in the language of shudders and groans and one long complaint from her rudder stock that I felt through my boots and into my teeth. Behind me the shoal water boiled white where the tide fought the rocks, and ahead of me the channel turned, and I turned with it, close enough to the Ox's shoulder that I could see the kelp streaming off her like hair in current.

"Captain," Edmund said from the companionway, white-faced, gripping the frame with both hands. "There's a rock—"

"I know about the rock."

"It's very close—"

"It is exactly as close as it needs to be, Mr. Joubert, and one foot closer than you need it to be, which is why you are below decks and I am not." I put the wheel over. The Kestrel leaned, groaned, and threaded the needle, and for about four seconds her keel sang over that gravel bar like a bowed string, and then we were through, into the deep green water beyond, and the shoal field closed behind us like a door.

I looked back once. The steamer had come to a stop well short of the white water, because the men who own steamers hire captains, and captains, whatever else they are, are not paid to die in somebody else's shoals. She sat there on the horizon, black and patient, and I swear on Thomas's grave she looked like she was taking notes.

* * *

We anchored at dusk in a pocket harbor behind Gannet Rock that appears on no chart because I have personally erased it from two of them, which is a story involving a hydrographic office and a grudge I'll tell you some other time. The crew came up on deck slowly, the way people do after they've been certain they were going to die and then didn't.

Pip was the first to laugh—that high, disbelieving bark teenagers produce when terror exits the body. Then Ida sat down on the hatch cover and put her face in her charcoal-blackened hands, and her shoulders shook, and I chose to believe it was laughing too.

Edmund came and stood beside me at the rail. For a while neither of us said anything. The light went copper, then gray, then gone.

"My father's homestead was seized over a debt," he said finally, to the water rather than to me. "Six hundred acres. I've spent nine years earning it back a piece at a time. That's why I took this job. Triple pay, no names in the field notes—I told myself the conditions were eccentric, not sinister." He laughed, without much humor in it. "I signed away a little of myself for it. I remember thinking the paper was strange. Who asks a surveyor not to write down names?"

"A man building a list," I said.

He looked at me.

"Think it through, Mr. Joubert. No names in the field notes. A crew of four people nobody would report missing. A client who never shows his face. And now a steamer eight days early, asking after our position on a private wire." I kept my eyes on the darkening water. "That's not a survey expedition. That's a grave being dug in advance, and we've been hired to lie down in it politely."

Below us the anchor chain ticked against the hull, patient as a clock. Somewhere south, past the shoals and the dark, that steamer was sitting out the night the way creditors sit outside a dying business.

"So we run," Edmund said. "We run for the capital, we go to the newspapers—"

"And tell them what? That a rich man's lawyer was rude to us?" I shook my head. "Men like Vane eat newspapers for breakfast, son. We run, we scatter, and in a year each of us is a story nobody believes, told by a person nobody remembers. He counted on that. It's why he picked us."

"Then what?"

I didn't answer right away, because the truth was still forming, the way weather forms—you feel it before you can name it. But I'd felt it coming all day, ever since Ida said the words aloud at the rail: *we were never meant to come back.* Fear does something to a body. It either empties it or fills it, and somewhere between the shoals and the dusk mine had filled.

"He thinks we're running," I said at last. "Good. Let him think it. We've got a day—maybe two—while his captain works up the nerve to follow me through that channel or goes the long way around. Two days is enough time to learn what's really in that cave worth killing for. And once we know that—" I turned and looked at the three of them, my ragtag congregation of the unemployable, "—once we know that, we decide what it's worth and who gets to pay."

Edmund studied me in the last of the light. "You could still take the Kestrel home. Nobody would think less—"

"Mr. Joubert, I have buried a husband and two charts' worth of illusions. I am done letting other people decide what happens in my waters." I put out my hand, and after a moment he took it, and his grip was steadier than his face. "Two days. Then we go back in."

Behind us, far to the south, a single light showed on the horizon where the steamer lay anchored—burning all night, like something that intended to be there in the morning.

And beneath our feet, faint through the hull, the tide began its long turn toward the Landfall.
