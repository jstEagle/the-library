## 10. The Confession in Salt

I have spent my professional life correcting other people's maps, so you will believe me when I say that the hardest lines to draw are the ones that run through a person.

We got Edmund out of the Landfall with eleven minutes to spare, which sounds precise because Ruth counted it that way and Ruth counts everything. The tide was coming back through the lower throat of the cave like a debt collector, patient and absolute, and when we finally hauled him over the lip of the entrance ledge he was soaked to the sternum, bleeding from a scalp wound he insisted on describing as "a scratch," and clutching something wrapped in oilcloth against his chest the way a drowning man clutches driftwood.

"Paper," he said, before anyone could ask if he was alive.

"You're welcome," said Pip.

"Paper," Edmund repeated, and held it up.

I should back up. Rescue scenes deserve their due, and mine was less heroic than I'd have liked. When the collapse came down behind him—when the sound rolled through the cave like God clearing His throat—I had been topside with Ruth, arguing about whether to go in after him at all. Ruth's position was that the sea had made its terms clear and we were in no position to renegotiate. My position was that Ida Marsh does not leave people inside mountains.

What I actually did was stand at the mouth of the cave with a lantern shaking in my hand while Pip went in along the safety line, following Silas Crane's carved marks—the ones cut into the rock by dead men seventy years ago, little arrows and notches that said, in the only language that matters underground: *this way, this way, don't stop here.* It is a strange thing to be guided to a living man by the handiwork of seven buried ones. I thought about that a great deal afterward. I am thinking about it still.

Pip found him wedged in the escape passage above the tide line, half-buried in rubble, cursing with impressive fluency for a man who'd been given up for dead twice in one afternoon. They came back together through water that climbed their ribs, then their shoulders, then their chins, and Ruth stood at the entrance hauling them out one-handed with the other hand white-knuckled on the Kestrel's rail, and none of us said anything clever until we were all safely aboard and Edmund spat seawater and held up his prize.

"Crane's last page," he said. "It was hidden in the passage wall. Behind a stone with his mark on it."

"The escape route," I said. "He hid it on the way out."

"He never made it out." Edmund's voice did something complicated. "But he meant to come back for it."

* * *

The Kestrel put in at a shingle beach two miles north, under an overhang of black spruce, because none of us could bear to sit in the harbor where Vane's steamer might appear on the horizon like a bad conscience. We built a small fire. Ruth made coffee that could have stripped paint and we drank it anyway, huddled around the flame while the storm season's first real wind worried the treetops overhead.

Edmund unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon and the hands of a man who'd been underwater an hour ago, which is to say they shook.

The page was smaller than the rest of the journal—a single leaf, folded twice, written on both sides in a hand that started strong and deteriorated toward the bottom, as though the man writing it knew his ink and his time were running out together. The salt air had stiffened it but not destroyed it. Whatever else you say about sea caves, they keep things. That is practically their profession.

"It's not a report," Edmund said. He read the first line silently and stopped, and looked at me, and I want to record that look accurately: it was the look of a man who has found something in a map that shouldn't be there, a river running uphill, a town that isn't on any chart.

"Read it aloud," Pip said. "If it's going to haunt us, it can haunt us all equally."

So Edmund read it. And I will set it down here as nearly as I can remember it, because I have carried every word since, the way you carry a stone in your shoe—not comfortably, but deliberately, because putting it down would feel like a kind of lying.

*My dear Margaret,*

*If you are reading this, then someone has done what I could not, and come back into this place. I write it in the escape passage because it is the only part of the cave the sea does not own outright, and because I hold a superstition, unworthy of an engineer, that a man's last words should be written on the road home.*

*I will not trouble you with the particulars of how Augustus Vane ordered the adit sealed with six of my crew inside it, or how I alone was above ground when the charge went, because the ledger tells it plainer than grief can. What I ask of you is smaller and harder. Remember them honestly. Not as victims—that word lets the living off too easy—but as men. Thomas Achebe, who sang worse than he thought he did. Henrik Lund, who was saving for a farm in Dakota. The Delgado brothers, who wrote letters home in Spanish so the foreman couldn't read them. Samuel Okafor, who taught me more about ore bodies than any professor ever did. James Whitlow, who was seventeen. And young Amos Fenn, who died holding the lamp so the others could see in the dark, which is the whole profession in miniature.*

*Vane will call it an accident. Accidents are what powerful men call the things they arrange. Do not argue with him; he owns the newspapers and half the judges. Just remember, and teach your children to remember, and let the remembering be exact. Names, ages, trades. Grief that stays specific cannot be bought.*

*I tried to get them out. You must believe that your father tried. There were marks to follow and I marked them better, and if I had been ten minutes faster, or ten years younger, or simply luckier—*

*Tell your mother the survey is finished, whatever Vane's men say. The line runs true. It was only ever the men who were expendable to him, and they were never expendable to me.*

*Your loving father,
Silas Crane*

Edmund's voice gave out on the last line, or near enough. Pip had gone very still. Ruth stared into the fire with an expression I recognized from funerals, the disciplined blankness of a woman keeping her face from doing anything expensive.

And I sat there with the names going through me like current through a wire, because I knew them. Not all of them. Two of them. Okafor and Achebe—my grandmother had spoken those names over her sewing on Sunday evenings for years, quietly, the way you speak the names of the dead you were not permitted to mourn publicly. She had a photograph, tin-type, cracked across one corner: two young men in work clothes squinting into sun, arms slung over each other's shoulders. *Her uncles,* she said. *They went north to build a railroad and the railroad ate them.* And when I asked what happened—children ask—she would say, *an accident,* in a voice that made it clear the word had been chosen for her by someone else, and that she had swallowed it for fifty years without once digesting it.

Samuel Okafor. Thomas Achebe. Taught Silas Crane everything he knew about ore. Sang badly. Died in the dark holding the lamp.

"Ida." Edmund was watching me. He is an observant man; it is his best and most annoying quality. "You've gone white as chart paper."

"I know these men," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like a transmission from far away. "Two of them. My grandmother's brothers. She kept their photograph her whole life and nobody ever told her—" I stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go. Nobody ever told her what? The truth had been sitting in a sea cave for seventy years, forty miles from her kitchen table, waiting.

"Margaret Crane," Edmund said slowly. "The daughter. She married?"

"I don't know. Grandmother never said how she was connected to any railroad family. She just—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "She had a letter she wouldn't show anyone. Kept it in the sewing box. After she died, my mother burned a lot of her papers, but I don't think that one burned. I think my mother couldn't read why it mattered." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I became a cartographer because my grandmother used to draw maps on the backs of envelopes. Little ones. Coastlines. She'd never seen most of the places she drew. I asked her once how she knew where things were, and she said, *a girl told me, once, whose father measured the world.*"

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Out past the point, the sea was beginning to make the noises it makes when it means business.

"Margaret Crane married a Marsh," Edmund said. "It would explain the envelope maps. An engineer's daughter teaching herself projection from memory."

"My grandmother was Margaret Marsh," I said. "Born Margaret Crane. And I have spent my entire career being struck off the rolls for telling the truth in places where the truth was inconvenient, and I told myself it was principle, and maybe it was, but Edmund—it was also inheritance. I've been walking toward this cave my whole life. I just didn't know the address."

* * *

Later, when Pip had gone to check the lines and Ruth stood her watch at the rail, Edmund and I sat by the fire with the letter between us, and he said the thing I was already thinking, because as I mentioned, annoying.

"Vane doesn't know this page exists."

"No."

"If he learned a descendant of Silas Crane was on this crew—if he learned what your grandmother kept—"

"He already chose all of us for leverage. You said it yourself, after he dangled the homestead. He picked a disgraced cartographer, a runaway, and a widow because he thought we had nothing to lose." I fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. "He was wrong about the nothing. But he'll figure out soon enough what I do have to lose, and it isn't property. It's this." I touched the letter. "Seventy years of silence, and the man who built it is coming north on a private steamer to make sure it holds another seventy."

"Then we decide what to do with it before he arrives," Edmund said. "All of us. No one decides alone. That's the rule now."

"That's a dangerous rule for people like us."

"It's the only rule worth having for people like us."

I looked at the letter again—at the deteriorating handwriting, the ink thinning where Silas Crane's strength had thinned, the words *let the remembering be exact* pressed hard enough into the paper to score the page beneath. An engineer to the end. Even his desperation was precise.

My grandmother had drawn coastlines on envelopes for a girl who wasn't born yet, from memory, from a father's measurements passed down like contraband. She had waited her whole life for someone to come back into the cave. She died waiting. But she sent me instead, in the only way she could—teaching my hands to measure the world, teaching my mouth to say *that's wrong* on a survey crew full of men who didn't want to hear it.

Somewhere south, Aldous Vane was steaming toward us with lawyers and guns and a checkbook full of other people's graves.

I folded the letter along its original creases, exactly, the way you fold a chart you intend to use again.

"We give it away," I said. "Every name. Every town. Exactly."

Edmund nodded slowly, and out in the dark, the sea began to rise.
