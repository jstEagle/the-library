## 12. Vane Comes North

I have been called a great many things in my fifteen years—runner, rat, thief of wire, and once, memorably, "you there, boy"—but the name I was born with is Prudence Adaeze Okonkwo, and I had buried it so deep I'd half convinced myself it belonged to somebody else. Somebody who signed apprenticeship papers with an ink-stained thumb because her uncle said a trade was better than a dowry. Somebody who slept in the telegraph office loft and learned to read Morse before she could read scripture, which in my uncle's house made me both valuable and damned.

So when the man on the private steamer looked straight at me across the water and said, "Miss Okonkwo, isn't it? Of Harrow's Bend?"—well.

The world went very quiet, the way it does just before lightning decides where it's going.

* * *

He came north three days after we ran the shoals, and he came the way rich men do everything: too much, too visible, and precisely calculated to look like carelessness. The steamer was white as a gull's breast with brass bright enough to signal ships, and she came up the drowned coast flying no flag at all, which is its own kind of flag if you know how to read one. Ruth read it from the Kestrel's wheel and said one word under her breath that I will not repeat here, mostly because Captain Ellery swearing is like a church bell cracking—you remember it forever.

We were anchored in the lee of Gullwatch Point, mending what the shoals had torn out of us, when the launch put off from the steamer. Six men in it, five of them furniture and one of them not.

"That'll be him," Edmund said. He was coiling line with his hands and watching with his whole self, which is how he does everything. "Aldous Vane."

"You've met?" Ida asked.

"No. But I've spent four years learning what his money looks like from the wrong end." Edmund set the coil down. "It looks like this."

I want to be fair to the man, because fairness is a thing nobody ever extended to me and I've decided to hoard it like a miser. Aldous Vane was handsome the way a well-drawn map is handsome—all clean lines and confident borders, and not one mark on it telling you where the water gets deep or the ground gives way. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, dressed for yachting in clothes that cost more than the Kestrel, and he stepped onto our deck as though he'd been invited, which is a skill, I grant him. Charm poured off him like bilge water off a scupper, and if you weren't careful you'd mistake the smell for perfume.

"Captain Ellery." He took Ruth's hand before she offered it, kissed the air above her knuckles, and I watched Ruth's jaw do something structural. "They told me you were the finest pilot on this coast. They undersold you—I've seen what you did to my captain's schedule."

"Your captain can drown," Ruth said pleasantly, "for all the notice I'll take."

Vane laughed like she'd said something delightful. That was the terrible part, I realized later. Everything was delightful to him. Graves, threats, the drowning of seven men sixty years dead—he received all of it with the same warm amusement, the way a banker receives bad news about someone else's account.

"And Mr. Joubert. The invisible man himself." Vane turned to Edmund with genuine, unfeigned pleasure, and that was worse than the charm, because it wasn't performed. He liked Edmund. You could see it. Some men collect paintings; Vane collected people who didn't want to be collected. "Do you know, I've been trying to find you for two years? Not to hire you—to thank you. Your surveys of the closed lines are the only honest maps my grandfather's railroad ever produced, and they were never supposed to exist. There's poetry in that."

"There's trespass in it," Edmund said. "The lines were abandoned, not closed. Abandoned means the land forgot who owned it."

"The land," Vane said, "never forgets anything. That's rather our problem, isn't it?" And he smiled at Edmund like they were two men sharing a joke over a fence, and Edmund smiled back the way a door smiles when it's being held shut.

Then Vane turned to me, and the temperature of the morning changed.

* * *

"But where are my manners." He crossed the deck to where I stood by the mast, doing my best impression of a person with no history. I'm good at it. I've practiced. "You must be the telegraph operator. Remarkable work on the old signal wires—the coastal trunk line has been officially dead since '91, and yet my office in the city received a test transmission last week traced to a relay nobody maintains. Someone walked that wire and found life in it." He stopped in front of me. Up close his eyes were pale gray and patient, like a fisherman watching a float. "Miss Okonkwo, isn't it? Of Harrow's Bend?"

I did not flinch. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger of small victories: Prudence Adaeze Okonkwo did not flinch. What I did was say, "You've got the wrong girl," in a flat voice, while every hair on my arms stood up and saluted.

"Mm," said Vane, in the tone of a man marking a card. "Perhaps. Harrow's Bend, though—it's a small place. One telegraph office. An apprentice there, fourteen, fifteen years of age, extraordinarily quick, who absconded in March with a company coat and forty dollars of petty cash. Her uncle posted letters of inquiry to every stationmaster within two hundred miles. I read correspondence for a living, Miss Okonkwo—or whatever we're calling you—and yours was among the most heartfelt I've encountered. He wants you found. Or so he says. Men who write letters like that usually want something else entirely, but I take people as they present themselves. It's a failing of mine."

"I don't know what you want me to say," I managed.

"Nothing at all. I simply believe debts should be acknowledged." He touched the brim of an imaginary hat. "You cut and re-soldered a junction splice at the Mile 40 relay in the dark, in the rain, in under nine minutes, according to the line current logs. I've employed telegraph men for thirty years who couldn't have done it in twenty. Whatever else happens on this coast, I wanted you to hear from someone in a position to know: you are wasted as a fugitive."

And then he moved on, to Ida, and I stood there with my heart going like a sounder key and understood, all at once and completely, what Edmund had been trying to tell us since the day we signed on.

We hadn't been hired. We'd been *assembled*. Every one of us was a lever, and Vane had built the machine himself.

* * *

Ida he greeted by her struck-off name, Delia Marsh, and complimented her grandmother's needlework, which hung—I learned later—in a historical society two counties from here, which meant he'd researched her family tree down to the embroidery. Ruth he addressed by her late husband's name, Thomas, and expressed condolences so smooth and sincere that Ruth's hand went to the rail hard enough to whiten her knuckles, because nobody had spoken Thomas's name to her in three years and the first one to do it was this. He knew about the ferry license revoked after the inquiry. He knew about the debt on the Kestrel. He knew Edmund's father's homestead had been seized for back taxes eight years ago and sold into a holding company whose beneficial owner, if you dug through three layers of paper, was a Vane trust.

He knew everything. That was the horror of him—not cruelty, not even really menace, but *knowledge*, worn lightly, like a man carrying matches he never intends to strike.

He gathered us aft, near the stern where the tide sucked at the rocks below, and got down to it with the air of a man discussing weather.

"Mr. Joubert. Let me save us all the theater. My grandfather Augustus built the Meridian & Coastal Railroad, and in the summer of 1893 he lost a survey crew of seven men in a cave on this coast. The official record says rockfall. The unofficial record, which I have reason to believe is in your possession, says otherwise." He paused, giving Edmund room to lie, and Edmund took none of it, standing silent as a piling. "I'll assume silence means confirmation. Now. Here is what I admire about you, Joubert: you have spent years making yourself unfindable, and you did it for a reason I actually respect. You wanted your father's land back. Section twelve, Harrow Township, ninety acres, orchard gone wild, farmhouse leaning, worth nothing to anyone but a Joubert. It sits inside my holding company like a coin in a pocket."

Edmund's breath changed. Just barely. But I was watching him the way I watch a needle, and I caught it.

"So here is my offer, and it is the only one I will make, and I make it once because I find repetition undignified." Vane produced a leather folio from under his arm—of course he'd brought it aboard a stranger's boat; the arrogance was load-bearing—and opened it on the deck box between them. Papers. A deed, fresh-inked. "Section twelve, free and clear, title guaranteed, taxes paid in perpetuity by the trust. Signed today, it's yours by nightfall. In exchange: the ledger, the journal, any copies, and your silence regarding the manner of those seven men's deaths. The ore claim stays quiet, the graves stay a rockfall, and everyone walks away richer than they deserve."

"And them?" Edmund asked quietly. "My crew. Do they walk away too?"

"They were always free to go," Vane said, with such perfect sincerity that I almost believed him, and then he glanced at me—one glance, light as a fly landing—"though I'd gently advise Miss Okonkwo against the overland route south. Roads have a way of producing paperwork."

* * *

Here is what I did while Edmund stared at the deed to everything he had wanted for eight years: I climbed to the Kestrel's little wheelhouse roof, where I kept my kit, and I put my ear to nothing, and I listened anyway. That sounds mad. It isn't. When you've lived your whole life in the language of current and silence, you learn that listening is less about ears than attention, and I gave the deck below my full attention while pretending to sort spools.

Ruth spoke first, low and iron: "Captain's word carries weight on her own deck, Mr. Vane. Get off my boat."

"In a moment, Captain. Business first."

Ida's voice, tight as binding wire: "You knew Silas Crane had a daughter. You knew she married a Marsh. Did you think I wouldn't do the arithmetic?"

"I hoped you would," Vane said warmly. "It's why you're here."

And then Edmund, and I heard something in his voice I'd never heard before—not the flat surveyor's cadence, not the wry deflection, but something older and quieter, like water finding a crack. "Mr. Vane. My father worked that orchard for thirty-one years and lost it to a tax bill computed by a county assessor your trust owns. I have dreamed about that kitchen table more times than I can count. So understand that when I say what I'm about to say, I am saying it with the full weight of wanting."

A pause. Even the gulls seemed to lean in.

"No."

Just that. No speech, no speechifying. And then: "Keep the deed. Frame it. Because the moment I sign your paper, those seven men die twice—once in the tunnel, and once in the record—and I'd sooner live in a ditch under my own name than in that farmhouse under yours."

Vane closed the folio. Slowly. And here is the thing I will carry to my grave: he *smiled*. Not the charming smile. A smaller one, private, almost fond, like a chess player watching a promising opponent refuse a draw.

"Well," he said. "That's a pity. I genuinely hoped you'd be sensible." He straightened his cuffs. "For the record, Mr. Joubert—no pressure attaches to my offer. Storms come north this time of year regardless of anyone's decisions. Boats drag anchors. Wires fail. These things happen without help from me, and I would deplore anyone suggesting otherwise." He turned for the launch ladder, then paused, and looked back up at me on the wheelhouse roof with those patient gray eyes. "Miss Okonkwo. If you tire of the company you're keeping, my steamer carries a proper telegraph office. Paid position. Real name on the payroll, if you want it—or any name you like. The offer stands until the storm season closes the coast."

"It'll close on you first," I said.

He laughed—genuinely, delightedly—and went down the ladder, and the launch pulled away, and the white steamer turned her brass-bright bow south, and the whole coast exhaled.

Except it didn't. Because Ruth was already looking at the barometer nailed by the wheelhouse door, and her face had gone the color of the fog, and she said, in a voice with no wryness left in it at all:

"Pip. How fast can you raise the harbor office at Port Cullis?"

"Why?"

"Because that steamer isn't heading south." She pointed, and I looked, and past the point of Gullwatch the white ship had come about—not running for open water but standing in toward the harbor town, slow and deliberate, the way a man walks toward a door he intends to lock from the outside. "She's putting men ashore. And that barometer's dropped a tenth since breakfast. First gale of the season is coming down on us in two days, maybe three, and Aldous Vane means to own every wire, every dock, and every door on this coast before it lands."

I was already pulling on my coat, already thinking in clicks and dashes, already mapping in my head the dead line to Mile 40 and the nine minutes of rain-dark soldering that Vane had so thoughtfully praised. He'd meant it as a leash.

He should have checked what the wire was attached to.
