## 14. Cut Wires

I want it on the record that I woke up before anyone else, which is why I got to be the one who noticed the Kestrel was gone.

Not gone-gone. Gone in the way that matters: still sitting at her mooring, riding the gray morning swell like nothing had happened, but sitting wrong. A boat has a posture, same as a person. Ruth's Kestrel stood proud at her lines, bow up, ready to argue with the sea. This thing wearing the Kestrel's paint sat low and sullen, and when I leaned over the dock rail I saw why.

The mooring lines had been cut.

Not untied. Cut, clean through, and then—this is the part that told me everything about who we were dealing with—retied with fresh rope. Sloppy knots. Bowlines tied by men who knew knots were a thing boats required but had never once been asked to care about one. They'd cut her loose in the night, let her drift on the ebb until she fetched up against the pilings two slips down, and then they'd resecured her so nobody would notice until they wanted us to notice.

Anybody else would've seen a boat at her mooring and gone back to sleep. But I'd spent fourteen months as an apprentice telegraph runner learning to read what messages *looked* like versus what they said, and this was the same trick in wood and hemp: the surface says all's well; the hand underneath says run.

I went and woke Ruth by knocking on the Kestrel's cabin door the way you knock on a stranger's door, because Ruth asleep was not a creature you startled.

"Lines were cut," I said through the planks. "Retied wrong."

Silence. Then the sound of a woman deciding not to swear, which is louder than swearing.

By the time she'd gotten boots on, Edmund and Ida were coming down the dock road, and I watched Ruth tell them, and I watched Edmund do the thing he does where his face goes very still and his eyes go very busy, like a man reading a telegram written in small print across the whole horizon.

"Harbor office," he said.

We got there in time to see it was already too late for the harbor office.

* * *

Here's what I know about telegraph offices, having lived in the back of one for most of a year: they are the nervous system of a coast town, and everybody forgets about them until they stop working. The Harbor Quay office was a squat cedar building between the chandlery and the fish cannery, staffed by one operator, Mr. Fenwick, a man whose entire personality was a brass key and a pot of tea kept warm on the relay batteries. I liked him. He'd let me practice on his dead line after hours when I first ran away, before I understood that kindness from strangers is sometimes just curiosity wearing a coat.

When we came around the corner, there were three of Vane's men standing outside it. Not breaking in. Standing. One was leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed like he owned the building, which, given what Vane's money could buy, maybe by lunchtime he would.

The door was open behind them. Inside, I could hear the sound of a man being talked to in a reasonable voice, which is the most frightening sound there is if you know what reasonable voices get used for.

"Morning," said the man leaning on the frame. He had the cheerful, empty face of a hired fist. "Lovely weather for it."

"For what?" Ruth asked.

"Whatever it is you're all up to." He smiled wider. "Mr. Vane sends his regards. Says the survey's concluded. Says you'll be wanting to head south before the season turns. Storm season," he added, gesturing at the sky as though the sky were his idea.

I looked past him into the office. Fenwick sat at his own key with his hands flat on the desk like a schoolboy caught cheating, and one of the other men stood beside him holding a pistol the way you hold a tool you're embarrassed by. The line wire came in through the wall insulator, and somebody had clipped a second key onto it—a intercept rig, crude but functional. Every message in or out of Harbor Quay was now going through Vane's hands first.

They weren't cutting us off. That was the clever part, and I hated how clever it was. Cutting the line tells the whole coast something's wrong. Leaving it open and *listening* means every word we send goes straight to the man we'd be sending it about.

"Storm season's why we're here," Edmund said mildly. "Surveys don't wait for fair weather."

"Mr. Vane disagrees."

"Mr. Vane," said Ida, in her driest register, "has been disagreeing with the truth since 1893. It hasn't improved his position."

The man's smile stayed exactly where it was, which told me he hadn't understood the reference and didn't care to. Men like him never did. "Boat's seaworthy. Lines are open. Nothing here's against the law." He pushed off the doorframe. "Yet."

We walked away, because walking away was the only move on the board, and I felt the trap close around us with a click so quiet you'd miss it if you weren't listening for clicks. Which, again: fourteen months of apprenticeship. I am always listening for clicks.

* * *

Back aboard the Kestrel—we'd moved her to a mooring Ruth trusted, which is to say a mooring Ruth had personally wrapped in chain and padlocked to a boulder—Edmund spread his charts on the cabin table and said nothing for a long time.

Ruth broke it first. "They've got the harbor office, they've got eyes on the water, and their steamer's anchored off the point where it covers both channels out. We can't send word south. We can't sail south without running under their guns. And the first gale of the season is—" she squinted through the cabin glass at a sky the color of dishwater— "two days out. Maybe three."

"The cave floods in the first real blow," Edmund said. "Everything inside goes to the sea."

"So we run for it now, in daylight, guns or no guns," Ida said.

"And arrive in the first port south with Vane's lawyers already waiting, and every word we carry worth nothing because they'll have wired ahead." Edmund tapped the chart. "No. If this ends, it ends loud. All at once. Everywhere."

"That's your broadcast plan," Ruth said. "Twenty stations. We settled that."

"We settled it when we had a working line and a working harbor office." His finger moved along the coast, north, past the town, past the cannery, up into the drowned country where the old trestles stuck out of the fog like the ribs of something that had died standing up. "We need a line Vane doesn't know exists."

And that's when I started grinning, because I knew exactly what he was going to say, and I'd known it since chapter four, so to speak, since the day we came north and he made me stop the boat while he climbed a rotting signal mast in the rain to check whether a dead wire was dead.

"The coastal signal trunk," I said.

Edmund looked at me. "You remember."

"You made me test it. Continuity check, end to end, forty miles of abandoned copper strung on poles that haven't had a linewalker since before I was born." I shrugged, trying to look casual and failing, because my heart was doing the thing it does when I'm right, which is hammering like a key sending SOS. "It read open circuit at the far end. But the near forty miles? Forty-one ohms to the mile, give or take. That wire isn't dead, Edmund. It's *sleeping*. Nobody cut it because nobody remembered it was there. It was the old storm-warning trunk before they built the new line inland. It runs up the coast through six—no, seven—settlements that still have wall-mounted receivers hanging in their general stores because taking them down was more work than leaving them."

Ida had pulled out her notebook and was writing fast. "Seven settlements. Are any of them connected to the main network?"

"That's the beautiful part." I tapped the chart where the old trunk crossed the new line at a junction called Solder's Gap, a place that was technically a town and spiritually a mudflat. "There's a cross-connection box at the gap. Rusty, probably full of spiders, but if it still switches—and junction boxes from that era were built like church bells—it ties our sleeping wire into every living line on the coast."

Ruth was frowning at it all, arms crossed. "So we send a message down a wire nobody's listened to in twenty years, through towns nobody wires anything important to, and somehow that beats a man with a private steamer and a hundred lawyers."

"It beats him because of exactly that," Ida said quietly. "Vane can buy silence. He can't buy *twenty* small towns' worth of operators who heard something strange come down an old wire and wrote it in their logs because that's what operators do. Pip's right. It's not a message. It's a record."

Everyone looked at me. I want to state for posterity that being looked at by those three was the best feeling I'd had since I jumped off the 6:40 out of Bellhaven with one bag and a forged letter of introduction.

"Well," I said. "First problem. The wire sleeps, but it doesn't wake up on its own. There's no current on it. The old battery room at the trunk's origin station burned down years ago—I checked the ash myself, thank you, Edmund, for letting me poke through ruins like a crow."

"I recall encouraging you," Edmund said.

"You recall standing there looking disapproving while I poked through ruins like a crow." I pointed at the Kestrel's engine room, aft. "But we've got wet cells. Ruth's spare bank for the searchlight. Sixteen cells, better voltage than the old trunk ever carried. Give me an hour with the wiring and I can put honest current on forty miles of forgotten copper."

Ruth looked at her battery bank the way a mother looks at a child being recruited into danger. "Those cost me a season's ferry fares."

"They're about to cost Vane an empire," I said. "Seems like a trade."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon while the sky lowered and the wind began to find its voice, coming off the water in long shoves that rocked the Kestrel at her chained mooring. Edmund and I took the dinghy up the estuary to where the old trunk crossed on its last surviving span of poles, and I will be honest: climbing that pole in a rising wind, splicing a tap wire onto copper older than my father, with my teeth chattering and the sea slamming the rocks below—if I'd imagined this life when I ran away from my apprenticeship, I'd have imagined more glory and fewer splinters. But there was a moment, when I closed the splice and Edmund held the tester and the needle swung hard and *alive*, that I made a noise I'm not proud of. Something between a laugh and a yell.

"She sings," I shouted over the wind.

Edmund, who does not shout, held up the tester so I could see the needle standing steady, and for one second he smiled his whole rare smile, and I thought: this is what it's like to matter to something bigger than yourself. The wire hummed under my glove, faint, forty miles of cold copper carrying our borrowed lightning up a coast that had forgotten it.

Then the wind shoved us both sideways and we got back to work, because glory keeps, but daylight doesn't.

* * *

Dusk brought the first spit of rain and worse news. Pip Okonkwo, professional runner of messages, went to run one—on foot, low-key, past the harbor office to count guns—and came back with a count of four, plus a fifth man on the Kestrel's old mooring slip, plus the news that Vane's steamer had shifted anchor to cover the northern channel too. Both exits, watched. And the barometer on Ruth's bulkhead had fallen so far it looked broken.

"They're not trying to catch us tonight," Edmund said, studying the harbor through the glass. "They're herding us. They want us boxed until the storm breaks and the cave floods and the evidence drowns. Then we're four witnesses with nothing to witness."

"Then we transmit tomorrow," Ida said. "Before the gale."

"Tomorrow they'll have men on the junction at Solder's Gap the moment they think of it," Edmund said. "If they think of it."

"Will they?"

He was quiet a moment. "Vane's grandfather sealed a tunnel with seven men inside rather than share a claim. Aldous Vane grew up eating at that table. He thinks in terms of tunnels and doors. Wires—" he glanced at me— "wires are beneath his attention. People who move money don't think about the copper."

"Then we go tonight," I said. "In the dark, in the front edge of the blow, before they've finished being comfortable. Ruth takes the Kestrel north to Solder's Gap, I throw the switch, and we put Crane's seven names down every live line on this coast before midnight."

Ruth looked at the barometer. Looked at the black water slapping the hull. I knew what she was doing—she does it every time the weather turns ugly; I've watched her do it for weeks. She was asking the sea's permission, or at least its opinion. Her husband went out in weather like this once, against her advice, and the sea gave its answer, and she'd been living inside that answer ever since.

"Heavy water in the gap channel," she said finally. "Shoals shift every season. Charts are lies."

"Your charts are lies," Edmund corrected gently. "Yours aren't."

She almost smiled. "One night's grace, sea," she said to the window, quiet, like paying a toll. Then she turned around and became entirely captain. "Pip, you're on the battery bank till it's rigged. Edmund, get Crane's text in final form—short, plain words, names first. Ida, log everything; if this works, the logbook is testimony. We sail on the turn of the tide, which gives us two hours of dark and then no dark at all."

I went aft to the batteries with my soldering kit and my cold hands, and I worked by lantern light while the rain thickened on the deck above, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I wasn't scared.

That frightened me a little, honestly. But underneath the fear-that-wasn't was something steadier: every skill anybody ever taught me, wanted or unwanted, had been waiting years in the dark like that wire, apparently dead, actually humming. The apprenticeship I fled had taught me code. The running away had taught me invisibility. Even Fenwick's dead line, even the long boring evenings practicing fists I'd never use—they'd all been leading here, to a night when a runaway kid with a soldering iron was going to put seven murdered men's names into the ears of a whole coast.

I closed the last connection. The needle stood up straight and true.

Outside, the wind rose another notch, and Ruth's voice came down the companionway, calm as arithmetic:

"Tide's turning. Everybody up. We're going to make some noise."
