## 17. The Kestrel Runs

The Kestrel was built in a year when shipwrights still believed wood ought to argue back, and she has been arguing with the sea ever since. I have owned her nineteen years and buried my husband at her rail, and I will tell you what I told him once, in calmer water: a boat is not a thing you keep. It is a thing you agree to lose, slowly, on terms.

Tonight we renegotiated.

The gale came off the open water in long black ranks, and I took her north with it quartering astern because there was no other direction that didn't mean turning broadside to something with teeth. Edmund lashed himself to the wheel housing beside me without being asked—he'd learned that much about my boat by now—and Pip wedged into the lee of the deckhouse with the telegraph key bolted to a plank across his knees, the wire spliced into the old signal line we'd lifted off the drowned coast two days before. Ida crouched below with the ledger copies, feeding Pip the numbers one town at a time, her handwriting gone ragged from spray and hurry.

"First station's Carrow Point," Pip shouted over the wind. "If the line holds past the trestle."

"If it doesn't?"

"Then we invent a new plan very quickly and pretend we had it all along."

I liked the boy for that. Fear that can make a joke of itself is fear you can steer by.

* * *

You want to know what running the drowned coast at night in a gale is like. It is like reading a letter by lightning—every flash shows you the sentence you're already inside of, and never the one coming.

I knew these shoals the way you know the scarred places on your own hands. My husband Thomas and I had spent ten years feeling our way along this shore with a lead line and a stubbornness the charts never bothered to record, because no chartmaker in his right mind would certify water that rearranged itself twice a day. The drowned coast: old trestles standing knee-deep out of the sea like the ribs of something enormous that died standing up, sandbars that migrated with the seasons, channels that were honest last spring and liars now.

Edmund had mapped them all from my rail in fair weather. Now he called them back to me from memory while the rain tried to scrub the words off the air.

"Trestle span in four hundred yards—port side of it, the gap's maybe forty feet."

"Forty," I said.

"At night. In this."

"Then we'd best not miss."

The Kestrel went through that gap the way a needle goes through cloth, and I felt the trestle timbers comb past us close enough to count their rot, and then we were through and the sea opened its hand again and threw us.

Somewhere behind us, out in the black where the horizon should have been, Vane's steamer showed us her intention: a muzzle flash, a beat of nothing, then the crack arriving late like bad news after the funeral. The shell fell wide, throwing up a column of white that the lightning found and admired.

"They're shooting blind," Edmund said.

"They're shooting rich," I said. "It amounts to the same thing. Hold on."

* * *

Pip worked through it all with his jaw set and his cap tied down, sending by feel more than sight. I could hear the key under the wind—a small, patient, human sound, tap tap tap, like someone knocking politely at twenty doors in a hurricane.

"Carrow Point acknowledges," he shouted at one point, and Ida whooped low, and even Edmund let go of something in his shoulders I hadn't realized he was carrying.

"Eleven miles of wire between here and Carrow," Pip said. "Half of it's older than me. Every mile is a coin toss."

"Then flip them fast," I said, "and don't watch them land."

* * *

We lost the mainmast's forestay an hour past midnight. There was no drama in it—one moment the stay was doing its work, the next it parted with a sound like a piano falling downstairs, and the mast whipped and groaned and held, barely, because the Kestrel was built by people who expected her to be abused. Edmund and Ida got a temporary lashing on it in the worst of it, soaked to the bone, swearing with a fluency that would have gotten them both struck off any church roll in the territory.

A shell burst short off our stern quarter during the lashing. Not close enough to kill. Close enough to teach. Splinters came aboard like angry birds; one of them opened a furrow along Ida's forearm, and she looked at it with what I can only call professional annoyance, wrapped it with a strip of her own shirt, and went back to hauling.

"You're bleeding," Edmund said.

"I'm aware," she said. "I'm also holding the rope. Priorities."

That is the crew I chose. That is the crew that chose me back, though none of us ever said so in daylight.

* * *

The wire ran ahead of us along the coast, station to station, and here is the beautiful, improbable truth of it: the old signal line Edmund had charted off a derelict trestle in fair weather—the line every railroad man assumed was dead copper—still carried current, because the company that abandoned it had been too cheap to pull the wire and too proud to admit the route existed. Nobody alive was watching it. Nobody but a runaway apprentice with quick hands and a grudge against silence.

Pip sent the message in pieces as we ran. The seven names first, always the seven names—Silas Crane, and the six men whose families had spent thirty years believing their fathers and husbands had simply walked off the face of the earth. Then the seam, the order, the date, the signature. Augustus Vane. Sealed with the crew inside.

"Harrow Junction copies," Pip reported. "Saltmere copies. They're asking if it's genuine."

"Tell them to ask Augustus Vane," Ida shouted up through the deckhouse hatch, "and watch his face."

* * *

Dawn did not so much arrive as leak in, gray and grudging, and with it the steamer gained on us. She was bigger, faster, and drier, which are three advantages that count for a great deal everywhere except in the water I was driving through. Her captain knew the open sea. He did not know my coast, and he was learning it at a price I could hear—once, distantly, a grinding shudder and a flare of distress rockets that meant he'd found a bar the hard way. I did not cheer. Well. Not loudly.

But he was not done, and neither were his guns. A shell took the Kestrel amidships just after full light—not a direct hit, but near enough that the hull planking stove in two strakes above the waterline and the sea began, in its patient way, to introduce itself below.

"We're taking water," Ida called up.

"How much?"

"Enough to be rude about it."

I put Edmund on the pump and Ida on the sailcloth and nails, and I kept the wheel, because the wheel was the one job on this boat that could not be shared, the way grief cannot be shared, the way a promise cannot. Thomas had stood at this wheel in weather worse than this—I have his marks worn into the spokes under my hands—and he had taught me the only prayer I've ever trusted, which is not words at all but attention. You attend to the water. You attend to the boat. You attend until attending is the same as living.

Pip never stopped tapping.

* * *

By midmorning we had passed nine towns' worth of wire, and the message was loose on the coast like sparks off a grinder. Saltmere. Harrow Junction. Carrow Point. Little fishing towns and whistle-stop towns, telegraph clerks waking to a story that would empty their offices by noon. Eleven stations confirmed before—

The line died at Bracken Inlet. We heard it die: Pip sent, and sent again, and the key answered him with the particular dead silence of copper parted somewhere in the dark, and his shoulders came down like a dropped sail.

"Eleven," he said. His voice cracked on it. "Eleven towns. The rest of the line's gone—or they cut it, or the storm—" He looked at me with a boy's naked fear. "Is eleven enough? Eleven out of twenty. Is that enough?"

I thought of Thomas, and of the sea, which takes what it takes and leaves you to do arithmetic with the remainder.

"Pip," I said, "when I buried my husband, half the county came and half stayed home. I spent a year thinking the ones who stayed home hadn't heard. Then a woman I'd never met crossed the whole territory to tell me how he'd pulled her brother's boat off a bar in '81. Half was plenty. Half was always going to be plenty. Eleven towns have those names now, and names are the one cargo nobody can sink."

He wiped his face with a sleeve that was mostly salt anyway, and picked up the dead key, and set it down again gently, like closing a friend's eyes.

* * *

We came off the drowned coast at noon with the gale still worrying our wake, the Kestrel listing to port and drinking through her wounds, and Vane's steamer somewhere behind us—crippled, I judged, or prudent, or both. The harbor at Saltmere opened ahead, gray and ordinary and the finest thing I have ever seen, and I brought her in on the flood with a hull that would need a fortnight's work and a crew that needed none of it.

Edmund stood beside me as the breakwater slid past. He was soaked through, hollow-eyed, and there was blood on his hands from the pump handles, and he looked—there is no better word for it—unburied. Like a man walking out of his own grave and squinting at the light.

"Eleven towns," he said.

"Eleven," I said.

"And Vane knows it."

"Oh, he knows it." I eased the wheel over. "And every clerk in those eleven towns knows it, and their cousins, and their Sunday congregations, and by tomorrow the newspapers that laugh at everything will be printing those seven names because printing them sells papers and ignoring them costs more." I looked at him. "You can't drown a story in eleven harbors, Mr. Joubert. Believe me. I've tried to drown things in one, and failed."

The Kestrel kissed the dock with a sound like a tired animal lying down. Ida jumped ashore with her bandaged arm and her chin up, and Pip came last, cradling the dead telegraph key like a relic, and behind us the storm rolled on toward the coast, toward the cave, toward the Landfall, keeping its appointment.

And far down the harbor front, a boy was already running—running flat-out, waving a flimsy yellow paper over his head, shouting words I couldn't catch yet but already knew by heart.

Seven names. Ours to carry. His to shout.
