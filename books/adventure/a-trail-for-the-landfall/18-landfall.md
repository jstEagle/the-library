## 18. Landfall

The tide came back the way debts do—slowly, then all at once.

I stood at the mouth of the Landfall with Silas Crane's ledger pressed flat against my chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and watched the sea undo in an hour what it had taken a season to give. The morning's low water had been a lie the coast told to catch men like me. Now the truth was rolling home in long gray swells, and behind me, down the passage, seven men lay under railroad spikes waiting to see what I was made of.

Vane's steamer sat off the point like a knife laid on a table, its lights coming up as the light went down. Between us and it, along the shingle, came six of his men with lanterns and rifles, walking the way men walk when they've been paid enough to stop asking questions but not enough to stop being afraid.

I could have run. The escape passage Crane carved was at my back; Ida and Pip were somewhere above on the cliff path with the copies, and Ruth had the Kestrel out in the worst water on the coast doing God knew what kind of foolishness for our sakes. I could have gone into the dark and let the cave swallow everything—the journal, the seam, the graves—and come out the other side of this life as nobody again, which had been my profession for eleven years and which I'd been tolerably good at.

Instead I stayed where I was, because I had finally understood something about myself that took a cave, a storm, and a dead man's letter to teach me: I was tired of being a man the map forgot.

"Mr. Joubert." Aldous Vane came up the shingle last, past his own guns, and I'll grant him this—he walked in front of them, not behind. He was smaller than his reputation, the way most terrible things are. A neat gray coat, a face like a bank door, and eyes that did all the arithmetic before you finished speaking. "You've cost me two boats, a telegraph office, and a very great deal of patience."

"You're welcome," I said. "It's the first honest work any of us has done in years."

He smiled. It was a good smile, the kind that had closed a hundred deals and probably a few coffins. "I made you an offer yesterday. I'm given to understand you burned nothing and signed nothing, which I confess surprised me. Most men would have taken the homestead."

"Most men aren't standing in a graveyard."

"They're spikes in rock, Mr. Joubert. Men die. Railroads are built over them every day. That is rather the entire principle of railroads." He stepped closer, and the first swell broke against the cave mouth behind me, throwing spray across the old survey camp's threshold. "The papers. The letter. Whatever copies your charming little crew has scratched out by lamplight. Give them to me, and by Thursday you'll hold the deed to land your father bled for, free of liens, free of courts, free of me. You'll be a landowner. A name on a county roll. Isn't that what you people want?"

*You people.* Eleven years walking closed lines had taught me to hear that phrase in all its dialects.

"No," I said.

His smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a recalculation, quick as a switch thrown. "No?"

"It's not what I want anymore. And it wouldn't be yours to give anyway, because here's the trouble with your offer, Mr. Vane: I don't have the originals. They're already moving. Have been since dawn." That was half a bluff and half a prayer, and I delivered both halves in the same voice, which is the only trick I ever learned worth passing on. "Your men cut the harbor wire. Pip Okonkwo put the message on the old signal line instead—the one your grandfather's company abandoned in '94 and never bothered to pull down. Twenty stations. Twenty towns. By now it's reached eleven of them if it's reached one, and every operator who tapped it through has read seven names into the public record."

I named them. All seven. Silas Crane first, because he'd earned first position on any roster he kept, and then the other six, and I watched the names go through those hired men like current through a dead wire—one or two of them flinched, and one, a big fellow with a scarred jaw, actually took his hat off before catching himself and putting it back on like a man embarrassed by his own decency.

"The ore seam stays buried," I said. "The cave takes the journal back tonight—that's between the sea and the dead. But the testimony doesn't drown. You can't buy back eleven towns, Mr. Vane. There isn't money enough in your grandfather's whole stolen fortune."

The tide shoved another swell into the cave mouth, deep this time, and the sound it made against the inner walls was the sound of a door closing slowly. Water was already sheeting across the shingle toward our boots.

Vane looked at me for a long moment, and then he did the thing I will remember about him longer than anything else: he turned to his own men.

"Well?" he said. "Seal it."

They didn't move.

I mean that literally. Six men with rifles, and not one of them shifted his weight. The scarred-jaw fellow spoke without turning around. "Seal it how, sir? With him in it?"

"With whatever's necessary."

"That's a tunnel full of graves, sir." He said it evenly, the way a man reports weather. "And there's a message gone out to eleven towns saying we were seen on this coast with orders to make sure nobody came back from it. I've got a sister in Harrowgate. She reads the telegrams posted at the station same as everybody."

Another swell. Higher. The water found our feet, cold as a creditor's hand.

Vane's voice dropped into something quieter and worse. "I am paying each of you more than you'd see in five years of honest labor—"

"And we took it," the man said. "Wages is wages. But there's wages and there's the other thing, and my father worked the Vane lines thirty years, and he always said the only difference between the Vane family and the tide is the tide comes back honest." He shouldered his rifle—not aimed, just carried, which somehow made the point better than aiming it. "We're not sealing anybody in a hole, sir. Not tonight. Not for you."

For a moment Aldous Vane stood alone on a beach of his own making, and I watched power do the thing power does when its servants stop believing in it: nothing. It stood there. It got wet. It discovered it had no hands except the ones other people lent it.

He looked at me then, and for just a second the bank-door face opened, and what was behind it wasn't rage. It was something almost like relief, the look of a man who has carried a debt so long that being called to pay it is nearly a kindness. Then it shut again, and he straightened his coat.

"You'll ruin yourself," he said. "Lawyers, courts, years of it. I have ruined better men than you for less."

"Probably," I agreed. "But they weren't standing in a graveyard when you tried."

The water was at our knees now, and the cave behind me was filling with a sound like the whole coast clearing its throat. Somewhere out in the dark I heard it—the Kestrel's engine, ragged but running, Ruth bringing her boat home through water she had no business surviving. Above on the cliff path, a lantern swung. Ida. Pip. Coming down to stand where I stood, because that was the whole plan and had been from the beginning: not to hide the truth, but to outnumber it.

Vane's men waded back up the shingle without being dismissed. All six. The scarred-jaw fellow paused beside me, close enough that only I heard him.

"Harrowgate's my town," he said. "If the name in the telegram's true—Josiah Finch, fireman—I helped lay his headstone when I was a boy. My mother knew his mother." He looked at the spike-marked graves inside the darkening cave, then at me. "Read them again next spring tide. Somebody ought to hear it who isn't being paid."

Then he was gone up the beach after the others, and Vane's steamer began, at last, to turn—slowly, without dignity, like a man leaving a church service early.

I stood in the rising water among the dead men's doorway and felt the ledger against my chest, going back where it belonged. The sea took the shingle first, then the threshold, then the lantern-light off the walls, and the Landfall closed its admission behind me without charging me anything but the truth.

Above, Ida's lantern reached the bottom of the cliff path, and her voice came down through the wind, breathless and fierce:

"Edmund—Pip says the line died at twelve towns—but the *newspapers*—there's a boy in Harrowgate already selling extras—"

I started climbing toward the light.

* * *

The Kestrel limped into the lee of the point an hour past midnight, listing to port and wearing her rail like a broken rib, and Ruth Ellery brought her in the way she does everything—without one wasted motion and without one word until the anchor bit. Then she looked up at the four of us crowded on the cliff, wet through and grinning like idiots, and said:

"Somebody owes me a new jib and the complete truth, in that order."

We gave her the second while we worked on the first, and by gray dawn, with the storm season's first real gale moaning offshore and the Landfall drowned fathoms deep, we sat in her cabin among charts and coffee and listened to Pip read the last confirmation off the repaired harbor wire, voice cracking on the eleventh repetition:

"*Harrowgate Gazette, front page: SEVEN NAMES IN THE SEA—WHO BURIED THE LANDFALL CREW?*"

Nobody spoke for a while. Outside, the gale took the first real swing at the coast, and the Kestrel rode it, and we rode with her.

Ruth poured the coffee. Ida spread the copied pages flat and smoothed them with both hands, the way you'd smooth a wound dressing. And I thought about a man named Josiah Finch, fireman, whose headstone some boy had helped lay forty years ago, and whose name was being shouted in the streets this morning by children who didn't know him either.

The wilderness forgives nothing and forgets less. I'd built a whole philosophy on that. But standing there in the cabin of a damaged boat with three exiles who had chosen me, I finally understood the rest of it: neither does it forgive nor forget the living. Every line you walk, somebody walks with you, whether you asked them to or not.

Pip looked up from the wire. "There's more," they said. "It's a summons. Court of inquiry, sitting in three weeks." A pause, and then, carefully: "They're asking for witnesses. By name."

Ida met my eyes across the chart table.

Mine was on the list. For the first time in eleven years, somebody official wanted Edmund Joubert exactly where he was.
