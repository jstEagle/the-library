## 20. Inquiries

The courthouse in Port Meridian smelled of varnish, wet wool, and the particular anxiety of men who had never before been asked to account for anything. I noticed all three smells within a minute of walking in, because noticing things is my trade. A cartographer who misses a detail redraws the world wrong; a witness who misses one simply lies by omission, which is apparently more forgivable and pays worse.

They had set up the hearing room in the old customs hall, which was fitting. For forty years that room had existed to decide what came into the country and what stayed out—cargo, contraband, people. Now it would decide what came into the public record: seven names that Augustus Vane had shipped out of history in 1894 and never paid duty on.

I wore my good dress, which is to say the one without ink on the cuffs. Under my arm I carried the survey portfolio Ida Marsh, struck-off cartographer, had once been forbidden to carry into any official building in the territory. Inside it were my charts, my field notes, and a copy of a dead man's ledger rendered in my own careful hand.

My full name, as entered on the witness roll, read *Ida Crane Marsh*.

I had not signed that name in public since I was nine years old, standing at my grandmother's knee while she taught me to letter coastlines. She said a name on a map was a promise: whoever drew it was swearing the line was true. Then she died, and the family name thinned out through marriages and moves until it was just something in a Bible nobody opened, and I went off to make maps under a name that got me struck off the rolls for the crime of telling a shipping company their harbor chart was wrong.

So there was a certain symmetry in being called back to testify precisely because of a name.

* * *

The commissioner presiding was a federal man named Halloway, dry as old biscuit, with spectacles he polished when he wanted to avoid looking at you. He'd been sent north because the territorial judges were all, in one way or another, Vane men, and everyone in the hall understood this without anyone saying it. The whole proceeding had the delicate quality of a dance performed on a floor everyone suspected of being rotten.

Counsel for the inquiry—a sharp young woman named Okafor whom the newspapers had already nicknamed "the government's terrier"—opened with the ledger.

"Mrs. Marsh. You state that this document is a true copy of a ledger recovered from the sea cave known locally as the Landfall."

"True in every particular," I said. "Including its errors. Silas Crane made three arithmetic mistakes in his ore tonnage estimates. I've marked them in the margins rather than correct them, because a copyist's job is fidelity, not flattery."

Someone in the gallery laughed. Halloway did not look up from his papers, but I saw the corner of his mouth consider it.

Okafor walked me through it the way you walk someone through a coastline in fog—one bearing at a time. The seam of copper-nickel ore, rich enough to have built a second railroad on its profits alone. The tunnel orders, dated and initialed. The payroll record showing seven men carried on the books through the winter of '94 and struck off in a single entry the following spring, annotated in Augustus Vane's own hand: *expedition concluded, no survivors, claim consolidated.*

"No survivors," Okafor repeated. "But there were survivors. Just none he intended."

"There were seven graves," I said. "Marked with railroad spikes. Silas Crane dug them—or started to. His journal suggests he was sealing the tunnel on Vane's orders while knowing what was under the seal, and that the knowledge killed him faster than the cave ever could have. He stayed behind to write it down. He knew the tide schedule better than anyone alive, and he chose the night the Landfall charges admission."

The gallery had gone very quiet. In the back row, Pip sat with their hands folded like a churchgoer, which for Pip is a posture requiring enormous discipline. Ruth stood against the wall with her arms crossed, hat brim low, watching the doors. Edmund was outside, having been advised by counsel that the man who'd refused Vane's bribe made a better absence than a presence until the deed question was settled. He'd hated the advice and taken it anyway, which tells you how much the stakes had grown teeth.

"And your connection to Silas Crane?" Okafor asked, though she knew the answer. Everyone in the room knew the answer by then; the newspapers had gotten hold of it eleven towns ago and wrung it dry. But hearings run on the record, not on gossip, so I said it plainly.

"He was my great-grandfather's brother. His daughter, Alice Crane, married a Marsh. Her daughter was my mother." I set my hand flat on the portfolio. "Alice Crane spent thirty years writing to railroads, land offices, and two governors asking what happened to her father. Every reply is in this file. They all say the same thing: expedition concluded, no records maintained. She died in 1911 believing her father had abandoned her, or worse—that he'd been incompetent, and the company had quietly absorbed his failure. She never knew he left her a letter. It took a runaway telegraph operator, a disgraced ferry captain, a man who mapped closed lines to stay invisible, and me—the granddaughter she raised on stories of a coast she wasn't allowed to chart—to find it."

I hadn't meant to say quite all of that. Wryness is my armor and I'd let it slip. But the gallery didn't laugh, and Halloway stopped polishing his spectacles, and Okafor, terrier that she was, gave me half a second of plain human silence before she moved on.

* * *

Vane's counsel arrived on the third day, and he arrived the way weather arrives: expensively, and ruining everyone's plans.

His name was Corliss, and he had the polished, apologetic manner of a man whose job is to be disliked on behalf of someone richer. He cross-examined me for four hours, and I will grant him this: he never once raised his voice. He didn't need to. His technique was repetition—asking the same question eleven ways hoping the answer would shift a degree, the way a current shifts a buoy.

"You copied this ledger by lantern light, in a flooding sea cave, under conditions of extreme stress?"

"I did."

"Stress affects memory."

"Stress affects memory of things you're trying to remember," I agreed. "What I was doing was transcribing. There's a difference, and it's the difference between my profession and yours. You remember arguments. I remember lines."

"And yet the original document is now—" he consulted his notes with theatrical regret— "unavailable. Destroyed. Submerged. Convenient."

"The original journal and ledger are inside the Landfall, behind fifty feet of seawater, exactly where Augustus Vane put them in 1894," I said. "If their location is convenient, sir, the convenience is his. We merely failed to improve upon it."

That got a laugh from the gallery that Halloway declined to gavel down, and I saw Corliss recalibrate. He switched tactics, as they always do, and went for the crew.

"Your associates include a cartographer removed from the professional rolls—"

"That would be me."

"—a telegraph operator of no fixed employment, a ferry captain with a documented record of running customs blockades during the herring disputes, and an Edmund Joubert, described in Vane Industries' own correspondence as 'a drifter of no account who surveys dead lines.'" Corliss spread his hands. "Mrs. Marsh, why should this commission accept the testimony of four people with every incentive to fabricate, regarding documents no one can produce?"

It was a good question. It deserved a good answer, and I'd had weeks to prepare one, so I gave him the only kind that matters.

"Because we don't ask you to take our word. Ask the eleven towns. On the night of the ninth, a message went down every working wire on the coast at once—twenty stations were targeted, eleven received it complete. Station masters logged it. Operators kept carbons. Newspaper boys shouted it in the streets before Vane's lawyers could finish their breakfast. Seven names, Mr. Corliss. Aldous Vane's own grandfather's payroll, read aloud from Wickhaven to Gray's Landing. You can subpoena any one of those towns. We scattered the truth specifically so that no one—not us, not you, not your client—could gather it back up and put it in his pocket. Fabrication doesn't work that way. Fabrication wants control. We gave the thing away."

Corliss had no answer for that, because there isn't one. Power's whole architecture rests on the assumption that truth can be owned, sealed, flooded, bought. Hand it to twenty stationmasters at once and the architecture develops a crack you can hear from the gallery.

Halloway adjourned early that day. On the courthouse steps, a newspaper boy was selling extras, and I bought one I didn't need just to put a coin in his hand. The headline ran seven names across the top in type tall enough to read from a moving train.

Pip appeared at my elbow, reading over my shoulder. "They spelled Okonkwo right," they said. "First time in my life anybody in print has spelled it right."

"Enjoy it," I said. "It's downhill from here. Next they'll get your age wrong."

"They already did. Says I'm nineteen." Pip grinned. "I'm keeping it."

* * *

The verdicts, such as they were, came down over the following fortnight, and they came down the way water comes through a cracked dam—not all at once, but in a way you could no longer stop.

The commission found that Augustus Vane had ordered the unlawful deaths of seven employees and the concealment of a mineral claim; that Aldous Vane Industries had knowingly employed means to suppress evidence, including the hiring of armed men against a civilian survey party; and that the Landfall claim itself, being founded on concealed homicide, reverted to the territory. The last finding was the one that mattered most, because claims are money and money is the only language empires are fluent in. Everything else was shame, and shame washes off. Reversion stuck.

Aldous Vane himself did not appear. He sent Corliss, then he sent junior counsel, then he sent no one, which the newspapers correctly read as an empire changing its mind about the value of the territory. The steamer was seen heading south with its flags half-hoisted, if you believe the harbor gossip, and I do, because harbor gossip is more reliable than harbor charts.

The dominoes fell in order after that. Two territorial legislators resigned before they could be named. A bank in the capital called Vane paper it had been politely ignoring for a decade. The shipping concern whose bad harbor chart had gotten me struck off the rolls suddenly discovered, via its own lawyers, that I had been entirely correct in 1908, and offered reinstatement with an apology so carefully worded it apologized mainly for the inconvenience to them. I filed the apology. I'll decide about the rolls later, or I won't. Being struck off taught me who my friends were, and I'm slow to unlearn a lesson that useful.

And then, on a gray Tuesday with rain coming in sideways off the harbor, the court issued its order in the matter of the Joubert homestead.

I was there when Edmund was served—not served, exactly; the clerk simply handed him the document the way you hand someone a hot dish, quickly and without meeting their eyes. Edmund read it standing in the corridor, and I watched his face do something complicated.

The homestead his father had lost—seized over debts that the record now showed had been manufactured by a Vane land agent clearing smallholders away from a spur line that was never built—was returned by court order. Restored. With costs.

Unsought. That was the part that sat strangely on him. He had spent years wanting that land back, wanting it the way you want a tooth you've lost, an ache with a shape. And now here it was, handed over by the same machinery that had taken it, without his asking, without his paying, without even the dignity of a fight. A gift from a system that had mugged him.

"Well," he said finally, folding the order along its existing creases, twice, the way a man handles something he doesn't trust himself to hold. "It's mine."

"It always was," I said. "The map just caught up."

He looked at me then, and there was something raw under the wryness, some grief for his father arriving decades late with interest. "He died believing he'd failed. Same as Alice Crane." He tapped the folded paper against his palm. "These courts keep handing out vindications to dead people. It's generous of them. It's also too late to matter to anyone who needed it."

"It matters to the living," I said. "We're the ones who carry the names."

He didn't answer that, but he stopped folding the paper, and later I saw him carrying it flat, in the portfolio, next to my charts. Which from Edmund Joubert is practically a speech.

Ruth took the findings harder than any of us, in her quiet way. The Kestrel's damage from the run down the coast had been patched in the Meridian yards—new planking forward, a scarred bulwark where Vane's steamer's wake of gunfire had kissed her—and she supervised the work personally and said almost nothing for two weeks. When the commission's report named the men who had fired on her boat, she read the list once, folded the newspaper in quarters, and used it to light the galley stove.

"Some things go in the record," she said, when Pip asked. "Some things go in the fire. A captain learns the difference or she drowns of it."

Pip, meanwhile, had become briefly famous, which is the worst thing that can happen to a teenager. The territorial telegraph board—scandalized less by the message than by the fact that a fourteen-year-old had executed it flawlessly on equipment officially decommissioned—offered a formal apprenticeship, with papers, with a salary, with a supervisor. Pip brought the offer to the Kestrel's cabin and read it aloud in the tone of voice usually reserved for ransom demands.

"'Proper supervision,'" they repeated. "I ran a message through eleven towns under gunfire. What exactly would he supervise?"

"Your spelling," Ruth said, not looking up from her engine logs. "It's criminal."

"I'm taking it," Pip announced, to general surprise, including their own. "On conditions. I want the old signal-line routes added to the maintenance rolls—all the wires everybody forgot, the ones that saved our lives. If I'm going to learn the trade properly, I want to learn all of it, including the parts the trade pretends don't exist." They looked around the cabin, daring anyone to object. "Somebody has to know where the dead lines are. Otherwise the next crew trapped in a harbor has nothing."

Edmund smiled at that, small and private, the smile of a man watching his own philosophy come back wearing a younger face. The wilderness forgets nothing. Neither, now, would the telegraph rolls.

* * *

On the last day of the hearings, Halloway called me back for one final formality: authentication of the transcript of Crane's letter, the one to his daughter, which had been read into the record the week prior. I signed the certification—*Ida Crane Marsh*, the pen dragging a little on the damp paper—and the clerk blotted it, and that was that. My grandmother's father's words, sworn into the permanent record of the territory, between a freight tariff dispute and a boundary complaint.

Walking out, I passed the customs hall's old manifest boards, the brass slots where incoming cargo had once been declared. Empty now, most of them. But the commission had posted its findings in one slot, typed on a card, available to any citizen: seven names, one crime, one restitution.

I stood there longer than I meant to. Vindication, I had learned, is a strange cargo. You spend years imagining how it will feel—heavy, sweet, triumphant—and when it arrives it turns out to weigh almost nothing and taste mostly like weather. The dead don't rise. Alice Crane stays dead. The best you get is that the lie stops traveling, and the truth, having been scattered to eleven towns like seed, starts coming up green in places you'll never see.

That's not triumph. It's bookkeeping. But honest bookkeeping is the whole trade, and I'll take it.

Outside, the rain had cleared and the light was doing that northern thing it does in late autumn, long and silver and merciless. Edmund was waiting on the steps with his collar up, holding two cups of the terrible coffee from the stand by the ferry landing, and he handed me one without comment, which is his version of eloquence.

"So," he said. "The charts."

I looked at him. "What about them?"

"The coastal surveys. All of them—the official ones, the old ones, mine, yours—are wrong in the same places, because they all descend from the same Vane-commissioned originals. The shoals Ruth threaded, the signal lines, the closed spurs, the Landfall itself." He blew on his coffee. "The territory's going to need new charts. Accurate ones. Drawn by somebody with no reason to flatter anybody." A pause, and then, carefully casual, the way he offers everything that matters: "I heard the board is taking applications."

I drank the terrible coffee and watched the harbor, where the Kestrel rode at her moorings with fresh white planking forward, scarred and sound, and where somewhere under all that gray water a cave kept its dead and its secrets and charged admission still.

"Applications," I said. "Well. Someone will have to make sure they spell Okonkwo right."
