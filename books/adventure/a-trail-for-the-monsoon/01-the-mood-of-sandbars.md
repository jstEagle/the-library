## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The river tells you its mood through the sandbars. Most captains read the water itself — the color, the current seams, the little wrinkles where a shoal is thinking about becoming an island. That's all well and good for the fair-weather crowd with their depth sounders and their paid pilots. But I learned early that the bars are the river's honest men. The water lies; the sand remembers.

That morning the bars were in a chatty mood.

"Half a fathom less than yesterday," Ilsa said at my shoulder, not looking up from her lead line. She'd been swinging it off the bow every thirty seconds since we cleared the last bend, and her voice had settled into that flat rhythm she used when she was counting something she didn't like.

"I heard."

"You didn't hear, you felt me say it. There's a difference."

"There is," I agreed, "and one of them keeps us off the mud." I eased the Kestrel's wheel two spokes to starboard, feeling for the current seam I knew lived just off Gull Point on a falling tide. The boat answered the way she always did — a half-beat late, like a woman being asked to dance by someone she'd already made up her mind about.

The tide was going out and taking our margin with it. Six hours ago there had been nine feet over Marrow Bar; now the channel markers were leaning like drunkards and the water between them had gone the color of weak tea, which meant silt, which meant the river was rearranging its furniture again. Somewhere down there, a sandbar was either dying or being born, and nobody had told me which.

This was the part of the work I loved, if I'm honest, and I try to be honest about the parts I love because there are so few of them worth lying about. Not the cargo runs, not the money — gods, never the money — but this: the narrow minutes when the Kestrel and I were one argument away from disaster and winning it anyway. A hundred tons of patched iron and older wood threading a channel that existed only as a rumor in my head.

"Marker four's gone," called Tam from the bow, young Tam, who still said things like they were news instead of weather.

"Gone or buried?"

"Buried. I can see the top of it."

"Then the bar's grown toward the north bank since spring." I did the arithmetic without wanting to. North bank growth meant the deep channel had migrated south, which meant the shortcut every other captain would take this morning would put them squarely on the new sand. Which meant, if I played it right, the Kestrel would be the only boat making the low-tide run today, and the only boat collecting the premium that came with it.

"South of the old line?" Ilsa asked. She already knew. She always already knew; asking was her way of checking whether I knew too.

"South of the old line. Give me the lead."

She swung it. "By the mark, five!" A pause, the rope hissing back up dripping. "And a half, five!"

Five and a half feet under a keel that drew five. A half-foot of grace, which sounds generous until you remember that a river's grace is measured in inches and spent in seconds.

I brought her through slow, engine barely above idle, letting the current do the steering the way you let a large animal do most of the walking. The Kestrel slid over Marrow Bar with maybe three inches to spare — I felt it in the wheel more than heard it, that faint thickening of the water's grip, the river touching bottom the way you touch a bruise to check it's still yours.

And then we were through, into the deeper green below, and Tam whooped, and Ilsa said "Hm," which from Ilsa was a standing ovation.

"Don't get sentimental," I told her.

"Wouldn't dream of it, Captain."

* * *

There are eleven people aboard the Kestrel when we're fully crewed, and I could find any one of them blindfolded by sound alone. Tam's boots hit the deck like he's apologizing to it. Old Piet snores in the engine room even while he's awake. And Ilsa moves quiet as a debt coming due.

We ran cargo that morning — rice and salt, nothing romantic — downriver to Corven's Landing, and the run went clean, and clean runs pay, and what clean runs pay goes out again almost before it lands. I stood at the rail watching the landing come up and did the sums I always do, the ones that end the same way no matter how I shuffle them. Fuel. Wages. The hull patch Piet swore would hold through monsoon season and I chose to believe him because the alternative was knowing. Dock fees. Interest.

Interest. The word has teeth if you say it enough times.

"Captain," Ilsa said, materializing beside me. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're adding numbers and losing."

"I don't lose at adding."

"No," she agreed. "You lose at having."

I laughed despite myself. Twenty years she'd been my quartermaster, back to before — back to the beginning, whatever the beginning was. There were whole stretches of my past that came to me secondhand these days, through Ilsa's telling, like stories about another woman. But Ilsa herself was bedrock. Whatever else the river took, it hadn't taken her.

"We'll make the Corven delivery, take the salt contract back up, and we're fine through the month," I said.

"And after the month?"

"After the month the monsoon comes, and nobody sails, and we live on air and loyalty."

"Loyalty's gone up in price," Ilsa said dryly. "Air's still free."

"That's why I deal in both."

She looked at me a moment longer than the joke required. Ilsa has a way of looking at you that makes you feel audited. "There's a man on the dock," she said finally. "Been there since we made the bend. Grey coat, grey hat, stands like he owns the ground under him. He asked the harbormaster which boat was the Kestrel."

My stomach performed a small, professional maneuver. "Debt collector?"

"He's carrying a ledger."

"They always carry ledgers. It's how they make you feel the weight before they quote it."

"His ledger's got a red ribbon in it," she said. "Fresh one. Like a bookmark in a book somebody reads often."

I watched the dock come closer. Grey coat. Grey hat. A man built like a filing cabinet, hands folded in front of him with the patience of someone paid by the hour to wait. Beside him, the harbormaster gestured our way with the enthusiasm of a man selling out a friend cheaply.

"Well," I said. "At least he waited for low tide to ruin my day. Some courtesy in that."

"You want me to handle him?"

I considered it. Ilsa handling a debt collector generally ended with the debt collector needing a minute and a drink, and while that was satisfying, it bought exactly one minute and cost goodwill we couldn't spare. Men like this didn't work alone; they worked for institutions, and institutions kept score in ways fists couldn't reach.

"No," I said. "I'll handle him. You handle the lines. Let's give Corven's Landing a nice show of a ship coming in tidy, so nobody watches the captain get audited on her own gangway."

* * *

Docking the Kestrel is the one thing I do better than reading water, and I say that as a woman who once threaded her father's boat through the Narrows with a broken wheel and a borrowed tiller. We came alongside soft as a kiss, lines over, fenders kissing, and I was on the gangway before the grey-coated man had finished unfolding himself to his full and unremarkable height.

He was younger than his coat suggested. Forty, maybe, with the kind of face designed to be forgotten — average brow, average chin, eyes the color of dishwater. Only his hands betrayed anything, and only because they were very still. Men who move fast are nervous. Men who don't move at all are dangerous.

"Captain Underhill." He said it like he'd practiced it, which meant he'd been told to say it correctly, which meant whoever sent him cared about the details. I filed that away and hated it.

"You have the advantage of me."

"Merrick. Acting for the Consolidated Harbor Trust." He produced a card I didn't take. "I'll be brief, as I understand your schedule is tidal."

"How thoughtful."

"Your vessel carries liens against three separate notes, totaling — " he consulted the red-ribboned ledger without opening it, which was a flourish, and a good one — "a sum I suspect you know by heart."

"I know it to the penny," I said. "I also know the Trust restructured those notes in the spring, extended terms, and accepted payment in kind against the salt contracts. So unless the Trust has developed a sudden interest in punctuality, you've come about something else."

Something flickered behind the dishwater eyes. Recalculation. I'd been told once — by whom, I couldn't anymore say, one of those secondhand memories — that I negotiated like a river: patient, persistent, wearing down whatever stood in the channel. I decided to believe it.

"The Trust," Merrick said, "has been approached by a third party regarding your obligations."

And there it was.

"A third party," I repeated. "The Trust doesn't sell paper. The Trust holds paper until it hurts."

"The Trust," said Merrick, "is not monolithic. And the third party offered terms the relevant parties found — attractive." He closed the ledger, gently, like tucking in a child. "You should expect a visitor, Captain. Someone you know, I'm given to understand."

"Everyone I know owes me money. It's usually the other direction."

"Then perhaps it's someone you knew." He tipped the grey hat a precise inch. "Enjoy the falling tide, Captain. It won't wait, and neither, now, will anyone else."

He walked down the dock without hurry, a man whose errand was complete, and I stood on my own gangway with the salt wind pulling at my collar and did not watch him go, because watching men leave gives them importance.

Ilsa appeared at my elbow with the manifest clipboard held like a shield. "Well?"

"We're fine through the month," I said.

"And after?"

"After the month," I said, "someone I used to know is coming to buy my debts and call it generosity."

She was quiet a moment. The tide sucked softly at the pilings beneath us, going out, going out, taking the day's margin with it. Out on the channel, the water over Marrow Bar had dropped another handspan since we'd crossed, and somewhere south, past the delta fingers, past the drowned country where the charts went vague and personal, a wreck sat in the shallows that everyone I trusted had sworn we'd never speak of again.

I didn't know yet why the name Meridian rose in my throat like a swallowed stone. I didn't know yet that the grey man's ledger and my father's grave and that sworn-off wreck were all tied together with the same red ribbon.

I only knew that the river had changed its mood, and I'd learned long ago to trust that instinct over any amount of paperwork.

"Get us loaded," I told Ilsa. "Salt contract, then home. I want to be underway before the turn."

"Captain," she said, and hesitated — Ilsa, who never hesitated — "whoever's coming. Do you want me there when he does?"

I looked out at the falling water, at the bars showing their backs like sleeping animals, at the honest sand telling truths the pretty surface wouldn't.

"No," I said. "Whoever it is, he's coming to talk to the woman I was. I'd rather he got the one I am."

But that night, coiling the lines alone, I caught myself listening to the river the way you listen to a house at night — and far down the channel, where the water went dark and the charts went thin, I could almost swear I heard something turning over on the sandbars, getting comfortable, settling in to wait for me.
