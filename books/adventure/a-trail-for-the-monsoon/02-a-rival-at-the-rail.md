## 2. A Rival at the Rail

Dario Fenwick came up the dock at slack water, which told me everything about him before he opened his mouth. A man who times his arrivals for when the river can't argue with him.

He wore a linen coat the color of unbleached sailcloth, spotless, and shoes that had never once been soaked in anything more threatening than spilled wine. Twenty years on the water and the man still looked like he'd been decanted from a bottle. He stopped at the foot of the Kestrel's gangplank and tipped his hat — an actual hat, with a band — to Ilsa, who looked at him the way she looks at a snag in the channel: something to be noted and then gone around.

"Captain Underhill." His voice carried the way voices do when they've been trained in rooms with high ceilings. "You're looking well."

"I'm looking busy," I said. "That's better."

"So it is. So it is." He came up the plank without waiting for an invitation, because Dario has never in his life waited for an invitation, and settled his hands on my rail the way a man settles into a chair he believes he owns. He gazed out at the harbor with an expression of fond appraisal, as though the mud and the mooring chains and the fish guts were all arranged for his pleasure.

I gave him thirty seconds. It was generous.

"Whatever you're selling, Fenwick, I'm not buying. The last thing you sold me cost me a season's profit and a tooth."

"You bit the man I sent to collect, if I recall."

"I recall it differently. Memory's a funny thing." I said it lightly. I don't know why I said it that way. Some jokes you make before you know they're true.

"Memory is indeed a funny thing." He said it softly, and something in the softness made the hair on my forearms stand up, though I'd have sworn on my father's charts that it was only the river breeze.

He turned then and looked at me properly, and I remembered — the way you remember a fever — that Dario Fenwick had been handsome once, in the way of a well-drawn chart that turns out to be forty years out of date. The lines had gone fine around his eyes. The eyes themselves hadn't changed at all. They were patient eyes. Patient the way a fishhook is patient.

"I'm not here to sell you anything, Coral. I'm here to give you something." He let that sit. "Salvage rights to the Meridian."

The name went through me like cold water through a hull seam — quick, and then everywhere.

I want to tell you I didn't react. I want to tell you my face stayed a locked door. But the truth is my hand went to the rail and my knuckles did the talking, and Dario watched them do it with the mild satisfaction of a man who has bet on the right horse.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need the terms. The Meridian is sworn off. By me, by my crew, by every captain who worked that reach. That wreck has cost this river enough."

"That wreck," Dario said, "is sitting in eleven fathoms of water with her strongroom intact, and the monsoon makes landfall in under a fortnight, and after the monsoon she'll be a rumor and a scatter of plating. The window is now. The rights are yours if you want them."

"Then they're wasted on me."

"Are they?" He smiled. It was a good smile. It had opened a great many doors and closed a great many others. "I wonder. Because I've been reading the harbor master's ledger, Coral, and I've been reading the chandlers' books, and I've been reading the book of a certain gentleman named Corvo who lends money to captains in the off-season and collects in ways I won't describe over breakfast."

The harbor went quiet around me. Not actually — the gulls kept up their racket, and someone two piers over was swearing at an engine — but the quiet that matters, the one inside.

"Your debt," Dario said, "stands at four hundred and twelve marks, principal and interest, and Corvo has sold the paper. Do you know who holds it now?"

"I expect you're about to tell me."

"I am." He spread his hands, a man offering a gift. "I do."

* * *

Here is what you should know about Dario Fenwick, and what I understood only later, standing at my own rail with my own hands shaking: he had rehearsed this. Not the speech — the speech he could do asleep, had probably done asleep, murmuring it into his pillow like a prayer. He had rehearsed the moment. The pause after *I do*. The tilt of his head. The way he would let the harbor noise fill the silence while I did the arithmetic of my ruin.

He had rehearsed it because he had been planning it for eleven years, and a plan that old gets polished the way a river stone gets polished, by passing over it again and again until every edge is gone.

* * *

From the deck of the Kestrel, Dario Fenwick watched Coral Underhill not-surrender, and permitted himself the private pleasure of admiration.

She was better than he remembered, and he remembered her very well. The last time he had stood this close to her she had been nineteen and laughing at something her father said, and the Meridian had been a week from sailing, and the ledger had been in the strongroom, and everything — everything — had still been possible. He had thought, in those days, that Coral Underhill would be an obstacle. She had turned out to be something rarer: a witness who didn't know she was one.

"You bought my debt," Coral said. Her voice was level. He admired that too, and made a note to admire it less; admiration was a leak in the hull, and he had sunk one ship already by admiring things. "Why would you do that? You hate me."

"I don't hate you, Coral. Hate requires effort, and I've never spent a mark on you I didn't intend to get back."

"Then say what you want."

"What I want," Dario said, "is the Meridian raised. Or gutted. Or stripped to her ribs — I'm not sentimental about method. What I want is what's in her strongroom, and I want it found before the monsoon writes the ending for us. You are the best salvage captain on this coast. Everyone knows it. Your father trained you, and your father knew that wreck better than the men who built her." He paused, delicately. "Better, I'd wager, than anyone alive."

"Careful."

"I'm always careful. It's my defining virtue." He watched her jaw set, watched the grief come up in her like a tide she was accustomed to holding back by main strength. Eleven years, he thought, and she still stands like him. She still squints into the current the way he did, left eye narrower than the right. He had counted on that too. He counted on everything.

"Here are the terms," he said. "You take the salvage contract. You find what's in the strongroom. You do it inside the monsoon window, and in exchange I tear up Corvo's paper and you owe nothing to anyone, ever again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I hold your paper, and I wait, and Corvo collects on my behalf, and the Kestrel goes on the block, and you go — where? You're a river captain, Coral. It's not a trade a woman walks away from twice." He said it gently, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "I'd rather not do any of it. I'd rather we were partners, as our fathers were."

"Our fathers were never partners."

"No," Dario agreed, and here he let the smile go out of his face entirely, because the next part worked best bare. "No, they weren't, were they."

She stared at him. He watched the question form behind her eyes — *what do you know* — and he watched her refuse to ask it, and he loved her a little for that, in the way he loved anything that resisted him: briefly, and with an eye to its breaking.

"Six days to think about it," he said. "The tide's turning, Captain. I can hear it. You always could hear it before anyone." He touched his hat brim. "I'll come back at slack water tomorrow."

He went down the gangplank without hurrying. Behind him he heard her say something to the big quartermaster woman, low and sharp, and he smiled at the boards of the dock, at the gulls, at the whole grudging shining world.

Eleven years, he thought. And now the river itself was on his side, and the storm coming up the coast like a hired knife, and the one person alive who could find the ledger was already standing on the water above it, held there by a debt he had purchased for less than the cost of the coat he stood in.

He had only to keep her from remembering.

* * *

I stood at the rail until he was a smudge on the far dock, and then a while longer, because my legs had opinions about holding me up that I didn't share.

Ilsa came and stood beside me. She didn't say anything, which is Ilsa's way of asking.

"Four hundred and twelve marks," I said. "I thought I had until the spring."

"You had until the spring with Corvo. Corvo sold." She spat over the rail, a commentary on the practice of debt-selling that needed no translation. "Fenwick's not a creditor, Coral. He's a fisherman. He bought the line because he knew there was something on it."

"He wants the Meridian."

"He wants something *in* the Meridian." Ilsa turned and looked at me, and her face had the flat gray set it gets before weather. "Nobody buys a debt to raise a hull, Captain. And nobody says a name like that — like he was laying it on a table to see what you'd do — unless they know what the name does to you."

I looked out at the channel, where the tide was indeed turning, the water going slack and then restless, the buoys beginning their slow lean. My father had taught me to read that turn before I could read letters. *The river tells you everything first, little Coral. It's people who lie.*

"I'm not taking the contract," I said.

"No," Ilsa said. "You're not."

But she said it in a strange voice, and when I glanced at her she was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read — something old and sorry, something that looked almost like guilt — and she turned away before I could ask, and went forward to yell at Pieter about the mooring line, which didn't need yelling at.

The tide turned. The Kestrel swung on her mooring, gently, like a thing asleep and dreaming of running.

And I stood at the rail with Dario's numbers ticking in my head — four hundred and twelve, six days, eleven fathoms — and I told myself the dread in my stomach was only the debt, only the money, only the ordinary arithmetic of a captain's life.

I believed it for almost the whole of that evening.

It was only later, going below with the lamp, that I noticed the chart case sitting where I always left it — and noticed, for the first time in eleven years, that my hand hesitated before I touched it. As if some part of me, some part older than my memory, already knew what was drawn there, and what was drawn on the charts of a drowned ship, and who had drawn it.

Six days, Fenwick had said.

The river tells you everything first. I just didn't yet know how to read what it was saying.
