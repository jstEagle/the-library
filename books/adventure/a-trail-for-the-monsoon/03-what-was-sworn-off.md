## 3. What Was Sworn Off

Ilsa found me in the chart room at an hour when honest people were asleep and I was pretending to be one of them.

She didn't knock. Quartermasters don't. She came down the companionway with two mugs of something hot and bitter — the ship's tea, which is to say hot water that had once been introduced to a tea leaf at a party — and set one down on the chart table without asking whether I wanted it. That was the arrangement between us. She supplied the things I would never ask for, and I pretended not to notice, and between us we maintained the fiction that the Kestrel ran on competence alone instead of competence plus stubborn loyalty.

"You've been staring at that chart for three hours," she said.

"I'm studying it."

"It's upside down."

I looked. It wasn't, but I turned it anyway, because arguing with Ilsa about orientation felt like a losing proposition on several levels. "Dario Fenwick came aboard today."

"I know. I watched him from the rail. He walks like a man who's never once worried about stepping in something." She lowered herself onto the bench across from me, joints cracking like small-arms fire. Twenty years of diving will do that to a body. Twenty years of diving had done considerably more than that to her hands, which were a topography of old scars and missing fingernails, but they were still the surest hands on any river I knew. "You're not thinking of taking his contract."

"I'm thinking about money."

"You're always thinking about money. It's what makes you boring at parties. But you're not thinking about *that* wreck." She wrapped both hands around her mug. "Coral. The Meridian."

"What about it?"

The lamp swung a little with the tide turning beneath us, and the shadows moved across her face, and for a long moment she just looked at me — looked at me the way you look at a door you're not sure you want to open because you can hear something breathing behind it.

"You don't remember," she said finally. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"Remember what? The sinking? Ilsa, I was fourteen. I remember my father's boat going down, same as everyone on this river remembers it. It's practically a folk song."

"No," she said. "Not the sinking. The night after."

* * *

Here is what I knew — or thought I knew — about the night the Meridian went down, arranged in the tidy order of things told so often they wear smooth:

A spring storm, early for the season. My father, Edric Underhill, master of the Meridian, running cargo upriver against the weather because the manifest was worth too much to sit out. The channel at Grayle Point, where the river bends around the drowned forest and the current runs treacherous even in calm. The boat striking the submerged snag — the official finding — and going down with most of her cargo and one man: my father, who went back for someone, or something, depending on which version of the story you got, and did not come up.

And then, the part I carried with less detail: the wake. The old crew of the Meridian — the men and women who'd sailed with my father for a decade — gathering on the dock at Corvane's Landing while the rain came down, and swearing. I remembered torchlight. I remembered Ilsa's voice, younger then, harder. I remembered the shape of a vow without its words, the way you remember the outline of a house you lived in as a child but not the color of the kitchen.

That was all. That was everything. And until tonight, sitting across from Ilsa with bad tea going cold between us, I had never once thought it strange that it was all.

"The night after," I repeated. "What about it?"

"Coral." She said my name the way she said *man overboard*. "Tell me what happened that night. All of it. Start to finish."

"Why?"

"Because I want to hear where you stop."

* * *

So I started, and she listened with her chin on her fist, and I got further than I expected before I hit the wall.

"We gathered on the dock," I said. "At Corvane's Landing. The whole crew. There was rain — no, wait, the rain had stopped by then, there was that fog that comes off the water after a storm, the kind that swallows sound. Torches. Someone had brought torches, though I don't remember who. You spoke. You said—" and here I reached for it, and my hand closed on fog, "—you said something about the river keeping what it takes. Or we keep what the river gives back. Something like that."

"Something like that," Ilsa said quietly. "Go on."

"And we swore off salvage. All of us. We swore we'd never dive the Meridian, never take a contract on her, never let anyone else profit from what she took down. We swore it on—" On what? I could see the raised hands, six or seven of them, silhouetted against the torches. I could see the fog moving through the light. I could see myself among them, a girl of fourteen standing where my father should have been standing. But the words themselves, the actual oath — gone. "We swore it on the water. On the river itself."

"That's right. On the water." Ilsa nodded slowly. "Keep going. What happened after the vow?"

"After the—" I stopped.

There should have been an after. Nights have afters. Vows end and people go home and the fog burns off and somewhere there is food and sleep and the gray morning after a funeral. I searched for it the way you search a pocket you're certain held your keys, and found nothing — not emptiness exactly, but that smooth blank quality of a thing that has been papered over. Like a wall where you know there used to be a door.

"There was a boat," I said slowly. "Someone left. By water. In the fog."

"Who?"

"I don't—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "Ilsa, why do I not know this?"

"That," she said, "is the question I've been carrying for eleven years."

* * *

She told me the rest, or the shape of it, in the flat careful voice she used for reporting depths and hazards.

After the vow, the crew had dispersed along the dock, and Coral Underhill — fourteen years old, fatherless for one day, already captain of nothing — had walked to the end of the pier and stood looking at the black water where the Meridian lay under forty feet of current. And then, according to Ilsa, I had turned around and said, clear as a bell in that fog: *She didn't hit a snag.*

"And then?" I asked. My voice sounded far away to me, like it was coming up through water.

"Then Dario Fenwick stepped out of the shadow of the warehouse, and you two talked, and the rest of us were herded off toward the harbor office by the harbormaster — who, I'll note, arrived very conveniently — and by the time we got free of him, you and Fenwick were gone. Gone-gone. Off the dock. Nobody saw either of you again that night."

"And in the morning?"

"In the morning you were aboard the Kestrel — your father's other boat, the little one, the one he kept at the upriver mooring — and you were yourself. Mostly. You told us you'd decided we should honor the vow, and we did, because it was your father's boat and your father's grave and your grief outranked ours. But you never spoke of that night again. Not once, in eleven years. And whenever anyone mentioned Fenwick, you got this look—" she gestured at my face, which I became aware was doing something I hadn't authorized, "—like that. Like you'd bitten tinfoil."

"I hate him," I said automatically.

"Do you? Why?"

"Because he's—" Smug. Rich. A rival. A man who smiled like the answer to a question was a favor he was granting you. Because every time our paths crossed on the river he looked at me like he was checking a calculation. "Because he's Dario Fenwick."

"That's a description, not a reason."

"Ilsa." I made myself look at her. "Are you telling me I spent a night alone with Dario Fenwick the day after my father drowned, and I don't remember a single minute of it?"

"I'm telling you that's what happened. What it means, I don't know. I've turned it over for eleven years, and I'll tell you the truth: it scares me. Grief does strange things to a mind, but it doesn't usually take a whole night and leave the edges clean. You don't misplace hours, Coral. Hours get *put* somewhere."

The lamp flame leaned as the Kestrel yawed at her mooring, and straightened. Somewhere above us, Brannick shifted his weight on deck, the old boards talking under him. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a boat I knew down to her keel bolts. And underneath them, for the first time since I'd taken the wheel of my own life, I heard the other sound — the quiet one, the one that lives in the hull of every vessel and only speaks when the water goes still: the sound of a thing that has been sealed over and is waiting.

"When Fenwick offered me the contract today," I said, "he knew I'd say yes eventually. He named my debt like he'd counted it himself. And now you're telling me he may have been the last person to speak with me on the worst night of my life — a night I can't remember."

"I'm telling you he walked out of that fog with you, and whatever passed between you two, one of you paid for forgetting it, and I've never believed it was him."

I looked down at the chart on the table — my father's chart, Grayle Point, the drowned forest marked in his tight, patient hand. Forty feet of current. A strongroom full of fortune. A ledger, if Fenwick was to be believed, and a truth, if I was.

"Ilsa," I said. "If I take this contract — if we break the vow we made on the water — what happens to the crew? To us?"

She was quiet long enough that the tea went fully cold, which on the Kestrel is a formal unit of time.

"The crew that made that vow is scattered or dead," she said at last. "Old Tammo's buried up at Harrow. The Vess sisters run a chandlery and haven't touched open water in a decade. Reyes—" something crossed her face, quick and guarded, "—Reyes stopped honoring anything years ago. It's you and me and Brannick and the kids, and none of the kids ever swore a thing." She drained her mug and stood, joints announcing each stage of the operation. "But vows aren't rope, Captain. They don't fray just because the hands that tied them are gone. They hold until somebody cuts them. So the question isn't what happens to the crew."

She paused at the foot of the companionway ladder, half in lamplight, half in dark.

"The question is what happens to *you*, when you cut it. Because I was there that night, Coral. I heard what you said at the end of that pier. *She didn't hit a snag.* Fourteen years old, soaked to the bone, and certain. Whatever you knew — whoever put it out of your reach — Fenwick's contract is going to take us straight over it."

Then she climbed into the dark, and I sat alone with my father's charts and the cold tea and the smooth blank place where a night used to be, and I understood that Dario Fenwick hadn't come to offer me a job at all.

He'd come to hire the only person alive who might dig up what he'd buried — and to make sure he was standing next to her when she did.

By morning I'd written the acceptance. Six tides, I told myself. Just money. Just the fortune.

Just the truth, said a smaller voice, that someone went to great lengths to drown.
