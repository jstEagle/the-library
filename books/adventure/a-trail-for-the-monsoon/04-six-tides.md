## 4. Six Tides

I signed the contract at dawn, which is the hour when a person is most likely to agree to anything. The light comes up soft and forgiving over the water, the coffee is still warm, and your judgment hasn't yet had time to put its boots on. Dario Fenwick knew this. He'd been waiting on my dock since before first light with two cups of coffee and a document folded into thirds like a man who expected it to be read rather than thrown.

"Money alone," I said, signing. "You understand that's what this is."

"Of course." He took the paper back, blotted my name with a handkerchief monogrammed in thread too fine for a river man, and smiled the smile of a man being told exactly what he wants to hear. "Salvage is salvage. Sentiment doesn't float."

"No," I said. "It doesn't. But it sinks things, sometimes. Whole ships."

He laughed at that — a real laugh, brief and unguarded, and for half a second he looked like the boy who used to race me up the mast of his father's barge, back before everything. Then the shutters came down behind his eyes and he was Fenwick again, all ledger-lines and lacquer.

"Six tides," he said. "The forecast office posted landfall for the day after tomorrow, nightfall at the latest. After that, nobody dives the Meridian until the season turns, and by then Reyes will have stripped her to the keel."

"I know how to count tides, Dario."

"I know you do." He paused at the head of my gangplank, one hand on the rail, and didn't look back at me when he said the next part. "I'm glad you took it, Coral. Truly."

And there was something in the way he said *truly* that sat wrong in my chest all morning, like a fishbone swallowed sideways. But I was busy, and busy is the best anesthetic I know.

* * *

The Kestrel's crew assembled on deck while I pinned the chart to the wheelhouse board and weighted its corners with four brass fittings I'd stolen from better boats than mine.

Ilsa stood at my left shoulder, arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Ilsa was a paragraph. Tam and the twins — Bo and Reave, identical in face and opposite in every other respect — crowded the chart table. Old Sef leaned in the doorway of the engine room with a rag in his hands because Sef without a rag was like the river without water: technically possible, deeply wrong.

"The Meridian went down eleven years ago off the drowned church shoal," I said. "Forty feet at low water, thirty-eight at high. She broke her back on the bar and slid into the channel-side hole, which means she's lying nose-down in silt with her stern section accessible if the sand hasn't shifted. The manifest says she carried specie, bullion, and papers. Dario believes the strongroom is intact."

"And you believe him?" Ilsa said.

"I believe the money is real whether he's honest or not. That's the beauty of salvage. You don't have to trust the man; you just have to trust the wreck."

"That's not true at all," Ilsa said mildly.

"It's true enough for six tides." I tapped the chart. "Here's the arithmetic. Landfall is night after next. That gives us six working tides — three lows, three highs — before the sea gets serious about killing us. Reyes has five, because his fleet is already anchored on station and we are not. So we're a tide behind, and the only way to win a race where you start behind is to go where the other fellow won't."

"Drowned church shoal," Tam said, and whistled low. "That channel's moved twice since the Meridian went down. Half those soundings are lies now."

"Then we'll take new ones. Bo, Reave — lead lines out, every fathom, no guessing. Sef, I want the engine warm within the hour and the spare prop shaft lashed down, because if we touch bottom I intend to come off it under power, not prayer. Tam, rig the dive gear and check every hose twice. Ilsa—"

"I know," she said. "Manifests, insurance filings, everything written about the Meridian since she slipped her moorings. You want to know what she was really carrying."

"I want to know why everyone who knows anything about that ship gets quiet when I say her name."

Ilsa held my eyes for a long moment. There was something in her face I couldn't read — or could read too well, and didn't want to.

"You'll have it by nightfall," she said.

* * *

We cleared the harbor on the ebb, riding the last of the falling tide down the outer channel with the wind quartering from the northeast, which was wrong for the season and therefore worth noticing. Monsoons announce themselves early if you know how to listen. The gulls had gone inland. The barometer in the wheelhouse had dropped a tenth overnight and kept dropping, slow as a confession.

The Kestrel ran sweet. She always did when I drove her angry, and I was angry, though I'd have told you I wasn't. I was telling myself a great many things that morning. That I'd signed for the money. That the debt collectors circling my dock like patient sharks were reason enough. That the Meridian was just a wreck, just a hull full of other people's gold, and the fact that my father's fortune rode inside her was coincidence — weather, not climate.

The trouble with telling yourself things is that some part of you takes notes.

"Coral." Ilsa came up beside me at the wheel and handed me a mug of coffee without asking if I wanted it, which was our oldest ritual. "Reyes's fleet is on station already. Saw their masts through the glass, past the point. Four vessels. He's brought the Marrow and the big crane barge."

"He would. Reyes never met a problem he didn't solve with tonnage."

"There's something else." She lowered her voice, though the nearest crewman was forty feet away and deaf with engine noise besides. "The night the Meridian sank. You've never asked me about it. Eleven years, Coral, and you never once asked."

"I asked plenty. Nobody tells me anything."

"No. You asked about the cargo, the insurance, the court findings. You never asked about the night itself. What happened on the water. Who was there." She turned the mug in her hands. "I used to think that was grief. People leave holes where the hurt is. But lately I've been wondering if it isn't the other way around. If the hurt left a hole."

The wheel hummed under my palms. Ahead, the drowned church shoal showed itself as a long brown seam in the green, the water folding over the bar in a way that looked gentle and was not.

"What are you saying, Ilsa?"

"I'm saying be careful what you dredge up. Wrecks keep. Some other things don't."

* * *

We made the outer marks by midday and began sounding the channel proper, and this is the part of the work I love without reservation or shame. Everything else about salvage is money and men and paperwork. This part is just you and the water, reading it line by line like a letter from someone who writes in riddles.

Bo swung the lead and chanted depths. Reave marked them on the fresh chart I'd gridded that morning. The old channel had indeed wandered — the flood had cut a new throat through the eastern spit and abandoned the western reach, which meant Reyes's charts, if he was working from the harbor office surveys, were pointing him at mud.

"Captain," Reave called, "you'll want to see this."

I went aft. He had the lead line coiled in his fist and a look on his face I didn't care for, the look of a man holding a fact he wishes were a rumor.

"Shallow patch dead center of the fairway, right where the chart says deep water. Twelve feet over soft bottom. And—" he hesitated, then opened his palm. In it lay a scrap of copper sheeting, green with age, torn at the edges. Hull plating. Recent enough to be sharp.

"Somebody's been down here," I said. "Recently."

"Or something came up," Bo said.

I looked east toward the point, where four masts stood against the gray sky like needles in cloth. A tide ahead of us and already diving. Reyes worked fast when he smelled money, but this fast? With the storm window still nominally open?

Unless he wasn't diving for money.

"Log it," I said finally. "Mark the obstruction. We'll work the eastern throat where the new channel cut through — deeper water, worse approach, but we'll arrive where they aren't looking."

Ilsa appeared at my elbow with the glass raised. She'd been watching the fleet, and whatever she saw made her jaw tight.

"They're not diving," she said, passing me the binoculars. "Look at the crane barge. She's rigged but idle. They're anchored in a picket line across the approaches. That's not a salvage formation, Captain. That's a fence."

She was right. The four vessels sat spaced across the mouth of the channel like beads on a string, and the message required no signal flags: *this far, no farther.*

"He knows we're coming," I said.

"He's known since you signed. Fenwick probably sent him flowers."

I stood at the rail a long moment, the wind pushing at my back like a hand between the shoulders, and felt the thing Ilsa had warned me about begin to move in the dark of me. Not doubt. Doubt would have been useful. This was older and hungrier — the pull of a question I hadn't known I'd been carrying for eleven years, waking up now that it smelled an answer.

One tide behind. Six tides to landfall. A fence across the channel and a rival who'd once taught me everything I knew about wrecks, including, possibly, how to lie about them.

"Sef," I called down the hatch. "How much more can you give me?"

The old man wiped his hands forever on his rag and grinned up at me with three teeth and total confidence. "Give me clean fuel and calm water, Captain, and I'll give you eight knots. Give me neither, and I'll give you seven and a story about it later."

"Make it eight anyway." I turned for the wheelhouse. "All hands. We're going through the eastern throat at the top of the flood, running hard, and if Reyes wants to stop us he can send a boat over and explain to my face why a salvage fleet is sitting on a wreck instead of working it."

Ilsa fell in beside me at the wheel. "And if he does?"

"Then I'll ask him about the night the Meridian sank," I said, "and watch his face while he decides how much of the truth I can survive."

She gave me a look I couldn't parse — fear or pity or something wearing their clothes — and went forward to rig the fenders, because whatever else happened, we were going to need them.

Behind us the sky had begun to organize itself into long gray ranks, and the barometer ticked down another notch, patient as a debt collector.

Five tides for Reyes. Six for us.

I intended to spend mine better.
