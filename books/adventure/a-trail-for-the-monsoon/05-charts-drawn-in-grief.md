## 5. Charts Drawn in Grief

I didn't sleep so much as lie down near sleep and let it pass over me like weather.

The Kestrel rode at anchor in the lee of Cutter's Point, her hull ticking as the tide went out from under her, that patient wooden arithmetic every riverboat speaks in the dark. Above me, in the wheelhouse, the barometer had stopped falling. It hadn't risen. It was just sitting there, like a man on a dock who hasn't yet decided whether to shake your hand or take your boat.

I gave up on the ceiling around the third bell of the morning watch and went below to the chart locker instead, because there are two cures for a night you can't make sense of — rum, and work — and I'd promised myself I'd stay sober until the Meridian was either raised or avenged. Whichever came cheaper.

The chart locker sits under the aft companionway, a cedar chest my father built before I was born, and it smells the way it has always smelled: oilcloth, graphite, and the ghost of pipe smoke that no amount of years has managed to evict. I knelt in front of it the way I have knelt in front of it since I was small enough to fit inside it myself, and I pulled out the charts of the lower channel.

Not the printed ones. The printed ones are for people who trust other people's rivers.

My father drew his own.

* * *

Elias Underhill charted water the way some men write letters — obsessively, personally, with an audience of one in mind. His charts were never just soundings. They were arguments. He'd pencil in a sandbar where the surveyors swore there was channel, and ten years later the surveyors would quietly redraw their maps to match his, and he'd say nothing about it, ever, which was its own kind of gloating. He marked eddies with little spirals and deadheads with tiny coffins, because my father believed a river should be legible to a child and honest to a corpse, and he managed both in the same line of ink.

The chart of the Sable Reach was soft at the folds from handling. Mine, not his. I'd carried it through every season since I took the Kestrel's wheel at nineteen, and I knew it the way you know the back of your own hand — which is to say, completely, right up until the day you actually look at it.

That was the thing about grief nobody warns you about. It doesn't fade evenly. It wears thin in the places you touch most and stays thick in the places you avoid, and after eleven years I had touched everything on my father's charts except the margins.

I spread the Sable Reach across the chart table and weighted it with the brass dividers and the ship's clock, and I started at the top left corner the way you start reading a letter from someone who's gone, because it is one, really. Every chart is a letter. *Here is where the water is deep. Here is where it will kill you. I am telling you this because I cannot tell you anything else.*

The main channel ran down the page in his confident hand, depth figures marching along it like beads on a string. The flood tide set marked in arrows. The monsoon anchorage behind Gull Island, circled twice. All familiar. All him.

Then I got to the eastern shore, where the current slackens against the clay bank, and I saw the annotation.

Small. Tucked into the margin beside the six-fathom line, in handwriting that was not my father's.

*Slack water holds 40 min past predicted. Verify.*

Three words and a correction. My father did not accept corrections. In thirty years of drawing charts he had never once written another person's observation onto his own work without testing it himself first, and if he had tested it, he would have rewritten it in his own hand, because that was the man — generous with everything except authorship.

So someone else had written it. Someone whose hand my father tolerated on his charts, which narrowed the field to roughly nobody.

And here is the part I keep circling back to, the part that made me sit down on the chart locker lid with my hands flat on the table like a woman steadying herself on a deck that isn't moving: I almost recognized it.

Almost. That's the cruelest word in the language. It's recognition with the payoff withheld. The letters were narrow and forward-leaning, the *y*s crossed long, the numbers made with a flat-topped seven that European schools teach and river schools don't. I knew that seven. I have seen that seven somewhere — on a manifest, maybe, or a letter, or a slate in a cabin where I once stood while something happened that I could not now remember —

No. I shut that door. It had been a long time since I'd shut that particular door, but I shut it, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

I turned back to the chart and looked properly at the margins for the first time in eleven years.

There were more.

A note by the Gull Island shoal: *Dredged '09? Check harbor master's ledger.* A sounding off the Meridian's old mooring, corrected upward by two feet, in that same narrow hand. And near the mouth of the Sable Reach itself, half worn away by thumbing, a single line that stopped my breath:

*E. says the channel lies. Trust the flood, not the ebb.*

*E.* Elias. Someone quoting my father to my father, or my father being quoted approvingly by someone who argued with him enough to earn the initial. You don't call a man by his initial unless you've earned the shorthand, and you don't write on his charts unless you've earned more than that.

I sat with the lamp turned low and let the memories come up the way silt comes up when you drag an anchor — not the clear water you wanted but the clouded bottom of things, fragments, textures without narrative. A cabin. Lamplight. Charts spread on a table exactly like this one, four hands over them instead of two, my father laughing — my father *laughing*, which he did perhaps six times a year and never in front of paying passengers — and a voice answering him, low, amused, with an accent that rounded its vowels the way that flat-topped seven suggested. A woman's voice? I couldn't say. A young voice. A voice that belonged in that cabin like it had been invited.

And me. I was in the memory too, or the edge of me was — small, or young anyway, sitting on the floor with my back against the chart locker, pretending to be asleep so they wouldn't send me to bed, listening to the two of them argue about tide tables the way other families argue about money.

That was all. It wasn't even a scene. It was a smell and a laugh and a lamplit blur, and it might have been any year, any evening, except that my chest hurt looking at it, the way your chest hurts when you wake from a dream about someone dead and can't get back inside it.

Because here's what I couldn't reconcile: I remembered my father's chart room as a solitary place. That was the story I had built my whole self on. Elias Underhill alone with his soundings, teaching his daughter the river because there was no one else, dying alone on the Meridian because he trusted no one's water but his own. Solitude was the load-bearing wall of everything I believed about him, and about what he'd left me, and about why I ran a boat the way I run her — alone at the wheel even when Ilsa is standing right there, because that's how he did it.

And these annotations said the room had held a second chair.

* * *

Ilsa found me there at dawn, or what passes for dawn under a monsoon sky, which is less a sunrise than a slow admission. She came down the companionway with two mugs of coffee and the expression she reserves for finding me doing something she'll later have to talk me out of.

"You're up early," she said.

"I'm up late. There's a difference and it matters."

She set a mug by my elbow and looked at the chart, and I watched her look at it, and I watched the exact moment she saw the marginal notes, because her face did something very small and very controlled, the way a deck does when a wave comes under it wrong.

"Where'd those come from?" she asked. Too casual. Ilsa does casual the way Dario does sincerity — competently, and only when needed.

"They were always there. I just never read them." I tapped the flat-topped seven. "You know this hand?"

"No."

"Liar."

"Coral." She sat down across the table from me, slowly, mug in both hands like a woman warming herself on a fire that's already gone out. "Eleven years ago you came back from that night with a hole in you the size of the Meridian, and the doctor called it shock and the crew called it grief and nobody — *nobody* — pressed you on it, because pressing you felt like kicking a woman who was already underwater. If I tell you I recognize that handwriting, I'm not giving you information. I'm handing you a shovel and pointing at ground you may not want opened."

"That's a lot of words for *yes*."

"It's a lot of words for *I don't know what it costs you*."

I drank my coffee. It was terrible, burnt the way ship's coffee gets when the person making it is distracted, and I loved her for the distraction. Outside, the light was coming up gray and bruised, and the water in the anchorage lay flat as poured metal, holding its breath.

"Ilsa," I said. "When we signed Fenwick's contract, you told me the job was cursed. You said it like you meant it literally, like there was a curse with a name and a date."

"I did."

"And last night you looked at me like you were deciding whether to tell me something. You're still deciding. I can see the scales working." I turned the chart around so the annotations faced her. "So here's my offer. Don't tell me what you know. Just tell me whether the person who wrote this was on the Meridian the night she went down."

The silence lasted long enough for the kettle on the galley stove to begin its small complaint.

"Yes," she said finally.

"And do I know them?"

Another silence, shorter, worse.

"You knew everyone on that river, Coral. That was always the problem."

She got up then, because Ilsa's courage has always been of the kind that works better in moving water than at anchor, and she took her mug with her, and at the companionway she stopped with her back to me.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your father trusted whoever wrote those notes with his charts. With his *charts*, Coral. Whatever else happened that night, that much was real, and it was good, and I'd hold onto it if I were you. People need one true thing about the dead or they start inventing the rest."

Then she was gone up the ladder, and I heard her calling the anchor watch to trim for the turn of the tide, ordinary orders in an ordinary voice, and I sat alone in the chart room with my father's letter spread out in front of me and a stranger's handwriting all through its margins.

* * *

I worked until noon. There was nothing else to do with the fear except organize it.

I went through every chart in the locker, all forty-one of them, and found the second hand in nine. Always in the margins. Always corrections, verifications, small acts of collaborative care — *verify*, *check*, *confirmed, flood sets stronger than drawn*. Nine charts spanning six years, ending abruptly the summer before the Meridian sank. Whoever it was had been at my father's elbow for six years and then simply stopped, the way a voice stops mid-sentence.

And the corrections clustered. That was the thing I kept coming back to, the thing that finally made me put the dividers down. They clustered around the Sable Reach. Around the channel that runs past the shallows where the Meridian lies. Around the exact water Dario Fenwick was paying me to dredge.

My father had annotated that reach obsessively in his final months — soundings repeated, depths questioned, a little coffin symbol drawn and redrawn beside a spot where no deadhead appeared on any survey. He'd been worried about that channel. Or suspicious of it. And someone had been helping him worry.

*Trust the flood, not the ebb.*

I thought about the official report, the one I'd read so many times the pages had gone soft: grounding due to navigational error in deteriorating conditions, ebb tide setting the vessel across the bar. Navigational error. My father's error, the report implied, politely, in the passive voice the sea prefers. The great Elias Underhill, misreading his own river.

But his own charts said the ebb lied there. Said it in his hand and in someone else's. He *knew* that channel lied on the ebb. He would sooner have steered the Meridian into a cliff than cross that bar on a falling tide.

Which meant either my father broke every rule he ever taught me, or the report was wrong, or —

Or the wreck wasn't where the truth was. The wreck was where the truth was *put*.

I rolled the Sable Reach chart closed with hands that weren't quite steady and stood in the chart room's dimness, listening to the Kestrel breathe around me, and I understood that something had shifted underneath me, quiet as a sandbar building. I had taken this contract for money. I had told myself the story of the money so firmly that the telling had nearly become believing. But Dario Fenwick hadn't come to me because I was cheap, and he hadn't come to me because I was good, though I am both. He had come to me because of whatever lived in the space between these annotations — because of the second chair in my father's chart room, and the night I couldn't remember, and the possibility that the daughter of Elias Underhill might dig up more than cargo if you pointed her at the right mud.

Outside, the first gust of the leading edge came down the anchorage and laid the Kestrel over a degree, just a degree, a rumor of what was coming. The rigging spoke in its sleep. Somewhere beyond the point, five tides closer than I liked, Reyes' fleet was doing its own arithmetic.

I climbed to the wheelhouse and took the glass and swept the eastern horizon, and that was when I saw it: a launch running down the far channel toward us, flying no colors, riding light and fast, the kind of boat that carries either news or trouble and usually charges for both.

Ilsa squinted into my eyepiece's direction before I lowered it. "Fenwick?"

"No," I said, watching the launch come on. "Smaller. Faster. Alone."

The wind gusted again, harder, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet anywhere in the world.

"Whoever it is," I said, "they're coming straight from the Sable Reach."
