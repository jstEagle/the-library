## 6. The Fleet at Anchor

We saw them at first light, or rather we saw what they wanted us to see: three masts standing off the Meridian shoals like a jury that had already reached its verdict and was only waiting for the accused to sail into view.

Ilsa spotted them from the bow, of course. She has eyes like a heron's and the same talent for standing motionless while the world delivers itself to her.

"Smoke," she said. "Three boats. Maybe four."

"Maybe four," I repeated, and did the arithmetic I didn't want to do. Four salvage vessels meant cranes, means divers, means a captain with enough credit to lease a steam winch in a season when every port between here and the capital was already spoken for. It meant money. It meant someone had decided, months ago, that the Meridian was worth the trouble everyone else had spent six years pretending she wasn't.

I took the wheel and brought us around the long way, hugging the channel where the water ran brown and honest over the sandbars, because I wanted to see them before they saw me. Old habit. Old, useless habit, as it turned out, because the lead vessel had a glass trained on us before we were within a mile, and by the time we closed to hailing distance there was a man at her rail who I would have known by the set of his shoulders in a fog bank, in a dream, in the dark at the bottom of the sea.

Captain Emory Reyes.

My mentor. My teacher. The man who taught me that the river forgives nothing and forgets less. And, for the last six years, a stranger.

* * *

The Kestrel is not an impressive boat to anyone who doesn't know her. Forty feet of river oak with a shallow draft and a wheel that pulls to port like a mule that's decided, on principle, against going where you want. I bought her wrecked and rebuilt her twice, and she has never once let me down, which is more than I can say for any person I've loved.

Reyes's flagship was another animal entirely. The *Providence* — and there was a joke in that name that only the two of us would get, and neither of us was laughing — was a proper salvage steamer, all iron and intention, with a derrick that could lift a locomotive and a hull that could take a punch from a dock. She sat at anchor with her two consorts flanking her like hired muscle, and the smoke from her stack stood up straight in the dead morning air, a flag of a country with one citizen.

I brought the Kestrel alongside at a respectful distance and held her there, engines just turning over, because you don't approach a man like Reyes bow-first and you don't approach him broadside either. You approach him the way you'd approach a sandbar you can't quite read. Slowly. Ready to back water.

"Coral Underhill," Reyes called across the water, and his voice hadn't changed. It was still that low, unhurried baritone, the voice of a man who had never once needed to shout at a river because he understood that the river would always win the shouting match. "You're a long way from your channel."

"You're a long way from yours," I called back. "Last I heard, the Providence was working the delta wrecks."

"The delta's picked clean." He came to the rail properly, and I got my first good look at him in six years. He'd gone grey at the temples, which was fair, because I'd gone grey in places I wasn't going to describe to a man at fifty yards over open water. He was broader than I remembered, or maybe I'd just been younger, and there was a new scar on his jaw that he didn't explain and I didn't ask about. Some debts you settle with silence. "You're working for Fenwick."

It wasn't a question, which was its own kind of information. News travels fast when someone pays for the speed.

"I'm working for money," I said. "Fenwick has it."

"Fenwick had it," Reyes said. "He's spent most of it on us."

* * *

Here is what I knew about the salvage fleet, assembled in the space of about four seconds while Reyes watched me do the arithmetic on my face and enjoyed it: three vessels, steam winch, a dive tender, and enough provisions for a month. Which meant Dario Fenwick hadn't hired a salvage crew. He'd hired a salvage *operation*, the kind you charter when you expect the job to be contested, or when you expect your other contractor to fail.

Or when you expect your other contractor to succeed and you want someone there to take the prize off her deck.

Ilsa moved up beside me at the rail, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, which in Ilsa's language was a paragraph. "Three boats," she murmured. "He told us he had no one else."

"He told us a great many things," I murmured back. "He told us the tide tables were current."

Reyes watched us confer with the patience of a man fishing. That was always the worst thing about him — not his skill, though his skill was the best on the river, but his patience. He could wait out a tide, a storm, a person. He'd waited out my father once, over a disputed wreck, and my father had gone to his grave still thinking he'd won that argument.

"Coral." He said my name the way he used to when I was nineteen and reckless and he was hauling me out of the water for the third time in a season. Not Coral Underhill, not Captain. Just Coral, and the familiarity landed like a hand on the back of the neck. "Come aboard. Ten minutes. Bring your quartermaster if you don't trust me alone with you."

"I don't trust you alone with me," I said.

"I know." And there it was, the ghost of a smile, the one that used to mean *good, you're learning*. "Bring her anyway."

I looked at Ilsa. Ilsa looked at the water between the two boats, measuring it the way she measures everything, and then she looked at me and gave the smallest nod, which meant: *I'll be right behind you, and if his crew so much as breathes wrong I'll put a boat hook through someone's ribs.*

We went aboard the Providence.

* * *

His cabin was exactly as I remembered it and that was the strangest part. The same charts on the walls, the same brass instruments on felt, the same smell of pipe smoke and linseed oil. Six years, and the man had changed his cabin not at all, which told me either that he was a creature of profound consistency or that he'd never expected to see me again and hadn't bothered preparing the room.

There was a third chair. He'd set it out. That, at least, was new.

"Tea?" he asked.

"You didn't bring me aboard for tea."

"No," he agreed, and sat down across from me, and for a long moment he just looked at me, and I let him, because I wanted to see what he was looking for. "You look like him," he said finally. "Your father. When he was working a problem."

"Don't."

"He'd have hated this. You know that. Fenwick, the Meridian, all of it. He'd have called it—"

"I said *don't*." My voice came out flatter than I intended, which is what I intended. "You don't get to invoke him. Not after—"

"After what, Coral?" And there it was, the question under the question, the one that had been sitting between us for six years like a body under a hull. "After I left? After you told me to go? You stood on your own deck and you told me that if I ever spoke to you again you'd run me down, and I have honored that request with a discipline I'd ask you to admire."

Ilsa, behind me, had gone very still. She knew the shape of this argument. She didn't know the words, because I'd never given them to her, but she knew the shape.

"You left the night it mattered," I said.

"I left because you told me to leave. I left because—" He stopped. Reyes does not stop mid-sentence. Reyes finishes his sentences the way he finishes his channels, all the way through, or he doesn't start them. That he stopped told me more than anything he'd said yet. He looked at the chart on his wall — not at me, at the chart, at some middle distance of it — and when he spoke again his voice had changed, dropped its polish. "I left because staying would have meant telling you something you weren't ready to hear, and I was a coward about it, and I have been paying for that cowardice for six years, and now Fenwick has put you on that wreck and I have run out of time to keep being a coward."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere above us, his crew moved about their work, boots on deck, the small industry of a ship at anchor.

"So tell me," I said.

"No," he said. "That's not why you're here, and we both know it, and if I tell you now, on my deck, with my crew overhead, you'll do what you always do, which is decide you've been managed, and you'll go do the opposite of whatever I advise, on principle, and you'll be right to, because I *have* been managing you since you were nineteen and it's the only language you've ever trusted."

"That's not true."

"It's the truest thing I've ever said to you." He leaned forward. "You're here because you want to know why I'm on this wreck, and I'm going to tell you that much, and then I'm going to tell you to leave, and you're going to refuse, and we're both going to have to live with that."

"Generous of you."

"Coral." He rubbed his eyes with one hand, and for a moment — just a moment — he looked old. Not grey. Old. The way a boat looks after a bad season, sound enough, but tired in the timbers. "The Meridian didn't sink the way the report says she sank. You know that. You've known that since the hull plate came up out of the shallows two days ago with a repair date on it that shouldn't exist."

I kept my face still. It cost me. Ilsa, behind me, did not keep hers still, and I heard her breath change.

"Your diver works fast," Reyes said, "and she dives quiet, and I have had a man on those shallows since Tuesday, so let's not pretend we're not both doing the same job for the same man with different ideas about who he is." He sat back. "I'm going to tell you one thing, and then you're going to get off my boat. One thing, Coral, because you deserve that much, and because your father deserves that much, and because I am tired — God, I am so tired — of carrying it."

"Then say it."

"The man who scuttled the Meridian didn't do it for the insurance." His eyes held mine and there was something in them I had never seen there before, not in six years of rivalry, not in the ten years before that of apprenticeship. Fear. Actual fear, in Captain Emory Reyes, who once stood on a burning deck and gave orders in a voice you could hear across the harbor. "The insurance was the cover story. He did it to hide something. And the something is still down there, and Fenwick isn't paying four boats' worth of money to raise cargo."

The cabin seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way it does when you've been below too long and your inner ear starts writing fiction.

"How do you know what Fenwick's paying for?"

"Because I've read his charter." He stood, and the audience was over; I could feel it in the way the room reorganized itself around him standing. "And because six years ago, the night your father drowned, I was supposed to be on that river, and I wasn't, and there are exactly two people alive who know why, and one of them is me."

"And the other?"

He walked me to the door. He opened it himself. And he said, in that low, patient, terrible voice: "Get off my boat, Captain Underhill. Take your crew and your debt and your Fenwick contract and go home, and let me raise what's down there, because I am the only person on this river who can do it without it killing someone else you love."

* * *

We crossed back to the Kestrel in silence. Ilsa took the wheel without being asked and held us steady while I stood at the rail and watched the Providence recede, her three masts and her steam winch and her captain who had just, in the space of ten minutes, dismantled everything I understood about the last six years and replaced it with nothing.

"He knows about the hull plate," Ilsa said at last.

"He's had a man on the shallows since Tuesday."

"Then he's found the strongroom by now."

"Maybe." I watched the fleet at anchor, sitting on the Meridian like gulls on a carcass. "Or maybe he's found it and can't open it. Or maybe—" I stopped.

"Maybe what, Captain?"

Maybe he's telling the truth. That was the thought I didn't want, because it was worse than the alternative. If Reyes was lying, I was dealing with a rival, and I have spent my whole life dealing with rivals; rivals are just weather. But if he was telling the truth — if my father's death was a cover story, if Dario Fenwick had hired me not to raise a fortune but to open a door I couldn't close — then the gap in my memory, the one night I had never once thought to question, wasn't a gap anymore.

It was a hole someone had dug.

"Course, Captain?" Ilsa asked.

I looked north, toward the shoals, toward the wreck that everyone had sworn off and no one had stayed away from. The water over the Meridian was flat and green and patient, and somewhere under it was a repair date that lied, and a strongroom, and a night I couldn't remember, and my father's last hour.

"Take us to the shallows," I said. "I'm going down."

Ilsa looked at me — at me, at the water, at the fleet, back at me — and for the first time in eleven years of working my deck, my quartermaster looked afraid.

"Not you," she said. "Captain. Not you. Not first."

"Then who?"

She didn't answer. And the silence, for once, was not a paragraph. It was a question, and neither of us wanted to read it aloud.
