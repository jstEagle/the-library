## 7. First Dive

I lost the argument the way I lose most arguments with Ilsa — by being right and being ignored.

"You're not diving," she said, already pulling on the canvas suit like a woman dressing for church. "You're the only one aboard who can read your father's charts without translating them into prayer. If you drown in four fathoms of green water because you wanted to be first, this whole expedition dies with you, and Dario Fenwick gets to write the ending."

"I've dived worse."

"You've dived worse *drunk*," she said. "That's not an argument in your favor, Captain. That's a diagnosis."

There was no winning that one. There rarely is, when someone has known you long enough to keep receipts. So I stood at the rail while my quartermaster — fifty-one years old, one bad knee, a scar across her ribs from a winch cable that had opinions about her career choices — spat over the side for luck and went down the ladder into water the color of bottle glass.

The shallows off the Meridian's grave are kind, as these things go. Twelve feet at low slack, sandy bottom, enough light to work by if the silt cooperated. The silt never cooperates. Eleven years of river had laid a blanket of fine grey mud over everything, and every kick of a fin raised it again, so that diving there was less like exploring and more like reading a letter through smoke.

I watched the bubbles.

That's the honest version of what I did for forty minutes. I stood at the rail of my own boat, in sight of the wreck that had eaten my father and half my childhood, and I watched air rise out of the water and counted the seconds between bursts, the way you count a pulse. Steady. Steady. A longer gap. Steady again.

Bray, my deckhand, stood beside me pretending to coil a line that had been coiled twice already. He was nineteen, all elbows and appetite, and he had signed onto the Kestrel because I paid late but paid, which in the salvage trade counts as loyalty. He kept glancing from the bubbles to me and back, working up to something.

"Captain," he said finally. "Is it true what they say about this spot? About the night it went down?"

"Everything they say about that night is true," I said. "That's the problem. It's all true, and none of it fits together."

He nodded slowly, the way young men nod when they understand nothing and hope it looks otherwise. Then: "And you were there?"

"I was there."

"And you don't remember—"

"Bray." I said it gently, which surprised both of us. "When I want to be interrogated, I'll hire someone better at it than you."

He went back to his line. The bubbles kept their time.

Here is what nobody tells you about a hole in your memory: it doesn't feel like a hole. It feels like a room you've never entered in a house you've lived in all your life. You walk past the door every day. You have no curiosity about it. Why would you? A door you've never opened isn't locked; it's just a door. It was only now, standing over the very water where that night had happened, that I understood I'd been walking past it for eleven years without once wondering what was behind it.

Eleven years. I'd grieved my father thoroughly, professionally, the way you grieve a man who drowned within sight of you. I'd built a whole architecture of that night out of other people's accounts — the storm, the shoaling, the Meridian breaking her back on the bar, the boats that went out too late. I knew the story the way you know a song you can't sing. And I had never once noticed that my own verse was missing.

Until Ilsa told me about the vow, and until Dario stood at my rail wearing my father's debt like a boutonnière, and until yesterday, when I'd said *I'm going down* and my quartermaster had looked at me like I'd proposed swimming to the moon.

Not you. Not first.

Why not first, Ilsa? What do you think I'll find down there?

* * *

She came up at the forty-minute mark, which meant she'd pushed it twenty minutes past sensible, which meant she'd found something worth drowning over.

We hauled her over the rail and she sat on the deck streaming river water, pulling the hood back, her grey braid plastered to her skull, and she didn't say anything. She just looked at me. And Ilsa's silences, as established, are paragraphs, and this one ran long.

"Well?" Bray said.

She held up one hand — wait — and breathed. Then she reached into the net bag she'd taken down and brought out a piece of the Meridian.

It was a hull plate, maybe two feet square, torn free along its rivet line. River-scoured, furred with weed, but sound. Sounder than it should have been, actually, and I registered that before I registered anything else, because eleven years in the trade teaches your hands before it teaches your head. I turned it over. The inner face was cleaner, protected from the current, and stamped there in the yard's familiar die:

**MERIDIAN — HULL SEC. 4-C — FITTED 14 MARCH 1891**

The Meridian sank in September of 1891.

Six months. Six months between that stamp and the night she broke on the bar. Which meant the plate was new, which meant section four of her hull had been repaired or replaced in the spring of her final season, which meant—

Which meant the official sinking report was wrong, or lying, or both. Because the report — the one the insurers accepted, the one Dario Fenwick's own company filed — described the Meridian as sound, seaworthy, lost to weather and shoaling through no fault of hull or owner. I'd read it a dozen times looking for my father in its margins. There was no repair in it. No survey. No dry-docking that spring. Nothing.

A ship doesn't get new plating by accident. Someone paid for it. Someone logged it. And someone made sure the log never reached the report.

"The plate was loose," Ilsa said. Her voice was rough from the river. "Working free. That whole section — I could feel the seams with my gloves. New steel, old framing. They patched her like a man patches a coat he intends to sell."

"Or a coat he intends to ruin," I said.

Nobody answered that. The river answered for us, slapping the hull in its patient way.

I crouched and looked at the stamp again. Fitted 14 March. My father had died in September. Five months during which somebody had put fresh steel into the Meridian's flank and then erased the fact of it. And my father — who kept books the way monks keep psalms, who annotated everything, who drew charts in a hand so precise you could measure his moods by the weight of the ink — my father had been master of that ship, and either he hadn't known about the repair, which was impossible, or he had known, and the knowledge had gone into whatever he'd sealed away below decks before the end.

The ledger. Dario wanted the ledger. I'd assumed it was accounts — cargo, debts, the usual paper ghosts men kill each other over. But maybe it was more than accounts. Maybe it was the record of a hull patched in secret and a report written clean.

"The past is lying to someone," I said aloud.

"To everyone," Ilsa said. "That's how it usually works. Question is who's paying for the lie."

* * *

We spent the rest of the tide working the shallows in relays — Bray down twice, me down once over Ilsa's dead-body objection, which I overrode and immediately regretted, because the visibility was exactly the smoke-through-letter misery I'd promised and I came up with nothing but a mouthful of silt and a bruised shin on some buried spar.

But even that dive told me something. The wreck field was wrong. Wrecks settle in patterns — heavy low, light scattered down-current, a trail of debris like breadcrumbs leading away from the break. The Meridian's field didn't trail. It clustered. As if she hadn't broken apart in the storm at all, but had been put down — settled, deliberately, in one place, like a woman laying down a burden she was tired of carrying.

Ships that sink scatter. Ships that are sunk gather.

I didn't say that part out loud. Some sentences change shape when you speak them, and that one would have come out of my mouth sounding like an accusation against a dead man's partner, and once said, it couldn't be unsaid, and I wasn't ready. Not yet. I wanted to be wrong first. I gave myself until dark to be wrong.

It didn't take until dark.

At slack water, while Bray cooked and Ilsa wrung the river out of her braid by the stove, I sat in my cabin with my father's charts spread across the desk, and I did what I should have done the day we left port: I read them like evidence, not like scripture.

His hand was everywhere — depth soundings in columns neat as fence posts, channel notes, little marginal sketches of landmarks, a drawing of a heron in the corner of the Sandbar Reach sheet because he was that kind of man. And there, on the sheet covering the approaches to the Meridian's last run, a cluster of annotations in the margin. Sounding dates. Repair references. A string of figures that meant nothing to me yet but had the rhythm of tonnage and displacement — the arithmetic of what a ship weighs, and what she weighs when she takes on water she shouldn't.

And beneath them, in that same second handwriting I'd found before and almost recognized and failed again to place:

*Ask him why the plates.*

Three words. Ask him why the plates.

My father wrote those words. My father, who trusted his own partner the way I had once trusted Dario Fenwick, had written a question down instead of asking it aloud, which is what careful men do when they already suspect the answer and aren't ready to hear it. He'd suspected. In the spring of 1891, five months before the bar took her, my father had held a piece of new-plated hull in his hands or seen the yard's invoice cross his desk, and he had written *ask him why the plates*, and then — what? Had he asked? Had he gotten an answer? Was the answer in the ledger, sealed in the strongroom with him?

Or had he stopped asking because the answer arrived first, riding in on a September storm?

I sat there a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, Bray dropped something and swore at it with real feeling.

Here's the thing I couldn't escape, the thing that sat in my chest like swallowed ballast: the handwriting was almost mine. That's what I'd never been able to place about it, all these years, looking at my father's charts and feeling that flicker of recognition under the stranger's script. The slant was different, the loops were looser, but the bones of it — the way the letters leaned forward like people hurrying somewhere — the bones were mine. I'd learned to write by copying someone. Every child does.

I had thought I learned it from my father.

I looked at *ask him why the plates* and felt the floor of my certainty tilt, gently, the way a deck tilts when the tide turns under you.

Because if the second hand on my father's charts belonged to my mother, that was one thing — sad, ordinary, the usual archaeology of grief. But my mother died two years before the Meridian did. I knew that the way I knew my own name.

Except I suddenly wasn't sure I knew my own name, was I? Not all of it. Not the parts written that September night.

Ilsa knocked — two raps, her knock — and came in without waiting, carrying two cups of coffee that tasted like boiled rope, which is how Bray makes it and how we drink it anyway. She set mine down, saw the chart, saw where I was looking, and went very still.

"You found the notes," she said.

"You knew about them."

"I knew there were papers your father sent off the ship the week before she sailed. I didn't know what was in them. Your father trusted me with rope and rum, Captain, not correspondence." She lowered herself into the chair across from me, joints cracking like small-arms fire. "What do they say?"

"'Ask him why the plates.'" I turned the chart toward her. "You dove today. You felt the seams. Tell me I'm reading this wrong. Tell me ships get secret repairs all the time and forget to mention it to their insurers."

She looked at the annotation for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet in the way it does when she's deciding to stop protecting me.

"I helped load that cargo, Coral. The manifest said machine parts and cotton. Machine parts don't get crated in iron and stowed below the waterline. Cotton doesn't weigh what that hold weighed. I was twenty-six and well paid and I didn't ask, because the man signing my wages was your father and I'd have followed him into a fire." She met my eyes. "But I've had eleven years to ask since. And here's what I know: a ship that heavy, loaded that low, doesn't need a storm to drown. She needs a bad sea and an open seam. And now we've found a seam that somebody patched in secret and never reported."

"So the storm didn't kill her."

"The storm was the alibi," Ilsa said. "Somebody built the murder and let the weather sign for it."

The lamp hissed. The river slapped the hull. Out beyond the point, invisible in the dark, the rival fleet lay at anchor with Captain Reyes aboard her flagship, and somewhere south of us Dario Fenwick was sleeping the sleep of a man whose plan was proceeding, and I sat in my cabin holding a chart that my father had annotated with a question he'd died before answering, next to a sentence in handwriting that was almost mine.

One thing at a time. That's seamanship. One thing at a time, and the first thing is always: what do I know, and who does it endanger?

What I knew: the Meridian was scuttled or sabotaged, the official report was a fiction, Dario had hired me to find a document that might prove it, and the man who co-signed that fiction — if Ilsa was right, if the weights were right — had stood at my rail three days ago calling it opportunity.

Who it endangered: everyone on my boat, starting with me, because I had signed a contract with the probable murderer of my father, and the contract had my name on it in my own hand, and I had read every clause except the one written in the past.

"Coral," Ilsa said. "Whatever you're thinking — think it slower."

"I'm thinking that Dario didn't hire the best salvager on the coast by accident. He hired the captain's daughter. He hired the one person with a moral claim to search that wreck, so no court, no insurer, no rival would ever question what we pulled out of her." I turned the chart back around and looked at my father's question. "He didn't hire me to find the ledger, Ilsa. He hired me to be the reason finding it looks legitimate."

"Or he hired you because you're good."

"He hired me because I'm good *and* because I'm hers." I tapped the margin note. "This hand. Look at it. Tell me whose it is."

Ilsa looked. And I watched my quartermaster — a woman who has lied to customs officers, press gangs, and two husbands without visible strain — fail, for the first time in our acquaintance, to meet my eyes.

"I never saw your mother write," she said.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I've got tonight." She stood, knees protesting, and gathered the cups. "Get some sleep, Captain. Tide turns at four. And Coral—" she paused at the door, half-turned, lamplight catching the wet silver of her braid. "If the past is lying to someone, maybe ask yourself who it's lying *for*. Sometimes a lie is a wall somebody built to keep you safe. Before you tear it down, make sure you're ready to see what it was keeping out."

She left. I sat with the chart and the lamp and the almost-familiar hand, and outside, far off across the dark water, thunder cleared its throat — the monsoon, still days out, practicing its opening lines.

Four tides, the forecast said. Four tides to raise what my father died sealing.

And underneath the fear and the arithmetic and the race, one sentence kept surfacing in me like a body that won't stay down:

*Ask him why the plates.*

I intended to. I intended to ask Dario Fenwick that question to his face, soon, with witnesses.

But first — God help me — I needed to know whose handwriting had taught me my own.
