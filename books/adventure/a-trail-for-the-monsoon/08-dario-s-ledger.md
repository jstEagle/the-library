## 8. Dario's Ledger

There is a particular kind of silence aboard a working riverboat, and I had forgotten how much I hated it. It isn't peace. Peace is a thing you choose. The silence of the *Kestrel* at anchor was the silence of a held breath — the creak of rope, the tick of cooling iron in the engine housing, and underneath it all the river going about its business without asking anyone's permission.

I stood in Coral Underhill's cabin and let the silence take my measure.

Her cabin was exactly what I expected, which is to say it disappointed me in the way that only honesty can. No trophies. No maps framed behind glass to prove she'd been somewhere worth being. A bunk made with military corners. A shelf of charts rolled tight as cigars. A chipped enamel mug holding pens that no longer wrote, because Coral Underhill was not a woman who threw things away; she was a woman who simply stopped using them and moved on, which is a colder cruelty altogether.

I had told her crew I'd come aboard to review the survey notes. Ilsa Brandt had looked at me the way a lighthouse looks at a ship it has already decided to watch founder, and said, "Don't touch nothing," which in Brandt's dialect meant *I know what you are, Fenwick, and I am allowing you aboard for reasons that will become clear to you later, probably when it's too late.*

I respected that. Ilsa Brandt was the only person on this river whose contempt I'd ever considered collecting.

But I hadn't come for survey notes. I had come for a ledger.

* * *

Let me tell you about ledgers, since everyone assumes men like me keep them out of vanity. Vanity would be simpler. A ledger is a confession written in advance by a man who believes he'll never be caught, and then it becomes a noose written in advance by a man who knows better. My late partner, Emory Voss — yes, *that* Voss, the name should be ringing bells for you the way it rings them for me at three in the morning — understood ledgers better than any man I ever met. He kept two sets. Every honest crook does. But Emory kept a third, and the third one was the problem.

The third ledger recorded what actually happened aboard the *Meridian* on the night she went down. Not what the manifest said we were carrying. What we were carrying. What I had signed off on, what I had insured, what I had arranged to have sitting in her hold when she took water off Cutter's Reach in weather that every pilot on the river knew was coming.

Emory hid it before he died. Hid it well enough that I spent four years and a small fortune in bribes trying to find where, and got nowhere, because Emory Voss did not trust me — a judgment I found insulting at the time and have since come to regard as the most accurate piece of accounting either of us ever produced.

Here is the arithmetic that keeps me up nights: if the ledger surfaces and lands in the wrong hands, I hang. If it surfaces and lands in *my* hands, I burn it, and I live rich. And if it never surfaces at all — if it sits in the strongroom of the *Meridian* until the river eats the paper — then I live rich anyway, just with a splinter in my mind that no amount of money can draw.

So why hire Coral Underhill? Why pay her salvage rights to the one wreck on this coast that every sane captain swore off?

Because Emory didn't hide the ledger ashore. I know this because I searched everywhere ashore, thoroughly, and once memorably at gunpoint. He hid it aboard. Which means the only way to that paper is through the wreck, and the only people alive who can dive the *Meridian*'s grave in monsoon season are Coral Underhill and Captain Reyes — and Reyes works for me now, though she doesn't love the arrangement, and love was never part of the contract.

I hired Coral because she's the best. I hired Coral because she's desperate, and desperation makes excellent ballast. But mostly — and I want to be honest here, since honesty costs me so little when it's private — I hired Coral because if anyone was going to find what Emory hid, it would be his daughter. He built his hiding places the way he built everything: for her. Half his charts had annotations only she could read. His whole life was a letter addressed to Coral Underhill, and I had gambled that the ledger was folded inside it somewhere.

Which meant I needed her to find it first. Before Reyes. Before the storm. Before she read enough of it to understand what she was holding.

You see the flaw in the plan. You see it immediately. Everyone sees it immediately. That's the trouble with plans that require a clever woman to find a dangerous document and hand it over unopened — they work beautifully right up until the moment they don't.

* * *

I started with the desk.

Not because I expected to find the ledger there — Emory wouldn't have left it within a thousand miles of his daughter's pillow — but because a man learns things from desks. Desks are confessions too. I opened each drawer the way you're supposed to open other people's drawers: slowly, noting the resistance, noting where the runners were worn bright with use and where they were still dark.

Top drawer: charts of the lower channel, annotated in a hand I recognized with a jolt I did not enjoy. Emory's hand. Small, precise letters pressed hard into the paper, the way he did everything — as if the ink might try to escape him otherwise. Depth soundings. Tide corrections. And in the margin of one chart, next to a sandbar that had shifted years ago, a single line: *C. — check this yourself. Never trust a chart drawn by a man in a hurry.*

He'd been writing to her for years. Little instructions tucked into the margins of her working life, like coins left under a pillow. I wondered if she'd noticed. I wondered if she'd noticed that some of the annotations weren't in his hand at all — but no, that was my paranoia talking. Probably.

Second drawer: logbooks, older ones, spines cracked. I flipped through them with gloved fingers — I'm not an amateur — looking for gaps. Logbooks lie by omission more often than by invention, and omissions leave scars: pages cut out, dates skipped, entries in different inks. Coral's logs were clean. Annoyingly clean. Either she was scrupulous or she kept her real records somewhere else, and given what I knew about what happened to her memory of the night the *Meridian* sank, I suspected the second.

Ah, yes. Her memory. Let's speak plainly, since no one else aboard can hear us.

Four years ago, after the wreck, Coral Underhill lost one night. Not her nerve, not her livelihood, not her taste for the river — just one night, gone clean as a page cut from a logbook. The doctors called it trauma. The old crew called it grief. I called it convenient, privately, and then I stopped calling it anything, because thinking about it too hard made my hands sweat.

Because here is what I know that she doesn't: she wasn't home that night. She was supposed to be ashore. Instead she was seen near the *Meridian*'s berth in the hours before she went down — seen by a dockhand I later paid to forget, paid generously, paid twice. If Coral ever remembers that night, she won't just remember her father arguing with someone. She might remember *me*. She might remember what I said to him on the quay, or what he said back, or — God help me — she might remember whether she saw the scuttling party go aboard.

That is the true reason I hired her. Not the salvage. Not the debt I hold over her berth fees, though the debt is real and I intend to collect it like a gentleman. I hired her because a woman hunting her own missing night is a woman too busy to notice what she's dredging up. I aimed her at the wreck hoping she'd find the ledger and never find herself.

And every day that passes, the storm comes closer, and the odds of both get worse.

* * *

Under the third drawer, false bottom. Of course there was a false bottom; Coral Underhill doesn't strike me as a woman who trusts drawers any more than I do. Beneath it: a canvas pouch of coin — emergency money, decent sum, none of my business — and a folded scrap of oilcloth wrapped around something flat.

My heart performed a small, undignified maneuver.

I unwrapped it on her bunk. A photograph. Coral, maybe nineteen, standing on the deck of the *Meridian* with her father's arm around her shoulders, both of them squinting into sun. On the back, in Emory's hand: *My captain, someday.*

I sat down on the bunk of the woman whose ruin I was arranging and looked at that photograph for longer than was professionally advisable.

I want to say something in my own defense here, and I ask you to understand that I've had very little practice. I did not kill Emory Voss. Whatever the ledger says, whatever the insurance investigators suspected, whatever Reyes implies with her talent for implying — I falsified the manifest, yes. I overloaded the insurance valuation, yes. I arranged for the *Meridian* to be worth far more dead than alive, and I made sure the weather report reached every broker but the one who mattered. That is fraud, and fraud has a face, and the face is mine.

But the scuttling party went wrong. The charges were meant to open her slow, below the waterline, in deep water past the Reach, where she'd settle soft and the claim would pay and no one would drown. Instead she broke early, in the channel, in the teeth of the front — and Emory was aboard because Emory was always aboard, because he trusted his ship more than he trusted me, and history has ruled his trust insufficient.

Did I know he was aboard? I have answered that question so many ways in the dark that I no longer know which answer is the true one. There is a version of the night where I knew and sent him anyway. There is a version where I genuinely believed he'd gone ashore. Both versions end with me standing on the quay watching her go down, and both versions end with the same sound, which was not the sound of the hull breaking. It was smaller than that. It was the sound of a man on the water shouting a girl's name — *Coral, Coral* — into weather that swallowed it whole.

She survived. She always survives; it's her defining feature and my recurring nightmare. And she came out of the water having lost the night, and I told myself the river had done me a kindness.

The river does not do kindnesses. The river does arithmetic. And someday soon it was going to present her the sum.

* * *

I put the photograph back exactly as I'd found it, angle and all. I finished the cabin in twenty minutes — under the mattress, behind the chart shelf, inside the seam of the bunk frame, the hollow leg of the washstand. Nothing. No ledger, no strongroom key, no note in Emory's hand saying *if you're reading this, Dario, you're closer than you deserve.*

Which confirmed what I'd already begun to suspect: the ledger wasn't aboard the *Kestrel*. It was aboard the *Meridian*, in the strongroom, exactly where the insurance survey said the strongroom was, exactly where Emory's last known position put him when the water came in. He'd died sealing it inside. He'd died choosing the paper over the lifeboat, or over the swim, or over whatever chance the river offered him. Men don't do that for documents. Men do that for daughters.

I closed the cabin door softly behind me and nearly walked into Ilsa Brandt.

She filled the companionway the way weather fills a horizon. She looked at me. She looked at the door. She looked at me again, and I watched her perform the entire calculation — what he touched, what he found, what he wants — behind eyes that had been counting cards since before I learned to cheat.

"Find your survey notes?" she asked.

"Every one of them," I said. "Thorough boat you run."

"Aye." She stepped aside just enough to let me pass, which was a courtesy and a threat in equal measure. "Captain says six tides till landfall. Fleet's got five. You want to tell me again why you're paying us to race a storm?"

"Because I believe in you, Brandt."

"No, you don't." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've seen this year. "But you need us. That's better. Needs keep a man honest longer than belief does."

I went down the gangway onto the dock feeling lighter and heavier at once, which I believe is the technical definition of guilt. Behind me the *Kestrel* creaked at her lines, patient as a trap. Ahead, the sky over the eastern channel had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom, and the harbor clock said I had five tides to make sure that when Coral Underhill opened her father's strongroom, she found the ledger —

— and never got far enough into it to learn what it cost her father to write.

Five tides. One daughter. One document that could hang me or free me, depending entirely on who read it first.

I tipped my hat to the storm clouds, because a man should be polite to the things that are coming for him, and went to find Captain Reyes.
