## 9. Night Currents

There are two ways to run a river at night. The honest way is with lamps lit and anchor down, hailing any vessel that crosses your path like a neighbor over a fence. The other way is the way I was doing it: every lamp doused, running black on a falling tide through water I could not see, trusting my hands to remember what my eyes were forbidden to know.

Ilsa stood at the bow with the lead line, swinging it in slow arcs, calling depths in a voice pitched just above the wind.

"Nine feet."

"Nine," I repeated, though she could hear me fine. You say the numbers back so the river knows you're listening. Superstition, maybe. But on the Kestrel, superstition is load-bearing.

"Eight and a half."

The channel was narrowing. Somewhere behind us, past the bend at Harrow's Point, Captain Reyes's fleet would be weighing anchor, lanterns blooming along her rail like a festival we hadn't been invited to. She'd have seen our lights if we'd shown them. She'd have read our heading, guessed our speed, and been waiting for us at the wreck with that patient, terrible smile of hers — the one she used to wear when she caught me miscalculating as a girl, before she taught me to stop making mistakes worth catching.

So: dark. No lights, no hail, no courtesy. Just the Kestrel and the current and me, steering by a map drawn inside my own skull.

"Eight," said Ilsa.

"I hear you."

"You say that like hearing is the same as doing something about it."

"It usually is."

"Not tonight." She swung the lead again. "Seven and a half."

That was wrong. The chart said seven and a half didn't come until the second reach past the split willow, and we weren't there yet — or we were, and I'd misjudged the tide more than I'd admitted to myself, which was possible, because admitting things to myself has never been my strongest suit. I eased the wheel a half-point to starboard, feeling for the deeper water on the outside of the bend, and the Kestrel answered with that sluggish, heavy-shouldered roll she gets when her keel is closer to the mud than to the sky.

Then the current took an interest in us.

Rivers do that at night. By day you can see the seams in the water — the slick lines where two currents meet and argue, the boil where a bar shoals up without warning. In the dark those seams vanish, and the river becomes one continuous lie. We crossed one of those invisible arguments and the bow swung hard to port, fifteen degrees, twenty, the wheel going light in my hands like a thing dying.

"Six," called Ilsa. Then, sharper: "Coral. Six *and soft*."

Soft. Mud under the lead instead of sand. Mud meant we'd left the channel entirely. Mud meant that in about four seconds the Kestrel was going to plant her nose in the bank at eight knots, and no amount of swearing — and I can swear in three languages, four if you count the dialect Ilsa's grandmother invented specifically for occasions like this — was going to unplant her.

I did everything right. That's the bitter joke of it. I threw the wheel hard over, kicked the engine astern, shifted weight aft — textbook, all of it, the moves Reyes drilled into me when I was sixteen and still cried at funerals. And the river ignored all of it, because the river does not read textbooks, and the current had us by the shoulder like a drunk friend who will not be reasoned with.

What saved us was Ilsa.

She didn't ask permission. She never does. She dropped the lead line, crossed the deck in three strides, and put her whole weight on the starboard rail — then shouted down the hatch for Tam and the boy, and the two of them came up half-dressed and threw themselves against the port side, shifting two hundred pounds of ballast and one hundred eighty pounds of quartermaster from one gunwale to the other. The Kestrel groaned, listed, argued — and then, grudgingly, like a mule deciding the alternative is worse, she came around. Her keel kissed the mud once, a long shuddering scrape I felt in my teeth, and slid off into deep water the way a hand slides off silk.

We drifted in the middle of the channel, engine idling, nobody speaking. Somewhere ahead a night bird made its idiot complaint. My hands were shaking on the wheel, and I watched them shake with a kind of detached academic interest, as though they belonged to someone I was studying.

"Well," I said finally. "That was nearly educational."

Ilsa turned around slowly. Even in the dark I could see the set of her jaw, which is a skill you develop after twenty years of sharing a boat with someone. "Coral."

"Don't."

"Coral, we just scraped paint off this boat's bones because you're driving her like a woman being chased. Which we are. Fine. But chased toward *what*?" She came aft, boots quiet on the wet planks. "Six tides ago you wouldn't say the word Meridian out loud. Now we're running dark past Reyes's fleet, burning our engine hours, risking the hull — my hull, I remind you, I hold the mortgage on your sense of self-preservation — for a wreck we all swore off standing over. So you're going to tell me what we're hunting. Not salvage. Salvage doesn't make you drive like this."

* * *

Here is what I should have told her: everything. That Dario Fenwick had come aboard with a smile like a knife laid flat, offering me the one job in the world I had sworn on my father's grave never to take. That he knew the exact figure of my debt to the last coin, which meant he'd done his homework, which meant he wanted something badly enough to pay for it in leverage. That the Meridian went down eleven years ago carrying my father's fortune and my father's body, and that I had been there that night, and that I remembered nothing of it — nothing, a hole in my head the size of a shipwreck, a gap I had somehow never once thought to question until Ilsa herself pointed at it and asked why I wasn't screaming.

Instead I heard myself say: "It's money, Ilsa. It's money and it's answers, and the two happen to be sitting in the same strongroom."

"That's not an answer. That's a brochure."

"The ledger's real. Whatever else Fenwick wants, the cargo manifest of the Meridian is worth enough to buy this boat twice over and retire us all to somewhere with better weather." I kept my voice level, which took work, because my voice wanted very badly to shake. "You want truth? Here's some. I don't trust him. I've never trusted him. But he's the only door to that wreck, and Reyes is the only other one, and I'd rather owe Fenwick than Reyes."

"Why?"

"Because Fenwick wants something from me. Reyes just wants me to go away."

Ilsa was quiet for a while. The current carried us, patient now, forgiving, the way rivers are between attempts on your life. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice she uses for the things that matter.

"There's something you're not saying. There has been since Fenwick stepped aboard. You look at him like a woman reading a letter she already knows the ending of." She leaned against the rail beside me, close enough that I could smell river water and tar on her. "Eleven years ago, the night the Meridian went down, you came back to this boat soaked to the skin and gray as ash, and you slept for two days, and when you woke up you never spoke of it again. None of us pushed. We'd lost your father; we figured grief does what grief does. But I've started wondering lately, Coral, whether grief is really what happened to you that night. Or whether something else did."

The words went through me like cold water through a cracked hull.

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I've watched you not-think about that night for eleven years. Very deliberately not-think. Like a tongue working a missing tooth." She paused. "And tonight, when I mentioned the vow — the one we all swore on the rail, the night we buried what was left — you looked at me like I'd spoken in tongues. You didn't remember the vow, Coral. You didn't remember *making* it."

"I remember—" I stopped. I reached for it, the way you reach for a name on the tip of your tongue, and found the same smooth blankness Ilsa had described, a wall where a memory should be. I had assumed, all these years, that I simply chose not to revisit it. Chosen. As if forgetting were a decision you could make and keep making, tidy as a folded chart.

But you can't fold a chart so well you forget the coastline exists.

"No," I said quietly. "No, I don't remember it. Ilsa — I don't remember any of it. Not the vow. Not coming back to the boat. Not—" My voice did the shaking thing after all. "Not how I got off the Meridian. Not whether I saw him. Nothing. There's just a night that ends and a morning that begins, and I've been sailing between them for eleven years like it was normal."

Ilsa put her hand over mine on the wheel. Hers was rough and warm and steady, and I hated how much steadiness I took from it.

"That's not grief," she said. "Grief hurts. This doesn't hurt. This is a room with the door bricked over." She squeezed once, then let go. "Somebody or something closed that door in you, Captain. And I think you took Fenwick's contract because part of you already knew the only way through that wall is from the other side."

* * *

We ran the rest of the reach slow, lights still dark but dignity restored, Ilsa on the lead line and Tam forward with the foghorn ready in case anything bigger than us came out of the night. Twice we heard engines upstream — Reyes's fleet feeling their way down with lamps blazing, cautious as tourists — and twice we slid into the willow shadow of the bank and let them pass, close enough that I could see the gleam on Captain Reyes's brass rails, close enough to count the dive barges lashed to her flanks. Five barges. She hadn't come to warn anyone off. She'd come to strip the wreck to the rivets.

"She chartered heavy," Tam murmured, watching the lights dwindle astern. "That's not a warning expedition. That's a war."

"That's a funeral," I said, and didn't examine why I was so sure.

By the small hours the sky had gone the color of wet slate in the east, and the wind had picked up out of the southeast with a new taste in it — salt and rot and something electric underneath. The monsoon was still days out by the almanac. But almanacs are written by people who sleep indoors.

Ilsa came up from below with two cups of coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in, and handed me mine without ceremony, which is her version of absolution.

"So," she said. "Strongroom. Ledger. Fenwick. And a hole in your head you've been politely ignoring since before Tam could shave." She settled against the rail. "Here's my condition, Captain, and it's not negotiable. From here on, no more brochures. Every time you learn something — about the wreck, about Fenwick, about that night — I hear it the same hour you do. Because whatever's down in that strongroom, it's got teeth, and I've seen what it's doing to your steering already."

"And if what I learn puts the crew in danger?"

"Then I'll tell you to turn this boat around, and you'll weigh it honestly, and maybe we will. That's what crew means." She drank her coffee. "But you don't get to carry it alone anymore. Not because I doubt you can. Because I've watched you try for eleven years, and look where it got us — scraping bottom in the dark, chasing a ghost for a man who smiles too easily."

I looked east, where the sky was beginning to bruise, and thought about my father's charts rolled in their oilcloth below, and the second handwriting in the margins that I couldn't stop seeing when I closed my eyes. *Someone annotated his charts,* I'd thought. *Someone who knew his routes.* Someone whose hand I almost recognized.

Someone whose face, I was beginning to suspect, was the blurred figure waiting behind that bricked-over door.

"All right," I said. "Everything I learn, you learn. Same hour."

"Same hour," Ilsa agreed. Then she nodded forward, toward the low green line of the coast where the river widened toward the sea. "Because unless my eyes are lying, that's smoke on the horizon ahead of us. And we're still two tides from the wreck site."

I raised the glass. Far off, where the channel met the open water, a thin gray plume stood against the dawn — not cooking smoke, not river smoke. Coal smoke, and plenty of it, hanging where no honest vessel had any business hanging.

Someone had reached the Meridian's stretch of water before us. Someone with engines to feed and time to burn.

Ilsa watched my face and read it the way she reads water. "How far?"

"Half a day, if the tide's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

I handed her back the glass and put the wheel over, and the Kestrel leaned into the turn like she'd been waiting all night for me to ask.

"Then somebody's diving my father's grave," I said, "and we're about to catch them at it."
