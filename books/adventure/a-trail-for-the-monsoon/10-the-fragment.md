## 10. The Fragment

The river tried to drown me on a Tuesday, which felt about right. Rivers don't respect your schedule, and neither do Tuesdays.

We were running the Sable Cut at half tide — a stretch of channel my father used to call "the river's bad temper" — because Reyes's fleet had anchored across the mouth of the delta like a hand closing into a fist, and the only way past was through water that no sane pilot would attempt with a boat this heavy. The Kestrel drew six feet loaded. The Sable Cut offered seven, sometimes. Sometimes.

"I can take us around," Ilsa said, standing at my shoulder the way she always did when she thought I was about to do something stupid. She'd been saying it since we were nineteen. She was right roughly half the time.

"Around is where Reyes is."

"Around is where the water is deep enough to hold the boat."

"That's debatable." I eased the wheel two degrees starboard and watched the current tell me lies. The Sable Cut runs fast when it runs at all, and the falling tide had turned it into something less like a river and more like an argument between the sea and the land, with us as the subject under dispute.

I want to be honest here, since honesty has been in short supply aboard this vessel: part of me wanted the danger. Not in the way fools want it — not for the taste of it — but because fear does something useful to me. It sands off the varnish. When the water gets mean, I stop thinking about ledgers and debts and Dario Fenwick's smile, and I become a single clean instrument: hands, eyes, river. For three days I'd been carrying the weight of that hull plate stamped with a date that shouldn't exist, and the weight was making me stupid. Fear would burn it off.

Or it would kill me. The river offers both services and doesn't advertise which one you've purchased until the transaction is complete.

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong on water: gradually, then all at once.

The first sign was a smell — green water over mud, the river breathing out before it holds its breath. Then the current shifted a full point against us in the space of a hundred yards, some back-eddy from the cut's blind bend folding over itself, and the Kestrel's bow swung left as if she'd changed her mind about everything.

"Hard aport," Ilsa said, already moving.

"No." I could feel it — the way you feel a lie in someone's voice. Aport was where the sandbar lived, the one my father's chart marked with three small crosses and a note in his cramped hand: *it moves. trust nothing.* "Hold her. Give me ten seconds of patience."

The river gave me four.

The stern caught first. I felt it as a shudder up through the deck plates, a long grinding kiss of keel on sand, and then the current took the bow and swung us broadside like a door opening, and suddenly the Sable Cut was pouring over our gunwale on the port side, cold and brown and absolutely indifferent to my plans.

"Off the wheel!" Ilsa shouted. "Coral — *off the wheel!*"

There's a moment in every disaster where you understand, with perfect clarity, that the thing you're doing is wrong and you keep doing it anyway, because stopping means admitting the disaster is real. I held that wheel for two more seconds. Two seconds is nothing. Two seconds is everything.

Then the water took me.

Not dramatically — rivers are never dramatic, they're just thorough. One moment I was braced against the helm, the next the deck tilted past vertical and the river folded over me like a hand closing, and I was inside it, inside the brown roaring dark, with no up and no down and the Kestrel's hull groaning somewhere above me like a large animal in pain.

I have nearly drowned twice before. Both times, I remember thinking clearly. This time there was no clarity. There was only the water, and then—

* * *

—only lamplight, and rain on glass, and my father's voice raised in anger.

I was standing in the *Meridian*'s cabin. I knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a dream — the brass fittings gone green at the seams, the chart table scarred where he'd driven a divot with a compass point, the rain coming down so hard against the windows that the harbor lights outside had smeared into long yellow wounds. My hands were smaller. I was wearing a coat too big for me, his coat, sleeves rolled four times.

He was arguing with someone.

"—won't sign it, Dario, and that's the end of it—" No. That wasn't right. He wasn't shouting. That was the terrible thing, the thing I'd forgotten without ever knowing I'd forgotten it: he wasn't shouting at all. He was quiet. My father quiet, the quiet he saved for weather and men he'd stopped respecting. "You'll file the manifest as it stands or you'll find another captain, and if you find another captain, I'll find another auditor."

And the other man said something I couldn't hear. His voice came through like sound underwater — which, I suppose, is exactly what it was — a murmur with a shape to it, smooth and reasonable and wrong. I strained toward him. In the memory I moved, stepped forward, and the lamplight fell across the table between them, across my father's hands flat on the wood, across the other man's hands — one hand resting easy, rings catching the light, a ring with a dark stone —

His face was a smear. Not shadowed, not turned away — *smeared*, as though the river had gotten into the memory itself and blurred him the way it blurs a reflection. I knew his build. I knew the way he stood, weight on one hip, patient as a creditor. I knew the ring.

"Coral." My father turned and looked directly at me, and his eyes were wet, and not from the rain. "Go below. Whatever happens tonight, whatever you hear — go below and stay there."

"Dad—"

"*Stay there.*" And then, softer, in the voice he used only for the worst truths: "Some debts get paid by the man who made them. That's not cruelty, little bird. That's arithmetic."

The other man laughed. Low, warm, horrible. And the lamplight guttered, and the rain redoubled, and the cabin began to fill — not with water, not yet, but with a darkness that rose like a tide, and my father's face was the last thing above it, and he was mouthing something, three words, four, and I couldn't read them, I couldn't—

* * *

Ilsa hauled me out of the water by the collar of my coat, which is how most of the important events in my life have ended: with Ilsa's grip on my collar and her opinion arriving shortly after.

The Kestrel had grounded herself on the bar, mercifully, port rail awash but settled, the current pushing us firm onto the sand instead of over it. Half the deck cargo was soaked. The dinghy was gone. Nobody was dead, which on the Sable Cut counts as a triumph worth framing.

I knelt on the tilted deck and vomited river water and then, when that was done, I couldn't stand up. Not because of the water in my lungs. Because of the water in my head.

"Coral." Ilsa crouched beside me. Her hair was plastered flat and her hands were shaking — hers, shaking, Ilsa's, which frightened me more than the grounding had. "Look at me. How many fingers."

"One. Ilsa, I—"

"How many."

"One. I'm fine. I'm—" I stopped. Started again. "I remembered something."

Her hands went still. "Remembered what?"

"The night the Meridian sank. I was there. Ilsa, I was *on her*. I was in the cabin and my father was alive and he was arguing with—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "With someone. I saw his hands. I saw a ring. I heard my father say *some debts get paid by the man who made them*, and then he told me to go below, and Ilsa — Ilsa, why don't I remember any of this? Why have I never remembered any of this?"

The question came out louder than I meant it to, and it kept going, gathering speed downhill. "Everyone talks about that night like it's scripture. You recite it. The whole crew swore the vow on it. And I nod along like I was there, like I grieved it properly, and the truth is I have a *hole* where that night should be and I never once wondered about the hole. What kind of person doesn't wonder? What kind of daughter—"

"Stop." Ilsa took my face in both hands, river-cold palms against my cheeks, and made me look at her. "You hit your head on the helm. You swallowed half the Cut. Your mind just handed you something it's been sitting on for years, and you're going to sit down, drink this—" she pressed a flask into my hand, "—and you're going to tell me all of it, slowly, once, and then we're going to float this boat. In that order."

So I told her. All of it. The lamplight, the rain, the manifest, the auditor, the ring with the dark stone, the smear where a face should have been. My father's hands flat on the table. *Little bird.* The words he mouthed at the end that I still couldn't read.

Ilsa listened without moving. When I finished she looked out at the Cut for a long time, at the water sliding past our grounded hull, brown and patient and keeping its secrets the way it always had.

"You were sixteen," she said finally. "You'd stowed away aboard the Meridian. Everyone knew except your father, who pretended not to, because pretending not to know things was how he loved people." She paused. "That's the story. That's the story we all agreed on."

"And the hole?"

"The doctor on Marrow Key said drowning-adjacent trauma does strange things. We let it be strange." Her jaw worked. "Coral. If your father was arguing about a manifest — if he said what you say he said — then the Meridian didn't sink. She was *sunk*. And somebody on that dock the next morning already knew it."

The flask was in my hand and I hadn't drunk from it. I drank now. It tasted like kerosene and forgiveness, which is roughly what Ilsa keeps in flasks.

"Dario Fenwick wears a ring with a dark stone," I said.

"He does."

"He wore it the night I met him at the rail. He was turning it while he talked. I noticed it and thought nothing of it."

"People rarely think anything of it," Ilsa said. "That's rather the point of Dario."

I got to my feet. My legs held, mostly. Below us the keel ground gently against the bar, and above us the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and far out over the open water the air had that pressed-in, breath-held quality that old pilots learn to read the way priests read scripture. The monsoon was early. Or we were late. With the weather, those amount to the same thing.

"Get the crew on the capstan," I said. "We lighten her forward, warp off the bar on the next flood pulse, and we make for the wreck site tonight if the sky gives us even one hour of grace."

Ilsa stared at me. "You nearly died thirty seconds ago."

"Ninety seconds ago I was chasing a fortune." I looked east, toward the delta, toward the shallow grave where the Meridian lay holding my father's last argument in her flooded ribs. "Now I'm chasing the truth. It's faster than money. I need more sail."

For a moment she just looked at me — the look she saves for the moments when she decides whether to follow me into the fire or drag me out of it. Then she turned and started bellowing names down the hatch, and the Kestrel came alive around us, ropes and boots and curses, my family assembling itself out of habit and loyalty and, I suspect, a certain grim curiosity about what their captain would do next.

Because here is what the river gave me back, along with my lungsful of Sable Cut: my father hadn't drowned by accident, and he hadn't drowned alone in his knowledge. He had known. He had stood in that cabin with death already in the room, wearing a man's cologne and a patient smile, and he had chosen to send me below rather than tell me — chosen to pay the debt himself, in the dark, with arithmetic.

Well. I had grown up. I had learned sums of my own.

And the first thing I intended to calculate was exactly how much a falsified manifest weighs, measured in the only currency Dario Fenwick understood.

The sky gave us forty minutes of grace. It would have to be enough.
