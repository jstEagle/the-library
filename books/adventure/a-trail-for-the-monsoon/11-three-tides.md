## 11. Three Tides

The barometer on the Kestrel's wheelhouse wall had been my father's, brass-cased and stubborn as a mule with opinions. It had hung in three cabins before this one and survived two groundings, one mutiny attempt, and Ilsa's cooking. That morning it read lower than I'd ever seen it outside of a hurricane warning, and it was still falling like a debt collector down a ladder.

"Three tides," Ilsa said, standing in the doorway with the morning coffee that passed, in our crew's generous accounting, for breakfast.

I looked at her over the instrument. "The forecast said four."

"The forecast said a lot of things." She came in and set the mug on the chart table without ceremony, which was how Ilsa did everything except dive, at which she was all ceremony, all ritual, checking her valves twice and kissing her own knuckles like a boxer before going down. "Radio station at Port Meridian pulled their bulletin an hour ago. Storm's curved north. Landfall tomorrow night, maybe early the day after. The leading edge gets here first."

I did the arithmetic the way you do when the numbers are bad — twice, hoping they'll improve out of shame. Three usable tides. Maybe. If the sea kept its appointments, which the sea had never once done in my experience but always claimed it would.

"And the fleet?"

Ilsa's jaw did the thing it did when she was choosing between honesty and kindness. She chose honesty; she always did, eventually. "They weighed anchor before dawn. Reyes is running lights and full steam for the wreck site. They'll be on the Meridian by this afternoon."

"They were supposed to wait out the front like sensible people."

"Sensible people don't have Dario Fenwick paying by the day," Ilsa said. "Or whatever he's paying them. Or promising them."

That was the part that sat wrong in my stomach, next to the coffee. Fenwick had chartered us both — I knew that now, knew it the way you know a bruise is there even through your shirt. Two crews, two sets of hands digging in the same mud, and only one of them meant to walk away with what was buried. He was playing us against each other like current against current, and somewhere in the middle of that turbulence was a ledger with my father's handwriting in it, and a letter I hadn't found yet but could feel, the way you feel weather coming through a bad tooth.

Forty minutes of grace, I'd told myself yesterday. I'd spent thirty-nine of them rigging extra lift bags and arguing with our engineer about whether the winch motor would survive salt water it had already survived twice before. The fortieth minute I'd spent looking at the horizon and not liking its face.

Now the horizon had come closer to make its point personally.

"All right," I said. "We run for the site. Full steam, inside channel until the Cut, then we'll see what the tide gives us."

Ilsa didn't move. "Coral."

"Ilsa."

"There's a fishing family on the flats off Gull Point. The Amara brothers. You know them — the ones who sold us mullet last season and shorted us half the basket and we let it go because their mother was sick." She said it flatly, but I heard the weight under it. "Their boat went down on the morning ebb. Skiff swamped taking the shortcut across the shoal because they were running from the same weather we are. Coast cutter's been recalled to port ahead of the storm. Nobody else is going."

I looked at the chart. Gull Point was a detour — ninety minutes, maybe two hours with the wind quartering the way it was building to. Two hours against a rival fleet that had a head start and no conscience.

Two hours against three tides.

"You're telling me this because?"

"Because you're the captain," Ilsa said, "and because I already turned the wheel two points toward Gull Point and I wanted to see if you'd notice."

I noticed. I'd noticed the moment she came in, actually — the compass card sitting a hair off the course I'd penciled the night before, the kind of small mutiny Ilsa committed perhaps once a year and never without cause. I drank the coffee. It was terrible. It was also, in its way, an argument, and so was the compass, and so was the falling needle on my father's barometer, and so was the memory of a man standing in a cabin with death wearing cologne, choosing arithmetic over his daughter.

There are choices you make with your head and choices you make with your hands. My head said: race. My father's ledger, my father's truth, my father's killers getting a two-hour head start on the proof. Every mile of detour was a mile of ocean floor someone else might strip bare while I played ferryman.

But my hands were already moving the engine telegraph to slow ahead, and my mouth was saying, "Take us in past the point, mind the third sandbar, it migrates after every blow," and I understood, with the particular clarity that comes too late to be useful and just in time to be borne, that this was the real salvage operation. Everything else was just diving.

* * *

Gull Point in a rising sea looks like the back of a drowning animal — a long gray spine of dune grass with the water climbing its flanks. We came around it with the wind on our port beam, pushing us toward the shoals faster than I liked, and I took the wheel myself because some helms you don't delegate.

The Amaras' skiff was exactly where Ilsa's information put it, or rather where it wasn't: awash to the gunwales, mast snapped at the partners, and the three of them — two brothers and a boy of maybe twelve who must have been a nephew — clinging to the hull's upturned bottom like barnacles with opinions about the weather.

"Line!" I shouted, and Tomás, our deckhand, had the heaving line coiled and ready before I finished the word, because that's why you drill, that's why you drill when the sea is flat and everyone grumbles about wasted rope. First throw went wide, snatched sideways by the gusts. Second throw caught the older brother across the shoulders and he wrapped it around his wrist with the desperate competence of a man who has hauled nets his whole life and knows exactly what a line is worth.

We brought them aboard one at a time. The boy last, because the boy was the worst off — he'd been under at some point, I could tell by the way he coughed, that deep wet rattle that means water where water shouldn't be. Ilsa wrapped him in blankets and worked him over with the brisk tenderness she reserved for the young and the foolish, which described most of humanity as far as she was concerned.

The older brother — Ravi Amara, face gray with cold, hands shaking so hard he couldn't hold the mug Tomás gave him — tried to stand to thank me and I pushed him back down onto the engine cover.

"Don't," I said. "You can thank me by never taking the shoal shortcut in a falling barometer again."

"We were trying to beat the storm to market," he said, and then, wretched: "The catch. The whole season's catch was in the hold—"

"Is in the hold," I corrected. "Skiffs sink slowly if they're loaded with fish and luck. We'll put a line on her and drag her off the flat before the tide finishes the job. Your catch will smell of diesel and regret, but it'll sell."

He stared at me. People do that sometimes, when they expect a lecture about the cost of rescue and get a towing operation instead. I didn't blame him. I'd spent enough years expecting the world to invoice me for every kindness that I'd stopped recognizing the ones that came free.

Except none of them come free. That was the lesson of the morning, arriving on schedule. While Tomás and Ilsa rigged the towline and the boy shivered himself warm against the boiler casing, I stood at the wheel and watched the east, where the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong — green-gray at the base, yellow-white above, the specific palette of a monsoon that means business. The wind had backed two points since dawn. The swell was coming in longer and deeper than the wind alone could account for, which meant it was being born somewhere far off, by something big, and it was traveling here with my name loosely attached to it.

Three tides. And I'd just spent the best part of one hauling strangers out of the water.

I want to be honest about what that felt like, because the wry version is easy and the true version costs more. The true version is that for about four minutes — while we waited for the towline to come taut, while the Kestrel strained against the skiff's weight and the shoal's grip — I hated them. Not the Amaras themselves, but the fact of them, the sheer gravitational pull of other people's emergencies, the way the river always seemed to know exactly when I had somewhere to be and produce precisely the obstacle calibrated to test whether I was the woman I claimed to be or just a woman with a good vocabulary for excuses.

Then the skiff came free with a suck of foam, and Ravi's brother — the younger one, Dev, who hadn't spoken since we pulled him aboard — looked at me across the deck and said, quietly, in the tone of a man settling accounts: "You're Underhill's girl."

"I'm Underhill's captain," I said.

"He talked about you. On the Meridian, before—" He stopped. The wind filled the pause with spray. "He said you read water the way other people read letters. That you'd know a lie in a chart the way you'd know a lie in a face."

My hands went very still on the wheel.

"When did he say that?" I asked, and I made my voice casual, and I failed completely, and Dev Amara was cold and frightened and honest enough not to pretend he hadn't noticed.

"Last season. He came out to our village buying fish for the boat's stores. Sat with my father talking about the old days." Dev shrugged, a small miserable motion. "He said the Meridian was carrying more than her manifest showed. He laughed when he said it. Like it was a joke he was in on."

* * *

We put the Amaras and their resurrected skiff ashore at the lee side of Gull Point, where their cousin kept a drying shed and a kettle, and refused the offer of payment twice and accepted a sack of smoked fish the third time, because refusing a third offer insults the cook, and Ilsa had raised me better.

Then we turned for the wreck site, and I stood at the wheel and did sums.

Lost: roughly two hours and forty minutes, counting the tow. The fleet had used those hours well; Ilsa spotted their masts from the crow's platform at midday, a cluster of dark shapes against the bruised horizon like teeth deciding whether to bite. Five vessels. Reyes' flagship, the Corvus, anchored square over the site with the confidence of a woman who owned the chart and the water and, as far as she was concerned, the past.

Three tides had become two and a half, and the half was dying fast.

"Coral." Ilsa came up beside me, salt-crusted, hair plastered flat. She nodded at the fleet. "Reyes will have divers down by the next slack. Shallow work first, mapping the debris field. The strongroom's deeper — she won't reach it today, not with that swell stirring the bottom. But tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow the storm owns everything," I finished. "So it's tonight or never."

"Visibility tonight will be garbage. Silt, surge, no light past ten feet."

"Yes," I said. "For us. Which means for them too."

Ilsa looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd already landed, the ugly arithmetic of it. Nobody dives well in conditions like tonight's. But somebody would dive anyway, because the prize was worth drowning for, and the question was only which somebody, and how many times, and whether the river gave them back.

"You're going down yourself," she said. Not a question either.

"I'm the only one who knows the Meridian's layout from the inside. I grew up on that deck, Ilsa. I know which passageway floods first and where my father kept the strongroom key taped when he thought nobody knew." I kept my eyes on the fleet. "And I'm the one the letter's addressed to, if there's a letter. Some doors open easier for the right hand."

"That's not how locks work."

"It's how grief works," I said. "Close enough."

She didn't argue. That was the thing about Ilsa — she argued with me about coffee, about charts, about the correct way to stow a mooring line, about everything cheap. About things that mattered she went silent as ballast and simply made sure she was standing next to me when the moment came. She turned to go below and check the dive gear, and then stopped, one hand on the companionway rail.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the Amara boy is going to live because of this morning. Whatever else happens out here — whatever we find or don't find, whoever beats us to that hole in the mud — that part's already banked. Nobody can dive it up and sell it."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't keep."

She grinned, quick and crooked, and went below.

I stayed at the wheel as the light thickened and the wind found its rhythm, long pulses with hard gusts threaded through them, the storm clearing its throat. Behind us, Gull Point sank into the haze. Ahead, the Corvus rode her anchor like a held breath, and I found myself thinking about Dev Amara's story — my father in a fishing village, laughing, saying the Meridian carried more than her manifest showed. Telling the truth to strangers because he couldn't tell it to anyone close. Sealing it somewhere instead, in ink and iron, and paying the postage with his life.

He'd known the water would bring it back to me someday. He'd said I read water like letters.

All right, old man, I thought, turning the wheel two points and feeling the Kestrel answer like she'd been waiting for the instruction all day. Let's see what you wrote.

The first rain hit the deck five minutes later, fat and warm and smelling of the whole drowned world, and the barometer needle settled at the bottom of its arc like a bird coming home to roost.

One tide left. Then the night, and the black water, and whatever my father had left me in the dark.
