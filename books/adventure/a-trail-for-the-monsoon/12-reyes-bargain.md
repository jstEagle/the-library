## 12. Reyes' Bargain

The truce flag went up at midmorning, which told me two things before I even raised the glass: Reyes wanted to talk, and Reyes thought I was stupid enough to believe a flag meant anything.

I'd known her twenty years. The flag meant she wanted to talk. Whether it meant anything else depended entirely on what happened after we finished.

"Captain," Ilsa said at my shoulder, "tell me you're not going to let her aboard."

"I'm going to let her aboard."

"Tell me why."

"Because if I don't, she'll say it on open water where my crew can hear, and half of what she says will be true." I folded the glass. "Better she lies to me in private."

"That's not a reason. That's a preference."

"It's the only kind of reason that's ever gotten me through a parley." I looked at her. "Rig the rail lines. If she so much as breathes wrong, cut her boat loose and let the current have her."

"And if she breathes right?"

"Then we'll both be disappointed."

* * *

Reyes came up the Kestrel's side like a woman boarding her own vessel, which was the oldest trick in her book and still worked, because for six years this had been her own vessel, before I bought her off her and renamed her and spent every season since proving the renaming wasn't vanity. She was sixty if she was a day, silver-cropped, hands like knotted rope, and she wore the same expression she'd worn the day I signed on as her deckhand at nineteen: the look of someone who already knows how your story ends and finds it mildly disappointing.

"Coral Underhill," she said. "Still flying the Kestrel's colors. Sentimental."

"Efficient. Everyone knows them, so nobody boards without asking." I gestured aft. "Coffee's terrible. Sit anyway."

She sat. She took the coffee. She drank it without flinching, which was either politeness or proof that she'd drunk worse, and knowing Reyes, both.

"You're anchored four hundred yards off my dive line," she said.

"Your dive line is over a wreck I hold salvage rights to."

"You hold paper. Paper burns." She set the cup down with the care of a woman who broke things professionally and regretted nothing. "I'm here to make you an offer before the weather makes it for you."

"The monsoon's early. I know."

"Two tides, maybe three. And you're diving blind on a hull nobody's mapped in nine years, with a crew of five and a rival fleet of three boats sitting between you and the deep channel." She leaned back. "So here's the offer. Fifty-fifty. My divers, my gear, my charts — and I have charts, Coral, better ones than your father ever drew — against your rights and your river sense. We lift the Meridian together, split the cargo, and neither of us drowns trying to out-stubborn the other."

I let the silence sit. It was a good offer. That was the problem. Reyes didn't make good offers; she made offers that looked good from the outside and had a hook buried in the middle like a baited eel pot.

"Fifty-fifty," I said. "You're generous all of a sudden."

"I'm realistic."

"You're never realistic. Realism is for people who can't afford ambition." I turned the cup in my hands. "Why now? You've had nine years to work this wreck. Why come to me with your hat out?"

Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt; I'd have bet the boat she wasn't capable of it — but something adjacent. Discomfort, maybe. Or the performance of it.

"Because I owe you a truth," she said, "and because I'd rather you hear it from me than from him."

"Him being Fenwick."

"Him being Fenwick." She watched me. "How much do you actually know about your contract, girl?"

"He hired me to raise the Meridian. He holds the insurance claim's residue. He pays me a percentage on recovery."

"And who negotiated your percentage?"

"He did. In his office. With his lawyer."

"With his lawyer," Reyes repeated, "who also drew up mine." She let that land. "Dario Fenwick chartered two crews, Coral. Two. You and me, same waters, same wreck, same contract language, signed within a week of each other. He calls it insurance. I call it what it is — he doesn't trust either one of us to come back honest, so he made sure that whatever we find, we find it competing, tired, and too busy watching each other to watch him."

The wind came around the point and pushed at the rigging, and the Kestrel leaned into it the way she always did, patient, like a horse waiting out a fly.

"Two crews," I said.

"Two crews."

"Which means whichever of us lifts the cargo first—"

"—delivers it to him. And whichever of us is slower—" She shrugged, a small, economical motion. "Well. He's paid us both advances. Advances are recoverable. There are clauses."

"There are always clauses."

"In yours there's a salvage-or-forfeit provision. Section eleven. Failure to effect recovery within the monsoon window constitutes abandonment of contract, and the advance becomes debt." She said it flatly, the way you read a depth sounding. "You signed it?"

"I signed a lot of things in a room that smelled like his cologne."

"Then you're not a partner, Coral. You're a second dart. He threw you at the board knowing that if you stuck, fine, and if you didn't, he'd throw me. And the part that should keep you up tonight—" she leaned forward, and for one unguarded moment the mentor I'd followed through three seasons of hell looked out of her face, old and tired and something like sorry, "—is that he doesn't need the cargo. Not primarily. Ask yourself what else could be on that wreck that a man like Dario Fenwick would spend this much money and this much risk to be first to touch."

I asked myself. I'd been asking myself since the hull plate came up with its impossible date stamped in the metal, since Ilsa surfaced holding nine years of official lying in her gloved hands.

"You know what it is," I said.

"I know what he's afraid of," Reyes said. "That's different, and it's more useful." She stood. "Fifty-fifty stands until sundown. After that I dive my line, and you dive yours, and may the best liar win."

"That's not how truces usually end."

"No," she agreed, moving to the rail. "Usually they end worse."

* * *

Ilsa found me at the wheel ten minutes later, which meant she'd been listening at the companionway, which meant she'd been listening at the companionway since before Reyes boarded, because Ilsa did not arrive at information, she camped on it.

"Two crews," she said.

"So you heard."

"I heard a woman who taught you everything you know offer to split your father's wreck down the middle and call it generosity." She planted herself in front of the wheel, arms crossed, blocking my view of the water as if the water were going to tell me something kinder. "And you're standing there doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"Not like this. This is the arithmetic where you decide whether to believe her." She dropped her voice. "Coral. If Fenwick chartered two boats, then everything he told you is a costume. The contract, the deadline, the percentage — none of it was ever about raising cargo. So what is he actually using us to find?"

"A ledger," I said, before I could stop myself.

It was the first time I'd said it aloud, even to myself, and saying it made it real in a way that thinking it hadn't. Ilsa's eyebrows went up a fraction, which on Ilsa was the equivalent of another woman screaming.

"You knew," she said. "You've known, or suspected, and you didn't say."

"I suspected. The repair date on that plate — the Meridian was patched eight months before she sank, in a yard that has no record of the work. Somebody fixed her hull quietly and then sank her anyway. That's not bad luck. That's paperwork." I gripped the wheel spokes. "There's a document on that wreck, Ilsa. Something that explains the repairs, the sinking, the insurance — something Dario needs found before anyone reads it. And he hired two crews to race each other to it."

"Why would he want it found at all?"

"Because found by a loyal hand is different from found by an enemy. He wants it lifted, delivered, and gone before anyone else looks at it." I heard my own voice go cold and distant, like I was reporting someone else's weather. "And if the wrong person finds it first—"

"He's covered. Clauses. Debts. Maybe worse." Ilsa studied me for a long moment. "And Reyes? Where does she sit in it?"

"She sits wherever it profits her. But she told me the truth about section eleven when she didn't have to, and that costs her leverage, and Reyes does not spend leverage for free." I turned the wheel a few degrees, unnecessary, just to have my hands doing something honest. "Either she's playing a longer game than Fenwick's, or somewhere under all that scar tissue she still remembers the girl who swabbed her decks."

"People don't change, Captain. They just run out of room to hide."

"Then she's running out of room," I said, "and I intend to be standing where she lands."

* * *

We weighed anchor an hour before slack water, which was earlier than any sane captain would move, and I took the Kestrel out along the eastern shallows where the chart showed a sandbar that hadn't been there when my father drew it and hadn't left since the river decided otherwise. The rival fleet sat heavy at their moorings, three silhouettes in the haze, and I felt their eyes on us the way you feel sunburn — not yet painful, but accounting for itself.

Reyes' offer turned over in my head with every mile. Fifty-fifty. Partnership. The word alone should have been enough to sink it; I had been somebody's partner once, on a night I couldn't remember, and the partnership had cost me a piece of my own mind and given me nothing back but the shape of the hole. But that wasn't what kept turning. What kept turning was the other thing she'd said, almost as an afterthought, the way you slip a knife into a conversation sideways:

Ask yourself what else could be on that wreck.

I knew what else. I'd known since the plate came up, since the fragments started coming back — my father's voice, alive, angry, arguing with a blurred figure on a dark deck nine years ago. He'd been arguing about something worth killing for, or dying for, and the Meridian had gone down with both the fortune and the answer sealed inside her.

And Dario Fenwick had hired me — me, specifically, the daughter of the man who'd drowned — to go down and fetch it.

Not despite who I was. Because of it. Because grief makes divers careless, and daughters make convenient deniability, and a woman missing one night of her memory will sign anything in a room that smells like cologne if the man across the desk tells her it's a chance to finish her father's work.

I was not a partner. I was not even a dart. I was a key, and keys don't get a share of what's behind the door.

"Captain," Ilsa called from the bow. "Bar's shoaling. Fifteen feet and falling."

"Come left two points. We'll thread it on the flood."

She came left two points. The Kestrel answered like she always did, and beneath my feet the old boat ran true over water that was lying about its depth, and I understood suddenly that I'd been piloting the same way for nine years — reading a surface that lied, trusting my feel for what lay underneath, and calling it mastery.

The bar fell away astern. Ahead, the channel opened toward the wreck site, toward the strongroom, toward whatever my father had died to seal shut.

"Tides?" I asked.

"Two full ones before the monsoon makes landfall. Maybe a third if the front drags its heels."

"Then we dive on the next one," I said. "Both of us. Rig everything."

Ilsa looked at me. "Everything?"

"Everything. Because Dario Fenwick sent two crews to that wreck, and one of them is going to come back with more than he bargained for." I put the wheel over. "It might as well be ours."
