## 13. The Blurred Face

The dream came back at slack water, which was fitting. Nothing moves at slack water. The river holds its breath, and so did I.

I'd been sleeping in the chart room again, half because the bunk in my cabin still smelled of Dario's cologne from the night I'd caught him pawing through my things — he claimed he was looking for a spare fathom of chain, as if a man like him had ever handled chain honestly in his life — and half because the charts were where my father lived now, spread out under the lamp with his careful hand all over them, soundings and shoals and little marginal notes in that cramped, patient script. *Sandbar shifts east after flood tide. Trust the birds, not the buoy.*

The second handwriting was there too, threaded between his lines like wire through rope. I still couldn't place it. But it was there, and it watched me while I slept, apparently, because the dream came anyway.

In the dream I was on the Meridian's aft deck, and it was raining the way it only rains before a monsoon — not falling so much as arriving, all at once, a wall of water with intent behind it. My father was shouting. Not at me. At someone whose face I couldn't hold, the way you can't hold water in a fist. And the figure raised one hand to point at him, or maybe to strike him, and on that hand —

A ring. Silver, heavy, worn on the index finger rather than the fourth, set with a stone the color of old bottle glass. Green-black. A man who wore his ring where it would catch light when he gestured, because he wanted to be seen doing whatever he was doing.

I woke with my heart going like a paddle wheel and my hand already closed around nothing, around a fistful of rain that wasn't there.

* * *

"You've got the look," Ilsa said at dawn, handing me coffee that could have caulked a hull. "The look that means you're about to do something the rest of us will have to live through."

"I remembered something."

She went very still. Ilsa can go stiller than anyone I've ever known; she once held position on a dive for four minutes while a bull shark made its opinions known, and she says the trick is deciding you're already dead, so there's nothing left to lose. She decided it now, standing on the deck of the Kestrel with steam rising off her mug into the wet air.

"Tell me."

"A man. On the Meridian's deck the night she went down. Arguing with my father." I turned the mug in my hands. "His face is gone. It's always been gone, every time. But this time I saw his hand."

"And?"

"Silver ring. Index finger. Green stone." I looked up at her. "You know what that describes."

"Say it anyway."

"Dario Fenwick wears that ring. Has worn it as long as I've known him. He used to turn it when he talked, back when we were young and stupid and racing each other upriver for sport." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected anger. What I got instead was something colder and more useful. "He was there, Ilsa. The night the Meridian sank. He wasn't in port like the report said. He was on her deck, arguing with my father, hours before she broke apart on the bar."

Ilsa drank her coffee slowly. "Or your memory gave you a face it knew. You hate the man. You're hunting a wreck he sent you to. Minds do that. They dress the villain in whoever's nearest."

"That's the sane interpretation."

"You don't want the sane interpretation."

"No," I admitted. "I want the true one. And I know exactly how to get it."

"How?"

"He doesn't know what I remember. If I show him the ring — describe it, watch him — he'll tell me whether it's true without meaning to. Men like Dario rehearse their lies, but they rehearse words. Hands are harder. Faces are harder. Give me five minutes across a table from him and I'll know."

Ilsa set down her mug. "And if it's true? If he was there?"

"Then everything changes shape." I looked south, toward the Meridian's grave, where the water lay brown and flat over forty feet of secrets. "Because if Dario scuttled her — if he killed my father — then he didn't hire me to salvage a fortune. He hired me to find the evidence. And every day I spend diving for him, I'm digging his shovel deeper."

* * *

Dario came out to the Kestrel at midday, which told me something already. Salvage men don't visit midday; they work midday. He came because he wanted to see my face, and men only want to see your face when they're checking it for damage.

We sat in the stern under the awning, and Ilsa poured three cups of coffee and then found urgent business forward, which was a lie so transparent it was practically a courtesy. Dario looked older than he had six tides ago. The fleet at anchor, Reyes' fleet, sitting fat and patient off the southern channel, had put lines around his eyes that hadn't been there before. Good. Worried men make mistakes.

"Coral." He said my name the way he always had, like he was tasting it for spoilage. "Your diver's been busy. Word travels."

"Word does. Water's shallow out there."

"Shallow enough to drown in." He smiled. He has a good smile; I'll grant him that. It reaches all the way to his eyes, which is what makes it dangerous — most liars smile with their mouths and their eyes stay home. Dario's eyes come along for the ride. That's why he's never lost a negotiation in his life. "A hull plate. Repair date after the official last survey. You must be pleased."

"I'm curious," I said. "Pleased comes later, when there's silver in it."

"Curiosity's expensive in this trade."

"So I've heard. Speaking of which—" I stretched, casual, and let my gaze drop to his hands folded on the table. The ring sat there. Silver, heavy, green-black stone, index finger. Exactly where memory had put it. "That's an ugly ring, Dario. How long have you worn it?"

Something crossed his face. I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left: it was not guilt. Guilt looks like flinching, like a man touching a bruise. This was calculation — the flicker of a man counting exits. Half a heartbeat, no more, and then the smile again, warm as bilgewater.

"This old thing?" He turned it with his thumb, slow, letting me look. "My father's. Wore it thirty years before me. Why?"

"Just noticed it. In a dream, actually."

"A dream." His eyebrows rose, amused, inviting me to share the joke.

"I dream about the Meridian sometimes. The night she went down. Funny thing, dreams — they fill in details you didn't know you'd lost." I kept my voice light, conversational, a woman making small talk over coffee. "Last night I dreamed about a man on her aft deck. Rain coming down hard. He was arguing with my father. I couldn't see his face." I paused, and let the silence do its work. "But I saw his ring."

And here is the thing I will carry to my grave, whatever else the river takes from me: Dario Fenwick did not stop smiling. He did not blink, or flush, or glance away, or do any of the hundred things an innocent man might do when accused by implication of murder. He laughed. A short, genuine-sounding laugh, and he leaned back and shook his head.

"There it is," he said. "There's the Coral Underhill I raced up the Sable years ago. You always did hunt ghosts, Coral. Your father too — God rest him, he chased phantom sandbars until one of them caught him." He spread his hands, ring catching the grey daylight, turning green. "Half the river wore rings like that. Dockmasters, pilots, every second mate with pretensions. You're dreaming of a stranger and dressing him in me because I'm the man you love to hate. It's flattering, truly. But it isn't evidence."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

"It's grief wearing a costume." He said it gently. Gently! As if he were doing me a kindness. "Coral, listen to me. I hired you because you're the best pilot on this coast and because — despite everything — I trusted you to bring my partner's fortune up out of that mud. There's no ledger, no conspiracy, no midnight argument. There's a wreck, a storm, a bad repair logged late, and two crews racing a monsoon. Everything else is the river talking in its sleep."

Everything else is the river talking in its sleep. I turned the phrase over while he kept talking — insurance shares, dive schedules, the shrinking window, Reyes moving early — and I understood, with a clarity so cold it felt like a new sense, that I was watching a master at work. Every word was placed. Every pause was measured. He'd had years to build this answer, and he delivered it like cargo, sealed and manifest-stamped.

But here's what he didn't count on. Here's what twenty years of reading water teaches you that no amount of rehearsal survives: he never once denied being there.

Listen to him again, the way I heard him. *You're dreaming of a stranger.* Not *I wasn't there.* *Half the river wore rings like that.* Not *this is the only one I've ever owned.* *There's no midnight argument.* Not *I never argued with your father.* He denied the conclusion and let the premises stand untouched, because a good liar knows that denying specifics invites verification, and verification is death. He lied the way you pilot a boat through fog — by never committing to a channel, by keeping open water on every side.

An honest man, hearing that story, would have said: *I was in Port Calloway that night, ask the harbormaster, ask anyone.* Dario said none of it. Dario talked about my grief instead.

"You're right," I said finally, and smiled at him, and I felt my own smile reach my eyes, because I'd learned that trick watching him. "It's probably nothing. Dreams are cheap."

"They are," he said, relieved, and relief is a confession if you know where to look.

He stayed another ten minutes, talking tonnage and tide tables, and I nodded in the right places, and when he'd gone over the side into his launch and the sound of his engine faded downstream, I stood at the rail with my hands white on the wood.

Ilsa came aft. "Well?"

"He was there."

"You can't know that."

"Yes I can." I turned to her. "He never said he wasn't. Twenty minutes of denial and he never once said *I wasn't on that ship*. Ilsa — he knew the argument happened. He just needed me to believe I hadn't seen it."

Ilsa was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Then why hire you? If you might remember—"

"Because he thinks the gap in my head is a wall." I looked south, toward the wreck, toward the strongroom I could feel waiting under the brown water like a held breath. "He thinks what I lost stays lost. But gaps aren't walls, Ilsa. They're doors. And mine is opening."

Below us, the tide had turned. I could feel it in the hull before the surface showed it — the long shudder of the river reversing itself, gathering strength, running toward the sea. Two tides of working water left before the monsoon closed the window, maybe less if the storm came early the way the glass was starting to say it would.

"Get your kit," I said. "We dive on the next slack. Whatever he's hiding, it's in that strongroom, and I'm done asking him politely."

Ilsa went forward to rouse the crew, and I stayed at the rail a moment longer, watching the current take the river south, carrying everything it carried — silt, driftwood, the wreckage of other people's plans — out toward the open water where the storm was building.

Dario had lied to me beautifully. But I had stopped needing him to tell the truth.

The river was telling it for him.
