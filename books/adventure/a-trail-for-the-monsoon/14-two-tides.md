## 14. Two Tides

The Meridian lay under us like a sleeping animal we'd finally tracked to its den.

We came over her at the turn of the morning tide, and I brought the Kestrel around slow, letting the current do half the work, because a riverboat handles like a drunk cathedral and you don't rush a drunk cathedral. Below us, forty feet down in water gone the color of steeped tea, was the hull that had ended my father's life and started every lie since. The lead line said thirty-eight feet. The soundings on his chart said thirty-six. The river had been eating her for eleven years, pulling her down into the mud one grain at a time, patient as a debt collector.

"Anchor's set," Tam called from the bow. "Holding good."

"Holding good," I repeated, though nothing holds good over a wreck. Anchors drag. Mud gives way. The whole business of standing still on moving water is a polite fiction we all agree to believe in.

Ilsa came up from below already dressed for the dive — canvas suit, weighted belt, the brass helmet sitting on the deck like something waiting to be fed. She'd been quiet since the confrontation with Dario. Not sulking-quiet. Calculating-quiet. There is a difference, and I have spent enough years around sailors to know which kind of silence to worry about.

"You're diving first," she said. It wasn't a question, but I treated it like one anyway.

"I am."

"Visibility down there will be nothing. You know that."

"I know it."

"And you're going anyway."

"Ilsa." I looked at her. "I've been diving this river since I could walk. My father taught me to read current by feel before he taught me letters. If anyone can find a strongroom hatch in soup, it's me."

"That's not what worries me."

"Then what does?"

She picked up the helmet, turned it over in her hands, set it down again. "You want it too much," she said. "People who want things too much underwater die of wanting them. They stay down past the line. They push the air. They tell themselves one more minute, one more fathom, one more try. And then the river keeps them."

I wanted to argue. The trouble with Ilsa is that she's usually right, and being right is an insufferable quality in a friend.

"Fine," I said. "You dive first. I'll tend your line. We'll trade off at the second slack."

Something eased in her shoulders. "That's all I'm asking."

"It's not all you're asking. You're asking me to be careful, and I've never once managed it. But I'll pretend, for you."

* * *

The first dive told us what the storm had already begun to suspect: the river was closing its accounts.

Ilsa went down at slack water with the air hose paying out behind her, and I stood at the rail with my hand on the line, reading it the way you read a pulse. For twenty minutes it spoke to me in small tugs — here a snag, there a shift, now the steady pull that meant she was moving along the hull. Then it stopped speaking altogether, and a tendline goes very loud when it goes silent.

"She's found something," Tam said beside me.

"She's found something or something's found her. Same signal."

We waited. The monsoon light had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had come around to the northeast, which it wasn't supposed to do until tomorrow. The forecasters in the port office had given us two tides. The river, which had never once read their bulletins, seemed inclined to give us less.

Then the line pulled twice — our agreed signal — and began coming up.

Ilsa broke the surface coughing, and when they got the helmet off her she was gray-faced and breathing hard, and grinning like a woman who'd won a hand she had no right to play.

"Strongroom," she gasped. "Aft hold, port side. Door's iron, hinges facing forward. It's buried to the top of the frame in silt, but the door itself—" she caught her breath— "the door is shut, Coral. Sealed. Eleven years underwater and sealed."

"Sealed how?"

"Handsome. Deliberate. Somebody closed that door from the outside and meant it to stay closed." She wiped her face with the back of her glove. "There's no rust-jamming, no collapse against it. It's just... shut. Like a tomb somebody tidied up."

I stood very still at the rail, and the river moved beneath us, and I thought about my father sealing a door and going down with it, and about Dario sending me to open it, and about how every person who'd touched this story had known more than I did, and how the only thing I owned outright was the gap where that night should have been.

"We go back down together," I said. "Next slack. We dig her clear, we get a purchase on that door, and we open it."

"Coral—"

"Two tides, Ilsa. Maybe less. Whatever's behind that door has waited eleven years. It can't wait through a monsoon, and neither can the truth."

* * *

The second dive was where the sea started collecting its interest.

Slack water came late and thin, the tide confused by the building pressure offshore, and by the time Ilsa and I went down together the visibility had dropped from bad to theological. I went first along the guide line with Ilsa a body-length behind, and the world reduced itself to what my hands could tell me: the cold layering of the water, the silt hanging like smoke, the hull rising out of the dark under my palms — plates furred with weed, rivets worn smooth as knuckles.

My father had walked these decks. He had stood in the wheelhouse above this exact spot, and the last thing he saw might have been this same brown blindness pressing at the glass. I put my hand flat against the plate and held it there a moment, the way you'd touch a grave marker, and then I made myself stop, because grief underwater is a luxury and luxuries cost air.

We found the strongroom door by counting frames from the stern, exactly as Ilsa had described. The silt had drifted against it in a soft dune, and we dug with our hands and a short pry bar, working blind, passing handfuls of riverbed into the current and letting it take them. It was slow, maddening work — the kind of work where you measure progress in inches and pay for it in breath — and somewhere in the middle of it I felt the first tug on my ankle line and knew, without seeing, without hearing anything, that the weather above had turned.

Tam's signal came down the line: three sharp pulls. Wind rising. Surface getting ugly.

Ilsa tapped my shoulder and pointed up. Go up. I pointed at the door. One more hour. She grabbed my wrist and squeezed hard enough to mean it, and I understood that if I argued she would simply haul me up by the collar like a stray cat, so we compromised: another twenty minutes of digging, and then the air question settled it for us.

It settled itself the way these questions always do — quietly, and too late to be comfortable. My gauge said I had fifteen minutes of air. Then it said ten. Then, with the silt cloud of our own digging hanging around us like a shroud, it said eight, and the door was still shouldered deep in mud, and the surface was forty feet of brown nothing away.

I remember thinking, with strange clarity: this is how it happens. This is how the river takes people. Not dramatically. Arithmetically.

Ilsa solved it the way she solves everything — by refusing the premise. She clipped my line to hers, put herself between me and the ascent, and towed. I kicked when I could and hung on when I couldn't, watching my gauge count down through numbers I didn't like, and the last thing I saw before the brown swallowed everything was the strongroom door, half-cleared, iron-dark, patient.

Waiting for us. Or waiting for me. With that door, I was starting to suspect, the distinction mattered.

We broke the surface into rain.

Not drizzle. Rain — the leading scouts of the monsoon, fat drops that hit the deck like thrown gravel, and the Kestrel already rolling in a swell that shouldn't have reached this far upriver. Tam hauled us aboard, and I came over the rail coughing river out of my lungs and found the whole crew looking at me with the particular expression sailors reserve for captains who come back from the edge of arithmetic.

"Air," I managed. "Ran short."

"I can see that," Tam said.

"The door's clear enough to work. One more dive. One."

Nobody said anything. The rain said it for them, drumming on the deck, on the wheelhouse roof, on the river itself, millions of small fists knocking.

Ilsa pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "Two tides," she said quietly. "That's what the forecast gave us. That was this morning." She looked east, where the horizon had stopped being a line and become a rumor of darkness. "I don't think we have two anymore, Coral. I think we have one. Maybe."

"Then we spend it well."

"On the door?"

"On whatever's behind it."

She studied me for a long moment, rain running off her chin, and whatever she saw in my face made her sigh the sigh of a woman surrendering to a captain she loved and despaired of in equal measure.

"One dive," she said. "And I go down with you, and if your gauge says turn, we turn, and you don't look at the door on the way up. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Liar," she said, not unkindly, and went forward to check the lines.

* * *

Dario appeared at dusk, which was its own kind of weather report.

His launch came alongside through the rain with more confidence than the conditions deserved, and he stepped aboard the Kestrel wearing oilskins too clean for a working man and an expression of solicitude I now knew how to read. Concern, in Dario Fenwick, was inventory. He kept it stocked for occasions exactly like this.

"Coral." He shook rain off his hat. "Reports say the front's accelerated. Port office is calling boats home."

"The port office can call whoever answers."

"I'm offering you an out. Take the salvage gear, take your crew, run upriver ahead of the storm. Come back after landfall and finish the job in calm water."

"After landfall the site'll be scoured or buried, and you know it. A monsoon moves wrecks, Dario. Sometimes it moves them out to sea. Sometimes it buries them under ten feet of new silt." I leaned on the rail. "You didn't come here to offer me an out. You came to see how close I am."

He smiled, and it was a good smile, and I hated how long it had taken me to see the machinery behind it. "How close are you?"

"Close enough that you're worried."

"I'm not worried. I'm invested. There's a difference." He glanced aft, toward the spot where the Meridian lay beneath us, invisible now in the rain and the failing light. "My partner kept meticulous records, Coral. Everything in its place. If it's down there, it's down there in order. You'll know it when you see it."

"Is that a hint?"

"It's a courtesy."

"It's a man telling me what to find before I find it, which makes me wonder what else is down there you'd rather I didn't see."

For half a second — less — something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. Guilt implies a conscience doing the moving. It was closer to recalculation: a man revising figures.

"Bring up whatever you bring up," he said lightly. "We'll sort it together. Partners."

"We're not partners. I'm under contract. There's a difference."

He laughed at that, genuinely, and clapped the rail twice, and went back down into his launch, and I watched his running lights shrink into the rain toward the north anchorage where his vessel rode at anchor — riding, I noticed, pointed downriver. Pointed to run.

Everyone was pointed to run except us. We were pointed straight down, at a door in the dark, with one tide left and the sky coming apart overhead.

* * *

We dove at midnight, into the teeth of it.

I won't pretend it was seamanship. It was obsession with a rope tied to it. The Kestrel bucked at her anchors, the rain came down in ropes, and the surface of the river was a chaos of white water and black water taking turns hitting us. Tam and the crew ran the lines from the rail in oilskins, lashed to the boat themselves, and Ilsa and I went down the guide line into water that had stopped being a place and become a condition.

Forty feet down, the storm was only a rumor — a distant drumming, a tremor in the current. The riverbed was calm. The dead were calm. Only the living were having a difficult evening.

We dug the last of the silt from the door by feel, four hands working in the dark, and then Ilsa set the pry bar in the hinge-side gap and we threw our weight against it, and the iron door groaned — a sound I felt in my teeth, in my chest, in the old bones of the wreck itself — and gave.

Not much. An inch. Enough.

Enough for the water inside to move, and for something to move with it — a slow, pale shifting in the gap, like pages turning. Papers. Eleven years of papers, sealed dry behind iron, and now the river reaching in at last to take its toll.

My gauge said six minutes. Ilsa's fist closed on my arm: turn, now, we turn. And I was turning, I swear I was turning, when my hand went through the gap — one reach, one grab, blind — and closed on a bundle bound in oilcloth, tied with cord, dry as a sermon.

And beneath the bundle, wedged in the corner where the door met the frame, my fingers found paper. Loose sheets. And on the top sheet, under my fingertips, I could feel the impression of ink — writing, pressed deep, written by a heavy hand I would have known anywhere in the world, because I had learned my letters tracing it.

I had time for one thought — *his handwriting* — and then Ilsa hauled, and the river took us both.

We came up into the full voice of the storm. The crew dragged us over the rail, and I lay on the deck clutching the oilcloth bundle to my chest while the rain tried to take it back, and Ilsa tore the helmet off me and shouted something I couldn't hear over the wind.

I shouted back the only thing that mattered:

"There's more down there! Letters — his letters — I felt them —"

Lightning lit the river end to end, and in that white instant I saw the Meridian's anchorage behind us — and Reyes' fleet, all three vessels, weighing anchor in the storm's teeth, coming on.

Coming for the door we'd left open.
