## 15. Inside the Strongroom

Ilsa said no. Ilsa said no three times, in three languages, and the third one was a language she invented on the spot, composed entirely of glares and one word that sounded like a boot hitting wet wood.

"I'm going anyway," I told her.

"Then you're going as a fool," she said, "and fools float badly."

The visibility had gone from bad to biblical. Two days of churned silt, the monsoon's leading edge still a hundred miles out but already pushing its weather ahead of it like a drunk shoving through a crowd. The water over the Meridian was the color of strong tea left too long, and every dive we'd made since finding the strongroom hatch had been conducted by touch, by memory, by prayer — and Ilsa didn't pray, so her dives ran on stubbornness alone.

She'd found the strongroom door on the last dive before her air went short. That was the problem. The door was there, sealed, its wheel crusted with two years of river growth, and it sat at forty feet in water you couldn't read your own hand in. She'd gotten the wheel to move a quarter turn before her gauge started tapping empty and she'd come up swearing in all her languages at once.

"One more dive," she'd said, "tomorrow, with fresh bottles and a line rigged proper—"

"And the fleet comes tomorrow," I said. "Reyes moves tonight or first light. You heard the same thunder I did."

"So we wait for dark and go together."

"We don't have a together-dive in us," I said. "The Kestrel's riding light, the compressor's coughing, and every hour we spend rigging lines is an hour Reyes spends cutting his own. It has to be now, and it has to be me."

Ilsa looked at me the way she looks at a hull breach — measuring how much time she has before she has to act.

"You've never dove a wreck interior," she said.

"No."

"You dive like a captain, not like a diver. You think in surfaces."

"That's flattering."

"It wasn't a compliment." She crouched by the rail, forearms on her knees, and looked out at the brown water as if she could see the Meridian lying under it, which after eleven years she probably could. "Coral. Listen to me once without arguing. That room has been sealed since your father drowned in it. Whatever's inside has been waiting two years. It will wait another six hours if it must. But you — you go down there half-trained, in soup, alone, and the river gets to keep you this time."

*This time.* She said it lightly, the way you set down something fragile, and pretended she hadn't said it at all.

I let the silence sit between us while I checked my gear for the fourth time. Gauge. Knife. Slate. The spare light clipped where my numb fingers would find it even if my brain stopped working, which was, according to everyone who loved me, a live possibility.

"The air inside might be foul," she tried again. "Two years trapped. You crack that door and breathe anything—"

"I'm not breathing anything. I'm taking a look and coming back."

"You're taking a *look*." She laughed, and it wasn't a kind laugh. "You're going to find whatever's in there and you're going to want to read it down there, in the dark, in the cold, and you're going to burn air doing it, because you are your father's daughter and he died doing exactly this."

That landed harder than she probably intended. Or exactly as hard as she intended. With Ilsa it's always impossible to tell, which is most of her genius.

"I'm coming back up," I said, "with the ledger, and we'll read it in daylight like civilized people."

She stared at me a long moment. Then she stood, took the second light off the rack, and clipped it to my harness herself, checking the seal with her thumb.

"If you're not back in twenty minutes," she said, "I'm coming down after you, and I'm worse in tight spaces than you are, so don't make me."

"Twenty minutes."

"And Coral." She caught my wrist. Her hands are rope-hard and river-cold and they have saved my life more times than I can count, mostly from myself. "Whatever you find down there — it doesn't get to take you. Not the paper, not the truth, none of it. You come back up. Promise me the boring thing."

I promised her the boring thing.

* * *

The water took me the way it always does, all at once and gently, like being swallowed by something that means no harm yet. Cold closed over my head and the world went tea-brown and then darker, and I pulled myself down the shot line hand over hand into the Meridian's grave.

Forty feet is nothing. Forty feet is a Sunday afternoon. But forty feet in that water is also a country with no borders, no horizon, no up except what your inner ear insists on, and my inner ear has been wrong about important things before. I counted my breathing. Four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm Ilsa drilled into me until it stuck. Bubbles rose past my face in silver strings, wobbling up toward a sky I could only locate by faith.

The wreck came up out of the murk the way wrecks do — not gradually but suddenly, a shape resolving out of nothing, and then I was inside its shadow and it was enormous. Everyone tells you a sunken ship feels small. Nobody tells you it feels like a cathedral. The Meridian lay on her starboard side in the channel muck, masts gone, superstructure furred with silt and weed, and she was still — God help me — beautiful, in the way that ruined things are beautiful when they were yours.

I swam her hull toward the stern, following the numbers Ilsa had scratched into my memory: past the collapsed davit, past the split where the sandbar had opened her like a letter, down to the deck level, to the hatch.

The strongroom door sat in its frame like a held breath. Ilsa's quarter-turn was visible — the wheel cocked slightly off true, rust broken in a bright arc that looked almost new down here where nothing stayed new. I got both hands on the wheel and pulled.

It didn't move.

I braced my boots against the frame and pulled again, putting my whole back into it, and the wheel groaned and gave a half-turn, and stopped. Corrosion had welded what the flood hadn't. Of course it had. Two years underwater and the door had decided, in its slow mineral way, to become part of the ship.

Fine. I'd brought the pry bar for exactly this, strapped across my back where it made me swim like a drunk crab. I got it seated in the gap and leaned, and the bar bent, and the door laughed at me — okay, it didn't laugh, but I heard one, somewhere behind my eyes — and I reset it and leaned again, and this time something deep in the frame cracked like a knuckle and the door came free all at once, swinging inward with a rush of displaced water that shoved me back and filled the passage with a blinding cloud of silt.

I hung there in the whiteout, heart slamming, counting. One. Two. Three. The cloud began to thin. My light carved a cone through it, and the cone fell on the inside of the strongroom, and I forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

Not treasure. Not gold bars stacked in neat rows, not the coin chests the salvage men dreamed about. Something stranger. The room was small — a strongroom is never big; the whole point of a strongroom is that small things worth much are easier to guard than large things worth little — and it was nearly empty. A shelf along one wall, bolted steel, bare except for a canvas satchel lying flat where someone had placed it, deliberately, carefully, in the center, squared to the shelf's edge the way my father squared everything.

He'd put it there. He'd had time — minutes, maybe less, in the sinking, in the dark, with the river coming in — and he'd used them to square a bag to the edge of a shelf.

I went in.

The doorway was narrow and I turned sideways to fit, and the moment I crossed the threshold I felt it, the way you feel walking into a room where someone died. Not cold, exactly. Not presence. Just a pressure, a rightness of grief, like the room remembered him better than I did.

My father's face is the first thing I lost. Not to the missing night — earlier than that, crueler, just to time — I can't summon it anymore, only photographs of it, only the idea of it. But his hands I remember. And those hands had been in this room. Had packed that satchel. Had known — and here was the thought I'd been outrunning for eleven years, catching me at last in forty feet of brown water — *had known the ship was going down.*

You don't pack a satchel calmly during a wreck. You don't square it to the shelf. Squaring takes calm. Squaring takes *time*, taken deliberately, stolen from panic. He'd known. Some part of him had known, and instead of swimming, he'd spent his last minutes writing.

I reached for the satchel and my glove knocked the shelf and a fine rain of silt drifted down through my light like snow falling indoors, and I thought, absurdly, *he'd hate the dust.*

The satchel was waxed canvas, waterproofed, buckled. Inside: an oilcloth packet, tied with cord, and inside that — my fingers clumsy, my air gauge reading a number I made myself not look at — a ledger bound in oiled leather, swollen with water but intact, pages fused but whole. And beneath it, a separate envelope, also oilcloth-wrapped, smaller.

My name was on it. *Coral.* In his handwriting, the handwriting from the charts, the confident slanting hand that had drawn every sandbar of my childhood. The ink had bled at the edges but the word itself was clear, was perfect, was aimed at me across two years and forty feet of river like a harpoon.

I should have gone up. Every rule, every plan, every promise — the boring thing, Ilsa's voice in my skull — said take it and go. Read it in daylight. Come back up.

But my hands were already working the oilcloth loose, because I am my father's daughter and Ilsa called that one exactly right, and the envelope opened under my thumbs, and the page inside was dry — dry, somehow, sealed against everything the river had thrown at it — and the first line was written in a steady hand, a hand that knew what it was doing, a hand that had written this knowing who would read it and when:

*Coral — if you are reading this, then Dario finally sent you, and I am sorry for what I am about to cost you.*

The river went very quiet around me. Quieter than rivers get. Somewhere above, my bubbles kept rising without me, silver strings climbing toward a surface I had suddenly, completely, forgotten how to find.

*I am sorry,* the letter said, *for what I am about to cost you* —

And my gauge, when I finally made myself look, told me the cost had already begun.
