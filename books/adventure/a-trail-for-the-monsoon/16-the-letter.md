## 16. The Letter

The first thing you learn about panic is that it lies.

It tells you the surface is farther than it is. It tells you your lungs are already empty when they aren't, that the dark water pressing on your mask is a mouth closing, that every second spent reading a dead man's handwriting is a second stolen from your own account. Panic is a debt collector of the body, and I had met enough of those to know their arithmetic was always wrong.

I made myself hang there in the flooded strongroom of the *Meridian*, thirty feet down in water the color of steeped tea, and I read my father's letter by the dying light of my lamp.

*If Dario sent you, then he believes the ledger is all I left behind. He is half right.*

My gauge said eleven minutes of air. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Grief, it turns out, can be steadier than fear. Fear wants something for itself. Grief only wants you to listen.

*I have watched Dario Fenwick for two years,* the letter went on, the ink feathered where the paper had drunk thirty years of river but the words still stubborn, still his — that small, precise hand that had drawn every chart I ever steered by, the hand that taught mine its trade. *He came to me in the spring with a cargo manifest and a smile, and I signed it because I trusted him, and because the freight was good and the season was lean. Then I found the second manifest. The one beneath the first.*

The strongroom around me was a green cathedral of silt and shadow. Iron shelving bolted to the walls. A clerk's desk, absurdly, still upright, its drawers furred with mud. And everywhere the slow snowfall of the river, drifting down through the broken skylight above in grains so fine they seemed less like sediment than like time itself, settling on everything we had lost.

*The Meridian was never meant to make port,* my father wrote. *Dario loaded her heavy and insured her double, and when she went down off Cutter's Reach he would collect on both manifests while the true cargo — and every witness to it — sat at the bottom of the channel. He did not plan on me finding the second manifest. He did not plan on me being aboard the night he opened her seacocks.*

I stopped reading.

Not because I wanted to. Because my body made me. The letter trembled in my grip and I realized the trembling was mine, transmitted through the waterproof sleeve Ilsa had rigged from sailcloth and tar, and beyond the sleeve my hands had gone white around the lamp housing, and beyond that the whole room seemed to tilt — though nothing down here tilts, nothing moves, the wreck has been perfectly still for thirty years except for the snowfall of silt and me.

He was aboard. He was aboard the night she sank. I had spent half my life believing he drowned at his desk ashore, or in some accident I'd been too young to be told the shape of, and here was his handwriting telling me he died in this hull, in this water, sealing a tin box shut with his own hands while the river came up the companionway to meet him.

And underneath that — under the grief, under the horror, down where the bedrock of a person lies — something else moved. Something older than either.

A sound. A night. Rain on a deck. A lantern swinging. A voice saying *Coral, go below, go below now* —

No. Not yet. Read first. Breathe later.

*You will want to know why I did not simply take the ledger and run,* the letter said. *Because I tried. He was watching the house. So I brought it aboard, into the one place he would never search until it was safe to search — the strongroom of a scuttled ship, guarded by the river itself. If you are reading this, Coral, then you dove for it. You always were the better swimmer. I hope to God you are also the luckier one, because Dario will not let that paper reach daylight. Whatever he promised you, whatever name he put on the contract, understand: he hired you to find this letter so that no one else ever could.*

Nine minutes. Maybe eight. The lamp's beam had gone the color of old honey, the battery surrendering to the cold, and the silt-snow fell thicker now as if the river knew what it was carrying and wanted it back.

*There is one more thing,* my father wrote, and his hand changed here — I could see it even through thirty years and tarred canvas, the strokes widening, hurrying, a man writing with water at his knees. *The night the Meridian went down, you were aboard. You stowed away because you were seventeen and furious with me, and because you had just learned I was sailing without you, and I have never blamed you for any of it. When Dario came below with the wrench in his hand, I sent you up the ladder and told you to jump. You would not leave me. So I lied to you. I told you I would follow. I told you to swim for the Kestrel and wait, and you believed me, because you were seventeen and I was your father and fathers do not lie about following.*

The strongroom went very far away.

Here is what memory is, when it comes back all at once after thirty years of living in a hole in your head: it is not a picture. It is weather. It comes in over you the way the monsoon comes in over the delta — a black wall with lightning inside it, and you stand in its path because there is nowhere left to stand that isn't already part of the storm.

Rain. The rail of the *Meridian* under my palms, wet and cold. The lantern light jerking across the deck as the ship took her list, and my father's face coming out of the dark, younger than the face on the letter, alive, alive, his hair plastered down and his eyes wide and fixed on me with an expression I had spent three decades calling *grief* because I had forgotten it was something worse. It was arithmetic. He was doing sums with my life against his, and he had already decided how the numbers fell.

*Go below,* someone said — no. *Swim.* His voice. *Swim for the Kestrel, Coral. Count your strokes. Don't stop till you touch her hull.*

*I'm not leaving you.*

*You are,* he said, and he put his hands on my shoulders, and he turned me toward the black water, and he said the thing I had carried home that night without knowing I carried it, the splinter that had festered into the gap in my memory, the wound I had dressed for thirty years as simple loss: *Whatever happens, this is not yours to carry. Do you hear me? None of it is yours. Swim.*

And I swam. I swam and counted strokes the way he taught me, and at forty strokes I looked back and the *Meridian*'s stern was already under, the lantern light gone out like a pinched wick, and I swam fifty more and touched the *Kestrel*'s hull and hung there in the black water screaming his name into the river until my lungs gave out, and then I climbed aboard and stood dripping on the deck while the crew looked at me, and Ilsa wrapped a blanket around my shoulders and said, *Where's Elias? Where's your father, girl?* and I opened my mouth to answer and —

Nothing.

That was the hole. That was the gap I had never questioned, the smooth blank stretch of that night that I had sailed over for thirty years the way you sail over a charted shoal without ever asking what made the chartmaker mark it. I hadn't lost the memory. I had *put it down*. Somewhere between the hull of the *Kestrel* and Ilsa's blanket, my mind had taken the one thing I could not survive and set it overboard, and the river had kept it for me, all these years, down here in the dark, in a strongroom, in a tin box, in my father's hand.

*Forgive yourself,* the letter said. *That is the last thing I will ever ask of you, and I ask it knowing you will hate me for asking. You survived because I chose it. There was no version of that night in which we both came up. I did the mathematics a father should never have to do, and I chose, and if you spend your life paying interest on my choice, then everything I bought with it is wasted. Live, Coral. Steer your own water. And when the monsoon comes — and it always comes — remember that the river does not keep accounts. Only people do. Put mine down.*

Six minutes. Five. The lamp guttered, flared, and I saw the whole strongroom once more in that last amber surge — the desk, the shelves, the tin box open in my hands, the letter, and beyond the letter the ledger itself, fat and bound in oilcloth, sitting in the box like a heart in a chest, worth more than every boat I would ever captain, worth enough to buy back the *Kestrel* twice over and the dock besides and a life with no debt collectors waiting at the end of any gangway.

Worth exactly what Dario Fenwick had sent me down here to fetch for him.

I got the ledger into the sleeve with the letter. I got my fins moving. I got four kicks toward the broken skylight before the arithmetic of my own air caught up with me, and it was bad arithmetic, panic-arithmetic, the kind that says *surface* when what you need is *slow down*, and I went up too fast through the wreck's broken spine with my heart slamming and my vision going to sparks, and the last thing I saw before the darkness closed in from the edges was the silt-snow falling upward past my face, silver strings of my own bubbles climbing without me toward a surface I had suddenly, completely, forgotten how to find.

Then hands. Hands in the green dark, gripping my harness, hauling — Ilsa, who had come down after me against every rule she'd ever enforced, whose face behind her mask was fury and terror in equal measure, and who did not waste one precious bubble on telling me I was a fool, because she could see I already knew.

We broke the surface together into rain.

Real rain, warm and vertical, the leading breath of the monsoon arriving early, hammering the channel into white noise. The *Kestrel*'s hull loomed out of the murk and hands pulled us over the rail, and I lay on the deck coughing river out of my lungs while the crew shouted and the rain came down and the ledger, in its tarred sleeve, thumped against my ribs with every convulsion like a second heartbeat.

Ilsa crouched over me, tearing her mask off. "You went down alone," she said. "You *lied*."

"I know."

"You nearly—"

"I know." I pushed myself up on my elbows. The rain ran off my chin. "Ilsa. The night the *Meridian* sank. Tell me what you saw when I came aboard."

She went still. Thirty years of quartermaster discipline holding her face together, and under it, something breaking loose. "You remember," she said. Not a question.

"He sent me up," I said. "He sent me up and he stayed, and he knew, Ilsa. He knew the ship was going down before she did. He *made* her go down — no. Dario made her go down. My father stayed to seal the proof inside, knowing what staying cost." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "He chose it. He wrote it down. He chose, and he told me to put it down, and I've been drowning in it for thirty years without knowing what I was carrying."

Ilsa sat back on her heels in the rain. Around us the crew had gone quiet, pretending to work the sails, listening the way family listens. Somewhere south, thunder rolled along the horizon like a drum being moved into position.

"The fleet," she said finally. "Reyes will have seen this weather same as us. And Fenwick—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Coral, Fenwick's boat weighed anchor an hour ago. Heading for the Reach. He didn't wait for the salvage window. He didn't wait for *us*."

I got my feet under me. The deck pitched, the rain hammered, and against my ribs the ledger beat its small dead heartbeat, and my father's voice was still in my ears, calm as a chart line: *the river does not keep accounts. Only people do.*

"Then he's sailing into the monsoon's teeth," I said, "to collect a debt that isn't his," and I turned for the wheelhouse, and the first true squall line of the season came walking across the water to meet us.
