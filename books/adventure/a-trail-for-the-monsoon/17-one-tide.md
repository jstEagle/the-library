## 17. One Tide

The first thing I noticed was that the birds had stopped.

You learn a river by its noises — the herons complaining at dawn, the sandpipers arguing over nothing, the endless civic business of gulls. When all of it quits at once, the river isn't quiet. It's holding its breath. And when a river holds its breath, you'd best hold yours too.

I was on deck with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a colicky infant when I heard the launch. Not Reyes' heavy diesel growl. Something sleeker, running with the throttle open, coming up the channel from the seaward side where no honest boat had any business being.

Dario Fenwick stood at the bow of his launch in a rain slicker worth more than my engine, and he had a pistol in his hand, and behind him were four men who looked like they'd been hired for their shoulders rather than their conversation.

"Captain Underhill," he called across the water, pleasant as a man asking to borrow a cup of sugar. "Might I come aboard?"

"You might not."

"I'm afraid courtesy has left the negotiation table." He raised the pistol slightly, apologetically, as if it were a social faux pas he'd been meaning to correct for years. "The ledger, Coral. That's all. Hand it over and everyone keeps breathing."

Behind me, Ilsa had already moved — silent as she always was, putting herself between me and the rail, a boat hook in her hands like it had opinions. Tam and the twins came up from below without being called. My crew. My family by contract and stubbornness.

"Dario," I said, "you scuttled your own ship. You drowned my father. And now you're going to point a gun at me on my own deck and ask nicely?"

"I'm not asking nicely." His smile thinned. "I'm asking *last*. There's a difference, and it's measured in what happens if you say no."

* * *

Here's the thing about standoffs nobody tells you: they're mostly weather.

Not the metaphorical kind. The actual kind. Because while Dario's launch idled ten feet off my starboard beam and four hired shoulders tried to look menacing in a rising chop, the horizon went the color of a bruise, and the wind changed its mind about which direction it had always blown from, and every sailor in that channel understood at the same moment that we had run out of tides.

The monsoon wasn't coming tomorrow. It was coming now.

"The ledger," Dario said again, and this time there was no sugar in it. "My partner stole eleven years of my life into those pages, Coral. Every falsified manifest. Every payment. Every name." His eyes flicked to mine, and for one unguarded second I saw something underneath the smoothness — fear, actual fear, the kind money can't buy back. "If it surfaces, I hang. Or whatever they do to men like me now. So yes. I drowned the Meridian. I'd drown this whole river before I let that paper see daylight."

"You just confessed," Tam said flatly. "In front of witnesses."

Dario didn't even glance at him. "Witnesses require a world afterward, boy. Check the sky. There may not be one."

And that was when Reyes arrived.

* * *

Her trawler came around the point under bare poles, driving hard before the first squall line, and she anchored — *anchored*, in a channel about to become a washing machine, because Captain Reyes has never once done anything convenient — close enough that her voice carried without shouting.

"Fenwick! Stand down!"

"Captain Reyes." Dario turned, keeping his pistol angled somewhere between my crew and her deck, a man trying to cover two directions with one small piece of iron. "How lovely. The whole committee."

"You chartered two crews," Reyes said. Her voice was steady but I could hear the crack running through it, the sound of a woman realizing she'd been played by the same man who'd played me. "You told me the Kestrel was insurance against *me*. Turns out I was insurance against her. Which one of us did you plan to pay, Dario?"

"The one holding the ledger, obviously." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, bright and hollow, a bell with nothing inside. "Ladies. Consider: neither of you gets paid unless I do. Neither of you sees another season unless that paper burns. We are, at this moment, partners in the only venture that matters — making sure the past stays sunk."

The wind hit then. Properly hit, the leading edge of the monsoon slamming the channel like a door thrown open in a house of cards. Rain came sideways. My rigging began to sing that high keening note I hate, and the Kestrel swung on her anchor, and Dario's launch yawed wildly, his hired shoulders suddenly occupied with staying aboard.

"Come up alongside!" I shouted at him, because apparently I'd lost my mind. "You want to have a gunpoint conversation? Have it on a deck that isn't sinking!"

He hesitated. Then — because Dario Fenwick has never once chosen the safe option when a dramatic one presented itself — he threw a line and came aboard.

* * *

Three parties on one riverboat, one gun, and a storm arriving like a creditor who's tired of letters.

Dario stood amidships with his back to my mainmast, pistol low but present, water streaming off his expensive slicker. Reyes had come across from her trawler on a heaving line with two of her people, and she stood at my port rail looking older than I'd ever seen her, rain cutting channels through the salt on her face.

"Coral," she said quietly. "Whatever's in that oilcloth — it isn't worth this."

"It was never about worth," I said. "It's about truth. You taught me that."

"I taught you salvage. Somebody else taught you sentiment, and I'd like five minutes alone with them."

"My father," I said, "taught me both."

Something moved through her face — grief, or guilt, or the particular expression of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like. "Then give the paper to the sea and be done. Fenwick can't hang for what can't be read."

"And he walks away rich," Ilsa said from beside me. "Off a dead man's ship and a dead man's name."

"He'll walk away regardless," Reyes said. "Look at the sky, girl. Look at your boat. Nothing you carry off this channel today is worth more than the people standing on it."

And God help me, some part of me — the part that had spent three weeks chasing fragments of a night I couldn't remember, the part that had read my father's letter by lamplight until the words wore grooves in me — wanted to agree. Wanted to throw the ledger overboard right then, before the storm made the decision for us all, and let the river keep its secrets the way it keeps everything eventually.

But Dario raised the pistol.

"Enough," he said. "The ledger. Now. I have watched the two of you conduct a morality play long enough."

"You're on my deck," I said, "in my storm, with four men who can barely swim, and you're going to threaten me?"

"Yes," said Dario Fenwick, and for the first time since I'd known him — since either of us had been young and poor and hungry on these same banks — he sounded entirely sincere. "Because I am a drowning man, Coral, and drowning men don't negotiate. They grab."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do, and slow, the way memory does afterward.

Reyes moved first. I'll give her that — twenty years my senior and she moved like a hatch cover slamming, stepping between the pistol and me with her arms half-spread, saying something I didn't catch over the wind. Dario swung toward her. One of his hired shoulders grabbed for the rail as the Kestrel took a wave broadside. Ilsa brought the boat hook around in a flat arc that caught the second shoulder across the wrists, and the pistol went off —

— into the sky, into the rain, a white flower of noise swallowed instantly by thunder answering from the south like the storm taking offense at the competition.

And then everything was motion. The launch broke loose and went wallowing away downwind with two men aboard and two in the water screaming. Reyes' trawler dragged anchor, her lone watchman hauling frantically. A wave came over my rail that weighed more than my childhood, and the Kestrel groaned, and the mainmast leaned, and for one long suspended moment the whole channel seemed to tilt toward the sea like a table clearing itself of dishes.

"We're dragging!" Tam bellowed from the bow, pointing at the anchor line angling wrong, wrong, wrong — the bottom giving way beneath us as the storm dug its fingers into the sandbar shoals.

Every debt called at once. That's what I'd get, if I survived to write the memoirs. The ledger in my hand worth a fortune. The man who killed my father bleeding at my feet where Ilsa had put him down with a forearm across the throat, alive, disarmed, useless. My mentor's ship dragging toward the same bar that would eat us all. And six miles of channel between us and shelter that I knew better than anyone alive — better than Reyes, better than Dario, better than any chart ever drawn, because I carried it in my blood.

"Coral!" Reyes had hold of my arm, shouting into my ear. "Your anchor's gone in an hour! Where's deep water?"

I looked at her. I looked at Dario, coughing on my deck, watching me with those calculating eyes even now, even soaked, even beaten — waiting to see what I'd trade.

I looked at the ledger.

"There's a cut behind Gull Spit," I said. "Two fathoms at high water, and the high water is coming early and angry. Nobody rides this out at anchor. We run for it — all of us, or none of us."

"And the paper?" Dario rasped.

I tucked it inside my coat, against my heart, where my father's letter had lived for three weeks.

"The paper," I said, "rides with me. And so do you — because if I let you drown, I'm just you with better navigation. Get everyone below. Reyes, get your people across. We're taking the Kestrel into the teeth of it."

Lightning walked down the channel ahead of us, showing me the way like a lamp lit by something that wanted to watch what happened next.

I took the wheel.

The storm closed behind us like a book.
