## 19. The Weight of Paper

The storm left the way a drunk leaves a party — loudly at first, then all at once, and then there was just the mess.

I woke on my back in wet sand with the taste of salt and blood in my mouth and no immediate memory of how either had gotten there. The sky above me was that washed-out grey the world turns after it has spent everything it had, like a man who has cried himself empty and can only stare at the ceiling. Gulls were screaming somewhere. Gulls always know when there's wreckage. They're the river's debt collectors, and they had come to pick over us.

I sat up too fast and the world tilted politely to port. My hands were shaking. Not from fear — fear had been wrung out of me somewhere around hour nine of the monsoon, wrung out and hung to dry and blown away. This was the plain animal tremor of a body that had done its job and now wanted, in writing, never to do it again.

"Kestrel," I said, or tried to. It came out as a croak. I tried again. "Kestrel."

She was there. Of course she was there; where else would I be? She lay heeled over on her beam ends forty yards up the beach, wedged between two blackened snags of driftwood like a drunk leaning on old friends, her starboard rail buried, her mast snapped off clean about eight feet up the stub. The stub pointed at the sky at an angle that could only be described as accusatory. Her hull was intact as far as I could see, which was a miracle I intended to inspect closely later, because miracles on this river always came itemized.

And Dario Fenwick's boat was not there at all. Neither was Dario Fenwick. Neither was Reyes' fleet, or most of Reyes' fleet, or — I counted heads along the sand, one, two, three, four, five, six — most of my crew, though the ones who were there were moving, which was the count that mattered.

Ilsa was twenty feet away, sitting with her back against a snag, methodically wringing out her braid like she was strangling an enemy she'd already beaten. She saw me sit up and didn't rush over. That's Ilsa. Rushing is for people who doubt you'll get there on your own.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've seen worse."

"So have I. On you." She tossed me a waterskin. I drank half of it before remembering that drinking fast after nearly drowning is a rookie mistake, and then I drank the other half anyway, because I had earned the right to be a rookie for one morning.

"How many?" I asked.

"All six of us made the beach. Tam broke his arm — bad set, we'll need the surgeon at Port Merrow. The rest are bruised, cut, and complaining, which means they're fine." She paused. "Reyes lost two boats. Maybe three. We saw one go down on the outer bar with people still waving from it."

I closed my eyes. Two boats. I did the arithmetic I always do and hate doing: how many souls per boat, how many of those could swim, how cold the water ran even in summer when the storm had churned it. The numbers came out ugly. They usually do.

"And Fenwick?"

Ilsa's jaw worked. "His vessel hit the sandbar off Cutter's Point. You saw it."

I had seen it. I would see it again every time I closed my eyes for a while, I suspected — the way his stern lifted, hung, and then the river simply took him, the way the river takes everything it decides belongs to it. I had watched from the wheel of a boat I was keeping alive by argument and blasphemy, and I had not looked away, because looking away felt like a mercy I hadn't earned the right to give.

"There were men on deck," I said.

"There were."

"Some of them might have—"

"Coral." Ilsa said my name the way you drop an anchor. "You couldn't have saved them. Nobody could have saved them. The sea wasn't taking passengers yesterday."

I knew that. Knowing it and feeling it are two different cargoes, and only one of them fits in the hold.

* * *

We spent the morning doing what survivors do, which is work. There is a peculiar grace in it — your hands need something to hold that isn't grief, so you hand them rope. We salvaged what we could from Kestrel: the galley stove, miraculously sound; two crates of dried goods that had stayed dry through sheer stubbornness; the tool chest; the longboat, which had washed up a quarter mile down the beach upside down and sulking. Tam sat propped against a log directing operations with his good arm and his full voice, because a broken arm has never yet stopped Tam from having opinions about knots.

It was past noon, with the sun finally burning through and the beach steaming like a kettle taken off the heat, when I found the ledger.

It was in the oilskin packet inside my coat, which was inside Ilsa's coat, because at some point during the worst of it — I don't remember when, the night is mostly a blur of water and shouting and the particular prayer you make when the wheel goes light in your hands — Ilsa had decided that if Coral Underhill went into the sea, the ledger was not going with her. She'd strapped it to me and then, apparently, reconsidered and strapped it to herself, and then at some point we must have traded coats or she'd moved it back, and none of it was in my memory. The ledger had survived the monsoon the way secrets survive anything: by being passed hand to hand among people who understood its weight.

I sat down on a piece of Dario's wreck — a length of carved rail, mahogany, salt-scoured, that had come ashore ahead of its owner's fate like a herald — and unwrapped it.

The ledger. *The* ledger. My father's hand filled page after page in that tight, patient script of his, cargo tallies and dates and figures, and threaded through them, in entries he must have thought no one would ever read, the truth: the falsified manifest, the insurance scheme, the payments, the name of the man who paid them. Dario Fenwick, written plainly enough that no court in the delta could pretend otherwise. Enough paper to hang him, if he'd lived. Enough paper to buy back the Kestrel twice over, clear every debt I owed, buy a fleet, buy a berth in any harbor from here to the capital. Enough paper to erase the last ten years of scraping and dodging and piloting other people's fortunes up other people's rivers.

Worth more than the boat. Worth more than the crew's wages for a lifetime. Worth, if I sold it to the insurers or the magistrates or whichever of Dario's creditors came sniffing, everything I had come for.

I held it and it weighed nothing. That was the strange part. A document that could remake my whole life, and it weighed less than the waterskin. All that power and it weighed nothing at all, unless you counted the weight of what it would cost to use it.

Because here's what the letter had taught me, my father's last letter, the one I'd read by lamplight in a flooded strongroom while my air ran short: the ledger was proof, and proof is a weapon, and weapons don't care whose hand they're in. If I sold it, I sold it to someone who would use it — against Dario's estate, against his partners, against anyone who'd ever shaken his hand, a vendetta that would ripple outward for years, dragging names through courts and mud, some of them names I knew, some of them maybe names I loved. And everyone who knew I held it would know I could be bought, or blackmailed, or drowned. Paper like this doesn't free you. It just changes whose hook is in your mouth.

But if I burned it — if I burned it, then my father died for nothing. Then the strongroom, the dive, the letter, all of it was for nothing, and the fortune sank a second time, and this time I was the one who scuttled it.

I sat there turning it over in my hands until the sun moved, and Ilsa let me. That's another thing about Ilsa. She knows which silences are hers to break and which aren't.

* * *

She finally came and sat beside me in the late afternoon, when the tide had turned and started its patient work of pulling the debris back out to sea. The gulls had gone quiet. Somewhere down the beach, Tam was asleep with his splinted arm propped on a crate, and young Piet was keeping watch over nothing in particular, because Piet believes watches matter even when there's nothing to watch for, and honestly, after this week, I'm inclined to agree with him.

"You've been staring at that book like it owes you money," Ilsa said.

"It does. Everything owes me money. That's rather the problem."

"Mm." She pulled a strip of dried fish from her pocket, tore it in half, handed me half without asking. "You've decided something. You always get quiet like this when you've decided something and haven't admitted it yet."

"Have I decided?"

"You tell me."

I looked at the ledger. The wind came off the water clean for the first time in days, carrying that rinsed smell the air has after a great storm, like the whole world had been laundered. Out past the bar, the sea lay flat and glittering and innocent, the great liar.

"When I was in the strongroom," I said, "reading his letter — my father wrote that he forgave me. For surviving. He wrote it like he knew I'd never forgive myself, and he was right, because I didn't even know I needed forgiving until I read it. Ten years I carried a hole where that night should have been, and the whole time, the thing I was missing wasn't the memory. It was permission."

Ilsa said nothing. She's very good at saying nothing. It's a professional skill among quartermasters.

"If I sell this," I went on, "every debt I have dies. The boat gets refitted proper. The crew eats well for a decade. And I spend the rest of my life knowing that the only reason I'm free is that I sold my father's corpse to the highest bidder. That's not freedom, Ilsa. That's just a bigger cage with better ventilation."

"And if you burn it?"

"Then I stay poor."

"That's not the question I asked."

I laughed — a rough, surprised sound, the first laugh since the storm, and it hurt my ribs, which told me exactly how long it had been. "No. It isn't."

I stood up. The ledger sat in my hands, light as a bird, heavy as a hull.

"The choice is yours alone," Ilsa said quietly. "That's what he asked for, in the letter. Yours alone. Not mine, not the crew's, not the river's. Yours." She looked out at the water. "For what it's worth — and it isn't worth anything, so don't quote me — I've watched you make hard calls with other people's lives for ten years, and I've never once seen you make the cheap one. Whatever you do with that book, you'll do it honest. That's worth more than whatever's written in it."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it. I'm concussed."

"You're not concussed."

"I might be. It would explain the compliment."

* * *

I walked down the beach alone, past the longboat, past the tideline of Dario's ship scattered like a dropped hand of cards — a hatch cover, a coil of rigging, a ship's bell half-buried and silent — until I reached the point where the sand met the black rocks at the island's northern tip, and there I stopped.

The ledger in one hand. My father's letter in the other. I had copied nothing out. I didn't need to. Every word of that letter was written somewhere in me now, in ink that wouldn't wash.

I thought about my father's face, the real one, the one from the returned memory — not the blurred ghost I'd chased for ten years but the living man, angry and afraid and certain, arguing with Dario on the night deck while I hid below and heard everything and remembered none of it until the river chose to give it back. He had known what was coming. He had sealed the proof in iron rather than let it float free, and he had gone down with it, and his last act on this earth was to leave his daughter a choice instead of an order. Even dead, he trusted me to captain.

You don't sell a dead man's trust at market rate.

I struck the flint three times before it caught — my hands were still not entirely steady — and touched the flame to the corner of the ledger's first page. The paper took it slowly, the way old things burn, reluctant and then all at once. I held it until the fire reached my fingers, then set it on the rocks and watched. The wind off the sea helped. The wind, for once, was on my side.

Pages curled and blackened and lifted, glowing, and the figures my father had kept so carefully — the sums, the proofs, the whole rotten arithmetic of Dario Fenwick's crime — went up as light and heat and ash, and the ash went out over the water, and the water took it, because the water takes everything, and for once I was glad of it.

I kept the letter. I folded it small and put it inside my shirt, over my heart, where important cargo rides.

When I turned back, Ilsa was standing a hundred feet down the beach, arms crossed, watching. She didn't ask what I'd done. She could read smoke as well as anyone.

"Well," she called. "Now what, Captain? We've got a boat on her beam ends, a broken arm, no wages, no cargo, and winter coming."

I looked up the beach at the Kestrel, lying wounded in the driftwood, her broken mast pointing at the sky like a question mark. My boat. Debt-ridden, storm-battered, and mine — and for the first time in ten years, the woman who captained her wasn't missing anything.

"Now," I said, walking toward her, "we do the hardest salvage job of our lives. We float her."

Ilsa grinned, slow and crooked. "Thought you'd say that. Come on, then. Tide'll be right at dawn, and Piet's already drawing plans nobody asked for."

Behind us, the last of the ash went out on the evening breeze, and the river, patient as ever, began to turn the page.
