## 21. Refloating the Kestrel

The morning after a monsoon makes landfall, the world apologizes the way a drunk apologizes — loudly, messily, and with no memory of what it broke. The sky over the anchorage was rinsed blue, the kind of blue that seems embarrassed by everything it had been hiding, and the water lay flat as poured pewter, as if the sea itself were trying to look innocent.

The Kestrel lay on her beam ends in the shallows off the eastern spit, half her port rail under, her sternpost showing a wound the size of a coffin lid where Dario's vessel had come apart against the sandbar and thrown a spar into us like a parting curse. She looked, I will admit, terrible. She looked like a boat that had been through something. Which, to be fair, she had. So had I, and I didn't look much better, and nobody was writing me off.

"High tide at ten past nine," Ilsa said, standing beside me on the spit with her arms crossed and her hair still crusted with salt into something resembling architecture. "We've got maybe five hours of usable water. If we're going to pull her off, we pull her off today."

"Then we'd best stop admiring the scenery."

"I wasn't admiring it. I was deciding whether to tell you the bad news first or the worse news."

Ilsa has this gift. Twenty years of sailing with her and she has never once delivered good news first, on the theory — she told me once — that good news keeps, and bad news ferments.

"Give me the bad."

"The capstan's seized. Salt in the works. And the worse: we're short two hands. Tamás went ashore with the fishing family and hasn't come back, and Odile's wrist is broken."

"Broken how?"

"She fell off the roof."

"Of the boat?"

"Of the house we put the fishing family in. She was fixing their shutters. She says the ladder was dishonest."

Of course she did. Odile could fall off a cliff and find a way to blame the cliff's character. I looked out at my wounded boat and felt something I hadn't let myself feel in a long time, which was the specific, unglamorous love of a captain for a vessel that has carried her through worse than she deserved. The Kestrel had taken a spar through her quarter and held. The Kestrel had taken me through a monsoon and held. The least I owed her was a morning's honest labor.

"Then it's the four of us and whatever the fishing family can spare," I said. "Get the blocks off her. I'll deal with the capstan."

"You'll deal with the capstan," Ilsa repeated, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later quotation.

"I have a relationship with that capstan."

"You have a grudge with that capstan. It's not the same thing, but it does produce results."

* * *

We worked through the falling morning, and I will tell you what nobody tells you about salvage, which is that most of it is not diving and treasure and drama. Most of it is mud. Most of it is a woman up to her thighs in gray silt, hauling on a line with four other people, counting in a voice gone hoarse, and failing, and counting again. The Kestrel came two feet off the bottom on the first pull and settled back like a dog deciding the walk wasn't worth it.

"Again," I said.

"Coral." That was Odile, one-armed, her splinted wrist wrapped in oilcloth, holding a line with her good hand out of pure spite. "The tide's still rising. We could wait an hour and pull with the water helping."

"We could. And the swell comes back with the tide, and she takes another knock on that sternpost, and we're doing this tomorrow with a hole instead of a scrape. Again."

They pulled. That's the thing about a crew that's become a family — you don't have to explain the arithmetic to them. They know that the sea charges interest, and the interest is due now, and waiting is just another way of borrowing.

On the third pull she came. Not gracefully — the Kestrel has never done anything gracefully in her life — but with a long groan of hull leaving mud, a belch of trapped air from her scuppers, and then that slow, sweet, sideways slide into water deep enough to call her own. I stood on the spit with the line burning my palms and watched her right herself, and I will not pretend my eyes were dry, because I've made my peace with being a person who cries over boats and not over much else.

"There she is," Ilsa said quietly.

"There she is," I said, and then, because sentiment unattended goes to rot: "Somebody get aboard and start the pumps before she remembers she's supposed to be sinking."

* * *

Reyes came at midday, in a dinghy pulled by two of her own crew, and I will give her this: she came alone to the rail. No escort, no show of force, no truce flag even, though the truce was understood. The monsoon had not been gentle with her fleet. I'd seen it from the spit that morning — three of her five vessels scattered or gone, her flagship riding low with a jury rig where her mainmast used to be, looking like a duchess in a borrowed coat.

"Coral." She said my name the way she always had, with that particular weight, as if I were a chart she'd drawn herself and was disappointed to find errors in.

"Captain Reyes."

"You could call me Marisol. Under the circumstances."

"The circumstances being that your fleet is wreckage and mine is afloat?"

"The circumstances being that we survived the same storm and only one of us is being smug about it."

She came aboard. I let her. Ilsa watched from the wheelhouse with the expression of a woman counting exits, and Odile watched from the deck with the expression of a woman who would happily provide one. Reyes took it all in — the patched rail, the mud still drying on our lines, the pumps going below — and something in her face shifted that I had never seen there in all the years she'd been my mentor, and then my rival, and then the thing that is neither of those and worse.

"You pulled her off with four hands and a broken wrist," she said.

"Five hands. The fishing family sent a boy. He's very strong and eats like a threshing machine."

"I had thirty hands and a full fleet," Reyes said, "and I lost the Meridian site, two hulls, and very nearly my life, because I spent twenty years learning to read water and never learned to read people. Dario Fenwick chartered me the way he chartered you. Insurance against betrayal, he called it." She looked out at the flat bright water, at the sandbar that had ended everything. "You were the betrayal he was insuring against. I was the backup. I've been doing this long enough to know what it means when a man hires two of you and tells neither."

"It means he expected to lose one of us," I said.

"It means he expected to lose both," Reyes said. "And he nearly managed it."

We stood there a while. The pumps thumped below like a slow heart. Somewhere aft, the fishing boy was laughing at something Odile said, and Odile was laughing too, which she does maybe twice a year and never admits to.

"I came to offer you work," Reyes said at last. "Real work. Honest charter, fair shares, my name on the papers. You and your crew. I've got contracts coming out of the storm — half the coast needs hauling and salvage and nobody's left to do it but the two of us. Come with me, Coral. Sail under my flag. I'll make it worth your while."

And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because I have spent most of this story being dishonest with myself and I'm trying to break the habit: for about four seconds, I wanted to say yes. Four seconds of imagining someone else's name on the papers, someone else's flag to stand behind, someone else to blame when the water turned. Four seconds of rest. It's the most dangerous offer in the world, rest, and it always comes dressed as respect.

"No," I said.

Reyes raised an eyebrow. In the old days that eyebrow had made me rewrite whole voyages.

"No," I said again, because the second one is for me. "But I'll make you a different offer."

"I'm listening."

"You're short a flagship and I'm short a fleet. The Kestrel sails under my flag, always, that's not negotiable, that's not even a term, that's the weather. But we'll take contract work with your name on the papers — salvage, hauling, whatever the coast needs — at shares we set now, in writing, before either of us is desperate enough to pretend we meant something else. Sixty-forty in your favor, since it's your name and your contacts. And one more clause."

"Which is?"

"You never charter with a man like Fenwick again. Not for him, not against him, not around him. If he surfaces — and men like him always surface — you come to me first. Not because you owe me. Because next time there might not be a sandbar with good timing."

Reyes looked at me for a long moment. I watched her do the arithmetic — pride on one side, a fleet on the other — and I watched pride lose, which is the soundest investment I know of.

"Sixty-forty," she said. "And the clause. You drive a hard bargain for a woman standing on a mudbank."

"I learned from the best," I said, "and then I learned from the worst, and it turns out they teach the same lesson. You just have to survive both of them to hear it."

She laughed — short, surprised, real — and put out her hand, and we shook on it over the rail of the Kestrel while the pumps thumped and the tide turned and the fishing boy asked Odile, in a carrying whisper, whether the tall woman in the fine coat was a pirate. Odile told him all former mentors are, and Reyes pretended not to hear, and I pretended not to notice her pretending, and that, I think, is what peace between rivals actually sounds like.

* * *

We spent the afternoon making her seaworthy, or what passes for seaworthy on this coast, which is a generous category. Ilsa and I took the wheelhouse apart and put it back together with the wheel true again — it had gone a half-point lazy in the storm, pulling always a little to starboard, like a dog toward the road home. Odile supervised from a coil of rope with a mug of something medicinal in her good hand, offering advice that was thirty percent useful and seventy percent entertainment, which is her standard ratio and we'd have worried if it changed.

The fishing boy — his name was Pell, it turned out, and he'd lost his family's boat in the same storm that took our rail — worked without stopping and asked questions without pausing for answers. Where the channel ran at low water. Why we kept the anchor watch in pairs. What the marks on the old chart meant, the ones in my father's hand, the ones I'd nailed up behind the wheel where I could see them.

"Those mean somebody loved this river enough to write it down," I told him.

"Did he get them all right?"

"Most of them." I touched the corner of the chart, where a second handwriting had crept in over the years — annotations I'd once almost recognized, and now, with everything returned to me, recognized completely. My father's own, later years, when his hands had gone unsteady and his certainty hadn't. *Trust the bar at ebb. Do not trust the man who asks where you keep your papers.* "The last ones are the truest. They always are. You write the early charts to impress people. You write the late ones to keep them alive."

Pell considered this with the serious face of the young, who believe everything is a lesson because they haven't yet learned how much of it is just weather.

"Captain," he said, "can I stay on? When we get to port. I can work. I don't eat that much."

"You eat like a threshing machine."

"I can eat less."

"You'll eat like a threshing machine and you'll work like one and you'll be paid like one, and if Ilsa says you're crew, you're crew. Ilsa?"

From the deck, without looking up from the block she was reeving: "He's crew. He held the line on the third pull when Odile slipped. That's the whole examination."

"That's not fair," Odile said. "I slipped because the line was dishonest."

"Everything's dishonest except the water," Ilsa said. "That's why we're sailors."

* * *

Toward evening the Kestrel rode at anchor in the channel, riding the way she always had, with a little swagger, as if the water were lucky to have her. The pumps had quieted to a murmur. The wound in her sternpost was patched and would want proper work in port, but she'd hold — I'd have staked my name on it, and my name was about the only thing I had left that hadn't been salvaged, sold, or sunk.

I stood at the wheel alone while the light went long and gold across the flats. Behind me the cabin held my father's letter, wrapped in oilcloth, kept dry the way you keep the one thing you'd reach back into a burning house for. In my pocket, the ash I hadn't bothered to wash off my hands — the ledger's ash, the fortune's ash, the whole poisoned inheritance of it, gone into the wind off the beach where it could buy nothing and cost no one anything ever again.

People will tell you that walking away from a fortune is the hard part. It isn't. The hard part is the hour after, when your hands are empty and you find out what's still in them.

What was in mine, that evening at the wheel: a boat. A crew that had chosen me back. A partner's handshake drying on the rail. A boy at the masthead learning the marks. And a channel ahead, running clean through the falling light, every bar and bend of it written in my blood and my father's hand and nobody else's.

Ilsa came up and stood beside me, and neither of us said anything for a while, because we're sailors, and we know when the talking's done and the water's started.

"Tide turns in twenty minutes," she said finally. "Port's a day's sail if we catch it."

"Then we catch it."

"You could rest first. You nearly drowned twice this week."

"Ilsa. Look at that sky. Look at that water." I put my hands on the wheel, on the wood my father had chosen and I had worn smooth, and felt her come alive under my palms the way she always did, the way she always would. "That's not a sky you rest under. That's a sky you sail under."

Ilsa smiled — the rare one, the real one — and went forward to call the hands, and I brought the Kestrel's head around onto the channel as the tide took her, and the river, which knows me better than I have ever known myself, carried us home.

But home, I was already beginning to understand, was not going to be simple. Because at the mouth of the channel, riding at anchor with no lights showing and no business being there, a boat waited — small, dark, and flying no colors at all — and as the last light caught her deck, I saw a figure standing at her rail, watching us come, and raising one hand in a salute that was either a greeting or a warning.

And I knew that walk. I had spent a whole missing night learning it, and a whole season forgetting it, and now, God help me, I remembered it completely.
