## 22. A Trail for the Monsoon

The figure at the rail turned out to be nobody at all, in the way that most of the things we fear turn out to be nobody.

By the time the Kestrel closed the last half-mile, the boat had shown her colors after all — or rather, her lack of them resolved into something worse than no colors: a survey pennant, the kind the harbor office flies when they want to look official without being answerable. And the man with the remembered walk was a harbormaster's clerk named Pell, who had once spent an entire missing night of my life buying me drinks and asking questions I'd been too young and too flattered to recognize as questions.

"You remember me," he said, when I brought the Kestrel alongside easy as a kiss. He looked older. Softer in the middle. The walk was the same, though — that rolling, patient gait of a man who has never once hurried toward anything he wanted.

"I remember everything now," I said. "That's the trouble."

He smiled like a man who thought that was good news. "The harbor office wants a statement about the Meridian. About Captain Fenwick's vessel. There are... irregularities."

"There are," I agreed. "I've seen them personally. Some of them I burned on a beach."

His smile flickered. Behind him, his little survey boat rocked on the leftover swell, and beyond him the channel opened out onto a sea scrubbed clean by the storm, blue as a promise nobody intended to keep.

"Captain Underhill," he said carefully, "the fortune alone would have set you up for life. Everyone knows what was aboard."

"Everyone knows what was aboard," I repeated. "Funny. Nobody ever asked what else was aboard." I let that sit while the tide turned under us, gentle as breathing. "Tell the harbor office whatever you like, Pell. Tell them the ledger went down with the strongroom. It's nearly true. Paper doesn't survive salt and fire both, and I made sure of the fire myself."

"And your father's letter?"

I looked at him for a long moment. "What letter?"

* * *

We cleared the harbor bar on the morning tide, three days later, with the Kestrel patched and proud and the whole crew on deck to watch the monsoon's wreckage recede behind us.

That's not strictly true. We cleared the bar because I decided we would, and because Ilsa had spent two days bullying the shipwright's boy into replacing a plank he swore couldn't be replaced, and because Fenwick's money — the advance, the only part of his fortune I'd ever touch — had bought us paint and canvas and a new jib with a scarlet arrowhead on it that Sef chose himself and dared anyone to comment on. Nobody commented. Sef had earned the right to ugly sails.

Reyes came down to the dock to see us off. That still sits strangely in my chest, writing it. Captain Emilia Reyes, who taught me to read water before I could read letters, standing on the pilings with her hands in her pockets and nothing left to command but her own regret.

"I've got a contract running cargo upcoast," she said. "Legitimate. Boring. Pays on time." She shrugged, which from Reyes was a speech. "Could use a pilot who knows the shallows better than my charts do."

"I know exactly how well your charts do," I said, and she had the grace to laugh.

"Terms?" she asked, because she'd learned that much about me, at least, in the end.

"Equal shares. My route. And when I say a sandbar's moved, the sandbar's moved."

"Done," she said, and shook my hand like it cost her something and was worth paying anyway. "You know," she added, as I stepped back aboard, "your father used to say the river gives back what you put into it. I always thought that was sentiment."

"It's hydrology," I said. "But it's also sentiment. That's what makes it true."

* * *

The first clear morning of the new season came up gold and windless, and I took the wheel myself, which Ilsa pretended not to notice and Sef pretended not to be delighted by.

There is a feeling I have chased my whole life and only ever caught in fragments — standing at the wheel at dawn with a fair tide under the hull and the whole day unspent ahead of you. Most captains will tell you it's freedom. It isn't. Freedom is just the absence of weight, and there's plenty of weight left in me: a father's letter folded in oilcloth against my heart, a burned ledger's worth of secrets sitting in my chest like ballast, a debt to a dead man that I pay off daily simply by remembering him correctly.

No. What it is, is *direction*. The difference between drifting and steering. For one missing night and one long season, I had confused the two, and called my ignorance peace.

"Where to, Captain?" Ilsa asked, coming up beside me with two cups of coffee and handing me the good one. She'd stopped asking where to months ago; she asked now the way you ask a question you already love the answer to.

I unfolded my father's charts on the binnacle box — the old ones, the hand-drawn ones, with his cramped annotations and, in the margins, in a second handwriting I finally knew as well as my own, my mother's corrections. *Bar shifted east after the flood of '09.* *Channel favors the north bank here at low water.* Two hands, arguing gently across decades about where the deep water lay. I'd spent years reading them as grief. Now I read them as conversation.

"We're not taking the standard route," I said.

Ilsa raised an eyebrow. "The standard route is the only route through the Narrows this time of year."

"The standard route *was* the only route." I tapped the chart, where my father had penciled a question mark thirty years ago and never come back to answer it. A side channel, unmarked, dismissed by every pilot since as too shallow, too twisty, too much trouble. "The flood moved the bar. His note says so. Nobody checked because nobody believed him." I looked up at the sky, clean and high and blue, and felt the tide lift the hull beneath us like a hand under the keel. "I believe him. I'm going to go check his work."

"That's not a route," Ilsa said. "That's a guess with scenery."

"It's a trail," I said. "Somebody has to cut it first."

She drank her coffee and watched me for a moment, and then she did the thing she does instead of hugging people: she nodded once, slowly, like a door closing softly on a room full of agreement. "Sef!" she bellowed aft. "Get forward and keep your eyes on the water! Captain's found a shortcut nobody's drowned in yet!"

"Yet!" Sef called back cheerfully, and the Kestrel swung her head toward the Narrows.

* * *

I won't pretend the passage was easy. The Narrows never are, and my father's phantom channel was narrower than even his notes suggested — twice we kissed the mud and twice I read it wrong and once I read it right by pure stubbornness, holding the Kestrel in a slot of green water no wider than her own shadow while the current argued and the banks slid past close enough to count the crabs.

But here is the thing I want to set down properly, because it matters more than the salvage, more than the fortune, more than Dario Fenwick breaking apart on his own illiteracy ever could:

The river knew me.

Not in the mystical way — I've no patience for the mystical, and neither did my father, who annotated his charts like a man filing complaints with God. In the practical way. Every bend arrived when I expected it. Every eddy turned where my hands already were. Somewhere between the wreck and the burning and the long slow refloating, the gap in me had filled not with memory exactly but with *fluency* — the lost night stitched back in, ugly seams and all, until the cloth was whole and mine again. I had gone looking for a fortune and found a self, and the exchange rate, I can tell you, was fair.

At the far end of the Narrows the channel opened suddenly onto open water, wide and shining, and the Kestrel surged out of confinement like a held breath released.

"Well?" Ilsa said.

"He was right," I said. My voice cracked on it, and I let it. "It saves four hours. Maybe five against the ebb." I took out my pencil and, on my father's chart, beneath his thirty-year-old question mark, I wrote in my own hand: *Confirmed. Deep water mid-channel. Favor the north bank at low water. — C.U.*

Two hands on one chart again. Arguing gently across generations about where the deep water lies.

* * *

We ran all that day and anchored at dusk in a cove I'd known since childhood, and I sat on the foredeck after the others turned in and read my father's letter one more time by lantern light.

*Forgive yourself for surviving,* it ended. *It is the only cargo worth carrying, and the only one you cannot sell.*

He was mostly right, as usual. You can't sell forgiveness, that's true. But you can spend it. I'd spent mine on a burned ledger and a saved fishing family and a rival given honest work, and I found the account wasn't empty. It never is, if you keep making deposits.

Ahead of us, upcoast, waited Reyes and a boring legitimate contract and a season of honest freight. Behind us, the monsoon was already becoming a story, the way weather does — already shrinking into the shape of a tale I'd tell in dockside bars for years, improving slightly each telling, the way all survivors improve.

And below me, under the keel, the tide was turning fair.

I stood at the rail a while longer, watching the first stars come out over water that held no wrecks I hadn't faced and no debts I hadn't paid, and then I went below to chart tomorrow's run — my route, my hand, my trail cut fresh through country everyone said was impossible.

The river knows me.

God help anyone who tries to sail it without me.
