## 1. Miles, Not Years

The map covered the entire north wall of the survey office, and Jude Eriksen had drawn every line on it himself.

Not the living lines — those belonged to the government, to the men in Cazadero who still believed the interior could be stitched together with iron and gravel. Jude drew only the dead ones. The closed spurs. The abandoned branches. The railroads that had been built with such confidence and then surrendered, one by one, to flood and landslide and the slow indifference of the country they were meant to serve. He inked them in a color no one else used — a gray so pale it was almost the color of forgetting — and he kept them current the way other men kept ledgers or graves.

Seven years of dead track. Four hundred and eleven miles of it, if you measured the way he measured: not in years, not in seasons, not in birthdays or anniversaries, but in miles of line that no longer carried anything but rain.

He was adding the latest when the telegram came. The Kesselung Branch, thirty-two miles of it, officially decommissioned that spring after a washout took out the trestle at Mile 19. He'd walked it himself in March, boots sinking into ballast gone soft as bread, cataloguing the decay with the particular tenderness he reserved for things that were past saving. Now he ruled the line through with his straightedge, wrote *CLOSED — WASHOUT '29* in his small, level hand, and stepped back to look at the whole of it.

From a distance the map looked like a circulatory system that had given up on its extremities. The trunk lines still ran dark and confident from the coast into the highlands, but the branches thinned and frayed until they simply stopped, mid-country, trailing off into blank paper like sentences no one had finished. Lena would have said it looked sad. She'd have said it in that way she had of saying true things lightly, so you could hold them without being cut.

Lena had been dead seven years, and he still heard her voice most clearly over maps.

He was reaching for the ink rag when the knock came — two raps, apologetic, the knock of the post rider who knew better than to expect conversation. Jude opened the door to a boy of maybe sixteen, rain-beaded, holding out an envelope the color of old teeth. Government stock. The embossed crest of the Department of Rivers and Railways sat in the corner like a wax seal on something unpleasant.

"Sign here," the boy said.

Jude signed. The boy was already turning away, already thinking about the next house, the next mile. Everything in this country thought about the next mile.

He didn't open the telegram right away. That was a discipline he'd taught himself years ago, back when telegrams still had the power to stop his heart: set it on the desk, finish the task in front of you, let the thing wait. Waiting made it smaller. Most things got smaller if you made them wait long enough.

He sharpened a pencil instead. He squared the map's lower corner, which did not need squaring. Then he picked up the envelope, worked his thumb under the flap, and read:

*DEPT RIVERS & RAILWAYS CAZADERO STOP UPRIVER SURVEY TEAM GARRISON PARTY SEVEN PERSONS SILENT ELEVEN DAYS STOP LAST POSITION ABOVE KARST NARROWS STOP YOUR KNOWLEDGE ABANDONED LINES AND CONVERSION SPURS REQUIRED STOP EXPEDITION DEPARTS WITH NEXT FAVORABLE TIDE STOP THIS IS A DIRECT REQUISITION UNDER SURVEY ACT SECTION 12 STOP RESPOND BY WIRE*

Jude read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still, the way he stood on bridges when he wanted to feel for vibration through his boot soles.

A requisition. Not a request. Section 12 gave the Department the authority to conscript licensed engineers for emergency work, and in twenty years of service he had never once seen it invoked, because invoking it meant admitting that the ordinary machinery of the country had failed somewhere and needed a man pried out of his life to fix it.

They wanted him upriver. Above the Karst Narrows, where the river went from wide and patient to narrow and murderous in the space of a morning. Where the old Kesselung extension had been pulled up and sold for scrap two decades ago, and where the survey teams now used the ghost of the grade as a towpath, because there was nothing else to walk on.

Eleven days silent. Seven persons.

He set the telegram down and looked at his map — at all those gray lines running toward the interior and stopping, stopping, stopping — and felt the familiar arithmetic begin. It was the arithmetic that had gotten him through the worst years, the one where grief was converted into distance and distance into work. Cazadero was ninety miles east by the coast road. From Cazadero to the Narrows, another hundred and forty by water, if the water allowed it. Two hundred and thirty miles between him and the place where seven people had stopped answering their radio.

Two hundred and thirty miles was nothing. He had spent seven years learning exactly how far nothing could reach.

He found the requisition form in the bottom drawer, under a decade of dust and a bottle of ink that had dried to lacquer, and began to write his refusal.

*E. JUDE ERIKSEN, LICENSED ENGINEER NO. 4471, DECLINES REQUISITION ON GROUNDS OF—*

He stopped. Grounds of what? Health? He was fifty-one and sound as a trestle. Hardship? His hardship was a map on a wall. Incompetence? They'd have laughed that one out of the wire office; his reputation was the last thing he owned outright.

Grounds of grief, he might have written. Grounds of seven years of carefully managed avoidance. Grounds of the fact that everything I love is buried along your abandoned lines and I have arranged my entire existence so that I never have to stand on any of them again.

He wrote: *DECLINES ON PERSONAL GROUNDS.* It had the advantage of being true and the disadvantage of being useless, and he sealed it anyway, and put on his coat, and walked down through the wet evening to the wire office to send it.

* * *

The town of Marrow Bend had been a rail town once, and it still wore the shape of that fact the way a widower wears a wedding band — out of habit, out of a failure to decide what else to do with the space. The depot was a hardware store now. The roundhouse was apartments. But the streets ran straight and wide where the sidings used to, and the water tower still stood over everything, dry for fifteen years, rusting into a shape that children said looked like a hanging man.

Jude had chosen Marrow Bend for exactly one reason: no line ran through it that connected to the valley where Lena died. You could live here your whole life and never be forced east, never be forced onto the coast road that passed within four miles of the town that drowned. Seven years of careful geography. Seven years of drawing dead lines on a wall so he'd always know which roads to refuse.

The wire clerk was a woman named Hesta Odom, sixty-odd, spectacles on a chain, who had transmitted every message Jude had sent since he moved here and had never once asked why a man who mapped abandonment for pleasure received so little mail. She read his refusal, tapped it into the key with quick fingers, and then paused with her hand on the switch.

"You'll want to wait for the acknowledgment," she said. "Requisitions get answered same hour. Department rule."

"I know the rule."

"Then sit down, Eriksen. You look like a man who stands too much."

He sat. The wire office smelled of ozone and cold tea, and rain worked at the windows in long diagonal strokes, and somewhere across the street a dog was barking at nothing with the tireless conviction of dogs. Jude watched Hesta's lamp flicker as the answer came back, letter by letter, and saw her face do something complicated before she transcribed it onto the flimsy.

She handed it over without reading it aloud, which was unlike her, which meant everything.

*REQUISITION NOTED AND REFUSED STOP BE ADVISED MISSING PARTY INCLUDES T ERIKSEN ASSISTANT SURVEYOR STOP DEPT EXTENDS SYMPATHIES STOP REQUISITION REMAINS OPEN SHOULD YOU REVISE*

The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when something large passes through them without touching the walls.

Tomas.

Lena's brother. The boy — no, not a boy anymore, forty-one now, though Jude's mind kept serving up the nineteen-year-old with the crooked grin who used to ride the freight cars out to the junction just to feel the speed — Tomas, who had stood at Lena's funeral with his jaw set like a man holding a door shut against weather, who had shaken Jude's hand once, hard, and then walked out of his life with the same terrible tidiness with which Jude had walked out of everyone else's. Seven years. No letters. No wires. Nothing but the knowledge, renewed each morning like a wound dressed in the dark, that the last of her family considered him a man who had failed her, and that the consideration was correct.

And now the Department was telling him, in the flat language of telegrams, that Tomas Eriksen was above the Karst Narrows, and had been silent for eleven days, and that the river out there was rising into its monsoon temper with every tide.

"He's family?" Hesta asked quietly. She'd read the name. Of course she'd read the name; she read everything, it was her trade.

"My late wife's brother."

Hesta nodded slowly and turned back to her key, giving him the privacy of her turned shoulders, which was the finest courtesy the woman knew how to extend.

Jude sat in the wire office for a long time while the rain came down and the dog across the street finally lost interest in nothing.

He tried to run the arithmetic and found it broken. This was the trouble with measuring your life in miles: miles were indifferent, and the moment something wasn't indifferent anymore, the whole system seized. Two hundred and thirty miles to the Narrows. What was that? Nothing, yesterday. Tonight it was the exact distance between him and the last person alive who remembered Lena laughing, who remembered her at nine years old falling off a dock at Cazadero and coming up furious, who remembered the wedding, who remembered the coffin going into the ground in the flooded churchyard because the ground wouldn't take her any deeper.

Grief interrupted by obligation. Or obligation interrupting grief — he couldn't have said which was the intruder. They stood in him together like two strangers caught in the same doorway, each waiting for the other to move first.

He thought about refusing again. He turned the refusal over and examined it the way he examined a cracked rail: looking for the flaw that would justify taking it out of service. And the flaw was this — there was no version of the rest of his life in which he could draw another gray line on that wall and not see Tomas's name at the end of it. No version in which the map stayed a ledger of safe distances instead of becoming what it would become: a record of everything he'd refused to save.

The monsoon was coming. Everyone on this coast could feel it in the barometer and in their knees; the air had that bruised, waiting weight it got for two weeks every year. Eleven days of silence above the Narrows, and the river rising, and a salvage fleet — the telegram hadn't mentioned salvage, but salvage fleets followed wrecks the way gulls followed plows, and Jude had been around this country long enough to hear the second story underneath the first — already smelling cargo.

He stood up.

"Hesta," he said. "Send a revision."

She swiveled back around, key poised, and waited with the patience of a woman who had known for seven years this wire would come someday.

*E JUDE ERIKSEN LICENSED ENGINEER NO 4471 ACCEPTS REQUISITION SECTION 12 STOP WILL REPORT CAZADERO DEPOT WITHIN FORTY-EIGHT HOURS STOP REQUEST ALL SURVEY RECORDS GARRISON PARTY INCLUDING PERSONNEL FILES AND LAST KNOWN POSITIONS STOP ADVISE EQUIPMENT REQUIREMENTS FOR UPRIVER WORK MONSOON CONDITIONS*

The key clattered. Somewhere out in the dark, past the drowned towns and the dead spurs and the four hundred and eleven miles of line he'd buried on a wall, the message went hunting for Cazadero through the wet night.

Jude walked home through the rain without hurrying. At the office he stood a long time before the map, coat dripping, and found his eyes doing what they always did: tracing the routes east, the ones he'd spent seven years refusing. The coast road. The old ferry crossing at Delvert. And beyond them, faint on the paper because he'd never had cause to ink them, the ghost grades above the Karst Narrows where a survey team had walked into silence carrying his wife's brother among them.

He unrolled a fresh sheet of drafting paper, weighted its corners, and began to draw the route to Cazadero in working black — the first new line on any surface in his keeping that pointed toward something instead of away.

It was ninety miles. For the first time in seven years, the number felt enormous.

He worked until the lamp burned low, and outside the rain thickened toward monsoon, and the river that ran somewhere below the town in the dark began, very quietly, to rise.
