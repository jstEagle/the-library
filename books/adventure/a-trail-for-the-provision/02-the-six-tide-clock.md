## 2. The Six-Tide Clock

Cazadero smelled of diesel and brine and rotting fruit, the way every river port at the end of a rail line smells when it has stopped pretending to be anything else. Jude Eriksen came down from the survey office with the telegram folded in his breast pocket like a splinter he hadn't yet decided to dig out, and stood on the quay watching the water do what water always did — carry things away without asking where they were going.

The port had been built for trains. You could still see the bones of them: the great iron gantries that had once swung freight cars over the shallows to barges, the stubs of track running out onto disused piers, rails furred green with salt. The government had closed the line eleven years ago, after the flood season that took the low country, and the town had grown around the absence like scar tissue around a wound. Warehouses now. Fish markets. A chandlery with its sign repainted three times.

Jude knew all this because his map told him so. Not the official map — the other one, the one he kept in oilcloth in his satchel, inked and re-inked across seven years, every closed line in the territory marked in red and annotated in his own small hand: *date closed, reason, condition of bed, last known traffic.* It was not a hobby. He had never been able to explain to anyone what it was, least of all himself, except that when Lena died he had discovered that grief was a country with no roads through it, and mapping dead railways was the closest thing to walking it that he could bear.

The telegram had come two days ago. GOVERNMENT HYDROGRAPHIC OFFICE REQUESTS SERVICES OF J. ERIKSEN, ENGINEER, FOR EXPEDITION UPRIVER STOP SURVEY TEAM SILENT NINE DAYS STOP COMPENSATION PER SCHEDULE D STOP RESPOND BY WIRE.

He had written *decline* on the reply form and left it sitting on his desk, and then he had gone out and walked twelve miles of abandoned spur north of the city because his legs needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Then yesterday evening, a second wire, one line long: OCHOA, MEI, LISTED MISSING STOP ALSO T. VASQUEZ.

Tomas Vasquez. Lena's brother. The man who had stood beside him at the funeral and never spoken to him since.

Jude had sat up all night with the reply form, pen uncapped, and by dawn the word *decline* had a line struck through it that might have been deliberate or might have been the pen slipping as his hand finally gave out. Either way, this morning he had wired *accept*, and now here he was on the quay at Cazadero, feeling less like a man who had made a decision than like a man who had been swept downstream and was calling it swimming.

* * *

The expedition boat was moored at the third pier, and she was nothing much to look at: a converted survey launch about eighty feet stem to stern, shallow-drafted, with a crane arm aft and a wheelhouse that had been patched with steel plate a different color than the rest of her hull. Her name was painted on the bow in flaking white letters — *Provision* — and beneath the name someone had stenciled, more recently, the crest of the Hydrographic Office, which gave her an air of borrowed authority, like a man wearing his dead father's coat.

A woman was standing at the gangway checking a manifest against crates being loaded by two deckhands, and Jude knew before she turned that this was the captain. She had the particular stillness of people who work rivers — a economy of motion, weight settled low, eyes doing the work the body declined to. When she saw him she didn't smile.

"Eriksen." Not a question. "You're late."

"The wire said noon."

"The wire said noon. The tide said ten o'clock, and the tide doesn't negotiate." Odile Ferran was perhaps fifty, gray shot through black hair cut short, forearms bare below rolled sleeves and mapped with old rope burns. She looked him over the way she'd look over a used engine: honestly, without cruelty, cataloguing faults. "You're the one who maps the dead lines."

It was not how he'd expected to be introduced. "I keep records."

"I've seen your records. My quartermaster bought a copy off a chart dealer in Corvo two years ago. Your annotations on the Karst Narrows spur were better than anything the Office ever published." She let that sit a moment, watching whether he'd be flattered. He wasn't, particularly, and something in her face suggested approval of that too. "Come aboard. Briefing's in the saloon, such as it is."

The saloon was a cramped room aft with a table bolted to the floor, charts pinned to every vertical surface, and a radio set occupying the corner like a shrine. Two people were already there. One was a boy — no, young man, though he couldn't have been twenty — thin as a mast, headphones around his neck, sorting spools of wire with hands that moved fast and precise. The other was a woman sitting on the table itself rather than a chair, dark-skinned, mid-thirties, turning a brass sounding-weight over in her fingers as if reading its future.

"Introductions," Ferran said. "Sef Okonkwo, radio. Priya Raghavan, salvage scout. They're who you get. Sit down."

"Charming as ever, Captain," Priya said, and slid off the table to offer Jude her hand. Her grip was dry and strong. Up close there was a faded scar along her jaw, old enough to be part of her rather than something that had happened to her. "The famous mapmaker. I've heard two stories about you and they contradict each other."

"Most stories do."

"Mm. The one I believe is that you can read a closed rail bed the way a pilot reads water." She tilted her head. "We'll see."

Sef nodded at him shyly and went back to his spools. Jude took the chair farthest from the radio, which hummed faintly even switched off, like something breathing in its sleep.

Ferran unrolled a chart across the table and weighted its corners with mugs. "Here's the shape of it. Nine days ago, a government survey team working the upper reach past the Karst Narrows went silent. Last confirmed position here—" she tapped a point above the narrows, near a tributary mouth "—at the old mission station reach. Their tender is believed holed; we don't know. Four personnel. We are the rescue element. There is also," and her voice flattened in a way that told Jude exactly what she thought of the next sentence, "a commercial interest in the area. Salvage fleet out of Corvo, three vessels, loading now. They claim to be after cargo from a wreck reported in the same reach. The *Andronica*, lost seven years back with general freight. The Office believes otherwise but can't prove anything, and their charter is technically legal, so we share the river. Any questions so far?"

"Seven years back," Jude said quietly. "That would be the flood year."

"It would." Ferran's eyes flicked to him, brief and unreadable. "Now. The arithmetic. The monsoon makes landfall in twelve days — the Office's meteorologist says eleven to thirteen, call it twelve. Once it lands, the upper gorge closes. No vessel goes up, none comes down, for weeks. To reach the mission station reach from here requires six good tides — six windows where the water stands high enough to clear the shoals and the current runs slow enough to make headway against the gorge. Six tides out, and if we want to bring anyone home alive, six tides back. Twelve tides minimum, plus time on station, plus weather margin. That is why you're late being a problem, Mr. Eriksen. Every hour we burn here is an hour the monsoon eats later."

"Twelve days, six tides each way," Jude said. "That leaves you almost nothing. What's the tide interval on that reach?"

"Thirteen hours, give or take. But half of them are useless — neaps, wind against current, debris after rain in the headwaters. In practice you get maybe four usable windows in any five days." She straightened. "So. We sail on tomorrow morning's tide, and we do not stop for anything that isn't dying."

The room absorbed that. Sef's hands had gone still on the spools. Priya was spinning the sounding-weight again, faster.

"And my role?" Jude asked. "I'm a railway engineer. You need pilots and divers, not track men."

"What we need," Ferran said, "is the first leg. The river channel below Corvo Bend silted out two years ago — officially. The Office wants us running the old Kestrel Spur route instead: the flood canal they cut along the rail alignment in the last decade of the line's operation, then abandoned. It's navigable according to nobody, because nobody has surveyed it since closure. Except apparently you've walked most of that alignment. Your notes say the canal bed holds depth at the culverts and fails at the old ballast washouts. If your notes are right, we save a full day and avoid Corvo Bend entirely — which matters, because the salvage fleet will be watching Corvo Bend."

"And if my notes are wrong?"

"Then we go aground somewhere quiet and lose the race, and some very tired people upriver stay missing." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Your notes are seven years old. Things move. I'm told you're the sort of man who knows exactly how much."

Jude looked down at the chart. The Kestrel Spur ran northwest from Cazadero through country he knew too well — and the line of it passed, inevitably, inevitably, within four miles of a town that no longer appeared on maps under its own name. He had known this would happen. He had known it when he wired *accept*, and he had wired anyway, and now the route lay on the table in front of him drawn in blue and black like a sentence he'd been composing for seven years.

"I'll need to see the alignment myself before we commit the boat to it," he said. "Tomorrow, at first light, before the tide."

"You'll see it from the wheelhouse as we run it," Ferran said. "We sail on tomorrow's tide. That's not negotiable either."

* * *

He spent the afternoon aboard, learning the *Provision* the only way he trusted — with his hands. He crawled the engine room with the chief, a silent Tongan named Maka who communicated in grunts and gauges and seemed satisfied by Jude's questions. He measured the draft marks himself, because he trusted no one else's paint. He stood in the wheelhouse while Ferran pretended not to watch him and studied the river below the piers: brown-green, sluggish, carrying sticks and a drowned chair and the smell of the low country coming apart ahead of the rains.

Late in the day he found Sef on the aft deck, splicing an antenna lead, tongue between his teeth.

"You're the radio man," Jude said. "Have you heard anything? From the survey team?"

Sef's hands stopped. "Nothing direct. There was — the Office won't confirm it, but there was a fragment two nights ago. Maybe forty seconds of voice on the emergency band, way down in the noise. Somebody saying coordinates, mostly. Then a word that might have been 'upstream.' Might have been 'upriver.'" He shrugged, uncomfortable. "Could be nothing. Could be a ghost — atmospherics ahead of the monsoon do strange things. But Captain Ferran made me log it, and we're sailing, so."

"Somebody's alive," Jude said.

"Somebody's alive," Sef agreed softly, and looked out at the water, and Jude recognized the expression on his face because he'd worn it himself once, years ago, standing on a different pier waiting for news that came in the worst possible form. Young. Hopeful. Not yet armored. Jude found, to his mild alarm, that he wanted to tell the boy to armor up early, save himself the cost — and equally found that he hoped the boy never would.

* * *

Dusk brought the salvage fleet into view.

They were moored two piers down, and they had not been there that morning: three black-hulled vessels, modern, fast, riding high and empty and taking on cargo crated in government-stamped timber. Floodlights on their decks turned the evening water white. Men worked with the crisp efficiency of people paid well and watched closely. At the berth nearest the quay, a fourth vessel — smaller, a scout launch — was having its name painted over. Jude could just make out the old name ghosting beneath the fresh black: something ending in *-star*. And above it, going on new, letter by letter, in gold: *Vane Star.*

"Vane," Priya said, at his shoulder. He hadn't heard her come up; she moved like the deck herself. "Marcus Vane's fleet. You'll hear plenty about him before this is done, most of it from lawyers."

"You know him?"

Something crossed her face, quick and deep, like a fish under ice. "I know of him." She nodded at the loading cranes. "Look at what they're carrying. Pump rigs, cutting gear, dive bells. That's not rescue equipment, engineer. That's a strip job. Whatever's lying in that reach, Vane means to take it apart and haul it out before the monsoon buries the site for a season — maybe forever. And whatever the survey team stumbled into up there—" she paused, choosing, "—well. Teams don't usually go silent all at once. Radios fail one at a time."

"You think Vane's fleet reached them first."

"I think nine days is a long silence," Priya said, "and I think a man who repaints his ship's name the week he sails doesn't want to be remembered under the old one." She turned to him, and in the floodlight spilling from the black ships her eyes were hard and bright. "Captain Ferran will tell you we're racing the monsoon. That's true. But we're also racing that, and the monsoon at least plays fair."

Below them, on the quay, a gangway clattered down from the largest of the black vessels, and a party came ashore: two men in suits that had no business on a working waterfront, and behind them a broad figure in a pale linen coat who stood a moment at the foot of the gangway looking — Jude was certain of it, though the distance was great — directly at the *Provision*. Looking, it seemed, at him.

Then the figure turned, and the party walked off up the quay, and the floodlights snapped off one by one, leaving the black ships to the dark.

"Six tides," Jude said quietly, to no one, to the water, to the red lines on a map folded in oilcloth over his heart. Tomorrow's tide would carry him up a dead railway's drowned shadow, toward a town he had spent seven years steering his whole life around, toward a brother-in-law who might be alive and might be a word in static, toward whatever Marcus Vane wanted badly enough to repaint his name for.

He had wired *accept*. Standing there in the dark with the clock already running — twelve days, six tides, one chance — Jude understood that the word had stopped being a choice somewhere between the telegraph office and the pier, and had become simply the direction the water was going.

Behind him, Sef's radio crackled awake in the saloon, and the boy's voice came quick and sharp across the deck:

"Captain! Captain Ferran — I've got the band again. Somebody's transmitting."
