## 3. Closed Lines

The map came out of its oilcloth before dawn, the way it always did when Jude couldn't sleep, and this morning he had not slept at all.

He spread it across the chart table in Odile Ferran's wheelhouse while the *Provision* still sat tied to her mooring at Cazadero, breathing with the tide. The map was his own work — forty years of surveying compressed into a single sheet of linen-backed paper, every line drawn in his own hand: solid ink for the living railways, and for the dead ones, a fine gray stipple that most men would have missed entirely. He'd been drawing closed lines since Lena died. Seven years of them. Every abandoned spur, every washed-out trestle, every branch line the companies had written off and let the jungle take back — he collected them the way other men collected debts or grudges, because a closed line was a place no one could ask him to go.

"Show me," Odile said.

She stood at the wheelhouse door with two tin cups of coffee, steam rising off both, and she did not sit down until she'd put one in front of him. She was a broad woman, gray-streaked, with hands like dock rope and a way of looking at a man as though she were already calculating what he could carry. Jude had known captains who shouted their authority and captains who bought it with flattery. Odile simply assumed hers, the way the river assumed its course.

"The government charts end here." He tapped the blue-line survey, the official one, where the navigable channel dissolved into a question mark of dotted lines above the Karst Narrows. "They stopped mapping after the flood year. Everyone assumes the upper reach is impassable by water until the monsoon swells it, and by then Vane's fleet will already be sitting on the wreck site."

"And you assume otherwise."

"I don't assume. I know." He turned his own map so she could read it. "There's an old logging spur that ran from Corvane Junction up to the slate quarries. Closed eleven years ago. The company pulled the rails but left the bed graded, drained, ballasted. It runs along the ridge above the north bank for thirty miles, then drops to the river at a place called Halloway Landing, where there's a stone quay built for the quarry barges. Still standing. I surveyed it myself in—" He stopped. Recalculated. "Some years ago."

"You're proposing we drive a boat up a railroad."

"I'm proposing we drive trucks up a railroad, to Halloway Landing, where your boat is waiting. Or will be, if you send Sef's telegram tonight. The *Provision* draws four feet light. The upper reach above the narrows is six feet even in dry season — I've seen the soundings. Vane's fleet can't follow. His ships draw too much, and the only road route is the one nobody remembers exists."

Odile drank her coffee and looked at the map for a long time. Outside, the gulls were working the fish market, and somewhere down the dock Priya Raghavan was arguing with a fuel merchant in three languages, two of them invented.

"Thirty miles of jungle grade," she said finally. "Bridges out?"

"Some. None that matter. There are three culverts I'd want to inspect, and the viaduct at Kilometer 19 is timber — I'd trust it with a truck, not with a locomotive. But nothing we can't cross."

"And you know all this because?"

Because I have spent seven years memorizing every place the world has abandoned, he thought, so that I would never again be surprised by loss. Because grief, given enough time, becomes cartography.

"Because it was my job once," he said. "Before I retired from it."

* * *

They loaded at first light: two flatbed trucks borrowed against Odile's name and a bond she signed without reading, crates of diesel, rice, chain, the disassembled pieces of Sef's radio rig wrapped in canvas, and Mei Ochoa's medical kit — because the government man in Cazadero had finally released the missing team's manifest, and a doctor's kit had been aboard, which meant someone had planned to need it.

Priya rode in the lead truck with Jude. She climbed into the passenger seat with the economy of a woman who had been getting into moving vehicles in bad places her whole life, settled her rifle between her knees, and looked at the map unfolded across his steering hand.

"That's not a government sheet," she said.

"No."

"You drew it."

"Yes."

She studied the gray stippled lines running up off the edge of the blue survey like veins off a leaf. "How many of these are there? Closed lines."

"On this sheet? Sixty-one."

"And you carry it everywhere."

"It's paper. It weighs nothing."

Priya made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter were required to commit to anything. "My brother used to say that about his knife." She said it lightly, and then went quiet in a way that told Jude the sentence had cost her something, and he understood they were alike in at least one respect: they both knew how to say a true thing and immediately bury it.

The trucks rolled out of Cazadero at 0540, ahead of the heat, ahead of the salvage fleet whose cranes they could see loading across the harbor — great patient iron birds being fed cargo nets full of winches and cutting gear. Vane's people worked fast and silent. That frightened Jude more than shouting would have.

Corvane Junction was ninety minutes inland, where the paved road gave out and the old spur began. And Jude had known, since the moment he'd traced the route for Odile with his finger, exactly which line it was.

He had known, and he had said nothing.

* * *

The spur began innocently enough — a grass-grown embankment shouldering out of the scrub, ballast stones showing through like bone through skin. The trucks climbed onto it with a lurch and a grinding of tires on old stone, and suddenly there was a road where the maps said there was none, running straight and level and eerily perfect through country that had never known a wheelbarrow.

Sef leaned out the window of the second truck and whooped. The boy was nineteen, all elbows and delight, a radio operator who treated every mile of the journey as a broadcast to some imaginary audience. "It's like driving on a ghost!" he called. "Jude! How do you even know this is here?"

"Company records," Jude said. "And field surveys."

"He surveyed it himself," Priya said from beside him, not unkindly. "In some years ago."

Jude drove and did not answer.

The line ran northwest along the ridge, and the country opened beneath them — the river appearing and disappearing in the valley to the south, a brown gleam through the canopy, carrying their boat upstream behind them by some other arithmetic of current and tide. Termitaries stood up out of the embankment shoulders like red sentinels. A monitor lizard poured itself off the grade and vanished. Once, crossing a meadow stretch where the jungle hadn't yet reclaimed the right-of-way, they flushed a herd of something horned and low-slung that went bounding away through the mist, and Sef laughed so hard he had to pull the truck over.

Jude watched the kilometer posts go by. The company had never removed them; they stood every half-mile, concrete, the numerals worn to shadows. Kilometer 6. Kilometer 9. Kilometer 12.

Kilometer 14.

He had known this would happen. He had traced the route knowing it, finger on the map, and had told himself that geography was geography, that the shortest line between duty and the wreck site happened to pass through the one town on earth he had spent seven years driving around, and that a man could pass through a place without entering it. Roads went around towns all the time. He would keep the trucks on the grade. The spur bypassed San Rafael del Agua on the high side, above the flood line, above everything. They wouldn't even have to slow down.

That was what he told himself, at Kilometer 12, and at 13, and at 14, where the trees thinned and the land fell away on the southern side and he saw, for the first time in seven years, the town.

* * *

San Rafael del Agua had drowned in March, seven years ago, in a night of rain that the rivershed had never seen before and has not seen since. The dam upcountry — a private hydro scheme, badly built, badly inspected — had failed at 0300, and the flood had come down the valley faster than a train, faster than any warning, and taken the town in eleven minutes.

Lena had been teaching a night class at the mission school. Jude had been eighty miles away, surveying a cutoff loop, sleeping in a company bunkhouse with his boots on, and by the time he reached the valley the water had fallen and the town lay under a foot of silt, and there was nothing left to do but dig, and dig, and dig.

They had rebuilt on higher ground, two miles east. New San Rafael, tin roofs and concrete block, a town with no history and therefore no ghosts. The old town they had simply abandoned, as the company abandoned its lines: too expensive to recover, cheaper to write off. The church tower still stood, because the church had been built of stone by people who expected their work to outlast them. Everything else was roofless walls and doorways opening onto air, streets gone to grass, a piano frame rusting in what had been a parlor, and the flood line still visible on every wall like a bruise that refused to yellow.

Jude took the curve at Kilometer 15 doing fifteen kilometers an hour, which was slower than the grade required, and told himself it was for the culvert.

"There it is," Sef said over the radio, bright with the harmless curiosity of the young. "What is that? An old town? Hello, old town!"

"Don't stop," Jude said.

His voice came out wrong. Flat, and too fast, and Priya turned her head and looked at him with those river-gray eyes that missed nothing that floated past them.

"Culvert's at 16," she said. "You want to check it, right? You said three culverts."

"I'll check it at 16."

But at 16 the culvert was fine — clear, sound, its masonry dry despite the season — and there was no reason to stop, and he stopped anyway, because the alternative was to drive on and he found he could not make his hands do it. He set the brake and got out and walked to the edge of the embankment with a surveyor's pretense of inspection, notebook open, pencil moving, writing nothing.

Below him, maybe six hundred meters across the flooded fields, stood the school.

Its walls were standing. He hadn't known that. In seven years of avoidance he had never once looked at aerial photographs, never asked, never let himself wonder whether the roof had held long enough, whether the walls had stood, whether the room where she had stood teaching verb conjugations to farmers' daughters had still been a room when the water reached it. Now he knew: the walls stood. The blackboard would be gone — wood floats — but the chalk tray might still be bolted to the wall, and the bell rope might still hang in the tower of the building next door, and the whole scene lay there in the morning light, green and ruined and utterly indifferent, like a page from his own map rendered in three dimensions.

Seven years, and he had never let himself see it. He had drawn sixty-one closed lines on a piece of linen and ridden up and down them like a man pacing a cell he'd built himself, and he had told Odile it was expertise, and told himself it was discipline, and it was neither. It was fear. It was a man walking the long way around a house because he cannot bear to look through the window.

"Jude."

Priya stood beside him. He hadn't heard her come over the tailgate. She didn't look at the town. She looked at him, arms folded against the rail, waiting with the patience of someone who had spent years watching water rise and fall and had learned that most things resolve themselves if you stand still long enough.

"Culvert's sound," he said.

"Mm."

"We should move. Tide won't wait at Halloway Landing."

"Mm," Priya said again. Then: "Whose town is it?"

The pencil in his hand had snapped at some point. He looked down at the two halves of it and set them in his breast pocket, next to the folded photograph he carried and never looked at, and answered her the only way he could.

"Nobody's," he said. "It's a closed line."

She held his eyes for another moment — long enough for him to understand that she had received the lie, catalogued it, and decided, for reasons of her own, not to spend it — and then she swung back over the tailgate and called the convoy forward, and Jude Eriksen drove on through the town where his wife had died, keeping his eyes on the grade, on the ballast, on the gray stipple of the only map he trusted, and said nothing to anyone about anything at all.

Behind them the church tower sank slowly below the ridgeline, and ahead the river waited at Halloway Landing, and somewhere up that river, alive or dead, Tomas Almeida was keeping a silence that had already begun to feel, to Jude, less like absence than like accusation.

At Kilometer 22, Sef's voice crackled over the truck radio, excited and thin: the boy had been fiddling with the receiver he wasn't supposed to power up yet, and he'd caught something — a fragment, a carrier wave, gone almost as soon as it came.

"Could be nothing," Sef said. "Could be somebody's set. But Jude — it was keyed. Somebody's keying."
