## 4. Priya's Bargain

Priya Raghavan had learned to read men the way other people read weather — by the pressure of their silences, the direction of their small movements. Jude Eriksen was a low-pressure system: still, heavy, drawing everything toward him without meaning to. He stood at the rail of the *Provision* that first night in Cazadero, long after the others had gone below, studying a chart by lamplight as if the river might confess something if he stared hard enough.

She had been watching him for three days. That was her job. It had been her job for eleven months.

The trouble with watching someone that long was that you started to see them. And the trouble with seeing them was that it made what she had to do harder — or easier. She hadn't decided which yet.

Below decks, Odile Ferran's voice rumbled through the hull, arguing inventory with Sef, whose laughter came up through the floorboards like bubbles through water. The *Provision* rocked against her moorings, and somewhere across the harbor the lights of Vane's fleet burned in a neat row — six ships, white and orderly and patient, like teeth waiting in a jaw.

Priya climbed the ladder to the aft deck. Her boots were quiet; she'd spent years making them so. But Jude heard her anyway. She'd noticed that about him too — he didn't look up from his charts, but his shoulders shifted a half-inch, the way a man's do when he's cataloguing a sound and filing it away. An engineer's habit. Everything measured, everything kept.

"Your tide tables are wrong," she said.

He didn't look up. "They're the government's."

"The government hasn't run this reach since the flood took out the lower beacon. The channel migrated two hundred meters east after the second monsoon season." She came to stand beside him at the rail, close enough to see the pencil annotations crowding the margins of his chart — dense, precise, obsessive. A grief ledger dressed as cartography. "You'll ground on the mudflats at half-tide if you trust those numbers."

Now he looked at her. His eyes were the color of river slate, and just as hard to see the bottom of. "And how would you know?"

"Because I've run this river since I was fourteen years old, and because I drew the corrected survey myself." She let that sit. Let him do the arithmetic. She watched him arrive at it — the flicker behind his eyes, the slow straightening of his spine.

"You worked for Vane," he said.

"I worked for Vane."

"And now you're here."

"Now I'm here."

He closed the chart. Not angrily — deliberately, the way a man closes a door when he wants to hear what's on the other side of it better. "Odile hired you as a scout."

"Odile hired me because I'm the best scout on this water and my references are real. All of that is true." Priya turned to face the harbor, the black water, the white ships. "But there are things Odile doesn't know, and things you don't know, and one of us is going to have to start telling the truth before we're all six tides deep in each other's company."

Jude said nothing. He had a talent for nothing, she was beginning to understand. Most men filled silence like a breach in a hull — panic, noise, more panic. Eriksen just held it, let the water rise around it, waited to see if it would hold.

So she gave him something to hold.

"Marcus Vane didn't hire me to find your missing survey team," she said. "Finding them is the last thing he wants. He hired me to make sure nobody finds them."

* * *

The wind came off the water carrying the smell of diesel and rotting pilings, the particular perfume of Cazadero at night, and Priya talked into it because talking into wind was easier than talking into a man's eyes.

"I've done contract work for Vane Marine for six years. Channel scouting, route verification, sometimes salvage assessment. Good money, paid on time, which on this coast counts for nearly as much as honesty." She gripped the rail. The paint was blistered under her palms, warm from the day. "Eleven months ago he came to me with a different kind of contract. A survey team was going upriver — government-sponsored, mapping the drowned reaches above Karst Narrows. He wanted a scout embedded with any rescue effort, should one form. My instructions were simple. Slow them down. Mislead them. If they got close to the wreck site—" she shrugged, a small, ugly motion, "—make sure they arrived too late, or not at all."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed." No flinching from it. Flinching was a luxury of people with clean hands. "I told myself it was just another job. Rivers eat people all the time. What difference did it make who got the credit for looking?" She laughed, short and without humor. "That lasted four days. Then I found out who else was on that team."

"Tomas Almeida."

The name landed between them like dropped cargo. Jude's hand tightened on the rail until the knuckles went pale, and Priya saw — really saw, for the first time — the thing underneath the engineer's calm. Not ice. A dam. Something enormous held back by sheer structural discipline, and she was standing downstream of it.

"Lena's brother," he said quietly. Not a question. He said the name *Lena* the way some men say a prayer and other men say a wound.

"You knew?"

"Odile told me he was on the manifest. I've known for three days." He turned to look at her fully then, and the lamplight caught the lines of his face — exhaustion carved deep, grief carved deeper. "Why are you telling me this? You could have sat on your orders until we were past the narrows. By then it would be too late for anything but sabotage, and you'd be gone over the side with Vane's pay in your pocket."

It was a fair question, and it deserved more than the answer she'd rehearsed. Rehearsed answers were Vane's currency. She'd decided, somewhere between the harbor lights and this rail, to stop spending it.

"Because of Arun," she said.

She hadn't said her brother's name aloud in three years. It came out rough, like a stone pulled from a riverbed.

"Arun was my younger brother. Also a river man — better than me, if I'm honest, though I never told him so while he was alive to hear it. Six years ago he took a contract with Vane Marine, deep-channel work in the Serrano Delta. Standard job. Fourteen days, in and out." The wind gusted, and she let it push her words faster, before her nerve failed. "The storm came early. Arun radioed that they needed extraction — two boats, maybe three hours' run. Vane's dispatch logged the call. And then Vane made a decision. Pulling boats off a paying job cost money. The delta crew was expendable. He wrote them off. On paper, an act of God."

Jude had gone very still.

"Arun survived nine days on a shoal island with two other men. They ate mussels and drank rainwater off tarps. Arun was the one who kept the other two alive — he was good like that, always the one holding the rope." Her voice did something she hated, thinned and frayed, and she pressed on before it could break. "A fishing boat found them by accident. Two of the three were alive. Arun wasn't. Fever. Nine days, and rescue was a three-hour run away, and Marcus Vane chose his quarterly margins."

The silence that followed was the honest kind — no comfort in it, no hurry. Just two people standing in the same cold air.

"So when Vane handed me this contract," Priya said, "and I asked myself what difference it made who found those surveyors — I already knew the answer. I'd known it for three years. I just hadn't wanted to hear it." She turned to face him squarely. "I'm not asking you to forgive me for taking the job. I'm asking you to believe that I never intended to carry it out. I've been looking for a way out since the day I signed. Your expedition is the way out. Those people upstream — Tomas, whoever else is alive up there — they're my way of paying a debt I can't otherwise pay."

"To Arun."

"To Arun." She nodded slowly. "And to every crew Vane has ever written off and called it weather."

Jude looked back out at the harbor. The white ships sat in their row, riding lights steady, generators humming a note too low to hear and too constant to forget. Somewhere on the lead vessel, a door opened and closed — a small yellow rectangle of light, then darkness again.

"Vane will know you've turned," Jude said at last. "Men like him keep ledgers too."

"He already suspects. Why do you think I volunteered for the hardest posting?" She smiled thinly. "Best place to hide a defector is in plain sight, working twice as hard as anyone else. But yes. When this is over, he'll come for me. Which brings me to the bargain."

"Which is?"

"My knowledge of this river — every channel, every sandbar, every dead beacon and drowned landing stage from Cazadero to the headwaters — in exchange for your protection. When this ends, whatever it costs, I want it on record that I broke with Vane before the mission concluded. Testimony, if it comes to that. A name attached to the truth." She held out her hand, palm open, an old river gesture — *nothing concealed, nothing carried*. "You're the one with the government contract, Eriksen. Protection flows through you."

For a long moment he didn't take it. She watched him weigh her — the scout who'd signed Vane's paper, the sister who'd buried a brother, the stranger offering truth at midnight on a stranger's deck. She watched him decide that he couldn't verify any of it, and that he was going to proceed anyway, and she understood suddenly why Odile had fought the port authority for three days to get this man on her boat. Some people you trusted because they proved themselves. Others you trusted because of how they carried what couldn't be proven.

Jude took her hand. His grip was dry and strong and brief.

"Conditions," he said. "One: you tell Odile. Tonight or tomorrow morning, your choice, but she hears it from you, not from me. This is her boat and her contract, and she's earned more than my filtered version of the truth."

"Agreed."

"Two: if Vane contacts you — and he will — I hear about it within the hour. Every word, verbatim. I don't care how innocent it looks."

"Agreed."

"Three." He paused, and something moved behind the slate of his eyes — not softness exactly, but the place where softness used to be before it hardened into something load-bearing. "Three: you don't get to die for Arun. Whatever happens upriver, you don't trade yourself to settle the account. I've seen what that does to the people left holding the rope."

Priya's throat tightened. She looked away, out at the white ships, and was silent long enough that the moment nearly curdled.

"That's not a condition I can promise," she said finally. "But I'll try."

"It's the only condition that matters."

* * *

They told Odile at dawn, in the wheelhouse, with the fog pressing gray against the windows and Sef pretending very badly to be occupied with his radio set in the corner. Priya laid it out plainly — the contract, the sabotage orders, Arun, the defection. She put Vane's original instructions on the chart table, written in her own hand the night before, every clause of them, because a confession you could hold weighed more than one you could only hear.

Odile Ferran listened without moving. She was a broad woman built like a bollard, and she had a way of receiving bad news the way a hull receives water — letting it press, testing where it strained, deciding whether to seal it or sink with it. When Priya finished, the captain was quiet for the length of three swells against the pilings.

"You lied to my face at the hiring dock," Odile said.

"Yes."

"You'll be on my boat for six tides minimum, possibly more, possibly in conditions where one wrong word gets us all killed."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me now because Eriksen made you." It wasn't quite a question.

"Because it was time," Priya said. "Eriksen made it easier."

Odile looked at Jude, then back at Priya, and something in her granite face shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground forgiveness gets quarried from. "Here's how it's going to be. You scout, you navigate, you earn your berth every single day, and the first time I catch even the shadow of Vane's leash on you, I put you ashore wherever we happen to be, contract or no contract. Are we clear?"

"Clear."

"And Raghavan." Odile's voice dropped, and the fog seemed to lean closer to listen. "If your information saves my crew — if it gets us to those people before the monsoon does — then we'll talk about testimony, and protection, and what comes after. One day at a time. That's the only currency I deal in."

From the corner, Sef cleared his throat. He was nineteen, all elbows and earnestness, with headphones pushed up around his neck like a crown worn slightly wrong. "For what it's worth," he said, "the lead ship in Vane's fleet transmitted a position request at oh-three-hundred. Addressed to a relay station upriver." He swallowed. "They're asking about *us*, Captain. Somebody told them our departure window."

The wheelhouse went very still. Through the fogged glass, the white ships sat in their patient row, and Priya felt the old familiar chill walk up her spine — the certainty of being watched, of being already counted in someone's ledger.

Odile's jaw set. "Then we leave early," she said. "Sef, tell the harbormaster we're taking on fuel. Eriksen — I want your closed-line route on my chart within the hour. Raghavan—"

"Captain?"

A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile crossed Odile Ferran's face. "Earn it."

Outside, the fog was beginning to lift off the water, and beneath it, far upriver past the gorge and the narrows and the drowned towns, six tides of favorable water were already running out — and somewhere ahead of them, moving upstream on foot through country that wanted them dead, the people they were racing to save had no idea help was coming at all.
