## 5. First Tide

The tide turned at 04:12, and Odile Ferran was awake to meet it, as she had been awake to meet every tide of her working life.

She stood at the wheel of the *Provision* with her boots planted wide on the wet deck plating and watched the gorge unspool ahead of them in the gray half-light. The river ran high with the season's first meltwater from the interior ranges, brown-green and muscular, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks. Somewhere above the fog that clung to the cliff walls, the monsoon was still a rumor — a pressure she felt in her bad knee more than saw. But it was coming. Everything upriver was coming down to meet them.

"Two fathoms under the keel," Sef called from the sounder station, his voice cracking on the second word the way it always did when he was excited or frightened, which at seventeen were often the same thing. "Maybe two and a quarter."

"That's a river being generous," Odile said. "Thank it anyway."

She glanced aft. Jude Eriksen stood at the rail with his charts spread across the cargo hatch cover, weighted at the corners with engine parts, his pencil moving in small precise strokes. He hadn't slept — she'd seen the lamp burning in the wheelhouse all night while he cross-referenced his closed-line maps against the river survey, hunting for the channel the official charts had forgotten. Priya Raghavan sat nearby on a coil of rope, watching him work with an expression Odile couldn't read yet: something between assessment and appetite. The salvage scout was a closed book, and Odile had learned long ago that closed books were either the most valuable or the most dangerous things aboard a boat. Sometimes both.

The *Provision* was an old river freighter, forty years of service written into her hull like rings in a tree — patched, welded, re-welded, stubborn. She'd hauled timber, then ore, then nothing at all for six years until Odile bought her cheap and loved her back to life. She wasn't fast. She wasn't pretty. But she drew less water than anything else in Cazadero that could carry their tonnage, and this morning, that was going to matter.

"Captain." Priya's voice came low across the deck. "Fleet's moving."

Odile followed her chin toward the downstream haze. Out there, faint as a smudge on glass, three shapes were sliding along the far shore — Vane's lead ship and two barges, running dark, no lights, hugging the shallows where the current was kind. They'd loaded through the night. They weren't waiting for the tide to be good. They were waiting for it to be possible.

"They'll ground out on the Serpent's Teeth if they cut that corner," Odile said.

"Or they won't, and they'll have six hours on us."

Odile looked at the sky, then at the water, then at the man with the charts. "Eriksen. How's my channel?"

Jude didn't look up. "You asked me for a line through the gorge that doesn't exist anymore. I'm giving you one that used to. The old spur bed runs along the north wall — they graded it level when they built the railway, and the river's been filling it ever since. It's deeper than the charted channel by a meter and a half, but it narrows at the old trestle foundations. You'll have maybe four meters of width."

"Four meters," Odile repeated.

"Three and a half, if you count the pilings I can't confirm are gone."

She allowed herself a thin smile. Three and a half meters was a doorway, not a channel. But doorways were how you got into rooms, and rooms were where you found people. "Sef. Sound every ten seconds. Eriksen, you stand beside me and call the turns. If we touch bottom, you're explaining it to the river personally."

For the first time since Cazadero, something moved behind Jude Eriksen's eyes that wasn't arithmetic. It might have been the ghost of a smile. "Fair terms."

* * *

The gorge took two hours to give them what they needed.

It began well. The old rail bed held its depth the way Jude had promised, and the *Provision* slid along the north wall with the fog peeling off the cliffs around them like bandages coming away from a wound. Odile worked the throttle in small increments, feeling the boat answer, feeling the river's push under the hull — the tide flooding inland now, lifting them, carrying them toward the wreck site at a speed neither engine nor current alone could manage. This was what a good tide gave you: the river agreeing, briefly, to be an ally.

"Mark!" Sef called. "Bearing three-one-zero, dead ahead — piling!"

"I see it." Odile eased the wheel over. The black stump of an old trestle foundation slid past the port side close enough to read the grain of the wood, close enough that Priya reached out and touched it as they went by, some small superstition Odile chose not to comment on.

Then the river changed its mind.

It happened the way it always happened in the gorge — no warning, just a rearrangement. A side channel they'd counted on as slack water was running instead, pouring out of a slot canyon on the south bank, and where the two currents met, the water stood up in ridges and folded over on itself. The *Provision*'s bow swung fifteen degrees before Odile corrected, and the correction put them into the standing waves, and the standing waves put the starboard rail under green water for one long, unforgettable second.

"Hold her! Hold—" Sef was braced against the sounder, charts sliding, the coffee pot going over with a clang that would ring in Odile's memory for years.

"Eriksen! Where's my deep water?"

"Ahead and left — there should be an eddy behind the second point—"

"There *should* be?"

"It's on a map from 1911!"

"Then let's hope the river reads!" She brought the wheel hard over, felt the engine labor, felt the hull shudder as the propeller bit into confused water — and then, abruptly, they were through. The eddy was there, exactly where a dead cartographer had promised, a pocket of smooth water behind a gravel point, and the *Provision* settled into it like a horse finding its stride, and the gorge closed behind them without swallowing them.

Silence, except for the engine and the water and Sef laughing — actually laughing, high and disbelieving, both hands flat on the sounder table as if steadying himself against the world.

Odile realized her hands ached. She opened her fingers off the wheel one at a time.

"Nineteen eleven," she said. "I'm putting that man's name on the payroll."

* * *

They cleared the gorge mouth at 07:40, and the river opened in front of them like a hand unclenching — wide, braided, island-strewn, running between forested banks that rose toward country none of them had seen. The sun came up through the last of the fog and turned everything the color of hammered brass. Odile sent Sef below to sleep and took the wheel herself, because she didn't trust anyone else with it yet, and because the truth was she liked the feel of it, the boat alive under her hands, the tide still pushing them upstream.

Priya came and leaned on the rail beside her. For a while neither spoke. That was one thing Odile had decided to like about the scout: she didn't fill silence with noise.

"You've run rescue work before," Priya said finally. It wasn't quite a question.

"Recovery work." Odile kept her eyes on the channel. "There's a difference. Recovery, you're looking for a boat, or bodies, or cargo worth the insurance claim. You go in knowing the arithmetic already came out wrong. Rescue is different. Rescue means somebody might still be breathing, and that changes everything about how you move — how much risk you take, how much fuel you burn, how much sleep you skip. Rescue makes you stupid in ways recovery never does."

"And which is this?"

Odile thought about the telegram in her cabin. *Survey team, seven souls, silent eleven days.* Eleven days. She did the arithmetic she didn't want to do and arrived at the number she didn't want to arrive at.

"As of this morning," she said, "it's recovery. We go up, we find the site, we document, we bring back what can be brought back. That's the contract."

Priya nodded slowly, and Odile caught her looking aft — at Jude, who had finally lain down on the hatch cover itself, charts under his chest, asleep in the sun like a man who'd been shot rather than tired.

"And him? He signed on for recovery too?"

"He signed on for Tomas. Whatever Tomas turns out to be."

* * *

At 11:15, Sef came up the companionway two steps at a time, and Odile knew before he said a word. She'd spent enough years around radio operators to know the walk. It was the walk of a man carrying something fragile in his hands.

"Captain. I need you to hear something."

The set was in the wheelhouse, a battered marine unit Sef had rebuilt twice with salvaged parts. He'd tuned it off the emergency frequency onto a narrow slice of spectrum where, he explained, breathlessly, he'd been sweeping since dawn on the chance the missing team's handheld survived. Static filled the wheelhouse. Then, threaded through it:

"—anyone— copy— position—"

A woman's voice. Thin, distorted, riding the static like a bird riding wind. Then gone.

Sef's hands hovered over the dial. "That's the third fragment in twenty minutes. Same voice each time. I think — Captain, I think it's a handheld, low battery, and they're transmitting blind, not even sure anyone's listening."

"Play it again."

He did. The fragments assembled themselves across three passes like a picture emerging from dirty water: *—anyone— copy— ... —on foot— upstream— ... —two of us— ... —the others—*

Then nothing but the river's static.

Nobody spoke. Odile became aware of the crew gathered in the wheelhouse doorway — Priya, and behind her Jude, awake now, standing very still, his face doing something complicated.

"On foot," Priya said quietly. "Upstream. Away from the wreck site."

"People don't walk away from a wreck site unless the site is worse than the walking," Odile said. Her mind was already moving, laying the geography over the transmission like tracing paper. The signal was weak, which meant close — a handheld's range at low power was maybe eight kilometers over open water, less in these hills. The bearing, such as Sef could take one, put the source somewhere in the reed flats below Karst Narrows. Two days' run at their speed. One day, if the next tide cooperated.

"Eleven days," Jude said. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man holding something down. "They've been silent eleven days. If someone's been walking for eleven days—"

"Then they've been surviving for eleven days," Odile said. "Which means they have water, shelter, and a reason to keep moving instead of staying put. All three of those are decisions, Eriksen. Dead people don't make decisions."

She turned to the wheelhouse window and looked upriver, at the brass-colored water and the darkening line of hills beyond, where the weather was already organizing itself. Six tides. That was the budget. The tide beneath them now was the first, and it was spending itself under her hull whether she used it or not.

"Sef. Keep that frequency swept. Every fragment, log the bearing and the time. If the pattern holds, we can triangulate by tomorrow morning." She raised her voice. "Priya — get forward and clear the foredeck. Anything we might need to land a shore party goes up there, ready to move. Ropes, med kit, the spare water cans."

"And the contract?" Priya asked. There was no challenge in it; she genuinely wanted to know where the line was.

"The contract says locate and recover." Odile put her hands back on the wheel. "The contract also says *all reasonable efforts*, and I wrote that clause myself, so I get to interpret it. We are now making reasonable efforts to find whoever is still breathing. Everything else follows."

She felt the change come over the boat — not in the hull, but in the people on it. Recovery was a funeral procession; it moved at the speed of duty. Rescue was something else. She watched it happen: Priya already moving forward with purpose, Sef bent over his set with his headphones clamped on like a man defusing a bomb, and Jude Eriksen at the chart table, redrawing the route in strokes that had lost their caution — pushing the line closer to the narrows, shaving hours, taking water risks he'd have refused yesterday.

Hope, Odile thought. Cheapest fuel there is, and burns hottest.

"Captain." Jude's voice behind her. Low. "If it's Tomas walking out there—"

"Then we'll be there to meet him," Odile said. "And if it isn't — if it's one of his people, alone, eleven days in the reeds — then we'll be there for that too. Either way, Eriksen, we're not bringing back a report anymore. We're bringing back people. Get your charts ready for a landing."

Ahead of them, the river bent north into country no map of hers had ever bothered to name, and the tide carried them toward it at six knots, and somewhere in the reeds ahead, a dying radio was whispering to anyone who would listen.

Odile Ferran listened. And for the first time in years, she found herself hoping harder than she was calculating.
