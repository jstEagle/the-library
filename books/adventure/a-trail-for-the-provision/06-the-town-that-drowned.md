## 6. The Town That Drowned

The supply stop was Odile's idea, and she announced it the way she announced everything — as a fact already settled.

"Vallenport," she said, tapping the chart with two fingers. "Fuel drums, fresh water, and Sef needs a new aerial mast because somebody—" a glance at Priya, who smiled without apology, "—snapped ours on the gorge rocks. We tie up at dawn, we're loaded by noon, we run on the afternoon tide."

Jude stood at the rail of the *Provision* and watched the river bend toward a place he had spent seven years bending his life away from.

He could have told her. He should have told her. There were other landings — smaller, meaner, forty minutes further upriver — and he knew them all from his maps, knew their depths and their rotted pilings better than the men who'd built them. He opened his mouth to say so, and what came out instead was nothing, which was how most of his sentences about Vallenport ended.

"You've gone quiet," Odile observed, not looking up from her chart. "Quieter."

"Fine landing," he said. "Deep water off the old coal wharf."

It was true. It was also a lie by omission, and the omission had a name, and the name was carved into a stone in a cemetery on a hill that the water had never reached.

* * *

They came into Vallenport on a morning the color of pewter, mist lying on the river like a sheet drawn up to the chin. Jude took the wheel himself rather than explain why he didn't want to look, which meant he had to look, which was the kind of arithmetic his life kept doing to him.

The town rose out of the fog in pieces.

That was the thing no one had warned him about, seven years ago, when the flood came through and the government reports used words like *inundation zone* and *managed retreat*. A drowned town doesn't sink all at once. It drowns in layers, and it stays drowned in layers, and every year the river takes a little more and gives a little back. The church tower still stood above the waterline, its green copper spire tilted like a man leaning into wind. Below it, the roofs of the mercantile row broke the surface at low tide, black triangles furred with silt and gull droppings. The upper streets clung to the hillside, half-abandoned, half-occupied — a town that had lost its legs but refused to lie down, shuttered windows staring down at the water where their neighbors used to be.

"We're tying up where?" Priya asked, coming forward with her hair tied back and a coil of line over her shoulder.

"The coal wharf," Jude said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, in a hollow way.

The wharf itself had been rebuilt after the flood — new pilings, new planking, a fuel depot in a corrugated shed — but it stood on the bones of the old one, and beyond it the water lay flat and gray over the district where the warehouses had been. Jude brought the *Provision* in slow, feeling for the current the way he once felt for a rail joint through the soles of his boots, and when the lines went ashore and the engine died, the silence that followed was full of small sounds: water lapping at drowned eaves somewhere out in the mist, a dog barking on the hill, the creak of the gangplank taking Odile's weight.

"Sef, you're with me for the mast," Odile said. "Priya, water and provisions. Eriksen—"

"I'll see to the fuel," Jude said quickly. "I know the depot man."

Odile looked at him a moment longer than the sentence required. She had a way of looking at people that felt like reading a gauge — not judging the number, just noting it. "Fine," she said. "Back aboard by eleven. Tide won't wait and neither will I."

* * *

The fuel depot man was a boy of maybe nineteen who had been born after the flood and did not know Jude Eriksen's face, and this was such an enormous mercy that Jude nearly had to lean against the pump to take it in. Seven years ago everyone in Vallenport had known him — the engineer whose wife taught music at the school on the hill, the couple who'd stayed when the evacuation orders came soft-spoken and optional, the ones everyone whispered about afterward. He had spent seven years making sure no one would ever look at him with that particular expression again. The boy just wanted a signature and a smoke, and gave both directions cheerfully, and Jude walked away from the depot with his heart going too fast for the distance he'd covered.

He should have gone back to the boat. Eleven o'clock; it was barely nine. But his feet took him up the hill road instead, because there is a gravity to grief that operates independently of judgment, and the hill road climbed past the school.

The school was gone. Not drowned — the hill had saved it from the water — but condemned, wrapped in chain-link and warning signs, its windows boarded, its playground fence rusted into orange lace. Weeds came up through the asphalt of the yard. Someone had tied a faded ribbon to the gate, years ago by the look of it, sun-bleached to the color of weak tea.

Lena had taught in the music room on the second floor, the one with the tall windows facing the river. She used to say you could watch the weather come up the valley while the children butchered their scales, and she loved it, she loved all of it, the bad acoustics and the broken radiators and the kids who couldn't carry a tune in a bucket but sang anyway with their whole chests.

The flood had come on a Tuesday in the spring melt, faster than the models said, faster than the sirens. They found the school on Wednesday.

Jude stood at the gate until his hands stopped shaking, and then he turned around and Sef was standing behind him.

The boy had a new aerial mast coiled over his shoulder like a fishing rod and a paper bag of something greasy in one hand, and he looked at Jude with the unguarded directness that was either his worst habit or his best quality, depending on the day.

"This is your town," Sef said.

"It's not my town."

"You walked here like it's your town." He nodded past Jude at the school. "You knew exactly where you were going."

Jude looked back at the water below, at the roofs breaking the surface, at the church spire holding its tilt against the sky. Down at the wharf, tiny with distance, Priya was directing the loading of water casks with the crisp authority of someone born to tell other people where to stand.

"There's a bench," he heard himself say. "By the overlook. If we're going to have this conversation, I'd rather not have it standing up."

* * *

The overlook had been a picnic point once, before the flood — a cut in the hillside trees with a view straight down the valley of the river. The bench was still there, warped silver, one slat missing. From it you could see the whole drowned district laid out beneath the mist: the grid of streets visible as darker shadows under the water, the bridge stumps, the mercantile row, the school's lower field gone to reeds. On a clear day, Sef said later, fishermen swore you could see a car roof down there, and a church bell someone had never managed to salvage.

Jude sat. Sef sat beside him and opened his paper bag, which contained fried dough, and offered it. Jude shook his head. Sef ate in silence, which was a discipline Jude suspected the boy had learned specifically for moments like this, having none of it naturally.

"My wife died here," Jude said.

He hadn't planned to say it. He had planned, insofar as he planned anything anymore, to say nothing at all, forever. But the town had pried the lid off, and the words came up like water finding a crack.

"Her name was Lena. She taught music at the school up the road. When the flood came—" His jaw worked. "The warnings were ambiguous. The county didn't want to panic anyone over a maybe. She stayed with the last of her students' instruments. The pianos, some of the violins. Things that couldn't be replaced." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Things she thought couldn't be replaced."

Sef had stopped eating. He held the bag in both hands and looked out at the water, and he didn't say *I'm sorry*, and Jude was grateful for that beyond measure, because he had heard *I'm sorry* so many times it had worn smooth as river stone and meant almost nothing anymore.

"After," Jude said, "I couldn't drive through here. Any route that passed Vallenport — I wouldn't take it. There were three ways around, if you went far enough north or south. Added hours. Sometimes days." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked. "I'm an engineer. I map routes for a living. So I started mapping closed lines. Every decommissioned spur, every abandoned branch, every track the companies wrote off. Anything that let me build journeys that never touched this valley. Do you understand? I built myself a whole country out of dead railways, just so I would never have to see this water."

"And now they've put you on a river expedition," Sef said.

"Now they've put me on a river expedition." He shook his head slowly. "Tomas. Her brother. He's the one who's missing upriver, and I keep telling myself I came for the contract, for the obligation, for Odile's six-tide clock. But I think—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I think part of me signed on because the river runs through everything I've spent seven years routing around. You can't get to the wreck site without passing the places. And some cowardly part of me thought if I was working, if I was needed, I could go past them without stopping. Without looking."

"You're looking now," Sef said.

"Yes."

Sef was quiet for a while. Down in the drowned streets, the tide was beginning to turn; Jude could read it in the way the current combed the reeds, in the slow revelation of a rooftop that had been submerged an hour ago. Water giving things back and taking other things, always both at once.

"I was orphaned at six," Sef said.

Jude lowered his hands and looked at him.

"Fever, both parents, same winter. I don't remember much of them. What I remember is that for years afterward, whenever anyone mentioned them, I'd leave the room. Physically leave. Walk out of houses. My aunt learned not to say their names." He turned the paper bag over in his hands, folding and unfolding it. "I told myself it was because it hurt too much. And it did hurt. But that wasn't why I left. I left because grief asks something of you, and I wasn't ready to pay. Grief says: sit down, look at what you lost, let it change the shape of you. Avoidance says: keep moving, keep the shape you have, pretend the loss is a door you can close instead of a wall you live inside." He glanced sideways at Jude. "I was twelve before I figured out that avoidance isn't mourning. It's just mourning postponed, with interest."

"That's a hard thing to know at twelve."

"I had help. An old woman who ran the mission hostel where they put me. She said—" Sef's voice changed slightly, softened, took on another woman's cadence. "She said, *the boy who won't cry at the funeral will cry at something smaller, five years later, and won't know why.*" He shrugged. "She was right. I cried over a broken radio when I was seventeen. Cried like the fever winter had just happened. Because it had. It was just late."

Jude looked out at the drowned town. The mist was lifting off the water in rags, and the light came through gray-gold, and the church spire threw a long shadow across the surface that moved with the current, so that the shadow seemed to be swimming.

"I mapped closed lines," he said, "so I would never have to arrive anywhere that remembered her. And it worked. That's the obscene thing. It worked perfectly. Seven years, thousands of miles, and I never once had to feel it head-on." His voice dropped. "And so I never felt it at all. It's all still sitting in me, undelivered. Like freight that never got unloaded."

Sef nodded, slowly, the way he listened to a difficult transmission — repeating it back to himself until it resolved.

"So mourn," he said simply.

"Just like that."

"No. Not just like that. It's the hardest work there is, harder than any viaduct or any narrows." Sef balled up the empty bag. "But you start by stopping. By letting the route go where it goes. You're on the river now, Mr. Eriksen. The river doesn't do closed lines. It goes through everything eventually." He stood, brushing crumbs from his trousers, and slung the mast coil over his shoulder. "For what it's worth — I think that's why Odile keeps you. Not just the maps. Rivers need people who understand what it means that nothing stays buried."

He started down the path toward the wharf, then paused.

"She sounds like she was loud," Sef said. "Your wife. In the good way. The kind of person who sings badly on purpose."

Jude's throat closed. He managed a nod.

"Yeah," he got out. "Yeah, she was."

"Then she'd probably be furious about the detours," Sef said, not unkindly, and went on down the hill.

Jude stayed on the bench a while longer. The tide was fully turned now; the water was rising, sliding up the drowned streets inch by inch, covering the rooftops it had briefly returned. He understood, watching it, that the river had never been hiding the town. It had been keeping it. Holding it the way you hold something fragile — not concealed, just carried. And that somewhere in these seven years of closed lines he had confused carrying with burying, and buried the wrong thing: not his grief, but the man his grief belonged to.

When he finally stood, his legs were stiff and his eyes were raw, and he felt — not lighter, nothing so cheap as lighter — but *opened*. Cracked, like ice at thaw. Water getting in where it had always been shut out.

He was halfway down the hill path when he saw Priya running along the wharf toward the boat, waving both arms, and heard Odile's voice carrying flat across the water:

"—transmission fragment. Get Sef on the set *now*."

Jude ran.
