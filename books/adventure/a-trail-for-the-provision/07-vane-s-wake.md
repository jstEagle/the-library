## 7. Vane's Wake

Priya Raghavan saw the smoke before anyone else did, and she knew its shape the way a woman knows the footsteps of a man she once trusted.

It came off the southern horizon at midmorning, a smudge of black-grey against a sky already bruising with monsoon cloud, and it did not drift the way woodsmoke drifts. It stood. It held its column upright against the wind like a mast, like a spine, and Priya stood at the *Provision*'s stern rail with her hands folded on the rail's wet paint and watched it and thought: *coal-fired, twin stack, running heavy.* She thought: *that is Vane's lead ship, the* Corvus, *and she has been following us since the gorge.*

She did not say anything. Not yet.

Three days out from Cazadero, riding the second tide's slack water through a reach the charts called the Long Straight, the river ran wide and brown and patient, the banks a green blur of cane and half-drowned kapok trees. Sef was up in the radio shack nursing a cup of tea that had gone cold an hour ago, headphones clamped over his ears, hunting the frequency where the missing survey team had last whispered. Odile stood at the wheel with her forearms bare and her jaw set, steering by a feel for the current that Priya had come, grudgingly, to admire. Jude Eriksen was below with his maps, because Jude Eriksen was always below with his maps, folding and unfolding his grief ledger like a man counting money he never intended to spend.

And Priya stood at the stern and watched the smoke of the man who had left her brother on a sandbar in the Sunda Reach, and she felt the old arithmetic turn over in her chest — the same arithmetic that had brought her to Odile Ferran's deck with her confession and her bargain, the same arithmetic that kept her awake at night doing sums she could never balance.

*You owe him a debt,* she told herself, meaning Vane. *Debts are not paid by running from the debtor.*

The smoke held its column. The *Corvus* held its course. It was not trying to hide, and that was the thing that made the skin crawl along Priya's arms. A hunter who hides is uncertain. A hunter who shows himself has already done his arithmetic.

* * *

She waited until the noon meal, when the whole crew was crammed into the little cabin and the rain had begun — a fine, needling pre-monsoon drizzle that beaded on the windows and turned the river the color of gunmetal. She waited because she had learned, in six years of running Vane's errands, that bad news delivered on an empty stomach curdles into panic, and panic on a river is a drowning with extra steps.

"There's a ship behind us," she said, without preamble, tearing her flatbread with more force than the bread deserved. "Coal smoke, twin stack, been holding our wake since the gorge. She's the *Corvus*. She's Vane's flagship, and she's not in a hurry, which means she doesn't need to be."

The cabin went quiet the way a held breath is quiet.

"How far back?" Odile asked. She didn't look up from her plate. That was Odile — she took catastrophe the way she took weather, as a condition of the trade.

"Four hours at her pace. Two if she pushes. She won't push." Priya set the bread down. Her own stomach had closed like a fist. "Vane doesn't push. Vane lets other people do the pushing while he sits back and counts. He's letting us find the channel for him."

Jude's fork had stopped halfway to his mouth. He set it down with the deliberate care of a man handling something explosive. "You're certain it's him. Not another salvage outfit."

"I ran his river reconnaissance for four years, Eriksen. I know his ships the way you know your dead lines." She heard the edge come into her own voice and didn't bother to file it down. "That's the *Corvus*. And if the *Corvus* is here, then Vane already knows more than we do about what's upriver. He always does. That's how he works. He buys the information before anyone knows it exists."

Sef, folded into the corner with his headphones around his neck, looked younger than his nineteen years, which was saying something, because Sef always looked younger than his nineteen years. "So what do we do? Outrun her?"

"You don't outrun the *Corvus* on this river," Priya said. "She draws less than we do and burns better coal. You don't hide from her — Vane's paid half the pilots on this stretch to keep their eyes open. And you don't fight her." She looked at Odile. "You talk to her. That's what he'll want. He'll want a conversation, and he'll want it on his terms, and he'll want it before we're too far upriver for his launch to reach us comfortably."

Odile finally looked up. Her eyes were the grey of the river in rain, and they rested on Priya with a weight that Priya had spent three days learning to carry without flinching. The captain had not forgiven her — Priya doubted Odile Ferran forgave anything, she simply filed things in a ledger of her own — but she had not put Priya ashore either, and on this river that was its own kind of currency.

"And if he wants a conversation," Odile said, "we'll give him one. On our deck, not his."

* * *

The launch came at dusk, running lights doused, sliding out of the *Corvus*'s shadow like a splinter leaving a wound.

Priya heard it first — the particular low burble of a Vane launch engine, tuned and maintained the way Vane maintained everything, expensively and for appearances — and she was already at the rail when Odile rang the engine to half and the boat swung broadside to meet it. The drizzle had thickened into a proper rain, soft and constant, and the river at dusk was all iron and ink, and the launch came on with a single white lantern at its bow like an eye opening.

Four men in it. Three were muscle — Vane never traveled with less, a habit Priya had catalogued years ago and never understood until the day she understood it. The fourth sat in the stern under an umbrella one of the muscle held for him, and even at forty meters, even in the failing light, Priya knew the set of those shoulders, the way he sat slightly forward as if the world were something he was leaning in to inspect for flaws.

Marcus Vane had not come himself. Of course he had not. But he had sent his face, which was worse.

The launch came alongside with a bump of fenders and a shouted courtesy, and the man in the stern — Calloway, Vane's factor, his voice, his pen — stood and raised both hands, empty, theatrical. "Captain Ferran! Marcus Vane extends his compliments and requests a word. No more than that. A word."

"Words are free," Odile called back. "Come aboard and spend one."

Calloway came up the ladder with the ease of a man who had boarded a hundred boats he did not intend to remember. He was dry where everyone else was wet, pressed where everyone else was rumpled, and he surveyed the *Provision*'s deck — the coiled lines, the rusted winch, Sef's radio antenna lashed with wire, Jude standing apart by the wheelhouse with his arms crossed — with the mild, inventorying interest of a man pricing a house he intended to buy cheap.

Priya kept to the shadow of the deckhouse and watched. She had learned long ago that in Vane's orbit the most useful place to stand was the place where no one remembered to look.

"Captain." Calloway produced an envelope from inside his coat, dry, cream-colored, obscene in its cleanliness. "My employer is a reasonable man, and this river is about to become a very unreasonable place. The monsoon is eleven days out. Your boat is, with respect, a river tug with ambitions. Your crew is—" his gaze flicked to Sef, to the shadow where Priya stood, and something in it sharpened for half a second, something that told her he had counted heads and recognized one, "—colorful. Mr. Vane is prepared to buy out your contract. Your costs, tripled. Your crew paid through the season. And a bonus for the captain's discretion, which I am authorized to make substantial."

Odile took the envelope. She didn't open it. She held it by one corner, the way she might hold a dead fish, and looked at Calloway with the expression of a woman deciding whether something was worth the effort of throwing back.

"And what," she said, "does Mr. Vane imagine I'm under contract to do?"

"Recovery," Calloway said pleasantly. "A rescue, technically, though we all know what the word means when the government pays for it. A body count and a paperwork exercise. Sad business. My employer grieves it." He smiled with exactly half his mouth. "He'd grieve it more efficiently, though, if the river were left to professionals. There's salvage up there, Captain. Wreckage, cargo, material of considerable value. Mr. Vane holds the salvage rights — purchased, perfectly legally, from parties who had no idea what they were selling. Your expedition is, strictly speaking, trespassing on his claim."

"Rescuing living people isn't trespassing," Sef said, from the rail, his voice cracking on the last word.

Calloway turned to him with genuine, chilling kindness. "Is anyone up there living, son? Has anyone actually heard a voice? Or just static?" He let that sit. Rain ticked on the deckhouse roof. "That's what I thought. So it's salvage. And salvage has an owner."

Priya's hands had closed on the rail behind her, and the old paint bit into her palms, and she thought about her brother, Arun, who had waited eleven hours on a sandbar in the Sunda Reach because Vane's schedule mattered more than Vane's people, and how the river had taken his left leg below the knee and Vane had taken everything else, including, in the end, the settlement, eaten alive by lawyers and clauses. She thought: *he is doing it again. He is standing on the bank deciding in advance that the people upriver are already dead, because dead men are cheaper.*

Then Calloway said the thing.

"Of course," he said, turning back to Odile, unfolding a chart case with a flourish, "if Captain Ferran prefers to continue, Mr. Vane would want her to have the benefit of accurate information." He spread the chart on the deckhouse wall, weighted the corners with his fingers, and there it was — the upper reach, the gorge country, and marked on it in red grease-pencil, precise and unambiguous, a set of coordinates.

The wreck site. Not approximately. Not a search box. *The* coordinates, down to the tenth of a minute.

Priya felt the deck tilt under her, though the river was flat.

"Your engineer's charts are very fine," Calloway said, with a nod toward Jude that was almost respectful. "But Mr. Vane has known where the *Meridian* sits since before your telegram was printed in Cazadero. He's known for six years. And he knows what she carries — not the manifest nonsense the government filed, but the real cargo. The heavy cargo. The cargo that was never supposed to be on that boat at all." He began folding the chart away, unhurried. "That's the trouble with rivers, Captain. Everything floats downstream eventually. Mr. Vane simply prefers to be standing at the bottom when it does."

The silence that followed had weight. Priya watched Odile's face do something very small and very cold, and she watched Jude, apart by the wheelhouse, go entirely still — not the stillness of a man thinking, but the stillness of a man whose whole interior architecture has just shifted on its foundations.

*He knows the cargo,* Priya thought. *He's known for six years. He let them send a survey team up there anyway.*

And beneath the thought, colder, arriving like the rain arriving, inevitable: *and he knows I'm on this boat. Calloway counted heads. Calloway recognized me. Which means Vane knows his saboteur is sailing upriver with the competition — and he let me come.*

*Why would he let me come?*

The answer assembled itself in her chest with terrible patience: *because I was never the only card. Because he wants us to find the wreck. He wants us to find it, and reach it, and open it — so that his fleet can take it from us at the far end, with witnesses, with our hands on his property, with the government's own expedition holding his cargo for him.*

*We aren't racing him,* she realized. *We're his dredge.*

* * *

Calloway left the envelope on the chart table — "for when the rain gets worse, Captain; it always gets worse" — and went down the ladder into the dark, and the launch's lantern drew away downstream until the rain folded it shut.

Odile held the envelope over the rail, considered it for a long moment, and let it go. The river took it. It rode the current a few feet, turned over once, and sank, and Priya thought there was a whole sermon in that if anyone had wanted one.

"Coordinates," Odile said at last, quietly, to the dark water. "He's had coordinates for six years." She turned. "Raghavan. You ran his river work. What cargo rides a riverboat that a man would sit on for six years rather than raise?"

Priya came out of the shadow of the deckhouse. All of them were looking at her now — Odile, Sef, and Jude, whose eyes in the lamplight had the flat shine of a man reading a chart of his own past and finding a line on it he'd never drawn.

"Weight," she said. "That's what he said. *The heavy cargo.* Vane doesn't sit on treasure for six years — treasure you sell. You sit on something for six years when raising it is dangerous. When raising it answers questions." She looked at Jude. "Your brother-in-law's team was surveying that reach. Channel soundings. Riverbed work. What would a survey team find, Mr. Eriksen, sitting on top of a wreck that a salvage owner needed to stay buried?"

Jude didn't answer. He had turned toward the rail, toward the upstream dark where the gorge waited, and the rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it, and when he finally spoke his voice was low and strange.

"The *Meridian* went down in the flood year," he said. "Eleven people. The board called it weather." A pause, and then, almost to himself: "Tomas surveyed that reach the spring before. He'd have had the soundings. He'd have known what the channel looked like before she sank."

He turned from the rail and looked at Priya, and there was something in his face she had not seen there before — not grief, not the careful walled-off absence she'd been navigating for three days, but a live, burning attention, a man's whole mind coming up from the engine room at last.

"Get me your old Vane charts," he said. "All of them. Every reach, every sounding you ever ran for him." His hand closed on the rail hard enough that the old paint flaked. "Because if Vane's known where that wreck is for six years, then somewhere in those charts is the reason why. And I want it before the *Corvus* catches our wake."

Behind them, far downstream, the *Corvus* lit her running lights — two white, one green, patient as a held breath — and the rain came down harder, and Priya stood in it and understood that the race they had been running had never been a race at all.

It was a trap with a tide clock. And they were already inside it.
