## 8. Third Tide, Rising Water

The second tide failed at 04:17 in the morning, and Sef knew it before Odile did, because Sef was the one holding the depth sounder and the number on the dial had stopped being a number and become an insult.

*Four feet two. Four feet one. Four feet one.*

The *Maren Voss* drew four foot six with her tanks half-empty.

"Call it," Odile said from the wheel, her voice flat as slate. She already knew. She always knew first; she just liked to make the instruments confess it out loud.

Sef called it. "We're aground in forty minutes if we keep this heading."

"Then we don't keep this heading." Odile swung the wheel hard over, and the river — wide here, brown, swollen with the monsoon's first outriders of rain — slid beneath them like something patient and enormous that had all night to think about what it wanted to do to them. "Jude. Get up here."

Jude Eriksen came up the companionway with his charts rolled under one arm like a man carrying a sleeping child, careful, proprietary. He'd been sleeping in the chartroom again, which nobody commented on because everybody understood: the man slept better surrounded by lines he could read than by people he couldn't yet.

"Shoal," Odile said. "Dead ahead where my chart says channel."

Jude unrolled his paper across the wheel housing, weighted the corners with a coffee mug, a wrench, Sef's spare headphones, and Priya's knife, which she surrendered without being asked and without taking her eyes off the water. His finger found the reach — the long slow bend below Karst Narrows where the river braided into three threads and then pretended it had only ever been one.

"This is old rail bed," Jude said. "Before they converted the spur to a barge route, this whole bend was causeway. The river's been eating it back for thirty years. Your chart shows the channel as it was surveyed — when?"

Odile checked the plate. "Six years ago."

"Vane's company ran that survey," Priya said quietly, from the rail.

There was a silence on the wheelhouse deck that had weight, like cargo shifting. Six years ago. The same six years since the wreck. Since the wreck that Tomas had vanished rather than stay silent about. Sef watched Jude's face do something complicated and private — the look of a man finding a loose thread and realizing the whole coat might come apart if he pulled it.

"So the channel may not be where anyone says it is," Jude said finally. "Fine. Then we don't trust the paper. We trust the water."

"And how do we do that?" Odile asked.

"We read it like track." He tapped the surface of the river with two fingers, as though the brown water were ballast stone. "Ripples lie, but color doesn't. Deep water runs dark green going on black. Shoal water goes the color of milk tea. You can see it from the mast if the light's right."

"The light isn't right," Sef said. "It's four-thirty and raining."

"Then we wait for the light," Jude said, "and we lose the tide doing it."

They lost the tide doing it.

* * *

By full morning the *Maren Voss* sat on the shoal after all — not because Jude's reading failed but because the river moved faster than any reading. The falling water dropped eight inches in ninety minutes, and the boat settled onto the bar with a grinding shudder that Sef felt in his teeth through the deck plates, a long gravelly groan like the river clearing its throat.

Then she simply sat there, listing two degrees to port, while the current combed past her hull in long brown ropes and the rain came down in sheets fine enough to be mist and heavy enough to be mockery.

A day. They'd lost the second tide entirely, and the third tide wouldn't build until tomorrow's dawn, and somewhere behind them the running lights of the *Corvus* were burning their patient colors, and somewhere ahead of them a survey team was either alive or wasn't, and the monsoon didn't care about any of it.

Sef sat in the radio shack with the door open so he could hear the argument.

It started small. It usually did. Odile wanted to lighten ship — pump fuel, dump fresh water, strip everything not bolted down onto the bank and haul it overland past the bar, then winch the boat off on the next rise. Jude said the ground on the north bank was old fill, unstable, would swallow a loaded sledge. Priya said there was a game trail on the south side used by rubber tappers, firmer footing. Jude said the south side meant doubling every carry by three hundred meters through flooded scrub. Odile said she didn't care if they carried the boat itself on their backs, they were getting off this bar today or she'd know the reason why.

Then it got less small.

"—and maybe if somebody had told us six years ago that the charts were bought and paid for—" Odile, arms crossed, not looking at Priya but aimed at her, the way you aim a weapon you haven't decided to fire.

"I ran soundings. I didn't write the reports."

"You ran soundings for a company that buried a ship and everyone aboard it, and you're telling me you never once wondered why your numbers never matched what the dredges found?"

"I wondered." Priya's voice went very quiet, which Sef had learned was worse than loud. "I wondered and I kept running soundings, because my brother needed the wage, because—"

"Your brother." Odile's jaw worked. "Your brother who Vane left stranded upriver on the Kessel job. Who waited nine days on a rock shelf for a pickup that came on the tenth because Vane didn't want to pay the overtime on the launch. That brother."

"Don't." One word, and the rain seemed to lean away from it.

Sef stood in the doorway of the radio shack and felt the whole fragile architecture of the expedition tremble around its joints. Seven days ago these people had been strangers with a contract. Now they were something else, and the something else was exactly what made this hurt — you could only wound family, real or invented, because family was the only place you kept things soft enough to cut.

He should have said something. He was twenty-three and the youngest aboard and mostly they treated him as a piece of equipment that happened to have opinions. But he opened his mouth anyway—

And that was when Jude said, "Where's the spare battery bank?"

Everyone turned. Jude was standing at the hatch to the engine room hold, and his face had gone still in a way Sef recognized from the chartroom, from the viaduct calculations, from every moment the engineer stopped being a grieving man and became an instrument himself.

"The spares," Jude said. "Two hundred pounds of lead-acid in the aft hold. I stowed them myself, lashed to the port frame. They're gone."

* * *

The search took an hour and made everything worse.

They pulled the aft hold apart in the rain, passing crates hand to hand down the line, and the battery bank was simply not there. Neither was the emergency tool roll. Neither was the box of signal flares, which was the one that made Odile stop speaking altogether, which was the most frightening thing Sef had seen her do.

Flares. Batteries. Tools. Everything a person would need if they intended to disable a boat quietly and walk away from it.

Everything a person would need if they were still, in some part of themselves, working for Marcus Vane.

Nobody said it. Everybody thought it. Sef watched it move through them like weather — Odile's narrowed eyes tracking Priya at the far end of the line; Priya feeling the look without seeing it, her shoulders climbing toward her ears; Jude standing in the flooded hold with his hands on his hips, saying nothing, which from Jude was its own kind of verdict pending.

"I didn't take anything." Priya said it to the crates, to the rain, to no one. "I want that recorded. Sef. Record it."

So Sef did the only thing he was good for. He went into the shack, keyed the log, and said in his flattest operator's voice: *"Day seven, oh-nine-forty. Raghavan, P., states under dispute that she has removed no equipment from vessel stores. Witnessed by full crew."* And then, because he was twenty-three and angry on someone else's behalf and couldn't help it, he added: *"For the record, I've been watching her since Cazadero. She hasn't been off the boat except to scout, and I've logged every departure. There's no window."*

"Sef." Odile's voice through the shack door, warning.

"It's true," he said. "It's in the log. Logs are true. That's the entire point of logs."

Priya looked at him through the rain, and something in her face cracked open — not gratitude exactly, more like the expression of someone who has been holding a door shut against a flood and feels, for the first time, another pair of hands on it.

* * *

They found the equipment at dusk, and it was worse than theft.

Jude found it, because Jude found things — he had the kind of mind that kept an inventory of the world whether it wanted to or not. He was walking the port-side gunwale checking lashings against the current's pull when he stopped, crouched, and reached down into the gap between the hull plating and the fender where the river slopped through.

He came up with the tool roll first. Then, one at a time, the flares — sodden, ruined, their casings split by swelling. The batteries they never found at all; the river had them, or the mud did.

"Not stolen," Jude said. He laid the wreckage out on the deck where the lantern light could reach it. "Look at the straps. These are our own lashing points. The lashings rotted."

"That's polyester webbing," Odile said. "It doesn't rot in a week."

"It doesn't rot in a week, no." Jude turned the strap over. The weave was gray-brown, fuzzy, coming apart in wet strings. "But this isn't a week old. This is old stock — somebody sold Odile surplus webbing marked as new, and it's been composting since the day it came aboard. Add seven days of monsoon humidity and immersion, and it let go. Everything lashed on the port frame slid aft in the grounding shock, piled into the scupper gap, and washed out through the freeing port." He looked up. "The river robbed us. Not anyone on this boat."

Silence. The rain filled it.

Priya hadn't moved from where she stood at the cargo net. She was looking at the ruined flares the way you look at a verdict that acquits you too late to undo what the trial cost. Sef saw her hands, hanging open at her sides, and decided — again, deliberately, the way you decide to keep steering through fog — that he was going to say the thing.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I'm sorry. All of you. That's not — we're tired and scared and the river ate our stuff, and we turned on each other because turning on each other is easier than being afraid." He swallowed. "My parents died when I was eleven. Flood, actually, if you want the irony. And the thing I learned afterward — the thing nobody tells you — is that fear makes orphans of everybody. You start believing you're alone in the boat even when you're standing shoulder to shoulder." He gestured at the sodden flares, the frayed strap, the four exhausted faces turned toward him in the lamplight. "We're not alone in the boat. That's the whole point of the boat."

Nobody laughed. But Odile put her hand briefly on the back of his neck, rough and warm, the way you steady a thing you intend to keep. And Priya crossed the deck and picked up the ruined tool roll out of the puddle it lay in, and began, silently, to re-lash the port frame with new webbing from the good locker — and after a minute Jude knelt beside her and held the buckles, and after another minute Odile brought dry blankets, and that was the whole apology, conducted entirely in rope and canvas and the language of work, which was the only language all five of them spoke fluently.

* * *

Sef had the midnight watch, which meant he had the radio and the rain and the long dark hours to himself, which suited him. He ran the sweep frequencies out of habit more than hope — the missing team's set had been dead or drowned for days, and Mei Ochoa's name had begun to feel less like a person and more like a frequency he was failing to raise.

At 01:52, the third night watch of the grounding, the static changed.

Not a voice. Nothing so kind. A carrier wave, weak, drifting, the kind of signature a damaged transmitter makes when its battery is dying and its antenna is half-submerged — but keyed. Rhythmic. Deliberate. Three short, three long, three short, in a stuttering loop that faded and returned with the movement of the water.

Sofar. SOS. Somebody out there in the drowned dark was still spending their last battery on the oldest sentence in the world: *we are here, we are here, we are here.*

Sef's hands were shaking so badly he keyed the intercom twice before he got it right.

"Odile. Odile, wake up. Wake up — I've got a signal. It's faint, it's bearing somewhere northeast of us, up the reed channels — and Odile, it's manual keying. It's a person."

Above him, bare feet hit the deck plates. The boat groaned on her bar, the rain hammered the river flat, and downstream — far downstream, patient as ever — the lights of the *Corvus* burned on through the dark, closer now, much closer, and none of them had seen them yet.
