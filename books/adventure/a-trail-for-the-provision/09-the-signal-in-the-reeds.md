## 9. The Signal in the Reeds

The transmission came again at dawn, and this time it was not a fragment.

Jude stood in the wheelhouse with a tin cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking, watching Sef work the radio the way a surgeon works a wound — gently, patiently, one hand steady on the dial while the other held a pencil poised over a scrap of paper. The river outside was the color of pewter, running high and brown with the monsoon's first distant offerings, and the reed banks slid past in unbroken walls of green so dense they seemed less like vegetation than like architecture.

*—say again, say again — moving north along the east channel — cannot travel by water —*

The voice was a woman's, cracked and thin, as if it had been shouted through wet cloth. Then static swallowed it whole.

"That's three times now," Sef said without looking up. "Same bearing. Northeast quadrant, past the Split Rock marker." He tapped his pencil against the chart pinned to the bulkhead. "Whoever it is, they're not transmitting from a survey camp. There's no generator hum behind the voice. Battery set, probably hand-cranked."

"Which means they're moving," Odile said from the doorway. She had a way of arriving that made the wheelhouse feel smaller — not unpleasantly, but like a ship taking on ballast. Her eyes went to the chart, then to Jude. "Surveyor's discipline. Short transmissions, low power, changing position between sends. She doesn't want to be found."

"Or she doesn't want to be found by *everyone*," Priya said from the stern rail, where she'd been standing since before first light, her binoculars trained on the water behind them. "Vane's lead ship dropped off our wake two nights ago. That's not retreat. That's repositioning."

Jude set down the untouched coffee. "If she's on foot along the east channel, she'll be in the reed marshes above Split Rock by now. The old floodplain. It's all braided channels and deadfall — a boat can't follow her in there, but neither can anything else."

"So we go in after her," Odile said.

"On foot. Through four miles of marsh. With the river rising." Jude heard his own voice flatten into the register he used for reporting bad track conditions, and hated it. "Yes. We go in after her."

Odile looked at him for a long moment, and something passed across her face that wasn't quite a smile but lived in the same neighborhood. "You could have argued harder."

"I've spent seven years arguing with maps," Jude said. "I know when the ground has already given way."

* * *

They anchored the boat in the lee of Split Rock itself — a granite shoulder thrust out of the floodplain like the knuckle of some buried giant — and went ashore in the skiff: Jude, Odile, Priya, and Sef, leaving the deckhand Marta and the engineer Boaz to guard the vessel and keep steam up. The marsh took them immediately, greedily, the reeds closing over their heads like green water.

Jude led. It was not a decision anyone announced; it simply happened, the way water finds the lowest channel. He had walked this floodplain once, years ago, in another life, when the rail spur still ran its elevated causeway along the valley's eastern wall and engineers could survey the marsh from the safety of steel trestle. Now the trestle was gone — or rather, it was everywhere, collapsed into the reeds in rusted segments, and he navigated by it, reading the drowned line the way other men read stars.

"Left here," he said, stepping over a tie that had rotted to the consistency of bread. "The causeway bed runs dry-ish for another eight hundred meters. After that we're wading."

"You've been here before," Sef said. It wasn't a question. The boy picked his way along the embankment behind him with the light, quick steps of someone who had grown up climbing things he shouldn't have.

"I surveyed this line. Before it closed."

"Before the flood."

Jude didn't answer. Ahead, Priya moved like she'd been born in reeds, silent and low, her machete doing more listening than cutting. Odile brought up the rear with the shotgun broken open over her arm, and the five of them made a strange procession through the green cathedral — the grieving mapmaker, the captain, the defector, the orphan, all of them following a dead railroad toward a living voice.

The water rose around their ankles, then their knees. The heat was a physical weight, and the air smelled of rot and lotus and something mineral, like struck flint. Twice Jude stopped them while he consulted the map in his head — the real map, the private one, the ledger of closed lines he carried behind his eyes — and twice he corrected their course along channels that existed only as memory and silt.

It was Priya who heard it first.

She raised a fist, and they froze. For a long moment there was only the marsh breathing — wind in the reeds, the far complaint of some wading bird, water ticking against deadfall.

Then, faintly, from somewhere ahead and to the right: a cough. Human. Deliberate, almost — the cough of someone who has decided that being found is better than dying quietly.

"There." Priya pointed with the machete. "In the cut where the old culvert washed out. I saw movement."

They approached in a spread line, hands visible, Odile calling out in a clear carrying voice: "Expedition party! We're answering your transmission! We're coming to you!"

Silence. Then, from the reeds, a voice — the same cracked woman's voice from the radio, closer now, ragged with exhaustion:

"If you're Vane's people, I'm armed, and I've had six days to decide I don't care what happens to me."

"We're not Vane's people," Odile said. "We're the Cazadero expedition. Ferran's boat. We came upriver looking for the survey team."

Another silence, longer. Water dripped somewhere. A reed stem bent and sprang back.

"Prove it," the voice said. "The telegram that commissioned the rescue. What did it call the missing team?"

Odile glanced at Jude. He stepped forward, reeds parting around his thighs, and spoke toward the green wall. "It called us a salvage-and-recovery contract. Clause nine gave the government first claim on any cargo recovered. We tore that clause out before we left port."

A beat. Then a laugh — small, disbelieving, half a sob. "Tomas said you would. He said, *the engineer will tear out clause nine or he isn't who Lena said he was.*"

The reeds moved, and Mei Ochoa came out of them.

She was smaller than her voice, which had filled the marsh. She moved on one good leg, the other wrapped in a filthy bandage stiff with dried mud and older, darker stains, using a section of broken oar as a crutch. Her surveyor's jacket hung off her in strips; her face was sunburned, insect-bitten, hollowed. But her eyes were clear and hard and utterly awake, and they fixed on Jude with an intensity that made him feel surveyed himself — measured, plotted, entered into a record.

"You're Eriksen," she said.

"I am."

"Tomas talks about you like a man describing weather in a country he can never go back to." She swayed. Odile caught her elbow. "He also said you'd come. He bet his life on it, in a way. So did I, apparently."

Sef was already there with the medical kit, kneeling in the muck, cutting away the bandage with the same careful hands he used on the radio. The wound beneath was ugly — a gash from knee to mid-calf, infected at the edges, but not the death sentence it might have been. "How did this happen?" Sef asked, cleaning it with water from their canteens while Mei hissed through her teeth.

"The wreck. When the survey boat went down, a plate came loose. Tomas pulled me out of the water and onto the roof of the overturned hull, and we sat there for a day and a night because we couldn't swim for it — the current through the narrows would have taken us." She laughed again, that same broken sound. "Six of us on that roof, Engineer. Six. By morning there were three."

Nobody spoke. The marsh held the silence for them.

"And then?" Odile asked quietly.

"Then Tomas made a decision that I didn't understand until later. He sent Rennert and Cole downstream on the raft we built, told them to signal any ship, flag anyone down, get word to Cazadero." Her jaw tightened. "Rennert and Cole are probably in Vane's custody by now, if they're alive at all, because Vane's fleet was already in the river when we wrecked. Tomas knew that. That's why he kept me and kept quiet — and why he took the radio and threw it in the river."

Sef's head snapped up. "He *what?*"

"He sank it. Weighted it with a chain and dropped it in eight feet of water off the hull." Mei met the boy's stare without flinching. "I thought he'd gone mad. I screamed at him. We had a working radio, and he destroyed it. And then he explained, and I wished he hadn't."

* * *

They got her back to the skiff by early afternoon, carried in shifts on a litter of trestle planking and reed bundles, Mei refusing morphine until she had finished talking, which took the whole four miles. By the time they reached the boat, the story had been told twice — once in fragments during rest stops, once whole, lying in the wheelhouse with her leg splinted and Sef hovering — and it had rearranged everything.

Jude stood at the chart table and felt the mission turn under him like a tide.

"He didn't disappear," Mei said. "That's the thing none of you understand yet. Everyone thinks the survey team was lost. Tomas *made* them lost. He wrecked nothing — the wreck was real, the channel failed, the boat hit a submerged sill that shouldn't have been there — but when they survived it, he saw what it meant. He'd been sounding that reach for three days. He had his readings. And the readings didn't match the published charts. Not by inches. By fathoms."

Priya had gone very still by the stern windows. Jude watched her watch Mei, and understood that Priya had known some version of this already — had perhaps suspected it since the day she'd stood in Odile's cabin and offered her knowledge of the river in exchange for protection. Knowledge bought from Marcus Vane's own offices.

"The charts were wrong," Jude said slowly. "Deliberately wrong."

"Falsified. Years ago. The channel surveys for the whole upper reach — the ones that determine draft limits, cargo loads, insurance classifications — were doctored. Channels marked deep were shallow. Hazards were erased. And every barge company operating on that falsified data was running overloaded, uninsured, through water that would kill them." Mei's voice was flat with exhaustion, but underneath it ran something molten. "Including the barge that caused the original sinking. The one nobody talks about. The one whose owner paid for those false surveys in the first place."

"Vane," Priya said. Just the name. It landed in the wheelhouse like a dropped anchor.

"Vane," Mei agreed. "His company drew those charts twenty years ago, and his company has been running the river on its own lies ever since, and when Tomas's soundings proved it — when he realized the wreck they'd survived was caused by a hazard that Vane's own falsified surveys had erased — he understood exactly what would happen next. Vane's fleet was already upriver. Not to rescue anyone. To make sure the wreck stayed buried, the evidence stayed buried, and the witnesses—" she touched her bandaged leg, lightly, "—stayed buried with it."

"So he killed his own signal," Jude said. The words came out of him like ballast dropping. "Threw the radio in the river so Vane couldn't triangulate it. Sent the others downstream as decoys. Took the documents inland and vanished into the marsh, and let the world believe he was dead."

"He let *you* believe it." Mei's eyes found him again, and this time there was something gentler in them, though no less relentless. "He told me about you. About Lena. He said — and these are almost his words — *my sister married a man who measures grief in miles, and if I let him come looking for me, he'll come for the wrong reasons and get everyone killed. But if he learns I'm alive, if he learns what I'm carrying, he'll come anyway. Because underneath all those closed lines on his map, he's still the man who checks every weld twice.*"

Jude turned away and looked out the window at the rising river, because there was nowhere else to put his face.

Seven years of mapping closed lines. Seven years of routing his life around the town where Lena died, around the anniversary, around Tomas's silence after the funeral — the silence of a brother-in-law who had blamed him and never said so, and whom Jude had blamed in return without saying that either. And all of it, the whole private atlas of avoidance, had been built on the belief that Tomas had simply gone — drifted off after the wreck like sediment after a flood, unreachable, uncaring.

But Tomas hadn't gone. Tomas had *hidden*. Had chosen to be mourned rather than be hunted. Had thrown away his own lifeline so that Vane's ships would search empty water, and had gambled everything — Mei's leg, Rennert and Cole's lives, his own — on the chance that somewhere downstream an estranged engineer would tear clause nine out of a contract and come anyway.

"He's alive," Sef said softly, beside him. The boy's voice had a crack in it. "Tomas is actually alive."

"He's alive, and he's inland, and he's carrying the only copy of twenty years of forged surveys," Odile said. She had planted herself at the chart table with her arms crossed, and Jude recognized the posture: the captain calculating. "And Vane knows it. That's why his fleet turned back two nights ago. He's not chasing us anymore. He's sweeping the banks. He's hunting a wounded man through the floodplain with a monsoon coming, and if he finds those documents before we do—"

"He won't use lawyers," Priya finished. "Not upriver. Up here, evidence drowns. People drown. It's cleaner."

The wheelhouse was quiet except for the tick of the engine cooling and the slap of brown water against the hull. Outside, the light was going copper; the monsoon's leading edge was still days south, but the sky had begun to remember it.

Jude looked down at the chart, at the neat pencil marks of his own route planning, and felt the entire architecture of the journey rotate beneath his hands. They had come upriver to recover bodies. Then to rescue survivors. Now—

Now it was a race for a dead man's papers, guarded by a living ghost, against a fleet that owned the river's every lie.

"Mei," he said. "Do you know where he went?"

She shook her head, then paused. "No. Yes. Maybe." She pushed herself up on one elbow, wincing. "There's a place he talked about. An old mission station, up the Kessel branch — abandoned since before the flood. He surveyed it years ago, back when we worked the same crew. He said if anything ever went wrong, if he ever needed to be a ghost, that's where ghosts go. Stone walls. Dry floor. A well." Her mouth twisted. "He was joking then. He jokes when he's terrified. You'd know that, if you'd ever—"

"I know it," Jude said quietly. "I know how he jokes."

"Three days' walk from here," Priya said, already leaning over the chart, her finger tracing the Kessel branch. "Two, if the old logging cut is passable. But Vane's sweep boats will be working the main channel, and the Kessel mouth is—"

"—is overlooked," Jude said. "Because it's not on the charts anymore. The flood took the approach channel, and nobody bothered to resurvey a dead branch." He looked up, and found Odile watching him with that expression that wasn't quite a smile. "But I have a map of dead branches. I have a map of *everything* this river abandoned."

"Then we have a heading," Odile said.

Sef was already reaching for the transmitter, and Jude caught his wrist — gently, but he caught it.

"Not yet. One transmission, short, low power, and only after we're moving. If Vane has anyone listening—" he glanced at Mei, at her ruined leg, at the six days of survival written on her face, "—we do this the way Tomas would. Quietly. Like ghosts."

Outside, the first fat drops of a passing squall began ticking against the wheelhouse roof, and downriver, somewhere beyond the copper light, a fleet that owned the river's lies was searching for a truth it had buried twenty years ago.

Jude unrolled his private map across the chart table — the grief ledger, the atlas of closed lines — and for the first time in seven years, he drew on it a line that pointed toward something instead of away.
