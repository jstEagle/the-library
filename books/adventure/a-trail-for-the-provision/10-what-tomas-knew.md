## 10. What Tomas Knew

Mei Ochoa had learned to count her pain in tides. It was a habit the river taught her, and like everything the river taught, it came with a cost. The first tide after the wreck, she had counted her breaths instead, lying in the reeds with her leg bent wrong beneath her, watching the survey team's boat settle into the brown water with the slow, indifferent dignity of a thing that had never been meant to float. The second tide, she had counted the names of the missing. The third tide, she had stopped counting altogether, because Tomas had come for her in the dark with a salvaged tarp and a river of apologies, and after that there was only the counting that mattered: the hours until his signal, the hours until the rescue he promised was coming, the hours until she could stop being the only witness to what he knew.

Now she sat in the stern of the rescue boat with her leg splinted and propped on a coil of line, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of diesel and river mold, and five faces watched her with the particular hunger of people who had traveled six days through a dying season for one answer.

She understood the hunger. She had carried the same one for eleven days.

"Start anywhere," the captain said. Odile Ferran was her name — Mei had heard the crew say it in the low, careful way people say the names of the people holding their rope. "Start wherever it makes sense."

Mei looked at her hands. They were river hands now, cracked and stained, and they shook slightly, though whether from the injury or the cold or the eleven days she could not say. She thought about starting with the wreck itself, the groan of the *Provision* going down in the channel off Serrano Point, the water coming through the cargo deck like something with intent. But that was the wrong place to start. The right place to start was three years before the wreck, in a survey office in Cazadero, with a man named Tomas Eriksen and a stack of charts that should never have been printed.

"Tomas and I worked the same hydrographic section," she began. "Channel surveys. Soundings. The unglamorous work — measuring how deep the water is, where the bars have shifted, what the river has moved since the last chart was drawn. It's the least romantic job on the water and the most important one, because every pilot, every captain, every shipping line in the territory is navigating off what we write down. A channel survey isn't a document. It's a promise. It says: *the water is where we say it is.*"

She paused. Sef, the young radio operator, had gone very still, the way young people go still when they sense a story is going to cost something.

"Three years ago, Tomas was assigned to re-survey the lower reaches — the commercial runs, the deep-draft channels that Vane Maritime's barges use. Standard work. Except the re-survey didn't match the existing charts. Not by a little. By meters. Whole stretches of the Serrano channel that the official charts showed at nine meters were, in fact, five and a half. Sandbars where the charts showed clear water. The river had moved — rivers do — but the charts hadn't been updated, and the reason they hadn't been updated was that Vane Maritime's survey subcontractor had been *filing the old numbers.* Year after year. Copying the previous survey's figures, signing them as new. Because a channel that shows deep water can carry heavier barges. Heavier barges mean more cargo, more cargo means more contracts, and more contracts mean more money — right up until the water does what water does."

"The *Provision*," Jude Eriksen said. His voice was quiet. Mei had noticed already that this man spoke quietly the way other people shouted — as though the words were heavy and he was rationing them.

"The *Provision* went down in the Serrano channel with a full load," Mei said. "Official finding: uncharted shoal, sudden shift, tragic accident, act of the river. Eleven dead. The board paid out settlements with a clause that closed the case. And the channel survey that would have shown the shoal — the real one, the one Tomas took — was never filed. The falsified one was. The one that said nine meters."

The boat rocked gently against its anchor. Somewhere upstream, water was moving the way water moves before weather — with a low, gathering sound, like a crowd going quiet.

"Tomas flagged it internally first," Mei went on. "He was like that. Procedural. He believed in the system the way some men believe in a religion — not because it was perfect, but because it was the only thing standing between the river and the people on it. He filed a discrepancy report. It came back stamped *resolved* without a single correction made. He filed again. This time the report didn't come back at all, and his re-survey assignment was quietly reassigned, and a man from Vane's office came to Cazadero to have what was described as a friendly conversation about the tolerances of measurement. Friendly. The way a knife is friendly."

Priya Raghavan shifted at the rail. Mei had been told — carefully, in fragments, by Sef, who told everything the way water finds cracks — that Priya had worked for Vane once, and had left the way people leave burning buildings. Mei watched her now and saw her jaw set, and understood that everything she was about to say would land on this woman like a stone dropped into her own history.

"What did he do?" Odile asked.

"He started copying everything. The original soundings. The falsified filings, with the subcontractor's signatures — the same handwriting signing surveys taken years apart, in ink that didn't exist in the first year. Correspondence between Vane's channel office and the survey contractor. And the worst of it — the part that made Tomas stop sleeping — the internal traffic studies. Vane Maritime had *modeled* the risk. Years before the *Provision* sank. They had run the numbers on what a shallow channel would cost them in draft restrictions versus what falsified surveys would save them in cargo, and the numbers said falsify. There was a memo. Tomas had the memo. A man had calculated eleven deaths the way you calculate fuel costs, and signed his initials in the margin, and the initials were M.V."

The name went through the boat like a current. Mei watched Jude Eriksen's hands tighten on the rail, watched the captain's face go from weathered to carved.

"He took it to the maritime board," Mei said. "And that was when he learned what Tomas Eriksen learned too late, which is that the board's review panel had a Vane appointee on it, and that an evidence submission could be *misplaced* the same way a survey could be falsified. He got a warning instead of a hearing. Not a threat, exactly. A warning. The kind that arrives as concern for his career, his family, his future prospects. And then —" Mei stopped. She looked at Jude. "Then Lena died."

The name fell into the middle of the boat and no one picked it up. Jude Eriksen did not move, but something in him moved; Mei had spent eleven days learning to read the small motions of people in extremis, and she saw it — the way a man goes still not from calm but from the effort of holding a door shut against a flood.

"I didn't know him then, not well," she said, gentler now. "I knew what the office said. That his sister-in-law had drowned in the flood at Marisol Bend, and that Tomas had gone to the funeral and come back a different man. Colder. Faster. He stopped filing reports and started building a case. He said something to me once — I've kept it eleven days in the reeds, so I'll say it exactly. He said: *They counted on grief to bury this. Grief is the cheapest cover a company ever bought. Everyone who might have pushed is too busy mourning to read a document.* And then he said, *I'm done mourning on their schedule.*"

Sef made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"The wreck of the *Provision* — this wreck, the one we came upriver for — that was the second one. A survey vessel, Vane-chartered, supposedly mapping the upper reaches for a new barge route. But Tomas found the charter papers, and the route it was mapping wasn't a route Vane needed. It was the route *over the old wreck site* — over the Serrano channel, over the *Provision's* grave. Because the river was shifting again, and the hull of the *Provision*, with its cargo manifest and its falsified draft marks, was going to surface into shallower water where anyone with a sounding line could find it and ask why a ship that deep had been told the water was deeper. Vane's survey vessel wasn't surveying. It was locating his own crime so he could bury it properly — salvage rights, demolition permits, a 'hazard to navigation' destroyed with official blessing. The river was going to eat the evidence for him, and he was going to pay himself for the meal."

"And the survey team," Odile said slowly. "His own team. The one that went silent."

"Was him," Mei said. "He assembled it. He picked the crew — people he trusted, or thought he trusted. The mission wasn't a survey. It was retrieval. The evidence had been hidden aboard the first vessel, in the chart room, sealed against water, because Tomas believed no office was safe — every filing cabinet in the territory could be reached by Vane's money. The only vault he trusted was the one place Vane's falsified charts said was too deep to reach. He hid the truth inside the lie. The wreck was the safe."

She let that settle. The river made its low gathering sound again, closer now.

"Then Vane found out he knew. We don't know how — a leak, an informant, a man in the office who watched Tomas copy too much, too fast. The survey team was declared overdue, the rescue was announced, and Vane's salvage fleet was dispatched with remarkable speed for a company that had spent three years unable to find a channel shoal it had charted itself. They weren't coming to rescue anyone. They were coming to finish the demolition and drown the last witness. And Tomas understood that the moment the fleet's departure was announced — understood it faster than the government did. So he did the only thing left. He sent the team's radio into the river, cut his own signal, and vanished inland with the documents, leaving one survivor — me — injured and alive as proof that there had been a crew at all, and a trail of fragmentary transmissions he trusted the river to deliver to whoever was stubborn enough to come looking."

"Why fragmentary?" Sef asked. His voice cracked on the second word. "Why not a full message? Coordinates, everything?"

"Because Vane was listening," Mei said. "Every band, every frequency. A full transmission would have brought the salvage fleet down on him in a day. Fragments were all he could risk — enough to be found by someone reading them carefully, not enough to be found by someone listening greedily." She looked at Sef, and then at the captain. "He told me: *The right people will come slow. The wrong people will come fast. I'd rather be found slow.*"

The boat creaked. Mei became aware that she had been gripping the blanket, and made herself let go, finger by finger.

"So now you know what I know," she said. "The wreck wasn't an accident. The first one or the second one. Eleven people died in the Serrano channel because a man decided the water was deeper on paper than it was in the world, and now he's half a day ahead of us with a fleet, waiting for a monsoon to do what his lawyers couldn't — close the case permanently. Tomas is up there in the ruins with a bag of paper that's worth more than every ship Vane owns, and the only thing standing between that paper and the bottom of the river is us."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The water talked instead, slapping the hull, patient, insistent.

It was Jude Eriksen who finally broke the silence, and he did not speak loudly, but the boat was built for quiet men and it carried him to every corner.

"The contract says we recover the survey team and secure the site," he said. "It says nothing about documents. Legally —" he stopped, and Mei watched him wrestle with something, watched the engineer in him try to calculate a load-bearing answer, "— legally, the clean thing, the contracted thing, is to find Tomas, take him and Mei into protective custody, and let the board sort out the paper."

"And?" Odile said.

"And Vane has an appointee on the board," Jude said. "And Vane's fleet is between us and Cazadero. And paper that goes into the wrong hands stops being evidence and starts being ash." He looked at Mei, and his eyes were the eyes of a man reading a chart of his own life and finding the soundings didn't match what he'd always believed. "The contract is a survey. What Tomas has is a channel. They're not the same thing, and we can't run both."

"We run the truth," Priya said, and her voice had an edge Mei recognized — the edge of a woman who had once been on the other side of a knife like this one and knew exactly how it cut. "Or we run the contract, and in ten years there's another *Provision*, and another eleven names, and another falsified chart, and some other family gets to learn the river lies because a man with initials signed a memo."

Odile looked downriver, toward the narrows, toward the storm that was coming the way all certain things come — slowly and then all at once.

"Then we need to move," she said. "Because I've just decided what we're contracted to recover."

Mei leaned her head back against the gunwale and closed her eyes, and for the first time in eleven days the counting stopped — because ahead of them, upstream, in a ruined mission station, a gaunt man with a bag of paper was watching the water and waiting for the slow people to arrive.

And behind them, she could hear it now, faint under the river's voice: engines. The fast people had found the fragments too.

* * *

They made the upstream anchorage by last light, and Mei, propped against the wheelhouse with her splinted leg and a borrowed coat, watched Jude Eriksen stand at the bow with his charts, and understood something about him that the others had perhaps already learned: that he was a man who had been navigating by closed lines for seven years, and that somewhere between the reeds and this anchorage, the river had begun, quietly and without his permission, to redraw his map.

The storm was a bruise on the southern horizon.

The tide would turn in six hours.

Mei counted it anyway. Some habits, she thought, are how you stay alive long enough to tell the truth.
