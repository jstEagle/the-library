## 11. Fourth Tide

Odile Ferran had run the Karst Narrows eleven times in her life, and she had a superstition about the number. Eleven times, and every one of them on a slack tide, at dawn, with the water lying flat as poured glass between those limestone walls. That was how you did it. That was how you stayed alive doing it.

She stood at the wheelhouse window of the *Provision* with her hands wrapped around a mug of coffee gone cold an hour ago, and she looked south, where the storm sat on the horizon like something patient. Something that knew it would win eventually and saw no reason to hurry.

Behind her, in the chartroom, Jude Eriksen was arguing with arithmetic.

She could hear him through the door — not the words, just the rhythm of it, the low mutter of a man working numbers he didn't like. Sef was in there too, cross-legged on the deck plates with the radio log spread around them like fallen leaves, reading out figures from the tide tables while Jude scratched corrections into his charts with a pencil worn down to a stub. Priya was up on the foredeck coiling line in the dark by feel, because she said lamplight made her eyes ache, and Odile suspected that was only half true — the other half was that Priya Raghavan did her best thinking with her hands busy.

Three tides left. She turned the phrase over the way you'd turn a stone over looking for what lived underneath it.

The wreck site lay two reaches upstream. The survey team — or what remained of it — lay beyond that, somewhere inland where Tomas Eriksen had burrowed in with his stolen documents like a fox in a culvert. To get there they needed water over the shoals at Marrow Bend, which meant a rising tide. To get back out with Mei's injured leg and whatever state Tomas was in, they needed water under them again, which meant another rise. Two tides to go up, two to come down, one held in reserve for the loading, the digging-out, the thousand small delays the river always collected like tolls.

That left three. It should have been enough. On paper it was enough.

But the monsoon had come north a day early, and Vane's fleet was half a day ahead of them, and the Narrows —

The Narrows were the problem.

* * *

She called them together at midnight, all four of them crowded into the chartroom that had been built for two, the lamp swinging gently overhead with the boat's breathing. The river slapped against the hull in a rhythm Odile had known so long she heard it in her sleep. Outside, the anchorage lights of the last trading post before the gorge glimmered on black water, and somewhere upstream, invisible, the Karst Narrows waited with their walls of white limestone going up two hundred feet on either side, and the flood channel running through them like a throat.

"Here's where we are," she said, and flattened her palm on the chart. "Vane's lead ship passed the lower beacon six hours ago. He'll make the Narrows on tomorrow morning's slack and be through before noon. We can't match that — not without running water we don't have." She looked up. "If we wait for our scheduled slack tide, we come through twelve hours behind him. By then he's got the site, he's got whatever's left to salvage, and if Tomas is still alive up there, Vane finds him first."

Nobody spoke. The lamp swung. Sef's knee was bouncing, a quick nervous percussion against the deck plate, and Odile noticed that Jude put one hand flat on the boy's shoulder without seeming to know he'd done it, and the bouncing stopped.

"So," Odile said. "We don't wait for the slack."

Priya's hands went still on the coil of rope she'd carried in with her, some habit of hers, always mending something. "You're talking about running it on the flood."

"I'm talking about running it on the flood."

"The flood isn't a tide, Captain. It's a war." Priya set the rope down. "I've seen the Narrows on a flood tide from the ridge above. The channel runs nine knots through the pinch. There are eddies down there the size of this boat, and whirlpools that will take a skiff under and give back nothing but kindling. No pilot runs it on the flood. Not the best ones. Not the ones who are still alive."

"I know what it does," Odile said. "I've watched it take a barge apart. I was standing on the bank when it happened, and I remember every piece." She let that sit a moment. "And I'm telling you it can be done. Not by fighting the current — nobody fights that current. You go through it the way the old loggers did, on a line. You anchor yourself upstream and you let yourself down through the gorge hand over hand, engine barely ticking, steering against the cable instead of the water. The current does the work. You just have to keep the bow pointed true while it does it."

"With what anchor?" Priya said. "There's nothing on either wall. I've scouted both banks. Rock face, slick as ice, no trees within fifty feet of the rim."

"There's a viaduct."

Everyone looked at Jude. He hadn't spoken since they'd gathered; he'd been sitting at the chart table with his pencil moving, and now he turned his chart around and pushed it into the middle of the table so the lamp fell across it.

"The Karst Line," he said. His voice was quiet the way deep water is quiet. "Passenger and ore service, abandoned thirty-one years ago when the gorge road came through. They never pulled the infrastructure out — too expensive, too remote. There's a viaduct crossing the upper entrance to the Narrows. Span's about ninety meters. Stone piers on both abutments, steel truss between them." He tapped the chart where a thin ruled line crossed the river's throat like a stitch holding a wound closed. "It's rusted, but stone doesn't rot. Those piers were built to carry loaded ore trains. They'll carry a cable."

Sef leaned over the chart, squinting. "You want to tie us off to a bridge?"

"A viaduct. And yes." Jude looked up at Odile, and she saw something in his face she'd been watching grow for days now, something she hadn't seen in him since Cazadero — a kind of forward lean, as though the man had spent seven years walking backward and had finally, without noticing, turned around. "The piers are sound. The question is the cable itself, the rigging, and whether we can get a line across ninety meters of open air in flood conditions. And whether the anchor point on our end holds. And whether—" He stopped himself. Odile watched him do the thing he did, the thing she'd learned to read: he ran the failure cases, all of them, and chose which ones to say aloud.

"Say it," she told him. "All of it. I don't want the version where you've decided for me what I can bear to hear."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or gratitude. "Whether the viaduct's still standing at all," he said. "My maps are thirty years old. If a span has come down, it might have taken the piers with it, or blocked the channel, or changed the hydraulics entirely. I won't know until we see it. And if we commit to the approach and it's gone, we won't have the water left to turn around and take the scheduled route. We'll have burned our margin."

"How much margin?" Odile asked.

"If the viaduct stands? Enough. Barely." He said it without flinching, and she respected him for it. "If it doesn't stand, we're not having this conversation, because we'll already be dead."

The lamp swung. Out on the dark water, something splashed — a fish, a log, the river practicing.

"Well," Priya said at last, dry as driftwood. "When you put it that way."

"It's not a vote," Odile said. "I'm the captain. But I'm not a fool, and I'm not Vane — I don't spend people like cargo. So I'll say my piece and then each of you says yours, and if any one of you tells me it can't be done, we take the long way and we live with being late." She straightened. "Here's mine. Vane ahead of us means Tomas dead or caged, means the truth drowned for good, means every name in those documents stays a number in a ledger nobody audits. I've buried enough people the river killed twice — once in the water and once in the paperwork. I am not willing to do it again. That's my piece."

Sef went first after that, which surprised her; the boy usually needed drawing out. "Mei said Tomas sent the radio into the river on purpose," Sef said. "He chose to disappear so the evidence would survive. He's been alone up there for weeks, eating God knows what, waiting for someone to come, and the only someones coming are us or Vane." The boy's jaw set. "I say we go. I say if we're slow, we should've been faster, but we shouldn't be slower than we can help."

"Priya?"

Priya was looking at the chart, at the thin line of the viaduct, and Odile saw her lips move slightly, running her own calculations — river math, the math of currents and margins that had kept her alive through more water than any of them. "The eddy below the east pier," she said finally. "If it's still there, it's a pocket of slack water right under the span. We could hold station in it while the line goes across. I can find it blind if I have to." She looked up. "I say go. And I say I want to be the one in the bow when we do."

"That leaves you, Eriksen."

Jude was quiet a moment. When he spoke, he wasn't looking at the chart anymore. He was looking at his own hands, at the pencil stub between his fingers.

"My sister-in-law used to tell me I loved the railway more than I loved anything with a pulse," he said. "She wasn't wrong, most years. Structures made sense to me. People didn't. A truss either carries its load or it doesn't, and it never lies to you about why." He lifted his head. "This viaduct was built by men who never expected anyone to thank them, on a line that's been dead longer than Sef's been alive, and if I've read my maps right it's been waiting thirty years for exactly one job. I say we go and give it that job."

Odile nodded slowly, and felt the decision settle into her the way ballast settles — heavy, but steadying.

"Then here's how it goes," she said. "We weigh anchor at first light. We reach the viaduct by mid-morning, and Eriksen and Priya go up and assess it while Sef and I hold station below. If the piers stand, we rig. Highline across the span, anchored to the east pier, fairlead through a block on the west. We come through on the flood, on the cable, bow-first, engine at bare steerage way. One pass. No second attempts — if we lose the line mid-passage, we're committed to the current and we steer for the lower pool and pray." She looked around the little room at the four faces lit yellow by the swinging lamp. "Questions?"

"Yeah," Sef said. "What do we do if it works?"

Odile almost smiled. "Then we do it again on the way home, in reverse, with a passenger and a bag full of documents, and less time. Get some sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we ask a dead railway to carry us through hell."

* * *

They weighed anchor at first light into a sky the color of pewter, and the river had already begun to change its mind about them. Odile felt it through the wheel before she saw it anywhere — a new restlessness in the helm, small crosscurrents worrying the rudder like dogs at a heel. The monsoon's leading edge was still a day south, but rivers hear weather coming before weather arrives, the way animals do, and the Karst watershed was already draining its nerves into the channel.

By mid-morning the walls rose on either side of them, white limestone streaked with black runnels of rain-rot, and the sound changed. Odile had been hearing the Narrows before she could see them — a low sustained roar ahead, the voice of a channel being squeezed. She brought the *Provision* around the last bend at bare way, and there it was.

The viaduct stood.

Ninety meters of rusted steel truss strung between two stone piers, high above the flooded channel, its deck empty, its rail ends trailing weeds into the air. One of the cross-braces had sagged, hung down like a broken arm, but the piers rose out of the floodwater square and unyielding, wearing thirty years of high-water stains like tide marks on a giant's legs. Below them, the channel ran brown and furious through the pinch, and even from a quarter mile upstream Odile could see the eddies turning, great slow wheels of water revolving in the lee of the rock.

"Sweet suffering," Sef breathed beside her. Then, louder, toward the bow: "It's real! It's actually there!"

"Of course it's there," Jude said. He was at the bow rail with his field glasses up, and his voice had gone into that other register, the engineer's register, calm and precise as a level bubble. "Pier caps look intact. East abutment shows spalling but the bearing surface is sound. I need to get up there." He lowered the glasses and looked back at Odile, and for a moment the captain and the engineer regarded each other across the length of the boat, across everything that had and hadn't been said between them since Cazadero. "Captain. Your boat, your call. But I'm telling you it will carry."

"Then go carry it," Odile said.

They put Priya and Jude ashore in the skiff below the east pier, in the eddy Priya had promised — a pocket of green water turning lazy in the shadow of the stone, big enough to hold the skiff while they climbed the old maintenance stair cut into the rock face, iron runts rusted to lace but holding. Odile watched them ascend until they were specks against the limestone, then turned the *Provision* to hold station in the lower pool, engine ticking over, and waited.

It took them two hours. From the wheelhouse she could see them moving along the viaduct deck, tiny figures against the sky, and she could imagine the work — Jude walking the truss with a hammer, sounding every member the way a doctor sounds a chest, listening for the dull note of rot inside the rust; Priya hauling their light line out along the span, testing, cursing, laughing once at something Odile couldn't hear over the roar of the channel. Twice the wind gusted hard off the southern sky and Odile's stomach clenched watching those specks sway, and twice they held.

At the end of the second hour, the radio crackled. Sef had rigged a portable set to talk to the viaduct, and Jude's voice came through tinny and wind-torn:

"*Provision*, Eriksen. Cable's across. Sixteen-millimeter wire, anchored through the original tie-down eyes in the east pier cap — the stonework took it without a whisper. Fairlead block is hung on the west abutment. Tension reads true." A pause, wind filling it. "The viaduct holds. Tell your boat it can stop being afraid of bridges."

Sef laughed out loud, a bright startled sound, and keyed the mic: "Copy that. See you on the water."

Odile stood at the wheel and looked down the throat of the Narrows, at the flood tide beginning to build, the channel whitening, the whole gorge gathering itself for the hour of its fury. Half a day behind Vane. Three tides left, and one of them already spent buying this passage. Everything she had, and everyone she had, riding on ninety meters of dead men's steel and a cable thrown across the gap by a man who mapped closed lines because he couldn't bear the open ones.

"Eriksen," she said into the mic. "Get down off my bridge and get aboard. We're going through."

She rang the engine to slow ahead, and the *Provision* turned her bow toward the roaring water, toward the cable stretched taut and gleaming across the flood, toward the impossible thing that was, as of this morning, simply the plan.
