## 12. The Viaduct

The Karst Narrows announced themselves an hour before they arrived, the way grief does — a change in the light, a tightening in the chest, the water going from brown to a strange, luminous green where the river squeezed between walls of limestone that rose like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Jude stood at the bow of the *Provision* with his map case open against the wind and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin: distance to the viaduct, height of the water, rate of rise. Numbers he could hold. Numbers that had never once held him back.

"Talk to me," Odile said from the wheel. She had her cap turned backward and both hands white-knuckled on the spokes, though the channel was still wide enough here to drive a locomotive through.

"The viaduct is four hundred meters upstream, on the eastern wall." He traced the line with one finger. "Built in '31 for the Cazadero–Verapaz line. Abandoned when the flood took the western abutment. The piers are still standing — I've seen photographs from the last survey flight, eleven years ago."

"Eleven years." Priya came up beside him, coiling rope with quick, economical hands. "Eleven-year-old stone in rising water. You want to hang our lives off that?"

"I want to hang our *boat* off it," Jude said. "Our lives are optional extras."

Sef laughed from the radio shack doorway, then caught himself and didn't.

The plan was simple in the way all desperate plans are simple. The Narrows ran too fast, too deep, too choked with whirlpools for the *Provision* to power through against the current — not with the third tide already failing behind them and the river climbing eight centimeters an hour. But the old viaduct's piers marched across the gorge like the vertebrae of a fossil animal, and if they could run a cable from the eastern anchor point to the western abutment — a span of nearly two hundred meters over open water — they could winch the boat across hand over hand, above the worst of the current, using the dead railway as a bridge it was never meant to be.

"It's insane," Odile had said when he first proposed it, two nights ago, over charts lit by a single lantern.

"It's arithmetic," Jude had answered. And she'd looked at him for a long moment and said, "Then do the arithmetic twice."

He had done it six times. He did it again now, standing in the wind, and got the same answer: it could be done, barely, if the cable held, if the piers held, if the water gave them three more hours before it swallowed the eastern landing entirely.

Three hours. He'd spent seven years measuring his life in miles because miles were honest — they never pretended to be anything but what they were. Hours lied. Hours felt infinite right up until they weren't.

"There," Sef called, pointing. "There it is!"

The viaduct emerged from the mist like something remembered rather than seen. Nine limestone piers, each thick as a chapel tower, marching out from the eastern cliff and stopping mid-gorge where the tenth had fallen — and beyond them, on the far side, the shattered stump of the western abutment, furred with vines, forty meters of empty air bridging the gap where the span had dropped into the river during the flood. The arches between the piers were black with shadow. The water ran beneath them in smooth, muscular ridges, the current so fast it made no sound, only a pressure Jude could feel in his back teeth.

Odile brought the boat in against the eastern wall below the first pier, engines backing hard, and Mei — who hadn't left her bunk since the shoal except to be sick — appeared at the rail on Tomas's arm, gray-faced but upright, and said nothing, which from her was a kind of blessing.

Jude went up first.

The climb was a rusted iron ladder bolted into the pier's flank, thirty meters of it, rungs thin as his wrist, the river roaring below close enough to wet his boots with spray. Halfway up he stopped and pressed his forehead against the cold stone and breathed, and found that what steadied him was not the arithmetic but the voices beneath him — Odile calling angles, Sef relaying, Priya answering in her clipped river-cant — and he climbed toward them instead of away from the drop.

The pier top was a platform of cracked concrete wide enough for four men to stand abreast, ringed by the stumps of railing posts. The old anchor bolts were still there, iron eyes set into the concrete by men seventy years dead, and Jude knelt and put his hands around the nearest one and pulled, testing, and it rang solid as a bell.

"It holds!" he shouted down.

"Then make it hold us!" Odile shouted back.

They worked the next hour in a rhythm that surprised him. Sef ferried gear up the ladder — coils of the steel hawser they'd bought in the trading post, blocks, shackles, the winch drum cannibalized from the boat's spare anchor windlass. Priya rigged the near anchor while Jude calculated the catenary, the sag, the load. They spoke in half-sentences. She'd hand him a shackle before he asked; he'd call a measurement and she'd already have the tape out. Once their hands met on the same block and neither pulled away, and Jude noticed it the way you notice a door left open in winter — briefly, and without any plan to walk through it.

The far side was the problem. Two hundred meters of open water, and someone had to carry the light line across — a throwing line first, then the messenger, then the hawser itself hauled over by degrees. There was no crossing the viaduct's missing span. There was only the boat, running the gauntlet of the first three piers into the lee of the fourth, where an eddy turned slow enough for a throw.

"I'll take her in," Odile said, and there was no discussion about it, because there was no one else.

The eddy was a pocket maybe ten meters across, spinning between the fourth and fifth piers like a coin settling on a table. Get in wrong and the current would take the boat and fold it against the stone. Odile took her in right — engines feathering, bow swinging, the hull kissing the calm water with barely a sound — and Priya stood on the foredeck with the throwing line coiled in her left hand, three fingers of the coil wrapped around her right wrist the way river people do it, and Jude, watching from forty meters up, thought: *she has done this before, many times, and every time was a gamble.*

Her first throw fell short and the river ate it. Her second caught the wind and whipped back across the deck. Below them the water level crept up the piers, patient as debt.

"Again," Jude called. "You have time." He didn't know if it was true. He said it anyway, and saw her square her shoulders at the sound of his voice, and understood suddenly, completely, why people did this — why they bound themselves to other people, why Lena had loved having a brother, why Odile kept a crew instead of sailing alone. Not because it was efficient. Because your voice could reach someone where nothing else of yours could.

Priya's third throw arced high and true and landed across the pier top at Jude's feet.

What followed was ninety minutes of hauling — light line, then messenger, then the hawser itself, heavy as a sleeping snake, Jude and Sef and finally Mei pulling hand over hand while the cable paid out across the gap and sagged down until its belly nearly touched the ridges of the current. On the far side, the western abutment's stump offered a single intact anchor eye, and getting the hawser around it required someone to cross.

"I'm lightest," Sef said immediately.

"You're also the one who can't swim," Priya said.

"I can swim *adjacent* to swimming—"

"I'll go," Priya said, and clipped herself onto the hawser with a sliding tether, and stepped off the pier into two hundred meters of empty air.

She crossed hanging beneath the cable like a spider on a thread, hand over hand, upside-down world swinging beneath her, the river's spray rising to meet her boots. Jude watched from the anchor, feeding slack, counting her progress against the cable's sway, and everything in him that was engineer wanted more margin, more redundancy, more certainty — and everything in him that had been changing for eleven days simply *watched her*, trusting her hands, and found the trust fit better than the fear.

She reached the far side, freed herself, and got the hawser around the anchor eye in under a minute. Then she stood on the broken abutment and raised both arms, small against the mist, and Sef cheered so loudly it echoed off the gorge walls and came back doubled.

* * *

The winching was where it went wrong.

The system was sound: hawser anchored both ends, blocks at either shore, the *Provision* slung beneath the cable's midpoint on a bridle, hauled across by capstan on the western bank with Priya and Sef tending the far tackle. Jude had done the loads. The cable would carry five times the boat's weight even wet. The failure wasn't in the numbers.

The failure was in the river.

They got the bridle on and the boat moving at mid-morning, the hull riding the cable's sag like a bead on a wire, Odile at the helm keeping her nose into the current, everyone else on the capstan or the tackles. The first hundred meters went almost easily — so easily that Jude let himself believe, which is the mistake he always made, the mistake that had cost him Lena, believing that because a thing is going well it will continue to go well.

At the midpoint, directly over the deepest channel, the current found the bridle's trailing line — a safety pennant, twelve millimeters of rope that should have been harmless — and wrapped it around the propeller shaft.

The boat slewed. The bridle loaded unevenly. And Priya, on the western bank tending the far block, saw the whole system about to foul and did the thing a salvage scout does without thinking: she waded out onto the submerged shelf at the abutment's base to free the line by hand.

The shelf gave way.

Later, Jude would reconstruct it in fragments: Sef's shout, high and cracking. The brown water closing over Priya's shoulders. The safety pennant whipping taut as the current took her downstream — and then not taut, because she'd been tethered to the block, and the block's holding pin, corroded by seventy years of river air, sheared clean with a sound like a rifle shot.

She went into the channel with the block's remains still clipped to her harness.

Jude did not think. That was the revelation of it, afterward — that all his life he had believed thinking was what saved you, calculation, margin, the honest mile — and in the moment that mattered, he did not think at all. He was already moving, already shouting "Sef! With me!" already running the pier top toward the eastern end of the hawser with a coil of the messenger line over his shoulder, and Sef was beside him, matching him stride for stride, this boy who couldn't swim running toward drowning water because Priya was in it.

"She'll swing on the main cable!" Jude shouted. "If we can get her a second line — Sef, the far block, can you haul the hawser?"

"I can haul *anything* right now!"

They threw together without planning it, the way the crew had been learning to do for days: Jude paying out the messenger line in great looping casts while Sef hauled the main hawser hand over hand with a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere larger than his body, dragging the cable — and Priya with it, swinging on her tether like a pendulum — back toward the eastern piers. The current had her under once; Jude saw the brown water fold over her and his heart stopped in a way that had nothing to do with engineering. Then the cable swung her up and out, gasping, one arm hooked through her tether, and Jude cast the messenger line across her path and she caught it — of course she caught it, she was Priya Raghavan, she caught things other people dropped — and for ten seconds the two lines held her above the channel, one taut in Sef's bleeding hands, one in Jude's, and the river pulled, and they pulled back.

"Odile!" Sef screamed. "Get me something to belay this to!"

The winch drum. Jude saw it at the same moment Sef did — still rigged at the pier's edge from the crossing — and they dragged the hawser to it together, wrapped it, took three turns, and let the drum take the load with a groan that traveled up through the concrete and into Jude's bones. Then it was just work: hauling her in meter by meter while the river spent its fury against a dead railway's bones, Sef on the drum, Jude on the messenger line, both of them grunting the count — *"heave — rest — heave"* — until Priya's hand closed over the pier's edge and Jude dropped flat and gripped her forearm and pulled, and she came over the lip streaming water and alive, *alive*, coughing the river out of herself and laughing, actually laughing, a raw barking laugh with terror still inside it.

"You—" she coughed. "You can't swim either. I've seen you."

"I wasn't swimming," Jude said. His hands were shaking so badly he had to look at them. "I was doing rigging."

Sef sat down abruptly on the concrete, wrapped his arms around his knees, and began to shake. Priya put one hand on his shoulder and one on Jude's, and none of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying. The boat, freed of the fouled pennant, finished the crossing under Odile's hands alone, sliding beneath the western arch into calm water as the rain began — the first fat drops of the monsoon's outriders, striking the river like fingertips testing a drum.

* * *

They moored in the flooded forest on the far side, in the green twilight under trees that grew straight out of the water, and Odile broke out the rum she'd been saving, and no one asked what they were celebrating, because it wasn't a celebration. It was a confirmation. Of what, exactly, nobody said.

Jude sat on the cargo hatch with a tin cup he wasn't drinking and watched Sef and Priya argue cheerfully about whose fault the sheared pin had been — hers for wading out, his for not checking the corrosion — and Mei asleep against Tomas's shoulder, and Odile at the wheel even now, because Odile slept at the wheel the way lighthouses sleep standing up.

Two days, he realized. It had been two days since he'd thought about miles.

The map case lay at his feet, the way it always did, and he didn't open it. For seven years the ledger of closed lines had been the only conversation he kept with Lena — every abandoned spur a sentence, every decommissioned junction a word in a language only the two of them had spoken. He had believed the map was how he mourned. Sitting in the dripping dark with these soaked, arguing, impossible people, he understood that the map was how he *avoided* mourning, and that Sef had been right in the drowned town, and that mourning — real mourning, shared mourning — looked less like a ledger and more like this: a boy shaking with relief, a woman laughing at her own death, a captain who wouldn't leave the wheel, a brother-in-law he hadn't forgiven yet sitting close enough to touch.

Lena would have liked them. The thought arrived without pain, which was how he knew it was true.

Tomas shifted, and Mei murmured, and Priya said something that made Sef snort rum through his nose, and Jude sat among them in the rain-shadow of the canopy and did the only arithmetic that mattered anymore: three tides left, Vane's fleet somewhere ahead, a monsoon coming down the sky like a falling building, and every single person on this boat worth more to him than his license, his map, his careful measured life.

"Dawn tide gets us to the mission station," Odile said quietly, appearing beside him with the pot of coffee. She poured two cups without asking. "Whatever's waiting there."

"Tomas is waiting there," Jude said. "Or whatever's left of him."

"And whichever he is," Odile said, "you won't face it alone. That's what this is now. In case you hadn't noticed."

He had noticed. That was the terrifying part. Somewhere between the pier and the drum, between Sef's bleeding hands and Priya's grip on his forearm, he had stopped being a man conscripted onto a boat and become a man with a crew, and there was no chart in his case for the route back.

Far upstream, past the black teeth of the narrows, thunder rolled — long, low, patient, the monsoon clearing its throat. Sef's radio crackled to life in the shack behind them, a fragment of voice threading through the static, and Sef leaned in, and went very still, and turned around slowly with an expression Jude had learned, over eleven days, to dread.

"That's the mission station frequency," Sef said. "Someone's transmitting. And Jude — they're asking for you. By name."
