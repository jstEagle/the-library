## 13. Vane's Offer

The trading post at Salto Verde sat on a bend of the river like an old man on a bench — settled, patient, watching everything go by. Jude saw it first from the wheelhouse, a scatter of tin roofs and drying nets strung along a shelf of high ground where the floodplain pinched narrow. Behind it rose the green wall of the interior; ahead, the river widened into the long reach that would carry them to the mission station and Tomas.

Odile brought the *Provision* alongside the rotting pilings with the ease of a woman who had been born doing it. "Twenty minutes," she said. "Fuel, water, and Sef wants wire for his antenna. Nobody wanders."

Jude was already off the boat before she finished talking, which was unlike him, and he knew it was unlike him, and he did it anyway. There was a restlessness in him since the viaduct — two days now, and he still hadn't found the bottom of it. He kept reaching for the old arithmetic, miles and years, the ledger he'd carried since Lena died, and finding it blank. It should have frightened him more than it did.

The trading post smelled of diesel, dried fish, and wet rope. A boy sold him kerosene from a drum with a hand-painted gauge. Sef haggled over copper wire with the ferocity of someone who'd grown up poor and never unlearned it. Priya stood at the rail-end of the dock, scanning the downstream bend with binoculars, because that was Priya — always watching the water for what came next.

What came next was a launch.

It rounded the bend at half-throttle, white hull, brass fittings gone dull, flying no flag at all, which was its own kind of statement. Priya lowered the binoculars slowly. "Jude," she said, and her voice had gone flat in the way it did when she was keeping something level inside herself. "That's Vane's tender."

* * *

Marcus Vane did not look like the men who owned salvage fleets. He looked like a surveyor, or a schoolmaster — lean, silvering, dressed in a linen jacket that had been expensive once and was now merely practical. He climbed the dock ladder without help and stood among the fish crates and fuel drums as though they were furniture in his own parlor.

"Captain Ferran," he said to Odile, who had come up from the boat with her arms crossed and her jaw set like poured concrete. "I hoped we might speak again under better circumstances."

"You hoped wrong," Odile said.

Vane smiled, unhurried. "Then perhaps I'll speak with Mr. Eriksen instead." His eyes found Jude's and held them, and there was nothing schoolmasterly in them at all. They were patient the way deep water is patient. "You're harder to find than your reputation suggests. The man who reads dead rail lines like scripture. The man who mapped every closed spur between here and the headwaters. I've admired your work for years, Mr. Eriksen. Truly."

"You've read my work," Jude said.

"I've bought it. Twice, through intermediaries. Your survey of the Karst approaches was the finest piece of channel analysis I've ever seen. It's a shame the government never understood what they had." Vane turned a slow half-circle, taking in the trading post, the river, the crew arranged around him like a jury that hadn't been sworn. "May I buy you all a drink? The post keeps a passable rum, I'm told."

"We keep our own counsel," Odile said.

"Of course you do." Vane's gaze slid back to Jude. "Then I'll be brief, and you can all hate me efficiently and get back to your schedule. You have three tides left, by my count. Perhaps four if the monsoon is polite, and it won't be. My fleet turned back this morning — I don't gamble ships, only money. Which means, Mr. Eriksen, that you are going to find Tomas Ravel first. And when you do, you are going to find a man holding papers that will ruin me, or so he believes. And I am here to offer you a reason not to care."

The word *ruin* landed in Jude's chest like a dropped tool. He kept his face still. Seven years of practice at keeping his face still.

"Say it plainly," he said.

"A million," Vane said. "In certified drafts, payable wherever you like. For the woman Ochoa — delivered to my agent at Cazadero, alive and unharmed, I give you my word — and for your own quiet withdrawal. No one follows you. No one asks questions. The expedition reports failure due to weather, which will be true enough by then, and everyone goes home to their lives." He paused, delicately. "Including Mr. Ravel, who need never surface at all. He can stay lost. Some men prefer it that way. I suspect you understand that better than most."

Sef made a sound low in his throat. Odile's hand moved — not far, just enough to touch the girl's shoulder and stop whatever she was about to say.

"And if I refuse?" Jude asked.

"Then nothing happens today," Vane said pleasantly. "I'm not a pirate, Mr. Eriksen. I'm a businessman whose company made a surveying error nine years ago, which lawyers will argue about for a decade regardless of what papers anyone finds. But I'll tell you what does happen, because I think you deserve honesty — you strike me as a man who has had too little of it." He stepped closer, close enough that Jude could smell the tobacco on him, cheap tobacco, and that small human detail unsettled Jude more than the money had. "If you deliver those documents to the maritime board, there will be a hearing. Hearings require witnesses. Witnesses require credentials. And you, Mr. Eriksen, will testify to what you saw inside that wreck — which means swearing, under oath, to conclusions drawn from a hull inspection performed by a man whose license lapsed four years ago. Whose last formal certification is stamped *inactive*. Whose mental fitness was questioned, informally, after the Alder Street flood. Do you see? You won't ruin me. You'll hand my lawyers a gift-wrapped reason to make the whole thing about *you*. Grief-stricken engineer, obsessed with closed lines, chasing ghosts upriver. They'll bury the truth under your file, and they'll enjoy doing it."

The river moved behind him, brown and indifferent. Somewhere on the dock a net-mender's shuttle clicked, clicked, clicked.

"You know a great deal about me," Jude said quietly.

"I make it my business to know people," Vane said. "Especially people who lose things. Loss makes men legible." He glanced toward the boat, toward Mei, who was visible in the wheelhouse window with her arm still bound, watching them through glass. "Your wife drowned at Marlowe Bend, didn't she? Flood year. You were on a bridge crew forty miles away. You mapped yourself a route home afterward that avoided the town entirely — added eleven miles to every trip, for six years, rather than drive past the churchyard. That's in your file too. Not the official one. The one I keep."

Something went cold and ringing in Jude's ears, the way the world goes after a near miss on the tracks. He was aware of Odile beside him, very still. Of Priya's hand drifting toward her coat pocket. Of Sef staring at Vane with open hatred, young and unhidden.

"Here is what I know about you, Mr. Vane," Jude said, and heard his own voice come out level, which surprised him. "I know your flag was on a barge that put a hole in a passenger steamer and left forty-one people in the water. I haven't seen it yet. But I'm going to. And when I do, no amount of money and no file full of my worst days is going to matter, because the truth doesn't need me to be respectable. It just needs to be told."

Vane regarded him for a long moment. Then he nodded, almost respectfully, and reached into his jacket — Odile tensed — and produced not a weapon but a card, heavy cream stock, and held it out.

"The offer stands until the sixth tide," he said. "After that, the storm takes the evidence, the lawyers take the delay, and I take the win. Men like me always do, Mr. Eriksen. It isn't malice. It's arithmetic." He tucked the card into Jude's breast pocket himself, gently, an intimacy that made Jude's skin crawl. "One more thing, since we're being honest with each other. You said the truth doesn't need you to be respectable. That's true. But it does need someone willing to be ruined for it. Ask yourself, before the tide turns: is that man you? Or is he the man you used to be, before the river took everything you built your name on?"

He tipped an imaginary hat to Odile, nodded to Priya — "Ms. Raghavan. We'll speak again, I'm sure" — and went down the dock ladder to his launch with the unhurried ease of a man who had never once been chased in his life.

The white hull receded around the bend. The clicking of the net-mender's shuttle went on.

* * *

They didn't talk about it on the dock. They talked about it below decks, in the cramped saloon where the lamp swung with the current and the walls sweated in the building heat.

"He knew about Marlowe Bend," Sef said. "How does he know about Marlowe Bend?"

"Files," Priya said. "Investigators. He's been building dossiers on everyone connected to this river for years — I told you that. I've probably got one." She was sitting with her arms wrapped around her knees, and she wouldn't look at Jude. "He called my name. At the end. He wanted me to hear it."

"Did he get to you?" Odile asked bluntly.

Priya looked up then, and her eyes were fierce and wet at the same time. "He left my brother on a sandbar with no radio and a leaking hull, and then he sent me a check and a letter saying accidents happen. So no. He didn't get to me. But he knows exactly where to push, and that's worse."

Everyone looked at Jude. He realized, distantly, that they were waiting for him — not for orders, Odile gave orders — but for him to say whether the offer had gotten in. Whether the number had lodged somewhere and started working at him the way water works at a piling.

"It got in," he said, because lying to these people had become impossible somewhere between the shoal and the viaduct, and he was tired of pretending otherwise. "Not the money. The other part. He's right about the license. He's right that my testimony gives their lawyers a target. Everything he said about me is true, and he said it in front of all of you, and—" He stopped. Started again. "For seven years my whole self has been the maps. Closed lines. The one thing I could do that grief couldn't touch. If I stand up in that hearing and they take the license, they aren't taking a job. They're taking the last version of me that works."

The lamp swung. Outside, the first fat drops of a pre-storm shower ticked against the deck above.

"That's not what you'd be losing," Sef said.

Jude looked at him.

"You think your identity is the license," Sef said. He was nineteen and orphaned twice over and he spoke with the terrible directness of the young. "But I've watched you for two weeks. The license is paper. What you are is the guy who did the math on the Narrows when it was impossible, and rigged the cable when Priya went in the water, and didn't sleep but stayed up anyway checking my splice work because you didn't trust it and were right not to. Vane thinks he offered you a million dollars. He actually offered you a choice about who you are. And he got it backwards."

"How do you figure?"

"He thinks you'd be ruined if you testify," Sef said. "You'd be ruined if you don't. You already know it. You just haven't said it out loud yet."

Silence, except the rain thickening overhead and the river muttering against the hull.

Odile broke it, standing, in the captain's voice that ended discussions. "We sail on the next tide. Mei stays aboard. Tomas gets found. The papers go to the board. Anyone who wants Vane's million can swim downstream and catch his launch." She looked at Jude last, longest. "Eriksen. Whatever you decide about who you are — decide it fast, and decide it on the water. Decisions made ashore are worth half."

She went up to the wheelhouse. Priya followed, pausing at the saloon door to say, quietly, "He knew my brother's name too. First time I ever heard anyone else say it." Then she was gone, and it was just Jude and Sef and the swinging lamp.

"You okay?" Sef asked.

"No," Jude said honestly. "But I've been not-okay for seven years. This is the first time it's pointed somewhere useful."

Sef grinned and went to check his radio, and Jude climbed out into the rain.

* * *

He stood at the stern rail while the *Provision* took on water and the sky went the color of wet slate. The trading post lights blurred behind them. Ahead, the long reach opened dark and wide, and beyond it, somewhere inland, a ruined mission station held a gaunt man with a satchel full of the truth.

Jude took Vane's card from his pocket. Heavy cream stock, embossed letters, a telephone exchange in the capital. It felt obscene in his hand, smooth as skin. He thought about the file Vane kept — eleven miles of detour, six years of avoidance, a man mapping dead railways so he'd never have to drive past his wife's grave. Vane had read him like a chart and found the shoals, and the humiliation of that sat in Jude's stomach like swallowed river water.

And underneath the humiliation, quieter, worse: the question. *Is that man you? Or is he the man you used to be?*

He thought of the viaduct — Priya's hand slipping from his, the water rising, Sef's voice steady in his ear calling the count. Two days since, and he still hadn't thought about miles. He tried to summon the old ledger now, deliberately, the way you press on a bruise to feel if it's still yours. Miles and years. Track numbers and closure dates. It came, but faintly, like a station announced over a bad speaker.

Maybe that was the ruin Vane meant. Not the license. The map itself — the private country Jude had lived in since Lena died, where grief had coordinates and loss had a scale bar, and no one could enter without his permission. Testify, and the lawyers would strip that country bare in open court. Refuse, and he'd keep it, intact, walled, alone, forever, with a million dollars to pave the roads.

The rain came harder. Downriver, lightning stitched the horizon where the monsoon waited, patient as arithmetic.

Jude held the card over the rail. He meant to let it go — he was sure he meant to — but his hand stopped, and the card stayed pinched between his fingers, turning in the wind, and he stood there a long time, unable to finish either the gesture or the thought.

From the wheelhouse above, Sef's voice came down the voice-tube, tight with static and urgency:

"Jude! Get up here — I've got the mission station on the band. Someone's transmitting." A crackle, then, barely audible under the storm-noise, a voice reading numbers in a cadence Jude recognized from a lifetime ago, from kitchen tables and Lena's laugh, from a family that had once been his.

"It's Tomas," Sef said. "And Jude — he's counting tides too. He says we have less time than we think."
