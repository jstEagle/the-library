## 14. Fifth Tide, Monsoon's Edge

Sef heard the storm before anyone else did — not in the sky, but in the static.

It came as a hiss beneath the harbor pilot's channel, a graininess in the background noise that most operators would have blamed on their own equipment. Sef had grown up on a radio. His father had run a coastal relay station on the eastern capes, a shack full of humming valves and coffee cups, and Sef had learned before he was ten that the atmosphere spoke its own language. Static wasn't noise. Static was weather somewhere else, arriving ahead of itself like a rumor.

He sat in the wheelhouse's corner with his headset on and the set's brass dials glowing amber in the pre-dawn dark, and he listened to the sky filling up with electricity, and he thought: *early.*

The monsoon was supposed to make landfall in two days. The charts said so. The forecast traffic out of Cazadero said so. But the ionosphere didn't care about forecasts, and the crackle in Sef's ears said the leading edge had already crossed the coast and was walking up the valley toward them like something with intent.

"Ferran," he said, and then, because Odile Ferran did not answer to her surname at four in the morning, "Captain."

She was at the wheel, as she always was before a tide, one hand resting on the spokes the way another woman might rest a hand on a sleeping child. She didn't turn. "Talk to me, Sef."

"The edge is here. Early. I'm picking up discharge static off the western ridgeline — it'll be on us by midday, maybe sooner."

Now she turned. Her face in the amber light was all angles and decision. "How sure?"

"Sure as I've ever been about anything."

Behind her, Jude Eriksen looked up from the chart table where he'd been hunched since before the anchors came up, pencil behind his ear, eyes red-rimmed. He'd been recalculating the fifth tide all night — the window, the water height at the Karst sill, the margin they'd need to make the last navigable reach before the river turned into something no boat could argue with. Sef had watched him work and understood, in a way he couldn't have explained, that the man wasn't doing arithmetic. He was doing penance. Every number was a prayer in a language Sef didn't speak.

"Early by how much?" Jude asked.

"A day. Maybe a day and a half."

Jude looked at the chart. Sef watched him look at it — watched the pencil come down from behind his ear, watched the man's whole body go still the way water goes still just before it goes over the edge of something.

"Then we run the fifth tide at first light," Jude said. "Not at slack. At first light, on a tide that's still building."

Odile's jaw worked. "That's not a tide, Eriksen. That's a fight."

"It's the only one we get." He tapped the chart, at the long narrow reach they'd named — half-joking, weeks ago, in a voice that wasn't joking anymore — *the last navigable*. Above it the river braided into flood channels and drowned forest that no survey had touched in thirty years. "If we miss this window, there is no sixth tide. There's no anything. We sit under the monsoon for a month and Vane's lawyers eat the world."

Nobody said Vane's name often, but it sat in the boat with them anyway, the way ballast sits — invisible, permanent, felt in every turn.

Odile looked out the wheelhouse glass at the river, gray under a sky the color of a bruise coming up. Then she looked at Sef.

"You're my weather now," she said. "You tell me the second it changes. Not the second you're sure. The second it *changes*."

"Yes, Captain."

"Then we run it." She turned back to the wheel. "Wake the others. We're moving."

* * *

The fifth tide came up the valley like a living thing, and it was already wrong.

Sef stood at the rail with the wind tearing at his jacket and watched the water do things water wasn't supposed to do. A tide was supposed to be a breath — slow, tidal, patient. This one arrived in shoulders and shoves, brown water stacking against the current, whirlpools spinning up mid-channel like the river had opened eyes. The leading edge of the monsoon lay along the western ridgeline in a wall of slate and green-black, lightning flickering inside it without sound, the thunder still a valley away, patient as a debt.

Priya was on the bow with the lead line, calling depths, and her voice had changed over the weeks from the flat professional cadence of a scout to something rawer. "Twelve feet! Twelve and falling — no — *rising*, it's rising, whatever it's doing it's doing fast—"

"Hold your line!" Odile shouted back. "Sef, give me the edge!"

Sef had one hand on the rail and one on the doorframe and his headset clamped on under his hood, and he was reading the sky through the radio the way Priya read the water through the lead line. "Still a valley west. Wind's ahead of it — we'll get the gusts before the rain. Twenty minutes, maybe."

"Twenty minutes to the reach," Jude called from the chart table, where he'd wedged himself against the pitching. "We make it. Barely. If we don't lose the channel."

The river, as if it had heard him, tried to lose them the channel.

It happened at the bend below the old customs rock — a place Priya had warned about, a place where the current doubled back on itself and the flood tide coming up met the river coming down and the two of them, between them, made a sea. The boat took it beam-on. Sef went to his knees on the deck, headset skidding, and heard — distantly, through the roar — Odile's voice, low and steady, saying *no you don't* to the river as if it were a person she'd known all her life and intended to outlive.

The boat came around. The boat always came around. Sef had stopped, somewhere in the last week, being surprised by that.

They came through the bend with the water over the foredeck and Priya soaked to the skin and laughing — actually laughing, a wild bright sound that the wind took and shredded — and then the reach opened ahead of them, long and narrow and, for the moment, still navigable, the last corridor of honest water between them and the drowned country above.

"The sill," Jude shouted. "Sef — time me the sill."

Sef checked his watch against the tide tables Jude had annotated in a hand so cramped it looked like it was trying to escape the page. "Ninety-one minutes of margin. Maybe eighty-five, if the surge keeps building."

"Eighty-five." Jude said it the way a man says a rope length when he's hanging from it.

They made the sill in seventy.

* * *

They made the last navigable reach with the first rain on their backs.

It didn't fall so much as arrive — a gray curtain walking down the valley at them, and then it was on them, and the world contracted to the width of the wheelhouse glass and the amber glow of the instruments and Odile's shoulders, hunched into the wheel, immovable. Sef sat at his set with the rain hammering the deck above him and did the only work that mattered now: he listened.

The reach was theirs. That was the victory, if it was one — they had beaten the tide, beaten the sill, beaten the storm's leading edge by a margin Jude later admitted, in the flat voice of a man who had counted his own pulse through the whole thing, had been closer to forty minutes than eighty-five. They were in the last navigable reach, with the wreck country above them and Tomas somewhere in it, alive, hiding, carrying the truth in a oilskin packet like a man carrying a live coal.

And then, through the rain-static, Sef heard the fleet turn back.

He almost missed it. Vane's ships ran on commercial frequencies, tight-beamed, encrypted where they could be, but the lead ship — the one they'd all learned to hate on sight, the one with the crane arm like a gallows — was old enough that her radio discipline was old too, and her captain talked to his tugs in the clear when he was tired or angry or both. Sef had been logging his voice for two weeks the way you'd log a predator's tracks.

*"…negative, negative, we are not running the narrows in this, tell him — no, tell him yourself, I'm not telling him—"*

A pause, the crackle of the storm walking across the frequency.

*"Then you tell him the fleet's standing down to the delta anchorage. All of it. We ride this out below the gorge and we go in when the river gives it back. He wants the site, the site will still be there. Everything will still be there. That's the whole point of waiting out a monsoon, for—"*

The transmission dissolved into static. But Sef had the shape of it, and the shape was enough.

He sat with it for a moment. Rain on the deck. Odile's hands on the wheel. The amber dials. He thought about what *standing down to the delta anchorage* meant, spelled out in full, and his stomach went cold in a way the wet and wind had nothing to do with.

Vane wasn't giving up. Vane was doing arithmetic. The storm would close the gorge for a month — would scour it, reshape it, bury the wreck under silt and rearranged river. A month was nothing to a man with Vane's lawyers and Vane's patience. The evidence Tomas carried was the only thing in the world that could hurt him, and the storm was going to do Vane's work for him: drown the site, drown the witnesses' route home, drown the story, and when the river gave it all back, Vane's ships would be first in, and whatever was left to find would be found by men he paid.

Unless the documents got out. Unless *they* got out.

"Sef." Odile's voice, without her turning. "You've got a look. What did you hear?"

He pulled the headset down around his neck and told her. All of it. The fleet standing down. The delta anchorage. The captain's voice, tired and obedient, saying *that's the whole point of waiting out a monsoon.*

Odile was quiet for a long moment. The wheelhouse drummed with rain. Through the glass, the reach ran gray and endless ahead of them, and somewhere up it, in a ruined mission station or a flooded quarry or a hole in the drowned forest, a gaunt man was guarding a packet of paper with his life.

"So he lets the sky do his killing," Odile said at last. "Coward's arithmetic."

"It's not coward's arithmetic," Sef heard himself say, and was surprised by his own voice. "It's *good* arithmetic. That's what scares me. He's not wrong. If we don't get out ahead of this — if the sixth tide doesn't take us down — then he wins without spending another ship. The storm is his fleet now."

Jude had come up from the chart table without Sef noticing, and he stood in the wheelhouse doorway, dripping, the chart rolled in his fist like a weapon he hadn't decided to use.

"Then we don't give the storm a month," he said. "The sixth tide. It's the last window down through the narrows before the river closes — I've been running the numbers since the sill. It's narrow. It's *ugly*. But it exists, and it exists for about four hours, and if we're on it with the documents and Mei and Tomas, we're out ahead of Vane's patience and under the storm's skirt before it can shut the door."

"And if we miss it?" Odile asked.

Jude looked at the rain. "Then we're on the wrong side of the gorge for a month, with Vane's ships waiting at the bottom like a bill come due."

* * *

They found the mission station an hour later, or rather Priya found it, reading the drowned forest the way she read everything — by what wasn't there. The channel bent where a channel shouldn't bend, and behind the bend, on a shelf of high ground the flood hadn't reached, stood a roofline. Half a roofline. The rest was ivy and rot and thirty years of river weather, but the bell tower still stood, and hanging in it, madly, impossibly, was a bell that the wind was ringing — a cracked, arrhythmic tolling that carried over the rain like something out of a story Sef's father would have told him and sworn was true.

They came ashore in the lee of the station wall, four of them, leaving Sef and the boat in the reach with orders to keep the engine warm and the radio hot. But Sef watched them go up the bank — Odile first with the lantern, then Jude, then Priya with her rifle slung, then Mei leaning on a crutch she'd made herself and refused to complain about — and he sat in the wheelhouse with the rain coming down like the end of the world, and he keyed the set, and he listened.

And at 19:40 by his watch, through the worst static he'd ever worked, he heard a voice.

It was thin. It was cracked. It was transmitting on the survey team's old emergency frequency, the one Tomas had supposedly drowned in the river, and it said, in a voice that Sef would know anywhere because he'd spent two weeks logging it off a stolen fragment and dreaming about it:

*"—any station, any station, this is Ochoa's team, this is — no. No. This is Tomas Eriksen. If anyone is on this frequency — if my sister's husband is on this frequency — we have very little time, and I need to tell you what's coming down that river behind you—"*

Sef's hands were shaking. He keyed the transmit.

"Tomás," he said. "This is Sef. We're here. We're coming up. And you'd better believe we're getting you out — but you need to hear what's waiting at the bottom of the river when we do."

Outside, the storm leaned on the boat. The bell in the ruined tower rang on, cracked and stubborn, and Sef, seventeen years old and the only one listening, held the frequency like a hand in the dark, and waited for the answer.
