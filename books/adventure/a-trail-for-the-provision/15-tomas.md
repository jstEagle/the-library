## 15. Tomas

The mission station announced itself before they saw it — a bell tower leaning out of the treeline like a man too tired to stand, its bronze bell long since gone to some scavenger's sledge. What remained was a shell of coral stone and river clay, walls furred with moss the color of old bruises, a roof that had surrendered in patches to the strangler figs threading through it. The river had crept into its foundations decades ago and never left; the whole structure seemed to be sinking by inches, patient as sediment.

Jude saw it first from the bow, because he had been looking for it for two days.

Mei had given them the bearing on the fourth night, feverish, her splinted leg propped on a coil of line while Sef changed her dressings: *the old station, past the drowned chapel, where the reeds go gold.* She had drawn it in the condensation on the cabin glass with one finger — a tower, a bend, a stand of bamboo — as if she were afraid that saying it aloud might summon something. Or someone. She had not spoken Tomas's name since the reeds. She only said *he'll be there*, and then, quieter, *he made me promise not to tell you what he looks like now.*

Jude hadn't asked what she meant. He had spent thirty years reading water and rails, and he knew when a thing was better left unread until he stood in front of it.

Odile brought the boat in against the last of the ebb, engine at a whisper, the hull kissing mud fifty meters off the bank where the channel shallowed into a fan of silt and golden reed. She killed the engine entirely for the final approach and let Priya pole them the rest, and the silence that came down over the river was so complete Jude could hear the bamboo creaking inland, could hear his own pulse in his ears, which was new, which he did not like.

"You want us to wait?" Odile said, low, beside him.

He looked at her. Her gray eyes held nothing but the question itself — no pity, no softness offered where he would have to refuse it. That was why he had come to trust her, he realized. She measured people the way he measured lines: by what they could bear.

"No," he said. "But give me the bank."

She nodded once and turned to the others without explanation, and they gave him the bank. Even Sef, who had not stopped watching him since Mei's confession, even Priya, who missed nothing and commented on less. They simply became very busy with mooring lines and bilge checks, and Jude stepped over the gunwale into knee-deep warm water and waded ashore alone.

* * *

The ground rose through reed and mud to a terrace of cracked flagstone, and the flagstone to a courtyard gone to weed, and the courtyard to the station's great doors, one hanging, one fallen inward years ago and half-swallowed by the floorboards rotting beneath it. Beyond them, the nave of the mission opened up like a ribcage. Light fell through the broken roof in slanted columns thick with insects. An altar remained at the far end, stripped of everything but a cloth gone gray as cobweb, and before it —

Before it, a man sat on an overturned pew with a shotgun across his knees, and he did not startle, and he did not rise, and he did not lower the gun. He simply watched Jude come through the doorway as though he had been watching that doorway for weeks, which, Jude understood in one cold drop of certainty, he had.

Tomas Reyes had always been a big man — big shoulders, big laugh, hands that swallowed Lena's whole when he shook yours. The man on the pew had been whittled down to wire and sinew, his beard streaked white at forty-one, his cheekbones standing out like things washed up. But the eyes were unchanged. Dark, level, unimpressed. Lena's eyes. Jude had spent seven years avoiding those eyes in photographs, and here they were, alive, furious, aimed at him across forty feet of ruined church.

"Jude." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "You're late."

Jude stopped in the center aisle. His mouth had gone dry, and every sentence he had rehearsed on the boat — in the gorge, on the shoal, lying awake under the netting while the river talked to itself — every one of them had evaporated.

"The telegram said missing," he managed. "Nineteen days. I came as fast as the water allowed."

"The water allowed exactly what you let it allow." Tomas shifted, and the shotgun stayed where it was, casual as a folded newspaper. "You mapped your way around this river for seven years, Jude. Around every line that touched her. You think I didn't know? You think she didn't know? She used to say my brother-in-law could find any route in the country except the one home."

"Don't." The word came out of Jude like something torn. "Don't you say her name like you have a right to it. You've been alive for nineteen days while we planned a recovery. While Mei bled. While Sef—" He stopped. Breathed. "You sent your own team's radio into the river, Tomas. You let them think you were dead."

"I let *them* think *I* was dead," Tomas said, and now he did rise, slowly, the pew groaning, and up close the gauntness was worse — the tendons of his neck standing like bridge cables, the tremor in his hands that he stilled by gripping the gun tighter. "I let Vane think I was dead. There's a difference, and the difference is that everyone who thought I was dead is still breathing. Ask yourself how many of Vane's survey crews are."

The light moved in the broken roof. Somewhere in the fig canopy a bird started up, three notes, and quit.

"You could have told me," Jude said. "One message. One name on a scrap of paper in a bottle. I'd have come."

"Would you?" And there it was — the thing underneath, surfacing at last, the way a wreck surfaces when the current shifts. Tomas came down the aisle toward him, and Jude made himself hold still. "Would you have come, Jude? For me? You wouldn't cross a county line for seven years. You built yourself a map of closed lines so you'd never have to drive past the place she died. Priya told me — no, don't look at her, she didn't tell me anything, I've had nineteen days to learn what you are, and what you are is a man who turns grief into geometry. She died and you drew a line through her and called it mourning."

Jude's hand closed around nothing. Seven years of nothing. "I carried her," he said, and his voice broke on the second word and he hated it, hated the crack running through him in front of this man, this ghost, this thief of nineteen days. "Every day. Every mile. You think a map is a wall? The map is the only place I could keep her. Every closed line on it is a day I couldn't get out of bed, and I marked them anyway, because if I didn't mark them they'd just be — holes. Unmarked holes swallow trains, Tomas. I know. I've seen what happens when nobody marks the water."

"And what good did all your marking do her?" Tomas's voice rose, cracked, filled the nave. "She drowned in four feet of floodwater in a town with a levee that failed because the same company that falsified these—" he struck his chest, where a waterproof tube hung on a cord, thudding against the bone of him, "—falsified the channel charts that put our survey boat on a shoal that isn't on any map! Fourteen people, Jude! Fourteen names in this tube, and Vane has spent six years making sure no one reads them! Where was your precision then? Where were your closed lines when it counted?"

The words landed one after another like spikes driven home, and Jude stood in the ringing aftermath and found, to his astonishment, that he could not argue. Because Tomas was right about the shape of it, if not the heart. He had turned grief into geometry. He had believed that measuring a wound was the same as closing it.

"She was my wife," he said quietly. "Not yours to guard."

"No." Tomas stopped three feet away, close enough that Jude could see the salt crust in his beard, the mosquito welts, the exhaustion sitting behind his eyes like a second face. "No, she was my sister. And when she died, you took her with you. You took all of her. You went silent, Jude. Nineteen days is nothing. You gave me seven years of silence. Do you know what it's like to lose someone and then lose the person who's supposed to be grieving beside you? To stand at her grave and look around and realize you're the only one left who remembers how she laughed?"

"She laughed at your terrible jokes," Jude said, and his throat closed, and the nave blurred, and he let it, for the first time in seven years, let it blur. "At all of them. Even the ones in Portuguese. Especially those. She'd translate them for me afterward and they were worse in English and she knew it and that's why—"

"That's why she married me instead of you," Tomas finished, wet-eyed, and the laugh that came out of him was half sob and sounded so much like hers that Jude had to turn away, had to grip the back of a rotten pew and hold on while seven years went through him like water through a breached hull.

They stood like that, two men in a ruined church, neither touching, both anchored, while the light columns crawled westward and the river breathed beyond the doors.

* * *

Later — an hour, maybe more — they sat on the altar steps with the tube between them, and Tomas ate the food Sef ferried in wordless installments, pretending not to notice the kid's careful, awed glances, and Jude watched the man rebuild himself bite by bite.

"Vane's fleet turned back at the fifth tide," Jude said finally. "Sef caught their traffic. He won't run the narrows in monsoon. He's betting the storm buries everything — the wreck, the evidence, us."

"He knows me better than that." Tomas wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "That's why he's not worried. He thinks I'll die up here eventually, or trade the documents for passage, or that the river will take them. He falsified the original surveys himself — initialled them, Jude. It's not a company crime. It's his signature."

"Then we get it downriver before the sixth tide closes." Jude looked at the tube, at the fourteen names inside it, at the initials he had not yet seen but already believed. "There's a window tomorrow morning. Odile says it's the last one the gorge will give anyone until October."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then: "You calculated it. Tides, windows, margins." A ghost of the old grin, worn thin. "Still turning grief into geometry?"

"No." Jude picked up the tube and weighed it in his hand — lighter than it should be, for what it held. "Turns out it was never grief. It was just waiting for a reason to open the map again."

Outside, the first fat drops of the monsoon's leading edge began ticking against the broken roof tiles, and from the bank came Odile's voice, cutting through the rain-shush with the calm of a woman who had already decided: *"Both of you — aboard. Now. We run on the morning tide, and God help us, we run fast."*
