## 16. Sixth Tide

Odile Ferran had run rivers that hated her before. She'd run the Karst Narrows with a cracked hull and a crew of two. She'd run the flood season of '09 with a cargo of frightened miners and a boiler that coughed like a dying man. But she had never run a river that was actively trying to become something else — and that was what the water below the mission station was doing now, hour by hour, tide by tide: ceasing to be a river and becoming a weather.

She stood at the rail of the *Provision* and watched the horizon eat itself.

The monsoon did not arrive the way the almanacs promised, as a front, a line, a thing you could mark on a chart. It arrived the way a debt collector arrives — suddenly, personally, and already inside the house. The southern sky had gone the color of wet slate overnight, and along its whole width the clouds were stacked in tiers, boiling upward, lit from within by a sick greenish glow that Odile had only ever seen twice in thirty years on the water. Both times, she'd tied off and prayed. This time there was no tying off. This time the water itself was the only door out, and it was closing.

"Sixth tide turns at fourteen hundred," Jude said beside her. He had the tide tables folded into one hand and his old rail map in the other, and he was looking at the gorge mouth the way he looked at everything — as if it were a line he could read, a language of gradients and clearances that the rest of them only heard as noise. "We have maybe five hours of favorable water. After that, the ebb runs against the storm surge and the whole reach turns to standing waves. No boat runs that. Not this boat. Not any boat."

"And if we wait for the next one?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough. There was no next one. The gorge would close like a fist, and they would sit in the flooded timber upstream of it, a crew of seven and a satchel of documents that could hang Marcus Vane, waiting out a monsoon with no food worth mentioning and a hull that had already given everything it had. Or worse — the storm would find them anchored, find them waiting, and simply take them. Rivers did that. Rivers were patient that way.

Odile had made her living on calculations like this one, and she knew the arithmetic cold: a window of five hours, a reach of thirty-two river miles, a storm whose leading edge was already chewing the far hills into gray smoke. The margin was nothing. The margin was a knife's width. But she had learned, over a long and bruising career, that the margin was never really where the decision lived. The decision lived in the crew. You looked at the crew, and you knew.

She looked.

Priya was already at the bow lines, coiling them into flaking loops with the quick, economical hands of a woman who had stopped being afraid of work a long time ago and had never quite stopped being afraid of everything else. Sef was on the cabin roof with the radio mast in his lap, re-lashing the stays, his thin arms working double-time, and every few minutes he'd duck under the tarp to the set and come back out shaking his head — nothing from downstream, nothing from the fleet, nothing but the storm's static chewing the whole band. Mei Ochoa sat on the cargo hatch with her splinted leg propped on a coil of rope, sorting the survey documents into oilcloth packets with a care that bordered on liturgical, because she understood — they all understood now — that those papers were the only cargo on this boat that mattered, that they were worth more than the *Provision* and everyone aboard her, and that the river did not care.

And Tomas.

Tomas Eriksen stood apart, at the stern, gaunt as a heron, watching the water with the fixed, hollow-eyed stare of a man who had spent weeks hiding in a ruin and was only now beginning to believe that hiding was over. He had come aboard an hour ago without a word, had taken the coil of rope Odile handed him without a word, and had tied off the stern fast without a word. But his hands had known the work. Lena's brother, Jude's ghost, the man whose vanishing had started all of this — and he stood at the rail like a piece of ballast that had finally been lashed down.

"Captain." Jude's voice, low. "It's your call."

She almost laughed. Seven days ago this man had treated her command like a formality he was tolerating, a government form to be filled out in triplicate. Now he was handing it to her like a rope.

"It's not my call," she said. "It's the river's call. We're just the ones who have to answer it." She turned, raised her voice to carry the length of the deck. "Listen up! Sixth tide turns in ninety minutes. We run the gorge on it or we don't run at all. Storm's going to hit us somewhere in the middle of the reach — I'd bet the narrows, maybe the bend below the old quarry. We will take water. We will take a lot of water. Anyone who wants to stay upstream and gamble on the storm passing gentle can say so now, and I'll say nothing about it ever."

Nobody said anything. The wind said something instead — a long, rising moan out of the south, the sound of a throat clearing.

"Good," Odile said. "Then let's load her like we mean it."

* * *

They loaded her like they meant it.

Everything came off the boat that wasn't hull, crew, or proof. The spare anchor went to the river bottom with a splash that sounded almost apologetic. The trade goods they'd taken on at the last post went onto the mission station's ruined floor, stacked under a tarp that would not survive the week. Odile kept the tools, the pumps, the fuel, the food, and the documents — and nothing else, and even the food was a calculation, because every crate was freeboard, and freeboard was life.

Mei came down the station steps on Sef's shoulder, hopping, refusing a second shoulder with a look that could strip paint, and they stowed her amidships in the lee of the engine housing, wrapped in canvas, wedged with fenders, lashed like precious cargo — which was, Odile reflected, exactly what she was. The documents went into a steel chart tube that went into a waterproof satchel that went, after a brief silent negotiation, into the space between Mei and the hull, where a body could fall on it and keep it from floating away.

Tomas took the wheel well without being asked. Odile watched Jude see it — watched something move across his face that was not quite forgiveness and not quite grief but had the shape of both — and then Jude went forward to the bow without a word, and the brothers were separated by the whole length of the boat and the whole length of seven years, and the river between them was going to have to do the rest.

"Cast off in ten," Odile called, and the *Provision* seemed to gather herself.

* * *

The tide turned at fourteen hundred exactly, because tides are the one honest thing left in the world.

Odile felt it before she saw it — a change in the way the current leaned against the hull, a loosening, a long exhale from the water. The river downstream of them had been running backward against the flood for hours, gray water shouldering upstream, and now, slowly, grudgingly, it began to give way, to turn, to run with them.

"Lines off," she said.

Priya threw the bow line, Sef the stern, and the *Provision* slid off the mission station's collapsed jetty into the current like a leaf leaving a branch. For one long moment the river simply took them — took them gently, almost tenderly, gathering the hull into its grip and carrying it downstream at a walking pace, then a trot, then a canter, and the banks began to move, the drowned trees began to move, and the gorge mouth opened ahead of them like a door.

Behind them, the southern sky came down.

Odile looked back once. Once was enough. The storm had made landfall in earnest now — she could see it, a wall of rain a hundred feet high walking up the valley they had just left, swallowing the hills, swallowing the mission station, swallowing the whole drowned country behind them with a sound like a freight train that never passed. The rain did not fall so much as arrive, sideways, in sheets, and where it hit the water it raised a fog of its own impact, a white chaos that erased the world.

"Engine to full," she said. "Sef — anything on the band?"

"Nothing but noise, Captain." His voice was steady, and she was proud of him for that, and furious at the world for making him practice it. "The fleet's gone quiet. Storm's got their antennas too."

"Then it's just us and the water. Good. Water I can argue with."

The gorge took them at a quarter past two.

For the first three miles it was almost kind. The channel ran deep and fast with the tide under them, and the *Provision* planed along at a speed Odile had never gotten out of her in her life, spray coming off the bow in a solid sheet, the drowned forest flickering past on either side like the spokes of a wheel. Jude stood at the bow reading the water aloud — "green channel left, boil on the right, hold center" — and Priya relayed it aft, and Tomas steered to it, and for a few minutes the boat was a single animal with a single nervous system, and Odile let herself believe, for exactly those few minutes, that they might actually make it.

Then the rain reached them.

It came all at once, the way a wave comes, not a gradual darkening but a blow — the sky simply dropped, and the world went white and roaring, and Odile could not see the bow of her own boat. She could not see the bow of her own boat. Visibility went from a mile to a boat-length to nothing in the space of thirty seconds, and the wind hit the superstructure like a fist and laid the *Provision* over ten degrees, and somewhere below, Mei shouted something that the storm took away.

"Jude!" she bellowed into the white. "I'm blind up here!"

"Then steer by my voice!" It came from the bow, half-drowned, and she realized he had tied himself to the rail with a line around his waist and was leaning out into the chaos, reading water he could barely see, calling it back to her by sound. "Hard to port — now! Rock shelf, starboard side, close!"

She put the wheel over. The hull groaned. Something scraped along the starboard strake with a long, screaming whisper — a rock, close enough to shave paint, not close enough to shave planking — and then they were past it, and the channel bent, and the storm bent with them, and they ran on.

The narrows came at three o'clock, and the narrows were where the river stopped being a river and became an argument.

The gorge pinched to half its width there, and the storm surge was already backing water up it from the sea, and the two forces — the ebbing tide running down, the surge pushing up — met in the pinch and stood up. Odile saw the standing waves before she heard them: a line of gray humps across the entire channel, steep and glassy and wrong, water that had forgotten which way was down. Thirty-two miles of reach, and the worst of it was here, and here was where the storm had chosen to be.

"Jude!" she shouted.

"Through the left third!" he roared back. "The waves break left! Priya — everyone down and hold on! Tomas — do not stop steering, whatever happens, whatever I say, do not stop!"

Odile got the engine to a scream. The *Provision* hit the standing water like a horse hitting a fence.

What followed was not navigation. It was survival with a rudder. The hull left the water twice — Odile felt it, that sick lifting, the engine howling at nothing — and came down twice, the second time so hard she heard something in the hold let go and go rolling. Green water came over the bow in tons. Sef appeared out of the white like a drowned cat, threw himself at the bilge pump, and started working it with both hands and a rhythm he'd learned somewhere and never needed until now. Priya was lashed at the midships rail, hauling on the fender line that kept Mei's splinted leg from becoming a second casualty, her mouth open in a shout that had no sound left in it.

And through all of it, thin and cracked and absolutely relentless, Jude's voice kept coming out of the bow. "Hold center. Hold center. Wave — bear off — now hold center." Like a man reading scripture in a burning church. Like a man who had spent seven years reading dead rail lines and had finally found one that went somewhere.

Tomas never stopped steering. Odile watched him through the white — watched this gaunt, ruined, half-mad man stand at her wheel with both hands locked and his jaw set like a man holding a door shut against the sea, and she understood, in a flash of clarity that the storm handed her like a gift, that this was his penance and his proof, all in one, that he had spent weeks hiding from the world and was done hiding, that he would steer this boat to the sea or die holding the wheel, and either way he would not let go.

The last wave hit them abeam and laid the *Provision* down until Odile's elbow touched water.

Then the gorge let go of them, and the river opened, and they were through.

* * *

The rain kept coming, but the killing water was behind them. Below the narrows the channel widened, the surge spread itself thin, and the *Provision* ran down the last twelve miles in a gray roaring twilight with the bilge pump going and the storm walking on their heels. Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the river hadn't already heard.

It was Sef, near the end, who broke the silence — not with words but with a sound: a laugh. A single, cracked, incredulous laugh, the laugh of a boy who has just realized he is still alive, and it went around the boat like a spark through dry grass. Priya caught it. Then Odile herself, helplessly, both hands on the wheel, laughing into the rain like a madwoman while the *Provision* wallowed and rolled and refused, with the stubbornness of all well-built things, to sink.

The estuary opened at dusk — or what the storm allowed to pass for dusk, a bruised half-light under a sky the color of an old bruise. The sea beyond it was a rumor, a long gray heave. Odile brought the boat around the last marker and into the lee of the breakwater at Port Meridian, and as the hull finally, finally went quiet beneath her feet, she looked back upriver one more time.

The gorge mouth was gone. Not hidden — gone, erased, a wall of rain and spray where a door had been. The monsoon had closed it behind them exactly as the tide tables promised, and somewhere on the far side of that wall was the wreck, and the truth in her hull, and the whole drowned country that had tried very hard to keep them.

"Captain." Jude stood beside her, soaked to the skin, blood on his temple from a knock she hadn't seen him take, and he was looking back upriver too. "The wreck. We passed the turn for it in the narrows. It's still down there."

"I know," Odile said.

"Vane's fleet turned back. The storm will sit on that site for a week, maybe two. When it lifts, his divers will be the first ones on her." He turned, and in the half-light his face was unreadable, but his voice was not. "The documents prove what he did. But the hull proves it too. Breached from the outside, on the starboard bow. I saw it. But a board of inquiry will want more than my word."

Odile looked at the wall of rain where the river had been, and then at the chart tube lashed between Mei and the hull, and then at the crew of her — her crew, and when had that happened, when had these people become hers — scattered across the deck, bleeding and laughing and lashing things down.

"The board will want what it wants," she said. "But the river keeps its own records, and it just showed us it can keep them as long as it likes." She put her hand on the throttle. "We made the tide. Now we find out what the tide made of us."

Behind them, upriver, the storm went on swallowing — and somewhere inside it, riding the flood surge toward the open sea, something else was moving that none of them had seen leave the narrows.
