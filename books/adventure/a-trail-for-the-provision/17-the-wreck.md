## 17. The Wreck

The river gave them the wreck before they were ready for it.

Jude saw it first from the wheelhouse — a dark shape breaking the brown water off the starboard bow like the back of some drowned animal, listing hard to port, its superstructure sheared away as if a giant hand had simply taken an interest and removed it. The *Provision* — because that was what the survey team's charter boat had been named, and Tomas had told Mei, and Mei had told them all in the flat voice people use for facts too large to hold any other way — sat in twelve feet of water where the channel bent around a gravel bar, exactly where the falsified surveys said there was thirty.

"Slack water won't last," Odile said. She stood at the wheel with her arms crossed, watching the wreck come at them the way she watched everything: without flinching, without hurrying. "Two hours, maybe less with the storm pushing the surge down early. If you're going down there, Eriksen, go now."

"I'm going down there."

"I know you are. I just like hearing you say it." She glanced at him sideways. "Sef will work the line. Priya has the second mask ready. And I want you back up before the current turns, or I'm cutting you loose and telling your brother-in-law you drowned doing something brave and stupid, which is the only kind of thing anyone in this family ever does."

"He's not my family anymore."

"No," Odile said. "He's worse. He's your family again. I watched you two argue all night in that mission station like brothers do. Now get in the water."

* * *

The river took him the way it always took divers — roughly, without ceremony, a cold brown fist closing over his head. Jude had dived maybe a dozen times in his life, all of them routine: bridge pilings, intake grates, once a locomotive boiler off a washed-out trestle, workmanlike descents into green murk with a checklist in his head. This was not that. The flood-swollen water was opaque within arm's reach, carrying silt and broken reeds and the smell of everything the monsoon had torn loose upstream, and he descended the shot line Sef had weighted to the wreck's rail with his eyes open and seeing almost nothing, one hand on the rope, the other holding the crowbar he'd brought for prying, of all things, truth.

At ten feet the light died entirely. At twelve his boots found steel.

He'd prepared himself for it to feel like a grave. That was what he'd told himself on the ladder, working the mask straps: *you are entering a tomb, be respectful, be quick.* But the wreck didn't feel like a tomb. It felt like a wound — raw metal edges, torn plating curled outward, the whole hull groaning faintly against the current the way a hurt animal breathes. And it felt, more than anything, like a question. Seven years of mapping closed lines had taught Jude that every disaster left a signature. Floods signed their work in mud lines and snapped timber. Washouts signed in the angle of fallen ballast. He had spent seven years reading other people's catastrophes from a distance, cataloguing them, never once touching one.

His glove found the breach.

It was forward, port side, low on the hull near the turn of the bilge — exactly where Tomas's documents said the impact would be. Jude ran his palm along it slowly, letting the steel tell him what his eyes couldn't. The plating wasn't torn. It was *punched*. A clean inward deformation, a crescent maybe four feet across, the metal folded along a straight leading edge and then ripped ragged where it had finally given way. He knew that geometry. Every engineer who had ever worked a rail line knew it, because they saw it whenever a train met something heavier than itself at speed: the signature of a collision, not a grounding. A rock bar tears upward and aft, gouging. This had come in level, square, with mass behind it — a flat-faced blow delivered by something with a bow.

He followed the edge of the breach until his fingers closed on something embedded in the fold of steel. Small. Wedged tight. He worked it free with the crowbar's chisel end, patient, the air in his suit going stale, Sef's line tugging twice above him — *status?* — and he tugged back once: *working.*

When he brought the object up to his faceplate and clicked on his lamp, the silt swirled off it in slow brown ghosts.

Painted steel. A fragment of plate, maybe eight inches long, sheared from whatever had struck the *Provision* and driven into her hull like a signature pen left in the wound. And on it, under the grime, under seven years of river, a stenciled mark still legible in faded white:

**V.C.M. — CAZADERO**

Vane Cargo & Marine. The old flag. The fleet Marcus Vane had built before he'd sold the barges off one by one and gone respectable, before the name changed twice on the letterhead, before the man himself started showing up at maritime board hearings in a gray suit to testify about how tragic accidents happen on wild rivers.

Jude hung there in the black water, breathing through his regulator in long deliberate pulls, and made himself look at the rest of it. Because looking was the job now. Not feeling. Looking.

He swam the length of the breach again with the lamp. Found the second strike point aft — glancing this time, a long scrape with paint transfer in it, the same faded white. Found the cargo hold hatch, sprung, and inside it the rotted shapes of the survey crates Tomas had described, and among them, caught in the netting, a hard hat with a name taped inside the brim. *R. Okafor.* One of the names from the documents. One of the men the official report said had been lost when the *Provision* grounded on an uncharted shoal in high water, an act of God, no fault assigned, case closed.

Uncharted shoal. Jude had read those words in the file Odile had pulled in Cazadero, and even then something in him had snagged on them the way his boot snagged on wreckage now. Rivers didn't hide shoals from surveyors. Surveyors hid shoals from rivers — or were paid to publish channels that weren't there, deep enough, wide enough, safe enough for a loaded barge run that would never have been legal otherwise. Vane's company had falsified the charts. Some other captain, trusting the published channel, had brought a barge downriver in the dark at full load, and the *Provision*'s crew — doing honest work, running an honest survey, probably the very survey that would have exposed the lie — had died for it.

The air in his tank was getting thin. He knew he should go up. Instead he put the painted fragment inside his vest, pressed it flat against his chest, and did one last thing: he took out the waterproof pencil and the slate Sef had rigged for him, and by lamplight, in the dark, on the hull of a dead ship, he sketched the breach. Fast, rough, but true — the crescent, the straight leading edge, the depth of the fold, the height above the keel. An engineer's testimony, taken at the scene. His hand only shook a little.

Then the current turned. He felt it come up the channel like a change of mind in the river itself, the slack water stiffening, the wreck beginning to hum against his boots, and Sef's line went taut and insistent, and Jude kicked for the surface with the proof against his heart and the dead man's name still taped inside his memory.

* * *

They hauled him over the gunwale and stripped the mask off him and Priya was already reaching for the slate, and for a moment nobody spoke. The rain had thickened to a fine gray needle-fall. Behind them the gorge mouth was a wall of cloud, and ahead the river ran wide and fast toward the delta, carrying them and the storm both toward the sea.

"A barge," Jude said. His voice came out scraped, diver's-rough. "The hull wasn't grounded. It was struck. Bow-on, port side forward, heavy — a loaded barge running the published channel at night." He held up the painted fragment, dripping. Water ran off the stencil and for a moment the letters seemed to swim. "Vane's old flag. It was wedged in the breach. It couldn't have drifted there — the fold of steel had grown shut around it, rusted in. It's been there since the night it happened."

Priya turned the fragment over twice, her jaw tight. "That's not evidence," she said. "That's a confession written in his own paint."

"It's corroboration." Jude looked at the slate, at his own sketch, and was surprised to find his hand steady now. "Tomas's documents say the surveys were falsified. My dive says the *Provision* was hit by a vessel drawing the depth only a falsified chart could promise. The board can call Tomas a liar. They can call Mei a liar. They can't call the hull a liar."

Odile had come down from the wheelhouse and stood dripping at the rail, studying him with an expression he'd learned to read over six tides and one impossible crossing of the Karst Narrows. It was the look she saved for moments when the shape of a thing changed underneath her.

"You went down to check a claim," she said. "You came up a witness."

Jude started to answer and found he had nothing. She was right, and the rightness of it sat in his chest like ballast. All the way upriver he had framed this as arithmetic — tides against days, fuel against distance, the contract's terms weighed against the crew's odds. Even after Tomas, even after the documents, some engineer's corner of his mind had kept treating the truth as cargo to be delivered, freight like any other, valuable but interchangeable. Standing in Cazadero he had calculated whether he could afford to finish this. On the viaduct he had calculated whether they could survive it. He had been calculating since Lena died, because calculation was the only grief that didn't bleed.

But hanging alone in the black water with his palm flat against a punched-in wound in a dead ship's side, holding a piece of a man's flag that the river had kept for seven years — that hadn't been calculation. That had been *knowing*. And knowing didn't weigh anything at all. It just pointed, the way a compass points, in one direction, all directions at once, home.

"The tide's turning," Sef called from the radio shack, and then, a beat later, sharper: "Odile. Current's making two knots against us already. We run now or we anchor and eat the surge."

"We run." Odile was already moving. "Eriksen — that fragment goes in the document tube with the rest. Double-wrapped. You carry it, nobody else. If we lose the boat, you swim with it."

"If we lose the boat—"

"We're not going to lose the boat." She gripped his shoulder once, brief and hard, which from Odile Ferran was a speech. "But if we do, the paper matters more than we do. That's the job now. Say you understand."

Jude looked at the tube in his hands. Inside it: Tomas's stolen surveys, Mei's sworn account, his own sketch, and a shard of painted steel with a dead company's name on it. Enough to indict Marcus Vane. Enough, maybe, to give R. Okafor and eleven others their names back. Enough to make every mile of closed track, every year of avoidance, every cold night on a map of things that used to matter mean something — not erased, none of it erased, but *aimed*, finally, at something worth hitting.

"I understand," he said. And meant it all the way down, past the calculations, past the miles, to the bottom of the thing.

* * *

They ran the last reach with the storm's outriders chasing them down the valley — gusts that came off the water in gray sheets, rain that hit the deck like thrown gravel. Sef stood his radio watch anyway, hood up, headset clamped against the wind, because Sef always stood his watch. Jude took the bow with Priya, reading the channel by eye against the charts he no longer trusted and the river he was learning to, calling the bends while Odile drove them hard through the building chop.

It was Priya who saw it first. She grabbed his sleeve and pointed astern, back upriver, where the gorge mouth had disappeared entirely behind the storm.

"At first I thought it was spray," she shouted over the wind. "But it's holding station. Look at the shape of it."

Jude looked. At the edge of the visible world, where the river narrowed into the falling dark, a pale shape was moving against the flood — small, low, riding the surge with the particular arrogant ease of a vessel built for exactly this water. A launch. Fast, shallow-drafted, the kind of boat a rich man keeps for the occasions when he decides the storm is someone else's problem.

No lights. No flag. But it was coming downriver at the speed of the flood itself, and there was only one man left behind them with a reason to run.

"Sef!" Jude bellowed. "Get Odile. Tell her we've got company on the surge — and tell her to give us everything the engine has left."

Behind them, the shape grew.
