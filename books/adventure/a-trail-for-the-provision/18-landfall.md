## 18. Landfall

**Jude**

The sky went the color of wet slate an hour before the wind arrived, and Jude knew what that meant. He had spent seven years reading landscapes the way other men read faces — the sag of a cutbank, the lean of a dead pine, the particular bruise-purple that gathered over high ground before water came down out of it. The monsoon was not coming anymore. It was here.

"Odile!" he shouted over the engine's laboring growl. "That wall of cloud — how far to the narrows?"

She stood at the wheel with her collar snapping in the gusts, gray hair plastered flat, and did not waste breath on optimism. "Twelve kilometers. Maybe less if the river's kind."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we find out what this boat is made of." She glanced at him, and beneath the gruffness there was something almost like a smile. "And what we're made of. Same thing, tonight."

The *Provision* ran heavy. Tomas sat wrapped in a tarp near the stern, guarding the oilskin packet of documents as though the storm itself might be one of Vane's men sent to take it. Mei lay below with her splinted leg braced against the bulkhead, calling up soundings from memory whenever Sef relayed her the depth gauge readings. Priya was at the bow with a boathook, reading the water for snags. And Sef —

Sef was everywhere at once, radio headset around his neck, hands flying between the bilge pump lever and the searchlight, seventeen years old and holding the whole expedition together with wire and nerve.

Jude crouched at the chart table lashed amidships and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since dawn. Sixth tide. Their window. Behind them the gorge would close like a fist; ahead of them the Karst Narrows waited, twelve kilometers of limestone throat where the river compressed into something less like water and more like a held breath.

The first rain hit like thrown gravel.

* * *

**Sef**

Sef had never heard rain make a sound like that before — not falling on the deck but *arriving*, a solid thing, a wall of it sweeping upriver toward them visible even in the dark because it blotted out the lightning behind it. Then it was on them and the world became noise.

He got his headset on and the set tuned by feel, fingers remembering what his eyes could no longer see. Static roared, but under the static there were voices — Cazadero harbor control, faint as a prayer: *— all river traffic advised — gorge closing — repeat, gorge clos—*

"Gorge closes behind us either way," Odile had said, and she'd been right. There was no going back now. Back was drowning too.

"Sef!" Priya's voice from the bow, raw-throated. "Deadfall! Starboard side, two meters!"

He couldn't see it. Nobody could see anything. But he threw his weight against the tiller relay anyway, trusting her eyes the way he'd learned to trust them across five tides and a thousand small moments — the way you trust a hand in the dark because it has never once let go.

The boat shuddered. Something scraped along the hull with a sound like a giant dragging its nails, and then they were through, and Priya whooped, and Sef realized he was laughing, actually laughing, in the middle of the worst night of his life.

Below him, muffled through the deck, Mei's voice rose steady as a metronome: *"Depth holding. Depth holding. Trust the channel."*

Trust the channel. Trust each other. It amounted to the same thing.

* * *

**Odile**

Forty years on the water, and Odile Ferran had run storms before — coastal squalls, typhoon remnants, once a cyclone off the delta that had cost her a mast and two fingers' worth of feeling in her left hand. She had never run a storm in a gorge, and she prayed to no god in particular that she never would again after tonight.

The walls of the Karst Narrows rose on either side of them, invisible except when lightning turned them white — sheer limestone faces a hundred meters tall, and the river between them swollen to three times its honest width, running brown and furious, full of the whole sky's worth of rain coming down at once.

"Jude!" she bellowed. "Your viaduct numbers — recalculate for this flow!"

He was already doing it. She could hear the pencil scratching even through the din, that man's stubborn, beautiful refusal to panic. He'd come aboard a stranger seven days ago, all closed lines and closed face, measuring everything in miles. Now he was soaked to the bone at her chart table calculating their survival to the decimal, and when he looked up at her his eyes were lit with something she recognized from her own mirror on her best days.

"Two hundred cubic meters per second more than my estimate," he shouted back. "But the constriction at kilometer eight works in our favor — the surge piles up *behind* us. If we stay ahead of it—"

"If we don't?"

"We won't be around to discuss it."

"Then we stay ahead." She hauled the wheel over as the current shoved them starboard. "Everyone hold onto something that loves you back!"

From below, faintly, Tomas's dry voice: "Nothing on this boat qualifies."

Odile laughed — a short, barking thing torn out of her by the wind — and understood, suddenly and completely, that she would not trade one soul aboard this boat for any cargo Vane's whole fleet could carry. That was new. That was dangerous. That was exactly the kind of thinking that kept crews alive.

* * *

**Priya**

The rope saved her life twice before midnight.

The first time was her own fault. She'd gone forward to clear a fouled line and a wave took the bow just as she reached for the rail, and for one long, spinning second Priya Raghavan was airborne above a river moving faster than any train she'd ever ridden. Her hand found the safety line Jude had rigged at the third tide — his rigging, his knots, his quiet insistence that *every hand gets a lifeline, no arguments* — and the rope snapped taut and swung her back against the deck hard enough to crack the breath out of her.

She hung there, half over the side, the river clawing at her boots, and felt the line begin to pay out through hands that weren't hers.

"Hold!" Jude's voice, right beside her ear somehow, though he'd been twenty feet away. His arm locked around her waist; Sef's fists closed on the rope behind him. Three people pulling as one body, and the river lost.

They dragged her over the rail and she lay gasping on the deck, ribs screaming, and Jude knelt over her with rain streaming off his jaw and said, not gently at all: "Don't you dare. Not tonight. Not ever."

"I wasn't—" she coughed. "I wasn't going anywhere."

"You were going exactly where your brother went," he said. "Not on my boat."

She stared at him. He stared back, unflinching, and then hauled her upright by the collar and pushed the boathook back into her hands, because there was still deadfall ahead and every pair of eyes mattered, and that — that was the whole of it, she thought. That was the entire difference between Marcus Vane and everyone aboard the *Provision*. Vane measured what people cost. These people measured what they carried.

The second time the rope saved her life, she didn't even notice. Years later Sef would tell her about the moment the searchlight caught the submerged piling and Jude's shout and Sef's turn happened inside the same heartbeat, seamless, wordless — and she would realize she'd been standing at the bow in perfect trust, seeing nothing, fearing nothing, because the two men behind her were watching the dark so she didn't have to.

* * *

**Jude**

Kilometer eight. The constriction.

Even in daylight it would have been a horror: the gorge pinched to a quarter of its width, the river stacking up into standing waves taller than the boat, spray blowing horizontally through the beam of the searchlight like driven snow. Somewhere in that chaos was the channel — Mei's channel, the one she'd sounded on the survey, the one Tomas's false charts had hidden from the world for years — and somewhere in the chaos was the truth that the only way through was straight down the throat of the worst of it, because the calm water on either side was calm the way a trap is calm.

"Mei!" Jude shouted down the hatch. "The constriction — talk me in!"

Her voice came up through the deck, thin and absolutely certain. *"Enter left of center. There's a rock tooth at the entry — you'll see whitewater breaking over it. Pass close enough to spit on it. The deep channel hugs the tooth, then swings right. Do not swing right early. Early means the shelf."*

"Close enough to spit on it," Odile repeated grimly. "I've always liked her."

Jude took the forward position, one hand locked on the rail, reading the water as best the lightning allowed. This was the work he'd been born for, he realized — not the closed lines, not the safe abandoned spurs of his grief ledger, but this: the live problem, the impossible calculation, the moment where everything he knew had to be worth more than everything he feared.

There. Lightning flared and showed him the tooth, black fang in white fury.

"Left of center! Hard left, NOW—"

The *Provision* leapt. The world became water. They passed the rock tooth so close Jude could have touched it, could feel the suction of the deep channel grabbing the hull like a hand, and then Odile swung them right — not early, not late, *exactly* — and the shelf that would have torn their bottom out slid past beneath them unseen, and they were through, shot out the far side of the constriction into the relative mercy of the lower gorge.

Behind them, the surge they'd outrun slammed into the constriction and detonated, a column of spray rising higher than the gorge walls, and the sound followed them like applause.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Sef, shaking, soaked, seventeen years old and grinning like a lunatic, raised both fists to the sky and yelled, "WE'RE ALIVE!" and the word went around the boat — alive, alive, alive — passed hand to hand like something precious.

* * *

**Sef**

The last hour was the hardest, because hope is heavier than fear.

Fear makes you simple. Hope makes you count — the kilometers remaining, the fuel remaining, the strength remaining in arms that had been hauling ropes and pumping bilge since sundown. Sef counted them all and watched the numbers get worse, and kept his headset on anyway, because someone had to listen, and listening was his.

At 0300 by his watch, the rain began to slacken. Not stopping — monsoons didn't stop, they breathed — but easing, the way a fist eases before it strikes again. And through the thinning roar, Sef heard the river change.

"Odile," he called. "Listen."

She cocked her head. The whole crew went still, six people straining their ears over the engine note, and there it was: the sound widening. Opening. The gorge releasing its grip.

"The narrows end," Jude said quietly. Beside him, Tomas — gaunt, defiant, half-dead and entirely stubborn — put a hand on the rail and finished the thought:

"Three kilometers of open floodplain to the delta. We're through."

They came out of the Karst Narrows as the sky behind them turned the color of a bruise going green, and Sef climbed onto the cabin roof — against every rule Odile had ever made, and she let him — and looked back the way they'd come.

The gorge mouth was swallowing itself. Water poured down the canyon walls in silver sheets, and the entrance they had cleared not twenty minutes ago vanished behind a curtain of collapse and spray, the river closing like a door.

Through it. All of them. The documents, the survivor, the brother, the truth.

Sef's radio crackled and he dropped back to the deck, hands finding the dial. A new signal, weak, from downstream — Cazadero, or what passed for authority out here. He listened, transcribed, and his stomach dropped even as his heart soared.

"Odile," he said. "Harbor master's office. They're saying the maritime board convened early — emergency session. They want testimony." He swallowed. "They want *us*. And there's a message flagged for Jude Eriksen specifically."

Jude looked up from the chart table, rain-worn and river-changed, a man who two weeks ago had measured his life in track miles and could no longer remember why.

"From whom?"

Sef read the transmission slip twice to be sure. "Marcus Vane's lawyers. They're demanding you appear — and they're saying," he hesitated, hating the words, "they're saying they intend to challenge your license before you can testify. Everything rides on what happens when we make landfall."

Odile opened the throttle. Ahead, through the last of the rain, the lights of the delta settlements glimmered like a promise.

"Then we'd better not be late," she said.
