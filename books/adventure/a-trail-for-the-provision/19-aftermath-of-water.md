## 19. Aftermath of Water

Priya felt the grounding before she heard it — a shudder through the deck plates that traveled up through her boots and into her teeth, the boat lurching sideways as the river simply gave up beneath her. Then the sound: a long, grinding groan of steel against silt and stone, the *Marrow's Mercy* settling onto a shoal it had no business finding in the dark.

"Everyone off the bow line!" Odile's voice cut through the rain, hoarse from a night of shouting. "Off! Let her lie!"

The monsoon had swallowed the world. Priya couldn't see the far bank, couldn't see the near bank, couldn't see anything but the grey churn of water and the ragged shapes of her crewmates lashed to the deck like laundry in a gale. Sef was still at the radio, strapped into the chair with a cargo strap, headphones crushed against his skull, shouting fragments of nothing into the static. Jude was beside the wheel, one hand on Odile's shoulder, the other braced against the housing, his face lit by the instrument glow — and Priya thought, absurdly, that he looked calm. Not peaceful. Calm the way a man is calm when he has already done every calculation available to him and found the answer is *wait*.

The boat groaned again, settled deeper, and held.

* * *

They found the depot at first light, or what passed for first light under a sky the color of wet slate. The storm had slackened to a steady, soaking rain, the kind that got under collars and into bones and stayed there. The *Marrow's Mercy* sat canted on the shoal, her port hull stove in below the waterline, three plating seams sprung, the engine flooded to the mounts. She wasn't going anywhere. Not today. Probably not this week.

"Rail depot," Jude said. He stood at the rail, rain streaming off his jaw, looking at the treeline upstream with the particular fixed attention Priya had learned meant he was reading something none of the rest of them could see. "Half a mile. There's a maintenance spur that used to serve the quarry at Karst Bend. The depot should still be standing — the line was closed, not demolished. Nobody spends money tearing down buildings nobody wants."

"And you know this because," Sef said, teeth chattering, "you keep a map of every dead line in the country."

"Someone has to remember where the bones are buried."

The depot was exactly where Jude said it would be: a long, low shed of corrugated steel and rotting timber, built on a shelf of high ground above the old right-of-way, which was itself built above the flood line — because the men who laid the rails, a century ago, had understood water in a way the men who'd falsified the channel surveys apparently never had. The big sliding doors were rusted half-open. Inside: dry-ish floor, an old potbellied stove, a workbench with tools fused into a single rusted mass, and a hand-pump well that, after the third try, coughed up water that was brown but moving.

Odile walked the length of the shed once, kicking at the floorboards, testing the roof trusses with the butt of a boathook, and then said, "We're here. Sef, get a mast up, see if you can raise Cazadero. Priya — with me. We're lightening the boat before the next surge takes her apart."

Priya looked back once, at the doorway, where Jude stood with his soaked map case clutched to his chest like a breviary, and Tomas beside him, the two of them staring at the rain as if it were a language they were only now learning to translate.

* * *

Two days they worked. Two days of hauling cargo off the grounded boat in a leaky skiff, of patching what could be patched, of Sef climbing the depot's roof mast in gusts that made Odile swear oaths in three languages, of Mei — her ribs strapped, her arm in a sling, refusing to be useless — sorting Tomas's documents under an oil lamp and reading names aloud in a low, steady voice.

It was the names that changed the weather inside the depot.

Priya noticed it first on the second evening. She'd gone in for dry socks and found them all gathered around the stove: Mei with the manifest pages spread across her knees, Tomas sitting very straight with his hands flat on his thighs like a man giving testimony, and the others — Jude, Odile, Sef — arranged around him in a loose, involuntary semicircle. Nobody was talking. Mei was reading.

"—Vasquez, Elena R., assistant surveyor. Okonkwo, Daniel, deckhand. Ferreira, Mateus, deckhand. Lindqvist, Astrid, cook. Ochoa, Renata—" Mei paused. "My aunt. She was on that boat. I didn't know. I was twelve."

The rain filled the silence.

"Nineteen names," Tomas said. His voice was scraped raw, and Priya realized he'd been talking for a while before she came in — that this reading had been going on for hours, that Mei had been reading names and Tomas had been telling her who each one was, because he had spent two years in the interior learning them, memorizing them, carrying them like ballast. "Nineteen. The board ruled seven. The company paid out seven settlements and called the rest unaccounted, lost with the vessel. There was no vessel left to lose. Vane's people broke her up for scrap in secret and sold the steel. These twelve—" his hand moved over the pages, "—these twelve were never even acknowledged."

"They were never buried," Sef said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"They were never *anything*," Tomas said. "That's what a falsified survey buys you. It doesn't just sink a boat. It un-persons everyone on her."

Priya stood in the doorway with wet socks in her hand and felt something old and cold turn over in her chest. She knew about un-personing. She knew what it was to have a brother who was officially *missing, presumed lost, claim closed* — a file in a drawer in an office in a city Vane had never visited, closed by men who had never looked at the water. Arun had been gone eleven years, and in all that time no one had ever said his name out loud to her in a room with a stove in it. She had said it herself, alone, on decks, at night. That was the whole of his rite.

"We should bury them," she said.

Everyone turned.

"Not — I mean—" She came into the light, awkward, her voice rougher than she intended. "The river took them and never gave them back. There's no grave, no marker, nothing. We've got a high shelf of ground, a storm that isn't leaving for days, and nineteen names. We could give them a grave. Even without bodies. Even just — the ground and the names. It's not nothing. It's not nothing to be *somewhere*."

Jude was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read at first, and then she could: it was recognition. The look of a man who had spent seven years keeping a ledger of closed lines because he had nowhere to put his dead either.

"Quarry workers used to bury their dead up on the knoll behind the depot," he said. "There'd have been a plot. Consecrated, probably, in the loose way quarry towns did things. It'll be overgrown, but the ground will be there."

"Then we dig," Odile said, in the tone she used for decisions that were not decisions.

* * *

They dug on the third day, in a break in the rain that lasted four hours and felt like a gift from a god none of them had been consulting.

The knoll behind the depot was a tangle of fern and secondary growth, but Jude found the plot the way he found everything — by reading what was no longer there. A line of fence posts gone to rot. The ghost of a gate. And then, when they cleared the fern with boathooks and bare hands, the stones: a dozen low markers, hand-cut, their letters softened by forty years of rain into suggestions. *Beloved.* *Taken young.* A single stone with no name at all, just a ship's wheel carved by someone who had clearly never held one.

They dug one grave, deep and wide, at the head of the old plot. The ground came up dark and heavy and smelling of iron, and they took turns — Odile first, setting the pace, then Jude, then Priya, then Sef and Mei and Tomas, six people passing two spades and a mattock in a rhythm that became, without anyone deciding it, something like a work song.

Priya found herself digging beside Jude, and found, to her surprise, that she was talking.

"My brother's name was Arun," she said, without deciding to say it. The spade bit the earth. "He went out on a Vane contract run, eleven years ago. Weather turned. Vane's dispatcher told him the window was good. It wasn't. The boat went down with six aboard, and Vane's company settled fast and quiet, and the file says *lost with all hands, claim closed.*" She spat on her palms. "I took salvage work after that because it was the only way to get on the water where he died. And then I took Vane's money because it was the only way to get *his* money, and I told myself I'd take him down from the inside, and then I just — stopped telling myself anything. You can stop, you know. You can just stop feeling it. It's very quiet."

"I know," Jude said.

"I know you know." She glanced at him. "That's why I'm telling you."

He didn't answer, but he dug, and after a while he said, "Lena's grave is in a town with no road to it anymore. I mapped closed lines for seven years so I'd never have to drive past it. I told myself it was a ledger. It was a coward's map." He drove the spade in. "Tomas said that to me two nights ago. Called it a coward's map. I wanted to hit him. He was right."

"Brothers-in-law," Priya said, "are licensed to be right at the worst possible moments."

Something happened to Jude's face then that she had never seen in three weeks of running rivers with him: he laughed. Short, startled, half-pain — but a laugh, pulled up out of him like a splinter, and Priya found her own eyes stinging and blamed the rain that wasn't falling anymore.

* * *

They buried the names at dusk.

There was no coffin, so Odile contributed the boat's oldest signal flag, worn through at the folds, and they wrapped the nineteen manifest pages in it — Mei had copied each name out fair, in her surveyor's hand, because Tomas said the original documents had to stay intact for the board, and Mei said names deserved better than carbon paper. They wrapped the pages, and Sef, who had produced from somewhere in his gear a tin that turned out to hold three candles, a length of wire, and a harmonica he had never once mentioned in three weeks aboard, wired the candles into jars and set them at the head of the grave.

Odile spoke first, because captains speak first even when they don't believe in anything but water. "We found you late," she said. "The river kept you and wouldn't say your names. We're saying them now."

Then they said them. All nineteen. Each crew member took a page and read the names on it aloud, into the darkening air, and the others answered each one with the old river-word — the one Odile used for the lost, the one Priya had heard her mutter over the stern in bad weather — *gone down, not gone.* Tomas read the last page, and his voice held, and when he finished, Jude put his hand on the wrapped flag-bundle before they lowered it into the ground, and said, quietly, in the voice of a man reading a document he had never expected to read:

"Recorded. Witnessed. Entered into the record."

Then the earth went in, and Sef played the harmonica — badly, sweetly, three bars of something that might have been a hymn or might have been a work song — and the candles burned in their jars, and the rain held off, as if the sky, too, were waiting.

Afterward nobody moved to go inside. They stood around the new grave in the old plot, six people who had been strangers a month ago, and Priya understood that something had finished being built among them — not in the storm, not in the narrows, not even at the viaduct, but here, in the turned earth. You could run rapids with people and not be theirs. You buried names with them, and you were.

Mei took Priya's hand. Priya let her.

* * *

The storm returned in the night, full-throated, and they sealed themselves in the depot and let it rage. The boat would need a salvage crane or a miracle; the river would need a week to drop; Cazadero was two hundred miles and a hearing away.

Priya woke near dawn to lamplight and low voices. Jude and Tomas sat at the workbench, heads bent over the documents, Mei's fair-copied name pages stacked beside the survey originals, and Tomas was saying, "—the board will want the originals, but they'll want a witness more. Someone who saw the hull. Someone with standing."

"I have standing," Jude said. "I have a license. That's what they'll take."

"That's what they'll *try* to take. There are ways to—"

"No," Jude said, and his voice was quiet and absolutely certain, and Priya, pretending to sleep in her bunk by the stove, felt the words go through her like the grounding had. "I saw the hull. I testify to what I saw. Whatever it costs."

Tomas was silent a moment. "Lena would have liked you for that," he said. "She'd have hated it, too. She'd have argued with you for a week. And then she'd have liked you for it."

When Priya rose, the lamp was out and the brothers-in-law were asleep on opposite sides of the stove, and on the workbench, in the grey first light, she saw that Jude's map case lay open — and that the old map of closed lines had a new sheet beside it, half-drawn, in Jude's own hand: a river channel, surveyed honestly, marked with soundings and a small careful note at the shoal where the *Marrow's Mercy* lay.

*Wreck of the Marrow's Mercy. Grounded in service. Crew all safe.*

She stood looking at it for a long time. Then the storm shifted, and the wind came around, and Sef sat up suddenly in his bunk with his headphones half on, saying, "I've got Cazadero. Weak signal, but it's Cazadero — and there's word from the maritime board."

He looked up at the rest of them, rain-worn and candle-lit, and read it out: the board would sit in six days.

Six days, a broken boat, and two hundred miles of flooded river between them and the only hearing that mattered.
