## 20. The Hearing

The maritime board met in the old customs house at Cazadero, a building that had not been repainted since before the war and smelled of salt rot, lamp oil, and the particular patience of institutions that outlast everything they judge. Jude Eriksen stood outside it on the morning of the hearing with his hands in his pockets and his license folded in his breast pocket like a letter he intended to burn.

He had not slept. None of them had, really — the monsoon had chased them downriver and then spent itself against the coast for three days, and the whole town had been busy with tarps and bilge pumps and the small economies of survival. But last night the sky had cleared to a hard, rinsed blue, and Jude had lain awake on Odile's deck listening to the harbor settle, going over the testimony in his head the way he used to go over gradients and load limits. What he saw. What he measured. What the hull told him.

*The hull was breached from the outside,* he kept repeating. *A collision. A barge under Vane's flag.* It sounded simple when he said it like that. It would not sound simple in that room.

Sef found him at the foot of the steps, carrying two cups of coffee that were mostly condensed milk.

"You look terrible," Sef said, handing one over.

"I've been told I always look terrible."

"You usually have an excuse. Today you don't." Sef glanced up at the customs house, at the brass plaque gone green with weather. "Priya's already inside. She's been there since dawn. She said she wanted to watch Vane's lawyers arrive so she could memorize their faces."

"That sounds like Priya."

"Mei's giving her deposition now. Tomas is next." Sef hesitated, and Jude heard what was underneath the hesitation. "He asked if you'd sit with him. Before you go in."

Jude looked at the coffee. Seven years of not sitting with Tomas Varga sat in the cup alongside the condensed milk, and neither of them dissolved.

"All right," he said.

* * *

Tomas was in a side room that had once been an office and was now mostly a table, a chair, and a window fogged with river damp. He looked better than he had in the mission station — fed, shaved, wearing borrowed clothes that almost fit — but the gauntness hadn't left his eyes. Hunger like his took longer than a few good meals to cure.

They stood looking at each other across four feet of scarred floorboard, two men who had shared a sister and lost her in different ways and had never once, until this month, said her name to each other.

"You came," Tomas said.

"Sef made it sound like a request."

"It was." Tomas pulled out the chair but didn't sit. "I owe you things, Jude. I know that. I've been making a list since Mei dragged me out of that station, and the list keeps getting longer, and none of it — none of it covers what I did by disappearing. By letting you think—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "By letting everyone think I was dead, or worse. It was easier. That's the truth of it. Hiding was easier than fighting, and I dressed it up as strategy because I'm a coward who reads a lot."

"You're not a coward."

"I hid inland for eight months eating rice other people gave me."

"So did I hide," Jude said quietly. "Just closer to home. I mapped dead rail lines for seven years so I'd never have to drive past the town where she died. You don't get to be the only coward in this family."

The word *family* landed between them like something dropped from a height. Tomas stared at him, and then his face did something complicated — grief and relief arriving together, badly, the way they always do.

"Lena would have hated both of us," he said, and his voice cracked on it.

"She'd have hated us efficiently. She was good at that." Jude set down the coffee. "Tomas. The hearing. They're going to come after me. Vane's lawyers. They'll dig up my file, my suspension, the incident at Kessel Junction—"

"They'll try to take your license."

"They'll take my license." He said it flatly, factually, the way he read off a grade. "I've done the math. To make the breach credible I have to testify to what I saw inside that wreck, and to do that I have to admit I entered a restricted site without survey authority, without a salvage permit, using methods no licensed engineer can defend on paper. I dove a wreck I had no right to dive. I'll be testifying as a man with no standing at all."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"The names in your documents deserve more than paper. Forty-one people, Tomas. The board has their names now because you kept them. But they need someone who put his hands on the hull." He shrugged. "It's just arithmetic. I've been doing arithmetic my whole life. This is the first problem where the answer costs me something and I don't mind paying."

Tomas crossed the floor and did something he had not done since Lena's funeral: he embraced him. Briefly, awkwardly, both of them too aware of their own bodies, but real. When he stepped back his eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"After this," he said, "the survey commission is being rebuilt. Clean people, new charter, honest charts. I'm heading it — temporarily, God help everyone. I want you on the team. Not for your license. For your maps. Every closed line you ever drew, every spur you memorized to avoid your own grief — we need all of it. The river routes, the old viaducts, the depots. You've been mapping this country's wounds for seven years, Jude. Come map its recovery instead."

Jude didn't answer. Not yet. Through the wall he could hear the board convening, the scrape of chairs, the cough of an old clerk calling order into a room that had never once obeyed it.

"I'll tell you after," he said. "When I know what's left of me."

* * *

The hearing room held perhaps sixty people and felt smaller, pressed as it was with salvagers, port officials, newspapermen, and the pale, expensive silence of Marcus Vane's legal team — five of them, gray-suited, arranged along one table like instruments in a case. Vane himself sat behind them, immaculate, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man watching weather he had already priced into his season.

Odile Ferran testified first. She gave her account in the same voice she used for shouting over engines — economical, unadorned, impossible to interrupt. Then Priya Raghavan, who laid out Vane's hiring of her with such precision that one of the gray suits actually laughed, a short disbelieving bark, and the board chairman reminded him where he was. Then Mei Ochoa, steady despite the arm still in its sling, describing the falsified channel surveys, the altered depth readings, the years of paperwork through which Vane's company had turned a known hazard into a profitable shortcut and a sunken crew into a closed file.

Then they called Jude Eriksen.

Vane's lead counsel was a woman named Corvus, silver-haired, courteous in the way of people who sharpen things. She let him establish his credentials — twenty-two years, chief engineer, the Kessel line, the flood surveys — before she began taking them apart.

"Mr. Eriksen. Your engineering license was suspended in your thirty-first year, was it not? Following the Kessel Junction incident?"

"Suspended pending review. Reinstated with conditions."

"With conditions. And those conditions restricted you from independent structural assessment of submerged vessels, correct?"

"Correct."

"So when you dove the wreck of the *Provision's Grace*, you were acting without authority. Without a permit. Without any professional standing whatsoever."

"Yes."

"Yes." She let it hang. "You'll forgive me, Mr. Eriksen, but what the board is being asked to accept is the underwater opinion of a man whose own regulator has twice found cause to question his judgment, obtained illegally, in zero visibility, in a current, while a monsoon approached. Isn't it possible that what you identified as a collision breach was simply catastrophic corrosion? That a grieving, exhausted, unqualified man saw what he needed to see?"

Jude had expected the question. He had rehearsed answers on the deck of the boat for two nights. But standing there, with Vane watching him from behind the gray wall of his lawyers, he found he didn't want the rehearsed answer. He wanted the true one.

"Ma'am," he said, "I've spent seven years mapping closed lines. Rail spurs nobody uses, bridges nobody inspects, towns nobody drives through anymore. I know exactly what decay looks like. I've catalogued it by the mile." He turned to face the board. "Corrosion eats from the inside out. It leaves lips. It leaves thinning you can put a thumb through. What I found on that hull was a hole punched from the outside — clean edges, inward-folded plate, paint transfer in the crease. And embedded in the seam, I recovered fragments of barge fendering stamped with the fleet mark of Vane Riverworks, retired from their barges six years ago. I brought the fragments. They're logged with your clerk. Your metallurgist can date the paint."

Corvus consulted her papers. "You entered the wreck how many times?"

"Twice."

"In conditions your own captain described as—"

"My captain described them accurately. I signed off on her account. It was dangerous. I went anyway." He paused, and then said the thing he had walked into the building carrying in his breast pocket. "And I want the board to understand that I am not testifying today as a licensed engineer. If entering that wreck stripped my standing, then I testify as a private citizen who put his hands on forty-one graves and read what was written on them. Take the license. I'd rather be an honest witness than a credentialed silence."

The room went very still. Somewhere behind him he heard Sef exhale.

Corvus tried for another hour. She took apart the chain of custody, the radio transmissions, Tomas's disappearance — *a man who admits to destroying evidence and fleeing the scene, gentlemen* — and each time Jude answered plainly, refusing to decorate, refusing to flinch. It was not eloquence. It was arithmetic, delivered by a man who had finally stopped hiding from the sum.

At half past four, the board recessed. At ten past six, the chairman returned and read the finding in a voice dried out by a long day: sufficient evidence to refer charges against Marcus Vane and three officers of Vane Riverworks — falsification of navigational surveys, negligence causing death, obstruction. An indictment. Bail denied, given the fleet moored in his name at the harbor and the tide of his money running toward open water.

Vane did not look at Jude as the constables came for him. He looked at nothing, jaw set, a man recalculating. But as they led him past, he slowed — half a second, no more — and his eyes found Jude's, and there was nothing in them but the flat acknowledgment of a debt newly opened. Then the door closed behind him, and the room exhaled.

Outside, the evening had gone gold. Sef was waiting on the steps with Priya and Mei and Odile, and none of them said anything clever, which was its own kind of eloquence. Priya clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to mean it. Odile said, "You'll want a drink," and it was not a suggestion.

Tomas came out last, moving slowly, carrying the document case that had cost him eight months and nearly his life. He stopped in front of Jude on the worn stone steps, in front of everyone, and didn't seem to mind.

"The offer stands," he said. "Survey commission. Your maps. All of them — even the ones you drew to stay away. Especially those." He held out his hand. "Lena's brother is asking. So is mine, if you'll allow it."

Jude looked at the hand. Behind Tomas, the harbor lay calm after the storm, masts swaying, the river running wide and brown and patient toward the sea — the same river he had spent seven years refusing to look at directly, and which had, in six tides, taken him apart and put him back together around different people.

He thought of the map in his cabin. Twenty years of closed lines, drawn in a careful, grieving hand. And beneath it, blank space — page after page of it — waiting for whatever he chose to draw next.

"Yes," Jude said, and took his brother-in-law's hand. "But I'll need a boat."

Odile grinned. "Do I have news for you."
