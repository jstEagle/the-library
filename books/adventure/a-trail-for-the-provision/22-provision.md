## 22. Provision

The river had a new voice this morning, and Jude Eriksen had learned to listen for it the way he'd once listened for rail joints under a locomotive's wheels — each sound a report on the health of something larger than himself.

He stood at the bow of the *Provision* as she came down the last reach before Cazadero, one year and eleven days since they'd run the gorge ahead of the monsoon with Tomas's documents wrapped in oilcloth against his chest. The boat was different now — refitted that first winter, her hull re-planked by Priya's stubborn insistence and Odile's grudging budget, her wheelhouse rebuilt tall enough that Sef could stand at his radio bench without stooping. She wore her scars openly: a patch of lighter wood where the viaduct cable had bitten into the gunwale, a new nameboard because the old one had been half torn away in the storm. But she was the same boat underneath, the way a river is the same river no matter what it carries.

"Channel's shifted again," Mei called from the stern, where she crouched over the sounding line with the patience of someone who had once spent nine days alone in the reeds waiting to be found. "Third sandbar this season. It wasn't there in April."

"It's never there in April," Priya said. She was coiling rope with quick, economical hands, not looking up. "That's why we come back."

"That's why *you* come back," Mei said. "I come back because you people can't read a contour map without me."

Jude smiled and said nothing. This was the liturgy of their mornings now — the same arguments, worn smooth as river stones, repeated in the same order like a prayer neither of them would admit to praying. He marked the sandbar on his chart, penciled it in with the date, and felt the small satisfaction of a man keeping an honest ledger.

His charts were honest now. That was the thing he came back to, over and over, when the work was long and the water was high. Every line he drew meant what it said. Every depth was sounded, every hazard marked, every channel open exactly as far as he claimed it was open. Vane's company had built an empire on charts that lied, and men had died of those lies — Lena among them, though it had taken him seven years and a drowned town to understand that the flood and the falsified surveys were two ends of the same rope. The hearing had untangled it all in public, in the ugly fluorescent light of the maritime board chamber, and Jude had fed his engineering license into that machinery like fuel, watching it burn, and had not regretted it for a single day since.

A man could live without a license. He could not live without a crew.

* * *

Cazadero smelled the way it always did in late summer — diesel and drying fish and the green rot of the floating market — but Jude noticed, tying off at the municipal dock, that he no longer flinched at the smell. For years the port had been a place he passed through with his collar up, a place of telegrams and bad coffee and the particular loneliness of a man who booked single berths. Now the harbormaster waved at him by name. The tea seller on the corner saved him the good leaves. A boy who couldn't have been more than ten ran alongside the dock asking if he was really the engineer from the Provision Route, the one in the newspaper, and Sef leaned over the rail and told him, gravely, that yes, and also that he snored.

"I do not snore," Jude said.

"You snore," said Odile, coming up from below with the manifest tucked under her arm. She was grayer than she'd been a year ago — the storm had taken something out of her hair and given it back as white — but her eyes were the same, sharp and amused and always three moves ahead of the river. "Like a winch with a seized bearing. Ask anyone."

"He does," said Priya.

"He really does," said Mei.

Tomas, hauling the last crate of survey equipment onto the dock, just grinned. His brother-in-law's grin still caught Jude off guard sometimes, still landed somewhere old and tender, because it was Lena's grin too — the same crooked lift on the left side, the same delight in being right about something small. They didn't talk about that. They didn't need to. Somewhere between the ruined mission station and the hearing room and the hundred shared watches since, grief had stopped being a wall between them and become a country they both lived in, and you don't explain your own country to someone who lives there too.

"Mail run?" Odile asked, nodding at the post office.

"There'll be a telegram," Sef said. There was always a telegram. The inland survey board wanted Jude back — the offer had grown more flattering with each refusal, until the last letter had used the word *prestigious* twice — and every few weeks the telegrams came, patient as tide tables, certain that a man of his reputation would eventually want his old life back.

Jude looked at the post office, and then at the boat, and then at the river running brown and strong toward the sea.

"I'll get it tomorrow," he said.

Odile raised an eyebrow. "You never leave mail overnight."

"I know." He shouldered his pack — light, these days; he owned less than he had in Cazadero, and carried less, and needed less. "There's somewhere I have to go first. It's a day's walk up the old spur. I'll be back before the evening tide."

Nobody asked where. That was the other thing about a crew: they knew which doors you kept, and they let you keep them.

Except Tomas. Tomas fell into step beside him at the edge of town, pack on his shoulder as if it had been decided years ago, and said only, "I'm coming."

Jude looked at him. "It's not—"

"I know it's not for me." Tomas's voice was quiet. "But she was my sister. And I've got a year of things to say to her that I couldn't say while we were still saying them to each other instead."

They walked.

* * *

The old spur left the main road two miles out of Cazadero and climbed through scrub pine along the shoulder of the valley, and Jude knew every joint of it with his eyes closed. This was the first line he'd ever mapped after Lena died — walked it end to end in a week of rain because walking it hurt less than driving past the turnoff, because motion was the only analgesic he trusted. He had mapped forty-one closed lines in seven years. He had thought of them as a ledger of grief, each abandoned track a page. It had taken a girl with a radio and no family to tell him what it actually was: avoidance wearing the costume of mourning, miles standing in for years, distance doing the work that grief refused to do.

Sef had been right. Sef, orphaned at nine, who had mourned properly and painfully and then gone on to love people anyway — Sef had looked at him across a galley table in a drowned town and said, *You're not mourning her, you're hiding from her, and she deserved better than that,* and it had cracked something in Jude that had never fully closed, and he had never wanted it to.

The town appeared through the trees the way it always did: gradually, then all at once.

San Verano lay in its shallow valley with the river running through what had been its main street. Half-submerged, the guidebooks said, though there were no guidebooks anymore. The flood seven years ago had taken the lower quarter whole — shops, houses, the church with its blue dome — and the government, in its wisdom, had condemned the rest and moved everyone out rather than rebuild on a floodplain the engineers had sworn was safe. Engineers whose surveys, Tomas's documents had shown, had been bought and paid for by the same company that later falsified the channel charts upstream. The wreck, the flood, the empty town: one continuous lie, drawn in ink that cost nothing and paid everything.

Jude stood at the overlook where the spur's old trestle had crossed the valley, and looked down at the roofs breaking the water like the backs of sleeping animals, and felt — for the first time in eight years — mostly peace.

Not all peace. He suspected he would never have all of it, and he had stopped believing that all of it was owed to him. But the sight of San Verano no longer felt like a wound reopening. It felt like visiting a grave, which is what it was.

"She loved that bell tower," Tomas said beside him. The church's dome was visible above the waterline, streaked with mineral stain, a heron roosting on the cross. "She climbed it when she was fourteen. Abuela nearly killed her. She said you could see the whole valley from up there, and she said—" His voice caught, steadied. "She said she wanted to live somewhere she could see the whole valley. And then the valley came to see her."

"Tomas."

"No. Let me say it here, where I practiced it." He wiped his face with the back of his hand, matter-of-fact, unashamed. "I blamed you. Eight years I blamed you, because you went numb and I went loud, and loud people always think numb people are the ones doing it wrong. You disappeared into your maps and I disappeared into my anger, and both of us left her alone in the middle." He turned to look at Jude directly. "I'm done with that. I wanted to tell her I'm done with that."

Jude found that his own eyes were wet, and let them be. "I hid from this town for seven years," he said. "Took a detour of four hundred miles every time I had business on this side of the range. Mapped closed lines so I'd never have an excuse to drive past the turnoff. And then a rescue contract put me on a boat, and a boat put me on this river, and this river—" he laughed, short and wondering, "—this river goes exactly where I spent seven years making sure I never went. Every current led here. I think maybe it always had."

"The river doesn't take detours," Tomas said. "People do."

They went down into the town together, wading the last stretch in their boots, water warm as bathwater around their knees, past the ghost of a bakery and the ghost of a schoolhouse, to the rise at the town's edge where the cemetery stood on ground just high enough to have stayed dry. The graves were tended — not by any official hand, but by hands; someone came, someone always came, families who had lost the town but not the dead. Lena's stone stood in the third row, granite going soft-green at the base, her name cut deep enough to outlast the weather:

**LENA ERIKSEN**
*Beloved. She saw the whole valley.*

Jude knelt in the grass and took the oilcloth packet from his pack.

He had considered flowers. He had considered it seriously, standing in the chandler's shop in Cazadero two days ago, looking at the paper ones and the plastic ones and the wilting real ones, and he had understood finally why none of them were right. Flowers were for people who could only bring beauty. He had something better. He had brought her the truth.

He unwrapped the packet: a copy of the corrected charts, printed on good stock, bound in canvas. The Provision Route entire — every channel sounded, every shoal marked, every depth verified by his own lead line and Mei's arithmetic and Sef's careful notes. On the title page, beneath the survey numbers, he had written the dedication he'd been carrying in his head for a year:

*For L.E., who taught me that maps are promises. All errors mine, all honesty hers. Open all tides.*

He weighted the charts under the stone's base, where the rain would find them slowly and the river — when the river rose again, as it would, as it must — would know that someone had finally told it true.

"I used to measure my life in track miles," he said aloud, to the stone, to the valley, to her. "Closed lines. Everything behind me, everything shut down. I thought if I counted the miles I wouldn't have to count the years." The heron lifted off the bell tower and crossed the flooded street on slow wings. "I measure in tides now. Odile taught me that. Six tides to save a life, six tides to outrun a storm, and there's always another tide coming. That's the whole secret, Lena. I thought time was something that ran out on me. It doesn't. It comes back. It keeps coming back, and if you're standing in the right boat with the right people, you can ride it."

Tomas put a hand on his shoulder, brief and hard, and said nothing, because there was nothing left that needed saying in words.

They waded back out through the drowned street as the afternoon light went gold on the water, and at the edge of town Jude turned once to look — at the roofs, the tower, the green rise of the cemetery — and found that looking cost him nothing at all.

* * *

The *Provision* was loading when they got back, lanterns already lit against the early dusk, and the evening tide was turning in forty minutes. Odile met them at the gangway with two mugs of tea and an expression that pretended it hadn't been counting the minutes.

"Well?" she said.

"Delivered," Jude said.

"Good." She handed him the mug. "Because there's mail. Two telegrams. One's the inland board again — they've doubled the salary, which means they're getting desperate, which means you should enjoy it."

"And the other?"

Odile's mouth curved. "Read it yourself."

He set down the mug and opened the thin paper with fingers that had rigged cables in a flood and pulled a man's brother out of a ruined mission station, and yet shook slightly anyway.

It was from the maritime board. Short. The new channel authority for the upper river was being chartered — independent, publicly funded, answerable to no shipping concern — and they wanted a chief surveyor who had testified under oath that charts could kill. They wanted, the telegram said, *the man who wrote the Provision Route.* Salary modest. Terms permanent. Berth and vessel provided, or surveyor may nominate his own vessel and crew.

He read it twice. Then he looked up at his boat — at Sef running antenna wire in the rigging, singing something tuneless; at Priya arguing with the harbormaster about docking fees on principle; at Mei correcting the harbor chart pinned to the wheelhouse door with a pencil she'd stolen from him months ago; at Odile watching him with the patience of a woman who had known the answer before he opened the envelope.

"Well?" Tomas asked.

Jude folded the telegram and put it in his pocket, next to his heart, next to the oilcloth ghosts of documents that had changed everything.

"They want the Provision Route," he said. "All of it. The whole river, mapped honestly, forever." He stepped aboard, and the deck rose to meet his feet the way it always did, familiar as a heartbeat. "Tell them yes."

The tide turned. The *Provision* cast off into the brown gold of the evening current, and the river took her the way it takes everything — without judgment, without hurry, keeping its old promise to carry whatever is entrusted to it. Jude stood at the wheel beside Odile and watched the banks slide past, and did not count the miles, and did not count the years.

He counted tides. There were always more coming.
