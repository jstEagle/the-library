## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The freight car smelled of creosote and old fish, which Ulyana Okonkwo considered a fair trade for the price of the ticket, which was nothing. She had wedged herself between a pallet of tar barrels and a crate stenciled LIVE LOBSTER — HANDLE WITH ENVY, and she had been riding the night freight south through the marsh country for three hours, long enough to learn that the lobster was not, in fact, alive, and to be grateful for it. A live lobster would have made the fourth hour difficult.

She had the lantern turned low — just a coin of light cupped in her palm, the way her grandfather had taught her, *a light you can smother with your thumb is a light you can keep* — and under it she unrolled the map.

It was the only thing she had ever inherited. No house, no money, no name worth anything in a bank. Just this: a sheet of drafting linen, soft as cloth now from fifty years of folding, covered edge to edge in her grandfather's hand-inked lines. Railway lines. Every one of them closed.

He had drawn them the way other men drew rivers — with affection, with attention, with little annotations in a cramped engineer's script that slanted uphill when he was excited. *Grade too steep after the flood of '09.* *Trestle sound but the county won't pay for paint.* *Good water here; a town could drink from this.* He had surveyed lines that were never built, lines that were built and abandoned, lines that had been built, abandoned, rebuilt by someone else, and abandoned again out of what Ulyana could only assume was spite. In the margins he'd inked tiny stations, each one labeled, each one a place where somebody had once waited on a platform for a train that stopped coming.

Forty-one towns. She had counted them so many times the number had worn smooth, like a stone in her pocket. Forty-one towns the railway syndicate had cut loose when they closed the lines, and forty-one towns that had dried up behind them like creeks in August.

Ulyana knew all of them by heart. She had worked in nine of them.

That was the thing about being good at everything: you were welcome nowhere in particular. She could drive a locomotive, survey a grade, repair a bridge joint, keep books, fix a boiler, argue a tariff case before a freight commission, and gut a fish if the situation demanded it. She had done all of these in the last six years, moving from dying town to dying town along her grandfather's inked threads, staying exactly as long as her competence was useful and no longer. The pattern was always the same. First they were grateful. Then they were comfortable. Then some councilman's nephew needed the job she was doing better than anyone, and she packed her kit, folded the map along its fifty-year-old creases, and caught the next freight out.

Rootless competence, she called it. Her grandfather would have called it something kinder, but then he had also believed the world would eventually apologize to him, and look how that had gone.

The marsh slid past the gap in the freight car door — black water, black reeds, an occasional startled heron lifting off like a piece of paper someone had thrown away wrong. Somewhere ahead, the line curved toward the coast. She felt it in the rhythm of the wheels, that long lazy lean eastward, and she pulled the map closer to check.

There. The thread of ink that followed the coast, and the small station drawn at the end of it, where the line ran out of land and gave up.

*Rivergate.*

Her grandfather had written more beside Rivergate than beside any other station on the map. Not engineering notes — she'd read those a hundred times, the tidal figures, the warning about the salt rot in the pilings. Beside Rivergate he had written only two words, small and pressed hard enough into the linen that she could feel them with her fingertip:

*Start here.*

He had never told her why. There hadn't been time. There was never time, with him — that was the whole trouble. He had died the way he lived, mid-sentence, mid-survey, halfway through explaining something important, leaving her a map full of beginnings and not one ending.

Ulyana sat back against the tar barrels and let the wheels talk to her. *Not going back. Not going back. Not going back.* The most honest thing a train ever said.

Here was her problem, laid out as neatly as one of his grades: she had worked her way down the map town by town, and each town had taught her something and kept her wages and given her nothing to stay for. Nine towns. Thirty-two left. At her current rate of quiet departures and pre-dawn freights, she would run out of map somewhere around age sixty, at which point she would presumably die in a depot waiting room, and some stationmaster would find among her effects a beautiful useless document and think, *what a strange woman.*

Unless one of these towns was different. Unless one of them had what she was actually looking for, though she'd have been hard put to say what that was. Proof, maybe. Her grandfather had been framed — she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, without needing evidence — blamed for the Meridian Star disaster, ruined, his lines stripped from him while the syndicate's men smiled at the inquiry. But knowing wasn't having. And she was tired of carrying a belief around like luggage. Luggage at least got set down sometimes.

Rivergate. Salvage town, according to everything she'd read in the newspapers she'd scavenged along the way. Built on wreck money and salt rot, half drowned twice a day, run — if the papers were to be believed — by a fleet family named Harrow who owned the harbor the way other people owned opinions: completely, loudly, and without much basis.

And it was the first station on her grandfather's map. *Start here.* Maybe he'd meant it as a surveyor's note. Maybe he'd meant it as advice. With him it was impossible to tell where the engineering stopped and the sermon began.

The train slowed — a junction, probably, or a herd of cows conducting their usual illegal occupation of the right-of-way. In the lull, Ulyana heard voices up the line of cars, two brakemen walking the tops, checking loads. She thumbed the lantern dead and sat in the dark, listening to her own pulse and the tick of cooling tar.

This was the part nobody warned you about, she thought. Not the danger — the danger was fine, the danger was almost restful, because danger came with instructions. It was this. The dark between towns. The moment when you had to admit that the next place might be exactly like the last place, and the one after that, and that a woman could spend her whole life being indispensable in rooms she was never once asked to stay in.

The train gathered itself and rolled on. She relit the lantern.

"Right," she said quietly, to the lobster crate, to the map, to her grandfather wherever he was — probably hovering over some celestial survey, muttering about drainage. "Rivergate."

She took out her pencil — his pencil, stubby, tooth-marked at the ferrule — and made a small mark beside the station. Then, because the mark looked lonely, she added the date. Then, because she was apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone, she added a third item, in her own hand, which was different from his and always would be:

*Last try.*

She didn't entirely believe it. She had written *last try* beside four previous towns, and the map was getting crowded with them. But there was something about the coastal line, the way his ink grew denser toward Rivergate, the letters pressing harder, the margins filling up with figures he'd calculated and recalculated until the linen wore thin. Whatever he'd found there, he'd found it with his whole heart.

The marsh opened ahead. Far off, past the black reeds, there was a smell on the wind that hadn't been there an hour ago — salt, and tar, and fish, and underneath it something older, like stone that remembered being under water. The tide country. They were close.

Ulyana folded the map along its creases, tucked it inside her coat, against the heartbeat, and settled in to wait for dawn and the end of the line.

She did not know — how could she? — that in three days the sea itself would pull back farther than any almanac promised, and open a door that had been shut for fifty years, and that every soul in Rivergate would turn and stare at the stranger stepping off the morning train.

But the map knew. Maps usually do.

* * *

Dawn found the freight crawling along a causeway with water on both sides, gray-pink and flat as hammered tin. Ulyana stood at the open door with her kit bag over her shoulder, hair whipping, watching the rails ahead converge and diverge and converge again — her grandfather's geometry, still holding, even out here where nobody maintained it. That was the thing about good work, she thought. It outlived the people who cared whether it was appreciated.

The brakes sang. Ahead, a water tower leaned over a scatter of rooftops, and a sign swung on its bracket, salt-eaten but legible:

RIVERGATE — POP. 1,204

Someone had crossed out the 4 and written 3 above it. Someone else had crossed out the 3 and written NOTHING WORTH COUNTING. Underneath, in a third hand, smaller: *come see the cave.*

Ulyana read it twice.

"Cave," she said, to the wind.

The train sighed to a stop at the edge of town, and she dropped to the gravel, straightened her coat, and walked toward the roofs and rigging and the smell of low tide — a woman with a kit bag and a folded inheritance, arriving, as her grandfather had instructed fifty years ago, precisely where she was supposed to start.
