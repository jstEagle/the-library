## 2. Arrival at Rivergate

The freight slowed for Rivergate the way old men slow for a doorway they're not sure they still fit through — grudgingly, with a lot of complaint from the couplings, and only because the schedule left it no choice.

Ulyana Okonkwo felt the change before she saw anything of the town. The rails beneath her boots went soft and singing in a new key, that low marsh hum she'd learned to read somewhere south of everywhere, and the smell came through the gaps in the boxcar door: salt first, then rot, then something sweeter under both — pitch, maybe, or creosote, the smell of wood that had spent its whole life arguing with water and losing slowly.

She'd ridden in worse places than a lumber car. She'd ridden in better ones too, but not recently, and not on purpose.

When the train shuddered to its halt at a platform barely long enough to be called one, she swung down with her kit bag over her shoulder and her grandfather's map rolled tight against her spine, wrapped in oilcloth where it had lived for eleven years. The conductor — a boy who couldn't have been more than nineteen and wore his cap like an apology — looked at her the way conductors always looked at women stepping off freights in towns like this.

"You want the station," he said. "This is just where we stop."

"Same thing, isn't it?"

"Not here." He nodded past her shoulder. "Station's been closed since before I was born. We stop out of habit."

Ulyana turned and looked at what habit had built. It was a wooden platform gone silver-gray with salt weather, its boards warped into gentle waves as if the sea had gotten impatient and started working on them early. A sign hung from two chains, reading RIVERGATE in letters that had once been gold and were now the color of old teeth. Beneath it, someone had painted a smaller sign, newer, in careful block letters:

**WELCOME TO RIVERGATE — GATEWAY TO THE BAY**

Someone else had crossed out *GATEWAY* and written, underneath, in a hand that didn't care about being careful: *dead end.*

"Charming place," Ulyana said.

"It grows on you," the boy said. "Like barnacles."

He climbed back up, the freight groaned, and she stood alone on the platform watching the last car slide away into the marsh country, taking with it the last easy way out. She noted this without alarm. It was simply information. Every town was a room, every room had doors, and the first thing she did anywhere was count them.

Habit, her grandfather used to say, is just fear that's learned to be useful. He'd said it about railway signaling, mostly. He'd meant it about people too.

* * *

Rivergate resolved itself as she walked, the way towns do — in layers, oldest first.

The layer nearest the water was the oldest and the richest and the most rotten, all at once. Warehouses leaned together along the harbor front like drunkards holding each other up, their pilings furred green to the high-tide line, their upper stories still proud, still painted, still bearing the names of salvage companies dead fifty years: *Kestrel & Sons. Bayard Deep Works. The Meridian Recovery Company.*

That last one stopped her for half a second. Just half. Then she kept walking.

Above the waterfront, the town climbed a low hill in streets that got progressively less wealthy and progressively more alive. There were houses with gardens losing their argument to salt spray. There was a schoolhouse with a bell tower. There was a church whose steeple had been re-shingled so many times it looked like a layered cake. Laundry lines. Cats. An old man on a porch who watched her pass with the frank, unhurried assessment of someone who had watched everything pass for eighty years and expected to go on doing so.

And above all of it, dominating everything, the cranes.

There were nine of them. She counted without meaning to — counting things was another habit, useful fear — great yellow-and-black lattice towers rising from a fleet of salvage barges moored along the northern quay. They stood against the sky like patient herons, or gallows, depending on your mood, and their shadows lay across the harbor water all morning long, moving as the sun moved, touching every boat in the bay whether those boats liked it or not.

Painted on the nearest barge's hull, in letters tall enough to read from the hilltop: **HARROW DEEP SALVAGE — WE RAISE WHAT OTHERS MOURN.**

"Well," Ulyana said quietly, "that's either a motto or a threat."

"A bit of both," said the old man on the porch, without turning his head. "Mostly the second."

She hadn't realized she'd spoken aloud. She adjusted her bag and kept walking, filing him away — porch, third house past the schoolhouse, hears everything — and filed the fleet away too, in a different drawer. Nine cranes. That wasn't a business; that was a government. Whatever happened in this town, it would happen with Harrow's permission or Harrow's opposition, and she'd need to know which before she needed to know anything else.

Her grandfather's map stayed rolled against her spine, warm from her back, silent. Maps were like that. They told you everything except when.

* * *

The chandlery sat at the crook of the harbor road where it bent inland, a squat stone building with a wooden annex tacked on sometime later, and a sign that read VANN'S CHANDLERY — ROPE, TAR, LAMPS, SUNDRIES. The word *sundries* had been added in different paint, different decade, possibly different century. In the window, arranged with the loving precision of someone who had nothing better to arrange, sat a display of lamp globes, coils of tarred twine, and a single brass diving helmet polished to a shine that suggested it was for sale the way a museum piece is for sale — technically, and never.

Inside smelled of hemp and paraffin and, faintly, of tea. Behind a counter stacked with ledgers stood a woman in her seventies, small and straight-backed, with white hair pinned so severely it looked load-bearing, and eyes the gray-green of harbor water in February.

"We're out of lamp oil," the woman said, by way of greeting.

"I don't need lamp oil."

"Everyone needs lamp oil eventually. It's the first thing people ask for and the last thing they think about until it's dark." She looked Ulyana over — boots, kit bag, rail dust, the particular posture of someone who slept sitting up — and arrived at a conclusion with visible efficiency. "You'll be wanting a room."

"The sign doesn't say rooms."

"The sign says sundries. Nobody's asked after sundries since 1974. Room's upstairs, two dollars a night, three if you want the window that shuts properly. Breakfast's included, which means tea and bread, which means breakfast."

Ulyana considered her. "You're Mrs. Vann."

"I'm Mrs. Vann. How did you know?"

"Lucky guess. You have the look of a woman whose name is on the building."

"My husband put his name on the building. I just refused to take it down." Something moved behind the harbor-water eyes, quick as a fish, and was gone. "Name?"

"Okonkwo. Ulyana Okonkwo."

Mrs. Vann wrote it in a ledger without comment, though her pen paused — a pause so brief anyone less observant would have missed it, a hesitation of perhaps half a heartbeat, there and gone like the fish. "Engineer?" she asked.

Ulyana blinked. "Why would you ask that?"

"Because you walked in looking at my floor joists instead of my merchandise, and because you counted the exits twice — door, window, back hall, and you checked whether the back hall had a second door, which it does, behind the rope rack." Mrs. Vann slid a heavy iron key across the counter. "Railroad people count exits. Sailors count them too, but sailors do it facing the sea. You did it facing the door. Upstairs, end of the hall. Mind the fourth stair; it lies."

Ulyana took the key. Her instincts, which she trusted more than most people trust their own mothers, were sending up two flags simultaneously. The first said: *this woman sees too much.* The second said: *this woman chose to tell you about the second door.* Neither flag knew quite what to make of the other.

"How much for a week?" she asked.

"Twelve dollars, and I'll throw in honesty, which around here costs some people considerably more." Mrs. Vann rang a small bell on the counter, apparently for no reason other than the satisfaction of it. "One piece of advice, engineer, free of charge. The Harrow fleet pays well and asks little, and the little it asks, it asks forever. If you've come to Rivergate for work, work for anyone else. If you've come for something else—" the pen tapped once on the ledger, right beside the name *Okonkwo*, "—then mind the tide tables. Ours run strange."

"Do they?"

"They run stranger every year lately. Ask anyone." She returned to her ledger. "Fourth stair. Tea's at six."

* * *

The room was small and clean and had exactly one window, which shut properly, and from it Ulyana could see nearly everything worth seeing in Rivergate.

She could see the harbor, gray-green and patient, with the nine Harrow cranes standing their vigil along the north quay. She could see the tidal flats beyond the breakwater, vast and glistening at low water, ribbed with sandbars like the bed of some enormous sleeping animal. She could see the roof of the old closed station, the crooked lane down to the water, the tavern with its painted sign (*THE DROWNED ANCHOR*, which struck her as either very honest or very optimistic), and, at the far eastern edge of the flats, a dark seam in the headland that might have been rock and might have been something else.

She set her kit down, unwrapped the map, and spread it on the bed.

Her grandfather's hand-inked lines ran across it like veins — the closed lines, the murdered lines, every route the syndicate had abandoned and buried and written off, each one drawn in that precise, stubborn script she knew better than her own handwriting. And there, near the bottom right corner, almost hidden in the coastal hatching, a small annotation beside the Rivergate headland. Not a line. Just four words, in ink slightly darker than the rest, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the ghost of them with her fingertip:

*Ask the keeper. Trust the ebb.*

She sat with that for a long time while the light moved across the room and the tea downstairs began, presumably, to brew.

Trust the ebb. Her grandfather had died before she was old enough to ask him what he meant by half the things he wrote, which she suspected was deliberate. Men who are framed for disasters learn to write in code. But the keeper — that was now. That was a woman downstairs with a pen that paused at the name *Okonkwo* and eyes that catalogued exits faster than Ulyana did.

Below the window, the tide was going out. She could tell by the boats settling onto their mooring lines, by the widening stain of wet sand on the flats, by the gulls following the water's edge out like scavengers escorting a departing guest. The sea was leaving, and it was leaving further than it seemed to leave yesterday — the sandbars showing bare where, by any almanac she'd ever read, they should still be under a fathom of water.

She made herself a surveyor's note of it, because making notes was how she kept the world from feeling too large: *Low water, day one. Extent unusual. Confirm against local tables.*

Then she heard it — a sound rolling across the harbor that she felt in the floorboards before she understood it in her ears. A bell. Not the church bell, which had a lighter voice. This was deeper, older, coming from somewhere out toward the headland, tolling slow and irregular, like something that had been ringing for a hundred years and had never once been rung for a good reason.

Down in the street, people had begun to come out of their houses. They weren't running. That was the strange part. They were walking — unhurried, grim, pulling on coats — the way people walk toward something they've rehearsed their whole lives and hoped never to perform.

Out on the flats, the water was still withdrawing, farther now, much farther, exposing channels and stones and shapes that had no business being visible, and at the base of the eastern headland, in the deepening shadow of the cliff—

—the whole town turned, as one body, to stare.

Not at the cave. Not yet.

At her window. At the stranger leaning in it.

Ulyana stepped back from the glass, heart tapping out a quick, professional rhythm, and reached for her kit bag with hands that were already, calmly, counting the stairs.
