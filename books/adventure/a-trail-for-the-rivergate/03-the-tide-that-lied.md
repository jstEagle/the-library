## 3. The Tide That Lied

Ulyana Okonkwo had learned to read water the way other people read newspapers: quickly, skeptically, and with an eye for the lies buried in the fine print. So when she stepped out of the chandlery at half past seven the next morning with a cup of coffee she didn't trust and a plan to walk the harbor wall, she noticed immediately that something was wrong with the bay.

The tide was going out.

That, in itself, was unremarkable. Tides did that. It was practically their whole personality. But this was not a going-out so much as a leaving — the water withdrawing from Rivergate's harbor with the hurried, guilty air of a guest who has remembered an appointment elsewhere. The moored fishing boats had settled into their mud skirts, listing gently, and the channel buoys lay over on their sides like tired sentries who had given up pretending to stand watch.

She stopped on the chandlery steps and consulted her watch against the almanac page she'd copied into her notebook the night before. Low water was predicted for 9:41. It was now 7:52, and the harbor was already drier than the almanac promised it would ever be.

"Either your almanac is a liar," she murmured, "or the sea is."

The coffee, she decided, was also a liar, but she drank it anyway. Waste offended her more than dishonesty.

* * *

By eight o'clock, half of Rivergate had gathered along the seawall, and by nine, all of it. Ulyana worked her way through the crowd with the practiced invisibility of a woman who had spent thirty years being the only stranger in rooms full of people who'd known each other since the cradle. She found a spot near the harbor office where she could see the whole swept curve of the bay without being seen much herself.

The sea kept going out.

It went out past the sandbar that the charts marked as the harbor's eastern limit. It went out past the old pilings from a pier nobody living could name. It went out until the water was a thin silver ribbon lying far off toward the horizon like a dropped scarf, and between the town and that ribbon lay acres upon acres of exposed seabed — glistening mudflats, tidal pools steaming faintly in the morning sun, sandbars ribbed like the backs of sleeping animals.

And there, dead center in the emptied bay, stood a cliff face that no chart showed at all.

Ulyana felt her engineer's mind reach for it the way a hand reaches for a railing in the dark. The cliff was maybe forty feet high, black stone streaked with white mineral lines, and set into its base — unmistakable even at this distance — was an arch. A cave mouth, tall as a church door, framed in rock that had been worn smooth and round, the way stone gets worn only by centuries of patient water.

"Mrs. Vann says it's the Tollgate," said a boy beside her, to no one in particular, with the sublime confidence of a nine-year-old delivering scripture. "My gran says her gran paid a penny to look at it when she was little, and her gran said *her* gran did too."

"How often does it open?" Ulyana asked before she could stop herself.

The boy looked up at her, took in her unfamiliar coat and her unfamiliar face, and answered with the frankness of his profession. "Never. Gran says the sea took it back before she was born. But Mrs. Vann keeps the key." He pointed, and Ulyana followed his finger to a small iron-banded door set into the cliff beside the arch — absurd, wonderful, a door with a keyhole the size of a fist. "She charges a coin. Used to, anyway. Before."

Before what, he didn't say, and the crowd around them went quiet in a way that told Ulyana the story had teeth.

She got her first good look at the locals' faces then, and what she saw there rearranged her understanding of the morning entirely. She had expected wonder. Wonder was present, certainly — it hung over the crowd like weather. But underneath it, plain as a seam of coal in limestone, was fear. Not the fear of something new. The fear of something old. The particular dread of people watching a debt come due.

An old fisherman near the harbor office crossed himself, which in a town this salt-crusted and practical meant something.

"The almanac says low water at twenty to ten," said a woman behind Ulyana, in the tone of someone reading a legal document aloud at a funeral. "It's not even nine."

"It's never been like this," someone else said. "Not in my father's time. Not in his father's."

Ulyana opened her notebook and began, quietly, to write down what she saw. Elevations. Distances. The angle of the light on the wet stone. It was habit more than intention; when the world grew strange, she measured it, because a thing measured was a thing that could be understood, and a thing understood could not hurt you quite so badly.

She was sketching the arch when she realized the crowd had stopped looking at the bay.

They were looking at her.

* * *

It happened the way these things always happen — gradually, then all at once. A woman two rows back turned her head. Then her neighbor. Then a knot of men by the harbor office, their conversation dying mid-sentence. The boy beside Ulyana edged away with the tactless honesty of childhood, and suddenly she stood in a small clearing of empty air, and every eye in Rivergate was on her like a lamp trained on a stage.

She knew the arithmetic they were doing. She could almost hear it. *A stranger arrives on yesterday's freight. The next morning, the sea does something it has not done in a hundred years.* Towns like this one ran on stories the way engines ran on coal, and she had just been shoveled into the firebox.

"I'm a railroad engineer," she said, to no question at all, because silence was worse than any answer. "I'm here about the closed lines. The old rail survey."

Nobody moved. Somewhere a gull screamed, with what Ulyana felt was poor timing.

Then the crowd parted, and Mrs. Vann came through it.

Ulyana had glimpsed her once the evening before — a small, straight-backed woman sweeping the chandlery porch with the unhurried authority of someone who owned both the porch and the broom and possibly the street. Now, at the seawall, she wore a long coat the color of wet slate and carried a canvas bag over one shoulder, and she walked through the staring townsfolk as if they were fence posts.

She stopped in front of Ulyana and looked her over from boots to brow with an expression Ulyana couldn't file under any heading she owned.

"You're the one off the night freight," Mrs. Vann said. "Room above the chandlery."

"That's right."

"You came alone."

"I did."

Mrs. Vann nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something she'd already written down somewhere. Then she turned to the crowd and raised her voice just enough to carry across the seawall — not shouting, merely pronouncing.

"Well," she said. "The Tollgate's open. First time since my mother charged a penny a nose, and I'll thank you all to stop gawking at the engineer and start deciding what you're going to do about it."

The crowd made a sound — half murmur, half exhale — and broke apart into clusters, and just like that Ulyana ceased to be the center of the universe and became merely its most recent visitor. She exhaled.

"They're not usually rude," Mrs. Vann said, without looking at her. "Just religious about the wrong things."

"And what are the right things?"

The old woman finally turned those gray eyes on her fully, and Ulyana had the distinct sensation of being weighed — not judged, exactly. Weighed. Like cargo. Like ballast.

"Come and see," Mrs. Vann said. "You'll want to, anyway. Everyone does. And I'd rather you saw it with me standing next to you than have you go poking about on your own and break an ankle on my mudflats. I charge for rescues too."

* * *

They walked out onto the seabed together, and Ulyana's boots found the ground treacherous in the way she'd expected — a skin of firm sand over deep, sucking silt, the kind of footing that forgave nothing and forgot less. Mrs. Vann moved across it like a woman crossing her own kitchen, picking a path without appearing to think about it, and Ulyana followed in her footprints with the humility of the competent learning a new trade.

Up close, the exposed bay was a museum nobody had curated. Old bottles lay in the mud like fallen soldiers. There were timbers — ship's timbers, rail timbers, she couldn't tell which at first glance, though her hands itched to try. A rusted anchor chain ran off toward the ribbon of distant water and vanished under it, pointing at some secret on the far side like an accusation.

And the smell. Low tide anywhere smells of salt and rot, but this was different — older. Brine and iron and something beneath both, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a locked room.

"The last time it opened," Mrs. Vann said conversationally, as though discussing the price of rope, "I was six years old. My mother stood where you're standing and charged a penny apiece, and the line went down the seawall and around the corner past the fish market. People came from three counties. They called it a wonder." Her voice flattened. "Then the sea came back early, and a man named Corrigan drowned in the shallows with three hundred witnesses, and after that the town decided the Tollgate was less a wonder than a bill. Nobody's seen it since. Until today."

"What do you think brought it back?"

Mrs. Vann gave her a sidelong look. "What do *you* think? You've been writing in that little book since you got off the train."

Ulyana considered lying, found she hadn't the energy for it, and settled for precision instead. "Tides this extreme aren't weather. Weather doesn't move that much water. This is either a seismic event or—" she hesitated, "—or something changed offshore. A channel opened, or closed. A bar shifted. The bay's plumbing is different than it was yesterday."

"Hm," said Mrs. Vann, in the tone of a woman filing a phrase away for later use.

They reached the cliff base, and Ulyana tipped her head back. The arch rose above them, black and wet-gleaming, its edges worn to a polish that spoke of unimaginable patience. Cold air breathed out of it, steady as a lung, carrying that locked-room smell again, stronger now. She put her palm flat against the stone and felt it — a faint, rhythmic push of air, in and out, in and out.

"It breathes," she said.

"Everything in this town breathes," Mrs. Vann said. "That's the trouble with it."

Beside the arch, the little iron-banded door stood shut. Up close Ulyana could see it was ancient — riveted plates, hinges gone to orange bloom, a keyhole furred with salt crystal. But the lock itself gleamed. Recently oiled. Recently *used*, her mind supplied, and she filed that away with the rest.

"My mother kept this door," Mrs. Vann said, resting one hand on the iron almost tenderly. "Her mother before her. When the Tollgate closed, we kept keeping it. Fifty years of oiling a lock for a door that leads nowhere, because the sea might change its mind." She smiled, and it was not entirely a warm smile. "This morning it changed its mind. So I suppose I'm back in business."

"What's through the door?"

"The same thing that's through the arch. The Tollgate proper. A tunnel runs back into the cliff — goes farther than any lantern I own has ever shown me. When I was a girl, the water stood waist-deep in the mouth at low tide, and folk would wade in a few yards to say they'd done it, and come out talking about hearing bells." She snorted. "Bells. It's the wind in the chimney cracks. But you'll hear them too, mind. Everyone does. And everyone swears they're real."

"Do you?"

Mrs. Vann was quiet for a moment. Out across the flats, the distant ribbon of the sea seemed to shimmer and thicken, very slightly, very far away.

"I think," she said at last, "that the sea takes something from everyone who goes in there. That's the toll. That's why it's called what it's called. My mother collected pennies at the front door and never once asked what the sea was collecting at the back of it." She turned and fixed Ulyana with those gray eyes. "So. Engineer. You've been measuring my cliff since you got here. What's your professional opinion?"

Ulyana looked at the arch. Really looked. And let herself feel, for one unguarded moment, what she actually felt — which was that the thing was beautiful, and that beauty on this scale was indistinguishable from menace, and that she wanted, with a hunger that startled her, to walk straight through it and find out what was breathing on the other side.

She thought of her grandfather's map, rolled tight in her bag back at the chandlery. She thought of how he had inked every abandoned line in careful brown strokes, and how, along this stretch of coast, there was a gap — a blank space where a line should have run, marked only with a symbol she'd never found in any engineering manual: a small arch, drawn twice, with the words *do not survey* beneath it in his hand.

She had assumed it was a quarry. Or a fault. Or a joke.

Standing here, with cold air breathing against her face out of the dark, she was no longer sure.

"My professional opinion," Ulyana said slowly, "is that this cliff is hollow, and has been for a very long time, and that whatever's inside it, the sea has been keeping it for us."

Something flickered across Mrs. Vann's face — there and gone, quick as a fish turning — and Ulyana would spend a long time afterward trying to decide whether it had been surprise or recognition.

"Hm," said Mrs. Vann again.

Behind them, across the wide and shining flats, a bell began to ring. The harbor bell — Ulyana knew its dull bronze voice by now — tolling out a warning she couldn't yet read. Down the beach, the townsfolk had begun to point, not at the cave, but at the water.

The ribbon of the sea had started to come back.

It came the way it had left — all at once, without apology — a gray wall of water walking up the flats on legs of foam, swallowing bottle and timber and anchor chain, climbing the sandbars two at a time. The crowd on the seawall scattered backward with cries and laughter and one or two genuine screams. In four minutes by Ulyana's watch, the bay that had lain naked all morning was full to the mark again, boats floating, buoys upright, the arch of the Tollgate drowned to its crown behind green water as if it had never been.

As if it had never been — except that three hundred people had seen it, and every one of them was now telling the story to every other one, louder and less accurately with each retelling.

Ulyana stood at the cliff base, water lapping at her boots, her notebook clutched against her chest, doing sums she didn't like.

A tide that lied to the almanac. A door kept oiled for fifty years. A blank space on a dead man's map. And a town full of people who had looked at her — the stranger, the outsider, the one who arrived the day before the miracle — with eyes that asked a question she had no honest answer for.

*Mrs. Vann says it's the Tollgate,* the boy had said. *Everyone who enters owes the sea something.*

The water rose another inch, cold through the stitching of her boots, and Ulyana Okonkwo — skilled everywhere, welcome nowhere, heir to a map and a disgrace — understood with perfect clarity that she was going to go inside anyway.

She just hadn't yet worked out what she'd be willing to pay.
