## 5. Six Tides

Ulyana Okonkwo had been thrown out of better rooms than this one, and she had done the throwing herself on at least two occasions, so she felt qualified to say that the loft above Vann's Chandlery was honest work for a room: one cot, one table with a wobble she could fix in four minutes if anyone would lend her a screwdriver, and one window that looked out over the harbor like an eye that never quite blinked.

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the tide.

That was only partly true. The other part was the nameplate, which kept replaying behind her eyelids in letters of green bronze: MERIDIAN STAR. And beneath it, smaller, half-eaten by fifty years of salt, her grandfather's handwriting — because of course it was his handwriting, because he had signed everything, because a man who inked maps by lamplight until his fingers went the color of tea also painted his name inside a salvage hull where no one was ever meant to read it.

*Why would you sign a ship you didn't own?*

She sat up, lit the stub of candle she'd found in the chandlery's odds drawer, and spread her grandfather's map across the table, weighting the corners with a spool of tarred twine, a brass fitting of no known purpose, and her boot.

The map was beautiful. That was the thing people never understood about engineering — that at its best it was an act of love with numbers in it. Every closed line was drawn in his hand, every abandoned station marked with a small patient cross, and along the margins ran his notes in a script so tidy it looked printed: *gradient 1:90, ballast gone soft after '09 flood, do not trust the culvert at Mile 41.*

He had trusted nothing and everyone, and he had written it all down anyway, so that fifty years later a granddaughter he'd never met could sit in a rented loft over a shop that smelled of hemp and pitch and know exactly how far it was from anywhere to anywhere else.

What the map did not have was tides. Railways, her grandfather had believed, were a conversation with gravity, and gravity was honest. Water was something else. Water negotiated.

So Ulyana got up, went downstairs past Mrs. Vann's shuttered counter — the old woman slept like a sentry, one ear open, Ulyana was sure of it — and let herself out into the blue hour before dawn, when Rivergate smelled least of fish and most of itself: cold stone, wet rope, coal smoke from the Harrow fleet's early fires.

The harbor was empty except for gulls and one boy mending a net who watched her with the frank suspicion of a cat. She ignored him. She had a tide table to steal.

Not steal, precisely. Copy. There was a difference, and the difference was that she left a coin under the harbor office's door with a note reading *for paper and ink,* which was either honest payment or a confession, depending on how the magistrate's morning went.

The harbor master's board stood outside the office under a lean roof, chalked fresh each day: high water, low water, heights, times. It was written for fishermen, which meant it was written for men who wanted to know when the bay would be full enough to float a boat home. Nobody wrote boards for engineers. Engineers had to do their own arithmetic.

Ulyana copied the week's figures onto the back of a chandlery receipt, then stood looking at them for a long moment while the gulls argued overhead about a dead crab.

"Right," she said aloud, to nobody, which was how all her best thinking began. "Let's see what you're hiding."

* * *

Back in the loft, she built the problem the way her grandfather would have: on paper, in pencil, honestly.

First fact: the almanac said yesterday's low water should have been 2.1 meters below datum. The ebb that opened the Tollgate had drained the bay what looked like four, maybe five. She hadn't measured it — she'd been standing in a crowd of tourists watching a sea floor appear like a stage set — but she had spent eleven years surveying riverbeds and she trusted her eye for vertical distance the way other people trusted their mothers.

Second fact: the keeper's board at the cave mouth, which she had photographed with her mind because photographing it with anything else seemed rude, listed the Tollgate's opening schedule as *spring ebbs, twice monthly, weather permitting.* Weather permitting. As though the sea were a railway company announcing delays.

Third fact, and this was the one that made her sit very still: an ebb that deep was not weather. An ebb that deep was a coincidence of forces — spring tide, yes, but stacked with something else. A storm swell offshore pushing water the wrong way. A pressure system sitting on the coast like a fat man on a seesaw. Whatever it was, it was temporary, and temporary things end on schedules.

She worked through the morning, and by noon she had it, drawn out in her grandfather's style of marginal notes because some inheritances skip a generation and land whole:

Six tides. That was the window.

The next low water came tonight, near midnight, but it would be an ordinary low — the anomaly decaying, the bay not draining nearly so far. The one after, tomorrow midday, lower again as the spring tide peaked. Then the third, the fourth — each one shallower than the last as the moon swung out of line and the pressure system moved off to ruin someone else's week. By the sixth tide, three days out, the bay would behave itself again, and the Tollgate would drown, and whatever was inside it would go back to being a legend with a ticket price.

Six tides. Three days. And within those six tides, only two — possibly three — true low slacks deep enough to reach the flooded chamber where the wreck's heart lay.

She drew a little table in the margin, boxes and arrows, the way she planned bridge inspections. Tide one: observe. Tide two: first real window. Tide three: marginal. Tides four and five: the last good windows, shrinking. Tide six: sealed. And underneath it, because she couldn't help herself, she wrote the engineer's prayer her grandfather had scrawled on a hundred drawings: *the sea keeps a schedule; woe to him who shows up late.*

It should have frightened her. Instead she felt the particular cold clarity that had been her companion since she was nineteen years old and had learned that competence was a place to live when no town would have you. The fear was there — she could feel it sitting politely at the edge of the thought, like a stranger waiting for a seat — but the arithmetic came first. Arithmetic always came first. That was the deal she'd made with herself somewhere between her third railway and her ninth, and it had kept her alive through collapses, floods, and one memorable evening in a signal box with a drunk stationmaster and a live rail.

A window, not a plan. Fine. Windows were how plans started.

* * *

The problem, laid out plainly, was this: the wreck lay in a flooded chamber behind the black shelf she'd seen at the ebb. At low slack, the gallery drained to knee-deep at its mouth and perhaps waist-deep at the shelf — she'd judged the gradient by the waterline stains on the walls, old high-tide marks layered like tree rings. But the chamber itself, the part where the Meridian Star sat upright with her holds intact, stayed flooded. The wreck floated in her own private sea, reachable only by going under, and Ulyana Okonkwo could swim about as well as a filing cabinet.

She wrote *learn to dive* on the receipt, looked at it, and crossed it out. You did not learn to dive in three days. You learned to dive in months, badly, and then for years, properly, and even then the sea occasionally reminded you that it had been doing this longer.

So: a diver. Which meant money, or a favor, or a fool, and she had none of the first, no claim to the second, and a strong aversion to being the reason for the third.

Which meant the Harrow fleet.

She sat with that for a while, chewing the end of the pencil. The Harrow cranes dominated the harbor the way cathedrals dominate towns that built them for God and got management instead. Their divers were famous up and down the coast — salvagers, wreckers, recoverers of lost cargo and drowned men. If anyone in Rivergate could put a diver in that chamber inside two tides, it was them.

And if anyone in Rivergate wanted the Meridian Star's manifest to stay exactly where it was, rusting and unread, it was also them. Her grandfather's railway had been broken by men with ledgers, and fleets like Harrow's were ledgers with cranes attached.

"There has to be another way," she told the map.

The map, being paper, declined to argue.

She spent the afternoon doing what she always did when a problem wouldn't move: she surveyed. Out along the shore road, past the toll booth where Mrs. Vann's assistant — a solemn girl of perhaps fifteen named Petra — was already chalking tomorrow's admission prices onto a slate. Ulyana walked the tideline below the cave mouth, boots sinking in the wet sand, and measured things with her eyes and her stride and a length of twine she'd borrowed without asking, which is called requisitioning if you're an engineer.

The cliff face told her things. Cliff faces usually did. There was a seam of softer stone running inland and slightly upward, and where it met the surface, forty yards back from the water, the ground dipped into a shallow depression choked with blackberry bramble and, when she pushed through it, breathing hard and bleeding modestly from both forearms, an iron grate set into the hillside.

Old iron. Rusted lace. And behind it, a darkness that breathed cool air against her face — air that smelled of salt and stone and, faintly, of creosote.

Creosote. On a railway man's nose, that smell was a signature. Treated timbers. Sleepers. Somewhere behind this grate, a tunnel.

Her heart did something unprofessional.

She knelt and peered through the bars, but the dark gave nothing back, and the light was failing, and somewhere down the shore a bell buoy had begun its idiot clanking, which meant the tide was turning. She marked the spot on her copy of the map — a small cross, her grandfather's symbol for *here lies a question* — and made a note: *grated entrance, collapsed?, former rail access? investigate at low water, bring crowbar and better trousers.*

Then she heard footsteps on the shingle behind her, and turned, and found herself looking at a man in a diving sweater who had been watching her with the expression of someone doing sums he didn't like the answer to.

* * *

He was younger than she expected, broad through the shoulders the way men get who haul their own gear, with salt-bleached hair and a face that was trying very hard to be blank and mostly succeeding. He stopped a polite distance away, close enough to talk, far enough to run, and she noted — automatically, uselessly — that he stood between her and the easiest path back to the road.

"You're the one off the night freight," he said.

"I'm several ones," Ulyana said. "I've been here two days."

"The engineer." He said it the way other people in Rivergate had been saying it since the ebb — *the stranger, the outsider, the one who doesn't belong* — but with a different weight on it, an appraising weight, like he was checking her load rating. "Surveying the cliff."

"Admiring the brambles. They're winning."

His mouth twitched, almost a smile, then thought better of it. "That grate's been shut since before I was born. Fleet property." He nodded at the hillside. "Everything under it, too."

"Is that so." She brushed sand off her knees, unhurried, though her pulse had picked up. "And who does the fleet answer to?"

"The sea," he said, "and Adelene Harrow. In that order."

"And you are?"

"Tomas Reinhart." A pause, the kind that carried freight. "Senior diver, Harrow Salvage."

Well, then. Of all the gulls on all the shores in all the world. She kept her face pleasant and her mind moving fast, laying tracks: senior diver, fleet man, standing at a sealed entrance he apparently considered worth walking to and checking on, alone, at dusk. Either the Harrows were thorough to the point of paranoia, or Tomas Reinhart had questions of his own.

"That's a fine sweater," she said, because the fastest way to learn a man was to make him talk about something he loved. "Guernsey pattern? My grandfather had one. Said a proper knit could keep you warm wet, which he claimed was the entire philosophy of coastal life."

Something shifted in his face — surprise, then a guarded warmth, the look of a man hearing an accent he thought had died out. "My grandmother knitted it. And that's about the philosophy, yes." He glanced at the grate, then back at her, and the appraisal in his eyes sharpened rather than softened. "You should know the fleet's putting divers on the Tollgate. Formal claim, filed this morning. Whatever's in that cave belongs to Harrow Salvage by law of salvage, and the magistrate's already stamped it."

"Already," Ulyana said. "That was fast."

"Adelene Harrow doesn't wait for tides." He said it flatly, but she caught the undertow in it, the small disturbance beneath a dutiful surface, and filed it away next to the cross on her map.

"Well," she said lightly, stepping past him toward the road, giving him a nod as she went, "then I'll just admire the brambles elsewhere. Good evening, Mr. Reinhart."

"Miss—?"

"Okonkwo," she said, and watched the name land. Not recognition — he was too young for that — but the flicker of a man who'd grown up on stories and had just heard one of them walk past him in the flesh.

She walked back along the shore road with the blood still trickling cheerfully down her forearms and her mind running the numbers all over again, faster now, colder.

Six tides. Two good windows. One flooded chamber. One fleet with a legal claim, a magistrate in its pocket, and a stable of the best divers on the coast. One grated tunnel that might be nothing at all, or might be the only door in Rivergate that Adelene Harrow didn't hold a key to.

And one diver who had come out at dusk to stand in front of a sealed grate, alone, for no reason the fleet would have ordered.

Back in the loft, she added a line to the margin of the map, beneath her grandfather's tidy hand, in her own less certain one:

*Tide two opens tomorrow, midnight minus forty minutes. Find the second door. Watch the diver.*

Below that, after a moment's thought, she added the family motto, the one her grandfather had inked on the title page of every survey he ever sold:

*The sea keeps a schedule.*

She blew out the candle. Down in the harbor, the Harrow cranes stood black against the rising water, and somewhere among them, a senior diver was presumably telling his employers everything he'd seen — including, she suspected, whether he'd seen her.

She would know by morning which way that sum had come out.
