## 6. The Harrow Claim

Tomas Reinhart had been diving for the Harrow fleet since he was seventeen, which meant he had spent eleven years learning the precise difference between what he saw and what he reported.

It was not lying, exactly. Lying was a word with teeth in it, and Tomas had never liked things with teeth. What he did was gentler. He reported what was useful. He omitted what was complicated. The sea was full of complicated things, and a diver who dragged all of them up onto the deck of a salvage barge would drown in paperwork before he ever drowned in water.

He had seen the second entrance at dawn, three hours before the briefing, and he had been carrying it around inside his chest like a swallowed stone ever since.

* * *

The Harrow fleet office sat on stilts above the harbor, in a building that had been a fish cannery, then a cannery office, then a bank, then a cannery again, and now pretended to be none of these things. The walls were paneled in wood that had gone the color of old tea. A model of the flagship *Providence Harrow* hung from the ceiling on wires, and every few minutes the harbor breeze through the propped door set it rocking gently, as if the little ship were still trying to leave.

Adelene Harrow stood at the head of the long table with her back to the window, which meant the morning light came through her and turned her into a silhouette with a voice. She did not need her face for this part. Her face was for the moments that required it.

"The Tollgate," she said, and the whole room went quiet the way a room does when money says its own name.

There were nine of them around the table. Four fleet divers, two survey men, the harbormaster's nephew who handled permits, an old hull-wright named Brannigan who had been with the fleet longer than Adelene had been alive and made no secret of it, and Tomas. Coffee went around in tin mugs. Nobody drank it. Drinking made noise, and noise suggested you had nothing better to do with your mouth than live.

"Yesterday's ebb drained the bay past anything in the almanacs," Adelene continued. "The old cave mouth on the north point — the one this town has been selling tickets to for a hundred years — opened all the way down. My survey skiff got eyes on iron inside. Wreck iron. Deep iron."

She let that sit. Adelene Harrow was very good at letting things sit. Tomas sometimes thought she could have made a fortune in real estate simply by standing in rooms and not speaking until the other party signed something.

"Brannigan. You were a boy when the stories started. Tell them what the stories say."

Brannigan, who was seventy-three and had the face of a man who had been told a great many stories and believed perhaps four of them, cleared his throat. "They say a salvage ship went down in there fifty-odd years back. The *Meridian Star*. They say she wasn't wrecked so much as *put* down. And they say whatever she was carrying, somebody on land wanted her carrying it straight to the bottom."

"And what do you say?"

Brannigan shrugged, a slow geological event. "I say the sea keeps what it's paid to keep. And I say somebody's finally stopped paying."

Adelene turned from the window. The light let go of her, and there was her face after all: composed, gray-eyed, patient in the way of a woman who had inherited a fleet and intended to bequeath an empire. "The wreck sits inside the cave in a flooded chamber. The chamber is reachable at low slack — a window of maybe forty minutes, twice a day, and the almanac says the cave seals for good within a week of tides. Six tides, the old keeper claims. Mrs. Vann has been charging tourists a coin to look at that cave mouth since before I was born, and if she says six, I'm inclined to believe her, because that woman has never once rounded down in her favor."

A ripple of something that was almost laughter went around the table. Almost. Nobody wanted to be the one who laughed at the wrong moment in this room. Tomas had seen a diver named Pell do it three years ago — laughed at a joke about the insurance rates — and Pell now worked a fish boat out of Corrick's Landing and was never spoken of.

"So we move now," Adelene said. "Permits are filed. The magistrate is — " she paused, choosing, " — sympathetic. I want the chamber surveyed, the wreck assessed, and a salvage claim posted on the cave mouth before the second tide. Whatever's down there belongs to the fleet by right of discovery, and I intend for the paperwork to agree with me."

She looked around the table, and her eyes came to Tomas last, which was habit, not suspicion. He was the best diver she had. They both knew it. It was one of the few things they had never needed to say out loud.

"Reinhart. You were on the point this morning. Report."

Here it was, then. The stone in his chest, and the table waiting for him to cough it up.

* * *

Tomas had gone out at first light to run a preliminary look at the cave mouth, because that was what he did — he was always the first one in the water and the last one to make claims about it. The ebb had been extraordinary. The bay had pulled back like a sheet drawn off furniture, exposing mudflats and old pilings and things the town had thrown off its docks for a century, and there at the north point, the Tollgate stood open like a held breath.

He had swum the mouth. That was standard. You never sent a diver into an unexamined entrance; you sent *Tomas*, because Tomas came back.

The cave went down and back at an angle, the floor shelving into black water that still held the last of the ebb. And there, in the beam of his lamp: ribs. Iron ribs, furred with fifty years of barnacle, curving up out of the gloom like the skeleton of something that had once been proud. He'd run his glove along a plate and felt the old rivet lines, the workmanship of a different decade. And on a section of hull that the silt had spared, letters.

He hadn't been able to read them all. But he'd read enough.

Then, on his way out, following the curve of the rock with one hand because the tide had started to turn and the cave had begun, very softly, to inhale — he had found the fissure.

It was on the landward side, up where the rock met what the maps called nothing at all. A crack wide enough for a man, angling up and back, breathing cold air against his face. He'd edged into it maybe ten meters, lamp held high, and the crack had opened into a low gallery — dry, or mostly dry, floored with old gravel and something that clinked.

Rail spikes. Dozens of them, scattered like dropped coins. And under the gravel, half-buried, the rusted twin lines of track, running back into the hill.

A tunnel. An old rail tunnel, collapsed somewhere inland, running out to the cliff above the cave — which meant that somewhere above the waterline, the *Meridian Star* had a back door.

He had lain in that gallery for a long moment, breathing the cold air, listening to the sea inhale behind him, and he had thought: *this changes everything.* A second entrance meant access that didn't depend on the tides. It meant the wreck could be reached before the fleet's survey, after it, or — his mind had gone down this path with the smooth inevitability of water finding a low place — *by someone who wasn't the fleet at all.*

And then he had thought: *that is not my problem.* And he had swum out, and climbed the point, and walked to the office with the stone in his chest, and now Adelene Harrow was looking at him with her gray eyes and her patient face, and the whole table was waiting.

"The mouth is viable," he said. "Shelving floor, good depth at low slack, maybe eight fathoms in the chamber pool. The wreck's intact — iron hull, riveted, pre-war build. I got a hand on her. She's solid."

"Name?"

"Plate was silted. I'd want a second look with the lamp rig." This was true. It was also not the whole truth, and Tomas felt the difference in his mouth like a bad oyster — not enough to spit out, too much to swallow clean.

"Condition of the entrance?"

"Narrows at the throat. One diver at a time, and only on the slack. The tide turns fast in there — the whole cave breathes with the bay. You go in on the ebb, you come out on the flood, and you don't argue with it."

Adelene nodded, satisfied, and turned to the survey men, and Tomas sat back and drank his coffee, which had gone cold, and found it tasted exactly right.

*Why?* asked a small voice in him, the voice that sounded like his mother, who had raised him on the fleet's payroll after his father drowned on it, and who had taught him to tie his first bowline and to keep his word. *Why keep it back?*

And Tomas, who had an answer for everyone, had no answer for her. He only knew that when he'd stood in that dry gallery with the rail spikes clinking under his boots, he had felt something he hadn't felt in eleven years of diving: that he had found something the sea had hidden on purpose, and that some things hidden on purpose were meant to stay that way until the right person came asking.

He did not know who the right person was. He only knew it wasn't Adelene Harrow, and it wasn't him.

* * *

The briefing broke up at nine. Brannigan clapped him on the shoulder in the doorway — a blow that would have felled a smaller man — and said, "Good report, boy. Short and dry. You'd have made a fine undertaker."

"I like to leave the drama to the water."

"The water's got plenty." Brannigan squinted at him with his old milky eyes. "You left something out."

Tomas kept his face the way he kept his gear: maintained, inspected, nothing loose. "Like what?"

"Don't know. That's what's interesting about it." And the old man shuffled off down the stilted walkway, whistling something tuneless, leaving Tomas to stand in the morning sun and feel, for the first time in his dutiful life, seen.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he always did between dives: maintenance. The fleet's equipment shed sat at the landward end of the harbor, and Tomas worked in it the way other men prayed — methodically, with his hands, saying nothing. He checked the seals on his mask. He packed the lamp rig with fresh batteries. He coiled line, tested regulators, and greased the winch, and all the while the second entrance sat in his head like a splinter working toward the bone.

The thing about a splinter, he thought, was that it never got better on its own. It either came out or it festered. And he was beginning to suspect that he was not the kind of man who could pull it out himself.

At two o'clock, Adelene came to the shed.

She did that sometimes — appeared without warning, in her gray coat, with her hands clasped behind her back like a general inspecting a front she trusted. She watched him work for a while. Tomas kept working. The two of them had a long history of comfortable silence, and he had always treasured it, and today it felt like a rope with weight on the other end.

"You were down there alone this morning," she said.

"Standard practice."

"Standard practice is a look from the skiff." She picked up a spare mask from the bench, turned it over, set it down. "You went in."

"The mouth was open. I had the window. It would've been a wasted tide otherwise."

"Mm." She set the mask down and looked at him, and there it was — the face, the real one, the one that had signed his first contract when he was seventeen and had never once, in eleven years, given him cause to regret the ink. "You know what's in that wreck, Tomas."

"I know there's a wreck."

"You know what the stories say is in it. And you know what it would mean to this fleet if we're the ones who bring it up. My grandfather built this fleet on salvage. My father built it into a company. I intend to build it into an institution. Institutions need foundations, and that wreck — whatever's in her hold — is a foundation." She said it without heat, which was the frightening thing about Adelene. Other people wanted things loudly. She wanted things the way tide wanted the shore.

"The survey team goes in on the second tide," she went on. "You lead it. I want that wreck named, measured, and claimed by Friday. Whatever's in her belongs to whoever posts first, and we will post first."

"And if the paper trail says otherwise?" It was out of his mouth before he could weigh it, and he watched her stillness change quality — not anger, but attention, the way a cat attends.

"What paper trail?"

"Old wreck. Old stories. There's always paper."

"There's always *paper*," Adelene agreed, "and then there's always a magistrate, and the magistrate in this town has been on the fleet's account since before I could sign my name. The paper will agree with us, Tomas. It always does." She smiled then, briefly, and it was a real smile, which was worse. "That's why I keep the fleet's word good. That's why I pay the divers well and the widows better. So that when the paper and the truth sit down at the same table, everyone in this town knows which one to believe." She tapped the bench once, twice, with one finger. "You've never given me cause to doubt you. Don't start on a Tuesday."

She left. Tomas stood among the coiled lines and the hanging masks and listened to her footsteps fade down the stilted walk, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water in the ears, exactly what he had just been told.

*The paper will agree with us.* Not *the truth will out.* Not *the law will decide.* The paper would agree with the fleet because the fleet would make it agree, and the whole apparatus — the magistrate, the permits, the claim, the widows paid better than anyone — existed to make that agreement look like justice.

His father had died on this fleet's payroll, and the fleet had paid for the funeral, and paid for his schooling, and paid him a diver's wage that no fish boat in the county could match, and Tomas had never once thought of any of it as a debt because it had all arrived so gradually, so reasonably, the way sand arrives in an oyster. But standing in the shed with the splinter working toward the bone, he felt the grain of it for the first time. The pearl around it was duty, and it was real, and it was heavy, and it had been built around something that was never supposed to be there at all.

* * *

He went out to the point at dusk, alone, and sat on the rocks above the Tollgate with his boots in the salt grass, and watched the tide come back in.

The cave mouth was closing. Slowly, the way a sleeper's mouth closes — the water rising up the throat of it, swallowing the iron ribs inch by inch, taking the wreck back into the dark it had kept for fifty years. In six tides, Mrs. Vann's sign said, it would shut entirely, and whatever was inside would belong to the sea again, or to whoever the sea's paperwork favored.

Tomas sat there a long time, doing the thing he was best at, which was waiting. And in the waiting, he turned the splinter over and over, and finally admitted what it was.

He hadn't kept the second entrance back to protect the wreck. He'd kept it back because of the rail spikes. Because a collapsed rail tunnel running to a wrecked salvage ship was not an accident, and a wreck that was *put* down, as Brannigan said, was not weather. Somebody, fifty years ago, had run a line into that cliff and sunk a ship at the end of it, and that somebody had built a back door — and back doors were built by people who expected to need one.

And Tomas Reinhart, who had spent eleven years reporting what was useful and omitting what was complicated, had looked at that back door and felt, for the first time, that he was standing on the wrong side of it.

The tide swallowed the last of the ribs. The Tollgate closed its mouth, and the bay went smooth and ordinary, and the first lamp of the chandlery across the harbor came on, and then the second, and then a string of them, the whole town lighting up against the dark like a fleet at anchor.

He was still sitting there when he saw the light move on the north point — not a lamp from town, but a small, careful, handheld beam, sweeping along the rocks just above the closed cave mouth. Somebody was out there in the dark, walking the point, measuring the ground with their eyes the way a certain kind of person does.

Tomas knew exactly one kind of person who surveyed ground at night by lamplight, and it wasn't a tourist, and it wasn't a fisherman, and it wasn't one of Adelene's people, because Adelene's people carried their lights in pairs and their opinions in triplicate.

He stood up slowly in the salt grass, and told himself he was going down there to identify a stranger on fleet-adjacent ground, which was, after all, his duty.

The stone in his chest, he noticed, had shifted — and for the first time all day, it didn't feel like something he'd swallowed.

It felt like something trying to get out.
