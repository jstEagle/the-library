## 7. A Diver on the Platform

Ulyana had been awake since four, which was not unusual, and lying about why since four-fifteen, which was also not unusual, though she was getting worse at it.

The chandlery's spare room had a window that faced the harbor, and the harbor at dawn had a color she had no name for — not grey, not green, something between pewter and a held breath. She lay in the narrow bed and watched the water and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the Tollgate opened: six tides, minus one already spent staring, minus whatever the almanac was lying about.

By six she was dressed, and by six-thirty she was on the old timber platform above the cave mouth with a surveyor's level balanced on a tripod she'd built out of a broom handle, two chandlery crates, and a spirit level borrowed — she intended to return it — from the landlady's husband's fishing tackle. The morning was cold enough to make her breath visible, and the bay was still drained, the sea floor exposed in long ribbons of bladderwrack and old rope, the Tollgate's black mouth breathing out cold air that smelled of iron and old salt.

She worked the way her grandfather had taught her to work, which was to say quietly, precisely, and with the assumption that someone was always watching.

He'd been right about that, as it turned out.

"You've got your datum wrong."

The voice came from behind her and below her, off the platform stairs, and Ulyana did not jump, though she would later maintain to herself that the level had simply shifted. She turned with the unhurried dignity of a woman who had absolutely not been startled.

A man stood at the foot of the stairs with a canvas bag over one shoulder and his diving boots in his hand, carrying them the way dockworkers carried boots they'd decided the day had not yet earned. He was maybe her age, maybe a little older, with the particular build of someone who spent his working life being squeezed by water — broad through the shoulders, economical everywhere else. His hair was dark and cut short, and he had the expression of a man who had rehearsed an opening line on the walk over and was now mildly annoyed that it had landed.

"Your datum," he said again, when she didn't answer. "The benchmark you've set on the harbor stair. You've measured from the top step, but the top step moves."

"Steps don't move."

"That one does. It's not a step. It's a hatch cover somebody welded over an old sounding tube in 1911, and it flexes with the tide. Every survey done off that step since the war has been out by four inches, and everyone in this town knows it except, apparently, you." He said it without malice. It was the tone of a man reporting weather.

Ulyana looked at the step. She looked at her tripod. She thought about the two hours of careful measurements she had taken off that step.

"Four inches," she said.

"Four and a bit, at spring range. Less today." He started up the stairs, unhurried, and set his boots down by the rail. "You're the engineer."

"I'm a woman standing on a platform."

"You're the woman who asked Mrs. Vann for the cave's soundings, and when she said there weren't any, you asked her for the *tide tables*, and when she said those were public, you asked her who'd surveyed the harbor stair." He reached the top and looked at her level with the frank professional interest of one tradesman assessing another's tools. "Broom handle."

"Crates."

"I can see the crates." He crouched and looked at her setup from the side, head tipped. "It's not bad, actually. The tripod's ugly but the geometry's sound. Where'd you train?"

"That's a long answer to a question you haven't earned."

He smiled at that, briefly, like a door opening and shutting in another room. "Fair." He straightened. "Tomas Reinhart."

"I know."

Something moved through his face — not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. "Right. Of course you do. The whole harbor knows the Harrow divers by now."

"I know you because you were on the *Petrel*'s deck Tuesday when the Tollgate opened, and you were the only man on that boat who looked at the cave instead of the crowd." She turned back to her level. "Everyone else looked at each other. You looked at the water and did math. I recognize the face. I make it myself."

"That's a very specific thing to say to a stranger."

"We're not going to be strangers long, Mr. Reinhart. You're here to find out what I'm doing, and I'm going to let you, because I want to know what you'll do about it."

He laughed — one short, genuine note that seemed to surprise him as much as her. "Well. That's honest, anyway."

"Honesty's cheaper than lying well. I never practiced."

She went back to her measurements, deliberately, giving him the choice: stay or go. He stayed. She heard him set his bag down, heard the creak of the rail as he leaned on it, and then — this was the part she noted, filed, and turned over later — heard him *watching her work* without interrupting. There were perhaps six people alive who could stand next to a survey in progress and not talk. Her grandfather had been one. She'd assumed the supply died with him.

The Tollgate breathed below them. At this hour, with the ebb still holding, the cave mouth stood clear of the water by a full fathom, a black arch in the exposed seabed, and the light coming up over the eastern headland reached just far enough in to show the first of the iron ribs before the darkness took over. Somewhere in that darkness, forty yards and one flooded gallery back, lay a ship with her grandfather's handwriting on the hull.

She did not look at the cave. She was proud of that. She looked at the level, and the stadia rod, and her notebook, and she wrote numbers in her small square hand, and if her pen pressed a little harder than the numbers required, that was between her and the paper.

"Your tide tables are wrong," Tomas said, after a while.

"They're the Admiralty tables."

"They're the Admiralty tables *for a normal year*. This isn't a normal year. You've noticed the ebb running long."

"I've noticed the ebb running *impossible*. The almanac says low water at 5:12. It's 7:40 and the Tollgate's still dry."

"Mm." He came over and, without asking, took the pencil out of her hand — took it, she noted, the way a colleague takes a pencil, not the way a thief takes one, and that distinction mattered more than she wanted it to — and flipped her notebook back two pages. "You've been computing your windows off the standard curve. Look." He sketched, freehand, quick and clean: a tidal curve, and then a second one beside it, stretched. "The bay's not draining on schedule. Something's changed the basin. Either the bar at the harbor mouth has shifted, or—" he paused, pencil hovering, and she watched him decide how much to give her, watched him decide, and give it anyway, "—or the Tollgate's open at the far end. Both mouths breathing. The cave isn't just a cave right now. It's a channel. The whole bay is draining *through* it."

Ulyana stared at the sketch.

Because it explained everything. It explained the unprecedented ebb. It explained the cold air breathing out of the arch. It explained why the locals had charged a century of tourists to look at a hole that had never, in living memory, opened like this. The sea had taken the Tollgate off its hinge, and every hour it stayed open was an hour the wreck's flooded chamber sat a little lower, a little more reachable, a little more *hers* — and every hour after that was an hour closer to the sea changing its mind and shutting the door with whatever was inside still inside.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out level. She was pleased about that too. "How long before it closes."

"That's the wrong question." He handed the pencil back, handle first, which she also noted. "The right question is: how long before it closes *without warning*. A channel like this scours. The tide's cutting the floor of that cave wider every cycle, and when the bar shifts back — and it will, bars always shift back — the flow reverses and the mouth chokes with its own debris in a single tide. Not six tides of warning. Maybe four. Maybe three. And the last one won't look different from the others until it's too late to be inside."

"You've been in."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't pretend it was. He was quiet for a moment, looking at the arch.

"Tuesday," he said. "First day. Before the fleet had its meeting, before anyone told me what I was allowed to know. I swam the outer gallery on my own hook." He said it flatly, but she heard the weight under it, the sound of a man setting something down that he'd been carrying carefully. "There's a wreck in there. Iron ship, big. Salvage-grade condition, which is to say she's dying but she's dying slowly. Nameplate's legible." He looked at her then, straight on, and his eyes were the flat grey-green of the harbor at dawn. "Meridian Star."

The name went through her like a current through a rail. She kept her face still. She had gotten very good, over the years, at keeping her face still in exactly the moments that mattered, and she hated that she'd had to get good at it, and she did it anyway.

"Local legend," she said. "Half the town's selling postcards of it."

"The other half's selling silence. I've met both halves." He turned back to the cave. "Here's my problem, engineer. I have a contract. It's a good contract, the best a diver in this country can hold, and it says everything under that water that isn't nailed to a private deed belongs to the Harrow fleet, and everything that is nailed to a private deed belongs to the Harrow fleet too, because Adelene Harrow wrote the contract. I'm bound by it. I've been bound by it since I was nineteen and it fed me when nothing else would." A pause. "And this morning I stood on the *Petrel*'s deck and listened to Adelene's foremen talk about the Meridian Star like she was a load of scrap copper, and I found myself doing math about how much of what I know I could avoid telling them."

"What did you decide?"

"I told them about the cave. I didn't tell them about the wreck's condition, or the layout of the galleries, or the fact that the inner chamber is reachable at low slack through a flooded gallery that their equipment can't transit." He said it evenly, but his knuckles had gone pale on the rail. "So I've already lied to them by omission, which means I'm already a dishonest man, which means—" he gestured, at her, at the broom-handle tripod, at the whole ridiculous morning, "—apparently I spend my mornings correcting the tide tables of strangers."

"Is that what you're doing?"

"It's what I'm telling myself I'm doing." He looked at her. "You're after something in that wreck. Something specific. You didn't come to Rivergate because of a legend; you came because of the *nameplate*, and you knew the name before you got here, and I'd very much like to know how, because the manifest of the Meridian Star has been sealed for fifty years and the only people who'd care about it are dead, ruined, or named Harrow."

The wind came off the drained seabed, cold and smelling of weed and old iron, and below them the Tollgate breathed its long slow breath.

Ulyana weighed it. That was the truth of the morning, she thought: they were both standing at a rail weighing things. He had a contract and a conscience, and she had a map and a grandfather, and each of them was holding out exactly the thing the other needed — he needed to know what she knew, because it was the only honest thing left in his week; she needed what he knew, because the sea kept a schedule and she'd just learned the schedule was shorter than the almanac said.

"I'll trade you," she said.

"Trade what?"

"Your tide corrections are worth something. I'll pay for them. Not with money." She closed her notebook. "I'll tell you one true thing now, and one true thing later, and you can decide at each step whether I'm worth the risk. First true thing: my grandfather was Silas Okonkwo."

She watched the name land. She watched him not pretend it hadn't.

"Okonkwo," he said. "The railway man. The one they—" He stopped. "The one who was blamed for the Meridian Star."

"The one who was *framed* for the Meridian Star. There's a difference, and the difference is sitting in a strongbox forty yards inside that cave, and I have six tides — no, thank you, three or four, per your arithmetic — to prove it." She held his eyes. "Second true thing comes when I decide you're not going to hand me to Adelene Harrow. And you should know that I will be deciding slowly, because I've been handed to people before."

Something in his expression shifted — not softening exactly, more like a load redistributing. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man finding out the stranger on the platform has also been carrying something heavy for a long time and has also gotten good at not showing it.

"They closed his lines," Tomas said. "After the wreck. My father worked one of them. The Marrow Vale branch. Closed it within the year and the whole valley went dark." He said it simply, without performance, and Ulyana understood that he had not planned to say it, and that it had cost him something, and that he knew it had just made them allies whether he liked it or not.

"Then you know what the map is worth," she said.

"What map?"

She almost smiled. "That's the second true thing. Not yet."

He laughed again — that same surprised note — and shook his head, and looked out at the cave, and for a while neither of them said anything, and it was, strangely, not an uncomfortable silence. It was the silence of two people reading the same drawing from opposite ends.

"The step," he said finally. "Move your datum to the third step up, the granite one. It hasn't moved since it was cut. And re-run your numbers with the stretched curve — I'll write it out for you, the correction factors. They won't be pretty. Your windows are going to be shorter and less predictable than any table says, and if you're planning to go in that cave, you'll want to know that *before* the water tells you."

"Why?" she said. "Why help me? You don't trust me. I can see it. You think I might be exactly the kind of person who'd burn you to Adelene the moment it's convenient. You might be right to think it. So why stand here correcting my tables?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he picked up his boots, and looked at the Tollgate, and said:

"Because twenty years from now, when someone asks me what I did the year the sea opened the Tollgate, I'd rather not have to lie about the whole thing. I'm already lying by omission. It's the first lie I've told that job, and I find I'd like to keep it the *only* one, which means the rest of what I do has to be clean." He slung the bag over his shoulder. "You're the cleanest thing I've found all week. That's not trust, engineer. That's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic I can work with," Ulyana said. "Trust takes longer."

He was halfway down the stairs when she called after him — because there was one more thing, and because she had decided, in the way she decided things, all at once and irreversibly.

"Mr. Reinhart. The chandlery keeps its old shipping ledgers in the back room. Fifty years of them. If the Meridian Star's last cargo was ever written down anywhere on paper that didn't sink — it'll be there."

He stopped on the stairs and looked back up at her, and she watched him understand what she was telling him: *I'm going to the ledgers. I'm telling you I'm going. Decide what that makes you.*

"Chandlery closes at six," he said. "Old Mrs. Okoye locks the back room, but the window latch hasn't worked since the flood year." A beat. "So I'm told."

"By whom?"

"By nobody. I'm a diver. We don't know anything about windows." And he went down the stairs with his boots in his hand, and Ulyana stood alone on the platform with her corrected tide tables and her shortened arithmetic, and looked at the black arch breathing in the drained bay, and felt the strange, unsettling, entirely new sensation of having told someone the truth and not immediately regretted it.

Three or four tides. A locked back room. A nameplate with her grandfather's hand on it.

She struck the tripod, packed the level, and went to find out what the chandlery's ledgers knew about the night the Meridian Star died.
