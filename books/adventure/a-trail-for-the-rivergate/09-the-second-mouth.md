## 9. The Second Mouth

Tomas Reinhart had told exactly one lie in his working life before this week, and it had been to his mother, at age nine, regarding the whereabouts of a pie. He still thought about that pie sometimes. The way guilt settles on a man like silt — soft at first, then compacting into something you have to dig through.

Now he was carrying a second lie around like ballast, and it was heavier than the first one had been by several hundred pounds.

*You haven't seen another entrance,* he'd told Adelene Harrow's foreman, and the words had come out smooth as oiled chain, which frightened him more than if they'd come out crooked.

So when he found Ulyana Okonkwo at dawn on the seawall, hunched over her grandfather's map like a woman reading a will, he made a decision that would have gotten the nine-year-old pie-thief disowned.

"You're surveying wrong," he said.

She didn't startle. He'd noticed that about her — she registered people the way she registered weather, as data arriving on schedule. She looked up, and her eyes did the thing they always did: took his measure, found him wanting, and moved on without wasting the effort of disappointment.

"I'm not surveying," she said. "I'm reading."

"Same mistake either way." He crouched beside her and tapped the map where the old closed line bent inland from Rivergate, up toward the bluffs. "Your tide tables assume the cave floods and drains through one mouth. It doesn't."

Her pencil stopped moving.

"The Tollgate breathes," he said. "In and out. Two mouths. And your tables are off by eleven minutes because you don't know where the second one is."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. The harbor was waking behind them — the Harrow cranes swinging their slow morning arcs, gulls arguing over fish guts, the whole salt-rotted machinery of Rivergate grinding into gear. Somewhere in that fleet was a foreman who believed Tomas had reported everything he'd seen.

"I know where the second one is," she said quietly.

That stopped *him*. "You what?"

She turned the map toward him. Her grandfather's hand-inked lines ran everywhere the railway had once run and no longer did — a spiderweb of abandonment, each dead spur annotated in a cramped, beautiful script. But here, inland from the bluffs, there was a gap. A blank stretch where a tunnel should have been, if the line continued the way every other line on the map continued.

"He never marked it," she said. "Fifty years of mapping every closed line on this coast, and right here—" she touched the blank, "—nothing. I thought it was damage. Water stain. But look at the paper." She tilted it toward the light. "It's not stained. It's *clean*. Somebody kept this section clean. Or kept something off it."

Tomas felt the strange vertigo of watching two halves of a secret approach each other. "The line runs into the bluff," he said. "Collapsed portal, maybe eighty years buried. My uncle used to cut timber up there. Kids used to dare each other to crawl the rubble until a boy got hurt and the town piled more rock on it to keep them out." He paused. "The tunnel doesn't end at the collapse. It runs through the hill and comes out under the bay. Under the Tollgate chamber."

Ulyana sat back on her heels. For the first time since he'd met her, she looked genuinely unmoored — not wary, not calculating, just a woman standing on ground that had shifted.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "You work for them."

"I work for the fleet," he said. It wasn't a correction; it was a distinction he was still trying to believe in himself. "The fleet wants the wreck. I want—" He stopped. What did he want? He'd been diving since he was sixteen, and somewhere in twenty years of salvage he'd lost track of the difference between the job and the point. "I want the wreck to be worth finding. And Adelene Harrow doesn't care whether it is. That's a bad combination, long-term."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's an opening bid."

Something almost like humor crossed her face — brief, dry, gone again, like a fish surfacing. "Divers," she said. "Everything's negotiation."

"Engineers," he returned. "Everything's accusation."

They regarded each other across the map, and Tomas understood that neither of them trusted the other, and both of them needed the other anyway, and that this arrangement — uneasy, provisional, held together by mutual usefulness — was probably how all the important partnerships in history had started. His mother would have said something wise about it. His mother also would have demanded the pie back.

"Show me," Ulyana said.

* * *

The trail up to the bluffs was a goat path wearing the ghost of a rail bed, ties rotted to brown lace, rails themselves long since pulled — except, Tomas noted, for one length of track half-buried near the switchback, too far from anywhere to be worth salvaging. Ulyana stopped at it and crouched, running her thumb along the railhead the way other people touched saints' bones.

"Nineteen-oh-eight pattern," she murmured. "Light section. Branch-line weight." She glanced up at him. "My grandfather laid rail like this. He could tell you the mill it came from by the sound it made under a wheel."

"You knew him?"

"I inherited him." She said it flatly, but her thumb stayed on the rail a moment longer. "He died when I was six. Everything I know about him is in ink and secondhand stories, and most of the secondhand stories call him a thief. So I read the ink instead."

They climbed on. The morning fog was burning off the marsh in long silver rags, and below them Rivergate arranged itself along its tideline — chandlery, tavern, the Vann woman's tollbooth shack squatting at the cave mouth like a toad that had learned commerce. The Harrow cranes stood over it all with the patience of gallows.

"Does the fleet know about this entrance?" Ulyana asked.

"No."

"Why not?"

Because I've already lied once this week and the silt is setting, he didn't say. Because Adelene Harrow pays me to find things and I've decided some things are better unfound by her. Because your grandfather drew a blank space on a map fifty years ago and I want to know why before anyone else does.

"Because a second entrance means a second claim," he said instead. "And the moment the fleet knows, lawyers get involved, and lawyers move slower than tides but hit harder. Right now the Tollgate is a tourist legend with a coin box. The day it becomes a legal question, nobody dives it — they litigate it, and the wreck rots while they do." He shrugged. "I've seen it happen. A brig off Cape Merrow, ten years back. Three families fought over her for six years. By the time the courts finished, there was nothing left but sand and a court order dividing the sand."

Ulyana walked in silence for a dozen steps. Then: "That's not the real reason."

He nearly laughed. "No. It's a reason. It's even true. It's just not the load-bearing one."

"What is?"

He stopped at the top of the rise, where the trees broke and the bluff grass began, and pointed. "There."

The portal was a wound in the hillside. Rockfall had buried it decades ago — a spill of granite and shale fanning down the slope like the mountain had vomited — but the arch of the original cutting still showed above the rubble, mortared stone going soft with lichen. And someone, at some point, had done more than let nature close it. The rocks were *placed*. Stacked. Interlocked, in the way of men who understood mass and wanted the closure to hold against more than weather.

Ulyana stared at it, and Tomas watched her engineer's mind do what it did — measure, model, test. He saw the exact moment the model came back wrong.

"That's not collapse," she said slowly. "Collapse scatters. This is *coursed*. Someone built that wall."

"Or rebuilt it," Tomas said. "After."

"After what?"

"After whatever they wanted to make sure stayed shut." He pulled his gloves on. "My uncle said the town sealed it after the boy got hurt. But my uncle also said the pie at the church social was homemade, so his testimony has limits." He caught her glance and grinned despite himself. "Long story. Involves my mother. Everything does."

But Ulyana wasn't listening anymore. She had the map out again, holding it up against the actual hillside, and her hands had gone very still.

"Tomas," she said. "Look at the wall. Look at the stones."

He looked. Granite, shale, granite — and set into the face of the pile, deliberate as a signature, three stones of pale white quartz. Small. Easy to miss unless you were looking.

On the map, in the clean blank space where the tunnel should have been marked, her grandfather had left nothing — except, at the very corner of the blank, so small Tomas had taken it for a blot, three tiny dots.

Ulyana's voice came out strange. Careful. Like someone walking on ice that had already cracked once under them.

"He didn't fail to mark the line. He marked it in a language only he expected to be read. Three dots. Quartz stones. He *built* the seal, or he knew who did, and he left a key on the map for whoever inherited it." She lowered the map. Her eyes were bright and furious and afraid all at once. "This whole time I thought the map was a record. It's not. It's a *letter*. And I'm the addressee."

Tomas said nothing, because there was nothing useful to say, and because — though he wouldn't admit this to anyone, possibly including himself — watching her understand it gave him a feeling dangerously close to awe, and awe was not a professional emotion.

* * *

Getting through took two hours and cost them skin.

The wall was built to defeat casual hands, not determined ones, and Ulyana turned out to be the most determined pair of hands Tomas had ever worked beside. She read stone the way she read tide tables — found the loaded blocks, the keystones, the one piece holding the argument together — and they eased three of them out with a rail jack from her kit (the woman traveled with a rail jack; he was beginning to develop theories about her luggage) until a gap opened barely wide enough for a diver's shoulders.

Cold air breathed out of it. Old air, mineral and wet, with a taste of iron underneath.

"Ladies first?" Tomas offered.

"Divers first," Ulyana said. "You know what's in there. I only know what's on paper."

Fair. He went through sideways, lamp up, and the beam opened onto a gallery that stopped his breath.

The tunnel ran straight and true into the hill — and it wasn't ruined. Dusty, yes; dripping, yes; forty years of bat politics coating every surface. But the arch held. The drainage culvert along the floor was clear. And along the walls, every twenty meters, iron brackets still carried the stubs of cable insulators.

"This line was maintained," he said. "After it 'closed.' Look — the drains were kept open. Nobody keeps drains open on a dead tunnel."

Ulyana squeezed through behind him, rose, and stood in the dark with her lamp raised, turning slowly. When she spoke, her voice echoed down the bore of the hill like something returning from far away.

"My grandfather's notes. There's a page I could never make sense of — costs. Ongoing costs, small amounts, year after year, for 'line keeping,' on lines that were supposedly abandoned. I thought it was embezzlement. Another entry in the case against him." She laughed, and it was not a happy sound. "He was paying to keep the tunnels drained. Out of his own pocket, probably. Keeping the evidence dry."

"Evidence," Tomas said. "You mean the wreck."

"I mean whatever he knew that got him framed." She started walking, fast, lamp swinging, and Tomas hurried after her. "Everyone says the Meridian Star sank in a storm with a hold full of salvage scrap. Salvage scrap doesn't get a man convicted of fraud. Salvage scrap doesn't need a hidden tunnel and quartz markers and fifty years of drainage fees." She stopped abruptly, and her lamp found the far end of the gallery — where the rails, instead of ending, curved down and vanished into black water standing perfectly still against a masonry lip.

An airlock of stone. A flooded station, built into the heart of the hill, its platform edge worn smooth by water that rose and fell with the breathing of the sea beyond.

And moored to a rusted bollard at the platform's edge, half-sunk, sat a small boat. Wooden, tarred, impossibly preserved in the still air.

"Oh," Ulyana said softly. "Oh, you clever, stubborn old man."

Tomas checked his watch, then the water line, then the faint movement of the surface — a slow pulse, in and out, the Tollgate breathing through the hill. Eleven minutes off her tables, just like he'd said. The chamber beyond was reachable at low slack, through a flooded section he'd swum twice now, and every time he'd swum it alone, telling himself he was scouting for the fleet.

"So," he said. "Second mouth confirmed. Your map agrees with the hill. Congratulations, the dead man wins again."

Ulyana crouched at the water's edge and put her palm flat on the surface, the way you'd greet a horse. When she looked up at him, the wariness was still there — it might always be there, he suspected; some people were built with it load-bearing — but underneath it was something new. Not trust. Not yet. More like a truce with ambitions.

"Why did you really bring me here, Tomas?"

He thought about lying. Found, to his mild horror, that he'd rather not.

"Because Adelene Harrow is going to take that wreck and turn it into ballast and brass fittings and a plaque in her office," he said. "And because I've spent twenty years pulling things out of the sea for people who never asked what the things meant. And because you looked at a blank spot on a map and saw a letter, and I looked at the same blank spot for thirty years and saw nothing at all." He shouldered his dive bag. "Also, your tide tables are terrible and it was hurting me physically."

That earned him the fish-surfacing smile again. Brief. Dry. Real.

"Low slack is in four hours," she said. "Can we swim it?"

"We can swim it." He hesitated, and then said the thing he'd been circling all morning, the thing that had woken him at three a.m. with the taste of silt in his mouth. "Ulyana. Once we go through that flooded section together, there's no version of this where I stay neutral. You understand that. The fleet finds out I showed you this, I lose everything — contract, diving rights, the lot. Adelene puts penalties in her contracts like other people put salt on eggs."

Ulyana studied him for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out the map, folded it with an engineer's precision, and held it out to him.

"Hold this," she said. "If we're doing something stupid, we should both be holding something incriminating. It's only fair."

Tomas took the map. It felt lighter than it had any right to — fifty years of a dead man's secrets, three dots of quartz, a whole ruined life pressed between two sheets of paper — and heavier than anything he'd ever carried out of the sea.

Behind them, deep in the hill, the water pulsed once against the masonry lip. In. And out. The Tollgate, breathing. Counting.

Four hours to low slack. And somewhere across the bay, in a fleet office that smelled of diesel and money, Adelene Harrow was sharpening her pen.
