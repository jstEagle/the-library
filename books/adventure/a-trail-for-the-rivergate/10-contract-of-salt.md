## 10. Contract of Salt

The Harrow fleet office smelled of lamp oil, old rope, and the particular staleness of paper that had been argued over too long. Tomas Reinhart had signed documents in this room before — his original indenture at nineteen, his diver's certification at twenty-two, the renewal that bound him through the age of forty — but he had never seen Adelene Harrow lay out a contract the way she laid out this one: flat on the table, squared to the table's edge, with her own pen placed beside it like a surgeon's instrument.

"Sit," she said, though there were only two chairs and both divers were already lowering themselves into them. Beside Tomas, Old Piet — forty years on the fleet, hands like driftwood, a man who had once held his breath four minutes in the flooded quarry at Kessel's Bend just to win a bet — settled with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions about chairs and about mornings and about most things generally.

Tomas counted the room the way he counted everything: one door, two windows, the strongbox under the chart shelf, Adelene's nephew Corwin standing by the stove with the expression of a young man who had been told to be intimidating and had settled for being present. The windows looked out on the harbor, where the fleet's three salvage barges rode at anchor, their cranes folded like patient herons. From here you could see the Tollgate's black mouth at the bay's edge, and the crowd of tourists being shepherded along the viewing rail by a boy with a collection tin.

"You've all heard what the ebb uncovered," Adelene said. She did not sit. Adelene Harrow had not sat in front of her divers in Tomas's eleven years with the fleet, and he suspected she considered sitting a concession to gravity that she would make only in private, and grudgingly. "The Meridian Star. Fifty years down, and the sea hands it to us gift-wrapped. Salvage rights are being petitioned through the magistrate as we speak, and the magistrate is a reasonable man." A pause that was not a pause so much as a place where reasonable men were reminded what they were reasonable to. "But the petition takes time. Six tides, perhaps fewer, before the cave seals again. So we work under fleet contract. Fleet terms. Fleet discipline."

She turned the paper around and pushed it down the table.

Tomas read. He was a slow reader and a thorough one, which his instructors at the diving academy had called a virtue and which Tomas privately knew was simply fear of missing something. He did not miss anything now.

The contract was three pages. The first page was generous — hazard pay doubled, a share structure that gave the divers a real cut of the salvage value, provisions for families in the event of loss. Adelene was many things, but she had never been cheap with the men who went down for her. That was the whole architecture of the fleet's loyalty: Adelene paid for courage, and the divers paid with it, and everyone understood the exchange rate.

The second page was where the architecture showed its foundations.

*No diver of the Harrow fleet shall communicate with, aid, abet, shelter, trade with, or otherwise assist any party not contracted to this salvage, in any matter touching the wreck, its cargo, its history, or its approaches, whether by sea, by tunnel, or by any other means now known or hereafter discovered.*

Tomas read the sentence twice. It was the *hereafter discovered* that caught him. Contracts were written for what was known. This one had been written for what was coming.

"Third page," Adelene said.

The third page was the penalty. *Any diver found in breach shall forfeit all shares, all wages accrued, all equipment issued, and all diving rights within the waters of Rivergate and its approaches for the term of his natural life.* Natural life. Not five years, not ten. The waters of Rivergate were Tomas's whole world. He had learned to swim in them before he could properly walk, had learned to read current and cold and pressure in them the way farm children learned soil. To be barred from them was not a fine. It was exile from the only country he had.

"Adelene," Piet said, in the tone of a man who had earned the right to use her first name and spent it carefully, "you've written us into a cage and called it a raise."

"I've written you into a *fleet*," Adelene said, "and called it what it's always been. The difference is that now there's something in the water worth stealing." She came around the table and stood where she could see them both, arms folded, and Tomas was struck — not for the first time — by how much she resembled the cranes on her barges: built for lifting enormous weight, and built, if you looked long enough, for holding things down. "There's a stranger in town. A woman. Surveying the cave mouth yesterday with instruments and no permit. Asking questions at the chandlery. Asking about tides."

Tomas kept his face the way he kept his hands underwater: still, functional, giving nothing away. The woman. The one on the sea wall at first light with a theodolite and a tide table she was reading wrong — or reading right, and working from the wrong table, which was worse, which meant she had her own numbers, which meant someone had taught her the old coastal reckoning that the fleet paid good money to keep out of general circulation. He had corrected her. He had stood there in the cold and corrected a stranger's tide tables, and he had told himself it was professional courtesy, the way you'd warn anyone off a bad descent, and he had not told Adelene about it, and he was not telling Adelene about it now.

"The magistrate's warrant covers interference," Adelene went on. "But warrants are slow and strangers are quick. If she's after the Star — and everyone who comes to Rivergate asking about the Star is after the Star — then she'll need a way in, and a way in means a diver, and every diver in this town is mine." She let that sit. "So. Sign, and we work the wreck together, and you'll all be richer than your fathers dreamed. Refuse, and there's the road."

Piet signed first. He signed the way old men do everything, without ceremony, as if to suggest he had signed greater things and would sign greater things still. "Fifty years," he muttered, dipping the pen again. "That wreck's been down there fifty years and now suddenly it's a matter of state."

"Fifty years it's been *safe*," Adelene said. "Safe things end, Piet. That's what makes them safe."

Tomas took the pen.

He was aware, as he wrote his name, of a sensation he had felt exactly once before — the moment on his first deep dive when the weight belt settled onto his hips and the boat deck tilted away and the water came up to meet him. The belt was not heavy. That was the trick of it, the thing civilians never understood. A weight belt weighed almost nothing on the surface. It was only below, only when you committed, only when the water closed over your head, that forty pounds of lead became the whole of your relationship with the world: too heavy to swim up with, too necessary to drop. Freedom was a buckle you could reach but were trained never to touch.

He signed. The pen scratched. Somewhere in the scratch was his life from this tide forward, and somewhere in the scratch, though he could not have said where, was a woman with the wrong tide table and a way of standing at the sea wall as if the sea owed her an answer.

* * *

The fleet mustered on the *Harrow's Fortune* at noon, and the work began, and the work was good, and that was the trouble with it.

Good work was the fleet's oldest seduction. There was no feeling in the world like a clean descent — the check of your gear, the nod to your line-tender, the cold closing over your head, and then the slow green quiet where a man's whole noisy soul went silent and only the work remained. Tomas had loved it since he was a boy hanging off the pilings watching the fleet divers walk the harbor floor like patient giants. Duty, his father had told him — his father who had died on a Harrow barge with his boots on — duty is just love with a schedule. And Tomas had believed it. He believed it still, most hours. The schedule was the thing that held.

They ran survey lines into the Tollgate on the afternoon ebb, three divers walking the exposed floor of the cave with measuring chains while the tourists watched from the rail and the boy with the collection tin worked the crowd. Tomas carried the forward line, and the cave opened around him in the failing light like the inside of a bell — ribbed, resonant, enormous. The Meridian Star lay in the flooded chamber beyond the second sill, her masts gone, her hull furred with fifty years of the sea's slow handwriting. He had seen her twice now. The second time had been through the inland entrance, the one that wasn't on any chart, the one that was a collapsed rail tunnel if you knew what collapsed rail tunnels looked like, and he had surfaced from that reconnaissance with a report in his mouth and had given Adelene only half of it.

He had reported the cave, the sill depths, the wreck's position. He had not reported the tunnel.

There was no good version of why. He had turned the reasons over on his cot the night before like a man counting coins that wouldn't sum. Professional caution — an unverified entrance was a hazard, not an asset. Fleet security — why advertise a back door to every ear in the tavern. And underneath both, in the place where the real reasons lived, the simple fact that a door only you know about is the closest thing to freedom a bound man owns. He had not thought of it as betrayal. He had thought of it as keeping a key in his own pocket instead of hanging it on the fleet's board.

Now the key had a penalty clause attached to everything else.

"Reinhart." Corwin's voice through the line, tinny and officious. "Depth mark. You've gone past the chain mark."

Tomas looked down. He had, in fact, drifted six feet past his survey station, standing deeper than the line allowed, looking at the wreck's stern where the letters still showed under the fur — *MERIDIAN STAR*, and below them, smaller, in paint that the sea had half-eaten, a shipwright's mark he'd have sworn he'd seen somewhere before and couldn't place. He moved back up the chain and marked the station and did not think about the shipwright's mark, and thought about nothing else for the rest of the dive.

* * *

They surfaced at slack water into a harbor gone gold, and found the town gathered on the sea wall like gulls on a rail.

Not the tourists. The townsfolk. Chandlery men, net menders, the fishwives, the tavern keeper, old Mrs. Vann from the cave kiosk sitting wrapped in wool with her collection box on her knees as if the century of charging tourists to *look* at the Tollgate had been practice for something. They were not looking at the cave. They were looking at the barges, at the divers, at the cranes — with an expression Tomas had never seen his town turn on the fleet before, and it took him the length of the swim-in to name it.

It was the look people give a thing they had thought was theirs.

He toweled off on the Fortune's deck while Corwin strutted the rail telling anyone in earshot about the contract's share structure, and Tomas watched the crowd, and the crowd watched him, and in the middle of it he saw her.

The stranger. Standing at the far end of the sea wall with her instruments packed and her collar up against the wind, not looking at the cave at all. Looking at the *barges*. At the cranes. At the fleet, the way an engineer looks at a bridge — not with longing and not with fear, but with arithmetic. He had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that somewhere in her head there was already a calculation running: load ratings, tide windows, the tonnage a crane could lift and the tonnage a crane could be made to lift. That she was not deciding whether to fight the fleet. She was deciding what the fleet was *for*.

Their eyes met across the water. She didn't look away, and neither did he, and the whole exchange lasted perhaps two seconds and contained, in Tomas's estimation, more information than the contract he'd signed that morning. Then she turned and walked up the chandlery stairs, and Corwin said something beside him about outsiders thinking they could just walk in and take what the fleet had earned, and Tomas said, "Aye," in the voice of a man agreeing, and heard his own voice the way you hear a line-tender's signal from underwater: distant, distorted, possibly not meaning what it was supposed to mean.

* * *

Evening found him at the fleet office again, because Adelene had posted a notice: all divers to attend, contract copies to be countersigned, no exceptions. He stood at the back with Piet and the others while Adelene read the penalty clause aloud, slowly, the way a priest reads the names of the dead, and watched his fellows sign one by one. Young Ferro signed eagerly — Ferro was twenty and the hazard pay was more than his father made in a season. The Brandt brothers signed together, as they did everything, one pen between them. Old Piet signed again, though he'd signed that morning, because Adelene wanted the ritual of it, wanted the whole fleet's name on the page in one unbroken column, a wall of signatures with no gaps.

Tomas signed his name in the column for the second time that day, and the pen felt heavier than it had at noon, and he understood that this was the design. The first signature bound you. The second one made you complicit in the binding — made you a man who had seen the cage in daylight and walked back in and shut the door behind him.

"One more thing," Adelene said, when the last name was down. She held up the contract. "This goes into the strongbox, and the strongbox goes into the magistrate's custody, and the magistrate will enforce it. I want there to be no confusion, from anyone, about where the fleet stands." Her eyes went around the room, unhurried, and stopped on Tomas for exactly as long as they stopped on everyone else, which was the point, which was the technique — you could never prove you'd been looked at harder. "The Meridian Star comes up under Harrow colors. Whatever she carried, whatever she *meant*, she's ours now. The past included. Especially the past."

*Especially the past.* Tomas walked home through the salt-dark streets turning the phrase over. He had grown up on fleet stories, and the fleet stories had a shape: the sea takes, the fleet recovers, the town endures. Nobody in the stories ever asked what the sea had taken *from*, or who had put it there, or why a salvage fleet fifty years later needed a contract forbidding its divers to speak to strangers about a shipwreck. Contracts were written for what was known. This one had been written for what was coming, and what was coming had a theodolite and the wrong tide table and a way of standing at the sea wall like the sea owed her an answer.

At his door he stopped. Down the lane, lamplight showed in the chandlery's back window, and a silhouette bent over a table — the stranger, up late, working. He thought about the tunnel. He thought about the fact that she had been surveying the cave mouth with instruments and no permit, which meant she was either a fool or a professional, and fools didn't own theodolites, and professionals didn't read tide tables wrong.

He thought about his father, who had died on a Harrow barge, and whose funeral Adelene had paid for, and whose grave Tomas visited every year with a bottle of the good brandy and a report, spoken aloud, of the fleet's doings — because his father had loved the fleet, and Tomas had never once, in eleven years, had anything but the fleet's doings to report.

The lamp in the chandlery window went out.

Tomas stood in the dark a while longer, feeling the contract settle onto his hips the way the weight belt settled — almost nothing on the surface, everything below. Then he went inside, and did not sleep, and in the morning he was on the *Fortune* at first light with his gear checked and his line-tender ready, because duty is just love with a schedule, and the schedule, whatever else had changed, still held.

It was three tides later, on the flooded gallery past the second sill, that the schedule first cracked — because it was there, in the green half-light, that he found fresh survey marks chalked on the tunnel wall. Precise. Professional. In a hand he had last seen correcting tide tables on a sea wall.

Someone had found the second mouth. And by the marks' freshness, they hadn't found it alone.
