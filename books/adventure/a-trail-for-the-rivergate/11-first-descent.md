## 11. First Descent

The tide table said low slack would come at twenty past four in the morning, and the tide table — Tomas's tide table, corrected in his blunt pencil in three places where her grandfather's almanac had drifted out of true — had not lied to her yet.

Ulyana woke before the alarm she hadn't set, because engineers don't set alarms for things they've already rehearsed forty times against the ceiling of a chandlery room. She dressed in the dark. Wool under layer, oilskin over it, the survey kit slung across her chest like a bandolier: chalk, plumb line, folding rule, the little brass rail jack that weighed more than her conscience and was worth about as much on the open market. She'd told herself she was bringing it for ballast. She knew better. You don't carry your grandfather's jack into a drowned tunnel unless you mean to introduce them.

Tomas was waiting at the sea wall, and he had brought coffee, which was either kindness or bribery, and she took the cup anyway.

"You slept," he said. It wasn't a question, exactly. It was an accusation wearing a question's coat.

"Four hours."

"I got two." He drank from his own cup and looked out at the bay, which lay flat and black as spilled ink, holding its breath. "You know what I keep thinking? That I've briefed Adelene Harrow on this cave. Drawn her charts. Told her men where the sills are. And tomorrow morning I'm going to swim through it with the one person in Rivergate she wants at the bottom of the harbor."

"That does sound like a conflict of interest."

"It sounds like unemployment with extra steps." He said it lightly, but his hand tightened on the cup, and she saw again what she'd seen on the platform — a man carrying his duty like a weight belt, buckled on so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. "There's a thing divers say," he went on. *'The water doesn't care why you're down there.'* It's supposed to be comforting. Mostly it means the water will kill you politely, without taking sides."

"Good," Ulyana said. "I've had enough of people taking sides. At least the sea is honest about wanting me dead."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, like a knot coming loose — and then the laugh faded and he looked at her with the expression she was beginning to dread, the one that meant he was about to be careful with her. "Last chance to ask the obvious question. Why you? Of all the people who could walk into Rivergate the week the Tollgate opens, why does it have to be the granddaughter of the man whose name is painted inside that wreck?"

"Because the sea keeps a schedule," she said, "and apparently so do I. Fifty years late, but here."

* * *

They went in through the collapsed rail tunnel above the tideline, where the old line ran out of the hills and simply stopped, as if the engineers of fifty years ago had laid track to the edge of the world and lost their nerve. The mouth was half-choked with rockfall, and Tomas went first with the lamp held high, calling out holds and footing in the flat voice of a man reading a grocery list, which Ulyana recognized as the voice of someone managing fear by giving it paperwork.

"Loose slab, left side. Step where I step. There's a rail spike under the gravel — mind your shin."

She minded her shin. The tunnel sloped down and the air changed, salt thickening it, and then the lamp caught water — black, still, mirror-flat, filling the gallery ahead like a road made of night. Her grandfather's map had shown this line. She had checked it a dozen times. The map showed the tunnel running clean to the headland and stopping at a junction symbol, unmarked, unexplained. It did not show water. It did not show a second mouth breathing into the sea.

"He knew," she said quietly. "He drew this line. He stood right here."

"Then he drew around the truth," Tomas said. "Divers do that too. You chart what you can bear to chart."

The water was cold enough to make her gasp, then cold enough to stop mattering. They swam the flooded gallery with lamps burning green-gold in the murk, and Ulyana counted sills the way other people count prayers. First sill: a shelf of rock they bellied over in six inches of air pocket, their breath loud as bellows. Second sill: deeper, a squeeze between fallen beams where the lamp showed her old survey marks chalked on stone — her own marks, from three nights ago, and beside them, newer, Tomas's neat professional crosses. A tunnel annotated by two liars.

Past the second sill the gallery opened, and the water lifted them, and the wreck came up out of the dark to meet them.

* * *

Nothing had prepared her. She had read the manifest fragments, the ledger lines, the court record with its careful lies. She had imagined iron ribs, the way you imagine a face from a photograph. But imagination doesn't include the smell — no, not smell, there was no smell down here, and yet her body insisted there was one, rust and tar and old rope, the smell of every yard she'd ever worked in — and imagination certainly doesn't include the size. The Meridian Star lay on its beam ends in the flooded chamber like a felled cathedral, masts gone, hull furred white with fifty years of growth, and the lamplight moved along its flank and found, just above the buried rail of the cargo hatch, a nameplate.

And beneath the nameplate, in paint gone brown as tea, a hand-written line. Small. Careful. An engineer's hand, lettered the way lettering is done when a man was taught that a number you can't read is a number that kills.

**OKONKWO SURVEY LINE — CLEARED FOR LOADING — U.O., CHIEF INSPECTOR**

Her grandfather had inspected this ship. Had signed his initials on her hull with the same steady capitals he'd used on the closed-line map folded against Ulyana's ribs right now, inside its oilskin sleeve, riding over her heart like a second heartbeat.

"Tomas." Her voice came out wrong, thin, bubbling. He turned, lamp swinging, and she pointed, and she watched him read it, watched his shoulders change.

"That's—"

"My grandfather. Yes." She pressed her glove against the letters as if she could warm them. Fifty years of cold water, and the paint still held. Paint holds, she thought. Men don't. Courts don't. But paint holds. "He cleared her for loading. Whatever sank her, it wasn't because she wasn't sound. He certified her himself."

"Which means either he was wrong—" Tomas began, and stopped, because they both knew the rest. Either he was wrong, which no one who knew him believed, or he was right, and the ship was sunk anyway, and the man who certified her was the safest man to blame.

The chamber pressed close around them, patient as a courtroom. Somewhere above, filtered through fathoms of green, the last of the ebb tide was turning, and Tomas tapped his depth gauge and then his watch and held up five fingers, then folded one down. Five tides left after this one. The sea keeping its schedule, polite as ever.

"Cargo hold," he signed, or nearly signed — he spoke into the water with short words, conserving breath, conserving nerve. "Hatch. Forward. Watch the spar."

* * *

The hold was a throat. They dropped through the cargo hatch into a space where the lamplight couldn't find the walls, and the Meridian Star groaned around them — long, slow complaints of metal settling, the sound of a ship talking in its sleep, and Ulyana hated how much comfort she took from it. Ships talk. Dead ships talk loudest.

Tomas swept the lamp along the deck cargo that had never been unloaded: crates slumped into rot, barrels banded shut, and along the inner bulkhead, lashed down with chain that had fused itself into the frame, a row of strongboxes. Official ones. Railway pattern. She knew the pattern the way you know your own front door.

They were all stamped with the syndicate's mark except one.

It sat apart, wedged behind a fallen beam as if someone had hidden it in a hurry and meant to come back, and the lamp found the stamp on its lid and Ulyana's heart performed a maneuver no manual had ever described — a stall, a drop, and a restart so hard she felt it in her teeth.

Two letters, interlocked, struck deep into the brass plate:

**O.K.**

Okonkwo. Her family's initials. Her grandfather's seal, the same double-letter die he'd used on every inspection certificate, every waybill, every page of the map she carried. He had put his own box aboard this ship. Or — the thought arrived cold and complete, the way truth arrives, all at once and unwelcome — someone else had hidden it here and marked it with his seal so that only his own blood would ever dare claim it.

Either way. Fifty years at the bottom of the sea, and the box had been waiting for an Okonkwo the whole time.

Homecoming, she thought, is a strange word for a place you've never been. But that's what it was. Terror and homecoming in the same breath, the breath she was currently using to keep from drowning, which was inconvenient timing for both.

"Tomas." She got his attention and pointed. His lamp steadied on the box, and for a long moment neither of them moved, two small bright creatures hanging in a drowned cathedral, and then he swam to it and tested the chains and looked back at her and shook his head slowly.

"Fused. Won't cut with what we brought." He turned the box gently, checking, and his gloved fingers found something on the underside and stopped. He beckoned her close, angled the lamp.

A hasp. Unlocked. Hidden beneath, facing the bulkhead, where only someone who already knew it was there would ever look.

Someone had locked the chains and left the box open. Locked it against thieves. Opened it for family.

Ulyana's hands were shaking now, badly enough that she had to press them flat against the lid to steady them, and through the glove she felt the box answer — a faint, shifting weight inside, paper-things sliding, the sound a strongbox makes when it is full of documents that have spent fifty years underwater.

Paper. Fifty years of water.

The joy went out of her like a tide going out, fast and total, leaving everything exposed.

"Tomas," she said, and her voice cracked against the regulator, "it's full of paper."

He understood immediately — she watched it land on him, the diver's arithmetic, the same cold clarity she'd felt working tide tables on the platform. Paper didn't survive this. Paper became pulp, became ghost, became nothing. Unless—

Unless it had been sealed. Wax-sealed, oil-wrapped, packed the way railway clerks packed documents for river crossings. Unless whoever hid it had known what they were doing.

Unless.

Above them the ship groaned again, longer this time, and a fine rain of sediment sifted down through the lamplight, and Tomas checked his watch and made the gesture that ended all arguments: thumb up. Ascend. The slack was dying around them; the chamber would begin to breathe in soon, and the Tollgate did not negotiate.

But Ulyana stayed one more moment, glove flat on the brass, on the interlocked letters, on fifty years of patience.

"Hold on," she told it, and knew how it sounded, and didn't care. "I'm coming back with wax and alcohol and a chandlery full of stubborn old women. Hold on."

She kicked for the light, and the Meridian Star let her go the way the sea lets go of everything — easily, and only for now — and behind her, in the dark of the hold, the strongbox settled another inch into the silt with a sound like a door being left deliberately, carefully, open.

They were halfway up the flooded gallery, in the narrow air pocket past the second sill, faces together in the lamplight, sharing the pocket's thin sweet air, when Ulyana noticed what the rising water had done.

The gallery behind them was already sealed — the incoming tide had closed the low passage back toward the second mouth hours ahead of Tomas's table. Ahead, the only way out was up and over the first sill, through the squeeze, into the tunnel — and from somewhere above them, faint but unmistakable, came a sound no tide table had ever predicted.

Boots. On gravel. Many of them, and lantern-light flickering down through the rockfall at the tunnel mouth, and a voice she knew from the harbor — Harrow's foreman, calling out orders in the cheerful tone of a man who has been paid to be thorough.

Somebody had followed their chalk marks home.
