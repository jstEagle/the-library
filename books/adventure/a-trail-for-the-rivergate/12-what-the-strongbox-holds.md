## 12. What the Strongbox Holds

The strongbox sat on the chandlery counter between a jar of pickled mussels and a coil of tarred rope, and it looked, Ulyana thought, exactly like what it was: fifty years of secrets wrapped in rust and barnacle.

"You can't open that here," said Mrs. Vann, not looking up from her ledger. "Salt water gets in everything. You want a dry room, a dry cloth, and a steady hand. In that order."

"I have a room."

"You have a room above a chandlery that smells of pitch and herrings, with a window that lets the fog in sideways." Mrs. Vann finally lifted her head. She was a small woman built like a mooring post — short, thick, immovable — with eyes the gray of old rope. "You want my back room. It's dry as a sermon and I keep the fire lit."

Ulyana hesitated. Everything in Rivergate had a price. The cave had a keeper who charged a coin and a warning; the harbor had cranes that charged by the hour; the air itself seemed to charge by the breath. But Mrs. Vann had already come around the counter, taken the strongbox in both arms as if it weighed nothing — which, Ulyana noted with an engineer's disapproval, meant the old woman was considerably stronger than she looked — and was walking toward the back of the shop.

"Are you coming, or are you going to stand there doing sums about what this will cost you?" Mrs. Vann called over her shoulder. "Because I'll tell you now: it costs a cup of tea and your attention. I've had enough of men who pay in coin and none who pay in attention, so you'll be a novelty."

Ulyana followed.

* * *

The back room was small and warm and smelled of beeswax and lamp oil. A fire tickled in a squat iron stove. A table stood in the middle of the floor, scrubbed pale with use, and Mrs. Vann set the strongbox down on it with a reverence that surprised Ulyana — the careful way you set down something that belongs to a dead person.

"Now." Mrs. Vann wiped her hands on her apron. "I'm going to ask you a question, girl, and I want the truth, because I'm old and I've earned it. That box came up out of the Meridian Star."

It wasn't a question, but Ulyana answered anyway. "The cargo hold. Under a false floor, under a coil of chain. It was hidden."

"Hidden." Mrs. Vann said the word the way other people said *amen*. "Fifty years I've wondered where they put it. Fifty years." She sat down heavily on a stool, and for a moment she looked every one of her years, and then some she'd been saving. "Open it. Slowly. And mind the hinge on the left — it'll be seized."

The lock was a simple warded thing, older than Ulyana's tools, and it surrendered to a flat pick and patience. The left hinge, as promised, shrieked like a gull defending a chip. Inside, on a bed of rotted wool that had once been packing, lay papers.

Ulyana's heart did something complicated.

There was a leather folder, swollen fat with water and time. Beneath it, a bundle of documents tied with a ribbon that had once, perhaps, been blue. And at the bottom, a small oilskin packet, still sealed with a dab of pitch — someone had known, fifty years ago, that the water would come.

"Oilskin first," Mrs. Vann said quietly. "That's the one they meant to survive."

Ulyana lifted it out. It was lighter than it should have been. She peeled the pitch away with her thumbnail, worked the fold open, and drew out a single sheet of paper — dry, brittle, browned at the edges, but whole.

She read it once in silence. Then she read it again, because the first time her eyes had refused to cooperate.

It was a confession. Signed, dated, witnessed. The hand was cramped and hurried, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, but every word stood up straight.

*I, Elias Marchetti, first officer of the salvage vessel Meridian Star, being of sound mind and unsound prospects, do set down the truth of this voyage...*

The Meridian Star had not sunk. It had been *sunk*. The ship had been carrying documents out of the capital — ledgers, contracts, testimony — evidence against the Consolidated Rail & Harbor Syndicate, the same syndicate that had bought the railway commissioners, closed the unprofitable lines, and needed a scapegoat for the bridge collapse at Kilnwater. The same bridge collapse they'd pinned on a junior engineer named Daniel Okonkwo.

Her grandfather.

The confession named names. Three commissioners. A harbor master. And at the top of the list, in a sentence the first officer had underlined twice, as if he'd known underlining was all the weapon he'd ever be given: *The whole design proceeds from the office of Chairman Aldous Wren, who ordered the cargo drowned and the ship with it.*

"They were going to be the proof," Ulyana said. Her voice came out strange, flat, as if someone else were using it. "The manifest. The testimony. The Star was carrying it out of the country, to people who'd publish it. And the syndicate found out, and they scuttled her in the one harbor they owned."

"Aye." Mrs. Vann was looking at the fire, not at the papers. "That's the story my husband told me once, late, after drink, when he thought I'd forget it by morning. I never forgot a thing in my life. It's my one talent and it's been nothing but trouble."

Ulyana set the confession down with hands that were not quite steady. Then she lifted the leather folder.

It did not want to open. The water had gotten into it over decades of tide and seep, and the cover had bonded to the pages inside like a shell to a rock. She could feel the paper beneath, soft as overcooked cabbage.

"The manifest's in there," she said. "The cargo list. The itemized proof of what the Star was carrying — every ledger, every sworn statement. It'll match the confession. Together they're—"

"They're paper, girl." Mrs. Vann's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse than if it had been harsh. "Wet paper. Look at it."

Ulyana looked. She made herself look properly, the way she made herself look at cracked welds and rotten pilings — because looking away never fixed anything. The edges of the folder had already begun to slough, a fine gray pulp coming away on her fingers like the shed skin of something dying. When she eased the cover apart, the top page came with it, tearing along a line of weakness, and the writing on it — columns of figures, a cargo list, her grandfather's case in ink — bloomed and blurred where her thumb had touched it.

One touch. One thumbprint of damage. Fifty years underwater and it had survived; one minute in warm air and it was starting to go.

"Oh," she said. It was not a large word, but she put everything into it.

"Aye." Mrs. Vann nodded slowly. "My husband used to say the sea keeps what it keeps and gives back what it gives, and it never gives back anything that isn't already leaving." She was on her feet now, moving with purpose, pulling things from shelves: a shallow tin tray, a bottle of clear spirit, a brick of beeswax, a stack of clean linen rags. "Right. You want to save that paper, you'll do it my way, because I've preserved more dead men's documents than you've had hot dinners. Fishing charts. Deeds. One love letter I still regret saving, but that's another story."

"Mrs. Vann—"

"Hush. Alcohol first, to drive the water out and kill the rot. Then wax, thin as breath, to seal it. Then flat, dry, and dark, and nobody breathes on it for a week." She poured a measure of spirit into the tray. "And you'll do it page by page, and you'll be patient, and if you tear one, you'll forgive yourself, because paper that's been drowned fifty years tears if you look at it crosswise. Now — how many pages?"

Ulyana eased the folder open as far as it would forgive. The pages inside were fused into a single swollen block, a paper reef. She could see edges, layers, the ghost of columns. She counted by touch, the way she'd once counted rail joints by sound from a moving cab.

"Thirty," she said. "Maybe thirty-five. Fused at the margins. I'll have to separate them as they dry — it's the only way. Which means every page I free is a page that starts dying the moment it's free."

"Then you'd best not stop for meals." Mrs. Vann said it dryly, but her eyes were on the confession, on the underlined name at the top of the list, and there was something moving behind her face like a fish under ice. "Aldous Wren," she said. "Chairman. He'd be dead by now."

"Dead, but the syndicate isn't. It just changed the letterhead. The man who sits in Wren's chair now —" Ulyana had spent three nights with the chandlery's shipping ledgers and the town's back issues of the harbor gazette, and the name had surfaced the way names do when you drag enough water — "is Corwin Vale. Wren's protégé. He was Wren's *clerk* when the Star went down. There are photographs. He's in two of them, standing behind Wren's left shoulder, looking like a man who remembers everything and regrets nothing."

"Vale." Mrs. Vann tried the name out and spat gently into the stove, which Ulyana gathered was the local equivalent of a formal indictment. "And the Harrow fleet does his salvage work."

"The Harrow fleet does everyone's salvage work. That's the point of owning the only cranes." Ulyana laid the first page — the outermost, the most damaged — into the spirit bath and watched the water bloom out of it in pale clouds. "If I can free the manifest, and the manifest matches the confession, item for item, then it isn't a story anymore. It's a record. Dates, weights, signatures. You can argue with a story. You can't argue with thirty pages of a drowned ship's cargo list."

"And what do you do with a record like that?"

Ulyana was quiet a moment. The spirit in the tray made a small sound, like rain very far away.

"I don't know yet," she said, and it was the most honest thing she'd said in Rivergate. "My first instinct is the one I've had my whole life: take it and run. Put it somewhere safe. Keep moving. That's what the map taught me — never stay anywhere long enough to be blamed for anything." She almost smiled. "My grandfather was the same. He kept his map of every closed line in the country folded up inside his coat for forty years. He knew exactly which towns had been robbed and by whom, and he told no one, because telling people gets you hurt and keeping maps doesn't."

"And how did that serve him?"

"It didn't. He died with the truth in his pocket and the blame on his name, and the lines stayed closed." Ulyana turned the page in the tray, gently, the way you turn a sleeping child. "So maybe the map was wrong. Maybe the point of knowing where all the closed lines are is that someday you open one."

Mrs. Vann looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her turn back to the fire and poke it harder than it needed poking.

"You sound like someone I knew," she said. "A long time back. Someone who also thought a thing hidden was a thing saved." She set the poker down. "Well. You've got your confession dry and your manifest dying by inches, and I've got wax to melt. And you'll want to know something else, I expect."

"What's that?"

"You'll not be doing this alone." Mrs. Vann jerked her chin toward the front of the shop, toward the street beyond, where Ulyana knew — because she'd catalogued the exits, because she always catalogued the exits — a certain Harrow diver had been standing for the better part of an hour, ostensibly examining a display of brass fittings he had clearly examined a hundred times before. "That young man has been circling my shop like a cold all afternoon. He dives for Harrow and he walks like a man carrying a trunk up stairs. And he's been asking after you — politely, mind, in the way of a man being polite so nobody asks him what he's really doing."

"Tomas." Ulyana kept her eyes on the tray. The first page was beginning to stiffen, the figures re-emerging from the blur like a town emerging from fog. *Item: one sealed case, documents, legal. Item: one sealed case, documents, personal. Item:—* Her chest tightened. It was real. It had always been real, but now it was *legible*, and that was different. "He corrected my tide tables."

"Did he, now."

"He was right, which was the annoying part."

Mrs. Vann snorted. "Men who are right are the worst kind. My husband was never right, which is why I kept him forty years." She stood, brushed off her apron, and moved to the door. "I'll see what he wants. You keep your hands in that tray and your ears open, and girl — if he asks what's in the box, you think very carefully before you answer. Not because he's a bad man. Because he's a *contracted* one, and contracts have a way of standing up in court while truth sits in the gallery."

She went out. Ulyana heard the low murmur of voices, Mrs. Vann's dry as driftwood, Tomas's lower, careful. Then a pause — the particular pause of a man being asked a question he'd hoped to avoid — and Mrs. Vann's voice, clear and carrying, with the gentle humor of a woman who had decided to enjoy herself:

"Salvage rights? *Salvage rights?* Young man, that woman is holding the only honest paper this town has produced in fifty years, and you're asking me about salvage rights. Sit down. Have a mussel. And tell me exactly how much Adelene Harrow knows, because I've a feeling it's less than she thinks and more than you've admitted, and I'd like the arithmetic."

Ulyana, alone with the tray and the dying pages and the confession that named her family's destroyers, found — to her considerable surprise — that she was smiling.

Then she looked down at the second page she'd freed, at the cargo list marching down in its drowned columns, and the smile went out like a lamp in wind. Because there, at the bottom of the list, in the first officer's cramped hand, was a line she hadn't seen before, a line that turned her cold:

*Item: one strongbox, documents, to be delivered to the keeper of the Tollgate, who has been paid and who has promised.*

The keeper of the Tollgate.

Mrs. Vann's voice was still talking to Tomas in the front room, warm and dry and utterly unbothered, and Ulyana stood with the page dripping in the tray and understood, with the cold clarity of the engineer, that the old woman had known about the strongbox before Ulyana had ever bought her coin at the cave mouth — had known for fifty years — and had said not one word.

The sea, her grandfather's map had noted once, in the margin, in his small precise hand: *keeps a schedule. So do its debts.*

Outside, the tide was turning. Ulyana counted the pages left to save — twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty — and began, very carefully, to work faster.
