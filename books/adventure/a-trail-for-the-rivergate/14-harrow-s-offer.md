## 14. Harrow's Offer

The invitation arrived the way invitations arrive in a town built on salvage money: by hand, on heavy paper, with someone standing too close while you read it.

Ulyana had been in the chandlery's back room, elbow-deep in a ledger from 1919, when Mrs. Vann's granddaughter — a solemn girl of perhaps eleven named Pilar who had appointed herself Ulyana's unofficial bodyguard sometime around the second tide — appeared at the door holding an envelope as if it might bite.

"She said to tell you it's not a trap," Pilar reported.

"Did she say why I should believe that?"

"No." The girl considered. "She said you'd be stupid to believe it either way, but she'd like to talk."

Ulyana dried her hands on her trousers. "That's the most honest invitation I've ever received. Tell her yes."

* * *

Adelene Harrow did not receive people on her ship. That was the first thing Ulyana understood about her. The *Corvus Ann*, flagship of the Harrow fleet, sat at anchor in the harbor like a statement nobody had asked for — three cranes, twin dive platforms, a hull painted the gray of a sky deciding whether to rain — and Adelene conducted business ashore, in the Harrow offices above the old customs house, where the windows looked out over everything she intended to own.

Ulyana climbed the stairs at the appointed hour and found the office exactly as she'd expected and nothing like she'd prepared for. She had imagined brass and menace, ship models and a desk arranged like a barricade. Instead there were plants. Actual living ferns, thriving in the salt air, and a kettle on a side table, and Adelene Harrow herself — small, silver-haired, reading glasses pushed up into her hair like a crown she kept forgetting she wore — pouring tea into two cups.

"You're punctual," Adelene said. "I respect that in an opponent. Sit. It's oolong. Don't make that face; I know what they say about me, and none of it involves good tea, which is their loss."

Ulyana sat. The chair was comfortable, which felt like another move in whatever game this was.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Adelene said, settling across from her. "Or is it Miss? Your paperwork is remarkably thin for someone causing this much trouble."

"It's Okonkwo. And my paperwork is thin because I don't cause trouble. Trouble finds me. I just survey it."

A smile flickered across the older woman's face — quick, genuine, immediately regretted. "There it is. Tomas said you were funny. He said it grudgingly, the way he says most things, which from him is practically a love letter."

"I wouldn't know anything about Mr. Reinhart's letters."

"No," Adelene agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. More tea?"

Ulyana accepted the cup because refusing seemed like conceding something, though she couldn't have said what. The tea was, annoyingly, excellent.

* * *

For ten minutes they talked about nothing. Tides, mostly — Adelene knew the bay's habits the way Ulyana knew rail gauges, intimately and with a professional's contempt for its inconsistencies. Then, without any transition at all, the older woman set down her cup and became a different person. Not crueler. Just load-bearing.

"The Meridian Star went down in November of '71," she said. "You know that. What you may not know is that my father was on the salvage contract before the water closed over her funnel. He held rights through the crash of '29, through two wars, through my mother dying and my brother leaving and every year of my life until he handed them to me with his last breath. Fifty-three years of Harrow money keeping that claim alive on the chance the sea would give her back." She folded her hands. "Now the sea has. So you'll understand that when I say the wreck belongs to my family, I am not being greedy. I am finishing a sentence my father started."

"The wreck belongs to whoever can prove what happened aboard her," Ulyana said. "And I think we both know your family has spent fifty years making sure nobody could."

Adelene didn't flinch. Ulyana gave her credit for that. Most people, caught mid-lie, performed outrage or hurt. Adelene simply refilled her own cup, unhurried, as if the accusation were weather.

"Let me tell you what I actually know," she said. "In 1971, the Meridian Star was chartered by men in the capital whose names I was never given, to carry cargo whose nature I was never told, and she sank in a storm that half the old-timers swear wasn't blowing hard enough to sink a rowboat. My father was paid a settlement to stop asking questions. He took it. Not because he was corrupt — because he had forty divers' families eating out of his pocket, and the alternative was watching them starve while he fought men who owned the courts." Her eyes came up, sharp and level. "Does that sound familiar to you, engineer? A man ruined for asking questions about a railway?"

The room got very quiet. Outside, a gull said something obscene to another gull.

"My grandfather," Ulyana said carefully, "was framed."

"Yes. And everyone in this town knows it, and everyone in this town took the money anyway, including my father, including the magistrate's father, including the grandfather of the woman who keeps your cave. That is the inheritance I'm describing, Miss Okonkwo. Not gold. Debt. And I have spent my whole life paying it down in the only currency that works here, which is keeping the fleet alive and the town fed and the questions buried deep enough that nobody drowns digging them up."

"And now I've started digging."

"Now you've started digging." Adelene leaned back. "So here is my offer, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide I'm a monster, because I've worked very hard at being something more complicated than that."

* * *

The offer, laid out plainly, was this:

One quarter share of the salvage value of the Meridian Star — and Adelene named a figure that made Ulyana's engineer's brain do involuntary arithmetic about rails, and jacks, and how many towns on her grandfather's map could be re-surveyed with that kind of capital. A full legal exit from Rivergate, papers clean, no warrant, no harassment, the magistrate's attention redirected elsewhere. Passage on any Harrow vessel to any port on the coast. And in exchange: the strongbox, unopened if possible, and Ulyana's silence, and her departure before the fourth tide.

"You keep the truth you already have," Adelene said. "Your grandfather's name cleared quietly — I'll even help with that, a petition, a pardon, whatever the courts will grant once the right palms are greased, and I know every palm on this coast personally. You get your family's honor back, officially, with a seal on it. What you don't get is the manifest published. Because here is what publication buys, engineer, and I want you to price it honestly." She counted on her fingers, unhurried. "It buys you the chairman's ruin, maybe, if the paper survives long enough to matter. It buys this town a scandal that kills the salvage trade for a decade — and the salvage trade is four hundred jobs and every roof in Rivergate. It buys you a fight against men who have buried evidence before and are considerably better at it than you are. And it buys you a grave in a flooded tunnel, because I know what's happening inside that hull, and so does Tomas, and neither of us sleeps well about it."

"You're threatening me."

"I'm pricing you," Adelene said. "Threats are for people who frighten easily. You don't. It's one of the things I like about you, and I want you to know that liking you is genuinely inconvenient." She slid a paper across the desk — the agreement, drafted already, in a hand too neat to be hers alone. "Take it. Read it. Take a day. Take two tides. But understand what mercy looks like when it comes from a woman like me: it looks like this, and it does not come twice."

Ulyana read the agreement. It was well-drafted. That was somehow worse. Every clause was fair on its face, generous even, and threaded through all of it, like wire through cable, was the single sentence that mattered: *the signatory affirms that no documents recovered from the Meridian Star shall be published, copied, or communicated to any party outside the Harrow Salvage Company.*

They wanted the confession dead. Not seized — dead. There was a difference, and the difference was that seized evidence could leak, could be subpoenaed, could surface in some future decade like a hull at ebb tide. This made it ash.

"And if I refuse?" Ulyana asked.

Adelene rose, walked to the window, and looked out at her fleet riding the gray water. For a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age — tired the way machinery gets tired, structurally, somewhere in the frame.

"If you refuse," she said, "I will do what my father would have done, and what I will hate doing, and I will do it thoroughly. You will find this town less hospitable. You will find the law suddenly very interested in how a railroad engineer came to be trespassing in a sealed cave. And you will find that the sea keeps its schedule whether you're ready or not." She turned back. "I'm not asking you to abandon justice, Miss Okonkwo. I'm offering you the only version of it that anyone has ever survived in this bay. The quiet kind. The kind with a signature instead of a body count."

* * *

Ulyana walked back down through the town with the unsigned agreement in her coat pocket and the tea still warm in her chest, and she hated how much of her wanted to say yes.

That was the thing she hadn't been prepared for. She had prepared for threats, for goons, for the flat menace of hired muscle. She had not prepared for a reasonable woman with good tea and a genuine grievance laying out a future in which Ulyana's grandfather died honored, her map got funded, and she walked away rich and clean and free. It was temptation dressed as mercy, yes — but it was tailored, and it fit, and that was precisely what made it dangerous.

She stopped at the harbor rail and watched the *Corvus Ann*'s cranes swing slowly against the sky, idle, patient, like herons waiting for the water to give something up.

Her grandfather's voice, in memory, dry as the ink he used: *A bribe isn't a price, Ulya. It's a mirror. They show you what you'd sell, and then you have to live with knowing.*

"But he never met this tea," she muttered to the gulls.

Pilar materialized beside her, as children do, silent as ballast. "You met her, then."

"I did."

"Are you going to take her money?"

"That depends," Ulyana said, "on whether I'm the kind of person who thinks a pardon with a seal on it is worth more than the truth with salt water on it."

Pilar considered this with the gravity of a harbormaster. "Mrs. Vann says you're not."

"Mrs. Vann," Ulyana said, "has known me six days."

"Mrs. Vann has known this town fifty years," the girl said, "and everybody in it, and she says the ones who took the quiet deal all still live here, and none of them sleep."

Ulyana looked down at the girl, then out at the cave mouth, far across the drained flats, dark and patient under the lowering sky. Somewhere in that flooded chamber sat a strongbox with her initials hammered into the lid, and inside it, dissolving hour by hour, the only honest sentence anyone had ever written about her family.

The tide tables in her head turned over, cold and clear. Two tides left before low slack. One before the fourth.

She took the agreement out of her pocket, folded it once, and tore it along the fold — then, feeling insufficiently dramatic, tore it again, and dropped the pieces into the harbor, where they floated briefly, white as gull feathers, before the current took them.

"Pilar," she said, "go tell Mrs. Vann I'll need wax, alcohol, and every jar in the chandlery. And then run ahead and tell Tomas I need him at the tunnel mouth tonight."

"Why tonight?"

Ulyana watched the first of Adelene's watchers detach himself from the customs house wall and begin, casually, terribly, to follow her down the street.

"Because," she said, "we just ran out of time."
