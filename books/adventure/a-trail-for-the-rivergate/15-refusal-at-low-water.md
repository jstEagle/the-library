## 15. Refusal at Low Water

The watcher following Ulyana down Salt Row was bad at his job, which she chose to find encouraging.

He wore the gray oilskin of the Harrow fleet with the collar turned up, as if that disguised anything, and he walked with the exaggerated looseness of a man pretending not to follow someone. He stopped to examine a barrel of tar with great scholarly interest. He paused at a window of fishing tackle and appeared to consider purchasing a lure. He was, Ulyana reflected, the kind of man who had been told to be inconspicuous and had interpreted this as a costume assignment.

She stopped at the corner, turned, and waited for him.

He walked straight into the conversation like a man stepping off a dock he hadn't checked.

"Evening," she said.

"Evening," he said. His name, according to the patch on his oilskin, was Dree. According to the set of his shoulders, he wished to be anywhere else.

"You're following me."

"No," said Dree.

"You've been following me since the customs house. You looked at that tar barrel for four minutes."

"Tar's interesting."

"It really isn't. Listen—" She kept her voice pleasant, almost conversational, the way she spoke to signalmen who were about to make an error. "You can tell Adelene Harrow that I've read her offer twice, and both times it said the same thing: *give me the box, forget your grandfather, and I'll let you leave town with your skin.* That's not an offer. That's a ransom note with better penmanship."

Dree shifted his weight. "She said you were stubborn."

"She undersold it." Ulyana stepped closer, and lowered her voice to something that wasn't pleasant at all. "You tell her this. I'm not selling the box. I'm not leaving Rivergate. And every hour she spends paying men to watch me is an hour the tide doesn't care about. The sea keeps her schedule whether Adelene likes it or not. Tell her to do her arithmetic."

Dree looked at her for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, he nodded — a small, tired nod, the nod of a man who had perhaps once had opinions of his own.

"I'll tell her," he said. "Though it won't help. Mrs. Harrow doesn't lose arguments. She outlasts them."

"Then she and the tide have something in common," Ulyana said, and walked on.

Behind her, she heard Dree fall into step, four paces back, resuming his post. She didn't mind, particularly. It was almost companionable, in the way a stone in your boot is almost a footwarmer.

* * *

The chandlery smelled of beeswax, lamp oil, and Mrs. Vann's particular brand of disapproval, which had its own note, somewhere between camphor and iron.

"You tore it up," Mrs. Vann said. It wasn't a question. Pilar, the chandlery's boy, had evidently run his errand and delivered his report with the thoroughness of a town crier.

"I tore it up."

"Into the harbor."

"Where it's doing more good than it would in Adelene Harrow's desk."

Mrs. Vann was at the long counter, laying out jars in a row with the precision of a woman arming a small glass artillery. Wax cakes. A bottle of grain alcohol the color of nothing at all. A roll of linen. She didn't look up.

"You understand what you've done," she said.

"I refused a bribe. It's not the first time. It's usually the last time, in my experience, that anyone offers a second one."

"That's not what you've done." Mrs. Vann looked up then, and her eyes were the flat gray of the bay in November. "You've told Adelene Harrow that you'd rather be an enemy than a client. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is that a client gets left alone. An enemy gets *managed*."

"Managed how?"

Mrs. Vann set down the jar she was holding, carefully, as if it were an egg with a grudge. "How do you think a fleet that owns the cranes, the customs house, the magistrate's boat, and half the fishermen's debts handles a problem it can't buy? It doesn't chase it. It *waits* on it." She tapped the counter once. "The tide is your timetable. So she'll make the tide her garrison. Watchers at the Tollgate. Watchers at the tunnel mouth. Watchers on your window, if she's feeling thorough. You'll find that every low water from now on comes with an audience."

Ulyana stood very still, and let the shape of it settle over her. She had spent her whole adult life reading timetables — freight schedules, tide tables, the closing windows of bridges and borders — and she could see this one as clearly as if Mrs. Vann had drawn it in ink. A siege didn't need to storm anything. A siege only needed to be patient, and the sea would do the rest. Six tides. She'd told herself she had a window. Adelene Harrow had just put bars on it.

"The cost of stopping," Ulyana said quietly.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. An old habit of talking to myself. My grandfather said engineers should narrate their mistakes so they learn them twice." She pulled out a stool and sat, and looked at the jars. "Then we work faster. Wax, alcohol, linen. How long to stabilize a page?"

"Depends how wet it is and how much of it there is. And whether the ink held." Mrs. Vann's hands had resumed their work, jar after jar, and her voice softened by perhaps one degree, which from Mrs. Vann was a warmth other grandmothers would have called an embrace. "Fifty years ago, when we hid that box, we thought about burning it. Did you know that? Emeric wanted to. He said paper was a thing that got people killed, and he was right, God forgive him. But I said — I said, a lie is heavier than paper. It sinks everything you put it in. And paper, at least, floats."

Ulyana looked at the old woman's hands, cracked and wax-stained, laying out the tools of rescue for a document she'd helped bury before Ulyana's father was born.

"Mrs. Vann. Why are you helping me? Truly. Not the version you'd give a magistrate."

Mrs. Vann was quiet for a while. Outside, the harbor bell rang the change of the tide, a slow bronze groan.

"Because I paid for that box once already," she said at last, "and I've been paying installments ever since. And because—" she stopped, and her jaw worked, and for a moment Ulyana saw that under the keeper's armor there was simply a very old woman standing in a very old debt. "And because when you walked into my cave and read that nameplate, you read it the way my son would have read it. Like it was a door. Everyone else reads a wreck like it's a purse."

* * *

Tomas was waiting at the tunnel mouth when she came up the old rail bed at dusk, and he was not alone in the way that mattered: there were two men on the ridge above the collapsed arch, sitting by a small fire with the studied casualness of men who had been told to sit by a small fire. One of them had a signal lantern at his feet, unlit, waiting.

"You see them," Tomas said. It wasn't a question either. It seemed everyone in Rivergate had given up on questions tonight.

"I see them. How long?"

"Since an hour after your meeting with Adelene. They rotated at the top of the ebb. Fresh pair coming at slack." He crouched and drew in the gravel with one finger — the tunnel's course, the flooded gallery, the chamber beyond. "She's covered both mouths. Tollgate's got four on the seawall and a boat standing off the cave line. Here, she's got the ridge and the old gatehouse. And she's posted a man on the chandlery street — Dree, probably, if he's the one who draws the long straw."

"He is. He's very committed to tar barrels."

"Then you know what this is." Tomas looked up at her, and the last light came through the broken arch behind him and put a copper edge on everything. "It's not a chase. She's not going to come at you. She's going to stand on your timetable until the timetable runs out. Four tides left, Ulyana. Maybe five, if the almanac's feeling generous. And every one of them, she'll have eyes on both doors."

"Then we go through a door she doesn't know about."

"There is no door she doesn't know about. I found the second mouth, and I told her about the Tollgate, and I've been lying by omission ever since, and omissions have a way of—" He stopped himself. Ran a hand over his face. "I'm sorry. I've been a diver for eleven years. You learn that panic underwater is just arithmetic done too fast. Let me do the arithmetic."

He crouched again over his gravel map, and Ulyana crouched beside him, and for a moment the two watchers on the ridge might have been scenery.

"Low slack tomorrow is at 3:40," Tomas said. "The flooded gallery takes forty minutes in, forty out, if the water's clean. It won't be clean — the tunnel sheds silt every time the tide turns. Call it an hour each way. That gives us maybe twenty minutes in the chamber. Twenty minutes to do whatever it is we're doing down there."

"The manifest's already out. It's in Mrs. Vann's back room under a jar."

"Then what's down there?"

Ulyana hesitated. She had not told him about the strongbox's second compartment, the shallow drawer beneath the manifest tray, and the oilcloth packet inside it — her grandfather's handwriting on the wrapping, *for whoever the sea gives this to*, and inside, she was nearly certain, the thing she had come three hundred miles to find: the closed-line survey. The original. The one with the annotations in the margins, the depths and the ballast and the bribes, the one that would make her printed map not a curiosity but a testimony.

"There's a log," she said. "The Meridian Star's log. If the manifest is the wound, the log is the knife. Names, dates, what was loaded, what was ordered loaded. It's the difference between accusing a syndicate and *proving* one."

"And Harrow knows that."

"Harrow suspects it. She's not sure what's in the hold. That's the one advantage we have — she's spending watchers on every possibility because she can't afford to be wrong about one."

Tomas was quiet. Above them, one of the watchers got up, stretched, and added a stick to the fire, and the sparks went up through the broken arch like slow orange birds.

"You understand," he said, "that when we surface tomorrow, they'll see us. Both mouths are watched. We go down at slack, we come up at slack, and there's a man with a lantern writing down the time."

"I know."

"And Adelene will know I was in the water with you. Not suspect. *Know.*" His voice was steady, but Ulyana had spent a night freight learning to hear the strain in things — rails under load, bridges under wind — and she heard it now. "The contract has a clause. Aid to an outsider. Forfeiture of everything. Diving rights for life. I signed it when I was nineteen because it was the only paper in town that would have me."

"Then don't come."

He looked at her.

"Don't come," she said again, and found, to her surprise, that it cost her something to say, and that the cost itself was information. "I can swim the gallery. I've dived wrecks in the North Sea with worse visibility and worse company. You go back to the fleet. You keep your contract, your rights, your name. I'll take the risk; it's mine. It's been mine since the night freight."

"That," said Tomas, "is the single worst piece of engineering I've ever heard proposed in this county."

"Tomas—"

"You'd be diving a flooded tunnel, alone, into a hull that's been sitting in brine for fifty years, with a fleet watching the exit and a tide that doesn't negotiate. Alone, you're not a diver. You're a *recovery*." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he said everything that mattered. "And besides." He paused, and something moved through his face that wasn't arithmetic at all. "Besides, I've been dutiful for eleven years, and I've noticed that duty and I have been growing apart for about four days now. I'd like to see how the separation goes."

Ulyana wanted to argue. She was good at arguing; it was most of what engineering was. But the tide bell sounded again down in the town, and the fire on the ridge crackled, and she looked at this man who was about to burn his own paper for a stranger's grandfather, and she found she had no argument that wasn't a lie.

"All right," she said. "Together. But we do it my way on the timetable. We're in the water before the watchers' rotation. Slack at 3:40 means the new pair arrives at 3:00, and the old pair leaves at 3:15, and for fifteen minutes the ridge has one man and he's watching the fire because watching fires is what he's been told to do."

Tomas blinked. "You've been watching them watch us."

"I've been watching everything since the customs house. It's a habit. I catalog exits." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "You'd be amazed how many exits a town has if you read it like a station."

* * *

She walked back down through Rivergate as the lamps came on, and the town watched her do it.

That was the new thing. Rivergate had been wary of her when she arrived — towns are wary of strangers, that was ordinary weather. But tonight the wariness had turned into something else: doors that stayed open a half-second longer, faces at windows, a fisherman on the quay who touched his cap and then, glancing toward the seawall where the Harrow watchers stood, pretended he hadn't. Word traveled fast in a town built on one industry, and the industry tonight was her.

At the corner of Salt Row, an old woman selling whelks pressed a paper twist of them into her hand without a word and waved off the coin. On the chandlery steps, Pilar sat keeping watch with a slingshot and an air of enormous responsibility.

"Mrs. Vann says to tell you there's a man on the lamppost corner," he reported.

"I know. Dree."

"He's been there two hours. I threw a whelk at him."

"Pilar."

"It missed," he admitted. "Mostly. It hit his boot. He didn't even move. I think he might be part of the lamppost."

"Leave Dree alone. Dree and I have an understanding." She looked down the street. Sure enough, the gray figure stood at the lamppost corner, collar up, radiating the patience of a man being paid by the hour to be furniture. "Besides, he's not the one we need to worry about."

"Who is?"

Ulyana looked past the lamppost, past the rooftops, to where the harbor cranes of the Harrow fleet stood black against the last of the light, and beyond them to the seawall, where a boat rode at anchor in exactly the wrong place to be fishing, its lantern burning steady over the drowned Tollgate.

"Her," she said. "And the tide. Mostly the tide."

Upstairs, in the narrow room over the chandlery, she laid her grandfather's map out on the bed and weighted the corners with the jars Mrs. Vann had sent up, and she did what she always did when the world got loud: she did the arithmetic.

Four usable tides. Two mouths watched. One tunnel, half-collapsed, silted, forty minutes in and forty out. One log, in a captain's safe, in a hull that had been groaning on the third dive and would not groan forever. One contract with a penalty clause, one diver who was about to break it, one old woman with wax and alcohol, one boy with a slingshot, one town full of people who had sold something fifty years ago and were beginning, door by door, window by window, to count what it had cost them.

And one fleet, with cranes and lanterns and a woman who did not lose arguments, standing on both doors of the sea.

Ulyana sat back and looked at the map — the closed lines running out of Rivergate like veins from a heart, her grandfather's ink, her own inheritance — and she thought: *he was besieged too. That's what a frame is. A siege with paperwork.*

She took out her pencil and, in the margin beside the tunnel line, wrote the only plan that was left, in the shorthand she used for schedules that could not be negotiated:

*3:00 — rotation. 3:15 — window. 3:40 — slack. Go.*

Below her window, on the lamppost corner, Dree shifted his weight, and out on the water the anchored lantern burned, and the first long breath of the flood tide began to move under the town — rising, patient, on Adelene Harrow's side of the arithmetic.

And then, from the ridge by the tunnel, faint across the rooftops, came a sound that did not belong to any timetable Ulyana had written: the deep, muffled *whump* of a charge being tested in the old gatehouse, and the rattle of stone, and the watchers' fire flaring up with sudden purpose.

She was at the window before she knew she'd moved.

They weren't just watching the tunnel anymore.

They were widening it.
