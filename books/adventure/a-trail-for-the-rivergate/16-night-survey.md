## 16. Night Survey

Tomas Reinhart had a rule about lying: never lie about numbers. Numbers had a way of surfacing on their own, like drowned men, and when they came up they came up angry. You could shade an opinion, fudge a schedule, tell Adelene Harrow that the visibility in the Tollgate was "manageable" when what you meant was "like swimming through cold soup with your eyes shut." But a measurement was a measurement. It would wait for you. It would find you.

Which was why he was going down at midnight, alone, without telling anyone, to measure the thing he was most afraid to measure.

He told himself it was diligence. The fleet had the salvage contract; the Meridian Star was, on paper, his responsibility now; a responsible diver surveyed his wreck before he sent men into it. That was the story he'd rehearsed on the walk from the bunkhouse, past the sleeping cranes with their hooks swaying like patient gallows, down the shingle to the second mouth — the one in the collapsed rail tunnel that he had not mentioned in his briefing, the omission sitting in his chest like a swallowed fishhook.

The truth was simpler and worse. He wanted to know if the wreck was going to kill someone, and he suspected the answer was yes, and he suspected the someone might be a woman with ink-stained fingers and her grandfather's map folded into her coat like a psalm.

* * *

The tunnel entrance was a black slot in the hillside above the tideline, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble and a century of forgetting. Tomas had found it the way he found most things: by falling into it. He'd been walking the cliff path after the briefing, turning over the shape of the lie he'd told Adelene — *one entrance, the sea mouth, nothing else* — when the ground had gone soft and he'd slid six feet down a bank of rotten ties and found himself staring at a tunnel mouth that no chart, no ledger, no Harrow survey had ever recorded.

His grandfather's map — no. Not his. *Her* grandfather's. He kept making the mistake, and it irritated him, the way a splinter irritates. Ulyana Okonkwo had a map of the closed lines, and this tunnel wasn't on it, which meant her grandfather hadn't known about it, which meant the tunnel had been sealed before his time, which meant —

Which meant Tomas was standing on information. And information, in Rivergate, was currency, and he was spending his on the wrong side of the ledger and couldn't stop.

He checked his watch by the light of a shuttered lantern. Slack water at the second mouth came forty minutes after slack at the sea mouth — the inland chamber filled and emptied on a delay, like a slow student echoing the lesson. That gave him a window. Forty minutes of stillness, then the current would come back through the tunnel like something with an opinion.

He dressed down to his diving suit by feel, the ritual steadying him: boots, suit, the weight belt whose heaviness he'd always found honest. A man in a weight belt knew exactly how much the world was asking of him. He wished everything else in his life weighed the same and said so.

The lantern went into the oilskin bag with the survey slate, the chalk, the sounding line, and a stub of pencil he'd sharpened with his knife because the chandlery wanted two coppers for a new one and he'd decided, on principle, to die on that hill.

Then he stepped into the tunnel and let the dark take him the way it always did — completely, and without negotiation.

* * *

The flooded gallery was a corridor of cold. His lantern made a small warm room around him and the water pressed in past its edges, and the walls ran with the mineral weeping of old stone, streaked white and rust-red like the cave was crying in two colors. The rails were still down there, under the silt — he'd felt them on his first pass, two smooth lines his boots kept finding, and the absurd comfort of it had nearly made him laugh inside the helmet. Even drowned and forgotten, the line kept its gauge. Even here, something had been built by people who expected it to hold.

He swam the gallery by count. Two hundred strokes to the junction where the tunnel forked, left fork choked with a roof fall, right fork opening into the flooded chamber proper. He'd done it once before, in daylight, with the whole of his attention split between wonder and guilt. Tonight he had only the guilt and a job to do, and the job was better company.

The chamber opened around him like a held breath.

His lantern found the hull first — the great curved flank of the Meridian Star, lying on her port side in the silt, her starboard plates furred with barnacles until she looked less like a ship than a reef pretending, badly, to be a ship. The nameplate was up near the bow, and even crusted he could read the letters by touch, the way you can read a coin. *MERIDIAN STAR.* Fifty years under the bay and the sea still hadn't managed to erase her name. The sea, Tomas thought, was a better archivist than the Harrow offices.

He got to work.

Survey work was prayer for men who couldn't kneel. Measure, mark, record. He ran his sounding line along the deck edge, counted the plates, noted which rivet lines had wept rust — the blood-colored streaks that meant iron giving up, iron confessing. He chalked the hull in a grid and worked the grid methodically, and for a while the world narrowed to something he could hold: numbers, depths, distances. Honest things. Things that wouldn't look at him across a chandlery table with mud on her boots and ask him whose side he was on.

He was forty minutes in when he found the crack.

It wasn't hidden. That was the terrible part. It was a structural fracture running along the starboard deck plating where the hull had settled against a shelf of rock, and it was long — he paced it off with his line, and it came to better than thirty feet — and it was *working*. Not static. Working, the way a joint works, opening a finger's width and closing with the slow tidal breathing of the chamber, fifty years of flex and he could see it in the metal the way you could see a hinge's history in its paint: bright scar where the crack had grown, older scar where it had stopped, bright scar again. The wreck wasn't just resting against the rock. The wreck was being *sawed*.

He put his palm flat against the plating beside the fracture, the way you'd check a horse's flank, and he felt it — faint, rhythmic, the whole hull shifting a hair with the pressure pulse of the tide beyond the sea mouth. A ship that size should have been dead weight. This one was breathing, and the breathing was the problem.

Tomas hung in the black water with his lantern trembling and made himself think like an engineer instead of a man.

The Meridian Star had gone down in the chamber with her cargo hold full. Fifty years of silt and sediment had packed into that hold like grain into a sack. The hull was a sack too, and it was full, and the fracture meant the sack was tearing along the seam. When it let go — not *if*; he was done pretending *if* — the packed sediment wouldn't drift out gently. It would move all at once, a brown avalanche, and it would take the weakened deck plating with it, and the plates would come down through the chamber like the blades of a guillotine with a fifty-year fuse.

And the chamber had a schedule now. Six tides. The almanac had never recorded an ebb like the one that had opened the Tollgate, and every old-timer in town had said the same thing with the same superstitious shrug: the sea was showing something it meant to take back. Six tides, and the cave mouth would close, and whatever was inside would be the sea's again — including, if his numbers were honest, the structural integrity of a wreck that three people intended to enter.

Three. He'd made it three. Ulyana, himself, and — he supposed — the strongbox, which had as much claim to personhood as anything else down here, and more legal standing than she did.

He wrote it on the slate, because the slate didn't judge: *Fracture, stbd deck, 30 ft, working. Sediment load est. 40 tons. Failure mode: progressive. Trigger: tidal cycle, hull stress, or one careless boot.*

Then he sat with the number he'd been avoiding since he'd found the crack.

Forty tons of sediment above a cargo hold that a woman with a family name and no family left intended to open with her hands.

* * *

He should have gone back. The honest survey was done; the data was in; a professional would surface, dry off, and think. Instead Tomas hung there in the lantern light, listening to the hull breathe, and did the thing he'd been not-doing since the briefing: he thought about Adelene.

He thought about the briefing room, the smell of lamp oil and coffee gone bitter, Adelene at the head of the table with her hands flat on the chart like a woman holding down a map that wanted to fly away. *The Tollgate is a gift,* she'd said. *The sea doesn't open its hand twice in a lifetime. We take everything it's offering.* And he had stood in front of the fleet's divers and given them the sea mouth and the tide tables and the dive plan, and he had not given them the tunnel, and the omission had felt, at the time, like a pebble in his boot. Small. Private. His.

Now the pebble had grown to the size of a wreck.

Because here was the thing he could not report: the Meridian Star was failing, and the fleet's plan was to send a full dive team into the cargo hold on the third tide with cutting torches and cargo nets and the cheerful confidence of men who had never met a sediment load they couldn't out-muscle. He knew the plan. He'd helped draw it. And every line of it assumed the wreck would hold still for them, the way a wreck was supposed to hold still, the way wrecks in the fleet's ledgers held still — obediently, profitably, on schedule.

The wreck was not going to hold still. The wreck was going to come apart, possibly on the third tide, possibly on the second, possibly — his chalk hovered over the slate — *possibly while someone was inside it.*

He could tell Adelene. That was the correct move, the dutiful move, the move that had gotten him fed and housed and trained since he was fourteen years old and she'd fished him out of the harbor, half-drowned and orphaned, and said, *Well, you're already wet. Might as well learn the trade.* He owed her a debt no contract had ever managed to write down, though God knew she'd tried. He could stand in that briefing room tomorrow and say, *The hull is failing, we need to slow down, we need shoring, we need to cut the sediment load before we touch the hold* — and Adelene would hear *delay*, and delay meant the tide window, and the tide window meant the contract, and the contract meant the Harrow fleet's whole future, and Adelene Harrow did not lose futures. She collected them.

And if he told her the truth and she moved faster instead of slower — if she rushed the team in to strip the hold before the collapse — then his honesty would be the thing that killed them.

He turned the lantern toward the fracture and watched the tide pulse move it, open, close, open, patient as a sawyer. Fifty years, the wreck had waited. It could wait six more tides. It was the humans who couldn't.

* * *

It was on the way back through the gallery that he found the boot print.

The water was shallow enough here, near the fork, that at slack the silt lay in a soft grey floor, and the lantern light caught the print the way it catches anything that doesn't belong: immediately, and with a kind of accusation. A boot print, small, narrow-heeled, pressed deep — someone had stood here, in the flooded gallery, in the dark, and stood long enough to sink in. And beside it, half-dissolved by the returning seep, a second mark that wasn't a boot at all. It was a chalk arrow. Pointing toward the chamber.

He knew the boot. He'd watched those boots walk the tideline below the chandlery with the stride of a woman who measured everything, including the distance between herself and other people.

Tomas stood in the black water and laughed — a short, helpless sound that the helmet turned into a fog of bubbles — because of course. Of course she'd found it. He'd shown her corrected tide tables on the platform like a man handing over state secrets one decimal at a time, and she'd gone and found the second mouth on her own, or half of it, and stood in this gallery reading his chalk marks from the first survey like a woman reading a letter addressed to someone else.

She was surveying his survey. She was checking his work. And the humiliating, infuriating, faintly wonderful truth of it was that he didn't mind. He minded about everything else — the contract, the penalty clause, Adelene's face when she said *anyone who aids an outsider forfeits everything* — but he did not mind being checked by Ulyana Okonkwo. Being checked meant being taken seriously. In the Harrow fleet, nobody checked your work; they checked your loyalty, which was faster and worth less.

He crouched — as much as a man crouches in twelve feet of water — and added, beside her arrow, in his own chalk, a small notation. Not words. Words could be read by anyone, and the fleet ran a diver through this gallery on rotation now, one of Adelene's watchers. Just a number. *30.* The length of the fracture, in feet, the way an engineer would write it, the way only two people in Rivergate would understand.

A message in a bottle, addressed to the one person the bottle could drown him for reaching.

Then his watch told him the slack was dying, and the current came back through the gallery with its cold opinion, and Tomas swam for the tunnel mouth with the slate under his arm and the dread riding his shoulders like a second weight belt.

* * *

He surfaced into a night so mild it felt like a rebuke. The stars were out over the bay, thick and indifferent, and the Tollgate's dark mouth showed as a deeper black against the headland, and somewhere down that black throat a fifty-year-old ship was quietly tearing itself along a thirty-foot seam, and somewhere up the hill a woman was probably awake, drawing lines on a map, and somewhere in the big house above the harbor Adelene Harrow was certainly awake, because she was always awake, counting tides like a miser counting coins.

Tomas sat on the shingle in his suit, dripping, and laid it all out the way he'd lay out tools before a job. Here was what he knew: the wreck was failing, the window was shorter than anyone believed, and the fleet's plan would get men killed. Here was what he couldn't do: report it, because the truth in Adelene's hands became a whip. Here was what he had already done, irreversibly, without deciding to: shown a stranger his tide corrections, hidden a tunnel entrance, chalked a number on a wall for her alone.

He had spent his whole life believing that duty was a thing you carried, and that carrying it well was the whole of a man. Tonight, for the first time, he understood that duty could also be a thing that carried *you* — down a tunnel you hadn't chosen, toward a wreck you weren't allowed to save, at a pace set by someone else's greed. And the only question, the one he'd been dodging since the briefing, sat in the sand in front of him as plain as the boot print in the gallery:

When the hull let go — and it would, his own numbers said so, and he did not lie about numbers — whose side did he want to be standing on?

He looked up the hill, toward the chandlery, where a single lamp still burned in an upstairs window. It was late. It was improper. It was, by the terms of his contract, the beginning of the forfeiture of everything he had.

Tomas got up, wrung out his suit, and started walking.
