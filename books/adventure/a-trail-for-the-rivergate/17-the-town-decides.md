## 17. The Town Decides

The Saltcellar Tavern had stood on Rivergate's waterfront for a hundred and ten years, and in all that time it had learned exactly two things about the people of the town: they would argue about anything, and they would argue loudest when the subject frightened them.

By seven o'clock the tavern was full past decency. Fishermen sat with their boots up on the rail, chandlery clerks still smelled of wax and turpentine, and three generations of Vanns' neighbors crowded the long table by the window where the light was best for reading other people's faces. Someone had nailed a fresh notice to the door on the way in — HARROW SALVAGE CO. SEEKS LOCAL LABOR. FAIR WAGES. SIGN AT THE OFFICE — and someone else had already written beneath it, in pencil, *fair for whom?*

Old Piet Osei held court from his usual stool, a man whose beard had gone the color of driftwood and whose opinions had not changed since the flood of '31, which he remembered personally and brought up often.

"I'm just saying what everyone's thinking," he said, tapping his mug for emphasis. "That girl's trouble."

"Which girl?" asked Marta Iwu, who ran the net-mending shed and had never once let an argument go by unattended.

"The stranger. The engineer." He said the word like it was a disease. "Been here nine days and already the Harrows are posting watchers at the Tollgate like it's a bank. Been here nine days and old Mrs. Vann is letting her into rooms nobody's seen the inside of since her husband died. Trouble."

Marta folded her arms. "Or," she said, "she's the first interesting thing to happen to this town since the ferry changed its schedule, and you're too old to admit you're curious."

"I'm curious," Piet admitted, "the way a man's curious about a hole in his boat."

A laugh went around the room, but it was a thin one, because underneath the joke was the thing itself: the hole in the boat. Everyone in the Saltcellar could feel it. The Meridian Star was real. It was down there behind the Tollgate, fifty years of legend made suddenly, horribly solid, and every family in Rivergate had a piece of paper somewhere in a drawer that said their grandfather had taken money to stop asking questions about it.

* * *

Dessie Okonfor had hers. She kept it in the tin that had once held throat lozenges, under the spare buttons and the photograph of her mother as a girl. It was a receipt, actually — one pound, four shillings, paid by the Railway Relief Committee, dated eleven days after the Star went down. Her grandfather's signature at the bottom, shaky, the way a man signs when he's signing something he doesn't want to read.

She'd shown it to exactly no one in forty years.

Now she sat in the corner of the tavern with her son Bram, who fished alone because he couldn't stand crews, and she turned the tin over in her hands like she could feel the paper through the metal.

"You should say something," Bram said quietly.

"What's there to say? We took the money, Bram. All of us. Granddad took his four shillings and bought a new net and never once said her name out loud again — the Star's name, I mean. Or the captain's. Or anyone's."

"He was paid off by men with lawyers. That's different from selling."

"It isn't, though." Dessie looked at the tin. "That's the whole trick of it. They made sure it wasn't different."

Across the room, Piet was still going. "And now this engineer woman comes sniffing round with her maps and her instruments, and we're supposed to what? Rise up? Against the fleet that pays half the wages on this waterfront? For a stranger?"

"For the truth," said a voice from the doorway.

Everyone turned. Mrs. Vann stood there with her shawl pulled tight and her cane planted like a flag. She was ninety-one years old and she had buried a husband and a son and most of her patience, and when she spoke in the Saltcellar, the tavern went quiet the way water goes quiet before a squall.

"There's no such thing as a stranger's truth, Piet Osei. There's just truth, and how long you've managed to sit on it." She came in slowly, taking the chair Marta rushed to offer. "My son went into that cave fifty years ago helping to hide something honest men wanted found. This town has spent half a century pretending he drowned for nothing. Well. Now the sea's given us our answer back, and some of us are going to have to decide whether we were ever really paid enough."

* * *

The debate caught fire after that, the way debates do when they've been smoldering for decades.

Bram Okonfor spoke first, to his own surprise. "The Harrow contract's got a clause in it now. Anybody helps an outsider forfeits everything — wages, diving rights, the lot. Adelene posted it at the office Tuesday." He said it flatly, letting the room do the arithmetic. Half the men at the long table had signed fleet papers. Some had sons who had.

"So we keep our heads down and take the fair wages," said Corwin Ashe, a crane operator with a mortgage and a daughter at the schoolhouse. "I'm sorry. I am. But my girl eats either way, and she eats better if I'm not blacklisted from every deck between here and Cape Mercy."

"No one's saying you don't eat, Corwin," Marta said. "We're saying maybe somebody ought to ask what the food costs."

"It costs nothing! It's wages!"

"It cost your grandfather a question," Dessie said, very quietly, and the room went still, because Dessie Okonfor had not spoken above a murmur in public since her husband died. She set the lozenge tin on the table. "One question. That's what they bought. Not silence exactly — just one question nobody was allowed to ask. And we've been paying interest on it for fifty years without ever seeing the bill."

Piet snorted, but it was a weaker snort than usual. "Sentiment. The Harrows'll salvage that wreck with or without us. At least with them, the money stays in Rivergate."

"Does it?" asked young Silas Penn, barely twenty, who worked the chandlery counter and read everything. "Adelene Harrow's books aren't public, but her cranes are. Count the new ones. Count the ones sold south. The fleet's been bleeding for three years — everybody knows it and nobody says it. Why d'you think she wants that wreck so badly? Salvage rights on a famous wreck are worth more than the wreck. It's a story she can sell to investors. And stories—" he shrugged— "stories need the ending she writes."

"And what does the engineer's story need?" Piet demanded.

Silas hesitated. "I don't know yet. But her grandfather built half the lines on that map of hers, and the syndicate closed them and framed him for it, and she came here with nothing but the map and her name. That's not a story I'd bet against."

* * *

It went on for hours. It always did. Rivergate could stretch a single disagreement across a whole evening the way a netmender stretches twine — patiently, thoroughly, until every knot showed.

Corwin counted reasons on his fingers: the warrant coming, the magistrate on Harrow's payroll, the watchers at both cave mouths, the clause in the contract. Marta answered each one with the same question — *and then what?* Take the money, let the fleet blast the tunnel or seal the cave or whatever it was Adelene meant to do, and then live in a town that had sold its history twice, knowing it this time.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Dessie opened the tin and laid the receipt on the table, face up, and nobody touched it, but everybody looked.

"One pound, four shillings," she said. "For my granddad's whole voice. And I used to think that was his shame. Now I think it might be ours to settle."

Mrs. Vann listened to all of it with her hands folded, and near midnight, when the arguments had worn themselves down to embers, she rapped her cane once on the floorboards.

"Here's what I know," she said. "Fifty years ago, a handful of people in this room's grandparents helped hide a strongbox in that wreck, because it held proof of what the syndicate did — to the railway, to the towns up the line, to a good man named Okonkwo. My husband was one of them, and he broke, later, and took a page out, and it ate him alive till the day he died. My son paid more than any of us. So don't tell me about strangers. That girl is carrying our mail. She's been carrying it since before she knew it existed."

She looked around the room, at each face in turn.

"Harrow will come to you tomorrow with numbers. Fair wages, signing bonuses, maybe a threat or two dressed up as caution. And every number will be true, and every one of them will be small. What she can't put a price on is the sixth tide — because after it passes, that cave seals, and whatever's decided gets decided for good. So sleep on it. But sleep on the right question. Not *what does it cost me.* Ask instead: *what did it already cost us, and do we pay it twice?*"

* * *

They drifted out into the salt-dark street in ones and twos, quieter than they'd arrived. Corwin Ashe stood a long time at the notice on the door, reading the fleet's fair wages and the penciled *fair for whom?* beneath it. Then he took out his pencil, and after a moment's thought, added a line of his own:

*meeting at the chandlery, tomorrow, sundown. bring your grandfathers' papers.*

Dessie saw it as she passed and stopped. "You sure?" she asked.

"No," Corwin said honestly. "But my daughter's learning local history this term, and I'd like there to be some worth teaching."

Down the hill, the lamp in the chandlery's upstairs window still burned. Behind it, unseen, Ulyana Okonkwo sat with her grandfather's map spread flat, drawing tide lines in red ink, unaware that below her the town was beginning — slowly, grudgingly, in the manner of all things in Rivergate — to turn.

And at the harbor's edge, a Harrow foreman nailed up a fresh notice of his own:

**ALL FLEET HANDS REPORT AT DAWN. THE COMPANY WILL BEGIN OPERATIONS AT THE TOLLGATE TOMORROW MORNING. NON-COMPANY PERSONNEL NEAR THE CAVE WILL BE PROSECUTED.**

Two notices, two dawns, one tide.

The town had until sundown to choose.
