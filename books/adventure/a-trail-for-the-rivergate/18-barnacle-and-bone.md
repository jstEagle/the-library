## 18. Barnacle and Bone

The second dive began the way all of Ulyana's worst ideas began: with arithmetic that checked out and a feeling in her stomach that didn't.

She sat on the cold stone lip of the inland tunnel entrance, legs in the black water up to her knees, running the numbers one more time because numbers had never lied to her the way people did. Low slack at 4:40. The flooded gallery ran two hundred meters from the tunnel mouth to the cargo hold. Forty minutes down, twenty minutes of work, forty minutes back — if nothing went wrong, if the tide kept its schedule, if the hull that had groaned like an old man climbing stairs on their first descent held together for one more visit.

"You're doing the thing," Tomas said beside her.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at your watch like it's going to confess."

"It's a perfectly good watch." She turned it toward him anyway. "And it says we have eleven minutes of slack after entry, which is eight fewer than I'd like."

"Everything you'd like comes with eight minutes more than the world offers." He was checking his regulator with the easy, repetitive motions of a man who had done this ten thousand times, and she found herself watching his hands instead of her watch. That was new. That was dangerous. She filed it under *later*, where she filed most things about Tomas Reinhart, and the *later* drawer was getting crowded.

They went in.

The tunnel swallowed them the way it always did — not dramatically, but completely, the way a mouth swallows a word. One moment there was grey daylight and gulls arguing over the harbor; the next there was only the green-black hush of the flooded gallery, their lamps cutting cones through water thick with silt. The old rail bed ran along the bottom, ties rotted to soft black combs, and above them the tunnel's brick crown wept mineral stains in long pale streaks like the walls themselves were crying and embarrassed about it.

Ulyana had dived wrecks before. She had dived culverts, cofferdams, a half-drowned roundhouse in the northern country where she'd spent a winter repairing track nobody would ever use again. But this was different, and she knew why: this was her grandfather's line. Not marked on his map — that was the part that still unsettled her, the blank space where this tunnel should have been inked in his careful hand — but built by the same crews, laid by the same gauges, and somewhere ahead of her, holding the paper that could un-frame him fifty years too late.

The gallery opened into the chamber and the Meridian Star rose out of the dark like a cathedral that had lost an argument with God.

She'd seen her once already, two nights ago, and told herself the awe would be manageable the second time. It wasn't. The ship lay on her port side across the chamber floor, masts snapped, hull furred white with barnacles so dense they looked like frost, like the sea had tried to knit her a sweater and given up halfway. Her nameplate was still legible beneath the crust: MERIDIAN STAR. And below it, small, almost hidden by a century of growth, a welder's mark — three letters in a looping hand she knew better than her own signature.

*OKO.* Her grandfather had signed the repair work himself, decades before the wreck, back when he was just a line engineer who fixed things instead of being blamed for them.

Tomas tapped her shoulder and pointed. Cargo hold, aft. She nodded and followed him into the ship's ribs.

* * *

Inside, the hold was a forest turned sideways.

Cargo spars and broken derricks hung at angles that made no sense until her engineer's brain rotated the whole room ninety degrees and then, suddenly, it all made sense, which was somehow worse. Barnacles covered everything in bone-white clusters. A school of small silver fish moved through the beams like a single nervous thought. Their lamp beams stirred up sediment that hung in the water like breath in winter air.

The strongbox was where they'd left it — wedged under a collapsed section of decking, stamped with her family's initials, its lock already sprung from their first visit. They'd taken the manifest out once and put it back, because the paper had been too fragile, too waterlogged, to trust to a first attempt. Tonight they came back with Mrs. Vann's wax-sealed tin, a rigid case lined with wool, and a plan Ulyana had rehearsed forty times in her room above the chandlery while pretending to read a novel she'd stopped following around page twelve.

She worked by feel as much as sight. The manifest lay folded in oilcloth inside the box, and when she lifted it, it moved wrong — not like paper, but like wet lace, like something that had been alive once and might come apart if you looked at it too directly. Her heart did something complicated. Fifty years underwater. Fifty years of tides pressing on it like a thumb on a secret, and here it was, soft as a moth's wing against her gloved fingers.

*Steady,* she told herself. *You've handled worse. You've handled live rail.*

She fed it into the tin with the patience of a woman defusing something, folding along its existing creases because new creases were wounds, sliding it flat between the wool layers, sealing the lid. The whole operation took six minutes. It felt like six years.

Tomas hovered at the hold's entrance, keeping watch the way he always did — positioned between her and the open water, angled so he could see both her and the way out. She'd noticed that about him early. He never stood between her and danger; he stood where he could see it coming. There was a difference, and the difference said more about him than any of his careful words.

Then the ship spoke.

Not a creak — ships creaked, she'd learned that much from him, creaks were conversation, creaks were fine. This was a *groan*, long and low and structural, rising up through the barnacled bones of the hull like a note played on a pipe organ the size of a church. The silver fish scattered. Sediment shivered off the overhead beams in slow grey curtains.

Tomas's hand closed on her arm. Above them, a spar — a loading boom, iron-cored, crusted white, longer than a freight car — shifted in its cradle of collapsed decking and settled two inches lower with a sound like a coffin lid testing its hinges.

"Out," he signed, crisp and calm, and she was already moving.

They swam for the hold entrance, and that was when the bulkhead behind them let go.

It didn't explode. It *exhaled* — a wall of rusted plating surrendering all at once, releasing a fist of trapped air and fifty years of silt into the water behind them. The visibility died instantly, green-black going to brown-black going to nothing, and the pressure wave shoved them forward like a hand between the shoulders. Ulyana tumbled, caught herself on a beam, lost her lamp — saw it pinwheel away into the murk, its beam spinning like a lighthouse gone mad — and then the world above her moved.

The spar was coming down.

She heard it before she understood it, a deep tearing groan followed by the particular rush of several tons of iron deciding on a new address, and her body did the math faster than her mind: *directly overhead, four seconds, no exit.* She pushed off the beam anyway, uselessly, a gesture, and then a hand closed on her harness strap and *wrenched* her sideways through the water with a strength that seemed impossible from a man her own size, and the spar hit where she had been.

The impact came through the water as a concussion she felt in her teeth, in her chest, in the fillings she'd been meaning to see someone about. Silt billowed. Somewhere in the brown blindness, metal rang against metal and settled, ringing fading, settling done.

His grip on her strap hadn't loosened. In the zero-visibility dark, his hand was the entire world, and she discovered she was breathing very fast and very shallow and holding onto his forearm with both hands like a drowning woman, which was, she supposed, technically accurate.

He got them moving. Hand over hand along the guide line they'd rigged, through soup-thick water, out of the hold, past the great barnacled flank, into the gallery, and only when the tunnel's brick walls began to firm up around them did the visibility start returning in grudging increments, brown to green, green to something like sight.

At the tunnel mouth, in the last ten meters where the water cleared and the grey daylight showed above like a coin at the bottom of a well, he finally stopped and turned to check her over — hands quick and professional at her straps, her gauge, her mask, cataloguing damage. She did the same to him, and found nothing wrong except that he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere.

They broke the surface in the tunnel's air bell, gasping, and the sound they made in the sudden open air was enormous after an hour of regulators — two people laughing and coughing at the same time, which is not a dignified noise, and neither of them cared.

"You dropped your lamp," he said.

"You dropped a building on me."

"I dropped it *near* you. There's a distinction. Ask any diver."

"Any diver would say you threw me like a sack of mail."

"Sacks of mail are valuable." He was wringing out his hood, and his grin was crooked and real and doing something to her chest cavity that she chose to attribute to adrenaline. "You okay? Actually okay?"

She took inventory. Knees scraped. Shoulder bruised where the harness had caught. Heart rate elevated for reasons she was declining to itemize. "I'm okay."

"The manifest?"

She lifted the tin. Water sheeted off its waxed lid. "Safe."

"Then today was a success."

"A building fell on me, Tomas."

"*Near* you." He said it again, gentler now, and reached over and knocked his knuckles lightly against her temple, the way you'd knock on a door. "Hey. You went back for the paper even when the ship started screaming. Most people would've bolted. You finished the job with the ceiling coming down." He shook his head slowly. "I've worked with salvage crews for fifteen years. Half of them wouldn't have done that for double pay."

"It's my grandfather's name on that paper."

"I know." His voice did something quiet. "That's not why I grabbed your strap, though."

The air bell was very small and very warm suddenly, or maybe that was just her face. She looked away, busied herself with the tin's seals, and said, in the tone she used for discussing ballast tonnage, "Thank you. For the strap. Professionally speaking, it was well executed."

"Professionally speaking," he agreed solemnly.

But neither of them moved toward the exit, and the silence that settled over the air bell wasn't professional at all. It was the kind of silence that has furniture in it. She became aware of the drip of water off the tunnel bricks, of the distant breathing of the tide, of how close his shoulder was to hers in the narrow space, and of the fact that she could not, for the life of her, remember what she'd been afraid of before the dive, because whatever fear was, it had apparently been saving itself for this exact moment, in this exact company, and it had nothing to do with collapsing spars.

"Tomas," she started, and had absolutely no idea what the end of that sentence was going to be.

* * *

They surfaced outside at dusk, hauling themselves up the rusted ladder rungs set into the tunnel's old maintenance shaft, and came out into a world gone copper and violet. The marsh grass burned gold in the last light. Somewhere a heron complained about the economy.

Mrs. Vann was waiting at the shaft head with a thermos and the expression of a woman who had counted the minutes twice. Her eyes went from Ulyana to Tomas to the tin in Ulyana's arms, and something in her face eased and tightened at the same time, the way faces do when relief and worry share custody.

"You're late," she said.

"The ship objected," Ulyana said.

"They usually do." Mrs. Vann poured three cups of tea that smelled of salt and ginger and handed them around with hands that trembled slightly, though whether from age or memory, Ulyana couldn't say. "My son used to say the Meridian Star never gave anything up without taking a bite first." She looked at the tin. "Is it—"

"Whole," Ulyana said. "Fragile. But whole."

The old woman nodded slowly, and for a moment she looked out at the darkening bay, at the place where the Tollgate cave slept under the turning water, and her jaw set in a way that made her look briefly, startlingly, like a much younger woman standing on these same tracks fifty years ago making a promise to the sea.

"Then get it dry," she said. "Get it safe. Because that ship has one more argument left in her — I felt it in my knees this morning, and my knees have never lied about that hull yet."

Ulyana laughed, because it was absurd, and then stopped laughing, because she remembered the groan. The long structural groan. The bulkhead exhaling. The spar settling two inches lower in its cradle, waiting.

Tomas caught her eye over the steam of the tea. He'd felt it too. She could see it in the set of his mouth — the professional dread of a man who knows exactly what that sound means and how little time it buys.

"How many tides?" she asked him quietly.

He looked at the bay, doing the math she couldn't do, the math of corrosion loads and hull stresses and a tide table that had already lied to everyone once this season.

"Two," he said. "Maybe three. After that, the chamber comes down on its own — and everything inside it goes with it."

Ulyana held the tin against her chest, feeling through the waxed lid the faint damp weight of fifty years of truth, and understood that the countdown had just gotten shorter than the plan.

There was one thing left in that wreck worth dying for — the captain's log, in the wheelhouse, in the safe — and the sea was about to close its hand around it forever.
