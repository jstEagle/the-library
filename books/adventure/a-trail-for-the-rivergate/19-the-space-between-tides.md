## 19. The Space Between Tides

The air pocket was the size of a modest pantry and twice as cold, a hollow of trapped breath beneath the Meridian Star's listing deck, where the flooded gallery met the upturned curve of the hull. Tomas found it the way he'd been taught to find everything — by feel, by counting, by trusting the arithmetic of his own lungs — and he pushed Ulyana into it ahead of him because her air gauge had been lying to them both for ten minutes.

She broke the surface with a gasp that echoed off the iron overhead, and for a moment neither of them said anything. There was only the sound of water dripping somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock, and their own breathing, ragged and shared.

"Well," Ulyana said at last. "This is cozy."

Tomas got his elbows over the ledge and hauled himself half out of the water, which was about as far as the ledge allowed. The pocket was barely tall enough to sit in, shoulders touching if they both breathed in at the same time. Above them, the hull curved away into blackness. Below, the gallery they'd swum through was already filling as the tide turned, a slow silver rising that would seal them in until slack.

"How long?" she asked.

"Slack's in two hours. Maybe a little more." He checked his watch by touch, its face too dark to read but his fingers knowing the crown, the bezel, the scratch on the crystal from a reef off Cape Verity. "We're not going anywhere."

"I noticed." She wrung out her braid with the grim efficiency of someone who had done it in worse places. "Your employer is going to wonder where you are."

"My employer," Tomas said, "thinks I'm running a bottom survey of the outer channel."

"And you're instead sitting in a shipwreck with a woman under warrant."

"With a woman under warrant," he agreed. "Yes."

* * *

They had entered on the fourth tide's low slack, through the inland mouth, down the collapsed rail tunnel that existed on no map except hers — and now, apparently, on no map except theirs, because Tomas had never marked it either, and that fact sat between them like a third passenger.

Ulyana had wanted the strongbox again. Or rather, what remained of it: the manifest pages she'd freed on the second dive were safe in waxed oilcloth above the chandlery, drying under Mrs. Vann's supervision, but the hold had yielded one more thing on this pass — a brass signal lamp from the wreck's chart room, stamped with the railway's old crest, which Ulyana insisted was evidence of something and Tomas suspected was evidence of nothing. He'd let her take it anyway. It seemed important to her, and he was past pretending he understood the difference between what mattered and what didn't in her ledger.

Then the tide had turned early. Not catastrophically — the almanac would have called it within tolerance — but enough. Enough that the gallery's ceiling had dipped below breathing height before they'd made the return leg, and Tomas, who knew this wreck the way other men knew their own kitchens, had pulled her sideways into the pocket he'd found on his first survey, back when he still believed he worked for a salvage fleet and not for whatever Adelene Harrow actually ran.

So now they waited.

The lamp — her prize, his burden — gave off a weak greenish glow once he cracked its chemical cartridge, and by that light he watched her do what she always did when the world stopped moving: work. She had a stub of pencil and the edge of the map, her grandfather's map, softened now by salt and handling until it felt less like paper than like skin, and she was marking the tunnel behind them. New lines. Her own hand, laid over his.

"You're surveying," he said. "In an air pocket. Underwater. While trapped."

"I'm trapped either way. I might as well be useful." She didn't look up. "The tunnel's bearing shifted after the collapse. If anyone ever reopens this line, they'll need the correction."

"If anyone ever reopens this line," Tomas said, "they'll need considerably more than a corrected bearing."

"That's true of every line my grandfather drew." She blew on the ink-wet graphite, though graphite needed no blowing, and he understood it was a habit, something inherited, something done in a kitchen fifty years ago by a man she'd never met as an adult. "He mapped forty-one closed routes. Do you know how many have been reopened?"

"No."

"None." She said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Forty-one towns cut off from the coast, and not one of them put a rail back on the ties. Because reopening costs money, and money answers to the same syndicate that closed them. My grandfather spent thirty years drawing a map nobody would use, and then they took his railway, took his name, and left him the paper." She folded the map along its old creases, careful as a nurse. "I used to think that was a tragedy. Now I think it was a message. He wasn't mapping the past. He was leaving instructions."

"For what?"

"For whoever came next." She looked at him then, green light on her face, water dripping from her chin. "Me, apparently. Though I'll admit the paperwork was unclear."

Tomas laughed — a short, surprised sound that bounced around the iron ceiling and came back changed. He hadn't laughed underwater before. He noted it, the way he noted all firsts, filed it under things the sea had given him that the fleet never had.

* * *

The water rose another hand's width while they talked about small things. His childhood on a dredge barge, sleeping in a hammock that swung with the pumps. Her years riding night freights, learning the marsh country station by station, welcome nowhere long enough to unpack. He told her about the time he'd been sent down to clear a fouled propeller in jellyfish season and had come up stung so badly the bosun had soaked him in vinegar and called him the fleet's most expensive pickle. She laughed at that, really laughed, head back against the hull, and the sound filled the pocket better than the air did.

Then the laughing stopped, the way laughing does, and what was underneath it stood up and introduced itself.

"Why diving?" she asked. "You could have done anything. You're good with numbers — I've seen your tide tables. You could have been an engineer. A harbor master. Anything dry."

Tomas considered the question honestly, because she had earned honesty and because there was nothing else to do with it in a space this small.

"My father dove for Harrow. And his father before him, for Harrow's father. Three generations of Reinharts on the fleet's books." He turned his watch over in his fingers. "When I signed my contract at seventeen, Adelene's father shook my hand and told me the sea keeps its accounts, and the fleet keeps ours, and a man who knows where he stands sleeps well. And I did sleep well. For eleven years." He paused. "It's a good trade, Ulyana. That's the trap of it. Steady pay, real work, a craft you can be proud of. They don't bind you with chains. They bind you with competence. Every year you're better at the job, the job gets harder to leave."

"I know," she said quietly. "That's how they kept my grandfather too. Thirty years of being indispensable to men who were robbing him."

"Yes." He hadn't thought of it that way, and the thinking hurt in a useful place. "And now?"

"Now I'm sitting in a wreck my employer wants to blast open, hiding from my own crew, correcting tide tables for a fugitive." He shrugged, and the shrug moved both of them, there being nowhere for it to go. "I find I'm sleeping very well these days. Terribly guilty. But well."

* * *

Somewhere above them, the hull groaned — a long metallic complaint, iron settling against iron, the ship talking in its sleep. They both went still. It was the sound they'd heard on the last dive, the sound that had ended with a bulkhead letting go and a spar swinging for Ulyana's head and Tomas's arm around her waist hauling her clear, and neither of them had spoken about it since, because some things are easier carried than discussed.

"The wreck's failing," Ulyana said. Not a question.

"Faster than it should. The tides this month are wrong — bigger range, longer ebb. Whatever opened the Tollgate is working on the whole bay." He ran his palm along the hull beside him, feeling the vibration come through like a pulse. "There's a plate aft, near the engine room, that's flexing on every tide now. When it goes, it'll take the deck above it. And the deck above that holds the captain's cabin."

"The safe," Ulyana said.

"The safe."

They looked at each other. The green light made her eyes strange and steady.

"The log's in that safe," she said. "If the page Mrs. Vann's husband tore out exists anywhere else, it's there. In the captain's copy. And we have—" she counted silently, lips moving, the engineer's habit, "—two usable slacks left. Maybe three if the fifth ebb runs long."

"One," Tomas said. "Maybe two. The chamber won't survive a third approach. I won't take you back in after the next window, Ulyana. I mean it. I've pulled bodies out of wrecks that talked exactly like this one talks, and they all talked right up until they stopped."

The words hung in the cold air. She didn't argue, which frightened him more than arguing would have. Instead she pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them, and for a while she just listened to the water rise, and he listened to her listening.

"My grandfather wrote me one letter," she said finally. "Did I tell you? No — I haven't told anyone. It came after the funeral, forwarded through three addresses, and it was mostly practical. Where the map was hidden. What the initials on the strongbox meant. How to preserve wet documents." She smiled faintly. "Fifty years dead and still giving me instructions. But at the end there was a line. He wrote: *The work is not the railway. The work is the people the railway was for.*"

The drip of the water counted seconds.

"I spent six years reading that line as a riddle," she went on. "Which town? Which route? Where was the work? And then I came to Rivergate, and I found a wreck, and I thought — ah, here it is, the work, the manifest, the proof. Get the paper, win the claim, go home." She shook her head slowly. "But there's no home in the sentence. There's no *go*. It just says the people. Sitting in taverns. Standing on tracks. Paying debts their grandfathers took money for." She looked at him. "Mrs. Vann. The dockhands who lied to Harrow's watchers for me. That boy who brought me bread every morning and wouldn't say who sent it. That's the work. I had it backwards."

Tomas found that his throat had gone tight, which was unprofessional, and that he didn't care, which was unprecedented.

"And you?" she asked. "What did you defer? Everyone defers something. Eleven years on the fleet's books — what did you postpone?"

He should have said *nothing*, or *a wife*, or some clean answer. Instead he heard himself say:

"A life with windows in it."

"What does that mean?"

"Divers live underwater or in bunkhouses. I told myself I'd quit at thirty. Then at thirty-five. I kept a list, actually — a real list, in a notebook — of things I'd do when I surfaced for good. Learn the violin. See the mountains — real ones, not from a train window. Stay somewhere long enough that a dog learned my name." He laughed at himself, softly, so the hull wouldn't hear. "I've added to that list every year. Never crossed anything off. The list became the life. Planning the leaving instead of leaving."

Ulyana reached over and, with great seriousness, held out her hand.

"Give me the list."

"It's in my bunkhouse locker."

"Then recite it. From memory. You know it from memory, don't you?"

He did. Of course he did. He recited it — the violin, the mountains, the dog, a garden despite having no talent, learning to bake bread from a woman in Port Sallow who'd offered twenty years ago, fixing the lighthouse keeper's clock on Gannet Rock, and the last item, the newest, added only weeks ago in handwriting he'd pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *ask her*.

He stopped before that one.

"The rest," he said. "That's the rest."

Ulyana looked at him across eighteen inches of cold air and rising water, and she was not a woman who missed things — she read gauges and tide tables and the micro-expressions of harbormasters for a living — and she knew. He watched her know. And he watched her decide not to make it easy, because she was Ulyana, and mercy for her had always taken the form of precision.

"*Ask her*," she repeated. "Ask her what?"

"You know what."

"I want it on the record. We're engineers. We document things."

"Fine." He turned to face her fully, shoulder to shoulder in the dark, the green lamp between them like a small stubborn sun. "Ulyana Okonkwo. When this wreck lets us out — if it lets us out — I'm not going back to the fleet. I'm going wherever your map goes next. Forty-one closed lines, you said. I can't fix railways, but I can read water, and I'm told I'm pleasant company in confined spaces, and I would like to spend the deferred part of my life finding out whether that's true. Ask her. There. Asked."

The water dripped. The hull groaned, farther away now, or resigned.

"You understand," she said, and her voice had gone rough in a way he'd only heard once before, on the second dive, when she'd touched the strongbox stamp with her family's initials, "that I am rootless by profession. That I leave places. That everyone who has ever—"

"Tomas," she said, and it was the first time she'd used only his name, bare, without argument attached, and it undid something in him that eleven years of contracts had built. "Yes. Obviously yes. I've been trying to figure out how to ask *you* since you corrected my tide tables on the platform, and I resent deeply that you beat me to it in a flooded shipwreck."

"Someone had to document it properly."

"Shut up and hold my hand, Reinhart."

He did. Her fingers were cold and ink-stained and certain, and the space between tides — that gray hour the sea grants to no one, the pause between its demands — held them the way the wreck held its secrets: temporarily, imperfectly, but holding.

* * *

They talked after that the way people talk when the future has suddenly become load-bearing: lightly, urgently, about nothing and everything. Which town first — she voted for Corren Marsh, the easternmost line, because the map showed a junction there that might still be intact; he voted for wherever the bread-baking woman lived, on principle. Whether a diver and a surveyor could share one kit bag (they could not; this was settled quickly). Whether dogs could learn to swim the flooded galleries (unresolved).

Once, the hull spoke again — closer, a deep groaning flex that set the water trembling around them — and they fell silent and gripped each other's hands and counted the seconds until it passed. When it had, neither of them let go.

"Two hours," she murmured eventually, watching the water climb the last foot toward the lip of the pocket. "Then we swim out, and you lie to Adelene Harrow, and I commit several more crimes, and we crack a dead captain's safe."

"When you put it that way it sounds almost romantic."

"This whole week has sounded almost romantic. That's the problem with our lives." She squeezed his hand. "Tomas. If the wreck comes down on the next dive—"

"It won't."

"If it does — I need you to know the letter was worth it. All of it. The warrant, the tides, the drowning-adjacent courtship. My grandfather waited fifty years for someone to read his work, and I got to be the one, and I got—" she lifted their joined hands slightly, gesturing at the entire absurd situation, "—company. Rootless competence is much better with an audience."

Tomas looked at her — salt-crusted, exhausted, wanted by the law, lit green in a dying ship — and understood that the list in his notebook had never really been about the violin.

"Slack in ninety minutes," he said. "Stay close to me on the way out. The gallery narrows before the tunnel fork, and the current sets hard to port."

"Port. Narrow. Fork." She nodded, committing it to the map in her head. "And Tomas? When we surface — before you go lie to Adelene — remind me to add something to my grandfather's map."

"What's that?"

"Rivergate," she said. "In my own hand. First entry. Some towns you don't cross off."

The water rose. The wreck groaned its long complaint overhead, counting down in iron, and somewhere beyond the hull the sixth tide was already gathering itself, patient as a debt, coming for them all.
