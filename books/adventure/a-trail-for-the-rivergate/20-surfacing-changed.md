## 20. Surfacing Changed

The air pocket had given them nothing but time, and time in the dark does strange arithmetic.

Ulyana had counted her heartbeats until they stopped meaning anything, then counted Tomas's breathing instead, because his was slower and steadier and made a better metronome. Somewhere in the middle of that drowned hour he had told her about his mother teaching him to swim in the harbor shallows with a rope around his waist, and she had told him about the summer she'd slept in a signal box on the Vell Line and learned to tell the time by which trains didn't come. Neither of them had said anything important. Both of them had said everything.

Now the water around them was turning the color of weak tea with honey in it — the first gray suggestion of dawn filtering down through the flooded gallery — and the tide had finally gone slack beneath their feet like a great animal settling to sleep.

"Slack," Tomas said, and the word came out rough, as if it had been waiting in his throat the whole night. "We go now, or we explain to Mrs. Vann why we've grown barnacles."

"I don't know," Ulyana said, treading water beside him in the narrow dark. "I've gotten rather attached to this particular problem. It's quiet. No one's trying to arrest me."

"That was yesterday. Give it an hour."

They swam.

The gallery took them up in stages — through the collapsed rail tunnel, along the seam of cold fresh water where the marsh bled into the sea, past the rusted hulk of a handcar that some crew had abandoned down here fifty years ago and no one had ever thought to mourn. Ulyana kept one hand on the guide line they'd rigged and one hand inside her waterproofed satchel, where the ship's log rode against her ribs like a second heartbeat. Every few strokes she pressed her palm flat against the oilcloth to be sure. A person could lose a lot of things in the dark. She was not going to lose that.

They surfaced in the old cutting behind the tunnel mouth just as the sun cleared the marsh, and the world came at them all at once — birdsong, salt wind, the outrageous ordinary gold of morning light on standing water. Ulyana broke the surface gasping and laughing, actually laughing, the sound startling her more than the cold had.

"We're alive," she announced to the cutting at large.

"We're alive," Tomas agreed, hauling himself onto the stone lip of the culvert, "and we are extremely visible."

She followed his gaze. The cutting opened onto the old rail bed, and the rail bed ran, straight and shameless as a confession, toward the harbor road. And on the harbor road, not two hundred yards off, stood a man with a Harrow fleet jacket and a spyglass he was pretending wasn't pointed at them.

"Oh," Ulyana said.

"Mm," said Tomas.

"He's seen us."

"He saw us about four minutes ago, I'd guess. He's been very politely watching us climb out of a hole in the earth together at dawn, and now he's deciding whether it's worth walking over here before or after his breakfast."

Ulyana wrung out her hair and considered their situation with the cold clarity she saved for things like bridge loads and men with spyglasses. The watcher was alone. That meant he'd been posted for observation, not interception — Harrow wanted to know who came and went, not yet to stop them. Or perhaps she did want to stop them and simply hadn't mustered the nerve. Either way, the man would report. The only question was what.

"Tomas." She kept her voice low, though the distance made it pointless. "What are you going to tell her?"

He didn't answer right away. He sat on the culvert lip with his elbows on his knees, water streaming off him into the dust, and looked at the watcher the way a man looks at a bill he'd hoped would get lost in the post.

"The truth has a certain efficiency," he said at last. "'Found them in the chamber, they had the log, I couldn't stop them.' It explains everything except why I'm not bringing you in."

"And the lie?"

"The lie is that I found the tunnel mouth disturbed during my survey and went to check it alone. Found signs someone had been through. Reported it." He rubbed the back of his neck. "It puts me exactly where I should have been all night, doing exactly what I'm paid for."

"And me?"

"You become a ghost. Someone who moves faster than a diver should credit." He glanced at her sideways, and there was something wry and tired and terribly fond in it. "You're good at being a ghost. You've had practice."

"I've had *necessity*," she corrected. "Practice implies I enjoyed it."

"Didn't you? Just a little? The part where you out-think everyone?"

She opened her mouth to object and closed it again, because the honest answer was *sometimes, when it works*, and he already knew that about her. That was the trouble with him. He kept learning things about her without her filing the paperwork.

The watcher had started walking toward them.

"Company," Ulyana murmured.

Tomas stood, unhurried, and shook seawater from his arms like a man with nowhere better to be. "Let me do the talking."

"When do you ever let me do the talking?"

"When the talking might get you arrested. Which, historically, is most of the talking you want to do."

The watcher arrived at a saunter that fooled neither of them. He was young — younger than Tomas, with the kind of jaw that hadn't yet learned what harbors did to optimism — and he stopped a respectful ten feet away and looked between them with the naked curiosity of a man who had been promised this would be boring.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," Tomas returned pleasantly. "Cold water this time of year."

"So I hear." The young man's eyes flicked to Ulyana, to the satchel, back to Tomas. "You're Reinhart. Fleet diver."

"I am."

"They said you were doing a survey run last night. Alone." The word *alone* was laid down carefully, like a card in a game whose rules the young man only half knew.

"Alone," Tomas confirmed. "Long run. The inland entrance silted worse than the charts say — half the gallery's choked with collapse debris. I turned back at the third junction." He said it easily, evenly, the way he read tide tables, and Ulyana marveled at it even as something in her chest tightened. This was the voice of a man who had never lied to the fleet. It was a very good voice. It was also, she realized, a voice saying its first lie, and if she could hear the hairline crack in it, then anyone who knew him well could too.

Did Adelene Harrow know him well?

The young man chewed on it. His gaze slid to Ulyana again. "And her?"

"A stranger who was poking around my survey site," Tomas said. "I sent her off. She doesn't listen well."

"No," Ulyana said, before she could stop herself, "he's quite right, I don't."

The watcher blinked at her. Whatever script he'd been handed clearly hadn't included the trespasser agreeing with the accusation. "You're the Okonkwo woman. The engineer."

"I'm an engineer," Ulyana said. "The rest is the town's invention. Though I'll grant it's a creative one."

"There's a warrant coming," the young man said, and then seemed surprised at himself for saying it, glancing over his shoulder as though the sentence might be traced back to him. "Magistrate signed it yesterday evening. Sabotage and theft. They're posting it this morning."

"Thank you," Ulyana said sincerely. "That's very useful."

"It's — you're not supposed to—" He stopped, visibly reorganizing himself. "Mr. Reinhart. Mrs. Harrow wants you at the fleet office by eight. There's a briefing. About the blasting."

The word landed in the cutting like a stone in still water.

"Blasting," Tomas repeated.

"Tunnel's coming open the fast way. Powder crews start tomorrow." The young man shifted his weight, uncomfortable now, a messenger who had delivered more message than he'd meant to. "That's all I know. Eight o'clock." He touched his cap brim — to Tomas, Ulyana noticed, and after a hesitation, to her as well, which was either stupidity or conscience — and walked back up the rail bed at a pace slightly faster than his saunter.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The marsh birds filled the silence with their small urgent business, entirely indifferent to warrants and powder charges.

"Blasting," Ulyana said again, quietly. "Through the tunnel. Through the gallery. Through the *chamber*."

"She's not salvaging the wreck," Tomas said. His voice had changed — flattened, stripped of the pleasant ease he'd worn for the watcher. "She's burying it. If the manifest surfaces, the claim dies and the fleet's name goes down with the ship. But if the wreck collapses with everything still inside—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to. Ulyana finished it herself, silently, in the place where she kept load calculations and other unbearable certainties: *if the evidence is under a hundred tons of rock, it isn't evidence anymore.*

"She'd destroy the Meridian Star," Ulyana said aloud. "A hundred-year legend. Her own grandfather's—"

"Her grandfather built the fleet on that legend," Tomas said. "Legends are only useful while they stay legends. You taught me that, or near enough." He turned to look at her fully, and the dawn light caught the salt drying on his face, and she thought: *he chose, just now, back there, and I watched him choose.* He had lied for her. Small lie, necessary lie, first lie — but the direction of it mattered more than the size. A man's duty bent a degree, and a degree was all a compass needed.

"You have a briefing at eight," she said.

"I have a briefing at eight."

"Where you will sit across from Adelene Harrow and listen to her plan to blow up the thing we nearly died for twice."

"Three times," Tomas said. "Don't forget the bulkhead. You always forget the bulkhead."

"The bulkhead was your fault for touching it."

"I touched it to *hold it up*, which you'd have known if you weren't busy arguing with a strongbox."

Despite everything — the warrant, the powder, the watcher surely still within eyeshot of his memory — Ulyana laughed. It came up out of her unbidden, exhausted and bright, and Tomas looked at her as though the sound were something he'd been waiting all night in the dark to hear.

* * *

They parted at the edge of the marsh path, because walking into town together was a signature neither of them could afford, and because Ulyana had learned long ago that the safest route between two points is rarely a straight line. She took the drainage cut behind the chandlery, climbed the trellis that Mrs. Vann pretended not to know about, and let herself into her room above the shop smelling of salt and iron and, faintly, of the lamp oil they'd used to seal the satchel.

Mrs. Vann was waiting in the stairwell with tea.

This was, Ulyana was learning, simply what Mrs. Vann did. The woman appeared in doorways with cups of tea the way weather appeared over the marsh — without announcement, without apology, and with a certainty that resistance was futile.

"You're dripping on my stairs," Mrs. Vann observed.

"I'm dripping on your stairs *triumphantly*," Ulyana said, accepting the cup. "The log's safe. The whole log. Captain's own hand, sealed in wax paper and lamp oil, drier than the magistrate's sense of humor."

Mrs. Vann looked at her for a moment over the rim of her own cup. Fifty years of keeping a cave and a secret had carved her face into something like a cliff face, but cliffs show weathering, if you know how to read them, and Ulyana was beginning to know how to read this one.

"My husband used to come home looking like that," Mrs. Vann said. "Wet and pleased with himself and lying about where he'd been." She sipped. "One of those things killed him eventually. I never did decide which."

"Mrs. Vann—"

"Eat something. You can't outswim a warrant on an empty stomach." She set a plate of bread and smoked fish on the stairwell ledge with the finality of a woman laying track, and then, pausing at her own door, added without turning around: "The boy who watched you come out of the cutting. Pell. His grandmother sold fish to my son the week he died. If he warned you about the warrant, it's because his grandmother raised him right after all." A pause. "People are better than their employers, Miss Okonkwo. Remember that when you're deciding who to trust with the next hard thing."

Then she was gone, and Ulyana stood on the stairs holding tea and bread and the growing understanding that in Rivergate, the walls had opinions, and most of the opinions were quietly on her side.

* * *

She spent the morning doing what she did best, which was making maps of trouble.

Spread on her floor: the tide tables annotated in three hands now — hers, her grandfather's, and Tomas's, whose figures were neater than either of theirs and wrong in exactly the ways that experience corrects. The closed-line map, soft as cloth from fifty years of folding. A charcoal sketch of the tunnel system from memory, the blasted sections marked, the powder crews' likely approach drawn in red. And at the center of it all, wrapped in oilcloth, the log — which she had not yet dared to read past the first page, because the first page had been enough to keep her awake.

*Meridian Star, final entry. We carry what they cannot allow to arrive. If we do not make port, let whoever finds this understand: the lines were not closed for the marsh. The marsh was closed for the lines.*

Her grandfather's handwriting was on the hull of that ship. Her grandfather's *friend*, possibly, or his enemy, or merely a man who had known the same truth and carried it the same way — and tomorrow, powder crews were going to turn all of it into gravel.

Unless.

Ulyana crouched over the sketch and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. The blasting would take a day to stage, maybe less. The sixth tide was two days out. That left her a window measured in hours, and every hour of it watched by men with Harrow jackets and a magistrate's paper with her name on it.

A knock at the shutter. Three taps, pause, two taps — Tomas's signal, invented last night in the dark with the gravity of men inventing a code and the silliness of boys doing it.

She cracked the shutter. He stood below in the alley, in dry clothes and a fleet jacket, looking like exactly what he was: a man carrying something heavy behind his face.

"Briefing?" she asked.

"Briefing." He glanced up the alley, down the alley. "Powder's already ordered. Two hundredweight, delivered tonight. She wants the inner gallery dropped first — seal the chamber from the tunnel side, then work outward. By the time anyone could reach the wreck, it'll be behind forty feet of rubble." His jaw worked. "She gave the speech, Ulyana. About legacy. About how the Star belongs to Rivergate's story, and stories need protecting." He looked at her. "Half the room nodded. The other half checked their contract clauses."

"And you?"

"I asked a question about blast radius relative to the hull's structural members. Very technical. Very loyal-sounding." A ghost of a smile. "And I volunteered to mark the charge positions myself, since I know the gallery. Which means tomorrow morning, I'll be the one placing the powder that buries your grandfather's letter."

The alley was quiet. Somewhere beyond the roofs, the harbor cranes groaned through their morning work, lifting other people's fortunes.

"So don't place them," Ulyana said. "Or place them wrong. Or—"

"Or I burn the last of my cover in front of the entire fleet, forfeit my contract, my diving rights, and the only trade I've known since I was fourteen, and become exactly the kind of man my mother prayed I'd never be." He said it without drama, the way he reported depths and times. Then he looked up at her through the shutter gap, and the wryness in his face softened into something else. "Which I notice I've already described as if the decision's made."

"Has it?"

He was quiet for a moment. Down the alley, a gull screamed once and thought better of it.

"Fifty years ago," Tomas said, "somebody in that fleet should have looked at the Meridian Star and said *no*. One man. One *no*, at the right hour, and your grandfather dies an honest man and Mrs. Vann keeps her son and I grow up in a town that isn't built on a lie." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; she could see it cost him. "I can't fix fifty years. But I can be the man who says no tomorrow morning. Seems late. Seems like the minimum."

Ulyana leaned out through the shutter and kissed him — quickly, salt-stained, in full view of whatever alley gods kept watch over conspirators — because some things shouldn't wait for slack water.

"Eight o'clock tomorrow," she said. "Mark your charges badly, Mr. Reinhart."

"I'll mark them *artistically*," he said, and was gone down the alley before his face could finish betraying him.

Ulyana closed the shutter and turned back to her maps, her heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear. Tomorrow, a diver would kneel in a flooded gallery with powder in his hands and the fleet's whole weight on his back, and he would choose.

And she had two days, a warrant, a town full of grandmothers' grandchildren, and one decommissioned locomotive sitting on a closed line that her grandfather had drawn and she intended, one way or another, to see running.

She picked up her pen and began, in her own hand, a new line on the map.
