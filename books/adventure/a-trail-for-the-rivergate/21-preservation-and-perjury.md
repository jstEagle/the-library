## 21. Preservation and Perjury

The chandlery smelled the way old boats dream: tar and turpentine, hemp rope, lamp oil, and beneath it all a low tide of beeswax that had been softening into the floorboards since before Ulyana was born. Mrs. Vann had cleared the back counter of everything except two oil lamps, a copper kettle, a stack of clean linen rags, and a bottle of grain alcohol that she set down with the reverence other people reserved for communion wine.

"Sit," Mrs. Vann said, pointing at a stool that had clearly outlived three owners. "And take those off."

Ulyana looked down at her boots. "My boots?"

"You've been in salt water up to your ribs twice in two days. You'll drip on my counter, and my counter has held things older than your grandfather's grudge." She said it without malice, the way she said everything, as though the world were a slightly disappointing nephew she'd long ago stopped expecting better from.

Ulyana sat and unlaced the boots. The floor was cold through her socks, and it occurred to her — absurdly, at this hour, with waterlogged evidence spread across a dead man's shop — that this was the first time in years she'd been barefoot anywhere that wasn't a railcar bunk. The thought lodged somewhere under her sternum and stayed there.

Mrs. Vann lifted the oilcloth bundle from the strongbox and unwrapped it with hands that were spotted and knuckled but steady as survey instruments. The pages came apart from each other reluctantly, clinging like siblings who didn't trust the separation. Even now, half-dried, they were translucent at the edges, brown as old tea, and the ink had gone the color of rust.

"Fifty years underwater," Mrs. Vann murmured. "Paper doesn't forgive. But paper can be bribed." She nodded at the kettle. "We heat the wax thin, we lay each page flat, we brush it, and the wax takes the place of the water before the air can get greedy. It's how we used to preserve charts off wrecked pilots' boats, back when I had knees that bent without commentary."

"And the alcohol?"

"For our nerves." A pause. "Also for the pages. A rinse first, to drive out the last of the seawater. But mostly the nerves."

Ulyana almost smiled. Almost. Her hands were doing something else entirely — hovering over the topmost page without touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping animal you're afraid to wake.

Because this was it. This was the thing her grandfather had died being called a liar for. The Meridian Star's cargo manifest, written in a clerk's careful hand fifty years ago, listing crates that no salvage ship had any honest business carrying: ledgers, correspondence, sealed testimony, an entire railway syndicate's sins packed in waterproofed tin for delivery to a federal investigator who had, presumably, waited at a dock that never saw the ship arrive.

She touched the corner of the page. It gave slightly, like the ear of a very old dog.

"All right," she said. "Show me."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while outside the windows Rivergate pretended to sleep, which mostly meant the tavern got louder and the Harrow watchers on the quay got more obvious about not watching. Ulyana rinsed; Mrs. Vann brushed. The wax went on warm and clear, and where it soaked in, the ink darkened and steadied, letters surfacing like swimmers coming up for air.

Ulyana read aloud as each page firmed, because reading aloud made it real, and because Mrs. Vann's eyes were not what they had been.

"Crate eleven. One ledger, bound, marked *Harrow & Sons, Wharfage Accounts.*" Ulyana stopped. "Harrow."

"The same Harrows. Adelene's grandfather built the fleet on the bones of that ship, if you believe the town, which the town does, loudly, after the third drink." Mrs. Vann drew the brush along a page edge with surgical patience. "Keep reading."

"Crate twelve. Correspondence, sealed, forty-one items, addressed to the Office of the Railway Commissioner." Ulyana's throat tightened around the next words. "Crate thirteen. Sworn statements regarding the Okonkwo line closures. Seven statements. Signed."

The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere upstairs, the chandlery's cat turned over on a bed of rope coils and resumed its opinion of the world.

"There," Mrs. Vann said quietly. "You see? Your grandfather wasn't a man who lost his railway. He was a man whose railway was stolen, and who knew exactly who'd taken it, and who was on his way to proving it when the sea got paid to swallow the proof." She set down the brush. "Fifty years I've wondered if I dreamed that box. Now here it is, dripping on my counter."

"You helped hide it." Ulyana kept her voice level, but the question underneath it was enormous and neither of them pretended otherwise. "Chapter fourteen of this story, you told me. Your son carried it into the cave."

"I did. He did. We'll get to all of that when there's more alcohol in both of us." Mrs. Vann's mouth did something that was almost humor and mostly grief. "Tonight we save the paper. Guilt keeps. Paper doesn't."

So they worked. Page by page, the manifest came back to life under the wax, and Ulyana felt something she hadn't let herself feel since the night freight through the marsh country: the specific, dangerous warmth of *almost done*. The engineer's disease. The belief that if you just finish the calculation, the bridge will hold.

She should have known better. Bridges always tell you they'll hold right up until the moment they don't.

* * *

It happened on page nineteen.

Ulyana had developed a rhythm by then — rinse, blot, lay flat, read — and she was halfway through the ritual before her brain caught up with her eyes. Page nineteen was a summary sheet. *Consolidated index of enclosed evidence,* the header said. And below it, in the clerk's tidy columns, a list of contents with page references.

Item nine: *Statement of A. Merridew, Chairman, Coastal & Interior Railway Syndicate, admitting direction of the Rivergate diversion and the closure schedule. Two pages.*

Ulyana counted forward. Page twenty began mid-sentence, in a different hand, a deposition from a harbor pilot. She counted backward. Page eighteen ended with a signature and a date and a full stop.

There was no statement of A. Merridew. There was no A. Merridew at all.

"No," Ulyana said, to the page, to the lamp, to the general injustice of physical objects.

"What?" Mrs. Vann leaned over, spectacles catching the light. Ulyana watched her read the index, then watch her own hands turn forward and back through the bundle, once, twice, a third time with increasing speed, until the old woman went very still in the way of people who have lived long enough to recognize catastrophe on sight.

"Torn," Mrs. Vann said finally. "Not damaged. Torn. Look at the stub — clean edge, pulled against a straight blade or a ruled edge. Somebody took that page out deliberately, and they took it out *carefully*, which means they knew what they were taking." She looked up. "When?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Ulyana laid the bundle flat and stared at the gap where history had been amputated. "Before the box went into the cave, or after. If before — someone aboard the *Meridian Star* removed it. If after—" She stopped, because the arithmetic finished itself, ugly and simple. "If after, then whoever hid the box opened it. Or someone they trusted did."

The silence that followed had weight. Mrs. Vann picked up the alcohol bottle, poured two fingers into a tin cup, drank half of it in one motion, and pushed the cup across the counter to Ulyana.

"Drink," she said. "Then think. In that order. Thinking dry is how young people convince themselves of terrible things about old ones."

Ulyana drank. The alcohol lit a small clean fire all the way down, and behind the fire, unhelpfully, her mind laid out the possibilities like track sections on a survey table.

One: the page had never reached the cave. Someone on the ship — the captain, the clerk, a passenger — had pulled it between the syndicate's offices and the wreck. Possible. But the whole point of the *Meridian Star*'s run had been secrecy; the manifest itself was hidden inside a false bulkhead, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a strongbox stamped with her family's initials. You didn't build a vault that careful and leave the crown jewel loose in a coat pocket.

Two: the page had been removed from the box during its fifty years in the flooded chamber. Which meant someone had been down there. Someone who could dive, or knew someone who could, sometime in five decades when the Tollgate opened — and the Tollgate, Mrs. Vann had told her, opened rarely, briefly, and only ever for people willing to pay what the sea charged.

Three—

Three was the thought she didn't want, sitting in the corner of her mind like a watcher on the quay. Three involved the person who had known exactly where the strongbox was, exactly what it contained, and exactly which single page mattered most. Three involved the only living soul besides herself who had touched that box before last night.

"Mrs. Vann," Ulyana said carefully, "when your son carried the box into the cave. Did he seal it? Was it already—"

"It was sealed." The old woman's voice had gone flat and distant, a voice reading from a chart of waters she'd sworn never to sail again. "I sealed it myself. Wax and canvas, my husband's kit." She stopped. Ulyana watched the word land — *husband* — watched it land the way a heavy crate lands, all at once and on whatever happens to be underneath.

"Your husband," Ulyana said. "The keeper before you."

"My husband." Mrs. Vann refilled the cup, though neither of them had asked for more. "Edmund. Who knew the cave better than any diver in the fleet. Who kept the Tollgate's books for thirty years. And who—" she laughed, one dry note with nothing behind it, "—who died in his bed four years back, respectable as a church collection plate, while I spent every night since wondering."

"Wondering what?"

"Wondering why a man who counted every coin that crossed that cave threshold, who recorded every visitor in a ledger finer than this one, never once wrote down what was in the box he helped bury." She turned the cup slowly on the counter, a small orbit, a planet with troubles. "I told myself it was mercy. That he spared me the knowing. Edmund was kind in his way, in the way of men who are kind to everyone except the one person standing closest."

Ulyana said nothing. Outside, the tide was turning; she could hear it in the pilings, a long exhale under the town. Six tides, she'd calculated, and two already spent. The clock under everything was still running.

"Here's what I know," Mrs. Vann said at last, and her spine straightened like a woman taking on cargo she intended to deliver personally. "That page named the chairman. Not the syndicate — the syndicate is a building, and buildings don't hang. A *man*. A. Merridew. And if that page is gone, then either the sea took it, or a hand took it, and I have spent fifty years learning the difference between what the sea does and what men blame on it." She fixed Ulyana with a look that had probably made grown sailors apologize for weather. "So tomorrow we're going to open Edmund's things. Every drawer, every book, every false bottom in that house, because if my husband tore that page out, he didn't burn it. Men who tear pages out are men who mean to use them later. Cowardice hoards. It doesn't destroy."

"And if we find nothing?"

Mrs. Vann smiled, and for the first time all night the smile had teeth in it.

"Then we find nothing," she said, "and I spend what's left of my reputation asking the fleet's divers, one by one, who's been down the Tollgate these past fifty years — starting with the ones Adelene Harrow pays best. Because somebody taught those divers that cave, and it wasn't the almanac."

Ulyana looked down at the preserved pages, nineteen survivors lined up on linen rags, waxed and steady and nearly enough. Nearly. The word sat in her chest like a stone placed just off-center — the load path wrong, the span a hand's width short. Proof that proved almost everything, missing the one name that would make it impossible to look away from.

She thought of her grandfather's map, every closed line inked in a hand that refused to forget. She thought of the hull groaning in the dark, of Tomas's hands hauling her clear of a falling spar, of a warrant with her name on it somewhere in a magistrate's drawer.

Almost enough was another way of saying *not yet*.

"Tomorrow, then," Ulyana said, and reached for the wax brush. "But first we finish these. All of them. Whatever's missing, what's here still deserves to live."

Mrs. Vann passed her the brush, and outside the tide kept its schedule, indifferent and exact, carrying them both toward morning.
