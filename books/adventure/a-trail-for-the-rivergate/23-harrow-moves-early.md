## 23. Harrow Moves Early

Tomas Reinhart had been summoned to Adelene Harrow's office at dawn exactly four times in his eleven years with the fleet, and he could rank them by how bad the coffee was. The first time — a diver lost off the *Kestrel* — she'd poured him a cup herself and it had tasted like forgiveness. The second and third times had been budget meetings, and the coffee had tasted like budget meetings.

This morning she didn't offer him any at all, which told him everything before she said a word.

"Sit down, Tomas."

He sat. Her office sat at the top of the Harrow fleet's main shed, all glass and brass fittings salvaged off three different wrecks, so that looking out the windows you saw the harbor through the eyes of dead ships. Adelene stood with her back to him, watching the cranes. She was a small woman who made small movements and somehow filled rooms the way ballast fills a hull — low, dense, immovable.

"Somebody was in the tunnel," she said.

The words went through Tomas like cold water through a suit seam. He kept his face still. Eleven years of practice. "Which tunnel?"

"Don't." She turned around. On her desk lay a photograph, grainy, taken from the ridge above the old rail cut: a figure in a wetsuit at the collapsed mouth of the inland tunnel, crouched over something. The figure was broad-shouldered. The figure was not Ulyana Okonkwo.

It was, unfortunately for everyone involved, a very flattering angle of Tomas himself.

"That could be anyone," he said. "Half the town owns a wetsuit."

"Half the town," Adelene agreed pleasantly, "does not own a fleet-issue dive light with a serial number I can read from this photograph because I paid extra for the good lenses." She tapped the image. "You were surveying my tunnel two nights ago without authorization. And you came back and told me the entrance was impassable."

"It is impassable. For salvage work. For a full crew with cutting gear, yes, it's—"

"Tomas." She said his name the way she said *slack water* — as a fact about the world that did not care what he wished. "The magistrate tells me the Okonkwo woman has been in Mrs. Vann's back room three evenings running. The chandlery bought forty pounds of paraffin wax this week, which is thirty-nine more than they've bought in any week since I've kept books. Somebody is preserving paper. Paper from my wreck."

*Her wreck.* He noted that. Not the Meridian Star, not the wreck — *my*. Adelene Harrow had never once in his hearing called it anything but hers, though no court had given her a deed, though the sea had held it fifty years before her grandfather's grandfather ever strung a net.

"I corrected her tide tables," Tomas said carefully. "That's not aiding an outsider. That's keeping a stranger from drowning on my watch. There's a difference."

"There is." Adelene crossed to the window. Below, the morning shift was mustering, forty divers and deckhands in oilskins, breath steaming. "I've thought about that difference myself. All night, actually. Here's where I landed." She turned. "If she gets that manifest out and it says what Vann's people whisper it says, the fleet doesn't lose a claim. The fleet loses its name. Every contract we hold, every harbor that lets us moor, every bank — gone inside a season. My father built this fleet on the Rivergate run. His father built it on the wreck money. You want to talk to me about the difference between aiding and not-aiding? I'm telling you there isn't one anymore. There's only before and after."

"So what do you want from me?"

"What I always want. Your professional assessment." She slid a folded drawing across the desk. It was a cross-section of the inland tunnel — his own survey lines, traced from his own notebook, which he now understood had been copied while he slept. Someone had drawn charges in the collapse zone. Six of them, spaced like teeth.

"We blast the fall open," Adelene said. "Pump the gallery, walk a crew down the old rail bed into the chamber, take the wreck apart with torches before the sixth tide. Manifest, strongbox, safe, nameplate — everything comes up as scrap, and scrap doesn't testify."

Tomas looked at the drawing for a long moment. He made himself look at it the way he'd look at any job, because if he looked at it like a man reading his own death warrant, she'd see that too.

"You can't pump that gallery," he said. "The fall isn't stable. It's not compacted rubble — it's a suspended arch of loose stone sitting on top of flooded workings. You put six charges in it, you don't open a door. You pull the one thread holding the whole ceiling up."

"The chamber floods worse. So what? We're divers."

"The chamber floods worse and *comes down*, Adelene. The hull's already failing — I told you that much. A pressure wave through a collapsing wreck, in the dark, with your crew swimming blind through debris—" He stopped. Made his voice flat, professional, the voice of a man discussing tide tables. "You'll kill divers. Maybe four, maybe eight. And you'll bury the manifest under forty tons of rock, which means the one thing you're trying to destroy becomes the one thing nobody can ever prove you destroyed. The story just... stays buried. Forever. Is that the outcome you're buying?"

For the first time since he'd known her, Adelene Harrow hesitated.

He watched it happen — watched something move behind her eyes like a fish under ice — and he understood, with a sick lurch, that she had already priced the divers in. That his question hadn't introduced a cost she hadn't considered; it had merely reminded her of a line item she'd rounded down.

"They knew the risks when they signed," she said quietly.

And there it was. Tomas felt something in his chest go still and cold, the way water goes still and cold below the thermocline, where you can swim through it and know you've crossed into a different sea.

Eleven years. She'd taught him knots. She'd sat with him in the shed at fourteen years old, both of them greasy to the elbows, rebuilding a regulator her father had written off, and she'd said, *the equipment doesn't owe you anything, Tomas, but you owe it everything, because it's the only thing between you and the deep.* He had built his whole life on that sentence. Duty ran downward through him like a keel bolt — through the contract, through the fleet, through the Harrow name stamped on his gear bags. He had believed, right up until this morning, that the duty pointed at something solid.

But duty to a woman who would spend her own crews' lives to bury paper wasn't duty. It was just obedience wearing its clothes.

"When?" he asked.

"Tomorrow's ebb. Low slack gives us four hours of working water in the cut." She studied him, and he gave her nothing back — the long practice holding, one last time. "You'll run the charge placement. Nobody knows that fall better than you."

"No," Tomas said.

The word came out of him with surprising ease, like a weight belt unclipped. Adelene's eyebrows rose a fraction.

"No?"

"I won't place the charges. I won't be part of it." He stood. His legs felt strange, lighter, as if he'd surfaced too fast. "You can have my opinion for free: it will fail, and it will fail expensively. Put that in your books."

"Careful, Tomas." Her voice stayed soft, which was worse than shouting. Soft was how she talked to men she was calculating against. "Your contract has clauses. You've read them. Anyone who aids an outsider forfeits everything — pay, rights, standing. For life. You've got eleven years vested. A pension. Your boat."

"I know what I've got."

"Do you?" She came around the desk, and up close he could see she was tired — genuinely tired, in a way that almost made him pity her. Almost. "Because from where I stand, you've got a choice between a family that raised you and a stranger who's been here three weeks. Families forgive, Tomas. Strangers don't even remember."

"She remembers," Tomas said. "That's rather the whole point of her."

He walked out. His knees were shaking by the time he hit the stairs, and he found he didn't mind. Shaking meant alive. Shaking meant the blood had remembered it had somewhere to be.

* * *

The harbor at mid-morning was all business, and the business was him pretending none of it was.

He crossed the yard with his kit bag over his shoulder, nodding to the deckhands, checking a mooring line that didn't need checking, doing the ordinary pantomime of an ordinary day. Old Piet was re-tarring the *Kestrel*'s rail and called out something about his face looking like a dropped pie. Tomas told him the pie should be flattered. Piet laughed. It occurred to Tomas, walking on, that this might be one of the last mornings anyone in this yard laughed at anything he said, and he made himself stop and enjoy it anyway. Grief could wait five seconds. Pie jokes couldn't.

The problem, he admitted to himself as he reached the seawall, was arithmetic.

Adelene would blast tomorrow at low slack whether he helped or not. If he refused outright and vanished, she'd simply use another charge man — Dree Halloran, probably, who was competent and careful and deserved better than a ceiling falling on her. If he reported the plan to Ulyana, they gained a day's warning and nothing else; you couldn't argue with dynamite, you could only be somewhere else when it went off. And if the charges went in as drawn, the fall would come down exactly as he'd said, the chamber would become a tomb of rock and rusted iron, and the manifest — the whole reason any of them were lying awake at night — would be sealed under the sea until the sun burned out.

Unless.

He stood at the seawall and let the idea arrive, the way ideas do when you've stopped fighting them: slowly, then all at once, like a tide turning.

Equipment failed. That was a fact of the trade. Compressors fouled. Detonators corroded — salt air, notoriously. Blasting machines developed faults in their magneto windings that no one could reproduce later, on a bench, in daylight. In eleven years Tomas had filed maybe sixty failure reports, and every single one had been true, and the trick — the entire art of it — was that a true report and a convenient one could wear the same coat.

His stomach turned over. Not at the deception itself; he found, examining himself honestly, that he didn't much mind deceiving Adelene anymore. What turned his stomach was the smaller thing underneath: that he was good at this. That eleven years of dutiful competence had made him precisely the man who could sabotage a demolition convincingly. Every skill she'd paid him to build was about to be used against her, and the skills didn't care. Tools never did.

"You're talking to yourself on a public wall," said a voice behind him, "which in some towns gets you a diagnosis."

Ulyana Okonkwo dropped onto the seawall beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, far enough that no watcher across the harbor would call it anything. She had chalk dust on her sleeve and the particular set of her jaw that meant she'd spent the morning doing sums she hated.

"How much do you know?" he asked.

"That the chandlery sold forty pounds of wax this week and someone's counting." She glanced at him sideways. "That you were photographed at the tunnel two nights ago by somebody with expensive lenses. And that you're sitting here looking like a man who's just read his own contract aloud in his head."

"They're blasting the tunnel. Tomorrow's ebb. Six charges in the fall, then pump and strip the wreck before the sixth tide." He said it flatly, all of it, because she deserved it flat. "I told her it would bring the chamber down. I don't think that changed her mind. I think it confirmed the price was acceptable."

Ulyana was quiet. Out past the mole, the water lay flat and silver, keeping its own counsel, keeping its schedule. When she finally spoke, her voice had the engineer's edge on it — grief planed down into geometry.

"If the chamber collapses, the manifest dies with it. Everything. Grandfather's letter, the log, all of it — under rock, unrecoverable, forever." She stared at the water. "Fifty years it waited. It gets one week, and she ends it with powder."

"So we have until tomorrow's low slack," Tomas said, "to decide what kind of problem we are."

"*We,*" she repeated, softly, testing the word.

He hadn't meant to say it. He examined the sentence retroactively, the way he'd examine a weld, and found he didn't want to repair it. "We," he confirmed. "I burned my cover this morning. Told her no to her face. There's a penalty clause with my name on it now, so as far as the fleet's concerned, I'm either with you or I'm unemployed forever, and I find those have started to look like the same thing."

She turned and looked at him fully then, and whatever was in her face, she held it a moment longer than was strictly necessary, and he held it right back.

"All right," she said. "Then here's our inventory. One tunnel, half-blocked, rigged for demolition in roughly twenty-six hours. One wreck, structurally failing, containing the last evidence in existence. One fleet, forty divers, six charges, and a boss who'd rather bury the truth than salvage it." She ticked them off on chalky fingers. "Against that: one engineer, one diver, one keeper, one town of uncertain loyalty, and—" she patted his kit bag "—whatever's in there."

"Spare regulators. A rail jack. Two sandwiches."

"One of those is going to matter enormously and I refuse to guess which."

Despite everything — despite the charges waiting in Adelene's drawer and the warrant he could feel being drafted somewhere above the magistrate's tidy office — Tomas laughed. It came up out of him rough and real, and Ulyana smiled like she'd been waiting all week for the sound.

They sat on the seawall while the harbor worked around them, two saboteurs-in-waiting sharing a view of the fleet's cranes, and Tomas understood that the man who had walked into Adelene's office at dawn was not the man sitting here now. The keel bolt had come out. He was adrift. And strangely, terrifyingly, it felt less like sinking than like finally being steered.

"Tomorrow," Ulyana said at last, watching the water begin its long slow exhale toward the ebb, "when they carry those charges up the cut... somebody's going to have to be standing next to the blasting machine."

Tomas looked at her.

"Somebody," he said, "with a documented history of equipment failures."

* * *

He spent the afternoon being scrupulously, boringly visible.

He serviced compressors in the yard where anyone could see him. He signed the fuel ledger. He ate lunch on the *Kestrel*'s deck in plain view and complained about the onions. Twice, watchers he recognized from Adelene's payroll drifted past the fence, and twice he gave them nothing but a man doing chores — because the beautiful thing about a reputation for reliability was that it made the perfect camouflage, right up until the moment you spent it all at once.

At dusk he climbed to the ridge above the rail cut and looked down at the tunnel mouth in the last light. Tomorrow six charges would sit in that fall, wired back to a machine no bigger than a lunchbox, and one turn of a handle would decide whether the truth lived or died under a hundred tons of stone.

He took out his notebook — the real one, the one whose copy had betrayed him — and began, in a neat professional hand, to draft a failure report for equipment that had not yet failed.

*Detonator circuit, line three: continuity fault suspected. Magneto output irregular under load. Recommend full stand-down pending inspection.*

Every word of it would be true by tomorrow evening. He closed the book and sat a while longer in the dark, listening to the tide come in below, patient as a creditor.

Twenty-six hours. Somewhere down in that black water, a strongbox waited with his future's initials stamped on it, and up here a man who had never broken a rule in his life was sharpening his first one.
