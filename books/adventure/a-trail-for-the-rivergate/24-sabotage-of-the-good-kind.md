## 24. Sabotage of the Good Kind

Tomas Reinhart had spent eleven years learning to read the Harrow fleet the way other men read weather. He knew what Adelene's silences meant. He knew which foremen smiled before they lied. And he knew — had known since the moment she'd said *open it by force* — that the woman who signed his pay was about to do something that no amount of salt water would ever wash clean.

The blasting charge sat on the dock in a wooden crate stenciled DYNAMITE — HANDLE LIKE A PROMISE, which Tomas thought was the most honest thing the fleet had ever printed.

* * *

The morning began badly and got ambitious about it.

Adelene Harrow stood at the head of the inland tunnel with a surveyor's map pinned to a board, and she did not look at her divers when she spoke, which was how they all knew this was not a briefing but a verdict.

"The tunnel mouth is half-collapsed," she said. "That's an engineering problem, and I don't employ engineers, because engineers ask questions. I employ men who can move rock." She tapped the map. "We blast at slack water tomorrow. The chamber floods, the wreck settles, and whatever paper the Okonkwo woman is chasing turns to porridge. Questions?"

There were none, because there never were, and that was precisely the disease.

Tomas raised his hand anyway.

Adelene's jaw tightened in a way he'd learned to interpret as *you are spending credit you have not earned back yet.* But she nodded, because Tomas Reinhart asking a question was still, in her ledger, cheaper than Tomas Reinhart going quiet.

"Structurally," Tomas said, "the tunnel roof is limestone under clay. You set off a charge at the mouth, the shock travels down the gallery like a hammer down a bell. There's a chance the whole passage seals itself behind your own crew."

"There's a chance," Adelene said, "that you're forgetting whose rope you're breathing from."

"I'm remembering," Tomas said. "That's why I asked."

She dismissed them with a flick of two fingers, and the divers filed out into the gray morning like men leaving a church where the sermon had been about them.

* * *

He told himself, walking back along the seawall, that there might be another way. That maybe the tide would turn against her first. That maybe Mrs. Vann's townsfolk would stand on the tracks forever and the standoff would simply calcify, everyone too stubborn to move, until the sixth tide came and closed the cave and settled the matter the sea's way.

He told himself this the way a man tells himself the leak in the hull will hold. It was true right up until it wasn't.

The problem was simple, and simple problems were the ones that killed divers: if the blast went tomorrow at slack water, the chamber collapsed, the Meridian Star went from wreck to rubble, and every page of paper inside it — the manifest, the log, Ulyana's grandfather's letter, fifty years of truth packed in a strongbox like a seed — became pulp. Not destroyed, exactly. Worse than destroyed. Made meaningless. The syndicate wouldn't even need to burn anything. The sea would do the burning for them, politely, free of charge.

And Ulyana would be standing somewhere on the tide line watching it happen, and she would look at him, and he would have nothing to say, because he would have been on the crew that did it.

Tomas stopped at the rail where the harbor cranes stood up against the sky like patient gallows, and he made the decision the way he made most decisions: slowly, all day, pretending he wasn't making it.

* * *

By afternoon he had a plan, and the plan was embarrassing, which is how he knew it would work. Good sabotage is boring. Nobody guards against boring.

The fleet's air compressor — the great wheezing beast that fed life to every diver on the payroll — lived in a shed near the tunnel staging area, and it was old enough to vote twice. Its pressure gauge was temperamental. Its fuel line was held together partly by brass fittings and partly by prayer. Every man on the fleet had cursed it, nursed it, and privately marveled that it ran at all.

At four o'clock, Tomas reported a fault.

Not dramatically. Dramatic faults get investigated. He reported it the way you report a headache: wearily, factually, with the tone of a man who has seen this coming for weeks. He showed the foreman the gauge reading — which he had adjusted that morning with a jeweler's screwdriver and ten minutes of privacy — and pointed out the faint smell of fuel that he had, an hour earlier, ensured existed by loosening a fitting exactly one quarter-turn. Enough to weep diesel onto the housing. Not enough to actually leak, if anyone checked closely. Nobody checks closely. Checking closely is for people who aren't in a hurry.

"Compressor's dying," he said. "Could be the governor. Could be the pump seals. Either way, you don't want it seizing mid-blast-prep with half the crew on air lines."

The foreman, a slab of a man named Cutter whose entire personality fit in a lunch pail, squinted at the gauge. "How long to fix?"

"Parts," Tomas said, with the gravity of a priest naming a sin. "Governor spring, probably. Nothing in Rivergate stocks it. Nearest supplier's two days' run up the coast."

"Two days." Cutter said it like the words were heavy stones he was being asked to swallow.

"Tides don't care," Tomas said, shrugging with his whole body, a masterpiece of helpless masculinity. "Machines don't either. That's the difference between us and them."

Cutter stared at the compressor. The compressor, sensing its moment, chose violence: it backfired, shuddered, and died with a sound like a whale clearing its throat.

Even Tomas flinched. He hadn't planned that part. He'd only loosened the fuel fitting; apparently the machine had heard its cue and decided to commit to the role. He would remember, later, feeling something close to affection for the ugly old brute. It was the first member of the Harrow fleet to take his side.

"Son of a bitch," said Cutter.

"Two days," said Tomas, sympathetically.

* * *

Word reached Adelene within the hour, as everything did, and she came down to the shed herself, which was bad. She stood over the open engine housing while Tomas knelt beside it with grease on his hands and a lie in his mouth, and she watched him work the way a hawk watches a field — not hunting yet, just pricing the rabbits.

"You checked the fuel line?" she asked.

"First thing," Tomas said. It was technically true. It was the first thing he'd checked, after adjusting it.

"And the gauge?"

"Gauge reads what it reads." He held it up so she could see the needle sitting in the red. "I can bleed the lines and try a restart, but if the governor's gone, forcing it could throw a rod. Then you're not losing two days. You're losing the compressor."

Adelene was silent for a long moment. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead, as gulls do, employed by nobody, loyal to nothing, and Tomas envied them with his whole heart.

"Borrow one from the *Kestrel*," Adelene said finally. "Rig a line across."

"The *Kestrel*'s compressor is older than this one. It's older than me. Running both fleets off it means air rationing, and you can't blast and dive on the same air supply without—"

"I know what it means, Reinhart." Her voice dropped, and that was worse than shouting. "I've known you since you were sixteen years old and scared of the dark water. You were the best man I had before you ever were a man at all. So look me in the eye and tell me this compressor is broken."

And here was the trap, laid flat and obvious between them, and Tomas understood that whatever happened in the next three seconds would follow him the rest of his life, whichever way he stepped.

He looked her in the eye. Eleven years of duty looked back at him through her face — the woman who'd paid for his certification when his mother couldn't, who'd put his name on the fleet roster next to men who'd dived wrecks before his grandfather was born, who had, in her terrible iron way, loved him the only shape love took in the Harrow fleet, which was as an asset.

"It's broken," Tomas said.

Something moved behind Adelene's eyes — not belief. Recognition. She knew. He could see that she knew, the way you know a tide has turned even when the surface still looks flat. She knew, and she was deciding, in real time, whether knowing was worth acting on.

"Get it running," she said. "However long it takes. And Reinhart—" she paused at the shed door, silhouetted against the harbor light, "—when this is done, you and I are going to have a conversation about what you owe."

"Yes, ma'am," said Tomas, to her back.

She left. Tomas knelt on the concrete with his hands in the guts of a machine he had murdered, and he found, to his surprise, that he wasn't shaking. He felt lighter, actually. Lighter and more doomed, which he was beginning to understand were frequently the same sensation.

Duty hadn't left him. That was the strange part. He'd expected it to feel like dropping a weight belt, all that ballast falling away into the dark. Instead it felt like the belt had turned around — like the weight had always been strapped to his front, pressing him down toward the things he actually cared about, and he'd spent eleven years mistaking the pressure for gravity instead of for direction.

He served the wreck now. The thought arrived without drama, the way the best thoughts do, plain as a depth reading. The Meridian Star was down there holding fifty years of a town's stolen conscience in its ribs, and somebody had to be on its crew. Somebody had to be its diver.

Fine. He was available.

* * *

He worked on the compressor until dusk, loudly, visibly, with much sighing and one theatrical burst of cursing when a bolt stripped — performance art, all of it, while underneath the noise his hands did the slower, quieter work of making sure the repair would take exactly as long as he needed it to. A governor spring ordered from up the coast. A fuel line cleaned and re-seated. Two days, give or take a tide.

Cutter brought him a sandwich around six and ate half of it himself, which was the closest thing to tenderness the foreman had ever displayed.

"You look different," Cutter said, mouth full.

"Tired."

"No. Different-tired." Cutter studied him with the limited but sincere perception of a man who had once noticed his own wedding anniversary. "You look like a man who's decided something."

"Machines'll do that to you," Tomas said.

Cutter grunted, unconvinced but unequipped to pursue it, and wandered off, and Tomas sat alone in the shed with the dead compressor humming its small silence, and let himself think about her.

Ulyana would be up at the chandlery or down at the tide line or wherever she went when the map in her head needed room to unfold. She didn't know yet. By tomorrow she'd have to know — the delay bought time, but time was the one cargo in this whole affair that spoiled fastest. Six tides, minus the ones already spent, minus the two days he'd just stolen for her. The arithmetic was getting ugly. The arithmetic was always getting ugly. That was the sea's whole business model.

He'd tell her tonight. After dark, past the watchers Harrow had posted on both mouths, some way he hadn't figured out yet, because of course he hadn't — he was a man who fixed compressors and lied to hawks, not a man who snuck past sentries. He'd figure it out. He'd been figuring things out underwater for eleven years, where mistakes cost lungs full of seawater, and he'd survived those. Dry land, he reasoned, should be manageable.

Then boots on gravel outside the shed, quick and wrong — not Cutter's plod, not a foreman's stroll. Two sets. Moving with purpose.

Tomas wiped his hands slowly, buying seconds, and the door swung open on a lantern and the lean silhouette of Pell, Adelene's youngest foreman, with a piece of paper in his hand and an expression that had never once contained doubt.

"Fleet meeting," Pell said. "Boss wants all divers present." He glanced at the open compressor housing, then at Tomas, and his voice went pleasant in the way that meant the opposite. "She also wants to know why the *Kestrel*'s spare governor spring is missing from stores."

The lantern light lay across the floor between them like a line drawn in chalk.

Tomas stood up, grease to the elbows, cover burned to the collarbone, and heard himself say, calmly, in the voice of a man stepping off a ledge into water he'd already decided was deep enough:

"Because I took it. An hour ago. It's in my kit, and it's staying there until the sixth tide passes — and if you want to know what happens after that, Pell, you can ask the wreck. She's the one giving orders now."
