## 25. Wanted in Rivergate

The knock came at half past four in the morning, and it was not a polite knock.

Ulyana was awake before the second blow landed — she had been awake most of the night anyway, lying in the narrow bed above the chandlery with the preserved manifest wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboard by the washstand, listening to the tide turn and counting, as she now counted everything, in tides. Two down since the third dive. Tomas was alive. Her hands still remembered the weight of him coming up the ladder of the lock chamber, gray-lipped and grinning like an idiot, and she fell asleep some nights on that memory the way other people fell asleep on a pillow.

The third blow splintered something.

She was dressed in ninety seconds. Boots, coat, map tube, strongbox key on its cord around her neck. She'd rehearsed this without meaning to — rootless people rehearse departures the way rooted people rehearse dinner conversation — and she knew the chandlery's exits the way she knew her own pulse: back stair to the salt shed, salt shed door to the fish-curing alley, alley to the seawall, seawall to eleven different directions depending on the tide.

But when she eased open the bedroom door, Mrs. Vann was already standing at the bottom of the stairs holding a lamp, and the old keeper said, in the tone of a woman who had been waiting up for trouble for fifty years and had gotten very good at it:

"Back. Not out. Out is what they want."

---

Downstairs, pressed to the gap between the shutter slats, Ulyana watched six men with lanterns stand in the street while a seventh hammered a paper to the chandlery's doorpost. Even folded into shadow she recognized the cut of their oilskins. Harrow fleet. And behind them, at a careful distance that wanted very much to look official, stood Magistrate Corben in his nightclothes and his good coat, holding the warrant against his chest like a hymnal.

She couldn't read the paper from there. She didn't need to. She could read the shape of a thing being made legal.

"Sabotage," Mrs. Vann said quietly. "That'll be the word. Sabotage of the blasting works, theft of salvage pending claim, obstruction of a lawful operation. Corben will have found three statutes and misquoted a fourth." She turned the lamp down until the room went amber and small. "He did the same for my husband's generation. Different paperwork, same pen."

"They can't prove anything," Ulyana said. "Tomas faked the equipment failure. There's no evidence pointing at me at all."

"No," Mrs. Vann agreed. "There isn't. That has never once stopped a magistrate who's paid by the month." She looked at Ulyana over the lamp, and her eyes were the flat gray of the bay at slack water. "Question is whether you're going to be clever about it or stupid about it, girl, because I've got room for either but not for both."

* * *

By sunrise, Rivergate had decided something.

Ulyana felt it before anyone said it — the way you feel a change in air pressure in your teeth. The town that had stared at her on the day of the great ebb, that had priced her like a curiosity at Mrs. Vann's cave mouth, was now moving around her differently. The fishwife at the market slipped two smoked herring into her basket and named no price. The ferryman, a barrel-chested man called Doss who had charged her double on arrival, poled her across the harbor channel for free and then pretended he hadn't seen her get on. A boy of maybe ten fell into step beside her on the seawall, walked with her the length of it in silence, and peeled off again without a word, having apparently accomplished whatever he'd set out to accomplish, which Ulyana eventually understood was simply: *I walked with her, and nothing happened to me.*

"It's the letter," Tomas said that evening. He'd come to the chandlery through the curing alley, hood up, moving like a man who had learned in the last two days exactly how many of his fellow divers could be trusted to look away and how few. He sat down heavily at Mrs. Vann's kitchen table and put his hands around the tea she pushed at him without drinking it. "Word's out about your grandfather's letter. What you read out — well. You didn't read it out yet, but Vann read parts of it to the tavern while you were sleeping. The part where he writes that the syndicate paid every man in Rivergate a settlement and bought his silence with it."

"They weren't paid off," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question; she'd done the arithmetic weeks ago, in ledgers older than she was. "They were *bought*. There's a difference, and everyone here knows the difference, and it's been sitting in their craw for fifty years."

"Forty-one years for some families," Mrs. Vann said from the stove. "My family got nothing, because my husband took his payment in a different coin." She said it evenly, the way she said everything now, guilt worn smooth as beach glass. "Point is, the town remembers what it sold. And Harrow's just reminded them."

"And so they're hiding me," Ulyana said. "In plain sight. In a town with one road in."

"In a town with one road in," Tomas agreed, "and one magistrate, and one magistrate is all Adelene needs." He finally drank the tea. "Corben signed the warrant at dawn. Deputies are deputized — which in Rivergate means Harrow foremen wearing badges over their fleet patches, and nobody's even pretending the ink is dry on the paperwork. They're not searching houses yet. They're waiting."

"For what?"

"For you to run." He set the cup down. "That's the whole plan, Ulyana. Adelene doesn't need to catch you. She needs you to run. The moment you run, the town's sympathy breaks — sheltering a stranger is romance, chasing a fugitive is a crime, and Corben will make an example of whoever helped you. She's not hunting you. She's hunting the town's nerve."

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the tide was making, filling the harbor channel with that patient green-gray muscle, and somewhere beyond the point the Tollgate cave was swallowing the sea toward its sixth sealing, and somewhere inland the half-blasted tunnel mouth stood guarded by men with lanterns, and she thought: *I have spent my whole life being good at leaving.* Trains, towns, jobs, names. Rootless competence. Skilled everywhere, welcome nowhere, gone before the welcome wore out.

And now a fishwife had given her herring. A boy had walked her seawall. An old woman was keeping watch at her stairhead with a lamp and fifty years of practice.

"I'm not running," she said.

"I know," Tomas said. "That's what scares me."

* * *

The deputies came to the chandlery on the second day, and Mrs. Vann met them on her own doorstep with her arms folded and her son's memory standing behind her like a second keeper.

The lead deputy was a Harrow man named Quill, broad and careful, with the particular politeness of a bully who has been told to be polite. "Warrant for the arrest of one Ulyana Okonkwo," he said. "Charges of sabotage and theft. We're authorized to search the premises."

"Authorized," Mrs. Vann said. "By Magistrate Corben."

"That's right."

"The same Magistrate Corben whose boatyard lease comes up for renewal through the Harrow agency every Michaelmas?"

Quill's jaw worked. "Ma'am—"

"Search, then." She stepped aside with terrible graciousness. "Mind the floorboards, they're older than your grandmother and meaner than you."

They searched for two hours. Ulyana spent those two hours forty feet away, sitting in the loft of Doss's boathouse among coiled lines and tarred nets, watching through a gap in the planking while the boy from the seawall — whose name, she had learned, was Pip, and whose father cured fish and whose grandfather had signed the settlement and hated himself for it in the specific way of men who keep the receipt — sat beside her eating smoked herring and narrating the search through the gap like a race commentator.

"They've got the washstand up. No — Vann's telling them about the washstand. She's telling them a story about the washstand. She's been talking for four minutes about a washstand." Pip snorted. "Old lady's a fortress."

"She is," Ulyana said, and meant more than the old woman.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find — the manifest traveled now in a wax-sealed oilskin inside a hollowed-out rail spike in Ulyana's survey kit, because if there was one place in Rivergate no Harrow man would ever think to look, it was inside railway equipment. But Quill, leaving, paused on Mrs. Vann's step and delivered his line with the flatness of a man reading someone else's words:

"Fleet says the stranger runs or the town pays. Harbor levies go up triple on any business found harboring her. That's lawful. That's posted." He touched his hat brim, a gesture that managed to be both apology and threat, and left.

Mrs. Vann stood in her doorway a long time after. When Ulyana came back at dusk, the old woman was at her table with the account book from the chandlery open in front of her, doing sums, her lips moving.

"Triple levies," she said, without looking up. "Doss's ferry. The Saltrow tavern. The Chandlery." She turned a page. "You know what I told Adelene Harrow's father, forty-one years ago, when he offered me money to forget my son was in that cave? I told him the sea already had my price and he couldn't match it." Now she looked up, and there was something almost gentle in the flint. "Levies don't frighten me, girl. I've been paying the sea's rates my whole life. But the others — Doss has daughters. Saltrow owes debt. You should know what staying costs them, so you choose it with your eyes open."

"I do know," Ulyana said. "That's why I'm choosing it."

"Hm." Mrs. Vann went back to her sums. "Then eat your herring and stop looking tragic. Tragic looks bad on engineers."

* * *

On the third night, Tomas came late, wet to the shoulder, and would not say where he had been, which meant he had been diving, which meant he had been below, alone, in the failing wreck, and Ulyana decided — deliberately, the way you decide to carry a load past its rated limit — not to ask yet.

He sat close to her on the chandlery's back step, in the dark between the salt shed and the wall, where the smell of brine and pitch covered everything including, hopefully, two people talking too low.

"Corben's posted a reward," he said. "Not for capture. For *information*. Anyone who reports your location gets a year's harbor rights free." He laughed, short and bitter. "A year's harbor rights. In a fishing town. Adelene knows exactly what things cost here."

"Has anyone taken it?"

"Not yet." He was quiet a moment. "Two days ago I'd have said someone would take it by now. Tonight I'm not sure. You should have seen the tavern. Saltrow had the whole room arguing about the settlement — actual numbers, actual names, grandfathers' signatures passed around like relics. There's a woman, Marta Saltrow, stood up on a bench and said her family's pub was built on blood money and she'd rather burn it than let Harrow build a fleet empire on top of the same lie." He shook his head slowly. "Fifty years they've carried it. All it needed was somebody handing them a reason to put it down."

"Somebody," Ulyana said, "with a warrant on her head."

"Well. Yes. There's always a price on the useful ones." He glanced at her sideways, and in the dark his face was mostly voice. "How are you? Actually."

Ulyana considered lying, considered deflection, and found she was too tired for either, which was perhaps what he'd been angling for. "Like a train that's been switched onto a line that isn't on any map," she said. "Still moving. Still sound, as far as I can tell. But going somewhere I didn't survey, at a speed I didn't set, and every bridge ahead is somebody else's engineering." She pulled her knees up. "I've been hunted before. Contract disputes, a syndicate lawyer in Kesswill who wanted my grandfather's maps badly enough to follow me three towns. I knew how to be hunted then. You run, you change trains, you arrive nowhere, you leave again. It's a skill. I have it."

"But?"

"But nobody ever fed me while they hunted me." Her voice caught slightly on it, and she let it. "Nobody ever stood on their own doorstep and insulted a deputy's grandmother to buy me two hours. Nobody ever—" she gestured vaguely at the dark town, the lamplit windows, the whole improbable organism of Rivergate, "—did *this*. I know how to be worth nothing to a place, Tomas. I don't know how to be worth something. It's much harder."

He reached over and took her hand. His fingers were cold from the water and rough from the work, and she held on.

"You'll learn," he said. "You learn lines. It's what you do."

* * *

The end came at the harbor, on the fourth morning, and it came not with lanterns and warrants but with a bell.

Ulyana was on the seawall checking the tide gauge — habit now, religion really, the sea keeping its schedule and her keeping hers — when the church bell began ringing wrong. Not the Sunday pattern, not the alarm pattern she'd been taught. A ragged, urgent, hand-pulled clanging, and then voices, dozens of voices, all pointed the same direction: inland. Toward the tracks.

She ran. Everyone ran. The whole town funneled up the salt-road like water finding a drain, and she came out at the head of the valley where the old line emerged from its cutting, and stopped as if she'd hit a buffer stop.

Harrow crews were at the tunnel mouth. Not watchers this time — a working party, a full blasting train, drills and powder crates and a steam pump coughing smoke into the morning, and Adelene Harrow herself standing on the ballast in a coat worth more than the chandlery, watching her men uncrate explosives with the calm of a woman attending a christening.

Between them and the tunnel, spread across the tracks in a thin, stubborn, absolutely insufficient line, stood Rivergate. Fishwives and ferrymen and Saltrow with her sleeves rolled, and old Mrs. Vann planted dead center on the rails themselves, upright as a signal post, and Pip somewhere near the front because of course he was, and Doss, and the boy's father, and a hundred more behind them, and the two lines of people regarded each other across forty yards of ballast with the sea's own patience.

At the edge of the crowd, Tomas found her. His face was white. "They moved early," he breathed. "She's not waiting for the sixth tide. She's going to blast the tunnel open today, take the wreck by force before the law can argue, and if the chamber comes down with the manifest inside—" He swallowed. "Ulyana. If that charge goes, the truth goes with it. Buried deeper than her grandfather ever buried anything."

Adelene Harrow turned then, as if she'd heard — as if the name had carried on the wind the way names do in small towns — and looked directly across the ballast at the woman with the warrant on her head, standing openly in the crowd that refused to give her up.

She smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which was somehow worse.

"Well," Adelene Harrow said, loud enough for both lines to hear. "Everyone's here. Good. Then nobody can say they didn't see how this ends."

Behind her, the first drill bit bit into rock.
