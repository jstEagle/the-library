## 26. The Tunnel Standoff

Tomas Reinhart had stood in a lot of uncomfortable places in his life — inside a collapsing cofferdam off Cape Merrow, in Adelene Harrow's office the day she'd caught him reading her father's ledgers, once, memorably, in a wedding he'd been too polite to leave. But he had never stood anywhere as uncomfortable as the mouth of the old inland tunnel at half past six in the morning, with forty of Rivergate's townsfolk on one side of him and thirty of Harrow's crew on the other, and himself exactly nowhere.

The tunnel was a black slot cut into the hillside above the salt meadows, its brick arch furred with fifty years of ivy and pigeon droppings. It had been the Rivergate spur once — the line that carried mail, milk, and every Sunday excursion to the coast until the syndicate closed it with a signature and a shrug. Now it carried nothing but cold air that breathed out of the dark like something sleeping.

And this morning, it carried Adelene Harrow's ambitions.

"Mr. Reinhart." Her voice came through the morning fog the way her cranes came through fog — large, inevitable, slightly rusted at the edges. She stood at the front of her crew in a coat worth more than the chandlery, hands clasped behind her like a woman inspecting a garden rather than contemplating an act of vandalism against a national railway bed. "You're standing on the wrong side."

"I'm standing on the side with the people," Tomas said.

"Yes," said Adelene. "That's what I said."

Behind her, the fleet's foremen were unloading crates from a flatbed — drilling equipment, charges, enough blasting gelatin to redecorate the hillside permanently. The plan was simple and ugly: blow the collapsed section of tunnel open, run a salvage line straight down to the chamber, take the wreck before the sixth tide sealed the cave and took the argument away from everyone.

The plan had one flaw, and the flaw was currently holding a thermos of coffee and wearing a fisherman's slicker three sizes too big for him.

Old Bram Oduya stepped forward from the town's line. He was seventy-one, walked with a cane he mostly used for pointing, and had been the Rivergate stationmaster back when there had been a station. "Adelene Harrow," he said, in the voice of a man who had announced train delays to three generations and considered it practice for moments like this, "that tunnel sits on land deeded to the Coastal & Marsh Railway."

"The Coastal & Marsh Railway," Adelene said pleasantly, "has not existed since 1961."

"The *deed* exists. I've seen it. My father helped file it." Bram tapped his cane on the gravel. "And more to the point, my granddaughter is standing behind me, and she has a very good arm, and we are between you and those crates."

Tomas glanced back at the town's line. It wasn't just fishermen this morning. It was the baker, still floury. It was two of the ferrywomen with their oars over their shoulders like pikes. It was a boy of perhaps twelve who had brought his dog, which was barking at the crates of explosives with what Tomas felt was appropriate moral clarity. And near the front, arms crossed, jaw set, stood Ulyana Okonkwo — wanted by the magistrate, sheltered by the town, and looking at the tunnel mouth the way other people looked at a lover they were about to lose.

She didn't look at him. That was discipline. He appreciated it and hated it in equal measure.

* * *

Mrs. Vann had dressed carefully for the occasion.

She put on her good wool coat, the navy one with the brass buttons, and her late husband's scarf, and she took from the mantel the small tin watch that had belonged to her son — the one lost to the cave fifty years ago, the one whose face still read 4:17 because the sea had stopped it mid-tide — and she put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, where a mother keeps things.

Then she walked out of the chandlery, past the young magistrate's deputy who was supposed to be watching for Ulyana Okonkwo and instead touched his hat brim and looked studiously at a seagull, and made her way up the salt meadow path toward the tunnel.

She arrived at the standoff the way weather arrives — gradually, then all at once. One moment the lines were shouting at each other; the next, a hush went down both sides of the gravel like a wave flattening, because everyone in Rivergate knew Edith Vann, and everyone knew what the cave had cost her, and nobody — not even Harrow's foremen, who were mostly local boys grown tall and hired back — wanted to be the one who made her walk further than she had to.

Mrs. Vann did not stop at either line. She walked between them, up the old rail bed, until she stood directly in front of the tunnel mouth, on the tracks themselves, the rails emerging from the weeds under her sensible shoes like the ribs of something long buried.

Then she sat down. On the rail. With the dignity of a duchess taking a throne.

"Oh no," said one of Harrow's foremen, a big soft-spoken man named Pell. "Oh, ma'am, please don't."

"Don't what?" said Mrs. Vann. "I'm resting. I'm seventy-eight years old. Resting is legal."

"You're sitting on the tracks we're meant to clear."

"Then you'll have to move me." She folded her hands in her lap. "I'd like to see the paperwork on that."

* * *

Ulyana watched from the town's line and felt something in her chest do a thing she didn't have engineering vocabulary for.

She had spent her whole life around structures — bridges, trestles, the bones of dead railways — and she knew load-bearing when she saw it. Edith Vann, sitting on that rail in her navy coat, was bearing more load than any pier or girder in Rivergate. Fifty years ago, her husband had been the cave's keeper, and he had sold out — torn the naming page from the manifest, taken whatever the syndicate paid, and let a framed man's name rot while his own family's grief calcified into silence. Ulyana had found that out two nights ago, in the chandlery's back room, lamplight and wax and the smell of alcohol, and she had watched Mrs. Vann hold her husband's shame in her lap like a sleeping animal she'd finally stopped pretending wasn't there.

*He did it for us,* the old woman had said. *That's the worst part. He thought he was protecting me and the boy's memory, and instead he protected the men who killed the boy.* A long pause. *Forgiveness isn't a feeling, girl. It's a debt you pay forward. Go pay mine.*

So here Ulyana stood, paying it, watching the creditor sit down on the tracks.

Beside her, Bram Oduya muttered, "Your grandfather would have liked Edith."

"He'd have liked her timetable better," Ulyana said. "Low slack is at 9:40. We have maybe three hours before the fourth tide starts turning, and if that tunnel gets blown before then—" She stopped. The math was bad and she didn't want to say it out loud where Harrow's people could hear. If the charges went off, the shockwave wouldn't just open the collapsed section; it would shake the whole gallery, and the gallery held a wreck that Tomas had privately told her was already shedding plates like an old coat. The evidence — the log, the letter, everything — would be under forty tons of hillside.

Three hours. And between them and the water: thirty crewmen, a crate of gelatin, and one old woman on a rail.

* * *

Adelene Harrow, to her credit, tried reason first. It was her habit, and it always surprised people.

"Mrs. Vann." She approached alone, hands still clasped, coat immaculate despite the mud trying its best. "Nobody wants you hurt. This is a commercial operation on contested ground. There are courts—"

"There are courts," Mrs. Vann agreed. "There's also a tide table. Which one of us do you think the sea answers to?"

"The sea doesn't answer to anyone. That's rather my point. In four days the cave seals itself and the wreck is legally accessible again next season, through proper channels—"

"Next season," Mrs. Vann said, "the paper in that wreck will be soup. You know it. I know it. That's why you're in a hurry, dear, and why you're lying badly." She patted the rail beside her. "Sit. It's colder than it looks, but so is the truth."

Adelene did not sit. Something moved behind her eyes, though — Tomas saw it from twenty feet away, the flicker of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. Adelene Harrow could out-wait a court. She could not out-wait a tide, and everyone on that gravel now knew it.

"Move her," Adelene said quietly, to Pell.

Pell looked at Mrs. Vann. Mrs. Vann looked at Pell. Pell had gone to school with Mrs. Vann's grandson, and Mrs. Vann had once chased him three streets for throwing stones at her windows, and had then fed him jam sandwiches, and he remembered both events with equal vividness.

"Ma'am," Pell said, "I'm going to need you to—"

"No," said Mrs. Vann.

"Ma'am—"

"*No,*" said Mrs. Vann, and then, in a voice that carried down the meadow and into the tunnel and possibly out the far flooded end into the bay itself: "Fifty years ago, men came to this town with money, and we took it, and we let them close these tracks and bury what happened in that water. I was married to one of them. I ate at his table. I kept his secret longer than I kept my son's watch wound." She laid one hand flat on the cold steel of the rail. "This town paid its money back a hundred times in silence. Today the bill comes due, and I am the first payment. Who's second?"

For a moment, nothing. Gulls argued overhead. The dog barked once, hopefully.

Then the baker stepped over the line. Then the two ferrywomen. Then Bram Oduya, planting his cane on the rail bed like a flag. Then a dozen more, and a dozen after that, until the town's loose crowd had become a wall of slickers and aprons and Sunday coats, filling the space between Harrow's crew and the tunnel mouth shoulder to shoulder, and Pell found himself looking at a human embankment and understanding, professionally, that some loads cannot be moved without breaking what holds them.

Adelene Harrow stood alone in the gap for a long moment. Tomas watched her look at her crews — local boys, every one, boys whose grandfathers had taken the payoff money, boys who were now looking at their boots or, worse, at the town's side of the line — and he watched her understand that the only thing she could order them to do this morning was lose.

"We're done here today," she said at last, crisp as a ledger closing. "But the tide turns tomorrow, gentlemen, and so does the law. Enjoy your morning."

The flatbed backed away down the meadow road. The dog barked at it triumphantly, having personally won the entire engagement.

* * *

They cheered, of course. Towns do. Bram shook his cane at the retreating truck with the satisfaction of a man returning a library book fifty years overdue. Someone started singing, badly, an old railway song about the Coastal & Marsh line, and got most of the words wrong, and nobody corrected him.

Mrs. Vann stayed on the rail a moment longer. Ulyana came up the bed and offered her a hand, and the old woman took it, and rose, joints announcing their positions like a stationmaster calling stops.

"You sat down on cold iron for me," Ulyana said.

"For the debt," Mrs. Vann corrected. "Don't go getting sentimental, girl, it slows the work." She pressed something into Ulyana's palm — small, metal, warm from her pocket. The watch. 4:17 forever. "When you go down on the next slack — and you will, don't pretend you weren't already planning it, you have your grandfather's face when you calculate — find the captain's cabin. Portside, below the wheelhouse. And bring me back what the sea owes. Not what it took. What it *owes*. There's a difference, and you of all people should be able to draw it on one of your maps."

Ulyana closed her hand around the watch and couldn't speak for a second. "The safe," she managed. "In the wheelhouse. You think the missing page—"

"I think my husband tore one page out of a strongbox," Mrs. Vann said, "but a captain keeps his own books, and captains are stubborn men who write things twice. Go find out. And—" her voice roughened, just barely, a crack in old glaze "—bring the boy back too. Both of you. I'm not losing anyone else to that water. I've paid my share."

* * *

Tomas found Ulyana afterward, up on the embankment where the ivy grew thickest, studying the tunnel arch with her survey kit open on her knees like a surgeon's tray.

"They'll come back tonight," she said, without looking up. "Not with crowds. With a night crew and a permit signed by our friendly magistrate. Adelene doesn't retreat; she repositions."

"I know."

"So we don't wait for the fourth tide at 9:40." She turned a page of tide tables, and he saw the columns of figures she'd been working since dawn, the pencil marks dense as birdshot. "Slack water comes early inside the gallery when the barometric pressure drops — your own fleet's logs say so, I read them last week while everyone assumed I was admiring the cranes. If the low holds through the morning, we can dive at first light. Beat them by hours."

"It's a deeper dive on shorter rest," Tomas said. "Third descent in two days. Your hands are shaking."

"My hands are *cold*," Ulyana said. "There's a difference, and you of all people should be able to measure it."

Despite everything — the warrant with her name on it, the contract with his, the wreck groaning in the dark below, the entire Harrow fleet repositioning somewhere beyond the fog — Tomas laughed. It came out of him sideways, unplanned, and she looked up at him with an expression that was almost a smile, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the ivy dripped, and somewhere below them the sea turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, keeping its schedule.

Then Ulyana's head snapped toward the meadow road.

Down in the gray light, small with distance, a single figure was walking back up toward the tunnel — not the flatbed, not the crew. One man, carrying a lantern though the sun was fully up, walking the old rail bed with the unhurried gait of someone who had every right to be there.

Bram Oduya squinted, lowered his cane, and said, in the voice he used for announcements nobody wanted:

"That's the magistrate's clerk. And he's carrying papers."
