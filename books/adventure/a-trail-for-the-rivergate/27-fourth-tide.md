## 27. Fourth Tide

The tunnel had a new voice.

Ulyana noticed it before she saw anything — a low, mineral groan that came up through the bedrock of the chandlery floor and into her boots as she stood at the window counting masts in the harbor. The Harrow blasting crews had worked through the night at the inland mouth, and though they'd only gotten halfway — Tomas's sabotage had bought them a day, maybe less — the mountain itself now knew what was being done to it. She could feel the complaint of it in her teeth, like standing too close to a locomotive taking a curve too fast.

"Stop listening to the rock," Mrs. Vann said from behind her, without looking up from the wax pot she'd been tending since dawn. "You'll start agreeing with it."

"I'm trying to figure out how much time we have."

"You have until the sea decides you don't." Mrs. Vann stirred the pot with a wooden paddle worn smooth as driftwood. "That's always been the whole arithmetic of this town. Everything else is just people arguing about the remainder."

Ulyana turned back to the table where she'd spread her grandfather's map under four brass weights and two teacups and one very patient cat named Barnacle who had claimed the eastern marshes as his territory and would not be moved by anything short of an act of God. She'd been staring at the same stretch of line for twenty minutes — the old spur that ran from the quarry road down to the collapsed tunnel mouth — and the numbers still refused to be kind.

Here was the problem, laid out in pencil like a confession: the fourth tide would turn at 3:40 in the morning. Low slack — the dead calm between ebb and flood, when the flooded gallery inside the Tollgate went still enough to swim — lasted perhaps forty minutes. Forty minutes to descend through half-blasted rubble, cross a chamber that was shedding its own bones, crack a safe that hadn't been opened in fifty years, and get out again before the sea came home.

And that was if nothing else went wrong. Something was always going wrong. That was the other arithmetic.

* * *

Tomas arrived at noon, coming up the chandlery stairs with the particular gait of a man who had spent the morning lying to people he respected and wanted very much to stop. He carried his dive bag over one shoulder and a paper-wrapped bundle under his arm, and he set both down on the table with the care of someone defusing something.

"Barnacle," he said to the cat, nodding. "Sir."

"He doesn't answer to sir."

"He answered to sir last time."

"He ignored you last time. There's a difference."

Tomas unwrapped the bundle. Inside were two diving helmets — not the fleet's modern rigs but old brass-and-copper hats, the kind with round faceplates and hand-cranked air pumps, salvaged or borrowed or possibly stolen; Ulyana had learned not to ask Tomas direct questions about provenance because his answers had developed a lawyerly quality she found both alarming and endearing.

"The fleet's gear is logged," he said. "Every tank, every regulator, every hour of bottom time goes in Adelene's book. If I sign out equipment tonight, she knows within the hour. These are Vann's. My father used them. Before him, my grandfather." He ran a thumb along a dent in one copper collar. "They're ugly and they leak a little and they'll keep us alive for forty minutes, which is exactly the specification we need."

"They're beautiful," Ulyana said, meaning it. Her grandfather had kept a helmet like this on a shelf in his workshop, polished to a mirror shine, never explained. She'd assumed it was a trophy. Now she wondered if it had been a promise — *one day I'll go down and prove it* — kept too late.

"There's a catch," Tomas said.

"There's always a catch."

"The pump has to stay topside. Someone cranks it, steady, the whole time we're down. Too fast and the lines over-pressurize. Too slow and we're breathing soup." He looked toward the wax pot. "It can't be either of us."

Mrs. Vann didn't look up. "I'm eighty-one years old, boy, and I've been cranking air for divers since before your father could walk. Ask me properly."

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Ulyana watched him wrestle with the instinct to protect the old woman from the work and the older instinct — the wiser one — of knowing better than to try.

"Mrs. Vann," he said finally, "would you pump air for us tonight?"

"I already said yes," she said. "I was waiting to see if you'd ask like a man or like a contract. Congratulations." She pointed the paddle at Ulyana. "And you. Sit down. You look like a woman who's been doing sums all night, and your sums are wrong."

"My sums are—"

"Wrong," Mrs. Vann repeated, "because you're planning to go down there and come back the same person. Nobody comes back the same person. Plan for the extra weight."

* * *

They spent the afternoon rehearsing.

Not diving — there was no water deep enough in Rivergate to rehearse what waited inside the Tollgate — but the sequence of it, walked through on the chandlery floor with chalk marks for bulkheads and a broom handle standing in for the captain's safe. Ulyana drew the wreck from memory: the Meridian Star on her port side, cargo hold aft, wheelhouse forward and above, the safe bolted beneath the chart table where a captain would want it, low and heavy and near the door.

"The safe is the problem," she said, tapping the chalk circle. "Everything else is swimming. The safe is engineering. Combination locks from that era are three-wheel, thirty numbers each. If I know the combination, ninety seconds. If I don't—" she did the math aloud, because saying it made it real, "—twenty-seven thousand combinations. At five seconds a try underwater, wearing gloves, in the dark, that's a year and a half."

"So we know the combination," Tomas said.

"We know the combination," Ulyana agreed, "or we don't go."

She'd spent the morning turning it over. A ship's captain chose a number he wouldn't forget — a birthday, a hull number, a date. And the Meridian Star's captain had known he was carrying evidence worth killing for. He'd have chosen something the syndicate couldn't guess but his own people could recover. Something tied to the ship herself.

"Hull number," Tomas said slowly. "Meridian Star, built what, 1890-something?"

"1893. Hull 4471, out of Corvane yards." She'd read it in the chandlery ledgers so many times she could recite it like a prayer. "But that's only three digits."

"Then it's a date. Launch date?"

"April ninth, 1893." She wrote it in chalk: 9–4–93. Stared at it. It sat on the floor looking plausible and useless, one guess among thousands.

"Or," Tomas said quietly, "it's something about the man himself. His daughter's birthday. His wedding day. We can't know."

"No," Ulyana said. "But his logbook might. Captains write everything down. If the log is in the wheelhouse — and logs live in the wheelhouse — there may be a note, a reminder, something." She looked at the chalk drawing of the wreck, at the little circle she'd marked for the safe, and felt the plan assemble itself the way a bridge does: not all at once, but load finding its path to ground. "So we take both. Log first, then safe. If the safe defeats us, we still come out with the log."

"And if the log is pulp after fifty years?"

"Then we come out with wet paper and our lives, and I will read every word of it anyway if I have to hold the pages up to the sun like stained glass."

Tomas laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody. "You've been spending too much time with Mrs. Vann."

"There is no such thing," said Mrs. Vann, from the wax pot.

* * *

Evening came down over Rivergate the way it always did, gold going to gray going to that particular blue the town wore like a uniform. From the chandlery window Ulyana could see the Harrow fleet's cranes against the last light, motionless now, and beyond them the dark line of the headland behind which the Tollgate breathed. Somewhere past the headland, floodlights burned at the half-blasted tunnel mouth, and men in Adelene Harrow's pay measured rubble and argued about charges.

Tomas stood beside her at the window, close enough that their shoulders touched, neither of them making anything of it, both of them making everything of it.

"My father dove the Tollgate eleven times," he said. "Before the cave closed. He told me once — I must have been twelve — that the worst part isn't the dark or the cold. It's the moment when you stop being afraid. Because that's when you make mistakes." He turned the dented helmet over in his hands. "I've been afraid since the day I met you, Ulyana. Properly afraid. Afraid of losing my trade, my name, everything the fleet gave me. And somewhere in the last week the fear stopped working. I keep catching myself not being afraid, and then I remember what my father said, and I get afraid of *that*."

Ulyana considered this. Outside, a gull screamed something uncharitable about the harbor.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I surveyed a bridge that was scheduled for demolition. Beautiful old thing, iron, my grandfather's era. Everyone knew it was coming down. And I found a crack in the third truss that meant it would come down on its own before the demolition crew ever got there — maybe with someone underneath. So I reported it. And the company thanked me, and demolished it early, and fired me for the delay, and I stood on the bank watching it fall and thought: *that's what competence gets you. A front-row seat.*" She looked at him. "I've been rootless for fifteen years, Tomas. Skilled everywhere, welcome nowhere. And I told myself that was freedom. It wasn't. It was just fear with a timetable."

"And now?"

"Now I'm terrified," she said, "and I'm staying anyway. I think that's the difference. Fear with somewhere to stand."

He was quiet a moment. Then, with the gentle humor that was his way of handling anything too large to lift directly: "Fear with somewhere to stand. I'm putting that on my next invoice."

"Put it on mine. I'll frame it."

* * *

At midnight they loaded the gear.

Mrs. Vann's old helmets went into a fish cart borrowed from a neighbor who asked no questions because Mrs. Vann had once pulled his brother's body out of the Tollgate and some debts outrank curiosity. The air pump went in last, gleaming with fresh grease, its crank handle wrapped in cloth. Ulyana packed her survey kit — wrenches, the rail jack, candles, waterproofed matches, and the strongbox's manifest wrapped in waxed canvas against her chest, because she had decided she was never letting it out of arm's reach again and had stopped pretending this was rational.

The town was quiet in the way of towns holding their breath. Word had traveled — it always traveled in Rivergate, faster than the ferries — and as the cart rattled down toward the headland road, Ulyana saw doors standing open along the lane, lamplight spilling out, people standing in the gaps watching them pass. No one spoke. An old man raised two fingers off his cap. A woman set a lantern on her gatepost as they went by, and then another house did it, and another, until the whole road to the headland was strung with small flames like a runway lit for a landing nobody could see yet.

"They know," Ulyana said.

"They've always known," Mrs. Vann said, cranking the cart around the last bend. "They sold their grandfathers' silence once. They're deciding, one lamp at a time, whether to buy it back." She glanced sideways at Ulyana, and in the lantern light her face was ancient and amused and terribly kind. "Don't drown tonight, girl. It would be rude, after all this."

* * *

The tunnel mouth was worse than she'd imagined.

Half-blasted meant half-eaten: the old rail tunnel's arch had been blown open at the crown, and rubble spilled down the incline like a broken tooth, timber splinters and shattered ballast and great slabs of the original lining lying where the charges had thrown them. Floodlights glared from the Harrow camp two hundred yards off, but the sentries posted at the mouth had been drawn away an hour ago — Tomas's final gift, a rumor planted about a magistrate's inspection at the harbor — and the entrance stood open and unguarded, breathing cold salt air.

Ulyana stood at the lip of the incline and listened. Below, muffled by stone, the sea moved in its chamber. The fourth tide was an hour from slack.

"Air line runs sixty feet," Tomas said, uncoiling hose. "Pump here at the top of the incline, dry ground, solid footing. We walk down to the water, hat up, and follow the guide line I set last week. Ninety feet to the gallery, forty more to the wheelhouse. Forty minutes of slack. We're out with ten to spare or we don't argue about it — we drop whatever we're holding and we swim."

"Agreed."

"If the hull starts talking — really talking, not groaning, *talking* — we leave. Even mid-safe. Even mid-turn of the dial."

"Agreed."

He looked at her then, in the wash of the distant floodlights, and whatever he saw in her face made him smile — tired, crooked, real. "You know the worst part? I used to brief crews before dives and my voice never shook. First time in my life I actually care whether everyone comes back up."

"That's not the worst part," Ulyana said. "The worst part is I'm going to spend forty minutes underwater thinking about what happens after, and I've never had an *after* to think about before. It's very distracting. I may crack the safe wrong out of sheer happiness."

"God help the combination lock," Tomas said, and handed her the helmet.

It was heavier than it looked, brass and copper and fifty years of other people's breath. She settled it onto her shoulders the way Mrs. Vann had shown her, feeling the weight distribute, feeling it become hers. Through the round faceplate the world shrank to a coin of lamplight and Tomas's face beside her, and beyond him the dark mouth of the water, waiting with the patience of something that had already outlasted a ship, a railway, and a century of secrets.

Above them, Mrs. Vann set her hands on the crank.

"Fourth tide," the old woman said, to the sea, to the tunnel, to the son she'd lost and the two fools she'd gained. "Turn gentle."

The water took them without ceremony — cold closing over the faceplate, lamplight bending into ribbons, the guide line pulling taut into the black. And far below, in the drowned wheelhouse of the Meridian Star, a safe sat bolted to the deck under fifty years of silt, keeping a page with a living man's name on it, waiting to learn whether the granddaughter of the man it was stolen from had guessed right.

Ulyana swam down into the dark, counting strokes, counting minutes, counting on a dead captain's sentimentality — and the first thing she saw when the wheelhouse loomed out of the gloom was the chart table, overturned, and the safe's dial glinting clean and bare where the silt had been recently disturbed.

Someone had tried it before her.

And recently.
