## 28. The Captain's Safe

The fourth tide had come and gone while they planned, which meant the fifth was coming whether they were ready or not. The sea, Ulyana had learned, did not extend courtesies. It kept its schedule the way her grandfather kept his ledgers — in a fine hand, without sentiment, and with every debt noted.

She stood at the mouth of the inland tunnel in the grey hour before dawn, checking her kit for the third time, and Tomas stood beside her doing the same, and they were both stalling and both knew it.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the second dive. You chew like my grandfather used to. Jaw going, words staying put."

Tomas exhaled through his nose, which was as close to a laugh as the tunnel mouth allowed. "The wheelhouse is the deepest point. Forty feet down, past the hold, through a corridor that's already shedding plating. The hull groaned twice on our way out last time. I counted."

"You count everything."

"It's kept me alive." He looked at her then, and in the lantern light his face was doing something complicated — the look of a man who had already made his argument to himself and lost. "If the hull goes while we're in the wheelhouse, there's no corridor to run back down. There's just water deciding where it wants us."

"Then we don't give it time to decide." She cinched the last strap on the strongbox sling — empty this time, riding her hip like a promise — and stepped to the water's edge. The tunnel water lay black and still, holding the lantern reflections like coins at the bottom of a well. "My grandfather used to say the river forgives nothing and forgets nothing, so you'd best make your mistakes worth remembering."

"Was he right?"

"He was framed for the worst disaster on the coast, so probably." She put the regulator between her teeth. "Let's go be worth remembering."

* * *

The flooded gallery took them the way it had before — a long green throat of collapsed tunnel, their lights sweeping over rails that ran into the dark and simply stopped, sheared off fifty years ago as if the sea had bitten them. Ulyana swam the centerline out of habit. Even drowned, a track was a track. You walked it or you didn't, and she had never in her life been the kind of woman who didn't.

Past the second mouth of the tunnel, the gallery opened into the chamber where the *Meridian Star* lay on her side in the silt, and Ulyana felt the wreck notice them. She couldn't have explained how she knew. It was the same feeling she got from old bridges at night — the sense of a structure that had been holding its breath for decades and was very tired of the arrangement.

Tomas led. He knew the wreck's moods better than she did, and he moved through the broken cargo hatch with a diver's unhurried grace, one hand trailing the plating like a man greeting an old, difficult friend. She followed, counting bulkhead frames the way he'd taught her — *frame, frame, frame* — a rhythm to keep the fear in its pen.

The hold was as they'd left it, minus the strongbox, minus the manifest, minus the confession that had already gone to Mrs. Vann's preserving shelf in the chandlery back room. The space where the box had sat for fifty years was a clean rectangle in the silt, and Ulyana looked at it for one breath longer than she meant to. A thing can wait half a century, she thought, and still be gone in an afternoon. That was the whole of the sea's philosophy, probably. That was the whole of most philosophies.

Tomas tapped her shoulder and pointed aft.

The corridor to the wheelhouse was exactly as bad as he'd promised. It ran along what had been the ship's port side, now the ceiling of the world down here, and the plating had begun to let go in long curled strips that swayed in the faint current like kelp made of knives. Their lights caught the rust blooms on the frames — orange flowers the size of dinner plates, soft-looking, treacherous. Ulyana went second, keeping Tomas's fins in view, and when the hull groaned — one long, low note, more felt than heard, traveling up through the water and into her chest — she felt him stop and hang there, listening, the way she listened to a bridge under load.

*One groan,* she told herself. *A structure can groan for a hundred years. It's the silence after the groan you fear.*

The groan faded. They went on.

The wheelhouse door hung open, half its frame gone to rust, and beyond it the captain's world lay tipped on its side and flooded and patient. Ulyana's light found the wheel first — brass, still bright in patches where the salt hadn't won, spokes reaching up out of the silt like the ribs of something that had once steered. The engine telegraph stood beside it, its dial frozen on a command no one had ever answered: *FULL AHEAD*.

Her throat went tight. She gave herself three seconds to feel it, the way her grandfather had taught her to give grief its ration and no more, and then she made herself look at the room like an engineer.

The safe was under what had been the chart table. Of course it was. Captains kept their papers low and aft and bolted down, because captains, like engineers, like mapmakers, understood that everything floats away eventually except what you fasten. It was a squat iron box, maybe two feet square, its door facing up at them out of the silt, the maker's plate still legible: *HARLOW & SONS, MARINE SAFES, PORT CERULE*.

Tomas shone his light on the dial and then at her, and even through the mask she could read the question. *Do you know how to do this?*

She had learned safes at nineteen, from a bridge foreman in the hill country who said any woman who could read a strain diagram could read a lock, and he'd been right, mostly, and she'd been grateful, mostly, and that was another town and another life and another Ulyana. She nodded.

* * *

The safe's dial was stiff but whole. She worked her gloves around it and felt the mechanism through the metal the way she felt a rail's health through the sole of her boot — a language of resistance and give, of *not yet* and *almost*. Three numbers. She'd found the first by feel before her arms stopped shaking from the swim.

The trouble was time. The trouble was always time. Her air gauge said forty minutes, and the corridor behind them said less than that, and somewhere above them the fifth tide was gathering itself like a debt collector. She turned the dial and listened with her whole skull pressed near the door, and the sea pressed back, and the wreck groaned again, longer this time, and Tomas put his hand flat on the plating beside her and shook his head — *not yet, not yet* — and she believed him, because he had counted everything his whole life and had never once been wrong about a structure.

Second number. The dial fought her. Her gloves were too thick, her fingers too cold, and the light trembled with the small honest shiver of her own arm, and she thought, absurdly, of her grandfather's hands — the way he'd held a pen, the way the map's ink lines never wavered even on the night, she now knew, that he must have been writing his own defense. Steady hands in a shaking world. That was the inheritance. Not the map. The hands.

She steadied. She turned.

The third number came up like a held breath released, and the bolt threw with a sound she felt rather than heard — a deep iron *chunk* that went through the water and through her chest and, she would have sworn, through fifty years.

The door swung open on a darkness that was not empty.

Her light went in, and the first thing it found was paper. Reams of it, wrapped in oilcloth and waxed canvas, packed the way a careful man packs things he expects to be drowned — tight, dry, layered. The captain had known. Whatever the *Meridian Star* had been hauling on her last run, her captain had known it was worth more than the ship, and he had put it in iron and put the iron in the deep and trusted the sea to keep his secret until someone came who deserved it.

Beneath the paper bundles, wedged in the corner, lay a leather-bound book the size of a hymnal, its cover stamped in faded gilt: *MERIDIAN STAR — SHIP'S LOG — FINAL VOYAGE*.

And beneath the log, in a place no one would look unless they already knew to look — flat against the safe's floor, wrapped in its own oilcloth, tied with a length of tarred twine that her fingers recognized before her eyes did — was a letter. She knew the handwriting on the wrapping before she'd loosened the first knot. She had spent her whole childhood tracing it. The tall, patient capitals. The *O* of *OKONKWO* drawn as a perfect circle, because her grandfather had believed a circle was the only honest shape, everything returning to where it began.

Ulyana Okonkwo hung in the flooded wheelhouse of a dead ship, forty feet under the sea, and read her own name written by a dead man.

*For my granddaughter, if the sea is kinder than the railway was.*

Her vision blurred behind the mask — tears in salt water, the most private kind of crying, indistinguishable from the sea itself — and Tomas's hand closed on her shoulder, steady, and she pointed at the log, and he took it, and she took the letter, and they turned for the corridor with the safe's inheritance between them.

* * *

They made the gallery before the hull's patience ran out, though not by much. The last stretch of corridor shed a strip of plating behind them with a sound like a door slamming in a house with no one in it, and Ulyana felt the pressure wave shove her fins and did not look back, because looking back at a collapsing thing is a courtesy the sea does not return.

They surfaced in the tunnel's air pocket — the low, echoing chamber where they'd once been stranded, where they'd once talked until slack water came, where she had learned that love can arrive in the one place neither person can leave. The lantern they'd staged there still burned. Tomas got his regulator out first and pulled hers for her, an old courtesy now, and for a moment they just breathed, the two of them, in the small yellow light, dripping, alive, holding fifty years of paper.

"Open it," Tomas said. Not *are you all right*. He knew she wasn't, and he knew she would be, and he knew the difference between those things was the letter. "Open it here. I'll watch the water."

The air pocket's water lay calm, mirror-still, holding their lantern upside down. Ulyana sat on the ledge with her boots in it and untied the tarred twine, and her grandfather's handwriting waited on the page inside like a man waiting at a station platform, patient, certain the train would come.

*My dear girl — you will be older than I can imagine, and I am sorry for it, because I had hoped to teach you the map myself.*

She had to stop there. Tomas glanced over; she waved him on; he went back to watching the black water with the particular stillness of a man guarding something more fragile than a woman crying.

*They came tonight. Harrow's men — the same name, girl, the name has been in this country longer than the rails, and it will be here after, unless you finish what I could not. The Meridian Star carried the manifest and the confession and the ledger of payments, and the syndicate could not let it dock. They put it on the rocks off the Tollgate and called it weather, and they put the blame on me, and the town took their money and looked away, and I do not blame the town. Fear is cheap and truth is dear, and most men buy the cheap thing and call it prudence.*

*The captain — Vann, a good man, an honest one, the only honest man the syndicate ever hired — hid the safe before they could search her. His wife knows. If you are reading this, the sea gave her back to you what it took from me, and you will owe Mrs. Vann more than a coin. Pay it. Whatever she asks. Pay it twice.*

*The lines I drew on the map are not abandoned, they are stolen. Every closed line is a town that was robbed of its own future and told to be grateful. Reopen them. Not the rails first — the truth first. A town that knows what was taken from it will lay its own track.*

Ulyana's breath was coming short. She read the last lines twice, because the first time her eyes would not hold still.

*I was not a perfect man, but I was an honest engineer, and I am leaving you the only inheritance I have: the map, and the truth, and the knowledge that the sea keeps a schedule and so should you. Do not let them make you small. Do not let them make you quiet. And if you find someone worth the water — the kind of person who counts everything and still takes the risk — then hold on with both hands, girl. The rails end. People don't have to.*

*Your grandfather,
E. Okonkwo*

The water in the air pocket lapped once against the ledge, soft as a hand on a shoulder.

Ulyana folded the letter along its old creases and held it against her chest, and Tomas, who had been watching the water, turned and looked at her across the little yellow room at the bottom of the world, and whatever he saw in her face made him come and sit beside her and say nothing at all, which was the correct engineering decision.

"He knew," she said finally. Her voice came out scraped. "Fifty years down there, and he knew you'd be — he wrote *hold on with both hands*, Tomas. He wrote it before either of us was born."

"Then he was a better surveyor than either of us," Tomas said. "He saw us coming."

She laughed — a wet, broken, entirely real laugh — and leaned into him, and the lantern flame stood still in the dead air, and for one whole minute the fifth tide and the Harrow fleet and the collapsing hull were all simply weather happening somewhere else.

Then Tomas's head came up. He'd heard it before she had — he always did — a sound traveling down the tunnel from the world above: boots, many boots, and the flat metallic voice of men who had come to the tunnel mouth in a hurry and without permission.

He looked at her, and she looked at him, and the letter seemed to grow heavier against her ribs.

"They're not waiting for the sixth tide," Tomas said quietly. "Adelene's moving now."
