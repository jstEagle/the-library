## 29. Collapse

The wheelhouse was a cathedral of green light, and Tomas had been in enough cathedrals to know that the roof was always the first thing to go.

He hung in the flooded doorway of the *Meridian Star*'s bridge while Ulyana worked the safe, her lamp beam jittering across the ceiling with every turn of the dial. The wheel still stood at its station, spokes furred with barnacles, pointing a direction no ship had gone in fifty years. Above it, the overhead plating sagged in a long slow curve, like a held breath.

"Tomas." Ulyana's voice came thin and metallic through the line. "I need you to stop watching the ceiling and start watching the dial. Third number's worn. I can't read it."

"Twenty-two," he said. "It's twenty-two. I could see it from the door."

"Then why didn't you say so?"

"Because you told me not to touch anything, and I've decided that includes numbers."

He heard her laugh — a burst of bubbles, quickly caught, the way she caught everything, filed everything, kept everything in that survey kit of a mind. Even now. Even here. Even with the sea pressing in on all sides like a landlord who wanted his rooms back.

The dial clicked. The handle turned with a groan that traveled through the hull and into his bones, and he felt the whole wreck shiver around them, the way an old dog shivers when it stands up.

*Easy,* he told the ship. *Easy, old girl. We're not here to rob you. We're here to carry your mail.*

The safe swung open.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow, impossibly, held against fifty years of salt, lay a ledger bound in canvas, and beneath it a letter, folded once, the ink gone brown as old blood but the hand still fierce, still upright, still unmistakably the same hand that had drawn the map of closed lines Ulyana carried against her chest like a second heartbeat.

He watched her lift it. He watched her lamp go very still, the way a person's whole body goes still when they've found the thing they've been walking toward their entire life and never quite admitted they were looking for.

"Ulyana." He said it gently. "We're at slack. Slack doesn't wait for reunions."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she didn't bother to hide it. "I know. I just — Tomas, it's addressed to me. To *us*. 'To whoever of my blood finds this.' He wrote it the night they took everything. He knew they'd come for him, and he —"

"Put it in the bag. Read it in the air. Read it at the surface. Read it at your wedding, if you're the type." He was already moving to the doorway, checking the gallery beyond, and what he saw there made the joke die in his throat.

The flooded gallery they'd swum in through — that long iron throat between the collapsed tunnel and the cargo hold — had changed. The silt that had lain along its floor like a carpet was drifting now, lazy gray ribbons curling up into their light. The hull plates along the starboard side had wept new rust, long orange tears running down and dissolving.

The tide had turned. He'd known it in his gut for ten minutes and hadn't said it, because she'd waited fifty years for that safe and he'd wanted her to have her moment. That was the trouble with love, he was beginning to understand. It made you generous with other people's time.

"Tomas." Her voice had changed. She'd seen it too. "The silt."

"I see it. Bag the log. Letter inside your suit, against your chest. We move on my count, and Ulyana — we don't talk in the gallery. We swim. Fast and smooth, no fin-scrape, nothing that shakes the plates."

"Understood."

"And if anything lets go —" He hesitated. There was a version of this sentence that was honest and a version that was kind, and he'd spent thirty years in the water learning they were never the same sentence. "If anything lets go, you keep going. You don't turn around. The evidence is the mission."

"The evidence is paper," she said. "You're —"

"Paper burns. I'm mostly scar tissue and stubbornness. Go."

* * *

The gallery took them in like a throat.

It was worse than he'd judged from the wheelhouse. The drifting silt had thickened into a haze that ate their lamps at six feet, and the hull plates overhead — he could count them now, one after another, the rivet lines marching away into the dark — had begun to talk to each other. Not the groan of a settling wreck, which he knew the way he knew his own pulse. This was a different sound. A ticking. Metal under load, deciding.

He put Ulyana in front of him. Not because she was slower — she wasn't, she moved through water with that same rootless competence she brought to everything, efficient and unwelcome nowhere and at home nowhere either — but because if the ceiling came down, he wanted to be the part of this expedition that was expendable.

Halfway. The collapsed tunnel mouth glowed faintly ahead, a gray coin of open water, and beyond it the inland shaft, and beyond that, air, and Mrs. Vann, and a kettle that whistled like it was paid to.

Then the *Meridian Star* made her decision.

It started as a sound he felt in his teeth — a single deep note, like a bell struck in the next room — and then the whole gallery lurched, and the silt went from haze to storm, and a plate the size of a barn door let go somewhere behind them with a shriek that he would hear for the rest of his life, in every quiet room, every night he ever tried to sleep.

"GO!" he roared into the mouthpiece, and shoved her toward the light.

She went. Of course she went — she was an engineer, she understood load and failure better than any diver he'd ever worked with, and she knew that a second person in a collapsing space is not a rescue, it's a second casualty. He watched her fins kick hard, watched the gray coin of the tunnel mouth swallow her, watched the lamp beam swing away —

— and then the second plate came down.

Not on her. On him.

It caught him across the shoulders and drove him down into the gallery floor, and the world became weight. Not pain, not yet — pain was a luxury the sea would deliver later, with interest — just weight, absolute and intimate, the way he imagined it felt to be a coin under a locomotive. His lamp was gone, knocked somewhere into the storm. His regulator held. Small mercies. The sea was a fair bookkeeper in its way.

He could still see, barely, in the churn — the plate had fallen at an angle, pinning him across the back at the shoulder blades, and it had left a gap. A gap the size of a letter. A gap the size of a folded page against a chest.

Through it, in the gray storm, he saw her come back.

Of course she came back. He'd told her not to. He'd made it a professional instruction, a dive protocol, the one order on this whole expedition she had no contractual obligation to follow, and she came back anyway, into a gallery that was eating itself, into zero visibility, into the exact shape of the thing she'd sworn she'd never do again — he knew that shape, he'd seen it in her face the night she told him about the last person she'd gone back for, a survey partner on a flooded line, and how she'd arrived four minutes too late and had done the math on those four minutes every day since.

"No," he said into the line, and his voice came out wrecked. "Ulyana. No. Go up."

"Shut up, Tomas."

"Your tide — the slack's gone, the current's already —"

"I said shut up." Her hands were on the plate. He felt them, absurdly, felt the pressure shift as she threw her weight against a hundred pounds of iron that weighed nothing underwater and everything, everything, all at once. "I'm an engineer. This plate is cantilevered on the frame member to your left. If we jack the frame —"

"You don't have a jack, you have *hands* —"

"I have hands," she said, "and a rail jack in the kit, and a tide that's turning, and you are not doing this to me. Do you hear me? You are not making me do the math again."

And there it was. The map of her life, tearing in half, right in front of him — the woman who catalogued exits, who kept a ledger of losses, who had walked into Rivergate with a room above a chandlery and two ways out of it, and the woman who was now under a collapsing wreck with her hands in the dark looking for a frame member because a diver she'd known for a month had been stupid enough to love her.

"I'm not going anywhere," he said, which was a lie, because the plate was pressing the air out of him in slow increments, and they both knew the arithmetic of that too.

"Then hold still and let me work."

* * *

She worked.

He'd watched her work tide tables, watched her work the chandlery ledgers, watched her work a safe dial in a drowned cathedral, and none of it was this. This was her hands finding the frame member blind, in silt so thick it was less like water than like weather. This was her voice in his ear, low and steady, reading out her own actions like a survey log — *found the flange, found the pivot point, wedging the jack, don't move, don't you dare move* — because that was how she kept the fear in its cage, she made it take notes.

The jack went in. He felt the plate lift — a centimeter, two, a miracle measured in millimeters — and he pulled, and the gallery floor scraped him forward through the gap like a man being born, and then the plate came down again behind him with a boom he felt through the whole hull, and the frame member above it cracked like a rifle shot.

The current hit them both at the same instant.

The slack was gone. The tide was coming home through the tunnel mouth, and it came the way the sea does everything — without malice, without mercy, on schedule. It took him first, because he was spent and half-crushed and swimming on one good arm, and it took her a half-second later because she'd turned back for him, because she would always turn back for him, and that was the most terrifying thing he had ever learned about another human being.

The tunnel spat them out into the inland shaft, and the shaft was a chimney of moving water, and the water was going up, and they went with it.

He lost her light. He lost her hand. He lost the sound of her breathing, which was the only sound in the world he had ever cared about keeping, and the shaft carried him up through the dark in a long tumbling fall that was not a fall, that was the sea returning what it had borrowed, and the last thing he thought, with the surface rushing up at him like a struck match, was:

*She has the log. She has the log, and she went back for me, and the tide is rising, and the hull is down, and she will go back down. She will go back down for the log if she lost it, and she will go back down for me if I don't surface, and the sixth tide is coming, and she will not stop, she will never stop —*

The surface broke over him like a bell.

Air. Gray dawn air, tasting of salt and rust and the inside of his own mask. He was in the flooded shaft, treading water in the circle of light from the collapsed tunnel mouth above — no, not collapsed, *open*, the tunnel mouth was open above him and the sky beyond it was going pale at the edges, dawn coming up over the marsh like a slow confession.

He hauled himself onto the shaft's iron lip, coughing, and tore the mask down, and shouted her name into the tunnel, and the tunnel gave back only his own voice, thinner.

Then a hand broke the surface of the shaft below him.

He was down the ladder — there was a maintenance ladder, of course there was, his grandfather's map had known about the ladder before he did — before his mind caught up with his body, and he caught her wrist, and her arm, and dragged her over the lip onto the wet iron, where she lay on her back with the canvas ledger clutched to her chest and her grandfather's letter inside her suit against her heart, and she was laughing.

Laughing. Coughing seawater and laughing at the same time, a terrible sound, the best sound he had ever heard.

"The safe," she gasped. "Tomas. The safe. I got it open and the log was *there*, it was there the whole time, fifty years, and he wrote to me, he —" She stopped. She looked at him. She saw the way he was holding his shoulder, the way he couldn't straighten his back, and the laughter went out of her like a tide.

"Tomas. The plate. How bad."

"It's a shoulder," he said. "It's mostly a shoulder."

"Mostly."

"There's a rib involved. Possibly two ribs. They're being very quiet about it, which I respect."

She sat up, and the dawn came through the tunnel mouth and found her face, and he watched her do the thing she always did — the cold clarity, the engineer's arithmetic — and he watched it fail. He watched the numbers refuse to come.

"The hull's down," she said quietly. "The gallery is gone. And the log is —" she pressed the canvas ledger against her chest, "— this is the log. But the captain's safe, the letter, the — Tomas, the manifest page, the *name* —"

"Is in your suit," he said. "You put the letter inside your suit. I watched you do it. You never put anything down in your life. You're the only person I've ever met who carries her own archives."

She stared at him. Then her hand went to her chest, under the suit, and came out with the oilcloth packet — water-spotted, salt-stained, whole.

And then her face changed, because she was already ahead of him, she was always ahead of him, and she had done the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do.

"The sixth tide," she said. "The hull came down on the gallery, but the chamber — the cargo hold, the strongbox chamber — Tomas, if the inner chamber held, the rest of the evidence is still in there. And the tide is rising, and Harrow is blasting the tunnel, and we have —"

She stopped. Below them, through the iron grating of the shaft floor, the water was rising. He could see it in the way the light from below changed, the green deepening, the sea climbing its chimney toward them with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

And somewhere out across the marsh, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of Harrow's crews starting their generators.

Ulyana looked at the water. Then at the letter in her hand. Then at him — at his shoulder, at the ribs he was pretending were fine — and he saw her make the decision he'd been dreading since the plate came down, the one he'd have bet his contract, his livelihood, his life on her not making.

"The fifth tide," she said. "I'm going back down on the fifth tide."

"Ulyana —"

"Alone," she said, and the dawn came up behind her like a fuse.
