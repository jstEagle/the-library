## 30. Fifth Tide, One Diver

The fifth tide came in gray and mean, the way tides do when they've been argued with all night.

Ulyana spent the hours between the fourth and fifth doing what she always did when her hands wanted to shake: she made lists. On the back of Mrs. Vann's tide table, in pencil, in her grandfather's cramped notation that she'd inherited along with everything else — the stubbornness, the handwriting, the habit of writing down what the water was going to do before it did it.

*Kit:* rail jack, twenty-ton, nine pounds. Two spare lamps, one working. Slate and pencil. Line reel, a hundred feet, borrowed from Mrs. Vann's late husband's gear, which smelled of fifty years of chandlery dust and something older than that. The log, wrapped in oilcloth, strapped to her chest where it couldn't be forgotten or dropped or traded away by any part of her that might panic.

*Tide:* low slack at 11:40, lasting — if the sea was feeling generous, which it had not been lately — eleven minutes. Maybe nine. The half-blasted tunnel had changed the hydraulics of the whole system; water moved through the broken gallery differently now, faster on the ebb, and every calculation she'd made two days ago was worth exactly as much as the paper it was written on.

*Man:* pinned under a hull plate at roughly forty feet, in a chamber that had already eaten one bulkhead and was chewing on a second. Ribs cracked, maybe worse. Left arm functional. Sense of humor intact, which she counted as a vital sign.

She looked at the list for a long time. Then she added a fourth line, because an engineer who lies to herself on paper is lying to herself everywhere:

*Probability of this working:* don't calculate it.

---

Tomas tried three more times to talk her out of it, which she respected, because she'd have done the same, and then he stopped trying, which she respected more, because he'd also have done the same.

"You understand," he said, while she checked the jack's release valve for the fourth time, "that if you go down there and the chamber comes down on you too, I will spend whatever's left of my life being the man whose stupidity killed Ulyana Okonkwo. That's a heavy thing to be. I've carried lighter things and complained more."

"Then I'll try not to die stupidly." She tested the jack's foot plate against the floorboards of the chandlery's back room. It bit and held. Good. "I'll die competently. It's how I do everything."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." She looked up at him. He was propped against the doorframe with his arm bound to his chest in a sling Mrs. Vann had improvised from sailcloth, and his face was the color of old rope, and he was watching her with an expression she was going to think about later, when there was a later, when there was air and light and no schedule kept by the moon. "Tell me about the pin. Exactly. Where the plate sits, what's under it, what's beside it."

He told her. He was precise about it — a diver's precision, a Harrow diver's precision, learned from people she was currently at war with — and she loved him a little for the precision, and hated herself a little for loving anything right now, because love was a distraction and distractions underwater were measured in lives.

"The plate's canted," he finished. "Port edge down, buried in the silt. Starboard edge is your gap. You'll need to lift maybe eight inches to drag me clear."

"Eight inches." She wrote it down even though she wouldn't forget it. Writing things down was how she prayed. "Twenty-ton jack gives you eight inches like it's nothing."

"And the deck above the plate?"

"Gone. You said."

"Gone *mostly.* There's a spar. When the bulkhead let go yesterday—" he paused, and she watched him decide not to soften it, another thing she loved him for, "—the spar dropped across the plate. It's taking some of the load. If you lift the plate, the spar may come down instead."

"So I lift slowly."

"So you lift slowly, and you listen, and if the hull starts talking—"

"I know what a failing hull sounds like, Tomas. I've been listening to failing structures since I was six years old. My grandfather used to put my hand on bridge girders so I could feel them complain." She closed the kit. "What I don't know is what a drowning man sounds like, and I'd prefer to keep it that way."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Mrs. Vann wants to go with you to the tunnel mouth."

"Mrs. Vann is seventy-four years old."

"Mrs. Vann," said Tomas, with the gravity of a man reporting intelligence, "has been loading a wheelbarrow with flares since dawn, and when I suggested she might stay here, she told me that she has been paying this cave's debts for fifty years and she does not intend to stop now, and then she called me 'young man' in a tone I felt in my sternum. Which, incidentally, is injured."

Despite everything — despite the tide, the wreck, the warrant with her name on it, the generators grinding out in the marsh where Harrow's crews were preparing their next assault — Ulyana laughed. It came out ragged but real.

"All right," she said. "Mrs. Vann walks me in. You stay here and be alive. That's your job. Consider it a contract."

"I've had bad contracts before," Tomas said. "This one I intend to breach constantly, by staying alive very aggressively."

---

The walk to the tunnel mouth took forty minutes through country that didn't know yet what was happening to it. Marsh grass bent in the wind. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a creature that had never once needed to check a tide table. Somewhere behind them, faint, the generators went on grinding, and Ulyana found she could hate the sound and ignore it at the same time, which was, she supposed, a kind of skill.

At the mouth of the collapsed rail tunnel, Mrs. Vann set down her wheelbarrow, lit the first flare, and planted it in the mud like a flag.

"My son wore a watch," the old woman said, apropos of nothing, watching Ulyana check her lamp. "Brass case. His father bought it the week before he went into the cave the first time. I've wondered, these fifty years, whether the sea kept it or threw it away. The sea keeps strange things and throws away stranger ones."

"If I see it," Ulyana said, "I'll bring it out."

Mrs. Vann looked at her for a long moment. Her eyes were pale and clear and terribly old. "You say that like it's a small thing."

"It's a small thing to carry and a large thing to mean. That's most things, I find."

The old woman huffed — almost a laugh — and pressed something into Ulyana's hand. A coin. The same coin, Ulyana realized, that she'd paid at the Tollgate a lifetime ago, six tides and one whole world back. "Admission," Mrs. Vann said. "And the warning, same as ever. Everyone who enters owes the sea something. Go find out what you owe, girl, and pay it quick, and come back."

Ulyana closed her hand around the coin. It was warm from the old woman's pocket, which felt impossible and wasn't.

Then she turned on her lamp, spat on her mask the way Tomas had taught her — *it breaks up the fogging, it's disgusting, it works* — and walked into the water.

---

The flooded gallery was exactly as wrong as she remembered and somehow worse for the half-blasted section, where Harrow's charges had torn the tunnel's crown open and left the rock hanging like a broken jaw. She swam through the wound in the hillside with her line paying out behind her, counting her breathing the way her grandfather had taught her to count rail joints — four counts in, six counts out, never hold, holding was how panic got its foot in the door.

Forty feet down, the gallery opened into the chamber, and the chamber opened into ruin.

The Meridian Star lay on her side in the green murk, and she was dying. Ulyana could see it even through the lamplight's narrow cone — the way the hull had settled further since yesterday, plates sprung along the seam like knuckles cracking, sediment pluming gently off the superstructure wherever the structure was quietly giving up. A shipwreck wasn't a thing. It was a process. And the process was accelerating.

She found the spar first, just as Tomas had described — a beam of black oak dropped across the fallen plate, taking load, waiting to take more. Then the plate itself, canted port-edge-down in the silt, and beneath it—

His lamp was still on. Still burning, a weak gold glow in the dark, because of course he'd rigged it to burn slow, because he was the kind of man who prepared for the rescue he believed in.

"Tomas." She tapped the slate against the plate, twice, three times — the signal they'd agreed on, *I'm here, answer me.*

A pause. Then his boot scraped stone. Once. Twice. *Here.*

She put her face close to the gap at the plate's starboard edge and saw his eyes, wide and lucid and furious with relief, six inches of green water away.

*Hello,* she wrote on the slate, and held it up, because she refused to make this solemn. He laughed — she saw the bubbles, heard the muffled bark of it through the water — and tapped the plate twice, which meant, in their private grammar, *you're insane, thank God.*

*Work now,* she wrote. *Talk later.*

She set the jack.

It was miserable work, the kind of work that makes an engineer question every choice that led to kneeling in silt at the bottom of a drowning ship. The footing was soft; the jack's foot plate sank as she pumped. She had to shim it with two pieces of ballast stone and a length of broken pipe, and every placement took three tries, and her air gauge crept downward with the patience of a creditor.

Pump. Check. Pump. Check. Listen.

The hull talked the whole time. Low groans, ticking stresses, the long complaint of iron that had held its shape for fifty years and saw no reason to keep holding it for her. She knew this language. She'd grown up with this language. Her grandfather's hand on hers on a cold girder: *Feel that? That's the bridge telling you what it needs. Everything that carries weight talks. Your job is to listen better than it complains.*

The jack took. Eight pounds of steel lifting tons of dead ship, one slow thread-turn at a time. The plate rose — an inch, two — and the spar above it groaned and shifted, and Ulyana froze with her hand on the pump screw and her heart going like a telegraph key.

The spar settled. Held. The load hadn't transferred. Not yet.

Three inches. Four. Under the plate, Tomas worked himself backward, dragging his pinned leg free inch by inch, and she could hear him breathing through his regulator in short controlled pulls — a diver managing pain, a professional refusing to rush the person doing the delicate work, and she thought, absurdly, in the middle of everything: *he trusts me. Down here, under a collapsing hull, with the tide turning, he trusts me completely,* and the thought was so warm and so dangerous that she nearly fumbled the screw.

Five inches. Six.

The spar chose that moment to speak.

Not a groan — a crack, sharp as a rifle shot, and the whole deck structure above them shuddered, raining silt, and the spar began to come down.

Later, Ulyana would not be able to reconstruct the next ten seconds except as a sequence of decisions made below the level of thought. She slammed the jack's release — not fully, just enough — and the plate dropped back two inches, catching the spar on its upper edge, wedging it, buying seconds. In the same motion she grabbed Tomas's good arm and hauled, and he came sliding out from under the plate through the narrowing gap like a cork from a bottle, scraping his shoulder raw, and they rolled together into the silt as the spar came down the rest of the way and hit the plate with a boom she felt in her teeth.

Silence. Silt clouding everything. Her lamp a dim halo in brown water.

His hand found hers in the murk and gripped it, hard enough to hurt, and neither of them let go for a long moment.

When the water cleared enough to see, she held up the slate.

*Still alive?*

Two taps on her arm. *Yes.*

*Good. Because we're leaving, and I'm not carrying you.*

He wrote back, his letters wobbling — exhaustion, cold, cracked ribs, fair enough: *You came back down alone. For me. With the log already safe. Do you understand what that—*

*Later,* she wrote. But her hand wasn't steady when she wiped the slate, and she suspected he noticed, and she suspected she didn't mind.

---

They swam out the way she'd come in, along her own line, through the broken jaw of the tunnel, and the wreck groaned behind them the whole way like a door being closed slowly by someone with infinite patience.

The last hundred yards were the worst, because the slack was ending — she could feel it in the water's new reluctance, the faint push against them as the flood began — and because Tomas's strength was going, and she ended up towing him the final stretch with his arm over her shoulder and her legs burning and her air gauge showing a number she decided not to look at again until she absolutely had to.

Then the tunnel mouth, and gray daylight, and the surface, and air.

Mrs. Vann hauled them out of the water with the strength of a woman who'd spent fifty years hauling things out of water, and Ulyana lay on the mud of the marsh with the tide coming in around her ankles and laughed until her ribs hurt, and then Tomas laughed too, wheezing, cradling his side, and Mrs. Vann stood over them both with her hands on her hips like a foreman reviewing substandard work.

"Well," the old woman said. "You're both idiots. But you're *alive* idiots, which is rarer."

Ulyana sat up. The log was still strapped to her chest — she'd never once thought about it during the dive, and it had never once been in danger, because some part of her apparently guarded it even when the rest of her was busy saving a man's life. She unstrapped it, checked the oilcloth, found it dry inside.

Both. She'd gone back for the man and come out with both. Love against duty, and somehow the ledger balanced, though she suspected the sea would send an invoice eventually. The sea always did.

Tomas was looking at her — really looking, the way he'd looked at her in the air pocket between tides, when there'd been nowhere either of them could go and they'd finally said the true things out loud.

"You came back down alone," he said again, hoarse, on the surface this time, in the light. "Nobody ordered you. Nobody paid you. Every contract you've ever had was with yourself, and you broke the sensible clause of it for me."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "That's generally what love is. Terrible engineering. Wonderful results." She leaned over and kissed him, salt-stung and shaking, while the fifth tide climbed their backs and Mrs. Vann tactfully studied the horizon.

Then, over Tomas's shoulder, Ulyana saw it — and the warmth in her chest turned to ice so fast it audibly clicked.

Down the marsh path, half a mile out, a boat. Harrow colors. And standing in the bow, glasses raised, a watcher who had clearly seen them surface — seen the two of them, seen the bundle strapped to Ulyana's chest, seen everything.

"He'll report within the hour," Tomas said grimly, following her gaze. "Adelene will know the log is out. And Ulyana — the sixth tide starts tonight. After that, the cave seals, and whatever's still inside that wreck stays inside forever."

Ulyana looked down at the log in her arms. One page missing — the page naming the chairman — torn out decades ago by a keeper's guilty hand. Proof that proved almost enough. Almost.

Unless the missing page wasn't only in the wreck.

She turned slowly to Mrs. Vann, who had gone very still, her pale eyes fixed on the middle distance, on fifty years ago.

"Mrs. Vann," Ulyana said carefully. "Your husband was the keeper before you."

The old woman didn't answer. But her hand went, unconsciously, to the pocket of her coat — the same coat, Uynana realized, that had held the admission coin — and stayed there.

And Mrs. Vann said, very quietly, "Come inside, child. There's something I've been keeping longer than the sea has."
