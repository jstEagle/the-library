## 32. Harrow's Last Tide

Adelene Harrow had learned to read the water before she could read words, and the water had been telling her for three days that she was drowning.

She stood at the window of her office above the fleet yard — her father's office, and his father's before him, the same salt-fogged glass, the same brass rail worn smooth by four generations of forearms — and watched the tide come in over the Tollgate cave like a lid closing on a pot. Below her, the cranes stood idle. The divers were still walking out, one or two a day now, leaving their gear in neat rows on the dock like men setting down tools at the end of a shift that had lasted fifty years.

Reinhart was gone too. That was the wound she kept touching. Not because he'd left — divers left, divers always left, they were a migratory species — but because of how he'd left. He'd signed the surrender papers with a steady hand, met her eyes across the desk, and said, "I'm sorry, ma'am," as though he meant it, which was somehow worse than if he'd spat on the floor.

"You could have lied to me," she'd told him.

"I did lie to you," he'd said. "For weeks. It didn't take."

Now there was a knock at her door, two knuckles, brisk, and her clerk Pell put his head through the gap with the expression of a man delivering weather news he didn't personally endorse.

"She's here," Pell said. "The Okonkwo woman. Alone. Says you asked."

"I did ask." Adelene turned from the window. "Send her up. And Pell — clear the building. Everyone out for an hour. If anyone asks, tell them I'm doing inventory."

"Are you doing inventory?"

"I'm doing something no one needs witnesses for. Go."

* * *

Ulyana Okonkwo climbed the stairs the way Adelene imagined she did everything: efficiently, without apology, taking in the room the moment she entered it. Her eyes went to the charts on the wall, the ledgers stacked on the sideboard, the window, the door. Cataloging exits. Adelene recognized the habit because she shared it. You didn't grow up in a salvage family without learning that every room was a hull, and every hull had a point of failure.

"Miss Okonkwo."

"Mrs. Harrow." The young woman's voice was level. There was a bruise of exhaustion under her eyes — five tides' worth of exhaustion — but her hands were steady, folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a train. "You wanted to see me."

"I wanted to offer you something." Adelene gestured to the chair across the desk. Ulyana didn't sit. Fine. Standing negotiations went faster anyway. "Sit or don't. This won't take long, because I know exactly what I'm going to say, and I suspect you know half of it already."

"Then say the other half."

Adelene almost smiled. She sat down herself, laid both hands flat on the desk — her father's gesture, performed so many times it had worn grooves in the wood — and looked at the engineer squarely.

"The manifest goes public, my fleet dies. Not wounded — dies. The Harrow name has been built on the Meridian Star for fifty years. My grandfather helped sink it. My father helped keep it sunk. And I have spent thirty years keeping the story afloat, because the story is the business. Take away the story and all I own is rust and rope." She said it plainly, without self-pity, because self-pity was a luxury the water never sold anyone. "So here is what I'm offering. Everything."

Ulyana's eyebrows moved a fraction. "Everything."

"The fleet. The claim — I'll sign the historical rights over this afternoon, witnessed, notarized, whatever your magistrate friend requires. The yards, the cranes, the contracts. And a name." Adelene leaned forward. "You've been a stranger in every town you've ever walked into. I can make you the owner of Rivergate inside a month. The Harrow name is poison now, and I know it, and I'm offering to hand you the whole poisoned estate and walk away clean. All you have to do is burn the papers."

"And let the chairman stand."

"And let the past stay where my family buried it. Yes."

The room was quiet except for the tide against the seawall below, patient as a creditor.

"You should understand what you're asking me to weigh it against," Ulyana said. "Because I don't think you do."

"Oh, I think I do." Adelene opened the bottom drawer of the desk — the drawer her father had told her never to open until she needed it, and she had needed it exactly once before today — and took out a photograph, yellowed soft as cloth. She set it on the desk between them. A young woman in a diver's suit, standing on the deck of a Harrow crane barge, grinning like the sun had been invented for her personally.

"My mother," Adelene said. "First woman to dive the Tollgate chamber. Died down there when I was nine. The official account says equipment failure. The real account is that my grandfather ordered the dive during a rising tide to beat a rival crew to a cargo claim, and the tide took her, and the family paid three people to forget it." She tapped the photograph once. "I found that out at nineteen, from a drunk old keeper who thought I deserved the truth more than I deserved a comfortable life. Do you know what I did with it?"

"No."

"Nothing. I put it back in the drawer and I ran the fleet. Because the fleet fed four hundred people, and my name was on the door, and duty doesn't care whether the thing you're being dutiful to deserves it." She heard her own voice go dry and strange. "That is the inheritance I'm talking about, Miss Okonkwo. Not the boats. The weight. Every Harrow for three generations has carried the same stone, and we have each convinced ourselves that putting it down would drown someone else. My grandfather drowned my mother with his. My father drowned the town's memory with his. And I—" she spread her hands, "—I am apparently prepared to drown a railway engineer's good name with mine, if it comes to that. So believe me when I tell you I am not offering you this out of mercy. I'm offering it because it's the only trade left on the table."

Ulyana looked at the photograph for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge.

"Why tell me about your mother? It weakens your position."

"Does it?" Adelene sat back. "Or does it tell you that I know precisely what I'm asking you to become? Because that's the offer, girl. Take the fleet, take the name, and in twenty years some stranger will climb these stairs, and you'll open that drawer, and you'll make them the same offer. That's the whole machinery. That's how it works. It worked on me, and I was raised in it. You'd be fresh meat."

"You think I'd turn into you."

"I think you'd turn into something with my job description." Adelene smiled thinly. "The title changes. The drawer doesn't."

* * *

They stood at the window together afterward, which neither of them had planned. The tide was near its top, the Tollgate a black seam under green water, and somewhere beneath it the Meridian Star lay in her failing ribs, holding the last of the truth in her flooded belly like a swallowed letter.

"My grandfather drew maps," Ulyana said quietly. "Lines nobody rode anymore. He inked them by hand, every abandoned branch, every closed station, because he believed a thing isn't dead while somebody remembers where it goes." She turned. "You're asking me to stop remembering where it goes."

"I'm asking you to choose the living over the dead. It's what I did. Look how well it's gone."

"It bought you a fleet and cost you everything else. I counted." Ulyana's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Your divers are leaving, Mrs. Harrow. Your magistrate can't find jurors. The chandlery printed six hundred copies yesterday and the whole town helped fold them. You're not negotiating from strength. You're negotiating from the last dry plank."

"I'm aware of the state of the plank." Adelene's voice hardened. "Which is why the offer expires at the sixth tide. After that, the cave seals, and whatever happens next happens without terms."

"And after that?"

"After that," Adelene said, "I do what my grandfather did. What my father did. Whatever keeps the name alive one more season." She hated the sentence even as she said it, and said it anyway, because it was true, and a bad bargain made honestly was still worth more than a good one made in lies. "I'm giving you the chance to make sure I never have to find out what that means. Take the fleet. Burn the papers. Be kinder in the chair than I was."

Ulyana was quiet for a long time. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

"When I was nineteen," she said finally, "I found my grandfather's trial transcript in a box of his things. Read it in one night. Every lie, every paid witness, every line the syndicate closed to bury him. And I remember sitting on the floor of that rented room thinking: someone offered him a deal too. Someone stood in a room like this one and said, sign this, deny that, and you'll be comfortable. And he wouldn't. And it ruined him. And I used to wonder, growing up rootless, moving town to town, whether he'd been a fool." She shook her head slowly. "He wasn't a fool. He just understood that some inheritances are debts, not gifts. Yours is a debt, Mrs. Harrow. You've been paying interest your whole life."

"And yours?"

"Mine is a map." Ulyana walked to the door, then stopped, hand on the frame. "Keep the fleet. Keep the name. I don't want either — I've seen what they cost. But hear this clearly, because I'll only say it once. At dawn tomorrow, every town on my grandfather's map gets the manifest, the log, and the letter. Including the towns your family's money built. Especially those."

"The chairman—"

"—will fall. And when he falls, some of it lands on you. I know. I'm sorry for that part, genuinely. But I won't bury the truth to spare the people who buried it first." She paused. "One more thing."

"Go on."

"Tomas Reinhart signed his contract away for nothing. No share, no claim, no name. He traded a livelihood for a wreck and a woman he's known three weeks." Ulyana's voice softened by a degree. "If you want to know why I'm refusing you, watch him. Duty that costs nothing isn't duty. His cost everything, and he paid it smiling. That's the man your fleet raised. Whatever else you broke, you built that."

She was gone down the stairs before Adelene could answer, boots quick and light on the boards, a woman with a heading.

* * *

Alone, Adelene sat at her father's desk for a long while, the photograph of her mother face-up beside the lamp, the tide turning below.

Pell came back at the hour's end, cautious, hat in hand. "Well? Did she take it?"

"What do you think?"

"I think the divers are still leaving."

"So do I." Adelene opened the drawer and looked at the photograph a last time. Her mother, grinning on the deck, thirty years dead, still waiting for someone in this family to tell the truth about how she died. "Put the word out. All crews to the yard at first light. Full muster."

Pell frowned. "Muster for what, ma'am? The claim's done. The town's against us. The law's embarrassed to know us."

"The cave seals at the sixth tide," Adelene said. "Tomorrow morning, low slack, the tunnel floods for the last time in a hundred years. After that, whatever's in that wreck belongs to the sea, and the sea doesn't testify." She stood, and took her father's coat off its peg, and felt the weight of it settle on her shoulders like the stone it was. "Harrow fleets don't surrender cargo, Pell. Not to engineers. Not to towns. Not to the truth. Get me the blasting powder manifests. If the Okonkwo woman wants to publish her grandfather's lines, then by God she can watch us close one of our own."

Pell hesitated in the doorway. "Ma'am — the tunnel's got townsfolk on it. Vann's people. They'll stand on the tracks."

"They stood on the tracks fifty years ago too," Adelene said quietly. "And everyone stepped around them, and look where stepping around them got us." She pulled the coat straight. "This time nobody steps around anybody. Muster at first light."

Outside, far off across the darkening bay, the Tollgate began to breathe — the long slow exhale of the ebb beginning, the sea keeping its schedule, indifferent as arithmetic, opening its black door one last time.
