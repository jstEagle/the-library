## 33. The Sixth Tide Begins

The sea told us first.

I was on the chandlery steps with a mug of Mrs. Vann's terrible coffee — she called it coffee the way Harrow called her salvage operation archaeology — when I felt it in my teeth before my ears caught it. A change in pressure. A long, low exhale from under the bay, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep and pulling the blanket back with it.

"The tide's turning," I said.

Mrs. Vann didn't even look up from the net she was mending. "Course it is. It's been turning since before your grandfather could hold a pencil. Question is whether you're ready for what comes with it."

I wasn't. Nobody ever is. But I'd spent five tides pretending readiness was a thing you could engineer, so I set down the mug and did what engineers do: I made a list.

The list was short and ugly. One: the cave mouth at the Tollgate would begin sealing within the hour, water climbing the chamber walls like a patient hand closing into a fist. Two: the inland tunnel — my grandfather's unmarked line, Tomas's secret door — was half-blasted rubble since Harrow's crew had gone at it with powder two days ago, which meant the passage in was narrow, unstable, and exactly the kind of thing Adelene Harrow would rather collapse entirely than let us use again. Three: the manifest, the log, and grandfather's letter were safe in oilcloth inside my survey kit, which meant they were safe nowhere, because anything I carried could be taken from me by a magistrate with Harrow money in his pocket.

And four — the one I wrote last, because writing it made it real — Harrow knew the sixth tide was her last chance too.

"She'll rush it," Tomas said when I found him at the harbor rail, watching his former fleet's cranes swing like gallows birds against the grey morning. He'd been sleeping in the chandlery loft since he burned his cover sabotaging the blasting charges, and he had the look of a man who'd traded a career for a conscience and was still waiting for the receipt. "Adelene won't wait for the cave to seal politely. She'll send everyone she has through the tunnel on this tide, take the strongbox contents by force if she has to, and let the historians sort out the wreckage after."

"Can she get through? The tunnel's half down."

"Half down is half open." He turned to me, and there was salt wind in his voice. "She's got forty divers and twice that many laborers, and she's paying them triple to move rock. By slack water she'll have a gap a man can swim. Maybe two hours behind us, but coming."

I looked out at the bay, where the water was already sliding backward off the flats, exposing the ribbed dark of the seabed, and beyond it the black seam of the Tollgate, drinking air while it could.

"Then we need two hours," I said. "Or we need a wall."

Tomas gave me the look he always gave me when I said something that sounded insane and then turned out to be engineering. "A wall."

"A wall." And then, because the idea had been sitting in my chest all night like a coal, I finally said it out loud. "Tomas. How much of the old Rivergate spur is still on its wheels?"

* * *

Here is a thing about closed lines that only people who close them understand: a railway never really dies. It goes to sleep standing up.

My grandfather's map showed the Rivergate spur running from the harbor yards up along the bluff, past the salt works, curving inland toward junctions that hadn't seen a timetable in fifty years. The syndicate had pulled the "operational" track when they abandoned the line — meaning they'd pulled the first hundred yards where investors might look — and left the rest to rust where it lay, because hauling three miles of rail costs money and no auditor ever climbed past the fence.

Which meant that somewhere up that bluff, sleeping standing up, was a locomotive.

Not a good one. Not a young one. The townsfolk called her *Barnacle Bess*, though her nameplate, when we found her under a tarp of gull droppings and ivy in the old salt-works shed, read something dignified and long-forgotten. She was a shunting engine, squat as a stove, built to push heavy things short distances and ask no questions. Her boiler was sound — Mrs. Vann's late husband had kept her oiled for years out of some stubborn sentimentality, and his widow had kept the oiling up out of some even more stubborn spite.

"I always thought it was foolish," Mrs. Vann said, handing me the oil can like she was handing over a family Bible. "Fifty years of keeping an engine alive for a railway that wasn't coming back. Marten used to say, *you never know what a town will need to say no with.*" She wiped her hands on her apron. "I thought it was just talk. Old men and their engines."

"It wasn't talk," I said. "It was a contingency plan. He just didn't live to file it."

Getting her moving took most of the morning and every pair of hands in Rivergate who wasn't standing watch at the tunnel mouth. Old Silas Marsh, who'd driven freight on the spur before the closure and claimed his knees still kept the old schedule, took the regulator. Three of the Harrow divers who'd walked out two nights ago — Tomas's defection had started something none of us had dared hope for — worked the wheels and journals with grease and profanity. A dozen kids ran messages and got underfoot and were chased off and came back, because that is what kids are for.

And I did what my grandfather's map had trained me to do my whole life: I read a dead line until it spoke.

The track was weedy but true. The culverts held. The one bridge over the tidal creek groaned when Silas rolled the first test length across it, and I stood under it with my ear against the iron abutment listening for the specific note of a structure deciding whether to fail, and it sang true, or true enough, and I climbed out and gave the thumbs-up with my heart going like a trip-hammer.

By early afternoon, Barnacle Bess stood at the head of the spur above the tunnel mouth, breathing steam into the cold air, and the whole town of Rivergate had gathered on the bluff to look at her.

You have to understand what that gathering was. These were people whose grandfathers had taken syndicate money to keep quiet about the Meridian Star. People who had watched the railway leave and the salvage fleet arrive and had learned to call both progress. They weren't a mob. They were quieter than a mob and far more dangerous. They were a community remembering what it sold, and holding the receipt.

Silas leaned out of the cab. "Where do you want her, Miss Okonkwo?"

I looked down the grade at the tunnel mouth below — the half-blasted wound in the hillside where Harrow's crews were even now hauling rock, their lamps visible in the gloom like a nest of patient fireflies.

"On the tracks," I said. "All the way down. Right up against the tunnel mouth."

* * *

Harrow's foreman, a wide man named Gretch with a voice like gravel in a drum, came up the bluff trail at four o'clock with two crewmen and a document he clearly believed was a weapon.

"This tunnel is subject to an active salvage claim," he announced to the assembled town, holding the paper up like a lantern. "Obstruction of a lawful salvage operation carries penalties. I'm authorized to have every one of you charged."

"You're authorized," Mrs. Vann agreed pleasantly, from her camp stool directly in the path of the locomotive, knitting needles clicking. She had positioned herself there an hour ago and announced she would be moved only by tide or death, preferably hers, publicly.

Gretch looked at the crowd. At the engine. At the woman knitting. Something in his jaw worked.

"That machine's been dead fifty years," he tried. "That track's a memory."

"So am I," said Mrs. Vann, and turned a heel.

I stepped forward, because somebody had to translate, and translation was becoming my whole life. "Mr. Gretch. That locomotive is registered property of the Rivergate Historical Society, chartered last Tuesday, membership forty-one and growing. It is parked on private right-of-way deeded to the society by the town council — also last Tuesday; it's been a busy week. If your crews want to remove it, they're welcome to apply to the society in writing." I smiled. "We meet monthly."

"We don't have a month," Gretch snarled.

"No," I agreed. "Neither does the sea. That's rather the point."

He blustered. He threatened. He waved the document at faces that refused to arrange themselves into fear. And underneath all of it I could see him doing the arithmetic every bully eventually does: forty-one names on a charter, plus the ones not on it, plus a magistrate whose nerve was visibly failing now that the town had stopped being polite, plus a locomotive full of steam and principle sitting on his only route to the wreck.

He went back down the trail. The lamps in the tunnel kept working anyway — they had other approaches, other hands, and desperation is a fine excavator — but slower now, and angrier, and looking over their shoulders.

Tomas found me at the engine's flank, one hand on the warm iron of her boiler.

"You know what this is?" he said quietly.

"A legal nuisance?"

"It's the spur." His voice had something in it I'd only heard once before, in the air pocket between tides, when he'd talked about the lives he'd deferred. "My father hauled salvage on this line when I was small. Before the closure. I used to ride in the cab and pretend the whole coast was ours." He looked up at Barnacle Bess's smokestack, feathering steam into the dusk. "They closed it to bury a crime. And now it's the thing standing between the crime and the grave."

I put my hand next to his on the boiler. "Your father's line."

"Our line," he said, and didn't correct himself, and neither did I.

* * *

The sixth tide began at sundown, and it began the way all the great betrayals begin: gently.

I stood at the Tollgate overlook with my grandfather's map open on my knee, watching the water come back for it. The ebb had given us six days of the cave's open throat, six days of iron ribs and cold galleries and everything my family had lost sleeping in the dark. Now the flood slid up the flats without hurry, without malice, filling the channels first, then the ledges, then climbing the black walls of the cave mouth inch by patient inch, and I understood that the sea had never been our enemy or our ally. It had simply kept its schedule, the way I kept mine, the way Harrow kept hers, and everything that happened inside that schedule — the love, the evidence, the standoff on the bluff — was just what living things did in the gaps.

Behind me, down the slope, Barnacle Bess sat on the rails above the tunnel mouth with her lamp lit and her boiler hot, and Silas Marsh sat in her cab with a thermos and a shotgun he'd promised was ceremonial, and forty-one members of the Rivergate Historical Society had formed a picket line that looked suspiciously like a town.

And beyond the tunnel, deep in the hill, I could hear it if I held my breath: the ring of steel on stone. Harrow's crews, digging through the night, racing the water.

"They'll break through before slack," Tomas said beside me. He'd counted the hammer-blows like a man counting a pulse. "Two hours. Maybe less. When they come through, they'll come fast, and they won't stop for charters or knitting."

"Then we'd better make sure what they find on the other side isn't worth stopping for," I said.

He looked at me. "The strongbox contents are already out. You're carrying them."

"I know what I'm carrying." I folded the map along its worn creases — grandfather's ink, my additions, the whole inheritance in one sheet of paper — and looked west, where the last light was going copper on the water. "But Adelene doesn't know that for certain. And a woman who'd blast a tunnel to bury evidence will spend her last tide digging for a box that isn't there. Every hour her divers spend in that flooded gallery searching an empty hold is an hour the water rises and the truth gets further away from her hands."

"And when she figures it out?"

I watched the flood tide slide into the Tollgate, sealing the great black mouth the way a book closes, and felt something settle in me that wasn't peace exactly, but was its cousin.

"Then she comes up the hill," I said, "and finds out what a closed line is actually for."

Down in the dark, the hammering changed rhythm — faster now, urgent, the sound of men who could hear the sea coming for their work. Silas opened Bess's cylinder cocks and the old engine sighed a column of steam into the evening sky, loud and white and unmistakable, a signal fire made of iron.

Mrs. Vann put away her knitting, picked up her lantern, and walked — slowly, deliberately, on legs that had waited fifty years — to stand on the tracks directly in front of the locomotive, facing down the tunnel.

"What are you doing?" I called.

"Guarding the debt," she said, not turning around. "Marten kept the engine. Somebody ought to keep the watch."

The water rose. The hammers rang. And on a bluff above a drowning cave, a town that had sold its silence half a century ago stood on its grandfather's rusted railway, weaponized at last for justice, and waited for whatever came up out of the ground.
