## 39. A Trail for the Rivergate

The first train to leave Rivergate in fifty years did not leave on time.

Ulyana stood on the platform at half past seven with her pack at her feet and Tomas beside her, watching a crew of six men argue with a switch that had rusted into an opinion of its own. The locomotive — the same decommissioned engine the town had rolled across the tracks during the standoff, still wearing its war paint of harbor mud and righteous fury — hissed and grumbled like an old man asked to dance.

"She'll go when she's ready," said Mrs. Vann, arriving at Ulyana's elbow with the unhurried gait of a woman who had outlasted two keeperships, one husband's shame, and an entire corrupt fleet. "Everything in this town goes when it's ready. That's why the sea likes us. We're the only people who don't argue with it."

"You argued with it for fifty years," Ulyana said.

"I negotiated." Mrs. Vann pressed something into her hand: small, cold, wrapped in chandlery wax paper. Ulyana didn't need to open it. She knew the weight of a pocket watch, and she knew whose watch this was — the keeper's son, lost to the Tollgate before Ulyana was born, recovered from the wreck and laid to rest on the tide line only to be dug up again by Mrs. Vann three days later with the announcement that dead boys didn't need watches but living travelers might.

"Mrs. Vann, I can't take—"

"You can and you will. It keeps terrible time. Loses four minutes a day, always behind, like everyone in my family ever was." The old woman's eyes were dry and bright as beach glass. "But it never stopped. Fifty years underwater and it never stopped. I want that on the line with you. When you're somewhere the trains don't run yet, you look at that watch losing its four minutes and you remember that some things wait."

Ulyana closed her hand around it. It was heavier than a watch should be, or perhaps it was exactly as heavy as fifty years should be, and she had simply never held one before.

On the platform, the switch gave up its argument with a shriek that scattered gulls off the chandlery roof, and the crew cheered as though they'd personally invented railroading. One of them — Denny, who had spent the entire siege ferrying stew to the tunnel mouth and asking increasingly detailed questions about tide mathematics — spotted Ulyana and cupped his hands around his mouth.

"Miz Okonkwo! You gonna fix our timetable too, or just the whole rest of the country?"

"Just the rest of the country," she called back. "You lot are beyond help."

This was received as high praise. Someone started clapping. Ulyana felt her ears go hot and busied herself checking the map tube slung across her back for the fourth time, which was three times more than necessary, because the map was fine, the map had survived water and fire and a magistrate's attempted seizure, and the map would survive her embarrassment too.

Tomas leaned close. "They're going to clap every time you touch a railroad spike for the rest of your life."

"I know," she said miserably. "I'm going to have to move to a country where they've never heard of me."

"There isn't one. You mailed them all the map."

It was true. Ninety-one towns. Ninety-one envelopes of onionskin paper and chandlery-sealed wax, carrying the manifest, the captain's log, her grandfather's letter, and the closed-line map reproduced in full — every abandoned spur, every starved-out junction, every town the syndicate had cut loose and left to rust. The press in the back of Vann's Chandlery & Provisions had run for eleven days straight until the ink roller wore a groove in it, and Mrs. Vann had charged nobody anything except a promise from each volunteer that they'd eat something while they worked.

And the letters were coming back now. That was the thing Ulyana hadn't been prepared for. Replies, arriving by whatever irregular post served the interior — pencil-written, coal-smudged, some of them barely literate and all of them furious in the way that only decades of patient grief can be. Towns that had thought their stations were condemned by engineering were discovering they'd been condemned by bookkeeping. Mayors were writing to the railway commission. Surveyors were coming out of retirement. In Corren's Hollow, population four hundred, someone had already cleared the platform and painted a sign: NEXT TRAIN WHEN IT GETS HERE, AND NOT ONE MINUTE SOONER.

The commission had ordered the Rivergate line reopened first — the chairman's arrest had loosened everything, money moving like water finding old channels — and so here stood Ulyana Okonkwo, engineer, granddaughter, thief of nothing anymore, about to ride the first mile of track her family name had been blamed for losing.

"All aboard!" shouted the conductor, who was also the stationmaster, who was also the man who ran the bait shop, because Rivergate.

* * *

She walked the length of the train before boarding. It was a habit she couldn't have broken if she'd wanted to — the walk-along, hand trailing the cars, reading the equipment the way other people read faces. These cars were older than she was, resurrected from a siding where the syndicate had left them to rot rather than pay to scrap them properly. Even in ruin, even after fifty years, the couplings seated true when the crew tested them. Her grandfather's specifications. His hand was in every measurement, the way his handwriting had been on the Meridian Star's hull, the way his letter had been in the captain's safe, folded around the log page like a man tucking a child into bed.

*I did not sell those lines,* he had written, fifty years ago, in ink that had waited under the sea to be believed. *I refused to abandon the towns, and so they made me the abandonment.*

Ulyana had read the letter maybe forty times since the court. She could recite the middle section in her sleep. But it was the last line she carried up the steps of the passenger car, the line that had made the magistrate stop writing and the gallery stop breathing:

*If anyone of my blood is reading this: the lines are not dead. They are sleeping. Wake them gently, and do not let them tell you what you're worth.*

The whistle blew. Rivergate slid away from the platform — the chandlery with its new coat of paint, the harbor where the Harrow cranes stood idle and listing like embarrassed giants, the Tollgate cave mouth already swallowed by the returned tide, keeping its own counsel about what else it might give up someday.

Mrs. Vann stood on the platform until the curve took her from view. She did not wave. Keepers didn't wave; they watched things out past the edge of their keeping, and then they went back inside and lit the lamp.

Ulyana watched her go and found, to her surprise, that she wasn't crying. She was smiling. Somewhere behind them, the old woman was probably already re-pricing her admission tickets to reflect the cave's new fame, and charging extra for the story, and giving the extra away to children who promised to remember.

Some inheritances were maps. Some were watches that lost four minutes a day. Some were the habit of standing at the edge of a thing and refusing to look away.

* * *

The line climbed out of the marsh country through cuts her grandfather had surveyed, and Ulyana rode with her face at the window like a child, cataloguing.

There — the ballast shoulder, wider than standard, because he'd specified drainage for the spring floods. There — the remains of a signal post, its arm long gone, its base mortared with the distinctive herringbone pattern she'd seen in exactly one other place: the foundation of the chandlery, built by the same mason, in the same year, when the line and the town had still been the same ambition.

"He built for a hundred years," she said quietly. "He built everything for a hundred years."

"And they sold it in ten," Tomas said. He sat across from her, hat off, watching the country come back. He'd been quiet most of the morning — not brooding, she'd learned to tell the difference, but settling, the way water settles after a hull passes. A man recalibrating to a life without a contract. "You know what Adelene said to me, the day she signed? She said, 'Your grandfather would have hated you.' And I told her no. I told her my grandfather would have understood me perfectly, because duty that can't bend is just ballast, and ballast doesn't care where the ship goes."

Ulyana reached across and took his hand. His knuckles were still scarred from the collapse, from the plate that had pinned him while the tide dragged her upward with the log against her ribs and her heart tearing down the middle. Five weeks healed and the scars still mapped themselves fresh whenever she looked.

"What will you dive for now?" she asked. "Nobody's paying."

"That's the best part." He grinned, and it was the grin from the air pocket, from the space between tides where love had arrived uninvited and refused to leave. "Now I get to pick. There's a wreck off Corren's Hollow the locals say is cursed. I looked at the charts last night. It's not cursed, it's sitting on a rip current over a rock shelf, which means whoever finds it first needs someone who can read water." He squeezed her fingers. "Someone like a certain engineer who corrected my tide tables within an hour of meeting me and has been insufferable about it ever since."

"I was right."

"You were insufferably right. It's different."

The train slowed — not a scheduled stop, there were no schedules yet, the whole line was running on hope and borrowed coal — and a cluster of buildings resolved out of the morning haze. Kiln's Bend. Population, according to her grandfather's marginal note in the map: 1,200 souls, grain elevator, junction yard. According to the reply letters: 300 souls, no elevator, no yard, one woman named Petra Ilves who had single-handedly organized the clearing of the platform and wrote letters in a hand like a fist.

The platform was crowded. Word had traveled ahead, the way word does when a town has spent fifty years being nobody's destination and suddenly finds itself on a route again. Children hung off the fence posts. An ancient man in a railway cap that predated the closure stood at rigid attention as though the last half-century had been a lunch break.

Ulyana stepped down onto the platform of Kiln's Bend, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds do when they're not sure whether they're allowed to hope out loud.

Then the ancient man removed his cap, and said, "My father laid this platform. They told him it was temporary." His voice cracked on *temporary*. "Fifty years. Nothing about it was temporary except the lying."

The cheer that went up rattled the grain dust out of the eaves.

* * *

She stayed two hours. It became three. It became nearly five, because there was a question of the junction switch — seized, but salvageable, and Ulyana found herself flat on her back in the ballast with a wrench and an audience of delighted children while Tomas held the lamp and offered commentary she pretended not to hear.

When the switch finally threw clean, the crowd applauded, and a boy of about nine asked her to sign his shirt, and she signed the map instead — a corner of it, Kiln's Bend, with the date and a small notation: *switch restored, platform sound, coffee terrible.* Then she relented and signed the shirt too, because childhood was short and heroes were rarer than they should be.

It was Petra Ilves who walked her back to the train, a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and eyes that had done arithmetic on Ulyana the moment she stepped off.

"The letters you sent," Petra said. "Mine's the one that said we don't want apologies. We want schedules."

"I remember. It was the politest threat I've ever received."

"Good. Here's another one." Petra stopped at the bottom of the steps and lowered her voice. "There's a line north of us. Forty miles of nothing, officially. Closed before the war, before your grandfather even. Not on any map the commission has." She held out a folded paper, soft with handling. "But my grandmother drew what she remembered, and my grandmother never forgot anything in her life except where she put her teeth. You'll want to compare it with yours."

Ulyana took the paper with both hands, the way you take something that might be alive.

"Why give it to me?"

Petra looked at her for a long moment, and then at Tomas, and then back.

"Because when your envelopes came, my husband cried at the kitchen table, and my husband hasn't cried since the elevator burned. Because somebody owes this country fifty years of truth and you're the first person who's been paying it back instead of collecting interest." She shrugged, enormous and unembarrassed. "And because I want my grandchildren to hear a train. Now go on. You've got a schedule to make, and we don't want apologies for that either."

* * *

The train pulled out of Kiln's Bend at dusk, and Ulyana spread the map across her knees in the emptying car, unrolling it with the reverence she usually saved for structural steel.

Her grandfather's ink. Her own additions now layered over it — Rivergate, the Tollgate, the Meridian Star's resting place marked with a small star, Kiln's Bend with its restored switch, ninety-one towns whose names she'd written in the margins as the replies came in, each one a place that had stopped being a dot and become a door.

She smoothed Petra's grandmother's paper alongside it. The old woman's hand was shaky but sure, lines drawn from memory with the confidence of someone who had ridden them. And there — forty miles north of Kiln's Bend, exactly where the official record showed nothing — a junction. A fork. Two branches running toward country Ulyana had never surveyed, toward towns that weren't even on her grandfather's map, which meant they'd been closed before he ever held the pen.

Which meant there were towns older than her inheritance. Places that had been forgotten twice — once by the syndicate, once by history itself.

"Tomas," she said.

He looked up from the chart he was sketching — the Corren's Hollow wreck, the rip current, the rock shelf, drawn freehand with a diver's economy.

"Look at this."

He came around the seat and bent over the papers, and she watched his eyes do what her eyes had done: widen, narrow, calculate. The fork. The branches. The blank country beyond.

"That's not on your grandfather's map," he said.

"No. It's older than his map." She picked up her pen — her pen, her ink, her hand — and drew the junction in, careful and clean, and beside it she wrote the words that had been waiting in her all her life without her knowing:

*Survey required. Bring someone who reads water.*

Tomas read it upside down and laughed, low and warm, and dropped into the seat beside her, shoulder against hers, as the train ran on through the darkening marsh country with its lamp lit and its whistle singing across the tops of the reeds.

"So," he said. "Where first?"

Ulyana looked down at the map — his lines and hers, the dead man's hand and the living ones, a trail that had started as an accusation and was becoming, mile by mile, an invitation.

"North," she said. "Before the next ebb."

Somewhere behind them, the sea turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, keeping its schedule.
