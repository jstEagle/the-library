## 40. The Next Ebb

### Ulyana

The train stopped at a water tower that no longer fed anything, and the conductor — a young man named Pell who had taken the job three weeks ago, when the line reopened, and still touched his cap like he was afraid it would evaporate — leaned into the car and said, "Fifteen minutes for water. Not that there's any hurry. There's one more train today and it's us."

Ulyana had been on reopened lines for four months now, and she had learned that this was how they talked: apologetically, hopefully, with a great deal of mention of what things were *not yet*. The platforms were not yet rebuilt. The timetables were not yet printed. The towns at the end of them were not yet sure they believed.

She stepped down onto the gravel with her survey kit over one shoulder and the map tube in her hand, and she stood in the middle of nowhere — which was to say, the exact center of somebody's life, once — and breathed in the smell of creosote and wet grass and coal smoke from a locomotive that was working again after fifty years of rusting into the marsh.

Tomas came down behind her, carrying both their bags because he had won the argument about it by simply picking them up while she was checking the bridge notes.

"Water tower's sound," she said, tapping the leg she'd been eyeballing. "Somebody kept painting it. Look — layers. Somebody painted it every few years like they expected trains to come back."

"Somebody did expect," Tomas said. "That's the whole trick of these towns, isn't it? They never quite let go."

He said it lightly, but she heard the weight under it, because she'd heard him say versions of it all spring — in Merrow's Crossing, where the stationmaster had cried into his ledger; in Halvatt, where they'd slept in a grain shed and been woken at dawn by children wanting to see the diver's helmet; in the town whose name she could never remember but whose bakery woman had pressed a loaf on them and refused payment, saying, *You're the ones who mailed the papers. My father drove that line forty years and died owing money to the men who closed it.*

The manifest had gone out in March. By April, the chairman had been arrested in a city Ulyana had only ever seen as an inked rectangle on her grandfather's map. By May, the reopening orders had started coming down, line by line, like a tide coming in — slow, then all at once.

And now here she was, standing on gravel in the middle of the day's fifteen minutes, holding a map that had two hands on it now instead of one.

"Pell says there's a road," Tomas said, nodding east. "Two miles, then the old junction. The one your grandfather marked."

"He didn't mark it." She pulled the map tube open and slid the sheet out into the light, careful of the wind. "He marked the *line*. The junction itself he left blank. See?" She pointed to the small empty space where the northbound spur met the coast route, a blankness so deliberate it might as well have been a signature. "He drew everything else on this map down to culverts. But not that junction. He drew around it."

Tomas studied it the way he studied water — patiently, assuming it knew something he didn't. "Why would a man leave one place blank?"

"Because he was going to go back and fill it in," Ulyana said. "And then they took everything from him, and he never did." She rolled the map up before the wind could argue. "So we'll fill it in. That's the job. That's the whole job now: finish his map. Every blank, every closed spur, every town he inked a question mark next to and couldn't answer."

"That's years," Tomas said.

"Yes," she said, and found she was smiling. "Isn't it wonderful?"

### Tomas

The junction, when they reached it, turned out to be a ghost with good bones.

Tomas walked it the way he walked a wreck: slowly, touching things, letting the structure tell him its history. Rails green with moss but true. A signal post leaning like a drunk uncle but still standing. And under twenty years of blackberry cane, the outline of a station house, its foundation square and stubborn.

"Someone will want this back," he said.

"Everyone wants it back," Ulyana said. She was crouched at the track junction itself, clearing leaves away from the switch stand with her bare hands. "That's the thing nobody told me when I was riding night freights through dead country. I thought these places were abandoned. They're not abandoned. They're *waiting*. There's a difference. Abandoned means nobody cares. Waiting means everybody cares and nobody can do anything about it." She looked up at him, dirt on her knuckles, sun in her eyes. "We're the something."

"We're two people and a rucksack full of instruments."

"We're two people and a rucksack full of instruments and a press run of maps in every town between here and the coast," she said. "You'd be amazed what people do when you hand them a piece of paper that says their grandfather wasn't crazy. I've watched it four times now. It's my favorite thing I've ever seen, and I've seen a shipwreck give up a confession."

He sat down on the old platform edge beside her, which was the closest thing the junction had to furniture, and got out the bread the bakery woman's cousin had given them at the last stop. He tore it in half and handed her the bigger piece without thinking about it, and she took it without noticing, and somewhere in the exchange Tomas understood — not for the first time, but with a new kind of clarity — that he had stopped keeping score.

That was the thing about Ulyana Okonkwo that no one warned you about. You met her and she was all edges: the engineer's squint, the cataloguing gaze, the way she entered a room and located every exit before she located the people. Four months ago he'd have sworn she'd die alone in a railcar somewhere, competent and unwelcome and perfectly fine about it.

Then he'd watched her walk back down into a collapsing wreck on the fifth tide, alone, with a rail jack, for him. And he'd watched her come up carrying both him and the evidence, because apparently *choosing* was a thing she refused to do when she could simply refuse to choose.

"You're staring at me," she said, around bread.

"I'm surveying you. It's professional."

"Mm. What are your findings?"

"Load-bearing," he said. "Sound for years. Possibly decades."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one that had taken him weeks to earn and that he now guarded like salvage rights — and leaned her shoulder into his, and for a while they just sat on the dead platform of a living line and ate bread while the wind came off the marsh smelling of salt and new growth.

Behind them, far off, the sea kept its schedule. He could hear it if he listened: that long patient breathing. He'd spent half his life listening to it professionally, timing his lungs to it, trusting it the way other men trusted God. Now it sounded less like an employer and more like a companion. Something that would keep going whether or not he showed up — and that seemed, lately, to be glad when he did.

"So the survey," he said finally. "North spur. What's the approach?"

"Flooded cut past the third mile marker, if the almanac's honest. Which it won't be." She tapped the bread crust against her knee like a gavel. "Which is why I brought someone who reads water."

"Flattery."

"Accuracy. There's a difference. Ask anyone who's confused the two."

He grinned and lay back on the warm planks, arms behind his head, and looked up at a sky doing its best impression of forever. "You know what Adelene used to say? Before a dive, she'd say: *the sea doesn't care about your plans.*" He paused. "She meant it as a threat. Keep your head down, know your place, the water's bigger than you."

"And what do you mean by it?"

He considered. Down the line, somewhere out of sight, the afternoon freight they'd ridden in on was coupling up for the return run, and its whistle crossed the marsh in two long notes, and a bird answered it, insolently.

"I mean it as permission," he said. "The sea doesn't care about anybody's plans. So you may as well make good ones."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then she unrolled the map across both their laps, weighted the corners with stones, and uncapped her pen.

### Ulyana

The blank space at the junction was smaller than a coin.

She'd been looking at it since she was nine years old, when her grandfather first spread this map on a kitchen table in a house they lost two years later, and traced the lines with a cracked-teacup finger and told her, *Every one of these goes somewhere, little star. Remember that. A closed line isn't a dead line. It's a held breath.*

She had spent her whole adult life believing him and being unable to prove it, and now she sat on the proof with her boots on it, and the blank space waited for her hand.

"What do we put?" Tomas asked. "Elevation? Bearing?"

"All of it. Eventually." She bent close. "But first —"

First, in the corner of the blank space, small enough to fit under a thumbnail, she wrote the date. Then, beneath it, in letters no bigger than grains of rice:

*Surveyed by U. Okonkwo & T. Reinhart. Line open.*

She sat back. Tomas read it upside down, the way he always did, and she watched his face do the thing it did — the quiet rearrangement, the man deciding what a moment weighed.

"Your grandfather's hand is all over this map," he said. "Mine's on it now too."

"That was the idea."

"No," he said, and took the pen from her, gently, asking with his eyebrows, and she nodded. He turned the map toward himself and added one more line beneath theirs, in a diver's careful block print:

*Reads water.*

"There," he said. "Credentials. So the next person who finds this knows the survey's honest."

"The next person," Ulyana said, and felt the phrase settle into her chest like a keel. That was the part she hadn't known how to want until Rivergate: not the vindication, not the chairman in handcuffs, not even the lines opening one by one like flowers on a time-lapse. The *next person*. Some kid in thirty years pulling this map out of an archive, or finding it nailed to a chandlery door in a town that smelled of salt rot and second chances, and knowing that somebody had come through and written down the truth and signed it.

Her grandfather had drawn a trail and died before anyone followed it. She was drawing one that people were already walking.

Down the track, the return freight whistled again, closer now. Fifteen minutes had become forty, which was the other thing about reopened lines: the schedules were aspirational.

"We should catch it," Tomas said, without moving.

"In a minute."

"It's the last train."

"It's also," she said, "going to wait, because Pell loves his job and has nowhere else to be. We have time. We have, as far as I can calculate, an unreasonable amount of time." She looked at him. "Do you feel it? The not-running?"

He thought about it honestly, which was one of the thousand reasons. "I keep waiting for the contract clause," he admitted. "Forfeit everything. Aid to an outsider. I keep bracing for the penalty, and then remembering there isn't one anymore. I'm free and it feels like falling."

"Good," she said.

"Good?"

"Freefall's just a dive you haven't named yet. You taught me that." She rolled the map, stood, and offered him her hand — not because he needed it, but because she liked the transaction. "Come on, diver. North before the next ebb."

They ran for the train like children, bags banging, gravel flying, and made it as the wheels began to turn, and Pell hauled them aboard grinning like they'd confirmed every hope he'd ever had about railways, and the carriage swallowed them into its lamplit dusk.

### Tomas

That night, in the guard's van they'd paid far too little for, Ulyana slept with the map tube in the crook of her arm like a child with a boat.

Tomas sat by the open door and watched the marsh go by under a fat moon, and did the accounting he did some nights — not of money, which was nearly gone, but of self.

He had surrendered his contract in April, signing his name above the line where his livelihood used to be, and the world had not ended. He had walked out of the Harrow fleet with the other divers, and Adelene Harrow had watched them go from her office window with a face like a closing door, and the world had not ended. He had learned typesetting on a chandlery press older than both their grandfathers, and hauled wet paper, and gotten ink under his nails that took a week to fade, and the world had not ended.

What had ended was the version of him that needed someone else's schedule to live by.

Except — he glanced at the sleeping engineer, at the map, at the moonlit blur of the next junction already rising out of the dark — except that he'd traded one schedule for another, hadn't he. Hers. Theirs. The sea's, always the sea's, patient under everything.

He found he didn't mind. A schedule shared was a different animal entirely. A schedule shared was a promise.

Ulyana stirred. "You're thinking loudly," she murmured, eyes still shut.

"How can you tell?"

"Your silence has a tone now. It didn't used to." She opened one eye. "What is it?"

"Tomorrow. The flooded cut. Third mile marker."

"What about it?"

"If the almanac's lying," he said, "how deep do you figure?"

She was silent a moment, running numbers he could almost hear clicking. "Twelve feet at worst. Fifteen if the culvert's silted."

"Fifteen feet," he said. "I've dived wrecks at a hundred and ten. Fifteen feet is a puddle with ambitions."

"But?"

"But nothing." He smiled at the dark. "It's just that I get to do it with you watching from the bank with a notebook, yelling corrections."

"I don't yell."

"You absolutely yell. You yelled at a magistrate."

"He deserved it. The culvert won't." She sat up, hair wild, moonlight on her face, and looked at him with that expression he'd once mistaken for severity and now recognized as the purest thing she had: attention. Full, undivided, generous attention. "Tomas. Say the actual thing."

He looked out at the marsh, at the rails running north into the dark like a sentence waiting for its verb, and said the actual thing.

"I used to think my life would be measured in dives. Bottom time, depth, tonnage salvaged. A number at the end." He turned back to her. "Now I think it'll be measured in miles of line reopened. Towns that stop waiting. Blank spaces on a map that aren't blank anymore." He reached over and rested his hand on the map tube in her arm. "Yours and mine. Both counts. Same life."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the sea turned over in its sleep, keeping its schedule, and the train ran north through the reeds with its lamp lit.

"Well," she said at last, voice rougher than usual, "we'd better get some sleep, then. We've got years of work."

"Years," he agreed, and lay down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, as the wheels sang their iron song across the tops of the marsh.

And far behind them, and far ahead of them, and everywhere the water touched, the ebb began — patient, faithful, drawing back from the land to show, as it always did, and always would, what had been hidden and what was ready to be found.
