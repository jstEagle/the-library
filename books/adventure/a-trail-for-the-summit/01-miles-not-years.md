## 1. Miles, Not Years

The desert did not keep time the way towns did.

Beatrix Dunmore had learned that in her first season at Redwash, when the station's clock froze in a June dust storm and nobody came to fix it for eleven weeks. The clock still hung on the wall behind the desk, its hands stopped at 4:17, and she had never wound it. It seemed dishonest to pretend the hours mattered here, where a day was measured in other units entirely: in miles walked before the heat rose, in gallons hauled from the deep well, in the slow migration of shadow across the canyon rim like the hand of some vast and patient dial.

Five years, the paperwork would have said, if there had been any paperwork. Five years since she'd signed on as ranger of this stretch of nothing—forty miles of red sandstone and greasewood flat, two waterholes that belonged to her by treaty of solitude, one windmill with a loose vane that sang all night like a woman mourning something she couldn't name. Five years, but Beatrix didn't count them that way. She counted what lay between them.

Fourteen thousand six hundred miles, give or take. She kept the tally in a ledger with a cracked spine, one line per day, the figures small and square as fence posts. Patrol to the north rim and back: nine. Circuit of the stock tanks: twelve. The long walk down Dry Fork to check on old Manuela's goats: seven, plus three for the detour around the flash-flood cut. She had once tried to explain it to a survey crew that passed through, a young man with soft hands who asked how long she'd been out here, and she'd said *about twenty-two thousand miles*, and he'd laughed, thinking it a joke, and she hadn't corrected him because correcting him would have meant explaining, and explaining was a door she kept nailed shut.

Miles were honest. Miles accumulated without asking anything of you. A year demanded you remember what you'd been doing in it—a birthday, an anniversary, a face—and Beatrix had spent five years making sure there was nothing in her years worth remembering. No photographs. No letters. No name in any register that could be traced back along its thread to the girl she had been before.

Before was another country. She had burned the bridges into it herself, stone by stone, and she told herself every morning that she'd done it gladly.

* * *

On the morning the thief came back, the desert smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

She stepped out of the station at first light and stood a moment on the packed-earth porch, coffee tin warming her palms, reading the air the way other people read newspapers. Ozone and creosote. The pressure sat low against her ears. Somewhere south, over the mesas past the state line, a storm was deciding whether to be born, and everything alive in Redwash knew it—the coyotes had sung strangely the night before, high and broken, and the ravens were riding thermals in tight, agitated circles above the rimrock.

Beatrix drank her coffee and watched the light come up the way it always did, pouring east to west across the flats until the whole world went the color of an open flame. It was the hour she loved most, though she'd have denied loving anything. In this light the desert forgave its own harshness. The twisted junipers looked sculptural instead of starved. The sand took on a grain like sugar. Even the windmill's mournful song softened into something almost sweet.

Then she saw the dust.

It rose off the old wagon road two miles east, a thin brown plume moving fast, too fast for freight, too steady for a rider in trouble. Someone who knew exactly where they were going and wanted to be there before the weather turned. Beatrix set down the tin, went inside, and did not hurry. Hurrying was for people who believed the desert might give them a second chance.

She saddled the dun mare, Ruthie—named without sentiment, after the only other female name she could think of that wasn't her mother's—and rode out at a trot, letting the distance close at its own pace. The plume of dust resolved as she drew near: a single horseman, dark-cloaked against the glare, riding a rangy gray with a long stride. He was a mile ahead when she first marked him, and he did not look back, which was itself a kind of signature. Men who were lost looked back constantly. Men running from something specific never did; they'd already memorized what was behind them.

Beatrix knew the road he was on. It ran southeast toward the Split Rock crossing, thirty miles of nothing, and beyond that the river country where a man could lose himself among the canyons for a season. She also knew something the rider apparently did not: the road forked at the old kiln site, and the southern branch washed out every spring. If he took it blind, he'd find his horse chest-deep in quicksand clay before noon.

She gave Ruthie her head.

The mare was fourteen hands of bad temper and good heart, and she ate up the miles in a rolling lope that Beatrix felt in her teeth. The gap closed. Two miles became one. The rider's cloak snapped in the building wind, and now she could see details: the gray horse was lathered, pushed hard from somewhere far off; the man rode light in the saddle, easy, a body that had grown up around horses the way driftwood grows around rope. There was a bedroll behind his cantle and nothing else. A man traveling empty. A man traveling *away*.

One mile became half.

And then, without any signal she could later identify—no turn of the head, no shift of weight—the rider simply *knew*. She saw it happen, the way you see wind move through a field of grass: a ripple of awareness passing down his spine. His shoulders dropped a quarter inch. His seat deepened. And the gray lengthened its stride from fast to desperate, hooves throwing red dirt in twin rooster tails.

Beatrix put two fingers to her mouth and whistled, the ranger's whistle, sharp enough to cut the wind. "Ranger! Hold up!"

He didn't hold up. Of course he didn't. But the whistle bought her something better than obedience: it made him choose. Ahead, the kiln ruins rose out of the flat like broken teeth, and the fork beyond them, and the man hauled left onto the southern branch—the washed-out one, the trap—because it was the more direct line and he was betting speed against terrain.

Beatrix smiled without warmth and swung wide onto the northern branch, the one that climbed the low mesa and overlooked the wash. She'd ride parallel, let him founder in the clay, and be standing on the bank when he did, calm as Sunday, with questions she had waited five years to ask.

Because she knew him. Not his name—she'd never gotten a name—but his shape, his silhouette against a doorway five years and four hundred miles gone, the night someone had lifted a leather document case from her saddlebag while she slept under a bridge outside Candelaria. Inside that case: her birth certificate. Her father's letters. Her original papers, the true ones, bearing the name she'd been born with and the history she'd spent five years burying. Without them she was Beatrix Dunmore, a name she'd chosen from a gravestone in a town she'd passed through once, a name with no roots and no branches, a name that was a coat she wore buttoned to the throat.

The thief had taken her selfhood for whatever a forged identity fetched in border towns, and Beatrix had followed rumors of him ever since—half a continent of rumors, each one colder than the last—until the trail went so stale she'd done the only sensible thing. She'd stopped chasing and started waiting. She'd taken a post in empty country, on roads a fleeing man might use, and she'd stayed. Fourteen thousand miles of staying.

Now here he was, or someone shaped like him, riding straight into her jurisdiction like an answer to a prayer she'd stopped believing in.

* * *

The storm arrived while she was still a mile from the wash.

It came down out of the south all at once, the way desert storms do—not building but simply *arriving*, a wall of purple-black cloud dragging a skirt of rain across the flats. The temperature fell ten degrees between one stride of the mare and the next. Rain hit Beatrix's face in fat, cold coins, and the smell of wet creosote rose up so thick it was nearly a taste, and the world shrank to fifty yards of gray roaring blur.

She reached the overlook above the wash and reined in, squinting through the deluge.

The southern road was gone. Not damaged—*gone*, dissolved into a brown torrent that filled the wash from bank to bank, carrying away mesquite stumps and fencing wire and, somewhere in its churning middle, the story of what had happened to the rider on the gray. She searched the floodline downstream, heart hammering, looking for a body, a horse, a hat—anything. The rain sheeted. Lightning walked the mesa tops on white legs, and thunder rolled through the canyon country like furniture being moved in a house the size of the sky.

Then movement, upstream. A flicker of dark against the red rock of the far bank.

The rider had made it across—or rather, had seen the flood coming soon enough to turn north and pick his way through the shallows above the confluence, the route Beatrix herself would have chosen, the route only a man who knew this country intimately would have known existed. He was a quarter mile ahead now, a smudge of darkness climbing the slickrock terrace on the far side, and the rain was erasing his tracks even as his horse made them.

Beatrix stood in her stirrups and shouted into the storm, a wordless sound, rage and frustration torn out of her by the roots. The figure paused at the top of the terrace—one heartbeat, two—and she would have sworn, later, that he turned and looked directly at her across the flooded wash, through the rain, across five years. She couldn't see his face. She never saw his face. But she felt the look land on her like a thrown stone, deliberate and aimed, and then he was gone over the rimrock and the storm swallowed everything.

She sat in the rain a long time, water running off her hat brim in ropes, watching the empty terrace.

* * *

By evening the storm had blown itself out over the mountains, and the desert steamed gently in the last light, giving back the moisture it had borrowed. Beatrix walked the wash on foot, reading the mud the way she read everything—carefully, without hope of tenderness. The flood had scoured the southern road clean. On the northern terrace she found what the rain had spared: gray horse tracks, half-dissolved, heading southeast toward the river country. Beside them, pressed deep where a man had dismounted to scout the crossing, a boot print with a split heel.

She crouched over it for a long moment. Then she took out her ledger, opened it against her knee, and wrote beneath today's mileage line: *SE road, gray horse, split-heel boot, headed for the river.* Her handwriting didn't shake. She'd trained it not to.

Back at the station, night came down soft and enormous. The windmill sang. Beatrix lit the lamp, heated beans, ate without tasting, and then sat with the lamp turned low and did the thing she did on nights like this, the ritual beneath the rituals: she took the tally book and read backward through it, mile by mile, letting the numbers stand in for everything they'd replaced. Nine. Twelve. Seven, plus three. Twenty-two thousand lines of distance, and not one of them leading anywhere she couldn't walk away from.

That was the design. That was the armor. A life built so that no loss could reach anything vital, because nothing vital remained. She had a roof, a horse, a well, a job that asked nothing of her heart. She had the desert, which never lied to her and never needed anything she couldn't give. People in the settlements downriver called her the ghost of Redwash—she'd heard them, through barroom windows, through the thin walls of supply depots—said she'd been out there so long she'd gone quiet as the rocks, said maybe she wasn't quite a woman anymore, just the shape of one the wind had filled.

Let them talk. Talk was weather. It blew through and left nothing behind.

But tonight, drying her socks by the stove, Beatrix admitted the thing she admitted perhaps twice a year, in the strict privacy of her own skull: that the armor fit too well. That somewhere in five years of measuring her life in miles, the miles had become the point—that she no longer knew what she was protecting, only that she protected it, vigilantly, endlessly, the way a sentry guards an empty fort because the guarding is all that's left of the war.

Her father would have known what to say about that. Her father, who had loved distances the way other men loved music, who used to tell her that a map was just a promise the land makes to break your heart. He'd been dead five years too—drowned, they said, on his last expedition upriver, though they never found the body—and she had run from that grief so fast and so far that she'd ended up erasing herself entirely, as if the girl whose father died could be left behind like a coat on a chair.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the desert breathed its long wet breath, and somewhere far to the southeast, in the black canyon country where the river began, a man with a split-heeled boot was making camp, unaware—or perhaps perfectly aware—that the ghost of Redwash had finally seen him again.

Beatrix banked the fire, checked the loads in her rifle out of habit rather than fear, and lay down on her cot with her boots within reach.

Tomorrow she'd ride southeast. Tomorrow the tally would begin again, new miles added to the old, the ledger growing one line longer. But as sleep took her, she thought not of miles but of the moment on the terrace—that pause, that look flung back across the flood—and for the first time in five years, the number she carried in her chest wasn't a distance.

It was a countdown.
