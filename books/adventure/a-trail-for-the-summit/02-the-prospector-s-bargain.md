## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The vultures found him before I did.

I'd been three days out from the Redwash station, working a loop I knew better than my own heartbeat—north along the rimrock, down through the juniper flats where the sand turned red as old blood, then west toward the dry washes that cut the mesa country into ribs. It was a route I walked every spring, partly because water might be there and partly because walking it meant nobody else had to, and a ranger who stopped doing her rounds was a ranger who'd started dying in place.

The birds circled over the second wash of the day, the one the old-timers called Coffin Draw on account of nothing anyone could remember. Vultures over desert country were common as heat shimmer—something dead always lay somewhere—but these weren't the loose, patient circles of a long-dead cow. They were tight. Interested. Dropping low and rising again like flies at a wound.

I turned Sorrel off the trail and rode up the wash's lip with my hand resting easy on the rifle scabbard, which is to say not easy at all.

He lay in the shade of an undercut bank, propped against the sandstone like a man who'd sat down to rest and been nailed there. Old, weathered to the color of his own leather coat, white beard gone yellow at the mouth. His shirt was dark from sternum to belt, and the smell reached me before the details did—that sweet-rot stink of a gut wound gone septic, a smell I'd known twice before and hoped never to know again.

"Goddamn birds," he said when I swung down. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Been arguing with 'em all morning. They think they're patient. They don't know stubborn."

I crouched a few feet away, close enough to see, far enough that if he had a gun under that coat I wouldn't die surprised. "How long?"

"Since yesterday. Maybe the day before. Days stop meaning much." He tried to laugh and it came apart into wet coughing. When he pulled his hand from his mouth it was red going brown. "You the law?"

"Ranger," I said. "Beatrix Dunmore."

Something moved behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but a kind of sorting, like a man counting coins he wasn't sure were real. "Dunmore," he repeated. "Huh."

"You know the name?"

"Everybody knows somebody by some name." He waved the question off with fingers stained to the knuckle. Then he looked past me, up the wash, with the sudden sharp attention of a man who has spent fifty years listening for things that want to kill him. "You come alone?"

"I come alone."

"Riders behind you?"

"Not for two days."

He nodded slowly, and some of the tension went out of him, or rather it changed shape—the tension of fear becoming the tension of decision. "Then we got time to do business. Help me sit up straighter. A man ought to do his dying sitting up."

* * *

His name was Elias Crane, though he offered it the way a man gives up a tooth—reluctantly, and only because keeping it hurt worse. I got him propped against my bedroll with his back flat to the stone, gave him water from my canteen in small sips, and did what little there was to do for a wound like his, which was nothing. The bullet had gone in below his ribs and out somewhere no doctor would ever find it. He'd bound himself with a strip of shirt and packed the hole with creosote mud, and he'd ridden—or crawled; he wouldn't say which—for the better part of a day before the strength left him. The mud had bought him time. Time was all it bought.

"You need a surgeon," I said, knowing it as useless words. The nearest one was two days hard ride, and Crane didn't have two hours.

"I need about six things," he said. "A surgeon's fourth on the list. First is a priest, and I fired mine thirty years ago. Second's a drink. Third—" He stopped. Looked at me a long moment with eyes the pale washed-out blue of river ice. "Third's you."

"I'm listening."

He fumbled inside his coat, and I'll admit my hand drifted toward my pistol, but what came out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, flat and rectangular and heavy-looking for its size. He held it on his knees with both hands, the way a man holds a child or a Bible.

"You know the Silverhead?" he said.

"Everyone in the territory knows the Silverhead."

"Everyone knows the river." He tapped the bundle. "Nobody knows where it's born. There's men who've spent fortunes making sure of that. Men who'd have shot me ten times over for this if they'd known I had it. Which, as it happens, they did know. Hence." He gestured down at himself with something close to humor. "Hence the present condition."

I said nothing. In five years of erasing myself I'd learned that silence pulls more truth than questions, and Crane was already bleeding truth the way he was bleeding everything else.

"Forty years I've prospected this country," he said. "Came out here young and dumb with a mule named after a girl who'd jilted me, and I've walked every wash from the Redwash to the high divide. And twenty-two years ago I found something I shouldn't have. Found the headwaters of the Silverhead. Up where the maps go blank and the air gets thin enough to bite." His hands tightened on the bundle. "And I found what was upstream of it too. What they'd put upstream of it."

"What did you find?"

He shook his head. "Not telling. Not because I'm cruel. Because if you know, you're dead, same as me, and I've watched enough dying this week." A cough took him, long and tearing, and when it passed he sat gray as ash and smaller somehow, as if the coughing had wrung him out. "But I'm telling you this: there's poison in that water. Has been for years. And every town downstream drinks it. Every ranch, every well, every child. And the men who put it there have killed to keep it quiet, and I am the last man alive carrying proof of where to look."

"The syndicate," I said. Not a question. Everyone in the territory knew the Consolidated Silverhead Mining Company owned half the land and most of the law between here and the railhead. Their stamps were on freight wagons and payrolls and, if rumor held true, on more than one coroner's report.

Crane smiled, and it was a terrible smile, yellowed and bloody and glad. "Quick," he said. "Good. Takes some men years to say that word out loud. Fear's got a way of renaming things. Call it the Company long enough and you forget it's just men with guns and ledgers." He looked down at the oilcloth package. "This map cost me everything except my life, and now it's costing me that too. So I mean to spend it right."

"Why me?"

"Because you came alone. Because you're the law and the law can't sell a thing like this without hanging for it. Because—" he paused, and his pale eyes did that sorting thing again, turning me over like a stone looking for what lived underneath, "—because you've got the look of a woman running from something, and in my experience a woman running from something will walk through hell to reach a thing worth reaching. Am I wrong?"

I stood up. It was reflex more than thought—the old armor swinging shut. "You're dying, Mr. Crane. You don't know me."

"No," he agreed mildly. "But I knew a man once who wore that same look. Same set to the shoulders. Like he was carrying a door on his back and wouldn't set it down for anybody." He settled deeper against the stone, and his voice dropped, went softer, almost gentle, which frightened me more than any threat could have. "Thomas Dunmore."

The wash went very quiet. The wind that had been moving through the junipers all morning stopped, or seemed to. Somewhere above us a vulture shifted its weight on the rimrock with a sound like a stick breaking.

"My father's name," I said, when I could make my voice work. "My father's name was Thomas Dunmore."

"I know," said Elias Crane. "That's why I sent for you."

* * *

He hadn't sent for me, of course. He'd been shot in the back at a place called Larkspur Crossing and left for the birds, and finding me had been luck, or providence, or the desert's occasional habit of putting the right people in the wrong places. But he stuck to the word *sent*, and I understood eventually that he meant something larger by it, and I let him keep it, because a dying man should be allowed his grammar.

We talked while the sun crossed the sky and the shadows of the wash walls slid across him like slow hands. He told me he'd known my father twenty-three years ago, when Thomas Dunmore had come west with a survey crew and a reputation for honesty that made him either beloved or inconvenient depending on who was paying. Crane had guided him partway up the Silverhead country. My father had been looking for gold, Crane said, or so the syndicate believed—and believing it, they'd sold him supplies, sold him horses, sold him a guide named Wren who was younger then and quieter and whose eyes already had that look of a man memorizing exits.

"Wren," I said. The name landed in me like a coal.

"Courier boy. Half-wild, quick as a whiptail lizard. Your father liked him. Trusted him, which was the mistake everybody made with that boy." Crane's breath was shortening now, coming in shallower draws, and I knew the afternoon was spending itself faster than I wanted. "Your father found what I found. Up at the source. Only he found it first, and he wrote it down, and he meant to bring it out clean. And the syndicate—" Crane's hand opened and closed on the oilcloth bundle. "They drowned him in the river he was trying to save. Called it an accident. Called it a prospector's greed gone bad. And every paper in the territory printed their version, because every paper in the territory prints what the Company tells it to print."

I couldn't speak. Five years I had carried my father's death like a stone sewn into my chest—drowned, the reports said, lost crossing the high river, a foolish man chasing gold—and I had built my whole erased life on top of that stone, and now an old man bleeding out in a dry wash was telling me the stone was a lie.

"He didn't drown," I said. It wasn't a question either. It was a door opening onto a room I'd been standing outside my whole life.

"He didn't drown," Crane said. "He was murdered. And the proof of it, or the shape of it, is in this map. The route he took. The camp he kept. What he found at the spring. I copied it from his own papers before they burned them—I was the only one they didn't watch close enough—and I've spent twenty-two years keeping it, waiting for the law to grow teeth. It never did. So now I'm giving it to a ranger instead, and praying she's got more spine than the institution that pays her."

He held the bundle out to me.

I didn't take it. Not yet. My hands were shaking and I wouldn't let him see, and besides, a bargain is a bargain, and I wanted its terms exact.

"What do you want for it?"

"Two promises." He held up fingers that trembled. "One: carry it to the summit, or burn it. Those are the only two ends it gets. You don't sell it. You don't trade it. You don't leave it in an evidence locker for some Company lawyer to lose. You carry it up that river and you look at what my eyes looked at, or you burn it where no one can follow. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Two." His voice cracked, and he steadied it with visible effort, jaw working like a man chewing on broken glass. "When it's done—when the truth's out or the map's ash—you find my grave a marker. I don't care what it says. *Here lies a stubborn man.* That'll do fine. But no marker means the birds win, and I've been arguing with them all morning, and I hate to lose."

"I swear that too."

"Then take it." He pushed it into my hands, and the weight of it was nothing—a few ounces of paper and oilcloth—and the weight of it was everything, twenty-two years and a murdered father and every mile I'd walked trying to outrun a name. "It's waterproofed. Oilcloth over tin foil, the way your father taught me. Keep it dry anyway. Deserts drown things too."

I started to ask him what he meant, *your father taught me*, but a cough took him then, the worst one yet, and when it ended he was bleeding freely from the mouth and his skin had gone the color of the sandstone behind him. I held the canteen to his lips and he drank a little and pushed it away.

"Time," he said, almost apologetically. "Days stop meaning much, but hours still count."

"Is there anyone? Anyone I should send word to?"

"A wife, forty years dead. A mule, thirty. You're the whole list, Ranger." He managed the ghost of a grin. "Might write that on the marker too. *He was somebody's whole list.*"

I laughed, or something that started as a laugh and ended somewhere else entirely, and I sat beside him while the shadows lengthened and the vultures waited with their terrible patience overhead. He talked in pieces near the end—about the girl the mule was named for, about a vein of turquoise he'd found in '61 and never told a soul, about the sound the high country makes at dawn, which he said was like the world clearing its throat before saying something important. And then, very quietly, with his eyes fixed on the strip of sky above the wash:

"Tell them Thomas Dunmore was honest. Tell them the water remembers."

And then he wasn't talking anymore, and after a while he wasn't breathing either, and the vultures came down off the rimrock one at a time, unhurried, like creditors at a reading of a will.

* * *

I buried him at dusk where the wash bent north, digging with the shovel off Sorrel's pack until my shoulders burned and my hands blistered, because the ground was hard as sin and twice as unforgiving. I laid him deep and stacked stones over the fill and cut his name into the largest one with my knife: ELIAS CRANE, and beneath it, after some thought, PROSPECTOR. WHOLE LIST OF NOBODY, AND SOMEBODY TO ME. It wasn't elegant. He'd have laughed at it. That was the point.

Then I sat on the stones with the oilcloth bundle in my lap and the stars coming out hard and cold over the mesa, and I untied the waxed cord.

Inside was a map, drawn in ink on treated linen, fine as engraving and dense as scripture. The Silverhead ran down its length like a spine, and every tributary, every rapid, every canyon wall was noted in a small precise hand. And along the upper reaches, where other maps went blank, this one went on—miles of river above the last surveyed point, climbing toward a mark at the top of the sheet: a circle, and within the circle, in letters small and firm, a single word.

SOURCE.

And stamped in faded violet in the lower corner, half-obscured by Crane's later annotations, was the mark I recognized from freight wagons and payroll chits and the letterhead of men who owned the territory: the Consolidated Silverhead Mining Company. The syndicate's own stamp, on the syndicate's own survey, annotated by a dead prospector and carried by a dead man's promise.

My father had died carrying this route. This map, or its twin. And the men who killed him had stamped it like property, because to them the river was property, and the truth about the river was the one claim they'd kill to defend.

I sat there a long time, the map across my knees, the cold coming down off the stars, and I thought about five years of miles. Five years of measuring my life in distance traveled, of walking loops around a desert to avoid walking loops around the truth. The thief who stole my name was still out there somewhere, and I'd chase him tomorrow and the day after, the way I always had.

But tonight, for the first time, the road ahead of me pointed somewhere instead of away.

I rolled the map, tied the cord, and tucked it inside my coat, over my heart, where a stone used to be.

At first light I would ride for the Silverhead. Upstream. All the way to the source.

And God help anyone who stood between me and what my father died trying to carry home.
