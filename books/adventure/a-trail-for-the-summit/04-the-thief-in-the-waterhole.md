## 4. The Thief in the Waterhole

The waterhole at Coyote Wells was a lie the desert told kindly—a brown lens of water no deeper than a man's chest, ringed with alkali-crusted mud, shaded by a single cottonwood that had given up on growing tall and settled for growing crooked. Wren had been lying in its shade for three hours, flat on his back against the roots, hat over his face, and for three hours he had listened to the woman across the water pretend she wasn't watching him.

She was good. Better than good. Beatrix Dunmore—or whatever name she wore this season, and he knew it wasn't Dunmore, not anymore, not since she'd burned her own trail so thoroughly that even the rangers' registry showed a gap like a pulled tooth—she sat her horse thirty yards out with the stillness of something carved. A canteen hung at her saddle horn. She didn't drink. She didn't shift. She let the horse do the small honest things horses do, switch its tail, stamp once, drop its head to nose at the mud, while she herself stayed a statue with eyes.

Wren counted her stillness the way other men counted money.

*Three hours,* he thought. *Most people would have ridden on by now. Most people can't stand their own company that long, let alone mine.*

He lifted the brim of his hat an inch, just enough to see her, and found her already looking at him, and neither of them moved, and the cottonwood dropped a leaf into the waterhole with a sound like a struck match.

"You've been following me," she said. Her voice carried clean across the water without effort or anger, which frightened him more than shouting would have. Shouting meant heat, and heat could be waited out. This was cold worked smooth as river stone.

"Water's public," Wren said.

"I didn't say you were following me here. I said you were following me." She dismounted—not quickly, nothing about her was quick—and led the horse down to drink, keeping the animal between them the way another woman might have kept a table. "Four days. Since Redwash. You ride a gray with a cracked left hind shoe. You camp cold. You never build a fire before full dark."

"You've been following me too, then."

"I don't follow," she said. "I track. There's a difference."

"There is," Wren admitted. He sat up slowly, hands loose and visible, palms out—the old courier's habit, the posture that said *I'm harmless* whether or not it was true. It had been true more often than not, back when he ran paper for the syndicate instead of running from it. "Trackers are looking for something. Followers are looking at someone."

"And which are you?"

He smiled, because smiling was cheap and she hadn't asked the real question yet. The real question was sitting in the oilskin packet inside his coat, wrapped twice and tied with waxed cord, and it weighed almost nothing and everything: four sheets of identity papers bearing a name that had never legally existed, a ranger's seal that had been forged by a man named Odell Quist, and—on the third page, in the lower margin, where a clerk's countersignature should have been blank—a looping, confident signature that read *T. Voss-Hale.*

His signature. Forged onto her papers five years ago by men who wanted a ghost attached to a payroll, a name they could hang expenses on and disown later. They'd chosen Wren because Wren was already a ghost himself, a boy from the Meridian Flats orphan rows who'd learned to ride fast and sign slow and ask nothing. They'd put his forged mark on a dead woman's documents because it was convenient, and then the woman hadn't died, and the documents had ended up in the hands of a desert ranger who'd spent half a decade hunting whoever had made her into nobody.

And now she'd found him. Sitting under a tree. Drinking her waterhole dry.

"I'm waiting," she said.

"For what?"

"For you to tell me why a man shadows a stranger through four days of hard country without robbing her." She crouched at the water's edge, filled her canteen, and did not drink from it. He noticed that. He noticed everything about her, had spent five years noticing her from a distance of ledgers and secondhand reports, and up close she was worse than he'd imagined—because up close she looked tired in a way that had gone past exhaustion into geology, worn down to strata. "You're not a bandit. Bandits don't camp cold. You're not a bounty man; there's no paper on me worth the ink. So either you're mad, or you want something I have."

*Or something I am,* Wren thought. *Or something I took.*

* * *

He had stolen her papers on a Tuesday, in the spring of the fifth year, from a strongbox in a freight office in Cinnabar Gap, and he had done it for the most unforgivable reason of all: because it was easy.

That was how he explained it to himself, anyway, in the first weeks, riding south with the packet against his ribs. Easy was a currency he understood. The freight office clerk was drunk. The strongbox lock was older than Wren was. And the papers themselves—he'd only known what they were because he'd seen the syndicate's requisition orders years before, a line item he'd carried himself once and never forgotten: *retrieval of documents, priority absolute, burn after reading.* He'd been the burn-after-reading man for six years. He knew what the syndicate considered worth killing for, and identity papers belonging to a ranger who'd vanished off every registry were high on that list, though no order ever said why.

It wasn't until the third night out, camped in a dry wash with a fire built small and mean, that he'd actually read them.

He'd expected forgeries to sell back to the syndicate. That was the whole shape of the plan: walk into the Meridian Flats office, lay the packet on Odell Quist's desk, and name a price that would buy him out of the country entirely. Argentina, maybe. Somewhere with rain. He'd rehearsed the negotiation a dozen times, and in none of those rehearsals had he opened the packet and read past the first page.

But he had opened it. Because reading was the one vice they'd never trained out of him, and because the name on the first page stopped his thumb like a burr: *Beatrix Dunmore.* And beneath it, in the space where her history should have been, a clerk's summary in Quist's neat hand: *subject claims original records destroyed; identity reconstructed per attached; verify countersignature.*

And there it was. Third page. Lower margin. *T. Voss-Hale.*

Wren had sat by that mean little fire for a long time, holding pages that proved a woman had been erased, and understanding—with the slow, sick clarity of a man watching his own reflection resolve in dark water—that the erasure had been done with *his* name in the margins. Not his hand. His name. The syndicate had used him as ink. Somewhere in their ledgers, Beatrix Dunmore's reconstructed identity was tied to a courier called Tomas Voss-Hale, and if anyone ever traced the forgery backward, it wouldn't be Quist who burned. It would be the ghost boy from the Flats who couldn't prove he hadn't held the pen.

They'd signed her death warrant with his name, and hers with his, and neither of them had known.

He hadn't sold the papers. That was the part he could never explain to anyone, least of all to her, standing across a brown waterhole with her canteen full and untouched. He'd meant to. He'd ridden toward Meridian Flats for two days with the plan intact, and somewhere on the second day he'd caught himself reading the pages again at every halt, and what he kept coming back to was not the forgery but the *gaps*. The places where a life had been cut out. Father: deceased. Mother: deceased, date uncertain. Occupation prior: none recorded. Next of kin: none.

None. None. None.

He knew what *none* looked like. He'd been *none* since he was nine years old, when the fever took the row of bunks beside his at the Flats home and the matron had entered him in the ledger as *surplus, male, healthy* and sold him to the first man with a wagon. He had spent his whole life being a line item. And here was a woman the syndicate had turned into one too—worse, they'd turned her into a *blank*—and they'd done it carelessly, the way you'd correct a clerical error, and they'd used his name to do it.

So he hadn't sold. He'd run the other way, south into the canyon country, and told himself he was hiding until the heat died down, and then he'd heard—through the courier grapevine, which never fully let go of a man—that the syndicate had a new problem. A prospector named Elias Crane had come down off the Silverhead headwaters with a map, and Quist had put riders on him, and the word passed down the line was the oldest word in the syndicate's vocabulary: *containment.*

And Wren, who had contained things for six years, who knew exactly what containment meant when the thing being contained was a man, had understood two things at once. First: Crane's map was the same map. The Silverhead source map. The one that had gone up the mountain five years ago in the satchel of an expedition leader whose name Wren had signed his own forged mark beneath, once, in a ledger he still saw when he closed his eyes.

Second: the ranger with the erased name was going after that map. Everyone in the syndicate's orbit knew it. She'd been asking questions in Redwash for months, patient questions, ranger questions, and Quist had started keeping a file on *her*, and files in that office were precursors to orders, and orders were precursors to riders.

Which meant that if Wren wanted the map—if he wanted the one document that could pull the whole rotten ledger into daylight and maybe, maybe, take his own forged name down with it—he didn't need to find it.

He needed to find *her*.

* * *

"So," Beatrix said. She had not drunk. The sun had moved the length of the cottonwood's shadow and her horse stood hipshot in the mud, drinking its fill, and she watched Wren with the particular patience of someone who had decided that time was a weapon she owned more of. "You've had long enough to invent a lie. Let's hear it."

"The truth costs more," Wren said.

"I'm not buying anything."

"No," he agreed. "You're arresting. Or trying to decide whether to." He got to his feet slowly, brushing alkali dust from his coat, and saw her weight shift forward onto the balls of her feet, saw her right hand drift toward the gun on her hip without seeming to move at all. Beautiful, terrible economy. He raised both hands again, higher this time, theatrical enough to be insulting, and watched her decide not to be insulted. "I stole something from you."

Her face did not change. But something behind it changed—the way light changes behind frosted glass when a figure passes. "Go on."

"Five years ago. Spring. Papers out of Cinnabar Gap."

The silence that followed had texture. The wind came down the wash and stirred the cottonwood and the waterhole wrinkled and smoothed itself, and Beatrix Dunmore stood absolutely still in the middle of it, and Wren realized he had planned this conversation for months and had imagined every version except the one where saying it out loud felt like handing her a knife handle-first.

"You," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a verdict.

"Me."

Five years. He watched five years move through her—not as tears, nothing so legible, but as a kind of recalculation, the whole architecture of her hunt rearranging itself around the fact that its object had walked up and sat down under a tree. When she spoke again her voice was quieter, which was worse. "Why?"

"Because I was paid to find them and I found them first." It was half true, and he watched her weigh it, and he hated himself for offering her the half. "And then I read them. And then I didn't sell them."

"Why not?"

"Because your name isn't the only forgery in that packet." He reached into his coat, slowly, telegraphing every inch, and drew out the oilskin packet—her packet, the one he'd carried against his ribs through five winters until the waxed cord had worn a groove in the leather there—and held it out across the water between them. "Third page. Lower margin. Read the signature."

She crossed the waterhole in eleven strides. He counted. She took the packet without touching his hand, without taking her eyes off his face, and opened it with fingers that trembled only slightly, only at the very tips, and he understood that the trembling cost her something enormous to permit.

He watched her read. He watched her reach the third page. He watched her stop.

"That's not—" she began, and stopped, because it obviously was. *T. Voss-Hale.* The director's own initial, the syndicate's crown signature, sitting in the margin of her reconstructed life like a spider in a web. "This says you—"

"It says my name. It isn't my hand. Quist forged it—I'd bet my life, and I have been betting my life, for five years, because if that signature is ever traced to its source, the trail doesn't end at Quist. It ends at the courier whose name they used. Me." He let his hands drop. There was no point in the theater anymore; they were past theater now, out on some bare plain where neither of them had maps. "They erased you with my name, ma'am. I've spent five years carrying the proof that we're tied together, and I couldn't give it to you, because giving it to you meant explaining why I took it, and explaining why I took it meant telling you I was the syndicate's man before I was anything else."

"Was." Her voice was flat. "Past tense."

"Past tense," Wren said. "As of the day I read your papers and found myself in the margins of your life. I've been running from them since. And now I'm following you, because I heard Elias Crane came off the Silverhead with a map, and I know what the syndicate does to men who come off the Silverhead with maps." He nodded at the packet in her hands. "You have it. Don't lie to me; I watched you take delivery at the Redwash crossing, and I watched you bury Crane yesterday morning, and I know what a man gut-shot by syndicate riders looks like because I used to be the one pulling triggers adjacent to the men doing it."

Something moved through her then—grief, he thought, raw and freshly reopened, and rage wearing grief's clothes. "You watched me bury him."

"I watched you promise him something. I couldn't hear it. But I've made promises over graves in that country, and I know the shape of them." He swallowed. The next part was the part he'd rehearsed least, because it was the part that was simply, nakedly true. "Those riders will come for that map. Not tomorrow, maybe. But soon, and in numbers, and they will kill you for it and burn it and the river stays poisoned and your father stays a drowned accident and my name stays in the margin of a crime I didn't commit. Unless somebody gets that map somewhere it can hurt them." He met her eyes. Hers were the color of the waterhole, brown and opaque and deep past any depth he could sound. "I know their territory. I know their payrolls, their routes, their captains, their codes. I'm the worst man you'll ever meet and the best guide you'll ever have, and I'm asking you not to shoot me."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she rolled the oilskin closed, tied the cord with quick precise turns, and slid it inside her coat, and he felt the loss of it like a tooth coming out.

"You'll ride behind me," she said. "Twenty yards. You'll make no fire, send no signal, and touch that map never again. And the first hour I believe you're lying, I won't warn you. There won't be a second hour."

"Fair terms," Wren said, and meant it, and was already calculating—which was the truth about him, the truth under all the others, the thing he could feel turning over in his chest like a stone in a current: that even now, surrendering the packet, promising honesty, some cold clerkish corner of his mind was noting that she had accepted him, that she had taken the bait he'd spent five years not knowing he was setting, and that the road upstream ran straight through the syndicate's heart, and that whatever waited at the summit spring would need a thief's hands as much as a ranger's conscience.

He told himself the calculation served them both.

He was almost sure he believed it.

Behind them, far down the western flats, a column of dust rose off the Meridian road—too regular for weather, too fast for freight. Beatrix saw it at the same moment he did. Neither of them said anything. She swung into the saddle, and Wren whistled his gray in from the scrub, and they rode north together into the canyon country, twenty yards apart, toward Split Rock, where the syndicate's riders were already waiting.
