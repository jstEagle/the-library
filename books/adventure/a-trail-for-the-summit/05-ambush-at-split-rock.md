## 5. Ambush at Split Rock

The rock split the sky like a wound that had never learned to close.

Beatrix saw it from two miles out, a black seam in the red mesa country where the earth had cracked open and healed crooked. Split Rock, the old surveys called it—a fin of sandstone standing alone in the flats, tall as a church steeple, cleft down the middle by some ancient violence. Water ran through the gap in wet seasons. In dry ones, like this one, it held only shadow and the smell of old rain.

She should have gone around.

That was the thought that came to her later, in the dark hours when she took the day apart and examined its pieces: *you saw the ambush ground from two miles off and you rode into it anyway*, because the map said the river bent near Split Rock, because the water there was good, because five years of solitary travel had taught her habits instead of caution. Habits were just another way of dying slowly. She'd known that once. She'd forgotten it somewhere on the long road between her old name and her new one.

The morning was cool enough that her horse's breath hung visible, and the desert smelled of sage crushed under hoof. Beatrix rode with the map rolled tight against her spine, sewn into the lining of her coat where Elias Crane's blood had stiffened the canvas. She'd slept badly, dreaming of dry washes and a dying man's hand pressing paper into hers, of a name spoken through ruined lips—*her father's name*—and she woke with grit in her teeth and grief sitting on her chest like a stone she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for part of her body.

At half a mile, she noticed the birds.

Not the vultures, which were always somewhere overhead in this country, patient as debt. The small birds—the finches and the rock wrens that lived in the crevices of Split Rock—they were gone. A canyon full of holes and no birds singing out of them. She reined up. Her horse flicked an ear back at her, reading her stillness.

Wind moved the sage. Nothing else moved.

But the light was wrong. There was a shimmer along the base of the rock where shade should have lain flat and blue-black—a heat-shimmer that wasn't heat, the kind of distortion made by glass, or by the oiled steel of rifle barrels catching sun they hadn't bothered to cover. Someone down there wanted her to think they were hidden. Or someone down there didn't care whether she saw them coming.

Both possibilities meant the same thing.

Beatrix turned her horse, slow, unhurried, the way you turned away from a dog you didn't want to trigger—and that was when the first shot came, from behind her, high on the mesa rim, and she understood that the shimmer at the rock's base was not the trap but the door of it, and she had already walked through.

The bullet cracked past close enough to part the air near her ear. Her horse screamed and bolted and she let it, lying flat along its neck, because a running horse was harder to hit than a thinking woman. Sagebrush whipped her legs. The second shot spanged off stone somewhere to her left, wild, and she heard shouting now—three voices, maybe four, spread along the rim behind her, driving her forward the way beaters drove quail.

Toward Split Rock. Toward the shimmer. Toward the men waiting in the shade of the cleft with their rifles resting on their knees.

They weren't trying to kill her. That realization arrived cold and complete, the way the worst realizations did. If they'd wanted her dead, the first shot would have taken her. They were herding her. They wanted her alive, and they wanted whatever she carried, and Elias Crane had died gut-shot in a dry wash because he'd been carrying it first.

Her father's route. Crane's map. The syndicate stamp pressed into its border like a brand on a steer.

*Someone killed for this,* she'd told herself last night by the fire. Now the someone was shooting at her, and the knowledge felt less like revelation than like arithmetic finally done.

She hauled the horse around the north shoulder of the fin, buying seconds, and the ground fell away ahead of her into a shallow draw choked with boulder and brush. She went down into it at a gallop, ducking branches, and for thirty seconds she believed—she actually believed—that she might thread the draw and come out the far side into the open flats where a single rider could not be cornered.

Then a rope dropped over her from above.

It came off the rocks themselves, thrown from a ledge she'd never have spotted, and the loop settled over her shoulders and cinched against her collarbone before her hands could find it. The pull nearly took her out of the saddle. She got one arm free, grabbed leather, and then the horse stumbled on loose stone and went down hard, and Beatrix went with it, rolling clear by instinct older than thought, coming up on her feet with her revolver already out and the world tilting sideways with pain from her wrenched shoulder.

Three riders came down into the draw at a trot. Syndicate men—she knew them by the gray dust-cloaks and the company irons on their saddle skirts, the double-bar and star that stamped every freight crate and payroll chit from Meridian Flats to the river's mouth. The lead rider was heavyset, face burned to leather, a scattergun across his thighs. He looked at her the way a man looks at a crate he's been sent to collect.

"Ma'am," he said. "You're carrying something that belongs to the company."

"I'm carrying nothing," Beatrix said.

"Crane gave you something." He said it mildly, almost kindly, which was worse. "We know he did. We tracked him three days. You've had it one."

Behind her, up the draw, the rim shooters were closing on foot. Two directions. No third. She calculated distances without wanting to—the arithmetic of a woman who had spent five years measuring everything in miles and escape routes—and the sum came out bad. Scattergun at forty yards. Rifles above. Her revolver against all of it, her shoulder screaming, her horse groaning in the stones behind her with a broken leg she'd have to end later if she lived to do it.

She raised the revolver anyway. It was what she had.

"That's a shame," the heavyset man said, and lifted the scattergun.

What happened next happened fast enough that Beatrix would spend years assembling it into order.

A shot rang out—not from the rim, not from the riders, but from the jumble of boulders at the draw's head, and the heavyset man's scattergun jumped in his hands as the charge tore into the dirt at his horse's forefeet. The animal reared. The second shot came before the first echo died, and it took the hat clean off the nearest rim man climbing down, a warning so precise it was almost polite. Then a voice, pitched to carry, easy and unhurried:

"Four of you, one of me, and I've got the high ground and nothing to lose. You know how that math works. Company taught me."

The syndicate men knew the voice. Beatrix saw it land on them—the heavyset rider's face changed, the mildness dropping away, replaced by something older and uglier. Recognition, and beneath it, fear wearing recognition's clothes.

"You," the heavyset man said. "You're supposed to be *dead*."

"Frequently reported," the voice agreed. "Third time now. It's getting embarrassing for whoever keeps filing the paperwork."

Another shot, closer, deliberate, and the rim men decided. Whatever orders they carried, they weren't worth dying for in a draw with a ghost, and they went up and over the rim at a run. The heavyset man held a moment longer, staring past Beatrix at the boulders, memorizing something, promising something. Then he wheeled his horse and followed, and the draw was empty except for dust settling, a wounded horse breathing its ragged breaths, and a woman with a gun still raised at a man who had just saved her life.

He came down from the rocks with his hands visible and empty, walking slow, and Beatrix saw him clearly for the first time.

Slim. Sun-browned. A face built to be forgotten—unremarkable in every line except the eyes, which were quick and dark and missed nothing, including the fact that her revolver had not wavered off his chest since he'd stepped into view. He wore a courier's long coat gone gray with road dust and a smile that arrived a half-second after everything else, like an afterthought he'd learned to attach.

"Beatrix Dunmore," he said.

Every hair on her arms stood up. Not the ranger name. Not the name on her current papers. Her *real* name—the one that existed nowhere anymore, erased from every ledger and register and roll of rangers, the name she'd spent five years burying under miles.

"No," she said quietly.

"The papers," he said. "Five years ago. A strongbox taken off a supply wagon outside Redwash Station. I know what was in them. I know who they belonged to." He stopped ten feet away, hands still open, and something in his face shifted—the smile gone now, and underneath it something that looked, impossibly, like exhaustion. "I've been following you since the wash. Since Crane."

And Beatrix understood, all at once, with the clarity of a struck bell, why the birds had left the rock, why the shimmer had been too obvious, why the ambush had found her so neatly. He hadn't saved her despite following her. He'd needed her alive, and he'd needed the syndicate to keep believing their ghost story, and both of those things had been worth more to him than whatever he'd risked coming down off those rocks.

She shot him.

Not to kill. She put the round through the meat of his right thigh, low, and he went down with a sound that was more surprise than pain, and she was on him before he finished falling, knee in his chest, revolver at his eye, stripping the pistol from his belt with her free hand while he blinked up at her through the dust.

"Fair," he managed. "That's—I want it noted that's fair."

"Shut up."

She took the rest of it methodically: a knife, a folded set of lockpicks, a canvas wallet that held papers she didn't read yet but recognized by their weight. From his coat's inner pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, came the thing that stopped her hands for a moment—a page torn from a journal, soft with handling, covered in a cramped, slanted handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her father's handwriting.

She didn't read it. Not there, not with him watching, not with her heart hammering loud enough that he could probably hear it. She folded it away inside her coat next to the map, and she bound his hands with the same rope the syndicate had meant for her, and she dragged him to the shade of the biggest boulder while she ended her horse's suffering with a mercy she paid for afterward in the currency she always paid in—silence, and distance, and work.

By the time the sun stood straight overhead, she had his horse, his gear, and him trussed against the rock with a rope harness that would hold a bull. She sat ten feet away with the revolver across her knees and ate jerky she didn't taste.

He watched her with those quick dark eyes and, to his credit, didn't talk. For almost an hour.

"You can't take me anywhere," he said finally. "One horse. Two hundred miles of syndicate territory between here and anywhere. And they'll come back with more men, because the man who ran just now is named Corliss, and Corliss doesn't file reports—he files grudges."

"I don't need to take you anywhere," Beatrix said. "I need to go upstream. You're not in that plan."

"And what happens to me? Tied here?" He tested the ropes without much hope. "Water's four days' ride back east. Vultures get bored faster than that, but not much."

"Yes."

The word landed between them and stayed. He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop smiling entirely, and that was when she learned his face was most honest when it wasn't performing.

"All right," he said. "All right. Here's what I know about Thomas Dunmore."

The name went through her like a bullet of its own. She kept her own face still—five years of practice—but her thumb pressed white against the revolver's grip.

"He didn't drown," the thief said. "Everyone downstream says he drowned in the spring flood, raft wrecked at the narrows, body never found. That's the story the company helped along. Paid for the funeral notice in the Meridian Flats paper themselves. But I was crew on his last run. Four of us went up the Silverhead with him, and four of us came down, and he wasn't in the boat." His voice had gone quiet and level, the voice of a man reciting something he'd rehearsed in the dark for years. "The company's men met us at the narrows. They were waiting. They took him off the river alive, Beatrix. I watched them do it. I ran, because I was twenty-two and scared and on their payroll, and I've been running ever since, and your papers—" he exhaled— "your papers are the only thing that proves I was ever anyone else besides the man who did that."

The wind moved through the draw. Somewhere above, a vulture turned on the thermal, patient as arithmetic.

"Why," she said. Her voice came out sanded flat. "Why did they take him."

"Because of what he found at the source." The thief's eyes didn't leave hers. "And because of what he was bringing back down to prove it. And because the map you're carrying—the one Crane gave you—is drawn from his survey notes. The company has been looking for that map for five years, and now it knows exactly where it is, because Corliss will report that we both have it." He shifted against the ropes, winced at his thigh, and went on anyway. "So here's my offer. Cut me loose. I'll take you up the river through country where every crossing, every waterhole, every toll bridge flies their colors. I was their courier for six years; I know their roads better than their road-builders do. In exchange, you don't leave me for the birds."

"And when we reach the source?"

"When we reach the source," he said, "you'll have the truth about your father. All of it. Even the parts that cost me."

Beatrix looked at him for a long time—at the sweat tracking through the dust on his face, at the blood soaking his trouser leg, at the journal page hidden against her ribs that she would read tonight by firelight and read again until the words wore grooves in her.

She measured the distance to the source in her head. Miles of syndicate country. A river running through all of it like a thread through a spider's parlor.

She cut the ropes on his hands and left the ones on his ankles, and handed him her canteen.

"Walk," she said. "You limp behind my horse, and if you fall behind, I'll assume the desert took you."

He almost smiled. "There she is," he said softly, mostly to himself. "Thomas Dunmore's daughter."

She pretended not to hear. But that night, camped in the rocks above the draw with the thief's wounded leg propped on a saddle roll and his name—Wren, he offered it, just Wren, like a bird that had flown out of someone else's story—sleeping fitfully across the fire, Beatrix unwrapped the oilcloth and read her father's handwriting by the coals' last light.

Only one line, torn from a longer page, the edges browned and soft:

*If anything happens to me, the proof is at the source. Don't let them make the river lie.*

She folded it away. Upstream, the darkness where the mountains stood was absolute, and somewhere in it, the water began.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and cold. Wren was awake, watching her over the dead fire, and the first thing he said was not about his leg or the ropes or the road.

"They'll have doubled the watch at the First Crossing by now," he said. "Corliss won't wait for orders. Which means we've got maybe three days before every ford on the Silverhead has a rifle behind it."

Beatrix saddled the horse, and looked north toward the mountains, and started measuring the miles.
