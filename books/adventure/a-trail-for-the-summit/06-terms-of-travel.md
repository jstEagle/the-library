## 6. Terms of Travel

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time they started talking terms.

Beatrix sat with her back against Split Rock, the map's oilskin wrapping pressed flat against her ribs where she could feel it with every breath. Wren sat opposite her, wrists still bound, watching her the way a coyote watches a snare—not afraid, exactly, but measuring the geometry of it. The rope, the slack, the knot. Everything about him was measurement.

"You could untie me," he said. "I'd make better conversation."

"You'd make better bait," she said. "Vultures have to eat too."

He almost smiled. She saw it happen and die in the same second, a flicker at the corner of his mouth like heat lightning over the mesa. "You're not going to leave me for the birds."

"No," she agreed. "Because you know something. And men who know things don't get left for the birds until they've said them."

"That's the coldest arithmetic I've heard in years." He shifted, settling his bound hands in his lap like a man making himself comfortable on a porch. "All right, ranger. Ask."

She asked. She asked for two hours while the stars wheeled and the desert gave up its day-heat stone by stone, and what she got was a man who answered everything and told her nothing—answers polished smooth as river stones, all surface, no purchase. He'd been a courier. He'd carried things. He'd stopped carrying things. He knew the syndicate's roads because he'd worn grooves into them with his own wheels and boots. When she pushed toward her father—the expedition, the river, the shooting—he went quiet in a way that wasn't refusal but something worse: a silence with weight behind it, like water behind a dam.

"Here's my offer," she said finally, when the fire had gone to ash and the cold had come up out of the ground to take its place. "You lead. I keep the map. You get me through syndicate country without syndicate eyes finding us, and when we reach the source, you tell me what happened to my father. All of it. Every name."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I ride out at dawn and you explain to the vultures why you're worth skipping."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. In the starlight his face was all planes and shadow, younger than his voice, older than his eyes. "You drive a hard bargain for someone who doesn't know what she's carrying."

"I know exactly what I'm carrying."

"Do you?" Something moved behind his face then—something that looked, for one unguarded instant, like pity. "Your father thought so too."

* * *

They broke camp before first light, moving north along the dry wash with the horses stepping careful as dancers over the slickrock. Beatrix rode point out of habit, then remembered the terms and dropped back, letting Wren set the line. It went against every grain of her training. Five years of traveling alone had built her instincts into a wall, and now she was supposed to walk through a door in it because a thief promised to be useful.

He led well. That was the worst part. He read the country the way she did—reading wind in the grass, reading cattle-sign that wasn't cattle, reading the particular quality of silence that meant riders somewhere within ten miles. Twice he turned them off the trail without explanation, cutting cross-country through a maze of sandstone fins, and twice, from the high ground after, she saw what he'd seen: dust plumes hanging in the distance like questions nobody wanted answered.

"Syndicate patrols run the river road every third day," he said, reining in beside her on the ridge. "Heavy since spring. They're looking for something."

"They're looking for this." Her hand went to her ribs without permission.

"They're always looking for something. This time it happens to be yours." He studied the dust plumes with an expression she couldn't read. "There's a route through the Needles. Old stock trail, pre-company. It adds two days but skips Meridian Flats entirely."

"We need resupply before the high country."

"Meridian Flats is a company town. Every bed, every barrel, every ear in it belongs to Voss-Hale. You walk in there, you might as well walk into his parlor wearing your father's name."

"My father's name is mine to wear."

"Is it?" He said it lightly, but the question had hooks in it, and she felt them set. "Funny thing about names. I've worn four. None of them fit."

That night, camped in the lee of a fin with the horses hobbled close, she dreamed of her father for the first time in months. Not the memory she kept polished—the last good morning, coffee on the stove, him teaching her to read contour lines—but the other one, the one she kept locked: the telegram. *Lost with party of five on the Silverhead. Remains unrecovered.* She woke with her hand fisted in her bedroll and found Wren awake across the coals, watching her.

"Bad dream?"

"None of your business."

"It's about the river." He said it flatly, not guessing but knowing, and something in his certainty made her skin crawl. "It's always about the river, with people like us."

"People like us," she repeated. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know you measure your life in miles." He poked the coals; sparks rose and died. "I watched you do it for three days before Split Rock. You count everything. Water holes. Miles. Days since the last town. It's how people travel when they're running from a number they can't count."

She didn't answer. But she lay awake long after, listening to the horses shift, thinking about the papers in Wren's possession—her papers, her erased life—and wondering what a man learned about a person from reading their records. Wondering what she'd learn about him from reading his.

* * *

His name hadn't been Wren, once.

He'd been nine years old when the company came through his mother's valley with survey chains and clipboards, and eleven when the payroll clerk first offered him a silver dollar to carry a sealed envelope to the next station downriver. Twelve miles, there and back, and a dollar that jingled like music. His mother never knew. By fifteen he was riding scheduled runs through three counties, and by seventeen he understood what he'd become: not a courier but a blood vessel in a body whose heart he'd never seen. Envelopes in, envelopes out. Some held money. Some held orders. One, when he was nineteen, held a list of names, and three weeks later two of those names were dead in a wash outside Copper Bend, and the payroll clerk smiled at him across the desk and said, *Good work, son,* and Wren understood that the smile was the whole of his employment: to carry things and never open them, to serve and never see.

He ran at twenty-two. Took a run to Meridian Flats and simply didn't come back, walked out of the company stable with nothing but the clothes he wore and a forged bill of sale for a horse, and learned in the years after that the syndicate forgave many sins but not departure. They hunted him the way weather hunts—a season of pursuit, then years of patience, posters aging on walls, his old face yellowing under staples. So he made new faces. New names. He became very good at paper, at ink, at the small forgeries that let a man exist in the cracks between registries. And that was how, four years ago, working a land-office job in a county far from the river, he'd come across a file that stopped his breath.

A ranger's certification. A woman's photograph, sharp-eyed, sunburned. And beneath the signature block, a countersignature he recognized with the sick lurch of a man recognizing his own handwriting—which it nearly was, because the man who'd signed it had taught Wren everything he knew about forging, back when Wren was a boy learning letters in the company office, and the signature itself was a ghost: the hand of a paymaster who'd been dead six years when those papers were supposedly witnessed.

Someone had built this woman's identity out of syndicate scraps. Someone had erased her so thoroughly that the only proof she'd ever been anyone else now existed in a file the syndicate owned.

He hadn't stolen the papers to sell them. He'd stolen them because he knew what lived in files like that, and he knew that someday the syndicate would open the drawer, and read, and understand what a lever they held. He'd meant to burn them. He'd meant to, for four years. The packet rode in his saddlebag through three territories like a coal he couldn't put down.

And then Elias Crane had come off the mountain with a map, and the syndicate had started killing for it, and Wren had understood that the river was calling in everyone who owed it anything.

* * *

On the fourth day they crossed the old wagon road, and Beatrix saw the stamp.

It was burned into a cottonwood at the crossing, deep and deliberate: a circle with a bar through it, the mark of the Silverhead Consolidated. Below it, smaller, someone had cut a date. Eleven years old. The tree had grown around the scar, swelling over it like flesh over a bullet.

"They mark their boundaries," Wren said, following her gaze. "Water, timber, grazing. Everything inside the circle is theirs on paper. Everything outside is theirs in practice."

"My father's last letter mentioned this crossing." She said it before she could stop herself, and immediately regretted it, because Wren turned in the saddle with the attention of a man who has just seen ore in quartz.

"Did it."

"He wrote that he'd provisioned here. That the company men were friendly." She kept her eyes on the scarred tree. "That was eight weeks before the telegram."

"Company men are friendly right up until they aren't." Wren nudged his horse forward. "Keep that in mind in Meridian Flats."

"We're skipping Meridian Flats. You said—"

"I said we should. I've been thinking." He didn't look back at her. "We can't skip it. There's no grain between here and the high country that isn't company grain, and we can't buy company grain without walking into the company store. So we go in clean, we go in separate, and we go in knowing that half the town will report our arrival before our horses finish drinking."

"That's madness."

"That's arithmetic." He glanced back, and for a moment the mask slipped and she saw the calculation running underneath, fast and cold. "Unless you'd rather explain to the high country why you starved at the treeline."

* * *

They made cold camp that night in a box canyon walled so high the stars were a handful, not a sky. Beatrix took first watch, the map against her ribs, a rifle across her knees, and let herself think about her father in this same country eleven years ago.

She'd been fourteen when he left on the Silverhead expedition. She remembered the packing more than anything: the way he weighed every item in his hand before it went into the panniers, as if testing each thing's honesty. Her mother had sewn a spare shirt and slipped a letter into the lining—she'd confessed this years later, laughing, though her eyes hadn't laughed—and her father had ridden out on a bay mule named Cicero with five men and a contract from the territorial survey office, and the last thing he'd said to her was *count the days wrong and the time goes faster.*

She'd counted the days right anyway. Eighty-three of them. Then the telegram.

Now she sat in a canyon full of dark, guarding a map her father might have touched, guarded by—or guarding—a man who had been on the crew, who had run when the shooting started, who knew something he was rationing out like a miser rations coin. She thought about pressing him. She thought about the rifle across her knees and how easy it would be to change the terms.

But terms were all she had. Terms were the only structure left in a life she'd dismantled board by board, and if she broke them now—if she took instead of traded—she'd be no different from the syndicate, which also took what it wanted and called it commerce.

Wren stirred in his bedroll. "Your watch," he said, or started to say, and then, half-asleep, mumbled something else. A word. A name.

*Cicero.*

Beatrix went very still. The bay mule. A name that had never appeared in any newspaper account, any telegram, any public record—only in family letters, only in the private geography of her childhood.

"Wren." Her voice came out low and level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "Where did you hear that name?"

He was awake instantly, fully, the sleep gone from him like water from a cracked cup. For a long moment neither of them moved, and the canyon held its breath around them, and somewhere above the rim a night bird called once and got no answer.

"Terms are terms," Wren said quietly. "But some debts come due early. Ask me again at the source, ranger. I'll tell you everything—about the mule, about the crew, about the last morning. I'll tell you what your father made me promise." He settled back, eyes open, fixed on the handful of stars. "Just get us both there alive. Because I'm not the only one who knows you're coming—and the man waiting at the top of that river has been expecting someone with your father's map for eleven years."

Far to the north, past the canyon rim, thunder rolled over the high country—dry lightning, no rain, the desert counting something of its own.
