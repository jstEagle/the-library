## 7. Downstream Town

They smelled Meridian Flats before they saw it.

It came on the wind an hour out—a tannery reek and coal smoke, the sour breath of a town that had stopped pretending it was anything but a company's mouth. Beatrix reined up on a low rise where the trail bent toward the river, and there it lay below: a scatter of tin roofs and boardwalks strung along the Silverhead like beads on a wire, the water running brown-gray with silt and something else, something that caught the afternoon light with a faint metallic sheen, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"That's not right," she said.

Wren came up beside her, leading both horses now, his shoulder still stiff from the ambush at Split Rock. He looked where she looked. "Tailings," he said, and the word dropped out of him flat and heavy, a stone into still water.

"From where?"

"Upstream." He didn't look at her when he said it. He'd been not looking at her for three days, ever since the terms of their truce had hardened into something like habit. "The Silverhead runs through the syndicate's high claims before it ever gets here. Whatever they're doing up there, this is what it costs the people downstream."

Beatrix studied the water a long moment. A dead fish turned slowly in an eddy below the crossing, its belly pale as a peeled egg. She had grown up on rivers that ran the color of glass and cold. This one ran the color of money.

"Meridian Flats," she said. "You've been here before."

"Many times." Now he looked at her, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not the easy, slippery charm he wore like a second shirt, but a kind of tired dread. "Which is exactly why you should listen to me before we ride down there. This isn't a town, ranger. It's a company store with a church. Everything in it belongs to the Silverhead Consolidated—the buildings, the wells, the doctor, the law. The people don't even belong to themselves. They belong to their debts." He shifted in the saddle. "We go in, we buy what we need, we keep our mouths shut, and we're gone before dark. You don't ask questions. You don't look at anyone longer than it takes to pay them. And you especially don't—"

He stopped.

"Don't what?"

"Don't say your father's name out loud." His voice had gone quiet, the way men go quiet around graves. "Not here. Not anywhere within a hundred miles of this river. Names carry, in company towns. They carry uphill."

* * *

Meridian Flats received them the way a trap receives a foot—gently, and all at once.

The main street ran parallel to the river, wide and rutted, lined with false-fronted buildings painted the same dusty white, as if the syndicate had ordered its paint by the barrel and the town had no choice but to wear its uniform. Every structure flew the same small pennant from its eave: a silver arrowhead on a field of black, the mark of Silverhead Consolidated. It hung over the assay office, the mercantile, the livery, the schoolhouse. It hung over the church, which struck Beatrix as either blasphemy or honesty, she couldn't decide which.

The people moved through the streets with a particular quality she recognized from her years as a ranger in company country—heads down, eyes quick, conversations that stopped a half-beat too soon when a stranger passed. Nobody stared at her and Wren. That was the first wrong thing. In a real town, strangers got looked at. Here they got *weighed*.

"Mercantile first," Wren murmured, dismounting in front of a long building whose porch sagged under the weight of crates stamped with the arrowhead. "Flour, salt, coffee, lamp oil. Then the farrier, then out. I'll talk. You keep your face like it is now."

"Like what?"

"Like you're counting exits."

"I am counting exits."

"Good. Keep it."

The mercantile was dim and smelled of canvas and cured ham. The man behind the counter had the gray, papery look of someone who had been standing in the same spot for thirty years, and he rang up their purchases without once meeting Wren's eye. But Beatrix noticed that when she stepped to the window while Wren haggled over lamp oil, the man's gaze followed her—quick, furtive, and afraid. Not afraid of her. Afraid *for* her, or afraid *of* being seen being interested in her. It was hard to tell the difference in a town like this, and she suspected the difference had been deliberately erased.

Outside, she saw the poster.

It was pasted to the side of the assay office, one of a column of them, sun-bleached and curling at the edges. Most were old—cattle thieves, a claim jumper, a man who'd shot his partner over a mule. But the bottom one was fresh. The paste still shone at the corners. The ink was sharp and black.

WANTED FOR THEFT AND FRAUD AGAINST THE SILVERHEAD CONSOLIDATED

And beneath the words, a face she had been sleeping ten feet away from for a week.

The likeness was good. Too good. It caught the thing Wren's face did when he thought no one was watching—the intelligence in it, the watchfulness, the humor worn thin as old gilt. The hair was longer in the picture, the jaw cleaner-shaven, but it was him. There was no mistaking him. Below the face, in letters sized to be read from a moving horse: *WREN. LAST SEEN IN THE HIGH COUNTRY. $500 ALIVE. $200 OTHERWISE. REPORT TO ANY CONSOLIDATED OFFICE.*

Five hundred dollars. Beatrix did the arithmetic without meaning to. Five hundred dollars was a year of a working man's wages. It was a herd of cattle. It was, in a town where everyone belonged to their debts, the price of a family's freedom—and it was nailed to a wall twelve feet from where Wren was currently haggling over lamp oil with his hat pushed back off his forehead, his face in full view of the street.

She crossed the porch in four strides, took his elbow—not gently—and turned him toward the door with his back to the window.

"Inside," she said, low. "Now."

He read her the way he read most things, fast and without asking. His eyes flicked to the window glass, caught the reflection of the assay office wall, and something moved through his face—recognition, then a strange, bleak amusement, then nothing at all, the shutters coming down.

"Five hundred," he said as she steered him into the dim interior. "Alive. That's almost flattering. Two years ago it was three hundred."

"You knew there was a price on you."

"I knew there'd *be* one. I didn't know they'd gotten around to the printing." He picked up a bolt of canvas, examined it, set it down, all of it theater for the papery man at the counter whose ears had grown visibly longer. "It changes nothing."

"It changes everything. Every man in this town is worth five hundred dollars to himself and knows it. We should ride out now, tonight, and never—"

"And starve in the high country because we've got flour for six days and a river to cross that doesn't care about our arithmetic." He leaned close, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "We need what we came for, ranger, and we need it without looking like we're in a hurry. Hurry is a scent. Dogs follow it. So do men."

She hated that he was right. She hated it more because she could see the shape of the trap he was describing and could not find a way around it. She bought the canvas. She paid in small, worn coin, the kind that left no story in a teller's memory, and she kept her face like it was now.

It was on the way out that she passed the barmaid.

The girl was delivering a crate of bottles to the mercantile from the saloon across the street—young, maybe twenty, with river-colored eyes and the kind of careful blankness the town seemed to issue along with its paint. She set the crate down on the porch, and as Beatrix stepped past her, the girl's hand brushed hers, deliberate as a pickpocket's, and something small and folded pressed into her palm.

Beatrix didn't flinch. Didn't look. She walked three more steps, unhitched her horse, and mounted, and only then, with her back to the street and her hands in the act of gathering the reins, did she open her palm beneath her sleeve.

A scrap of paper, torn from a ledger. A pencil line, hurried and small:

*Saloon. Ask for the back room. Come alone. — R*

She looked up. The girl was already crossing the street, the empty crate on her hip, not once glancing back.

* * *

The saloon was called the Silverhead Rest, which was the first joke the town had made, and possibly the only one.

Beatrix went in alone at dusk, as the light was failing and the lamps were being lit. She'd left Wren at the livery with the horses and a warning that if he followed her she would hog-tie him to his own saddle, and he'd smiled that thin smile and said, "I'd only slow you down anyway. I have my own appointments with this town's ghosts," which had not made her feel better at all.

Inside, the Rest was all smoke and lamplight and the clack of a faro table. The bartender had the syndicate's arrowhead tattooed on the back of his hand, and Beatrix noted it the way she noted everything now—automatically, coldly, the way her father had taught her to read country. *Everything tells you something, Bee. Even the things that are lying.*

The barmaid—river-eyed, careful-faced—was working the room. When Beatrix took a stool at the far end of the bar, the girl brought her a beer she hadn't ordered and said, "On the house. You look like you've come a long way."

"I have."

"Downriver or up?"

"Up."

The girl's hands went still on the bar for just a moment. Then she wiped down the wood in front of Beatrix, slow, methodical, her eyes never lifting from her work. "My name's Ruth," she said, so quietly it was almost lost under the faro table's noise. "I knew your father. Don't react. Just listen."

Beatrix kept her face still. It cost her something physical, like holding a door shut against wind. "I don't know what you mean."

"Eleven years ago a survey crew came through here, headed upriver. Eleven men and a cook and a man with a compass he wouldn't let anyone else touch. He sat where you're sitting. He bought my mother a drink and told her the river was the most honest thing in this valley, and that he intended to keep it that way." Ruth's voice stayed flat, professional, the voice of a woman reciting a stock order. "I was nine. I carried his plate to the back table. He asked me if I could keep a secret, and I said yes, and he said good, because the river was going to need keepers."

The room seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a deck shifts underfoot. Beatrix looked down at her beer. "What was his name?"

"You know what his name was." For the first time Ruth looked up, and her river-colored eyes were wet and furious at once. "I've spent eleven years watching this street. Watching the water go gray. Watching the fish float belly-up past the church. Watching men cough blood in the winter and get told it's the dust. And I've kept his secret the way he asked, because I was nine and he was kind to me, and because I didn't understand until too late what kind of secret it was." She set down the rag. "You have his compass hand. I saw it when you took the glass. The way you hold your wrist."

Beatrix's hand, she realized, had gone to her belt, to the worn brass housing of the compass that had been her father's before it was hers, that she carried the way other people carried saints' medals. She hadn't even known she'd done it.

"He didn't drown."

The words came out of Ruth like something that had been held behind her teeth for eleven years, and they landed in Beatrix like a bullet lands—no pain at first, just the impact, the terrible displacement of everything solid.

The room noise receded. The faro table, the lamps, the smoke—all of it went far away and small, and there was only the bar, and the girl's wet furious eyes, and the five words that unmade a decade.

"They told us he drowned," Beatrix said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; somewhere inside her, something was screaming. "The company sent a letter. Survey accident. The river took him at the high narrows. They sent his effects in a box."

"They sent a box." Ruth nodded, slow. "I helped carry it. I was tall for nine." Her jaw tightened. "The river didn't take him, miss. I've lived on this river my whole life. I know its moods like I know my own hands, and I know what it does and doesn't give back. And I know what the company men said in this room when they thought the help wasn't listening, because the help is never not listening, that's the first thing you learn here." She leaned in, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, and her hand found Beatrix's wrist above the compass and gripped it, hard, the grip of someone passing a live coal. "They said the old man found something at the source he wasn't supposed to find. They said he was going to carry it downstream to the territorial office. And they said—" her breath caught, held, released— "they said Voss-Hale's riders caught him at the narrows, and it was quick, and the river was kind enough to do the rest. Those words. *The river was kind enough to do the rest.* I've heard them in my head every night for eleven years."

The lamp above the bar hissed. Somewhere a man laughed at the faro table, and the sound was obscene.

"Eleven years," Beatrix said. Not a question. A reckoning. The number turned over in her like a stone in a current. Eleven years her mother had believed the letter. Eleven years the river had been called a killer when it was a witness. Eleven years, and the map in her saddlebag had been carried by a man who died for it, and the man sleeping ten feet away in the livery had been on that crew, had been on that crew and run, and had said *ask me again at the source.*

"Who else knows?" she asked. "Here, in this town. Who else remembers?"

Ruth let go of her wrist and straightened, and the barmaid's blankness came down over her face again like a shutter, professional and complete. "The old ones remember. The old ones always remember. But the old ones have debts." She picked up her tray of empty glasses. "There's a woman two streets over, Mrs. Calloway, the seamstress. Your father mended a boot strap of hers with wire, the night before they went up. She kept the wire. She's kept everything—letters, the company's water ledgers, the doctor's lists of the dead and what they died of. She's been waiting eleven years for someone to come down that river asking questions." Ruth paused at the edge of the lamplight. "She's old now. And the company watches her house. So if you go to her—and you should go to her—go at first light, when the night men are changing shift, and don't use the front door."

"Why are you telling me this?" Beatrix asked. "You don't know me. You're risking—"

"I told you why." Ruth's voice cracked, just once, and she steadied it. "The river was going to need keepers. He said that to a nine-year-old girl, and she never forgot it, and nobody ever came." She lifted her chin toward the door, toward the darkening street, toward the north where the river ran gray toward the mountains. "Until now. You have his wrist. So finish what he started, and get it downstream, and don't you dare drown."

She was gone into the smoke and the noise, and Beatrix sat alone at the end of the bar with a beer she hadn't ordered and a dead man's compass under her hand, and outside, far up the darkening canyon, thunder rolled again over the high country—the same dry, patient thunder, counting.

She was still sitting there when the saloon door opened behind her and the room's noise changed pitch, the way it does when the wrong kind of men walk in. She didn't turn. She didn't need to. She saw them in the back-bar mirror: three riders in the dust of the road, wearing the silver arrowhead on their collars, and between them, held by the arm, hatless, his face carefully, deliberately blank—

Wren.

One of the riders peeled off toward the bar, boots heavy on the boards, and stopped an arm's length from Beatrix's stool. He dropped a folded paper on the wood in front of her. The assay office poster, fresh off the wall, paste still wet at the corners.

"Ma'am," he said, pleasant as a man offering directions. "You were seen talking to this one at the livery. Care to tell me what business a woman has with a five-hundred-dollar thief?"

And in the mirror, over the rider's shoulder, Wren's eyes found hers—and he winked.
