## 8. The First Crossing

The river told them before they saw it.

Beatrix heard it first as a change in the canyon's voice—a low, grinding undertone beneath the usual whisper of wind through the junipers, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. By midmorning the sound had become a roar that lived in her back teeth, and when they rounded the last bend above the narrows, she reined in and sat looking at what the spring melt had made of the Silverhead.

It was not a river anymore. It was a brown animal, muscled and furious, filling the canyon wall to wall. Whole cottonwoods went by standing up, their roots trailing like drowned hair. The water came through the narrows in a single compressed channel no wider than forty feet, and it moved with the kind of speed that did not forgive mistakes—she could see boulders the size of wagons being shouldered aside, submerged, swallowed whole.

"We're not crossing that," she said.

Wren swung down from his horse and stood at the edge of the gravel bar, hat pushed back, studying the water the way he studied everything—with a courier's flat assessment, weighing risk against necessity. "We're crossing that," he said. "There's no other way upstream for thirty miles, and thirty miles means going around through Voss-Hale's grazing leases. We'd be riding past their line camps in broad daylight." He pointed to a place where the water split around a midstream shelf of rock, white water braiding on either side of it. "There's an eddy behind that shelf. If we can reach it, we can rest, then take the second channel. It's shallower."

"*If* we can reach it."

"*If*," he agreed. "That's why they call it a crossing and not a road."

She hated him a little for the joke, and hated more that some tired corner of her had almost smiled at it. Five days of travel had done something she hadn't authorized: worn grooves of habit between them. He knew how she took her coffee—scalded, bitter, no sugar. She knew he talked in his sleep, low and urgent, words she couldn't make out but whose tone she recognized from funerals. They were becoming legible to each other, and she resented every page of it.

The horses were the problem. A man could swim the first channel if the cold didn't stop his heart—the snowmelt would try—but a packhorse in floodwater was a drowning animal with opinions. Beatrix stripped the packs and built them into two bundles wrapped in oilcloth, lashed tight, buoyant enough to float if they floated at all. Wren did the same without being asked, working beside her in a silence that had stopped feeling hostile somewhere around day three and now felt like something else she didn't have a name for.

"Horses go loose," he said finally. "We drive them in from downstream, let the current carry them along the shelf—they'll make for the eddy on instinct, animals always do. Then us."

"And if they don't."

"Then we walk out of this canyon on our own feet, and I'll carry your share of the coffee." He glanced at her, and there it was again, that flicker under the flatness—not quite humor, not quite apology. Something that kept insisting on existing despite everything she'd done to starve it.

They drove the horses in.

It went wrong almost immediately. The bay mare hit the current and the river took her like a debt collector, rolling her under, and for three full seconds Beatrix watched only the surface—brown water, white lace, nothing—and understood with total clarity that she was watching something die. Then the mare's head broke water twenty yards down, eyes white-rimmed, and the current slammed her sideways toward the shelf and the slack water behind it, and instinct did what Wren promised: she fought for the calm. The dun followed her in, then the gray, all three of them piling into the eddy in a scrum of blowing breath and scrambling hooves, alive, furious, alive.

"First channel's honest," Wren called over the roar. "It does what you think it'll do. It's the second one that lies."

He went in first.

Later, Beatrix would replay it frame by frame the way she replayed everything—the discipline of a woman who'd taught herself that memory was evidence. He entered the water on the upstream side of the shelf, rope coiled over one shoulder, swimming hard and angled, and for ten yards it looked almost easy, almost controlled. Then the second channel took him.

It didn't look like drowning at first. That was the terrible thing about it. It looked like swimming—arms reaching, head up—and then it looked like swimming badly, and then it didn't look like anything at all except a dark shape flickering under brown water, gone, gone, surfacing for half a breath with his mouth open in a sound the river ate whole, gone again. His hat spun away downstream, absurdly serene, a little island riding the flood.

Beatrix was already moving.

She would think about that too, afterward—that there had been no decision in it, no weighing of judgment against judgment, none of the arithmetic she'd have sworn defined her. Her boots were off, the rope from her saddle in her fist, and she was wading into the first channel before any part of her mind could file an objection. The cold hit her like a blow to the sternum. It wasn't temperature; it was *removal*, the water pulling the heat out of her body faster than her blood could argue, and the current hit her thighs and then her waist and then her chest and each time it doubled its opinion of her.

The rope paid out behind her. She'd knotted it to the saddle horn—the dun was planted in the eddy, ears back, holding against the strain better than any man could have—and she swam the angle Wren had tried, harder, steeper, letting the river carry her down-channel toward where she'd seen him go under.

The water found her under the surface and turned her. Up became a rumor. She felt the whole weight of the Silverhead press her down against the rocky floor of the narrows, felt her bundle of supplies tear free of its lashings, and for one long suspended moment she hung in the brown roaring dark like a thing already surrendered. Then her hand closed on cloth. On an arm. On a wrist, thin and corded and *alive*, jerking once in blind animal panic.

She got her legs under herself against a submerged ledge and pushed.

They surfaced together. His weight nearly took her back down—drowning people climb, everyone knows it, they will climb over you and under you and through you to get one inch higher—and she took a forearm across her cheekbone that rang her skull like a bell. But the rope was there, and the dun was there, and the eddy's slack water was there, and somehow, in pieces, in increments, with the river fighting them the entire way, the three of them—woman, thief, horse—ended up on the gravel bar below the shelf, coughing, shaking, sprawled like wreckage the flood had decided not to keep.

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. There wasn't breath to spare.

Wren lay on his side and vomited river water, great heaving convulsions of it, and then lay still with his cheek against the stones, breathing in shallow sips. Beatrix sat with her back against the shelf rock, arms locked around her knees, shivering so hard her vision stuttered. The sun was warm. It meant nothing. The cold had gotten into the architecture of her and would live there for hours.

"You," Wren rasped finally, "are the worst rescue in the history of rescues. You nearly killed us both."

"I know." Her teeth chattered through it. "I did the arithmetic anyway."

"That's not arithmetic. That's—" He lifted his head, and whatever he saw in her face stopped him. He put his cheek back down on the stones. "Thank you," he said, very quietly, to the gravel.

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended. "Don't thank me. I want to be clear about what happened here. Every part of me said leave you. You're a thief. You've lied to me since Split Rock, you're lying to me still—I can smell it on you like smoke—and the smart move, the move I've trained five years to make, was to let the river settle accounts." She pulled the wet hair off her face with a hand that wouldn't obey her. "I didn't let it. And I don't know why. And I hate not knowing why."

He was quiet for a while. The river roared its one long sentence over and over.

"I know why," he said.

"Enlighten me."

"Because you're your father's daughter." He said it to the ground, and then, when she didn't answer, he rolled onto his back and looked up at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and stripped of every layer of courier's flatness she'd ever seen him wear. "I keep waiting for you to ask me the right question, Beatrix. You ask good ones—where's the syndicate's patrol route, how deep is the ford at Meridian Flats, all the questions a ranger asks. But you've never asked me the one that matters."

Her heart was doing something strange. Not the cold. Something else. "Ask it yourself."

He sat up. It cost him visibly—he shook, he winced, water still ran out of him in small ways—but he sat up and faced her across four feet of gravel bar, and Beatrix Dunmore, who had spent five years making herself unreadable, felt read anyway.

"My name isn't Wren," he said. "Or—it is now. It's been mine longer than my real name ever got to be. My real name is Tomas Reyes, and six years ago I was a courier for the Silverhead Consolidated Mining Company, and the last run I ever carried was a supply drop to an expedition camp in the high country." He swallowed. "Your father's expedition."

The river filled the silence. Beatrix discovered she had stopped shivering, which frightened her more than the shivering had.

"He hired local crew," Wren said. "Guides, a cook, couriers. Me. I carried mail and flour and dynamite up the trail for three weeks. He was—" A breath. "You want to hear what he was like?"

"No," Beatrix said.

"He was the only man in that company who learned everyone's name. The mule skinner's kids' names. Mine. He'd sit up past midnight writing in a journal by candlelight, and when I asked him once what he was hunting up there that was worth the paper, he laughed and said, 'Nothing, son. I'm hoping we find nothing at all.'" Wren's voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "I didn't understand that until later. Nobody understood it until later."

"What happened." Not a question. A door held open, barely.

"The company sent riders up the week after he radioed down his findings. Four men. I knew two of them—they'd ridden escort for me on payroll runs. They came into camp at dawn and they shot Halloway the cook first, in his bedroll, and then they went for your father's tent, and your father—" Wren's hands had curled into fists on his knees. "Your father came out of that tent with his journal held over his head like it was the only thing in the world, shouting that they could take everything else, just not that, just not the book. And they took it. And then they—" He stopped. Started again. "I ran. I was nineteen years old and I ran. I was up the talus slope before the second shot, and I didn't look back, and I have looked back every single day for six years."

Beatrix found that she was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. The gravel tilted under her boots and the world had gone narrow and bright-edged, and the grief that she had buried in five years of miles came up out of the ground like water out of a cut bank.

"They shot him," she said. "You watched them shoot him."

"I watched them shoot him, and I ran, and then I spent six years stealing everything the company owned because I couldn't undo the running. Their ledgers. Their payrolls. Their maps. I told myself it was penance. Mostly it was just running in a different direction." He looked up at her, and he did not flinch, and she realized that was the bravest thing she'd seen anyone do in five years—to hold her gaze while handing her this. "Your papers weren't a job, Beatrix. They were the first thing I ever stole that I kept because I couldn't stand what they were. A whole life erased. A woman who used to exist, filed away and burned. I know exactly what that costs, because the company did it to me too. Tomas Reyes died in that camp. Wren is what crawled out of the canyon instead."

The anger in her was enormous and it had nowhere to go. That was the unbearable discovery—not that he'd been there, not even that he'd run, but that the fury she'd carried like a canteen for five years suddenly had too many mouths to feed. The syndicate. The riders. The boy on the talus slope. Herself, for every year she'd spent tracking a thief when the men who actually pulled the triggers had never once been chased.

"Why tell me now?" Her voice came out low and scraped. "Why not at Split Rock? Why not the first night?"

"Because at Split Rock you'd have left me tied for the vultures, and you'd have been right to." He managed something that wasn't quite a smile. "And because I nearly died in that river, and I found out I'm a coward in exactly one direction. I can't die with you not knowing. I can carry the bullet or the rope or your hatred, whichever comes. But not that."

Above them, the dun whickered softly, and the sound broke something open in the morning. Beatrix turned away and looked at the river—the same river her father had followed upstream, the same water, still falling, still going where it had always gone—and felt the contempt she'd worn like a second hat begin, against her will, to crack. Not to fall. To crack. Light getting in where light had no business being.

"There's a camp," she said at last, without turning around. "Upstream. High country. If any piece of him is still up there—journal pages, anything—"

"Then we find it," Wren said. "Together."

She picked up her boots. The sun climbed. Somewhere far behind them, faint as a lie, she thought she heard hoofbeats on the trail below the narrows—many horses, moving fast—and when she turned to look, Wren was already staring that way too, his face draining of everything she'd just been given.

"They followed us across," he said. "Get your boots on. Now."
