## 9. Canyon of Echoes

The canyon found them before they found it, a seam in the earth so narrow that Beatrix rode past it twice before Wren called her back.

"Here." He had dismounted and was standing at the crack in the sandstone, one hand resting against the rock as though greeting an old acquaintance. "The water goes in but it doesn't come out. See how the gravel fans are all swept toward the entrance? Everything that washes down this drainage ends up in there. Including, if my memory serves, a certain stubborn man with a survey kit and a habit of camping where no one would think to look."

Beatrix looked at the slot—a darkness no wider than a doorway, walled in stone the color of dried blood, breathing out cool air like something alive. Her father had camped here. The thought arrived without evidence and settled in her chest anyway, the way certain knowledge does, arriving ahead of its own proof.

"You've been here before," she said. Not a question.

"Once. Five years ago." Wren did not look at her. "I was the courier. I carried his reports downstream. That was my whole job—carry things and ask nothing." He paused. "I carried the last one too. Or I was supposed to."

The horses stamped. Somewhere above them a raven said something sour to the sky. Beatrix swung down from the saddle, and the ground seemed to tilt under her, the desert suddenly a room she had been walking through blind.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

The slot canyon swallowed the light a few steps in, and then gave it back in pieces—slivers of blue sky overhead, reflected glow off the polished walls, a soft amber luminance that seemed to come from the stone itself. The walls closed to shoulder width in places, then opened into chambers where the echo of their footsteps came back doubled, tripled, a congregation of ghosts walking with them. Water had carved this place over millennia, and it had polished everything: the stone under their boots was slick as river glass, grooved with the memory of currents.

Beatrix had been in slot canyons before. She knew their moods. But she had never entered one carrying this particular weight, this certainty that the walls remembered her father's shoulders brushing them, that his boots had left their prints in the same silt she was now erasing.

"His camp was in the third chamber," Wren said quietly. "Past the choke where you have to turn sideways. He liked it because you can't see the entrance from inside. He said—" Wren's voice caught, and he cleared his throat. "He said a man should be able to sleep without watching the door."

Beatrix stopped walking. "You knew him well enough to quote him."

"I carried his mail," Wren said. "For two years. You learn people from what they write." He started forward again, and she followed, and neither of them spoke of it further, but something had shifted in the narrow dark between them—some wall of its own coming down, or going up. She couldn't tell which.

The choke came as Wren had described it: a place where the canyon pinched and a fallen slab forced them to turn sideways, pressing their backs to one wall and their chests to the other, edging through a gap that scraped the canvas of their packs. Beatrix held her breath without meaning to. On the far side the canyon opened into a chamber perhaps thirty feet across, circular, its ceiling a hundred feet up where the light fell in a single pale column, and there—

There it was.

A camp, five years frozen.

A small tent of waxed canvas, sagged but whole, its guy-lines still taut where they were anchored to stone cairns. A fire ring with wood stacked beside it, dry as bone, waiting for a hand that would never come. A tin plate on a flat rock, holding the fossilized remains of a meal. A surveyor's transit on its tripod, still leveled, still aimed at a patch of sky through the light column, as if its owner had stepped out to check a bearing and meant to come back. A wool coat folded on a stone. A tin cup, and beside it a clay pipe, and beside the pipe a small brass tin of tobacco gone to dust.

Beatrix stood at the edge of the chamber and could not make her feet move.

Everything was arranged with her father's particular orderliness—the tent flap folded in thirds, the tools hung on a line strung between two boulders, each item in its place. He had always been like that. A place for everything, because a man who moved through wild country, he used to say, owed the wild the courtesy of not scattering himself across it. She had grown up in a house where the scissors went back on the third hook and the boots went toes-out by the door, and she had hated it as a child, and now she stood in the perfect preservation of it and understood that she would give anything to be scolded one more time for leaving her rifle uncleaned.

"He meant to come back," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened by the stone. "Look at it. He meant to come back for all of it."

"Or he never got the chance to mean otherwise," Wren said.

She crossed the chamber slowly, her boots silent on the silt, and touched the transit's tripod. Cold brass. She touched the folded coat—still faintly smelling of him, of pipe smoke and saddle leather and the liniment he rubbed into his shoulders, and the smell went through her like a bullet, and she stood there with her hand on the folded wool for a long time, breathing.

The journal was in the tent.

She knew it would be before she found it. She knelt at the tent flap and drew it back, and there, in the center of the sleeping roll, weighted with a flat stone, lay the oilcloth-wrapped bundle she had known her father to keep since she was six years old. She lifted it with both hands. The oilcloth crackled. Inside, the journal's leather cover had gone stiff, and the brass clasp had tarnished green, but it opened, and the pages were dry, and his handwriting marched across them—small, level, patient, the handwriting of a man who measured things twice.

The last entry was dated five years and two months past. Beatrix read it once in the amber light, and then read it again, and the chamber seemed to grow larger around her, and colder, and the light column above turned from amber to the color of old bone.

*The assays are confirmed. Three samples from the source pool, three from the outflow, all showing the same tailings signature—lead, arsenic, mercury in concentrations that will poison every soul drawing water below Redrock Narrows. This is not seepage. This is deliberate. Someone has been running mill waste into the headwaters and letting the river do the distributing. I have documented the outfall pipe. I have photographed it. The syndicate's own survey marks are on the pipe flanges.*

*I sent my report down with the boy. If it reaches the territorial office, this ends. If it doesn't reach them, then I will know exactly who wanted it not to, and I will carry the evidence out myself, overland, and I will not stop until it is in a judge's hands.*

*There is no gold here. I want that written plainly, in case these pages outlive me. There was never gold. There is something worth more than gold and worth killing for, and it is the health of ten thousand people who will never know my name, and I am content with that.*

*Bea, if you ever read this: I did not come up here to get rich. I came up here to come back clean. Tell your mother I kept the bargain.*

Beatrix read the last line four times. The words did not change. The canyon did not change. But something in her changed—something structural, a load-bearing wall coming down or going up, she could not have said which, only that the whole architecture of her grief shifted and resettled around a new and terrible shape.

Not gold. Never gold.

The syndicate hadn't killed her father for treasure. They had killed him for the opposite of treasure—for a report, for a set of photographs, for the truth about a pipe discharging poison into the water that every town from here to the lowlands drank. They had killed him the way you'd kill a man who'd found a flaw in your foundation. Not in anger. In bookkeeping.

And she had spent five years hunting a thief for a satchel of papers, measuring her life in miles, wearing her loneliness like armor, and all the while the true weight of what her father had carried sat here in a slot canyon, dry and patient, waiting for her the way he had waited for her mother's forgiveness he never lived to collect.

"Read it," she said, and held the journal out to Wren without looking at him.

He read. She watched his face while he did—the play of light from the column moving over his features as the sun crawled, the tightening around his eyes, the moment his jaw set. When he reached the line about *the boy* he stopped, and Beatrix saw him go very still, the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing.

"The report I carried," he said. "The last one. It never left my hands. I was supposed to take it to the relay station at Meridian Flats, and Voss-Hale's men were waiting there, and they asked me for it, and I—" He closed the journal carefully, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child. "I gave it to them. I told myself they'd only read it. I told myself a courier isn't responsible for what he carries." He looked up at her, and in the strange light his face was stripped of everything, all the calculation and the charm, down to the bare rock beneath. "They killed him because of what I handed over. Because I showed them exactly what he knew, and they came up here to make sure there was no more of it."

The echo took his words and gave them back twice, so that the chamber was full of *make sure, make sure, make sure.*

Beatrix felt the rage come up through her like water rising in a well shaft—cold, not hot, and all the more terrible for it. Five years she had hunted a thief. Five years she had believed the story the desert told, the story everyone told: prospector finds gold, prospector dies of gold, gold makes graves. And the truth had been squatting in the dark all this time, patient as stone, and it was so much worse than the story, because the story was about greed and greed was human, and this—this was a company deciding that ten thousand lives downstream were a rounding error, and a boy with a mail satchel deciding his own skin was worth more than a stranger's report.

"Voss-Hale signed the order," Wren said. "I've seen the payroll. I told you that's why I took your papers—the forged signature, my name on the syndicate's books. But there's more in those ledgers than my name. There's a line item, five years old. *Special disbursement, source expedition, final.* I used to think it was a bonus." His voice was ash. "It was a payment for a killing. I've been carrying the receipt in my head for five years, and I didn't know what I was carrying."

Beatrix took the journal back. Her hands were steady. That surprised her—she had expected them to shake, expected the rage to come out of her hands, but it stayed in her chest instead, cold and compact, a thing with edges.

"He documented the outfall pipe," she said. "He photographed it. Where are the photographs?"

Wren shook his head slowly. "The syndicate took everything. That was the whole point of the expedition they sent up—the one he didn't come back from. They burned the camp at the source. They took his samples, his plates, his notes. What's here is what he kept back. He kept the journal back." He looked around the chamber, at the transit aimed at the sky, at the coat, the pipe, the plate. "He knew. Don't you see? He knew they might come, and he left the journal here because he trusted the canyon to keep it. He trusted—" Wren stopped.

"Say it," Beatrix said.

"He trusted whoever came looking next to be worth trusting."

The light in the column above had gone from amber to gray. Somewhere high on the rim, wind moved through the junipers, and the sound came down the slot like breath through a flute. Beatrix wrapped the journal in its oilcloth and tied it, and her hands were steady, and inside her the cold rage settled into something with a purpose and a direction, the way water settles into a channel.

She had spent five years erasing herself. Every record, every register, every roll—she had scrubbed Beatrix Dunmore out of the world so thoroughly that the woman who remained was mostly miles. And now she understood that the erasure had been a kind of surrender dressed as discipline. She had hidden because hiding felt like action. She had hunted a thief because hunting felt like justice. And the whole time, the real work—the work her father had died mid-sentence doing—had been sitting in the dark, waiting for someone to finish it.

"They'll come for this," she said. "The syndicate. If they knew this camp existed—"

"They don't. I never told them where he based. I didn't know myself, not exactly. I knew the drainage, not the chamber." Wren rose, wincing, his graze-wound stiff. "But the map leads here, Beatrix. The route on that map you're carrying—it passes this drainage. If Voss-Hale is following the same route—"

"Then we go faster," she said. "And we take everything. The journal. The transit. His notes. All of it comes with us, and none of it stops moving until it's in a courtroom."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. "You sound like him."

She turned away so he wouldn't see what that did to her face. She busied herself striking the camp—folding the coat, taking down the tent, coiling the guy-lines, putting everything back in its place and then taking the places away. It felt like a desecration and a rescue at once. The chamber that had held her father's last ordinary evening would hold nothing now but the shape of his absence. The transit came off its tripod and into her pack, aimed at nothing, its work unfinished.

At the mouth of the tent she found one last thing: a small tin, and inside the tin, wrapped in waxed paper gone brittle, a lock of hair tied with thread, and a scrap of paper in her mother's hand. *Come home safe. Come home clean. We will be here.* Her mother's handwriting. Her mother, dead two years now, in a grave Beatrix had not visited because visiting meant a name in a register and a name in a register meant a trail.

Beatrix closed her hand around the tin until the edges bit.

* * *

They climbed out of the slot canyon in the last light, burdened now with a dead man's camp, and the desert received them with its usual indifference—the long violet shadows, the first stars pricking through above the rim, the cold coming down off the high country like a tide.

It was Wren who stopped first. He stood with his head cocked, listening, and Beatrix followed his stillness before she understood it.

Down the wash, half a mile maybe, where the terrain flattened toward the river: dust. Not the rising dust of animals grazing. The low, moving, organized dust of riders, several of them, traveling fast, and behind them, faint on the wind, the thin metallic sound of a bugle—no, not a bugle. A signal whistle, the kind the syndicate's trackers used, three notes and a hold.

They had been here before, Beatrix realized. Or they were following the same map.

Wren looked at her, and in the failing light his face was unreadable, and Beatrix's hand went to the journal in her pack, and the cold rage in her chest turned its edges outward and sharpened.

"They know the drainage," he said quietly. "Ride. Now."
