## 10. Riders Behind

They rode hard for an hour before the first shot came.

It arrived as sound before meaning—a flat crack rolling up the canyon walls like a door slammed in a stone house—and then the meaning arrived, and Beatrix pressed herself flat against her mare's neck as dust jumped from the rock beside her head. The echo came back wrong, doubled and tripled, so that she couldn't tell where the shooter stood. That was the point of this country. The canyon spoke in many voices and none of them were honest.

"Left fork!" Wren shouted ahead of her, already cutting his bay toward the narrower channel. "The right one dead-ends at a pour-off!"

She didn't argue. She had learned in five years of desert work that when a man who used to be a courier for the Silverhead Consolidated Mining Syndicate told you which drainage would keep you alive, you took his word and hated him later.

The left fork swallowed them. The walls closed in until the sky was a ribbon of bruised copper overhead, and their hoofbeats became a drumming that filled the whole world, and behind them—fainter now, but there—the drumming of other hooves answered.

*Three riders,* Beatrix counted, by ear. Maybe four. They weren't chasing to run them down. They were herding. She'd seen syndicate men do it to stray prospectors: push them up a canyon until the canyon did the work, until horse and rider ran out of ground and the shooting could be done close, quiet, cheap.

"They'll have riders on the rim," Wren called back. His voice was stripped down to its wire. "Watching the forks. We need dark."

"We have two hours of light."

"Then we spend them well."

* * *

They spent them badly, or the country spent them badly; afterward Beatrix could never decide which.

The badlands beyond the fork were a landscape gone wrong, as if some older, kinder desert had been seized by the throat and wrung out. Gullies branched like black lightning. Hoodoos stood in crowds, leaning, wearing hats of harder stone, casting shadows that moved as the sun dropped and made the whole terrain seem to crawl. There was no trail. There was only the map's thin ink line and Wren's memory of a route he'd ridden once, years ago, carrying satchels he wouldn't describe, and even his memory kept failing against ground that erased itself every flash flood.

Twice they heard shots behind them, wild, ranging shots, meant to hurry rather than hit. Once, glancing back across a mile of broken ground, Beatrix saw them clearly: four riders strung along a ridge, small against the enormous sky, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew the land would do their killing for them.

Her mare was bleeding. She'd found it at the second water stop—a gash high on the shoulder where a spur of hidden rock had opened the animal in the dark—and though it wasn't deep, the mare's stride had shortened, and no amount of walking her cool would lengthen it again. Horses were not built for the badlands. Nothing was built for the badlands. It was a place that existed purely to end things.

"We should split," she said, at the third stop, kneeling with her hands full of the mare's mane, feeling the animal tremble under her palms. "One horse draws them, one slips away. You take the map—"

"No." He said it fast, without thinking, and then seemed to hear himself, and looked away toward the ridgeline where the last light was dying. "They know my face. They don't know yours. If we split, I'm the one who dies, and then you're alone in country you don't know with a map you can't read." A pause. "That's just arithmetic, Dunmore. Don't make it more than it is."

But she had already made it more than it was, and she suspected he knew that too, and neither of them said anything else about splitting.

* * *

They lost the horses on the second day, in the hour before dawn, and Beatrix would carry the sound of it for a long time.

Wren had wanted to push through the night—to put distance between themselves and the trackers while the cold kept the horses willing—but the moon had failed them, and the badlands at night were less a landscape than a series of collisions waiting to happen. So they'd walked the animals, leading them, feeling each step forward with their boots before trusting it, and they'd gained maybe four miles in six hours, and then the ground simply opened.

There was no warning. One moment Beatrix was leading her lame mare along what felt like solid shelf; the next the shelf was gone, and the mare screamed—a sound Beatrix had never heard a horse make and hoped never to hear again—and went down into darkness, and the lead rope tore through Beatrix's gloved hands, burning, and then the rope was gone too.

Below, in the black, there was a long terrible sliding, and then nothing.

Beatrix lay on her stomach at the edge with her arm down into the void, calling the mare's name in a whisper because shouting would bring the trackers, and the whisper was worse than any scream. Wren held her belt. She didn't remember him getting there. Afterward she couldn't account for any of it except the smell of the dust rising out of the hole, cold and mineral, like the inside of the earth breathing.

"She's gone," Wren said finally. Not unkindly. Just true.

Beatrix stayed where she was a moment longer. Then she drew back from the edge, and sat, and found that her hands were shaking—not from fear, she realized, but from the rope burn, and from something underneath the burn that had no name yet.

His bay stood three feet away, ears pricked toward the hole, trembling.

"He won't make it either," she said. Her voice came out flat, ranger-flat, the voice she used for reporting deaths. "Not lame-leading over this ground with four riders behind us. You know it."

"I know it." He was silent a while. In the east, the first gray suggestion of dawn had begun, and it showed her his face: drawn, dust-caked, older than it had been two days ago. "I bought him off a remittance man in Meridian Flats," he said. "Paid too much. He bites."

"You paid too much because you liked him."

"I paid too much because I'm a bad negotiator and a sentimental fool, and if you ever repeat that I'll deny it under oath." He unsaddled the bay with quick, practiced motions, and then stood with his hand on the animal's neck for a long moment, forehead nearly touching its cheek. Whatever he said to it, he said too quietly for her to hear.

Then he stepped back and drew his pistol, and Beatrix turned away, and the single shot went out over the badlands and came back from a hundred hoodoos, multiplied, as if the whole desert were mourning with them.

They went on foot into the dawn, carrying saddles they couldn't bear to abandon and would abandon within the day, water bags, rifles, and the map.

* * *

The trackers found their sign by midmorning, and after that it was arithmetic again, and the arithmetic was ugly.

On foot, over broken ground, they could make perhaps three miles an hour if they were reckless and two if they were sane. Riders could cover the same ground at eight. The only currency they had left was concealment—the badlands' endless supply of stone—and they spent it the way poor people spend everything: carefully, painfully, and never enough.

They moved through the shadowed side of every draw. They crossed bare rock wherever they could find it, leaving no prints. Once they waded a quarter mile up a shallow alkali streambed because Wren remembered that the alkaline mud held a print like plaster. Twice they climbed, hauling themselves and their packs up slopes of shattered shale that slid away beneath their boots with a sound like slow applause, and lay in the shade of a hoodoo watching the flats below.

The riders appeared at noon.

Four of them, spread wide, working the ground the way Beatrix herself worked ground when she was tracking—methodically, patiently, reading the country like a page. Even at this distance she recognized the posture of the lead rider, the way he sat his horse with his weight slightly forward, always looking, and she understood with a cold settling in her gut that these were not hired guns riding a rumor. These were professionals. Someone downstream had decided that whatever they carried was worth real money and real men.

"That's Brannock," Wren breathed. He'd gone very still beside her, in the way of a man trying to become part of the landscape. "The big one, on the dun. He ran Voss-Hale's enforcement line on the lower river. He taught me how to read sign." A pause, and something bitter moved through his voice. "He was good at his job. That was the problem with all of us. We were good at our jobs."

"Will he find us?"

"He'll find where we *were*. Whether he finds where we are depends on the next hour and how stupid we get." Wren's eyes tracked the riders below, and Beatrix watched him watch them, and saw the calculation running behind his eyes like water under ice. "There's a place. Two hours north. An old mine adit in the side of a mesa—collapsed halfway, but the back half holds. Water seep near it. If we can reach it before they cut our trail—"

"Then we're rats in a wall with a hole behind us."

"Rats in a wall live longer than rats in the open." He was already moving. "Come on."

* * *

They didn't reach it clean.

The adit was exactly where Wren's memory placed it—a black horizontal wound in the mesa's flank, half-choked by fallen rock, breathing cool air—and they were fifty yards from it, crossing the last open fan of talus, when the first rifle spoke from the ridge behind them.

This time there was no mistaking the range. Three hundred yards, maybe less, and closing. Beatrix heard the bullet crack past and threw herself sideways behind a boulder the size of a wagon, and gravel sprayed her cheek, and Wren—Wren wasn't behind cover.

He was standing in the open, turning back toward her, and she understood a half-second too late that he'd been covering her retreat, watching the ridge instead of his own feet, making sure she got clear.

"Down!" she screamed.

The second shot came, and he jerked as if the air itself had struck him, and then he was moving, stumbling, and she grabbed his collar and hauled him down behind the boulder as the third round rang off stone above their heads and rained sharp chips across their shoulders.

"Are you hit?"

"Graze." His voice was tight. Blood was coming through his shirt sleeve, high on the upper arm, welling dark and steady but not pumping. "Scalping cut. I've had worse shaving." He tried to grin and didn't quite manage it. "Probably."

She got his sleeve open with her knife. The furrow was long and ugly, plowed through the meat of his arm, but the bone was whole and the bleeding was surface-deep. She packed it with the clean cloth she carried for bandages, bound it hard enough to make him hiss, and all the while her hands were steady and her mind was doing something it had no business doing: cataloguing the fact that he had turned back. That he had stood in the open with bullets coming and turned *back*, toward her, when every instinct of self-preservation he'd confessed to over two hundred miles of trail should have sent him the other way.

Thieves ran. Thieves, he had told her himself, in the waterhole country, in one of his rare moments of honesty. Thieves survived by never being where the trouble landed.

"Stop looking at me like that," he muttered.

"Like what?"

"Like you're revising a ledger."

She tied off the bandage. "Hold still."

* * *

The adit saved them, but barely, and only because the light failed.

They crawled through the collapsed throat of the old mine into a chamber that smelled of cold stone and bat guano and time, and behind them, as the dusk came down, they heard the riders arrive at the base of the mesa. Voices, faint and flattened by rock. The strike of a match. Then, for an hour, the occasional ring of a rifle ball against the talus fan—probing, testing, the way a man taps a wall listening for hollow places.

Then silence, except for the seep water ticking somewhere in the dark, drop by drop, patient as a clock.

Beatrix sat with her back against the wall and her rifle across her knees and listened to Wren breathe beside her. The breathing was ragged. Fever would come by morning if the wound went bad; she'd seen it start a dozen times in a dozen dry camps, and she knew the signs, and she knew she had maybe a spoonful of whiskey left and no more.

"They'll wait us out," she said quietly. "Brannock will figure the adit goes nowhere, post two men, and ride for the telegraph at the toll bridge. By tomorrow night there'll be twenty men between us and the summit."

"Probably."

"And we've lost the horses, you're shot, and we have—" she tilted the water bag, judging by weight and slosh, "—perhaps three days of water between us, over forty miles of the worst country I have ever walked."

"Also probably." In the absolute dark, his voice had a strange lightness to it, worn thin. "You paint a fair picture, Dunmore. You should illustrate maps."

"Why did you turn around?"

Silence. The water ticked. Somewhere far above, wind moved over the mouth of the mesa with a sound like a long exhalation.

"When the shooting started," she said. "You were watching the ridge. You saw me clear and you turned back into it. Why?"

Another silence, longer. When he finally spoke, the lightness was gone, and what remained was raw and unwilling, dragged up from somewhere deep.

"Because on your father's crew, when the shooting started, everyone turned away." She heard him shift, and the soft catch of pain he didn't let become a sound. "Every man there. Me included. We turned away and we ran and we let it happen, and I have spent nine years telling myself it was the only thing a sensible man could do." A breath. "Today my legs started to turn and they wouldn't finish the job. I don't know what to call that. But I know I'm done being the man who turns away. Whatever it costs."

Beatrix sat in the dark with that, and found that her chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

Outside, faint but unmistakable, came the sound she had been dreading: hoofbeats, departing—one pair, then another, riding north through the night toward the toll bridge and the telegraph and the twenty men to come. But two pairs remained, and she could hear, distantly, a match strike again, and a voice give an order, and she understood that Brannock had left watchers.

And then, closer—much closer—a loose stone clicked on the talus fan outside the adit's throat.

Wren's hand found her wrist in the dark and gripped it, and neither of them breathed, and the stone clicked again.
