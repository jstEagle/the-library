## 11. Night Without Fire

### Beatrix

The badlands after the chase were a country of teeth—ridges of wind-carved sandstone jutting from the earth like the jawbones of something enormous that had died here and been forgotten. They made camp in the lee of one of those ridges as the light failed, and they made it cold. No fire. Not tonight, not with riders still working their backtrail somewhere in the purple distance, reading sign the way her father used to read weather.

Beatrix laid out the bedroll with the economy of long habit: ground cleared of stones, saddle blanket folded double beneath the canvas, rifle within reach of a hand that wouldn't have to look for it. Across the small hollow of shadow, Wren did the same, and she watched him do it without meaning to—the way he tested the ground first with his palm, the way he positioned his boots pointing toward the open desert, the way he kept his canteen on his left side even though he was right-handed. A man who had slept in places where waking slowly could kill you.

He caught her watching. He didn't smile. Five days ago she'd have expected a grin, some thief's charm deployed like a coin flipped across a bar. But since Split Rock, since the graze along his ribs that she'd cleaned and bound in silence while he bit down on a strap of leather, something had gone quiet in him. Or maybe honest was the better word. She wasn't sure yet which frightened her more.

"Your shoulder's stiff," he said.

"It's fine."

"You've been favoring it since the second wash crossing."

"I said it's fine."

He let it go, the way he'd learned to let things go around her, folding his hands behind his head and studying the sky. The stars came out the way they only came out in the deep desert—not scattered but *poured*, a spill of white across black so complete it felt less like looking up than falling. Somewhere east, a coyote began its ragged hymn, and another answered, and then the whole dark country was talking to itself.

Beatrix pulled her father's journal from her coat—she'd taken to sleeping with it against her chest like a splint on a wound—and found the page she'd read eleven times already. His handwriting slanted hard to the right, urgent, a man writing fast by firelight:

*The water is wrong. I can taste it in the gravel bars. Whatever they're doing at Silverhead, it isn't mining, it's burial.*

She closed it. Opened it again. Reading it was like pressing a bruise to check if it still hurt. It always still hurt.

Across the hollow, Wren's breathing had gone shallow and careful, the way it did when he was awake and pretending otherwise. She knew the trick. She'd invented it herself, years ago, sleeping alone in ranger stations where the walls had ears and the payroll had eyes.

"You're not asleep," she said.

"No," he admitted.

"Then talk. If we're going to sit in the dark waiting for men with rifles to find us, we might as well spend the time."

A dry sound from him, almost a laugh. "That's the most inviting thing you've said all week."

"I'm not trying to be inviting. I'm trying to keep from thinking about my shoulder."

Silence, but a different kind now—a held breath rather than a wall. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped its performance entirely, and what was underneath was younger than she'd expected. Rougher.

"You asked me once," he said, "back at the waterhole, why I never sold your papers. You thought it was sentiment. Or stupidity."

"I thought it was both."

"Fair." She heard him shift, canvas whispering. "I want to tell you the truth of it. Not because you'll forgive me—I don't expect that—but because we're walking into high country tomorrow, or the day after, and if one of us doesn't come back down, the other should know what they were carrying."

Beatrix turned onto her side, facing the shape of him in the starlight. "Talk."

* * *

### Wren

He had told this story to no one. That was the strange thing about it—he'd carried it five years the way she'd carried her erasure, pressed flat and secret, and now it wanted out of him with an urgency that felt almost physical, like a bullet working toward the surface.

"I was born in Meridian Flats," he said. "Not the town you saw. The town before it. Before the syndicate paved the streets and painted everything company green. My mother worked the assay office. My father ran mule trains up the river trail, back when there was a river trail anyone could walk."

The stars wheeled above him, indifferent. He found the pattern he always found when he looked up—the crooked line of stars his father had called the Mule Team, hauling their load forever across the sky—and took a breath.

"When I was nine, the company bought the water rights. All of them. Every spring, every well, every hand-dug seep within forty miles. My father signed a petition. It had two hundred names on it." He paused. "You know how many names were on the syndicate's list afterward?"

Beatrix didn't answer. He hadn't expected her to.

"My father lost his mules first. Then the contract. Then, when he kept talking, he lost the right to buy feed anywhere in the valley, because every supplier suddenly needed paperwork he couldn't produce. We lasted fourteen months. My mother went to work for the company that had broken us, because the alternative was starving, and I have hated myself for understanding that choice ever since." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced level. "They erased him, Beatrix. Not killed—erased. By the time I was twelve, men who'd known him twenty years called him *the troublemaker*, like it was his name. Like he'd been born that way. There was nothing left to point to and say: here is a man who worked, who built, who asked for nothing but the river."

In the dark, her breathing had changed. Still guarded. But listening with more than her ears.

"So when I got old enough, I did what children of erased men do. I went to work for the people who'd done the erasing. Courier, clerk, courier again. I told myself I was learning the machine so I could break it someday. Mostly I was just feeding it." He laughed, short and bitter. "And then, five years ago, a job comes through. Simple thing. A ranger station outside Redwash, papers to lift, a woman who keeps to herself. In and out. And I'm going through your documents by lantern light, deciding what's worth money—"

He stopped. The memory rose in him whole: the little room, the wax smell of the sealing kit, the ledger of a life spread out under his hands. A birth record. A commission signed in a federal hand. A letter from a mother, worn soft at the folds from being read too many times. A photograph of a girl on a horse in front of a house with a crooked porch rail.

"And none of it was worth anything," he said quietly. "That's what stopped me. A name, a career, a family—all of it real, all of it documented—and to the syndicate it was worth nothing, because it belonged to someone they couldn't use. And I sat there realizing that the only difference between your papers and mine was that yours still existed. Somebody, somewhere, could hold them and know exactly who Beatrix Dunmore was." He turned his head, found her eyes catching starlight. "I'd spent my whole life being nobody on paper. And there you were, spending yours becoming nobody on purpose. And I couldn't do it. I couldn't make you match me."

The coyotes had gone silent. The desert listened.

"You could have just left them," she said. Her voice was rougher than he'd ever heard it. "Taken nothing."

"I should have. But I needed the forged signature—the one linking me to the payrolls—it was in your file because the syndicate had investigated you years ago, after your father. Taking it was the only way to cut myself loose when the time came." He exhaled. "So I stole from you to free myself, and told myself the rest was mercy. It wasn't mercy. It was theft with a clear conscience, which is the worst kind." He paused. "But I didn't sell you. That part was true, whatever else wasn't."

For a long time she said nothing. He lay in the cold and waited, feeling the graze on his ribs throb with his pulse, feeling the truth sit in the air between them like something newly uncovered—raw, exposed, unable to be buried again.

When she finally spoke, it wasn't forgiveness. He hadn't earned that, and she was too honest to counterfeit it. It was something else.

"My turn," Beatrix Dunmore said.

* * *

### Beatrix

She had not planned to say any of this. She had a lifetime of practice at not saying it. But the night was vast and cold and honest, and he had handed her his worst self and waited without flinching, and there was something about a man lying wounded in the dirt telling you the ugliest true thing he owned that made your own ugliness restless in its grave.

"I had a home," she said. "Real one. Crooked porch rail, windmill that squeaked on north winds, my mother's roses growing wild over the fence because she refused to prune them." The words came slowly, each one pried loose. "My father was gone half the year on survey contracts, and when he was home the house was loud—two voices arguing about maps, my mother singing off-key, me pretending to be embarrassed. I used to count the days until he left again so I could have the quiet back." Her throat tightened. "You know what I'd give now for one of those loud mornings?"

Wren didn't move. Didn't fill the silence with comfort, and she was grateful, because comfort would have cracked her open.

"After the expedition, after they told me he drowned—" She made herself say it plainly. "My mother lasted eight months. Grief, the doctor said, but I knew it was the other thing. It was the not-knowing. A body with no answers buried in it. She used to set his plate at dinner for weeks after. I'd come downstairs and there it was, fork laid straight, bread and salt, like he was coming up the trail any minute." Beatrix stared at the stars and let the memory do what memories do. "After she died, I went back to the house once. Squatters had burned the porch rail for firewood. Roses gone to seed. Windmill fallen. And I stood in the yard and understood that home isn't a place you lose. It's a place that stops existing, and you keep knocking on a door that isn't there."

"That's why you measure in miles," he said softly.

She turned to look at him. In the starlight his face was all planes and shadows, the thief's handsomeness burned away, leaving something plainer and more durable underneath.

"Miles don't leave you," she said. "Miles don't die or burn down or get sold to a mining company. A mile is the same whether you're running toward something or away from it. Five years I've been counting them—Redwash to the border, border to the flats, flats to wherever the next trace of him turns up—and every mile is a way of not counting the other thing. The years. What they cost." She heard her own voice go thin and hated it and kept going anyway, because he had given her his worst and she owed him hers. "I told myself I was hunting the man who stole my identity. Truth is, I was hunting the last person alive who might know how my father died. The thief was just the trailhead."

"And now?"

"Now I have a journal that says he didn't drown." The words came out steady, which surprised her. "Now I have a map to the place that killed him, and a partner I arrested, and a shoulder full of badlands grit, and tomorrow we walk into syndicate country with no fire and no backup and no plan worth the name." She almost smiled. "And I'm not measuring miles anymore. I measured them this morning and they meant nothing. From here on, I'm counting something else."

"What?"

"Honesty," she said. "One day at a time. Starting with this one."

The silence that followed was different from all the silences before it. Not a wall, not a truce. Something with weight and warmth to it, fragile as frost, that neither of them moved to disturb. She became aware of the small distances between them—six feet of cold sand, the width of the world—and of how, for the first time in five years, the distance felt chosen rather than imposed. Intimacy without touch. Two people lying apart in the dark, closer than they'd ever been.

"Beatrix." His voice, very low.

"Wren."

"If we get down off this mountain—if Voss-Hale falls and the truth comes out and they put me in a cell for the courier years—" He hesitated, and she heard him choose honesty over charm, the new habit taking root. "Would you wait? Not for me. Just—would you remember that I told you the truth when it cost everything?"

She looked at the sky a long moment, at the Mule Team hauling its eternal load.

"I'll remember," she said.

He slept then, really slept, his breathing evening out into something unguarded. Beatrix stayed awake a while longer, the journal warm against her chest, and let herself feel the strangeness of it: trust, arriving sideways, in a cold camp with no fire, in the company of a thief.

It was near midnight when she heard it—faint, from the south, carried on the wind off the flats. Not hoofbeats. Not voices.

A bell. The deep bronze toll of the syndicate's bridge bell at Tollgate Crossing, ringing the night watch, ringing and ringing, far more times than any hour required.

Someone had crossed the toll bridge tonight. Someone important, moving fast, heading for high country.

Beside her, Wren's breathing had stopped.
