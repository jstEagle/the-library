## 12. The Toll Bridge

The river told them where the bridge was before they saw it. For two days it had been a rumor in the canyon walls—a sound like breathing, like something vast asleep beneath the stone—and then the gorge opened its throat and there it was: the Silverhead running wide and brown and muscular, carrying snowmelt from the high country down to towns that had no idea what else it carried.

Beatrix reined up on the last shelf of rock above the water and looked at the crossing with the flat attention she gave to anything that might kill her.

The bridge was iron and timber, older than the syndicate, built by some optimistic territorial government that had imagined stagecoaches where there would only ever be mules and greed. It spanned the narrows in a single sagging arc, and at its near end stood a tollhouse of raw plank and corrugated tin, a windlass gate, and four men with rifles who were not pretending to be anything other than what they were. Company men. The syndicate's colors—black and rust, stitched crude on their coat shoulders—hung in the morning air like a warning nailed to the sky.

"There's no other crossing," Wren said beside her. He'd been quiet for an hour, which for Wren meant he was either planning or afraid, and Beatrix had learned that the two conditions looked identical on him. "Not until the high passes, and those are three weeks of climbing this time of year. This is the door."

"Then we knock."

"We don't knock." He swung down from his horse and stood working his jaw, looking at the tollhouse the way a man looks at a house he grew up in and burned down. "We walk through like we own the key."

* * *

They made camp that night in a fold of the hills where no firelight could reach the sky, and Wren laid out his past on a saddle blanket between them the way a gambler lays out a hand he's already lost with once.

A leather wallet, cracked and sweat-darkened. Inside it, papers. Beatrix held one up to the moon and read by its thin light: **SILVERHEAD CONSOLIDATED MINING & WATER — FREIGHT AND COURIER AUTHORIZATION**, stamped twice, countersigned by a name she'd seen carved into freight crates all the way down the river. The photograph was younger than the man sitting across from her, but it was him—the same narrow face, the same eyes that never quite finished deciding whether to look at you or through you.

"Courier," she said. "You carried their payrolls."

"I carried everything they didn't want riding the public roads." He took the paper back gently, as if it still had teeth. "Payroll gold. Letters between directors that would hang them. Once, a strongbox I wasn't allowed to ask about that screamed." He said it without drama, the way you'd report weather. "This authorization gets us over any syndicate bridge in the territory. It got me over forty of them."

"And when they check the ledger and find your name?"

"That's the part you're not going to like."

"I haven't liked any part yet."

He almost smiled. In the dark it was mostly teeth. "The courier who owned these papers is listed as dead. Killed in a rockslide on the Meridian road, four years back. Officially. Unofficially, the man who owned them ran, because he'd read too many letters and kept copies of the wrong ones." He tapped the authorization. "So when I show this tomorrow, the captain is going to do one of three things. Wave us through because the paper is good and the dead stay dead. Or arrest us. Or—" he paused, and Beatrix heard him choose the words carefully, which was itself a kind of confession, "—or recognize me, and let us pass anyway, because he'll want to know where I'm going and who sent me. And he'll send word behind us."

Beatrix sat with that. Above them the stars came out in the cold hard way they did at altitude, sharp as nailheads driven into black timber.

"You're telling me the trap might already be set."

"I'm telling you the trap was set four years ago, the day I died on paper. We're just now walking into range of it." He folded the wallet away. "I can get us across. That I promise. What happens after the far side—I can't promise, because I don't control it, and neither do you, and lying to you about that would be the first lie I've told you since Split Rock."

She believed him. That was the worst of it. Five years of reading men across a rifle sight had taught her every species of falsehood, and Wren, sitting in the dark with his dead man's papers in his lap, was telling the truth the way drowning men tell it—flatly, with no strength to spare for decoration.

"Then we cross," she said. "And after the far side, we ride like the paper is already burning."

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the kind of morning that felt held, like a breath before a shot.

They dressed the part. Wren dug out of his pack a courier's long coat, black gone green at the seams, with the syndicate's rust-colored piping worn to thread at the cuffs. On Beatrix he pressed a plain gray duster and made her bind her hair under a shapeless hat and put away the ranger's star she still wore out of habit rather than authority—the badge belonged to a woman who no longer existed in any record, and today of all days, invisibility was armor.

"You'll play my hired guard," Wren said. "Silent type. You've got the face for it."

"I'm going to remember that compliment."

"It wasn't one. It was tactics." But his mouth twitched, and for a moment, tightening each other's straps like soldiers before a wall, they were almost easy together—and Beatrix caught herself noticing how quickly that ease had grown, and how dangerous it was, and put the noticing away somewhere deep.

They walked the horses down the switchback to the bridgehead with the sun not yet fully up, and the tollhouse resolved out of the shadow of the gorge: the windlass gate, the plank walls, the four riflemen, and the captain.

He was a big man gone soft in the way of men who sit, with a beard trimmed too carefully for the country and a ledger open on the table before him. He looked up when they stopped at the gate, and Beatrix watched his eyes do what trained eyes do—hands first, then hips, then faces—and come back to Wren and stop.

For three full seconds, nothing moved but the river below.

"Paper," the captain said.

Wren produced the wallet and laid the authorization on the table without flourish, the way a man does who has done it a hundred times. "Freight run to the upper camps. Two riders, four horses' worth of gear, nothing taxable."

The captain read. His finger traced the countersignature. His finger stopped.

Beatrix heard her own pulse. She kept her eyes on the middle distance, on the far side of the bridge where the trail climbed into pine shadow, and let her body go slack and bored, a hired man waiting out another hour of somebody else's business.

"This authorization," the captain said slowly, "belongs to a dead man."

"Papers don't die," Wren said. "Men do. The company issued this number. If the company wants it retired, the company can retire it. Until then, it crosses bridges."

Another silence. One of the riflemen shifted his weight, and the plank floor creaked, and somewhere downstream a bird cried out over the water, thin and lonely.

The captain looked up. And Beatrix saw it happen—not recognition exactly, but something adjacent to it, the way a dog knows a scent it has been taught to hate. He looked at Wren's mouth, at the scar along his jaw that hadn't been in the photograph, and whatever arithmetic he was running behind his eyes, it came out to a number he liked.

"Rockslide on the Meridian road," the captain said. "Four years back. I signed the manifest for the box they shipped home. Light box, for a man your size." He closed the ledger. "Empty, some said."

"Some say a lot of things."

"They do." The captain stood, and he was taller than he'd seemed sitting, and Beatrix's hand drifted with terrible casualness toward her hip. "Gate's twenty cents a head. Company rate for company paper."

Wren set down two bits. They rang on the wood like small bells.

The captain swept them into his palm, and then—he did the thing that Beatrix would turn over in her mind for days afterward, turning it like a stone looking for what lived underneath—he smiled. Not broadly. Just enough to move the beard.

"Rough country up top," he said. "Storm coming out of the west, if the mule trains are honest. Roads wash out. Men get separated. Happens all the time." He waved a hand at the gate, and the windlass groaned, and the timber arm lifted. "Safe crossing, courier."

They rode through. Beatrix did not breathe fully again until the bridge planks were under them, iron singing faintly with the horses' weight, the brown water sliding past thirty feet below with all the patience of something that had drowned better men than these.

Halfway across, she risked one glance back.

The captain was standing in the doorway of the tollhouse, watching them go. He wasn't writing in the ledger. He was holding a small glass signal lantern, and even in the daylight she could see him checking it, thumbing the shutter, making sure it worked.

* * *

They climbed for the rest of the day, up out of the river gorge into country where the pines began and the air thinned and cooled, and Beatrix waited until the bridge was hours behind them and the light was going copper before she said it.

"He knew you."

"Yes."

"He knew you, and he let us through, and he was cleaning a signal lantern while he did it."

"Yes." Wren rode hunched, and when he turned in the saddle his face was the color of old ash. "There's a relay station eight miles north of here. Old telegraph spur the syndicate keeps for freight coordination. A lantern signal from that bridge reaches it inside a minute. By tonight, there will be a wire saying a dead courier crossed the Silverhead heading for the summit, traveling with a woman, armed, gray duster."

"Then why let us cross?" Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Why not take us at the gate? Four rifles, a windlass gate, a choke point. It was the easiest place in a hundred miles to kill us."

"Because killing us at the bridge makes noise. Bodies need explaining, manifests need signing, and the captain doesn't know what we're carrying or who sent us." Wren pulled up his horse and looked back down the trail, the way they'd come, at the empty miles and the gathering dark. "But a director who finds out a ghost from his payroll is climbing toward his source—that man doesn't want us dead at a tollgate. He wants us dead quietly, up high, where the story writes itself. Lost in a storm. Fell at a crossing. The high country eats people; everyone knows that." He turned back to her, and in the failing light his eyes were very clear. "He let us through so he could follow. So he could learn what we know before we die of it."

Beatrix sat with that, the reins loose in her hands, the horses blowing softly in the cold.

Every instinct she owned—the five years of instincts, the ones that had kept her alive and erased and moving—told her to turn off the trail now, cut cross-country, vanish into the badlands and let the map and the truth rot together in some canyon. It would be safe. It would be nothing. It would be the rest of her life spent measuring miles.

Instead she looked up the trail, where the pines climbed into shadow and the storm the captain had promised was building itself out of the west, great blue-black towers of it, silent and enormous, like something rising from behind the world.

"How far to the source?" she asked.

"Three days. Two, if the weather holds. It won't."

"Then we ride ahead of it," Beatrix said. "And we ride faster than whoever's following."

They pushed on into the trees as the first drops came down, fat and cold and few, striking the dust like the opening notes of something that would not stop until it had washed the whole mountain clean—or buried them in the attempt. Behind them, far down the darkening trail, so faint it might have been thunder, Beatrix thought she heard hoofbeats.

She didn't look back. Looking back was a luxury for people with names.

But she counted the sound against her heartbeat, the way she counted everything, in miles and minutes and the distance left to cover—and for the first time in five years, the distance felt like it was closing on her instead of opening ahead.
