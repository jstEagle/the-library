## 14. Alone Above the Treeline

She left before the sky had decided on its color.

The fire had been dead for hours—she'd drowned it with the last of the water, stirred the ash into the dirt with her boot heel until nothing remained but a gray bruise on the stone—and in that darkness she'd sat with Wren's wrists in her hands, working the knots, and then, with the knots half done, she'd stopped. Untied him. Looked at his face in the starlight, at the graze along his ribs she'd stitched two nights ago by feel, and retied them.

"You'll freeze up here," he'd said. Not begging. Just stating it, the way he stated most things, like a man reading a ledger he already knew the totals of.

"Then I'll be the only honest thing on this mountain," she'd answered, and pulled the rope tight enough to sting.

He hadn't argued. That was the worst part. He'd watched her saddle her horse with the particular stillness of a man who had already run the arithmetic and found no sum that ended well for either of them, and when she swung up he said only, "The spring's real. Whatever else—" and she'd ridden out of the sound of his voice before he could finish, because she was afraid of what she wanted him to say, and more afraid that he'd say it.

Dawn came up gray and cold behind her, and the mountain came up gray and cold ahead, and between the two of them she rode a narrow seam of dark that felt like the inside of her own chest.

* * *

The treeline took her an hour after sunrise, and it let her go reluctantly.

The pines thinned first, then twisted, then gave up entirely—stunted whitebark bent like old men leaning into a wind that had been blowing since before there were names for it. Then there was nothing to lean against the sky at all, just rock and the low scrub that clung to it, and the wind, which she had thought was strong in the canyon country, revealed itself down here to have only been practicing.

She led the horse. There was no riding this. The trail—if the game path she followed deserved the word—switchbacked up through scree fields where every stone she displaced went clattering down the mountain like gossip, carrying her position to anyone within a mile. She stopped often and listened. Once she heard something that might have been hooves and might have been the mountain shifting, and she stood for ten full minutes with her hand on the horse's neck, feeling its heartbeat through her palm, until the sound resolved into nothing and she moved on.

The air thinned as she climbed. She noticed it first in the way her breath came shorter, then in the way sounds changed—the horse's hooves, her own footsteps, all of it slightly flattened, as if the altitude were pressing the world under glass. By midday her head ached in a band across her forehead, and the light off the rock fields was so bright and so total that she rode with her eyes half closed, navigating by the feel of the slope under her boots.

She had water for two days. Food for three. The map in her jacket, and the journal page folded inside it, and a pistol with eleven rounds, and a promise to a dead man that she was beginning to suspect had been a promise to herself all along.

That was all. She took inventory of it at noon, sitting on a shelf of granite with the world falling away below her—the badlands where they'd lost the horses, the canyon country beyond, the whole red sprawl of the country she'd spent five years crossing like a woman pacing a cell—and she found the inventory strangely comforting. So little. It had taken her thirty-one years to need so little.

The horse couldn't go much past the next shelf. She'd known it since the scree. She watered it from the canvas bag, let it graze the sparse bunchgrass in the lee of a boulder, and when she broke camp an hour later she left it hobbled there with a slap on its neck and no ceremony, because ceremony was for people who believed in farewells, and she had stopped believing in them the day she'd burned her own name out of the territorial records.

She climbed on alone.

* * *

The storm found her at the saddle below the final ridge, and it did not find her gently.

It came off the peaks to the west like a thing with intent—a black wall with white teeth, swallowing the summits one by one as it came. She had perhaps twenty minutes of warning, which was twenty minutes more than the mountain gave most people, and she spent them badly, because there was no good way to spend them. No shelter above the treeline except what the rock offered, and the rock offered a shallow overhang on the lee side of the saddle, barely deep enough for a body if the body didn't mind being folded.

She folded. The storm arrived.

It was not like the desert storms, which came with theater—dust walls, dry lightning, a show of force and then gone. This was a siege. The temperature dropped twenty degrees in as many minutes. The snow came sideways, then came in every direction at once, filling the air so completely that up and down became matters of opinion. The wind found the overhang anyway, found every seam of her coat, every gap at her cuffs, and worked at her with the patience of water working at stone.

She pulled her knees to her chest, put her back to the rock, and waited.

Waiting, in the desert, was a skill she had honed to a fine edge. Five years of watching waterholes, of lying in arroyo shade through the dead hours, of tracking a thief across a country that did not want to be crossed—waiting had been her trade. But this was different. This was waiting with the cold in her bones and the dark coming on and no way to know if the storm would last three hours or three days, and somewhere below her a man she'd bound to a boulder was waiting too, and she caught herself doing the arithmetic—*he has the tarp, he has the firewood she'd cached, he'll live*—and then catching herself doing it, and hating how much of her mind the man occupied, like a splinter too deep to dig out.

She made herself think of her father instead.

That was easier said than done. For five years she had kept him in a sealed room in her mind, the way you'd keep a wound bandaged—not healing, just contained. But the journal page had cut the bandage off. She'd read it a dozen times since the Canyon of Echoes, by firelight, by dawn light, once by lightning, and each reading peeled back another layer, and here in the dark with nothing to do but endure, she took it out one more time and read it by feel, tracing the ink where she'd memorized it anyway.

*Found the source. Not what they said it would be. Not what I hoped. The water runs orange below the tailings—Hale's men have been dumping since before we signed on. Every town downstream is drinking it. Every town.*

*I told Marta I'd come back clean. I meant honest. I didn't know those were different things out here.*

*If anything happens to me, the map is the proof. Burn it or carry it. Never sell it.*

She had read that last line fifty times and it had always struck her as practical advice, a dead man's logistics. Tonight, with the storm screaming and her fingers too numb to feel the paper, she finally heard what it actually was.

It was a message. Not to whoever found the journal—to her mother. *I told Marta I'd come back clean.* He'd written it knowing that Marta was the one person who would understand that *clean* didn't mean *rich*, didn't mean *successful*, didn't mean *alive, even*. It meant *unbought*. It meant he'd rather come home with nothing than come home with blood on the money. And when the syndicate had offered him the usual choice—silence or a grave—he had chosen the grave, and the map had been his way of making sure the choice outlived him.

Burn it or carry it. Never sell it.

She had spent five years thinking she was carrying his map. She understood now, in the cold and the dark, that she was carrying his answer. And that the answer had never been about the map at all. The map was paper. The answer was what you did when the choice came for you.

Her father had died clean. She had spent five years trying to disappear, and disappearing was not the same as clean. Erasure was just another way of hiding, and hiding, she was beginning to suspect, was just another way of being bought—paid in safety instead of silver, but paid all the same.

The thought sat with her through the long dark like a second passenger, and it was colder than the wind, and it was more true.

* * *

The storm broke near dawn, the way sieges break—abruptly, leaving the besieged unsure whether to celebrate or simply keep crouching.

She crawled out of the overhang into a world that had been remade. Snow to her knees where it had drifted, a dusting everywhere else, and the sky scoured so clean and so blue it looked like it had never held weather in its life. The peaks around her stood white and silent and enormous, and the silence after the storm was so complete she could hear her own blood moving.

She was, she realized, higher than she had ever been in her life. Higher than the survey peaks, higher than anything in the canyon country. The air was thin enough to taste—metallic, mineral, like sucking on a coin. Each step up the final ridge cost her three breaths. Her head pounded. Her lips had cracked and split in the dry cold, and when she licked them she tasted iron and old blood.

She climbed anyway.

The doubt came with the altitude. It came in the thin places, where the mind, starved of oxygen, starts feeding on itself. *You left him tied to a rock.* *You don't know the spring is what he says it is.* *You don't know your father's journal is true—maybe he was wrong, maybe he was mad, maybe five years of your life have been spent chasing a dead man's mistake.* *And even if it's all true—what then? You're one woman with a pistol and a notebook, and they're an empire with guns and lawyers and a bridge and a town and a payroll a thousand names long. What exactly do you think you're going to do?*

She answered it the only way she knew how. Not with argument. With steps. One step. Another. The desert had taught her that: doubt was weather, and weather passed, and the only thing that didn't pass was the trail under your feet. You didn't have to believe in the summit. You only had to believe in the next hundred yards, and then the next, and the summit took care of itself or it didn't.

Her father had believed in something bigger than the next hundred yards, and it had killed him, and he had died clean.

She wanted, suddenly and ferociously, with a hunger that had nothing to do with food, to be clean. Not safe. Not avenged. Not even restored—she had wanted her name back for five years and she understood now, with the last of the doubt burning off like the storm, that the name had never been the point either. The point was to stand at the source of a thing and tell the truth about it, whatever it cost, whoever it burned, including herself.

Especially herself. Because she had left a man bound to a boulder in a storm because she couldn't trust him, and the truth was she couldn't trust herself not to need him, and that was a kind of dirt too.

She would come back down for him. After the spring. If there was an after.

She climbed.

* * *

The ridge crested at what her body insisted was noon and the sun confirmed, and the world opened.

Below her, on the far side, the mountain fell away into a high cirque—a bowl of rock and old snow cradling a meadow that even at this season held patches of stubborn green. And at the head of the cirque, spilling from a fracture in the summit rock itself, a thread of water caught the light.

She stood at the crest with the wind pulling at her coat and looked at it for a long time. The source of the Silverhead. The beginning of the river that watered every town from the high country down to Meridian Flats and beyond, the vein that the whole country drank from. From up here it looked innocent. A thread of silver on gray stone. Everything wicked that had been done to it had been done downstream, out of sight, out of mind, in the canyons where the water ran orange and the towns drank it anyway.

She started down into the cirque, and the thin air made every step a negotiation, and she didn't care. Her father's journal was in her jacket against her ribs. Her pistol was on her hip. Eleven rounds, and she found herself hoping she wouldn't need them—not because she was afraid of the shooting, but because she had finally understood what her father had understood at the end: that the shooting was never the point. The proof was the point. The truth, carried clean.

She was a hundred yards from the spring when she saw the smoke.

Thin, gray, deliberate—campfire smoke rising from behind the rock shelf at the cirque's head, exactly where a party would camp to watch the trail she'd just come down. Not a prospector's fire. Prospector fires were small and desperate. This one was fed. This one was tended.

And below the smoke, half-hidden in the shadow of the shelf, the light came off something metal. Not a cook pot.

A rifle barrel. Two of them.

She dropped flat behind a drift of old snow and lay still in the cold, her heart hammering in the thin air, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the bridge: they hadn't followed her. They had been here first. The trap at the toll bridge hadn't been for her at all—it had been to flush her onto this trail, up this mountain, to this bowl, where the syndicate could deal with her father's daughter at the source, far from any witness, any town, any law.

The spring was a hundred yards away. The rifles were fifty.

She put her hand on the journal through her jacket and began, very quietly, to move.
