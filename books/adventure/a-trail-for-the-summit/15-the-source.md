## 15. The Source

She moved the way her father had taught her to move in broken country: weight on the outside edges of her boots, breath let out slow through the nose, body reading the ground before the eyes did. Fifty yards was nothing and everything. In open snowfield it was one bad step, one glint of rifle glass, one cough from the altitude that had been clawing at her throat since the treeline fell away behind her.

The drift gave way to scree, and scree was kinder. She went up it on all fours, stone cold through her gloves, and each time she lifted her head she counted the two men again—one standing at the bowl's eastern rim, one crouched near a cluster of boulders with his rifle across his knee, both facing downtrail. Watching for her from below. Nobody watched the sky side of a mountain. Nobody but a ranger.

*They were here first.* She turned the thought over as she climbed, testing its edges like a coin she suspected was shaved. If they'd been posted here since the bridge, then Voss-Hale had known her route before she'd chosen it. If they knew her route, they knew Wren had talked—or Wren had told them exactly what he wanted them to believe. Either way, the arithmetic came out the same: whatever waited at the top of this mountain, the syndicate wanted it kept. And men who killed her father to keep a secret did not post two riflemen to guard something worthless.

The wind came off the summit ridge in long, flat gusts that smelled of stone and old ice and, beneath it, something else. Something mineral and wrong. She noticed it the way you notice a wrong note in a hymn—half a second before you understand why your skin has prickled.

She reached the rim of the bowl an hour past noon, belly-down in the last band of boulders, and looked at what her father had died carrying a map to.

* * *

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful, and that was the obscenity of it.

The spring rose out of the mountain's crown like a wound that had learned to shine—a pool perhaps forty feet across, fed from some deep fracture in the rock, so clear at its center that she could see the blue-green veins of stone beneath, and ringed with a crust of white mineral that caught the sun and threw it back in pieces. Snowmelt trickled in from the ridgelines in a dozen silver threads. Beyond the pool, the ground fell away northward into the first shallow channel of the Silverhead—the river entire, the river she had followed for three hundred miles, beginning here as something no wider than her arm.

Five years she had measured her life in miles along that river's course. Every town downstream—Redwash, Split Rock, Meridian Flats, all the small hard places where people drank what the mountain sent them—every one of them began here, at this cup of water on the roof of the world.

And the water was killing them.

She saw it before she understood it. The white crust around the pool was not uniform—it lay in terraces, in overlapping fans, and the newest fans were gray. Not the gray of stone. The gray of ash, of slag, of tailings crushed fine and washed bright. A seam of it ran down into the outflow channel and stained the young river a faint, milky opalescence that would be invisible by the time it reached the canyons, diluted into innocence, into ten thousand gallons to every ounce of poison. Invisible everywhere except here, at the source, where the crime was laid out in the sun like a confession nobody had been meant to read.

There was machinery.

That was the part that stopped her breath. Not a mine above the water—she had braced herself for that, for the obvious sin of shafts and sluices—but *below* it, or rather inside the mountain itself. Set back against the bowl's northern wall, half-shuttered behind a screen of dead timber planted in rows like a fence, stood a low complex of sheds and corrugated roofs, a tram line running up from somewhere beneath the ridge, a settling pond lined with black sheeting. Tailings brought over the mountain from workings on the far side, she realized. Crushed ore from mines the syndicate never reported, milled in secret, and the waste—laced with arsenic, with lead, with whatever else the Silverhead's ore body carried—piped and hauled and dumped into the one place on earth where no one would think to look for it. The source. The purest water in the territory. The perfect grave for poison, because everyone downstream trusted the mountain.

Her father had found it first.

She took out the journal with hands that shook—not from fear now, not entirely—and read the page she had read a dozen times since the slot canyon, but read it differently, the way you reread a letter after a death and find the whole of it changed.

*Day 41. Followed the seep above the falls to its rise. There is no gold worth the name on this mountain, and I will write plainly what there is instead: the water itself is the treasure, and it is being murdered. Found tailings in the outflow where no workings exist on any survey. Someone is using the spring as a sump. Sampled three vials, sealed and marked. If anything happens to me, the vials and this book together are the proof. Tell Bea her father came back clean.*

*Came back clean.* Not rich. Not famous. Clean—as in unsoiled, unbought, uncompromised. All these years she had carried the phrase like a riddle, some prospector's cant about honest claims, and it had only ever meant what a father means when he tells his daughter he tried to do right. He had found the syndicate's secret. He had gathered the proof. And they had put him in the river for it, and called it drowning, and the map he died carrying had spent five years in the hands of thieves while every town downstream drank the evidence.

Beatrix lowered the journal and looked at the spring again, and the awe curdled in her chest and went over, like milk, like blood in water.

A child in Redwash had died the summer she turned nineteen. Fever, the doctor said. Bad water, someone else said, and got laughed at, because the Silverhead ran sweet and always had. An old woman in Meridian Flats with sores on her arms that wouldn't heal. Wren himself—she thought of it suddenly, sharply—Wren with the cough he tried to hide, the one he blamed on canyon dust. Five years of it. Ten years of it, maybe twenty. Whole generations raised on water that had passed this bowl and carried away, dissolved and invisible, the price of the syndicate's unreported gold.

And they had posted two riflemen on the rim to make sure no one ever wrote it down.

* * *

She stayed in the rocks until the light went long, and she worked.

The camera was a ranger's instrument, boxy and battered, and she used it the way her father had taught her to use everything—deliberately, without waste. She photographed the pool and its gray terraces. The outflow channel, with a rule laid beside the stain for scale. The settling pond, the tram line, the pipe that emerged from beneath the ridge and ended, casually, criminally, at the water's edge behind a screen of planted trees. She filled three vials from the outflow and two from the pool itself, sealed and marked them in her father's own style—*S-1, S-2, date, elevation*—and understood, as she wrote it, that she was finishing his field notes thirty years late. Her handwriting was nothing like his. It didn't matter. The mountain would know whose work it was.

Twice the riflemen moved, and twice she flattened herself into the shadow of the boulders and let the wind take her stillness. They were not searching. That was the thing she kept coming back to, watching them from her shelf of stone. They were *waiting*. One smoked. One slept in turns. They faced downtrail, toward the route any pursuer would climb, and their boredom had the settled quality of men who had been told the quarry was coming and needed only to be patient.

Which meant the trap was not for her. Or not only for her. Which meant—

She stopped, vial in hand, the cold glass biting her fingers.

Wren. Bound at the camp below the treeline, wrists raw, telling her with that crooked half-smile to go on alone, that he'd only slow her down, that he'd wait. She had left him because leaving him was the safest thing for both of them, and because some part of her that she hadn't yet forgiven had wanted to finish this without him. And now two riflemen sat on this rim watching the wrong direction, and the syndicate's director was somewhere behind them with a party large enough to travel in state, and none of it added up unless someone had told Voss-Hale exactly when and where to come.

*Or so he claims.* Her own words to him at the bridge, thrown back at her now across a thousand feet of cold air. He had sent a signal. He had admitted it. A message meant to draw the director away from the source, he'd said, so the evidence could be taken downstream. She had wanted to believe it because believing it made him the man she had begun—against every instinct she owned—to think he might be.

But if Wren had sold her the truth wrapped in a lie—if the signal had drawn Voss-Hale *to* the source, not from it—then the man lying bound in the high timber wasn't bait for the syndicate. He was the lure, and she was the fish, and the hook was this bowl with its two rifles and its poisoned shining water.

She looked at the spring one more time—at the threads of snowmelt gathering themselves into the Silverhead, at the gray stain spreading fan by fan across the white, at the small dark shed where men had decided, year upon year, that towns full of strangers were a fair price for gold no ledger would ever show.

Then she did what her father would have done, and what she had spent five years learning how to do.

She hid the vials and the photographs inside her jacket, against the journal, against her heart. She memorized the bowl—the angles, the sightlines, the dead timber, the tram line's path—because paper burns but memory walks downhill on its own legs. And she began to move, not downtrail toward the waiting rifles, but along the rim, west, toward the storm clouds stacking themselves against the evening sky like a wall being built for her alone.

Halfway along the rim, below her, at the edge of the treeless ground, she saw them coming up the mountain.

Not two men. Twelve. Mounted, in column, with pack animals and a closed carriage-chair lashed between two mules, and riding at their head a figure in a gray coat who sat his horse the way men sit when the mountain already belongs to them. The wind carried up a scrap of sound—a voice giving orders, unhurried, almost gentle.

And behind the column, walking in the rope of a hired gun, hands free but watched, moving with the loose-shouldered ease of a man keeping his own count of every rifle present—

Wren.

He had led them here. He had walked into their ropes with his eyes open, and as the column paused at the lip of the bowl, he lifted his head and looked—not at the spring, not at the guards, not at the gray coat—but up, along the western rim, straight at the place where Beatrix lay hidden in the failing light.

He couldn't have seen her. No one could have seen her.

He found her anyway, and touched two fingers to his brow, and smiled.

Then the wind rose, the storm came off the ridge like a tide, and the first snow of the high country began to fall on the source of the Silverhead River—on the poison, on the proof, on the twelve riders and the two riflemen and the daughter of the man they had drowned—falling soft and thick and white, covering everything, hiding nothing.
