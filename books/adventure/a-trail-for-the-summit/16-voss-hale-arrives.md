## 16. Voss-Hale Arrives

Beatrix heard them before she saw them—the deep, arrhythmic percussion of many hooves rising through the thin air of the high country, carried on a wind that smelled of iron and coming snow.

She had been crouched at the edge of the summit basin for the better part of two hours, and in that time her body had learned the mountain's grammar. The wind spoke first from the west, then reversed. The pikas went silent in their rockpiles. A raven lifted off the scree with a sound like a book snapping shut. And beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, came the drumbeat of riders.

She pressed herself flat against the granite and looked down into the bowl of the source.

It was uglier than grief. That was her first coherent thought, kneeling there above the treeline where no water should have been so wrong. The spring itself rose clean from the rock—a thread of silver, cold and honest, welling up between two shoulders of stone as it had for ten thousand years. But below it, maybe four hundred yards downstream, the syndicate had built their works: a tailings sluice cut into the riverbank, settling ponds the color of old bruises, a low processing shed roofed in corrugated tin that flashed dully in the high sun. A pipe ran from the shed to the water's edge, and around its mouth the riverbed was stained orange and white, mineral bloom spreading like lichen across every stone. The contamination her father's journal described was not a leak. It was an architecture. Someone had designed this. Someone drew plans, hired crews, signed payrolls—Wren's among them—and called it progress.

She had spent the morning documenting it. Her father's journal lay open inside her coat, wrapped in oilcloth; beside it, in her pack, were the water samples sealed in glass vials, and the camera she'd traded half her ammunition for in Meridian Flats. She had photographs of the pipe, the ponds, the stained stones, the dead zone downstream where nothing grew—not even moss on the north faces. She had photographed the syndicate stamp burned into the sluice timbers, the same stamp that had been pressed into the border of the map. Evidence enough to hang them, if evidence could survive the journey down.

The hoofbeats grew louder. She counted through the wind: eight horses, maybe nine. A supply mule. They were still below the last switchback, hidden by the shoulder of the peak, but they would crest within the hour.

*They're coming to the source,* she thought, and then corrected herself with a coldness that felt borrowed from someone else. *He's bringing them.*

Because there was only one way Voss-Hale could have found this place so fast. Only one man who knew she'd come here, who knew the route, who had lain bound in the rocks above the toll bridge while she struck out alone at dawn—and who had somehow, in the hours since, gotten free, gotten word out, gotten himself attached to the director's party as guide or prisoner or both.

Wren had led them here.

She should have felt betrayed. Instead she felt something worse: understanding. He had told her once, in the canyon of echoes, sitting across a fire he wasn't allowed to light, that a courier learns to read the road ahead the way a blind man reads a hallway—by the feel of the air. And she understood now what the air had told him. If the syndicate came to the source and found it abandoned, they would scrub it, move the works, poison some other watershed under some other name, and the evidence in her pack would describe a crime scene that no longer existed. But if they came while she watched—if they came *believing themselves unobserved*—

Then everything they did here would be testimony.

*He's trading himself,* she thought. *Buying me time.* Or he was selling her, and she couldn't yet know which, and the not-knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She moved back from the rim, low and slow, and found the position she'd chosen the night before: a shelf of granite behind a fallen fin of rock, overlooking the basin from sixty feet above, with two escape routes—one down the talus toward the lake country, one along the ridge toward the pass. She settled in, checked the camera, checked the revolver she hoped she wouldn't need, and waited.

* * *

They crested the switchback just past midday.

Voss-Hale rode at the head of the column on a gray mare too fine for the country, and Beatrix saw him plainly through the camera's long lens, close enough to count the silver threads in his beard. He was smaller than she had imagined—all those years the syndicate had been a weather system, a pressure over the whole territory, and here was only a compact man in a dust-colored coat with careful eyes and the posture of someone who had never once needed to hurry. He wore his power the way other men wore spectacles: as an instrument, taken off and put on.

Behind him came six riders—hard men, armed, moving with the loose competence of professionals rather than the swagger of gunhands. Two more led the mule. And tied by the wrists to a lead rope behind the last rider, walking because they hadn't given him a horse, came Wren.

Her breath caught despite herself.

He looked bad. His face was swollen on one side, the graze from the badlands chase crusted dark along his jaw, and he walked with the deliberate evenness of a man conserving himself for something. But when the column halted at the rim of the basin and Voss-Hale turned in the saddle to survey the spring, Wren lifted his head and looked—at the rocks, at the ridgeline, at the specific shelf where Beatrix lay hidden—and his eyes passed over her without a flicker. Not a pause. Not a blink. As if she were stone and sky.

*He knows I'm here,* she realized. *And he's telling me he doesn't.*

The column descended into the basin. She put the lens on Voss-Hale and photographed him arriving, photographed the riders, photographed the moment the director of the Silverhead Consolidated Mining Syndicate stood at the headwaters of the river he was poisoning and looked at the clean water rising from the rock with an expression she could not read—satisfaction, or nostalgia, or simple accounting.

"Get it open," he said, and his voice carried up the bowl of the basin with strange clarity, the way sound does at altitude. "I want the flow numbers before the weather turns."

Two riders dismounted at the shed. The others spread out in a perimeter that made her stomach tighten—they were good, better than the trackers in the badlands, sweeping the high ground with field glasses. She flattened deeper into the shadow of the rock fin and did not breathe while the glasses crossed her position. They moved on. Sixty feet of granite and a lifetime of learning how to be invisible held.

Below, Wren stood at the end of his rope like a piece of equipment someone had forgotten to stow. One of the guards finally untied him from the saddle and shoved him toward a boulder near the sluice. "Sit," the man said. "Touch anything and you'll wish you hadn't."

Wren sat. And Beatrix, watching through the lens, saw him do something small and deliberate: he shifted until his body blocked the guard's view of the upstream bank—the stretch of shore where her own tracks, days old and half-frosted, still stitched the mud. It might have been accident. It was not. She had spent five years reading people the way she read trails, and she knew a screen when she saw one.

*What are you doing?* she thought. *What did you trade?*

* * *

The afternoon ground down toward evening, and the works of the syndicate performed their banal theater.

Riders measured the flow of the poisoned channel with instruments and shouted numbers to a clerk with a ledger. The mule was unloaded—crates, drums, a new length of pipe. Voss-Hale walked the line of the settling ponds with his hands clasped behind his back, nodding, and Beatrix photographed him doing it, again and again, winding the film with hands that had gone steady somewhere in the last day and stayed that way. Fear had burned off in her sometime during the climb above the treeline, the way morning fog burns off a valley, leaving the hard bright landscape underneath. What remained was purpose, and purpose did not shake.

Then Voss-Hale finished his inspection, dismissed the clerk, and walked over to the boulder where Wren sat.

Beatrix put the lens on them and stopped breathing again.

The director stood over the thief for a long moment, studying him the way a man studies a tool that has failed him—or perhaps a tool that has surprised him. Then he crouched, unhurried, until they were nearly eye to eye, and said something too quiet for the wind to carry. Wren answered. The conversation went on, low, five minutes, ten, the director patient as a spider, and Beatrix ached with the effort of reading lips she couldn't see, postures she could only guess at.

At last Voss-Hale raised his voice—not much, just enough.

"The girl," he said. "Where is she?"

The basin went quiet. Even the riders at the shed paused in their work.

"She took the map and left me at the bridge," Wren said. His voice carried clearly; he wasn't bothering to keep it down, which meant he wanted everyone to hear whatever came next. "Bound. Left me for you."

"And you led us to her anyway. Out of loyalty." Voss-Hale smiled without warmth. "You always were sentimental about your debts."

"I led you to the source because you paid me to find the map, and the map comes here or nowhere. She's three days gone, maybe four, headed east with papers she can't prove and a story nobody will print. You want her, send men down the river and lose a season looking." Wren shrugged, and the gesture cost him something—Beatrix saw the wince he buried. "Or take what you came for and let me ride out. That was the arrangement."

"That was my arrangement," Voss-Hale said pleasantly. "Yours was to be useful. There's a difference, and you've reached the end of it." He rose, brushing dust from his knees. "The map. Where is it?"

"Gone. She has it."

"The map, Wren. Or I'll have these gentlemen begin with your fingers and work upward until we find a part of you that remembers where she keeps it."

There was a silence in which the wind moved the poisoned water and the clean water alike, indifferent to both.

"She has it sewn into her coat lining," Wren said. "Left side, over the ribs. She sleeps with the journal under her hip and the samples in the left pannier, and she checks the pannier before she checks the horizon, because she trusts locks more than eyes. That's everything I know about where she keeps things." He looked up at the director, and even from sixty feet and through a lens, Beatrix saw the change in his face—the calculation draining out of it, leaving something older and plainer underneath. "But she's not here. And I'm done helping you hunt her."

"Why?" Voss-Hale asked, and it was almost gentle. "Five years you ran my paper. You know what happens to people who cross me. You know it better than anyone alive."

"Yeah," Wren said. "That's why."

Whatever else he said was lost, because Voss-Hale straightened and nodded to the big rider standing nearest, and the man crossed the gravel and hit Wren across the mouth with a gloved hand, once, twice, unhurried, the way a man breaks a stubborn horse. Wren went down on his side in the mud of the tailings bank and got up again before they could pull him, blood stringing from his lip, and stood there swaying with his bound hands hanging and his eyes fixed on the middle distance—on the rocks, on the ridge, on nothing—and said, distinctly:

"I don't know where she is."

"You knew where she slept."

"I knew where she slept *last week.*"

Voss-Hale studied him for a long time. Then he sighed, a small economical sound, and turned away, and Beatrix understood with a lurch that the interrogation was over not because it had failed but because it had never really been about the map at all.

"Leave him tied to the sluice," the director said. "If the cold doesn't finish him, the coyotes will. We ride down at first light—I want to be at the toll bridge before the weather closes the pass." He paused, and looked back once at the spring, the clean water rising out of the living rock, and said softly, to no one, to everyone: "Ten years I kept this quiet. Ten years, and it ends with a thief bleeding in the mud. Well. Everything ends somewhere."

The camp began to organize itself around the evening meal. Fires bloomed. The smell of coffee and frying salt pork climbed the basin, obscene against the smell of the tailings, and Beatrix lay in the cold shadow of her rock and shook—not with fear now, but with the effort of holding still while everything in her screamed to go down there.

*He bought me the whole day,* she thought. *Every hour they spent searching the high ground, every hour they spent measuring their poison, he was paying for with his own skin. And tomorrow they ride out, and he stays tied to the sluice in the dark, and the temperature is already falling.*

She looked at the camera. Fourteen exposures left. She had photographs of Voss-Hale at the source, of the works, of the pipe, of the stamp. She had the journal. She had the samples. Everything her father had died carrying, everything the map had promised, was in her possession now except one thing: proof of what happened next. Proof of intent. And intent was lying by a fire below her, laughing at some rider's joke, planning to leave a man tied to his own crime scene to freeze.

She wound the film and waited for full dark.

* * *

Night came down hard and clear, the kind of altitude night where the stars are close enough to hurt. The syndicate camp burned two fires, and sentries walked the perimeter in slow rotations—she counted forty minutes between passes on the upstream side, thirty on the downstream, and marked the gap in the rotation when the cook's fire drew both men on the south line to refill their cups.

Wren lay where they'd left him, tied wrist and ankle to the sluice timbers, half in the lee of the structure and half out of it. From above he was a darker shape against the dark wood, motionless. Too motionless. She watched him for a long quarter hour through the lens, waiting for the shiver that would tell her he was still alive, and when it finally came—a long involuntary tremor that wracked him and stilled—she found that her decision had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a river decides its course below the surface of the ice.

*Not tonight,* she told herself. *Tonight I finish the work. Tonight I photograph the ledger, the crates, the drums—proof they meant to continue. Tomorrow, when they ride, I go down.*

But even as she formed the plan, she knew the flaw in it. Voss-Hale had said *first light*, but directors change schedules the way weather changes, and if they broke camp early—if they cut him loose an hour ahead of dawn to save themselves the trouble of explaining a frozen corpse—then everything Wren had paid would buy nothing at all.

She looked at the fires. At the sentries. At the shape tied to the sluice.

Five years she had measured her life in miles, in distance put between herself and everything that could hurt her. Distance had kept her alive. Distance had also brought her here, alone above the treeline, watching a man freeze slowly for her sake and calling it strategy.

The sentries' rotation brought them together at the cook's fire. Forty minutes.

Beatrix slid the camera into its case, checked the revolver, and began to move down the talus, silent as snowfall, toward the fires and the sleeping camp and the man who had refused to give her up.

Behind her, far to the east, the first clouds of the storm were already climbing over the pass.
