## 17. What Beatrix Burns

The storm had spent itself by midnight, and what remained of the sky was a bruised silver, clouds tearing apart above the summit ridge like old cloth. Beatrix crouched in the rocks above the syndicate camp, watching the fires below gutter and hiss in the wet, and she did not move for a long time. Below her, lanterns swung between tents. A mule brayed somewhere in the dark. And at the center of it all, tied to a tent pole with his wrists behind him and his head bowed like a man praying to nothing, sat Wren.

She had watched them bring him in an hour ago. She had watched Voss-Hale walk a slow circle around him, unhurried as a man inspecting stock, and she had heard—faintly, on the wind that came up the gorge—the director's voice asking questions and Wren's voice giving back nothing but weather talk and trail talk and the flat insolence of a man who has already decided how he is going to die. She had watched Voss-Hale signal two of his men, and she had seen the canvas tarp they carried toward Wren, and understood its purpose without needing it explained.

They were going to make him disappear up here, where no river ran, where nothing flowed downhill to carry word to anyone.

Her hands were shaking. Not from cold—she had been cold for three days and past caring—but from the arithmetic running in her head, the same brutal sum over and over: one of her against thirty of theirs, one rifle with eleven rounds, one exhausted body that had climbed four thousand feet since dawn. If she went down there, she died, and then Wren died, and then everything her father had found at the spring would rot in this high silence with no witness left alive to carry it down.

So she did not go down. She stayed in the rocks, and she hated herself for it, and she made herself useful instead.

* * *

She built her case by lantern-light under an overhang of granite, working with the deliberateness of a woman defusing something explosive, because that was exactly what she was doing.

First the journal. Her father's handwriting, cramped and slanting, water-stained but legible—the pages from the Canyon of Echoes camp, five years frozen in that dry slot, plus everything she'd added since Meridian Flats in her own hand, dated, signed, witnessed by no one because there had been no one to witness. She read through it once more by the small shielded flame, though she knew it by heart now. *Contamination at the source. Tailings seepage from the Silverhead workings entering the watershed above every settlement downstream. They are not mining silver here. They are mining the river.* His last entry, written the night before he drowned—or before they drowned him—ended mid-sentence, the ink smeared as if the page had been closed in haste or in fear.

Then the samples. Six glass vials wrapped in wool, drawn from the summit spring itself, each stoppered and sealed with wax and labeled in her own hand: location, date, hour. The water had looked so clean coming out of the rock. That was the obscenity of it. It ran clear as glass over stones the color of bone, and it carried enough leached metal in solution to poison a valley, tasteless, invisible, patient. Downstream it became Meridian Flats' wells. Downstream it became the barmaid's cough, the dead children nobody counted, the whole slow quiet murder the syndicate had been committing for years with a shovel and a sluice.

Then the photographs. She had taken them yesterday morning in the grey light before the storm, kneeling at the spring with the camera Elias Crane's map had led her to and her father's journal had told her how to read. Twelve plates. The tailings channel cut into the hillside above the source, disguised as survey work. The seep stains, orange as rust, feathering into the clear water. The syndicate stamp on the boundary markers—*V-H*, the interlocked letters she now knew belonged not to some distant company but to the man himself, Voss-Hale, who had signed his name across an entire watershed like a man signing a confession he never expected anyone to read.

And last, the map.

She unrolled it across her knees and held it a long time without looking at it directly, the way you look away from a wound.

Waterproofed linen, waxed until it gleamed, tough as hide. Elias Crane had carried it gut-shot through forty miles of desert because he believed it mattered more than his life. Her father had carried it first, folded against his chest, all the way up this mountain and all the way back down to the river that killed him. Two men had bled for this rectangle of cloth. One had named her father's name with his dying breath and made her promise: *carry it to the summit or burn it, never sell it.*

She had kept the promise. She had carried it to the summit. Now she knelt above the syndicate's camp with it spread across her legs, and she finally let herself understand what Elias had known and she had refused to know: that the map was the secret. Not evidence of the secret—the secret itself. Every route in, every seep, every marker, the whole architecture of concealment rendered in ink. In the wrong hands it was worth more than silver. In the right hands—the syndicate's—it was the thing that let them prove their claim, seal their sources, bury their crime under paper legitimacy forever. In her hands it was only ever going to be a target painted on her back, and on Wren's, and on anyone who stood near either of them.

But it was also her father's. The creases were his creases; she could feel where his thumbs had worn the folds soft. Somewhere on this cloth was the ghost of his hands, and burning it felt like burning him a second time, and she sat with that feeling until the fire below had burned down to coals and the camp had gone quiet except for the sentries and the wind.

*They will kill him at dawn,* she thought. *Or they will take him down the mountain and kill him slowly, and either way the map does not save him. Only the truth saves him—if it reaches downstream.*

Truth needed no map. Truth needed testimony. Hers. His. The journal, the vials, the plates—all of it could stand in a courtroom without a single line of ink showing where the spring lay. Let the syndicate keep its secrecy. Secrecy was the crime.

Let the map go.

* * *

She waited for full dark, then moved.

Not down—never down, not tonight, not with thirty men between her and Wren and her eleven rounds. She climbed instead, higher, along the spine of rock above the spring, until she stood on the bare shoulder of the summit with the whole broken country laid out below her: the black gorge, the far glitter of the river beginning its long journey down toward every town that drank from it without knowing what it drank. The wind up here came off the snowfields, clean and enormous, and it pulled at her coat and her hair like something alive.

She took out the map and unfolded it one last time, weighting the corners with stones against the wind.

By starlight she traced the route she had walked. Split Rock, where Wren had stepped out of nowhere and caught a bullet meant for her. The flooded narrows where she had hauled him gasping from the water against every judgment she owned. The slot canyon where her father's camp had waited five years to tell her the truth. The toll bridge. The treeline. The spring. Five years of measuring her life in miles, and here at the top of all those miles was the place where the measuring stopped meaning anything.

"You said come back clean," she said aloud, to her father, to the wind, to whatever part of him lived in the worn creases under her fingers. "Not rich. Clean." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I'm bringing it home clean, Da. All of it. Just—not this."

She struck a match.

The flame bent hard sideways in the wind and she cupped it, and touched it to the lower corner, and the waxed linen caught with a soft whump of blue fire that ran up the fold lines like the map remembering every mile at once. She held it as long as she could bear—until the heat bit through her gloves, until her father's route was a lattice of glowing veins, until the syndicate stamp curled and blackened and the interlocked *V-H* bloomed and vanished—and then she lifted it overhead and opened her hands.

The wind took it.

It went up in pieces, burning scraps rising and tumbling and scattering down the dark face of the mountain, dozens of small falling stars winking out one by one over the gorge, over the camp, over the tent where Wren sat bound and waiting for dawn. Ash drifted back across her face, warm, smelling of wax and char. She stood in it with her empty hands open at her sides and felt something leave her that she had been carrying so long she'd mistaken it for her spine.

For five years she had hunted a thief for stealing papers that proved who she was. She saw it clearly now, standing in the ash: the papers had never been the point. The name had never been the point. Her father had died carrying proof of what he knew, and she had nearly died the same way, and the only inheritance worth keeping was the knowing itself. You could burn a map. You could burn a name. You could not burn what a woman had seen with her own eyes and was willing to say out loud.

She packed the journal deep against her spine, strapped the vials in their wool, checked the camera plates twice. Then she looked down at the camp, at the dying fires, at the small bound figure by the tent pole, and the shaking in her hands stopped all at once, replaced by something colder and steadier than she had felt in five years.

Dawn was three hours off. Voss-Hale would want to be moving early, taking his prisoner down before the weather turned again—which meant Wren would be alive until they reached the gorge, which meant she had three hours and eleven rounds and the whole storm-broken slope of the mountain to work with.

Below her, faint on the wind, she heard the camp begin to stir. A bell. Voices. The ring of an axe being pulled from a stump.

Beatrix Dunmore shouldered her pack, checked the loads in her rifle one last time, and started down into the dark.
