## 18. The Descent Fight

The mountain came down to meet her in stages, and Beatrix took each one like a debt being called.

First the scree, where every stone she shifted went clattering into the dark ahead of her like a warning she couldn't take back. Then the talus fields, great broken ribs of the peak, where she moved in the shadow of the boulders and counted her breaths instead of her steps because counting steps meant counting distance and tonight she refused to measure anything but the work in front of her. Then the treeline, bent and twisted into krummholz thickets that clawed at her sleeves and held the storm's cold in their needles like a grudge.

Below all of it, cupped in the throat of the gorge where the summit spring became the Silverhead, the syndicate camp glowed with the orange smear of a cookfire behind canvas. Six tents. A picket line. Two sentries she could see, and the mountain had taught her long ago that the sentries you could see were the ones who wanted to be seen.

She knelt in the wet needles and let the rain find her, let it soak through her hat and run cold down the back of her collar until her body stopped arguing about the temperature. Eleven rounds. A knife. A coil of rope. A storm coming down off the divide behind her like something with teeth.

And somewhere down there, in a tent with a guard on the flap, a man who had taken a bullet graze for her and been traded for it.

*Don't make it mean more than it is,* she told herself, and began to descend.

* * *

The sentry at the picket line died without knowing he was in a fight.

Not dead—Beatrix put the barrel of her rifle against the base of his skull as he stood hunched under a gum poncho, watching the rain, and said, very quietly, "One sound and you don't get to make any more sounds after it." He went to his knees when she took his rifle, and she lashed his wrists to the picket post with the rope from his own saddle, gagged him with his own neckerchief, and left him blinking at her in the dark like a man waking from a strange dream. He was young. They were always young, the ones the syndicate hired to stand in the weather while the men in the canvas tents slept warm.

The second sentry was at the far end of the camp, and he was the problem, because he had a lantern and a habit of walking the perimeter, and the perimeter passed within ten feet of the prisoner tent. Beatrix watched him make two full circuits from the cover of a gear cache, learning him the way she'd learn a trail: the drag in his left boot, the way he paused at the horse lines to scratch a mare's nose, the moment he turned at the gorge wall where the lantern light fell short and the darkness pooled like standing water.

On his third circuit, when he turned at the gorge wall, she took him.

He was heavier than the first, and younger in his fear, and when her forearm came across his throat from behind he made a sound like a hinge and they went down together into the mud. She got the gag in before he found his voice. She got the wrists bound before he found his strength. What he found instead, staring up at her through the rain with the lantern guttering ten feet away, was her face, and something in it made him stop struggling altogether.

"You're the ranger," he whispered. "The one Voss-Hale's been—"

"Quiet." She checked the tent line. No movement. The cookfire hissed under the rain. "How many in camp besides the director?"

"Eight. Nine with the prisoner's guard." His eyes were wide and wet. "Ma'am, I never signed on for—"

"Nobody ever does." She tied him to the cache ring and took his lantern, and turned downwind toward the tent where they were keeping Wren.

The guard on the flap was the ninth man, and he was the one she'd counted on being careful about, and she was right to, because when she came around the tent's corner with the lantern held high like she belonged there, he looked up from under his oilskin hood and said, "You're not—"

She drove the lantern into his chest and the oil did the rest.

He went backward through the tent flap in a bloom of flame and screaming, and the camp came apart the way camps do—shouts, canvas tearing, a horse screaming on the picket line, men spilling into the rain with their pants half-buttoned and their rifles clutched like rosaries. Beatrix was already through the burning flap, knife out, cutting Wren's bonds where he sat bound to the center pole with a split lip and a look on his face that was equal parts disbelief and something dangerously close to laughter.

"Took you long enough," he rasped.

"Shut up and run."

* * *

They ran.

The gorge took them the way a throat takes water—narrowing, steepening, the walls rising on either side into black wet stone that the lantern light couldn't reach the top of. Behind them the camp burned orange against the rain, and the shouting had organized itself into something worse than shouting: orders. Voss-Hale's voice, carrying over the storm with the flat authority of a man who had never once been contradicted in his life.

"Two riders up the west rim! Cut them off at the narrows!"

Beatrix pulled Wren into the shadow of a fallen slab as the first rifle shots came down, spanging off the rock above their heads in bright vicious sparks. The rain was thickening, and the river beside them was rising, and she could feel the storm behind them on the mountain like a hand pressing down.

"West rim's a dead end in this rain," Wren gasped. "The narrows bridge is washed out—I cut it myself two years ago when I was running for them. There's a scramble on the east side, up through the broken country, but—"

"But?"

"But it's exposed for two hundred yards, and there's a man with a rifle on the rim who can see all two hundred of it."

Beatrix looked up at the east wall, at the scramble she couldn't see but could feel, and did the arithmetic she'd spent five years doing in her head: distance, cover, time. Two hundred yards. One rifleman. Two of them, one limping.

"We go at the thunder," she said.

Wren stared at her. Then, slowly, that look again—the one that was almost laughter. "You've done this before."

"I've done everything before." She watched the lightning count down. "Three. Two—"

The thunder came down the gorge like the mountain clearing its throat, and they ran.

The scramble was worse than it looked, which was saying something, because it had looked like a graveyard of broken stone. Beatrix went first, hauling herself up through the rain-slicked jumble, and Wren came behind her with his bound wound weeping and his breath coming in ragged pulls, and the rifleman on the rim fired four times and hit nothing but rock, because thunder is the best cover God ever made and Beatrix Dunmore had learned to pray in thunder.

They came over the top of the scramble into a saddle of broken country, and the storm arrived.

It didn't break so much as it came down—the whole western sky folding over them at once, rain turning horizontal, wind screaming through the broken stone with a sound like the mountain itself objecting. Beatrix got them behind a shelf of granite and crouched there, gasping, Wren's shoulder against hers, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing to say that the storm wasn't already saying better.

Then the lightning came again, and in its light Beatrix saw the rim above them, and the men on it.

Four of them, descending. And behind them, smaller, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man walking his own property, a fifth figure in a long oilskin coat.

Voss-Hale.

"He's not sending men after us," Wren shouted over the wind. "He's coming himself. He wants the map."

"He can't have the map." Beatrix's hand went to her coat, to the oilskin packet against her ribs. "He can't have any of it."

"He doesn't want it to read, Beatrix." Wren's eyes were bright and feverish in the lightning. "He wants it to be the last one. He wants there to never have been any other. You understand what I'm telling you? He's not chasing a piece of paper. He's chasing the idea that anything survived him."

* * *

They went down.

There was no other direction. The saddle they'd gained was a dead end in the storm—the broken country above it climbed into country where a man could freeze standing up, and behind them the syndicate men were coming down with the patience of wolves who know the quarry is limping. So they went down, into the gorge again, down to where the river ran white and insane with the storm's water, down to where the walls narrowed to a slot no wider than a wagon, and the water in it was chest-deep and moving like a freight.

"We can't swim that," Wren shouted.

"We don't swim it." Beatrix found what she was looking for: a deadfall of pine, storm-thrown across the gorge at head height, its root plate torn from the east wall and its trunk bridging to the west. "We cross on that."

"It's rotten—"

"It's a bridge. Everything's a bridge if you don't think about it too hard."

She went first, out over the roaring water, the deadfall flexing under her weight with a sound like a ship in weather, and the rain came down and the river reached up for her heels and she did not measure the distance, she did not count the feet, she simply moved, hand over hand, until her boots hit stone on the far side and she turned and shouted for Wren to come.

He was halfway across when the deadfall began to fail.

She heard it before she saw it—a groan in the root plate, a shift in the trunk's angle—and then the whole bridge sagged six inches and Wren went down to his chest on it, arms wrapped around the wet wood, legs swinging over the white water, and the syndicate men came around the bend of the gorge behind him with their rifles up.

"Leave him!" one of them shouted. "He's done! The woman's the one the director wants!"

And Beatrix Dunmore, who had spent five years learning to leave things behind, who had erased herself from every record in the territory, who had measured her whole life in miles precisely so she'd never have to measure it in what she couldn't carry—Beatrix went back out onto the failing bridge.

She got her hands in Wren's collar and hauled, and the deadfall groaned and dropped another foot, and the first rifle shot came and took a splinter off the trunk a hand's width from her knuckles, and she hauled again, and Wren's boots found the trunk and he came, crawling, both of them moving like animals now, until the root plate tore loose with a crack like the mountain splitting and they dropped the last six feet together onto the west bank and rolled clear as the bridge went into the river and was gone.

The gorge wall was between them and the rifles now. The river was between them and everything.

Wren lay on his back in the mud, laughing, actually laughing, a raw cracked sound with blood in it. "You came back," he said. "I had a whole speech ready. I was going to tell you not to."

"Save it." She pulled him up. "We're not done."

* * *

They weren't done, and the mountain knew it, and the mountain was not finished with them.

Below the narrows the gorge opened into a long scoured bowl where the river spread wide and shallow over a floor of white stone, and it was there, in the lightning, that they saw the camp's second party—the packers, the ones who'd been holding the horses below the narrows, now standing around a fire that the rain was killing, and now, in the next lightning flash, reaching for their weapons because the storm had carried the sound of the bridge going down to them and they were not fools.

Beatrix shot the rifle out of the nearest man's hands—her tenth round, a clean hit on the barrel—and the sound of it in the gorge was like the end of the world, and then they were running again, through the shallows, the white stone slick under their boots, the syndicate men firing wild behind them because no one shoots straight in a storm and everyone knows it and everyone shoots anyway.

And then the bowl narrowed again, and the walls rose, and there was a bend in the river, and around the bend—

Around the bend was Voss-Hale.

He had come down the west rim alone while his men took the east, and he stood on a shelf of rock above the river with the rain streaming off his hat brim and his coat and his long gray face, and he held a rifle he had never needed to use in his life because other men had always used theirs for him, and in the lightning his eyes found Beatrix's coat, found the shape of the oilskin packet against her ribs, and he smiled.

"The map," he said. His voice carried over the water the way his voice carried over everything, flat and certain and entitled. "Give it to me, and you walk out of this gorge. Both of you. I'm not a monster, Miss Dunmore. I'm a businessman."

"You're a man who poisoned a river," Beatrix said. "You're a man who killed my father."

"I'm a man who employs two hundred people in Meridian Flats," Voss-Hale said, and began to descend the rock shelf toward her, unhurried, inevitable, "and every one of them eats because of what flows out of this gorge. You'd burn that down for a piece of paper? For a dead man's obsession?"

The lightning came.

And in the lightning Beatrix did the thing she had decided to do on the summit, in the wind, with her father's journal open in her hands—did it now, here, with Voss-Hale descending toward her and the storm at its full fury and the whole poisoned watershed of the Silverhead roaring past her boots.

She took out the map.

She struck the match she'd been carrying dry against her skin since the treeline.

And she burned it.

The flame caught the oilskin and the oilskin caught and the map went up in her bare hand, a bright orange blossom in the gray rain, and she held it until the fire reached her fingers and then she opened her hand and let the wind take it. Burning fragments lifted off the rock shelf and swirled up the gorge, glowing, spinning, a scatter of small bright ghosts rising into the dark, and Voss-Hale made a sound she would remember for the rest of her life—a sound like a man watching his own name burn.

He lunged for them.

It was not a decision. She could see that even then, even in the middle of everything—she could see that it was older than decision, deeper, the lunge of a man who had spent his whole life believing that anything he wanted could be held, and who could not, could not, watch the last piece of his secret go up in smoke and do nothing. He came off the rock shelf with both hands out, reaching for the burning fragments as they swirled past him, reaching for the ashes, and the rain-slick stone took his boots out from under him, and for one full second—she would count it for the rest of her life, one full second—he hung in the air above the river with his hands full of nothing.

Then the river took him.

It didn't even slow down. The Silverhead, poisoned and rising and indifferent, folded over him the way it folded over every stone in its path, and he was gone—down, and under, and downstream, toward the towns he had spent twenty years sickening, carried by the water he had claimed as his own.

Beatrix stood on the bank with her hand burned and the rain coming down and Wren beside her breathing hard, and neither of them said anything, because the mountain said it for them—a long rolling crack of thunder that went up the gorge and over the summit and away, like a door closing.

* * *

They walked out of the high country in the gray hour before dawn, when the storm had spent itself and the clouds were breaking apart into rags and the wet world smelled of stone and pine and, faintly, of smoke.

Wren's wound had opened. Beatrix's hand was blistered where the map had burned. They were both of them soaked through, half-frozen, out of ammunition, and alive.

At the treeline Beatrix stopped and looked back once, up the long gray shoulder of the mountain to where the summit spring ran down out of sight. Somewhere up there was her father's compass, his journal, the truth of what the Silverhead carried down to every town that drank from it. Somewhere up there was everything she'd climbed for.

She turned away from it, and went down.

"Where now?" Wren asked, when the gorge had opened into the foothills and the foothills into the long brown plain.

"Downstream," Beatrix said. "Meridian Flats." She flexed her burned hand and watched the dawn come up over the water. "There are people down there who've been drinking that river for twenty years. It's time somebody told them what's in the glass."

Wren looked at her sidelong, and there was no calculation in it now, none of the old courier's arithmetic. Just a tired man, walking beside a tired woman, in a country that was finally, finally beginning to dry out.

"They'll hang me there," he said.

"Maybe." Beatrix kept walking. "Or maybe they'll listen first. Either way—" she glanced at the sun coming up over the eastern ridges, at the road ahead of them, long and wet and ordinary, "—we're done measuring in miles. From here on out, we walk it together."

And the river ran beside them all the way down, carrying its secret to the sea.
