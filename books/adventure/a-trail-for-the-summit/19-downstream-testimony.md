## 19. Downstream Testimony

Meridian Flats smelled of creosote and river rot, and Beatrix had never been so glad to smell anything in her life.

They came down off the mesa on the third morning after the gorge, walking because they had no horses left to ride, Wren's arm slung across her shoulders where the fever had made him unsteady. The town rose out of the brown haze like something half-drowned: the company store with its painted signage, the water tower, the assay office, the long low shape of the Meridian Hotel where the syndicate had kept its ledgers and its secrets for twenty years. Downstream of everything, at the river's edge, stood the federal survey tents that had appeared like mushrooms after rain—because Wren's message from the toll bridge had not only drawn Voss-Hale up the mountain. It had drawn the world.

"You're limping worse," Beatrix said.

"I'm limping better. Yesterday I was crawling." His voice was sandpaper, but there was a flicker of the old sardonic light in it. "You're the one who looks like death, Dunmore."

"Beatrix," she said, and then stopped, because the name felt strange in her mouth. She had not said it aloud, to another living person, in five years. It sat on her tongue like a coin she'd forgotten she was carrying. "Beatrix Dunmore. That's the name they'll need."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. His face was still gray from the fever, the graze on his ribs bandaged with strips of her shirt, and yet he managed to look at her the way a man looks at a door opening after a long imprisonment. "You sure?"

"No," she said. "But sure isn't what this requires."

* * *

The survey magistrate was a woman named Okonkwo, sent up from the territorial capital with two clerks, a stenographer, and a warrant that named the Silverhead Consolidated Mining and Water Company in language so dense it read like a geological survey. She had heard the rumors already—everyone in Meridian Flats had heard the rumors by now, the way a town hears anything: through the barmaids, the freight drivers, the children who ran messages for pennies. The spring at the source was poisoned. The tailings ponds above the high camp had been seeping into the watershed for years. Every town on the river, from the headwaters down through Meridian Flats and the farming settlements beyond, had been drinking it.

Okonkwo received them in the assay office because it was the only building with a lockable room and a table long enough to spread documents on. She was a compact woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes that had the patience of someone who had spent her career waiting for liars to finish talking.

"You're the ranger," she said. Not a question. "The one from the Redwash district."

"I was." Beatrix stood with her hands flat on the table because if she didn't give them something to do they would shake. "My commission lapsed five years ago. I let it. I let everything lapse." She took a breath. "I'm going to start with my real name, and I want it entered into the record exactly as I give it, because the name I've been using is a forgery I bought in a border town, and the woman who sold it to me is dead, and none of that matters except that I need you to understand why I hid."

Okonkwo nodded once, to her clerks. The stenographer's fingers hovered over the keys.

"Beatrix Marion Dunmore," Beatrix said. "Daughter of Thomas Dunmore, surveyor, who led an expedition up the Silverhead five years ago and did not come back down."

The words went into the machine with a small dry clatter, and Beatrix felt something in her chest loosen and ache at the same time, the way a fist aches when it finally opens.

She told it all. It took four hours. She told them about the prospector Elias Crane dying in the dry wash with a map waterproofed in wax, and the name he'd spoken with his last breath—*Dunmore, tell Dunmore's girl the river keeps its receipts*—and how she had followed the river upstream not knowing yet that the map was the same one her father had carried. She told them about Split Rock, about the syndicate riders who had shot first and asked nothing, about the canyon camp where she'd found her father's journal frozen in time with its last entry half-finished. She read the entry aloud. *Contamination at the source, not gold. The tailings are in the water. They will kill for this, and I am writing it down so that someone, someday, will know I wasn't crazy.* She put the journal itself on the table, its cover swollen and spotted with five years of weather, and watched Okonkwo's clerks handle it like scripture.

She gave them the water samples in their corked vials, labeled in her own hand with dates and elevations. She gave them the photographs she had taken at the summit spring with the camera she'd carried up through the storm, of the tailings seep cutting its orange scar through the meadow, of the dead willows, of the spring itself running clear over stone that was stained the color of rust.

And when Okonkwo asked her, gently, why a district ranger with a clean record had chosen to erase herself rather than report what she suspected, Beatrix looked at the wall for a long time before she answered.

"Because I thought my father had failed," she said. "Because I thought he'd gone after gold like everyone said, and gotten himself killed for it, and I was ashamed of him. And because I was afraid that if I asked questions, the people who killed him would notice me. So I made myself small. I made myself a rumor. I measured my life in miles so I wouldn't have to measure it in years." She turned back and met Okonkwo's eyes. "I was a coward for five years. Write that down too. It's part of the record."

The stenographer looked up. Okonkwo shook her head slightly—*no, we won't write that*—but Beatrix saw something in the magistrate's face that was not pity. It was recognition. Everyone in that room had been afraid of the syndicate for years. That was the whole architecture of it. That was how it stood.

* * *

They broke at noon, and Beatrix walked out into the street because she could not stand the smell of ink anymore, and found the town gathered the way towns gather when something is happening that they are afraid to name.

They lined both sides of the main road, outside the assay office, outside the company store with its windows still bearing the syndicate's painted crest that someone had already thrown a brick through. Farmers with sunburnt necks. Barmaids in their aprons. The freight drivers who hauled ore and never asked what was in the water. An old woman holding a child whose hair had come in thin and brittle, the way hair comes in downstream of the tailings seeps, and Beatrix had seen that child's hair before in a dozen river towns and had told herself it was the hard water, the altitude, anything but the truth.

They didn't cheer. They didn't speak. They just watched her, and some of them nodded, and the old woman with the child stepped forward and pressed something into Beatrix's hand—a jar of honey, the wax seal dusty—and said, "For your father," and went back to her place in the line as if she'd only asked for directions.

Beatrix stood in the middle of the road holding a jar of honey and cried for the first time since the dry wash, openly, in front of a hundred strangers, and found that she did not dissolve. That was the thing no one told you about being seen: you did not burn up. You just stood there, wet-faced and ordinary, and the world went on holding you up.

* * *

Wren testified in the afternoon.

She sat in the room while he did it, because he asked her to, and because she understood what it cost him. He stood—sitting hurt his ribs—and gave his name, and the name was not Wren. It was Elias Wren Calloway, and he had been born in a company town not unlike this one, and his father had died in a tunnel collapse that the syndicate's inspectors had signed off on with a signature that turned out, years later, to have been forged by a junior clerk. A junior clerk who had been promoted for it. A junior clerk who had eventually trained a boy of seventeen to carry sealed ledgers between the high camps and the assay office, and to ask no questions, and to lose his own name somewhere along the way because a courier with no name and no papers could never testify.

"I stole the ranger's identity papers," Wren said, and Beatrix watched the room tighten around him, the clerks exchanging glances, Okonkwo's pen pausing. "Five years ago. In the Redwash district. She's wanted me for it ever since, and she had every right." He looked at Beatrix then, and did not look away. "I took them because her father's signature was on a syndicate payroll voucher I carried. A dead man's signature, dated four months after he died. I needed to know who was forging it and why, and her papers were the only thread I had. I never sold them. I never used them. I carried them for five years like a stone in my pocket, and I'm surrendering them to this court now, along with every voucher, every ledger page, and every route map I kept from my years as the company's courier."

He set a oilskin packet on the table. It made a sound like a heavy book.

"I am guilty of theft, of forgery by association, of carrying the instruments of a conspiracy I did not fully understand for seven years and understood fully for the last two, during which I did nothing." His voice did not waver, but Beatrix saw his hands, flat on the table the way hers had been flat, holding themselves still by force. "I'm not asking for mercy. I'm asking to be entered into the record. All of it. The syndicate built itself out of men like me—men it made invisible so we couldn't be witnesses. I'm done being invisible."

The stenographer's keys clattered. Okonkwo asked her questions—careful, patient, exhaustive—and Wren answered every one, including the ones about the toll bridge, about the signal he'd sent, about Voss-Hale. He told the truth about the trap he'd sprung and the gamble he'd made with both their lives, and Beatrix sat in the corner and let it all go into the record, because that was the bargain now. Everything into the record. Nothing held back. That was the only water clean enough to drink.

When it was over, near dark, Okonkwo dismissed the clerks and stood at the window looking down at the street, where lanterns were coming on one by one.

"You understand what happens tomorrow," she said, not turning around. "The territorial papers will have your name. Both your names. The syndicate's lawyers will come at you with everything—your forged commission, his thefts, the years you both spent outside the law. They will try to make the story about you instead of the river."

"Let them," Beatrix said.

Okonkwo turned. For the first time she smiled, a small dry thing. "I hoped you'd say that. Because there's one more thing. We pulled records from the assay office safe before the fire damage spread to the lower drawers." She set a single folder on the table, thin, its edges charred. "Personnel. And there's a name in here I think you'll want to see. Someone who was on your father's expedition. Someone whose employment with the company didn't end five years ago."

Beatrix opened the folder.

The name stared up at her, and the floor of the world tilted gently, the way it does when the ground you thought was solid turns out to be a bridge.
