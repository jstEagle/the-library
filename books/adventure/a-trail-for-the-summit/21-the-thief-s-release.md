## 21. The Thief's Release

The prison at Cold Hollow sat in a fold of the mountains like a stone dropped in a pocket—gray granite, gray slate roof, gray men in gray coats shoveling the first snow of October off a walkway that would be white again by noon. Beatrix had been watching it since dawn from the ridgeline east of the yard, the way she used to watch waterholes for game, the way she used to watch roads for riders. Old habits. Old eyes. She had spent a year teaching herself new ones, and still the first thing she did with any morning was count the exits.

Three, from here. The main gate, the coal chute on the north face, the drainage culvert that stank even at this distance. She caught herself cataloguing them and made herself stop. Made herself look instead at the aspens going gold along the creek, at the smoke rising straight from the prison kitchen chimney, at the ordinary, unremarkable morning of a place she had no reason to fear and no reason to flee.

A year. Twelve months since she had stood in the Meridian Flats courthouse with her hand on a Bible she didn't believe in and given the clerk a name she hadn't spoken aloud in five years. *Beatrix Dunmore.* It had come out of her rusty, half-swallowed, like a coin pried from frozen ground. The stenographer had asked her to repeat it. She had repeated it. And that was the moment the erasure ended—not with the verdict, not with the syndicate's collapse, not with the headlines that ran her photograph beside her father's—but in that small, absurd instant of saying her own name twice.

Since then she had been learning to live in a world where her name preceded her. It was harder than being nobody. Nobody had been simple. Nobody could walk into a town and walk out again without a single soul the wiser. Beatrix Dunmore, by contrast, was a story now, and stories collected people the way carcasses collected flies: reporters, petitioners, the wives of sick miners, a man from the territorial water commission, a woman in Redwash who swore Beatrix's father had once pulled her boy from a flash flood and could she please, please sign this letter. Beatrix signed it. She was learning that too—learning that a name was not a wound to be guarded but a tool to be used, and that it dulled if you kept it sheathed.

The gate opened at nine.

She heard it before she saw it—the groan of iron, the shout of a turnkey, the barking of the yard dog—and her heart did something it had no business doing after everything they had survived. It jumped. Not fear. She knew fear intimately, knew its taste and its weight and the way it narrowed the world to a corridor of escape routes. This was something else, something with no name she trusted, and she stood very still on the ridgeline and let it move through her like weather.

A figure came out alone.

She had told herself she would know him at any distance, and she did, though a year had worked changes on him. He was thinner. The prison had put gray into his brown hair at the temples, or perhaps the year had done that and the prison had merely kept count. He wore a coat that didn't fit him, issued and shapeless, and he carried everything he owned in a canvas sack over one shoulder, and he paused just past the gate with the bewildered stillness of a man turned loose into a country too large for him.

Wren. She still thought of him as Wren, though the court records said Thomas Aldery, a name he answered to the way a horse answers a name given it by its third owner. He had told her once, on the badlands night with no fire, that he'd worn so many names he'd begun to suspect there was no one underneath them at all—just a succession of men, each borrowed, each returned. She had understood that better than he knew. She had been a succession of women herself.

She came down off the ridge.

She had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating truth of it—she, who had faced down syndicate riders and crossed the flooded narrows and testified for six hours under cross-examination from lawyers paid by men who wanted her dead—she had rehearsed this meeting on the long ride north, composing openings the way she'd once composed routes. *I brought your horse.* No. *The survey season starts in spring.* Too soon. *You look—* Dangerous ground. In the end she had settled on nothing at all, on trusting her legs to carry her the last hundred yards and her mouth to manage whatever came after, and now her legs carried her and her mouth produced:

"You're standing in the road."

He turned. And there it was—the face she had first seen across a waterhole with a rifle sight between them, the face she had pulled half-drowned out of the river, the face she had watched through the rocks at the summit spring while Voss-Hale's men asked him questions and he bled and refused, refused, refused to give up her name. A year of stone walls had not changed the eyes. They were still quick and still guarded and still, underneath the guard, looking for the exit.

"So I am," he said. His voice was rougher. Prison did that, or disuse. "I was waiting to see if the road was real. They tell you in there that everything outside is a story they let you believe so you'll behave."

"Is it?"

"I'll let you know." He shifted the canvas sack on his shoulder. His hands, she noticed, had a fine tremor he kept trying to still. "You came a long way."

"Redwash to Cold Hollow is eleven days, if you don't push."

"You counted."

"I count everything. You know that."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if he remembered how to build one. "I do know that." He looked past her, up the valley, at the aspens and the creek and the snow-dusted peaks beyond, and she watched him do what she had done at dawn: count the exits. Find the edges. Measure the cage he was no longer in. "They gave me back my effects. My old papers." He patted the sack. "Half of them are forged. The turnkey asked me which ones were real, and I realized I didn't know."

"Thomas Aldery is real," she said. "I looked him up. There's a birth record in Cane County. A mother. A farm that burned."

He was quiet a moment. The wind came down the valley and moved the aspen leaves with a sound like small applause. "You looked me up."

"You looked me up first. Five years ago. You read my papers, my real ones, and you didn't sell them." She held his gaze. "I've had a year to think about why a man like you does a thing like that. I've decided I'd rather hear it from you than keep inventing it."

"I already told you. That night without the fire."

"You told me you understood what it cost. That's not the same as why."

He looked down at the road then, at the snowmelt puddles going to ice at their edges, and when he spoke it was slow, the words coming up from somewhere he'd had to dig. "Because I read your father's letters. The ones folded in with your identity papers. He wrote to your mother about you—about a girl who measured the world in miles because miles couldn't leave her. And I was carrying papers for a man who measured everything in money, and I thought, there are two ways to erase a person. You can take their name, or you can make their name the only thing they are. The syndicate did the second to me. Somebody had done the first to you. And I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought if I kept them, then somewhere in the world there was still one set of papers that said who you really were. Even if nobody ever read them. Even if you never came for them. It felt like—" He made a gesture, open-handed, defeated. "It felt like holding a door open in a house that had burned down."

The wind moved through the aspens again. Beatrix found that her throat was tight, and that a year of learning to be a person had not taught her what to do with that.

"I came for them," she said.

"You did."

* * *

They walked. Neither suggested it; it simply happened, the way water finds the low ground. Down out of the prison fold, along the creek, through the gold aspens, with the canvas sack between them on his shoulder and her rifle slung on her back out of habit rather than need. The turnkey had told her, when she'd arranged the meeting by letter, that released men usually wanted three things: a drink, a woman, and a train ticket, in whatever order fortune allowed. Wren wanted none of them, or wanted them in a silence he couldn't name, and so they walked, two people who had spent their adult lives being hunted, now hunting nothing, with an entire open morning in front of them and no idea what to do with it.

"There's a town eight miles down the valley," she said finally. "There's a hotel. There's a restaurant that isn't very good. There's a livery where I've got two horses, and one of them is yours."

"The dun?"

"The dun. He got fat and opinionated without you."

"He would." And then, because the silence had come back and he seemed to fear it the way she feared closed country: "The trial. After I was sentenced. Did they—were you there for it?"

"I was there for all of it. Every day."

"You didn't have to be. The testimony was done. My part was done."

"I know."

He glanced at her, and she kept her eyes on the trail. "Why?"

"Because for five years I measured my life in miles put between me and a thief. It seemed wrong to stop measuring the day the thief went to prison. It seemed like the miles would stop meaning anything if I wasn't the one choosing them."

"That's the loneliest sentence I've ever heard," he said, "and I once shared a cell with a man who talked to his boots."

She laughed. It surprised her—the sound coming out of her unbidden, rusty as her own name had been. He looked at her as if he'd heard something rare, which, she supposed, he had.

They crossed the creek on stepping stones, and he offered his hand and she took it before she could decide whether to, and his grip was still the grip she remembered from the narrows, from the descent fight, from the moment in the gorge when he'd shoved her behind a boulder and taken the graze across his ribs. The tremor in it was new. She didn't mention it. Some things a person got to carry unremarked.

At the top of the far bank they stopped, because the view opened—down the whole golden length of the valley, the creek stitching through it, the prison already small behind them and the world enormous ahead—and Wren stood looking at it with an expression she recognized from her own mirror. The look of a man standing at the edge of a country with no fences in it and no idea which direction was home.

"I don't know how to do this," he said quietly. "Be a person. Walk around in daylight. Have a name that's only mine. I was a courier at seventeen. Before that I was nothing anyone wrote down. I don't have a trade that isn't theft or riding. I don't have—" He gestured at the valley, helpless. "I don't have anywhere to be."

"Yes, you do," Beatrix said.

He looked at her.

"The survey team goes back up the river in spring. Restoration, the whole watershed—engineers, hydrologists, a crew of forty. They need guides who know the high country. They need someone who knows every syndicate trail from Meridian Flats to the source, because the syndicate built those trails and the maps of them burned." She paused. "They need someone the river people will talk to, because the river people remember you carried water and medicine down to the sick towns when the company wouldn't. I told them that. In writing. It's in your file now—Thomas Aldery, recommended guide, character reference: Beatrix Dunmore."

"You put your name on me."

"I put my name on a lot of things this year. It's getting easier." She watched the wind move his hair, the gray at the temples, the eyes still hunting exits and finding, for once, only country. "You don't have to say yes. You don't have to say anything today. The job starts in April. There's all winter between here and there, and the winter is yours—first one in a long time that nobody's hunting you through. That's not nothing. That might be everything. I just—" She stopped, and found the true thing at the bottom of her throat, the thing she'd ridden eleven days to say. "I didn't want you to walk out of that gate and have the road be empty. That's all. I know what an empty road is. I lived on one for five years, and I'm telling you, Wren, it looks like freedom and it eats you alive."

He was silent for a long time. A hawk turned high over the valley, riding the cold air, going nowhere fast and nowhere slow. When he finally spoke, his voice was not quite steady.

"You said once you'd stopped going home because home stopped existing."

"I did."

"Do you think—" He looked down the valley, then at her, then down again, a man navigating by feel. "Do you think a thing that stopped existing can start again? Somewhere new? With new people in it?"

The question went into her like rain into dry ground. She thought of the grave she had finally visited, of the letters she had finally read, of the compass she had left on the summit stone and the walk down unburdened. She thought of the survey crew, of the barmaid in Meridian Flats who had pressed her hand at the trial, of the woman in Redwash and her signed letter. She thought of the miles she had stopped counting.

"I think it can," she said. "I think it's starting. I think it starts slow, and it starts ugly, and most of the time you won't be sure it's happening. And then one day you'll notice you made a plan for spring, and the plan wasn't an escape."

He laughed then—short, disbelieving, real. "Listen to you. A year ago you'd have said that with a rifle."

"I still have the rifle."

"I know. I saw it. It's very reassuring."

They walked on, down toward the town and the livery and the dun who had gotten fat and opinionated, and the morning opened around them, ordinary and enormous. After a while he asked, not looking at her, in the tone of a man asking about weather:

"Do you still measure in miles?"

Beatrix considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. She considered the eleven days behind her, counted not in distance but in the changing of the aspens, the first snow on the high peaks, the geese going over in their long ragged lines. She considered the year behind that—testimony and verdicts, a grave visited, a compass left, a name said twice in a courtroom until it fit her mouth again.

"No," she said. "I'm learning to measure in seasons instead."

He nodded slowly, as if filing it somewhere important. "And what season is this?"

She looked at the gold valley, the cold bright sky, the man beside her with his canvas sack and his borrowed name and his unsteady, unguarded hands, standing at the start of a road with nothing hunting him and everything ahead.

"This one," she said, "is the first one I've ever been sorry to see end."

He stopped walking. She felt him look at her, and she let him, and the hawk cried out somewhere high above them, and far to the north, past the town, past the winter waiting, the river they had walked up together ran silver and patient toward the sea—carrying, at last, nothing but water.

"Then let's not waste it," Wren said, and took the first step.
