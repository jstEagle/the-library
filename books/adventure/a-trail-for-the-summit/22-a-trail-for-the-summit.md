## 22. A Trail for the Summit

The river was lower than she remembered, and that was the first miracle of it.

Beatrix Dunmore stood at the edge of the water where the trail left Meridian Flats and watched the current move—clear now, or clear enough that you could see the stones on the bottom, the small silver flicker of fish holding in the eddies. A year ago this water had run the color of old milk, thick with tailings, carrying the syndicate's poison down through every canyon and every town like a secret told in a whisper too loud to keep. Now it ran bright. Not clean—not yet, not all the way—but honest. You could look into it and see what was there.

Behind her, the survey team was loading the mules. She could hear them without turning: the creak of pack frames, the soft argument between Okafor and the youngest of them, a girl named Pell who had signed on because her brother's farm sat two miles from the river and his well water had killed two of his horses before anyone understood why. There were six of them altogether. A hydrologist with sunburned forearms and a laugh like gravel. A geologist who talked to rocks as if they might answer. Two lab men with their glass vials packed in straw. And Wren, who had come back to the flats three weeks ago with his parole papers folded in his shirt pocket like a letter he hadn't decided whether to answer, and who had looked at her across the barroom of the Dry Creek Saloon and said, "I hear somebody needs a guide who knows where all the bodies are buried."

She had not laughed. But she had not said no.

"Ranger." Okafor came up beside her, squinting at the water the way he squinted at everything, as if it owed him an explanation. "You want to tell me again why we're walking up instead of riding? The toll bridge is open now. Company's gone. We could be at Split Rock by dark."

"Because the river doesn't care about your schedule," Beatrix said, "and neither do I. We go at its pace. That's the whole point of what we're doing."

He grunted. He was a good man, Okafor, patient in the way of people who had learned patience the hard way, but he still measured the world in schedules and supplies, in miles that could be logged and days that could be accounted for. She had been that way once. Five years of measuring her life in distance traveled, in ground put behind her, in the space between herself and everything she'd lost. She had thought if she walked far enough, fast enough, the past would fall behind like a country crossed.

It didn't work that way. The past wasn't behind you. It was underfoot, all the way up.

"All right," Okafor said. "At its pace."

They moved out at midmorning, six people and four mules strung along the bank, and Beatrix rode point out of habit and let the habit be a comfort rather than a cage. The canyon opened around them the way it always did, red walls going gold where the light struck them, shadow pooling blue in the narrows. Swallows stitched the air above the water. Somewhere upstream a hawk cried once and fell silent, and she found herself listening for it the way you listen for a voice you know.

Split Rock came up on the second day, and she made camp there deliberately, though there was better ground a mile on. The riders had come out of those shadows five years ago—or nearly five years ago, or however long ago it was; time had stopped being a thing she counted in years. She stood where Wren had stepped out of nowhere with a rifle already leveled, where she had bound him to a juniper and threatened to leave him for the vultures, and she tried to feel what she had felt then: the coiled readiness, the loneliness worn like armor, the certainty that everyone who came close wanted something and would take it if they could.

What she felt instead was the evening wind off the water and the smell of Okafor's coffee and Pell laughing at something one of the lab men had said, and the armor felt less like protection than like weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"You're smiling," Wren said beside her. He had come up quiet, the way he always did, though these days it seemed less like thieving and more like courtesy—an old skill repurposed. "I'd have bet money against that, once."

"Don't spend it yet."

"I never do." He looked out at the rock, at the water sliding past below. "I keep thinking I'll remember it worse than it was. This place. Most places I remember worse than they were." He turned his hands over, studying the palms as if checking for calluses that had healed. "This one I remember about right."

"That's growth," Beatrix said. "Or senility."

"One of those runs in my family." He glanced at her sideways, and in the last light his face was younger than it had any right to be, the thief's wariness burned down to something plainer. "The spring at the summit. You think it'll look different?"

"It already does. Everything does."

* * *

They climbed. The trail took them up through the canyon country she knew like the lines of her own hand—the waterhole where Wren had nearly drowned and she had hauled him out against her own judgment, the slot canyon where her father's camp had waited five years frozen in time, the badlands where they had run on foot after losing the horses, where Wren had taken a bullet graze shielding her from a rider's shot without stopping to decide. At each place she stopped the team and showed them what needed showing: where to read the water, where the old contamination had pooled in the stone, where the syndicate's tailings had leached into the tributaries for years while every town downstream drank the proof without knowing it.

Pell took notes until her pencil wore down to a stub. Okafor asked questions that were really objections, and then objections that were really questions, and somewhere along the way he stopped measuring the trip in miles too, though Beatrix doubted he'd ever admit it.

On the fourth night, in the Canyon of Echoes, Beatrix walked alone to the place where her father's camp had been. The canvas was gone now—she had taken the journal herself, and the rest had been collected as evidence, catalogued and boxed and carried down to a courthouse where it had helped hang a syndicate. But the fire ring was still there, blackened stones in a circle, and the flat shelf of rock where a man had slept his last nights believing he would go home to his daughter.

She sat down on the shelf and stayed a long time.

"He didn't drown," the barmaid had told her, in another life, in another town. It had taken her a year to understand what the woman meant—that drowning was just the story, the convenient lie, the thing the syndicate had printed on his death record the way they printed everything, in ink that looked official and meant nothing. Her father had died on this rock, or near enough to it, shot for knowing what the water carried. And she had spent five years running from a name because the name was all anyone had left of him, and she had thought erasing herself was grief, when really it was just grief with nowhere to go.

Now it had somewhere to go. It went into the testimony, into the verdicts, into the restoration crews that would work this watershed for a decade. It went into the survey she was leading, up the same trail, toward the same summit, not fleeing this time but guiding. Grief, it turned out, could be carried like water if you gave it a channel.

Wren found her there near midnight. He didn't say anything, just sat down at the edge of the fire ring with his bedroll and his silence, and after a while he said, "Cold night for sitting on a dead man's porch."

"He wouldn't mind. He liked company."

"Yeah," Wren said. "I know. I only worked for him eight months, but I know." He fed a twig into the dead fire ring, an old habit, then seemed to catch himself and set it aside. No fire tonight. Some things you did in the dark. "You know what I keep coming back to? The night we camped without fire, up high. When I told you why I kept your papers."

"You read them," Beatrix said. "And you understood what it cost to be erased."

"I read them," he agreed. "And I thought, here's a woman who built herself into a ghost so thoroughly that even her own name couldn't find her. And I thought, I know that architecture. I lived in it myself for eleven years." He was quiet a moment. "I'm not going to live in it anymore. I want you to know that. Whatever comes next. I'm done being a ghost."

Beatrix looked at him across the cold ring of stones, at the man who had stolen her identity and saved her life and testified against himself to help destroy the men who owned him, and she felt the strange, unsteady thing that had been growing in her since the prison gate a year ago—the thing she had refused to name because naming things had always felt like losing them.

"We should sleep," she said.

"Yeah," he said. But neither of them moved for a while, and the canyon held them both in its long stone echo, and above them the stars came out the way they always did, indifferent and enormous, and for the first time in her life Beatrix Dunmore did not feel small beneath them. She felt held.

* * *

Above the treeline the air went thin and the storm caught them on the second day, as storms did up here, arriving without negotiation. They sheltered under an overhang and waited it out, and Beatrix reread her father's journal by the gray light, though she no longer needed to. She knew it by heart now, every page, every entry. *Found what they're doing to the headwaters. If anything happens to me, Bea will know what to look for.* And underneath, in smaller script, almost hidden: *Promise kept, Sarah. Coming home clean.*

Coming home clean. Not rich. Not triumphant. Clean.

She closed the journal and watched the rain sheet off the overhang and understood, finally, at the top of the world with a team sleeping behind her and a man who used to be a thief sitting watch, that her father had never been asking her to avenge him. He had been asking her to finish the walk. To carry the truth up and carry it back down and give it away, the way you give away water, the way rivers do, holding nothing.

The storm broke at dawn and the sky came out scrubbed and enormous, and they climbed the last mile through wet grass and melting snow to the summit.

* * *

The spring was exactly where the map had said it would be, which no longer mattered, because the map was ash somewhere over the gorge, scattered on a wind that had also taken Voss-Hale. What mattered was the water itself, rising cold and clear out of the living rock, pooling in a basin no wider than a wagon, spilling over into the stream that would become the Silverhead River and run two hundred miles to the sea.

The syndicate's works were gone. Crews had come up in the spring and dismantled the tailings ponds, hauled out the machinery, broken the flumes and burned the shacks. Where the poison had been stored there was now raw earth, terraced and seeded, and already the first green was coming up through it—stubborn, provisional, alive. The hydrologist knelt at the basin and took his samples with hands that shook slightly, and when he straightened up he said, "Clean. As close to clean as mountain water gets. Give it ten years and nobody downstream will ever know what happened here."

"They'll know," Beatrix said. "That's the other half of the job."

She sent the team down to make camp below the treeline. This last stretch she wanted alone—or nearly alone. Wren understood without being told. He stood at the edge of the basin with her for a moment, looking at the water, at the view that ran away south in ridges and haze, and then he touched her shoulder, briefly, and went down the trail, and she heard his footsteps fade into the sound of the spring.

Alone, then. Truly alone, for the first time in five years, and it didn't hurt.

She took her father's compass out of her coat.

It had been in the evidence boxes for a year, released to her with the verdict, the brass worn bright on the north side where his thumb had rubbed it for thirty years of walking. She had carried it up the whole trail without deciding what she meant to do with it, and now, standing at the source of the river he had died for, she understood that she had known all along.

She crouched at the edge of the basin and held the compass in both hands. The needle swung, settled, pointed north, steady as truth. Somewhere downriver were towns that drank this water, farms that would grow again, a girl named Pell whose brother's wells would run sweet within the decade. Somewhere south was a courthouse with her name in its records, her real name, spoken aloud in daylight, belonging to her again. Somewhere below her, walking down through the wet grass, was a man learning how to be a person instead of a fugitive, and learning it badly and stubbornly and honestly.

Her father had carried this compass up this trail and never carried it home. She could keep it. Keep it the way she had once kept everything—locked, guarded, measured, hers. Or she could do what the river did.

Beatrix Dunmore set the compass on the flat stone at the spring's edge, needle pointing north, brass catching the morning sun, and left it there.

"For Dad," she said aloud, to the water, to the mountain, to the man who had promised to come home clean. "Trail's finished. Go ahead and rest."

The spring said nothing, the way springs do, and kept rising anyway.

She stood a moment longer, letting the wind move through her, feeling the altitude press thin and bright against her chest. Then she turned and started down.

The trail unwound below her in switchbacks through the new green, and her team was waiting at the treeline with coffee and bad jokes, and Wren stood a little apart from them, watching for her, and when she reached him he didn't ask what she'd done up there. He just fell into step beside her, matching her pace, and they walked down together through country that was learning, slowly, how to heal.

She had spent five years measuring her life in miles—ground gained, distance kept, the length of the road between herself and everything else. Walking down now, with the river running beside her toward towns that would thrive on it, she found the old arithmetic quietly failing. What she felt could not be counted in miles. It was slower than that. Larger. It was the green coming up through the poisoned earth. It was a name spoken without flinching. It was seasons, maybe. Seasons, and the people you walked them with.

Halfway down, Wren glanced over at her. "So," he said. "What's next? For the great Beatrix Dunmore, restored ranger, scourge of syndicates, terror of directors?"

She considered it. The valley was opening below them, gold in the afternoon light, the river threading through it silver and sure. There would be work—a decade of it, courts and crews and watersheds, years of showing people what needed showing. There would be winters, and letters, and a cabin somewhere with a stove that drew well. There would be this man beside her, probably, if she was brave enough to ask, and she was beginning to suspect she was.

"Next," she said, "we go home."

And the river, hearing her, ran on.
