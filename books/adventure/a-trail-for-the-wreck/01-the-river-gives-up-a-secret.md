## 1. The River Gives Up a Secret

The Vell ran brown that morning, the color of old tea left too long in the pot, and Dominic Yarrow stood on the deck of the *Marrow's Luck* with a sounding line in his hands and eleven days of wages owed to him and no reason to believe he'd see any of it.

"Depth," called Brannick from the wheelhouse, a squat man with a voice like gravel poured through a tin funnel.

"Fourteen feet," Dominic answered, hauling the lead back up. "Bottom's soft. Silt over gravel."

Brannick grunted, which was his way of saying the information had been received and filed in whatever drawer he kept such things. The dredge behind them groaned and shuddered, its chain of buckets dipping into the river and coming up heavy with mud, sand, the occasional drowned log. The survey was simple work, insultingly simple for a man who'd once held certification to grade mainline rail across three provinces: run the line, sound the channel, mark where the dredge could bite and where it would foul. It paid four silver a day and a bunk that smelled of bilgewater and onions.

Dominic had taken worse jobs for less. He'd taken this one because the syndicate's collectors had learned his name in Corren's Landing, and Corren's Landing was a day's ride downstream of where the *Marrow's Luck* worked her slow way along the lower Vell. Distance was a currency now. He spent it carefully.

He was thirty-one years old and everything he owned fit in a canvas bag: two shirts, a set of drafting instruments wrapped in oilcloth, a letter of reference signed by a man who was probably dead by now, and a copy of his grandfather's engineering folio, the only inheritance anyone had ever offered him. The folio was worth nothing on the open market. Every railway Karvel had ever built belonged now to the Consortium, and every engineer who'd drawn those lines had been struck from the rolls when the nation itself was struck from the maps. *Dissolved*, the treaties said. Nobody had ever explained to Dominic what a country dissolved *into*. It seemed to him the way sugar dissolves into tea — still there, everywhere, just no longer permitted to be seen as itself.

"Yarrow." Brannick again. "You're drifting. Sound it again."

Dominic dropped the lead. Sixteen feet. The channel was deepening here, swinging east around a bluff of red clay where the current chewed at the bank and dropped what it stole into the deep water below. He'd noticed it yesterday — an eddy that turned against the season, circling in a slow deliberate way, like something stirring a pot.

The dredge bucket came up and swung out over the sorting table, and the mud that spilled across it was different. Darker. Richer. And in it, glinting wet, a corner of metal.

"Hold the chain," Dominic said.

Brannick leaned out of the wheelhouse. "We're on schedule, Yarrow."

"Hold the *chain*."

Something in his voice made the dredge hand — a gap-toothed boy called Pell — actually throw the brake. The bucket hung swaying. Dominic climbed onto the sorting table, boots sinking into the muck, and dug with both hands.

It was a chest. Lead-sealed, the size of a sea chest but heavier than its size should have allowed, crusted with river clay except where the bucket's teeth had scored bright wounds in the metal. Stamped into the lid, beneath a century of silt, was a crest: two stars, one above the other, joined by a single line.

Dominic knew that crest. Every child in the river provinces knew it, the way they knew the shape of their own hands. It was on the old bridges. It was cut into the lock gates upstream, worn nearly smooth. It was the mark of Karvel, the nation that had built the great works and then, in a single parliamentary season forty years ago, stopped existing.

"That's not salvage," Pell said nervously. "That's grave goods, maybe. We should put it back."

"We should absolutely not put it back," said Brannick, who had come down from the wheelhouse with the unhurried gait of a man who smelled money. "Anything in the channel belongs to the charter holder. Charter holder is me. Yarrow — pry it open."

"It's lead-sealed," Dominic said. "You don't seal a chest in lead unless you never want it opened. Or unless you want it to last."

"Then it'll last until we open it. Crowbar's in the tool locker."

They opened it on the aft deck, in the flat gray light of late afternoon, with Pell hovering and Brannick standing too close. The lead seal sheared away in strips. The hinges, protected under all that metal and mud, moved without a sound — oiled, recently, impossibly, or else made of something that did not know how to rust.

Inside, on top, lay a map.

It was hand-drawn on linen-backed paper, and it was beautiful in the way that only working drawings are beautiful — every line purposeful, every elevation noted, contour hatching so fine it looked like weather. It showed the upper Vell. Not the surveyed upper Vell, the tame one in the Consortium atlases that ended at the last lock with a polite printed note reading *navigation impracticable beyond this point*, but the real one: the gorge country, the headwaters, the passes. Rivers that appeared on no other chart Dominic had ever seen braided together like veins in a wrist. And in the corner, in a small precise hand:

*Surveyed and drawn by E. Venn, Engineer, Karveli Corps of Works. For whoever is meant to find this.*

Below the map, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age, were books. Ledgers. Dozens of pages of columns in faded iron-gall ink — names, sums, dates, interest calculated in a neat clerk's hand. Debts. Owed by whom, Dominic couldn't yet tell, and to whom, and for what. The earliest entries predated the Dissolution by twenty years.

"Paper," Brannick said dismissively, though his eyes didn't agree with his mouth. "River gives up a dead man's paperwork. There's a fortune in scrap brass on that wreck somewhere and you're mooning over receipts."

But he took the map when Dominic wasn't holding it, and studied it longer than a man interested in nothing would study anything, and gave it back without comment.

* * *

That night, moored against the dredge's muddy pontoon with a lamp hung from the boom, Dominic sat with the ledgers while Pell slept and Brannick pretended to sleep and the river talked to itself in the dark.

He told himself he was reading them for pay. That was the arrangement he'd offer Brannick in the morning — I can read the old script, I can make sense of the accounts, my share for the work. It was even true. But it wasn't the reason he didn't stop.

The ledgers were debts, yes. But they were structured like an engineering report, which was the first strange thing. Each entry carried not just a sum but a specification: *for 400 tons rolled rail, delivered to Cut Nine; for the labor of 60 men, winter rate; for blasting powder, the good kind, not the Consortium kind.* This was not a merchant's book. This was the account of a construction project — a vast one, decades long — recorded debt by debt, payment by payment, in a hand that grew more hurried as the pages turned, as though the writer were racing something.

And every few pages, in the margin, small annotations in a second hand. Corrections. Confirmations. Once, beside an entry for *the diversion works, final phase*, a single word in that second hand, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page beneath:

*Remember.*

Dominic turned pages until the lamp burned low, and the river breathed against the hull, and somewhere in the middle of the third ledger he stopped breathing.

There was a name in the debtor column. Not a company. A man's name, written in full, the way engineers signed off on their own work because they intended to stand behind it:

*A. Yarrow.*

His grandfather's initials. His grandfather's name. Aurel Yarrow, who had died before Dominic was born, who existed for him as a folio of bridge calculations and a photograph of a stern man in a collarless shirt standing in front of a viaduct. Aurel Yarrow, whose name appeared nowhere in any surviving record because the Consortium had struck the Karveli rolls clean — Dominic had looked, once, young and hopeful, and been told politely at the provincial archive that no such engineer was registered, that perhaps he was thinking of someone else.

Here he was. Page after page after page. Aurel Yarrow, contracting for steel. Aurel Yarrow, drawing drafts on the Corps of Works. Aurel Yarrow, in the final ledger, signing — the signature unmistakable, the same forward-leaning *A* Dominic had practiced copying as a boy — receipt of *the whole of the remaining obligation, to be discharged to the bearer upon completion.*

Discharged to the bearer.

Dominic sat very still. The lamp hissed. Out in the dark the eddy off the red-clay bluff turned and turned, patient as it had been patient for however many years this chest had waited in the silt — except it hadn't waited, some cold part of his mind insisted, chests don't drift into eddies, chests are *placed*, and lead seals don't survive rivers, and hinges don't turn like that unless someone wanted them to turn, soon, by the right hands—

"You've gone white," Brannick said, from the shadow of the wheelhouse. He hadn't been sleeping after all. "What is it? What's it worth?"

"I don't know," Dominic said, and it was the truest thing he'd said in weeks.

Because here was what he knew, the thing every broke man on the lower river knew without being taught: the creditors' syndicate did not chase debts the way other men chased money. They chased *paper*. Provenance. They bought up obligations from dead estates and vanished firms, and wherever a debt's history got complicated — where records burned, where nations dissolved — the syndicate arrived soon after, polite and patient, and made the complications disappear. He'd assumed they hounded him because of his father's defaulted loans, small ugly sums, ordinary greed.

But his grandfather's name was in this chest. Written in a dead nation's hand. An obligation, *to be discharged to the bearer* — and Dominic was, God help him, the bearer.

The lamp guttered. Downriver, far off, a whistle sounded — one of the syndicate's packet boats ran the night lines, and Dominic had heard that particular two-note call often enough lately to know it in his sleep. It was miles away. It was heading upstream.

So was the chest's road, if the map meant anything. Upstream, past the last lock, into the gorge country where navigation became impracticable and nations went to be buried.

Dominic closed the ledger and held it a moment against his chest, feeling its weight, the weight of a thing that had crossed forty years of dark water to find him specifically, and understood that he was no longer hiding from the syndicate.

He was carrying something they wanted.

"Brannick," he said quietly. "How much to buy out my survey contract?"

The old man smiled without warmth. "Now that," he said, "depends entirely on what you just pulled out of my river."
