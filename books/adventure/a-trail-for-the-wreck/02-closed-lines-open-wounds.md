## 2. Closed Lines, Open Wounds

The chest sat in the corner of Dominic's rented room for three days before he could bring himself to open it again.

He had told himself it was caution. The dredge survey had ended, the pay was spent down to a few coppers and a stub of pencil, and the landlord at the Weirhouse Inn had begun leaving his door unlocked on purpose—a courtesy that was also a message. A man with nothing to steal has no reason to lock anything. A man with a lead-sealed chest stamped with a dead nation's crest is another matter entirely.

But it wasn't only caution. It was the name.

He'd seen it on the third page of the ledger, in a clerk's copperplate hand so fine it might have been engraved: **Yarrow, E. — Chief of Line, Karveli Northern Extension.** Below it, columns of figures. Below those, a signature. Not his grandfather's signature—he had never seen that, not once in his life—but a signature made by a hand that had learned its letters in the same schoolrooms, held its pen at the same angle. He knew because he held his own pen that way, and his father had before him, and neither of them had ever been able to say where the habit came from.

On the fourth day, with rain ticking against the shutters and the river running brown and swollen below the town, Dominic cleared the table, set a lamp at its center, and opened the ledger to the beginning.

It was a debt book. That much he had understood from the first night. But debts are never just numbers; they are stories told in arithmetic, and this one began sixty years back, in a hand steadier than any clerk's—the engineer's own.

*Item: 4,200 rails, forged Vellside, delivered to Mile 118. Payment deferred against completion bonus.*

*Item: Passage for 300 laborers, contracted through the Consortium office at Greyharbor. Wages owed: see Schedule C.*

*Item: One bridge, iron, over the Vell narrows. Cost overrun borne personally by undersigned.*

Page after page. Rails and wages and bridges, timber and blasting powder and the price of feeding a thousand men through a winter in the passes. And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, entries that did not belong in any railway ledger:

*Item: One hundred forty names struck from the rolls at the Consortium's instruction. Obligations transferred to the dissolved estate.*

*Item: Village of Sarn-Karvel, requisitioned. Compensation: none recorded.*

*Item: By order of the Provisional Authority, all maps of the upper line surrendered. Receipts withheld.*

Dissolved. There was the word again, the one that appeared in every history he'd ever read about Karvel, always in italics or quotation marks, always unexplained, as if the nation had been a sugar cube left in tea. *Karvel, dissolved following the Consolidation Accords.* Dissolved into what? Into whom? No atlas in the Weirhouse's common room showed it. No boundary commission had ever ruled on it. The railways were real—Dominic had surveyed their bones for years, the dead spurs and rusted junctions that paid his bread—and every one of them ran toward a border that appeared on no map, drawn along a river that officially had no headwaters.

His grandfather had built those lines. His father had never said so. His father had said almost nothing at all, except once, drunk on festival wine when Dominic was fourteen: *We came downriver. Before you were born we came downriver, and we don't go back up.* He had died still owing money to men whose faces he never described, and Dominic had grown up assuming the debt was ordinary—the kind that comes of bad harvests and worse luck.

Now he turned the pages with hands that weren't quite steady, and understood that the debt was not ordinary, had never been ordinary, and was not even, strictly speaking, a debt. It was an accounting. Someone had kept track. Someone had written down every rail, every wage, every village, every hundred-forty names—and then sealed the whole account in lead and given it to the river, like a letter posted to whoever might be strong enough to pull it out.

*Yarrow, E.* Elias Yarrow. Dominic said the name aloud, alone in the room, and it sounded like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known his family carried.

* * *

The collector came on the sixth day, in the hour between lamplighting and supper, when the inn's common room was half full and everyone in it was minding his own business with great care.

Dominic knew him before the door closed behind him—not by sight but by the way the room changed. Conversation didn't stop; it thinned, the way a crowd parts around something without appearing to move. The man was ordinary in every visible particular: grey coat, good boots, a face you would forget while looking at it. He carried a leather document case and nothing else. That was the frightening part. Men who carry knives want you to know it. This man wanted Dominic to understand that whatever instruments existed, they were elsewhere, patient, and already paid for.

"Master Yarrow." He set the case on the table across from Dominic without asking, and sat. "My name is Corvess. I represent certain parties with an interest in river salvage."

"I don't do salvage anymore."

"No. You did one job." Corvess smiled without warmth, the way a door closes without slamming. "The dredge contract off the lower Vell. You pulled up more than silt. I'll come directly to it, because I respect your time and mine is valuable too: a chest bearing the Karveli double-star was raised from the riverbed near Mile 9 under your supervision. Its contents are the property of my principals. Its provenance is a matter they wish very much to have resolved quietly."

"The chest went back in the river," Dominic said. "Mud shift, chains rot, things come up and go down. Ask the dredge master."

"I have. He remembers differently. He also has a daughter in a debtors' ward in Greyharbor, which makes him a cooperative witness." Corvess opened the case and produced, of all things, a paper of tea, setting it on the table between them like a gift. "I'm not here to threaten you, Master Yarrow. Threats are inefficient. I'm here to make you an offer of professional courtesy. Return what you found, tell no one, and the outstanding obligations against your family name—which I should mention total a sum your grandfather's estate has been accruing interest on for six decades—will be reviewed favorably."

"Reviewed."

"Favorably." He let the word sit. "You've read the ledger, I think. You have the look of a man who has been reading accounts all week and sleeping badly. Then you know the sums are real. Your grandfather signed for them. Every page. The Consortium honored its side; the estate did not. Debts pass to blood, Master Yarrow. It's an old principle and a sound one."

Dominic looked at the tea. Looked at the man's hands—clean, unhurried, folded now atop the document case like a cat's paws. Sixty years of interest on rails and wages and a hundred forty struck names, compounded by clerks who had never seen the river. "And if I can't pay?"

"Then the principals will recover assets of equivalent value." Corvess rose, retrieved his tea with two fingers, and inclined his head with genuine, chilling politeness. "You're an engineer. You understand collateral. Think about what you're holding, and what it weighs."

He left coins on the table for the tea he hadn't drunk, and the room exhaled behind him.

* * *

Dominic didn't sleep. He sat with the ledger until the lamp burned dry, then sat longer in the dark, listening to the river.

The arithmetic was simple and he hated how simple it was. Give them the chest, and the weight lifted—not just the collectors, but the whole drowned inheritance: the struck names, the requisitioned villages, the grandfather who had signed his family into servitude building a nation that was then erased from the map like a chalk line licked by a thumb. Let it stay dissolved. Let the river keep its secrets. Buy back a small life on the closed lines, anonymous, solvent, safe.

That was the offer. That was what *favorably* meant.

But he kept coming back to the entries that weren't debts. *One hundred forty names struck from the rolls.* He had spent the evening trying to imagine what kind of bookkeeping strikes names instead of recording payments, and there was only one answer that fit: a list of men whose wages stopped being owed because they had stopped being counted as living. And *all maps of the upper line surrendered*—surrendered to whom, and why, if the line was merely unfinished? You don't confiscate maps of a project. You confiscate maps of a crime scene.

His grandfather had known. That was the thing Dominic couldn't get past, the thing sitting on his chest heavier than any creditor. Elias Yarrow hadn't just signed the debts. He had copied the map—Dominic was sure of it now, sure from the drafting hand, the same slanted numerals, the same way of drawing water—and hidden it inside the account of everything taken, and sent the whole testament downriver to be found by whoever carried the name. Not a treasure. A summons. *Here is what we built. Here is what they took. Here is where it happened. Come and count.*

Inheritance, his father had taught him without meaning to, was a debt you couldn't discharge, only carry. Dominic had thought he understood that. He had carried poverty and disgrace and his father's silence, and called it carrying.

This was heavier. This was a hundred and forty names, and a valley with no map, and a word—*dissolved*—that everyone used and no one explained, because explaining it would mean admitting what dissolution was done with.

Toward dawn the rain thickened and the river rose, loud against the pilings below the window, and Dominic made himself one promise that felt less like courage than like surrender to a fact already true: he was not going to give them the chest. Whatever else he decided—whether he ran, whether he fought, whether he went upriver into a country that officially did not exist—the ledger was not going back into their hands to be balanced into silence.

Which meant, he realized as the first grey light came, that he needed help. Someone who could read the map's cipher, someone who knew the upper river, someone who wanted the truth of it as badly as he now feared it.

He was still staring at the map's margin—those strange paired glyphs marching along the gorge like a sentence waiting to be translated—when he heard footsteps on the stair, light and deliberate, stopping outside his door.

A knock. Two raps, precise as a surveyor's chain.

Through the wood, a woman's voice, low and certain:

"You have something of my father's, Master Yarrow. I've come a long way, and I'd rather not discuss it in the hall."
