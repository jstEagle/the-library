## 3. The Cartographer's Daughter

Ilsa Venn had been watching Dominic Yarrow for six days, and in that time she had learned three things about him: he drank cheap and slow, he sat with his back to the wall but his eyes on the door, and he checked the chest's hiding place twice a night like a man checking on a sleeping child he didn't trust.

The third thing was the one that told her how to take him.

She waited until the fourth check. The Gull's Rest tavern in Corren's Landing emptied at midnight, its lamplighter long gone home, its proprietor snoring behind the bar with a bottle for a pillow. Dominic slipped out the back into the alley where the rain had turned the cobbles to black glass, and Ilsa counted to two hundred before she followed.

He knelt at the base of a drainage culvert, working loose a stone with his fingers, and when his hand came out of the dark holding the oilcloth bundle, she stepped off the wall where she'd been perched and let her boots ring on the wet stone.

"Don't run," she said. "You'll drop it."

He didn't run. He rose slowly, the bundle pressed to his chest, and turned to face her with the particular stillness of a man who has already calculated every exit and found them all closed. In the thin light from the tavern's rear window, his face was all angles and exhaustion.

"You've been following me since Marrow Quay," he said.

"Since before that." She let him see the satchel strap across her body, the rolled papers it held. "Since your dredge boat put in at Tallow's Bend and you asked the harbormaster about Karveli river marks. That was careless of you."

"And you're not careless?"

"I'm careful enough to know what you're carrying." She held out her hand, palm up. Rain ran off her fingers. "Show me the map, Yarrow."

His grip tightened on the bundle. "It's mine."

"It's *mine*," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, which she hated, which she had sworn would not happen, and so she made her voice flat again, cold as the river. "My father drew it. Every line of it. Aldous Venn, chief cartographer of the Upper Vell Survey, commissioned year eleven of the Consolidation. He spent nineteen years on that map, Yarrow. Nineteen years, and then he went up the river to finish it, and he never came back."

Dominic said nothing. The rain ticked against the culvert stone. Somewhere down the alley a dog turned over in its sleep.

"They told me he drowned," Ilsa went on. "They told everyone he drowned. But my father swam the Serrano Rapids twice for survey work when I was a girl. He read water the way priests read scripture. He did not drown on a river he knew better than any living man." She took a step closer. "So either the map killed him, or someone did. And either way, I have a right to see it."

"You have a claim," Dominic said carefully. "That's not the same as a right."

"No," she agreed. "Rights are what people with power call their claims. I learned that from the syndicate men who came asking about my father's papers after the funeral. They were very polite. They offered to buy everything he'd left. When I refused, they stopped being polite." She watched his face change—watched the recognition move through it like a fish under ice. "You've met them."

"I've met collectors."

"Then you know they don't stop. You know what happens to people who hold things they want." She let that sit. Then: "The tavern. Ten minutes. You can keep the chest between us like a hostage if it makes you feel safer. But you'll show me the map, because you need me, and by the end of ten minutes you'll know it."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. His jaw worked; his eyes went past her shoulder to the mouth of the alley, the way out he wasn't taking. Then he nodded once, curt, and walked back inside without waiting for her.

* * *

The Gull's Rest at midnight was a country of shadows and one guttering lamp. Dominic set the chest on the table between them and opened it, and Ilsa felt her breath go strange in her throat.

She had imagined this moment for years—the map, her father's map—and imagination had prepared her for everything except the reality of his hand. There it was in the linework: the way he doubled his contour strokes at steep ground until they looked like harp strings, the little hooked arrows he used for current direction, the marginal notes in his cramped, slanted script. *Fording unreliable above the third lock. Karveli masons built better than we deserved.* She touched the paper and her fingers were shaking and she did not care if he saw.

"This is the upper gorge," she said. "Past the Serrano. Nobody surveys past the Serrano. It's closed water—consortium edict, twenty years old."

"Your father surveyed it."

"My father died doing it." She traced the river's course upward, past locks drawn in loving detail, past a valley marked only with a symbol she hadn't seen since childhood—a double star, inked small and precise. "Where did you get this?"

"A dredge. The lower Vell gives up whatever the current takes. This came up in a lead-sealed chest with a ledger inside." He hesitated, and she saw him decide something. "The ledger has my grandfather's name in it. Every page. Debts owed to the Karveli railway works—wages, materials, land bonds. All of it unpaid. All of it transferred, somehow, to the syndicate after the Dissolution."

"The Dissolution." She said the word the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "Nobody will even say what it was. A nation doesn't dissolve, Yarrow. Nations are dissolved. By someone. For something."

"That's what I intend to find out."

"Do you." She leaned back. "Or do you intend to find out whether your family's debts can be bought quiet? Because those are different journeys, and I've watched men start the first and finish the second."

Something flickered in his face—shame, quickly buried. She was glad she'd put it there. Trust between strangers should be earned with discomfort, her father used to say. Comfortable alliances are the ones that break.

"Tell me why I need you," Dominic said.

Ilsa pulled the roll of papers from her satchel and spread them beside the map: her own work, years of it. Soundings copied from her father's field books. Pass profiles. Notes on the lock system compiled from fragments—consortium manifests, an old pilot's drunken reminiscences, a single photograph of the third gate taken by a smuggler who'd charged her a month's wages for it.

"Because the map is a drawing and the river is a fact," she said. "Your grandfather could read rails and grades and load tolerances. My father could read terrain—the way water cuts, where rock lies shallow, where a hillside is lying to you. I inherited that. And the upper Vell is a liar, Yarrow. The monsoon turns it into something else entirely. Eleven weeks from now, maybe less, the passes close and stay closed until spring. Anyone up there when that happens is up there until spring—with the syndicate, if they follow us, and without supplies, because nothing moves in the wet season."

"So we go now."

"We go now, and we go correctly, or we don't arrive." She tapped the map, at the locks. "And there's this. Look at the gates. Look at the carvings notation—my father marked cipher symbols at each lock. He thought the locks themselves carried a message. He never decoded it. Maybe he died close to it." Her voice caught again and this time she let it. "I'm not offering you partnership out of sentiment. I'm offering it because alone, you're a man with a document the syndicate wants erased, and documents get erased along with their holders. Together, we're harder to kill and smarter about it."

Dominic studied her work in silence for a long moment. She watched him read it the way she'd watched other engineers read things—with his finger hovering just above the paper, never touching, following the logic of it like a route.

"Your soundings are good," he said finally. Grudging. "Better than good. Where did you train?"

"I trained under a dead man who couldn't hold a commission because of the nation that employed him stopped existing." She shrugged. "The consortium wouldn't license a Karveli surveyor's daughter. So I licensed myself. I've been taking contract work on the closed lines for six years. Small jobs. Quiet ones."

"Closed lines." He almost smiled, and it transformed his face briefly into someone younger, someone less cornered. "That's my trade too. Or it was."

"Then you understand the work. We read what everyone else agreed to forget." She closed her hands flat on the table, on the space between the map and her father's notes. "Here are my terms. Equal shares in whatever the ledger proves worth proving. The map stays with both of us or neither—I don't care which, so long as neither of us can sell out the other without losing it too. And when we find what happened to my father, I want it named. Publicly. Whatever it costs."

"And if what it costs is everything?"

"Then I'll have spent it on something finished." She held his gaze. "You know what I mean. Don't pretend you don't. Men like us don't want treasure, Yarrow. We want to complete something. Your grandfather left a debt. My father left a map with no destination reached. I am tired of inheriting unfinished things."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and far off, thunder muttered over the hills like something clearing its throat.

Dominic extended his hand across the chest. "Equal shares," he said. "And nobody sells anybody."

She shook it. His grip was dry and hard, a working hand, and she noted that he kept his eyes on hers through the whole of it, which was more honesty than she'd expected and exactly as much as she required.

* * *

They were still bent over the map an hour later, arguing happily about the third lock's approach grade—he insisted the original survey must have erred by four degrees, she insisted her father never erred—when the tavern door opened without a knock.

Two men came in dripping. They wore oilskins of expensive cut and carried themselves with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the weather. The taller one scanned the room, found the lamp, found them, and smiled with no warmth whatsoever.

"Engineer Yarrow," he said pleasantly, crossing the floor. "And company. How fortunate. My principal dislikes sending second letters."

Ilsa's hand found her knife under the table. Beside her, she felt Dominic go very still, the stillness of a man hearing a bridge groan under weight it was never rated to carry.

"We leave at dawn," she murmured, not moving her lips. "There's a barge. Smuggler. Teodor. He charges in secrets instead of coin."

The tall man stopped at their table and laid a card upon the map itself, upon her father's linework, as though he had every right.

"Now then," he said. "Let's talk about provenance."
