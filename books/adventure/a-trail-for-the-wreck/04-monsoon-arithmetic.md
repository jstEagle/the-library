## 4. Monsoon Arithmetic

The almanac in the harbor office at Corren's Landing was a foot wide and printed on paper the color of old teeth, and Dominic read it three times before he let himself believe the arithmetic.

*Monsoon landfall: eleven weeks, four days.*

He copied the figure onto the back of his survey stub, then did the harder sum. The upper Vell narrowed to gorge country above Karvel's Reach, and the passes there—the Venn Passes, though Ilsa never called them that—closed when the first sustained rains swelled the tributaries. Ten weeks. Maybe nine if the weather turned mean early, which it always did in the years the fishing was bad, and the fishing this year had been terrible.

So: ten weeks to travel a river that honest boats didn't sail past the Reach, carrying a map drawn by a dead man from a dead country, pursued by men who asked polite questions with knives.

Dominic folded the stub into his coat and went to find Ilsa.

She was where he'd left her, at the end of the fish-market pier, sitting cross-legged with her father's atlas open across her knees like a woman reading scripture. She'd been at it since dawn. She read maps the way other people prayed—not for comfort, but because silence without them was unbearable.

"Ten weeks," Dominic said. "Maybe less."

She didn't look up. "I know. I've known since we came off the dredge."

"You could have said."

"I wanted to see how long you'd take." She turned a page. "You're slower than my father would have been."

"He'd have taken one look at those passes and gone home."

"My father never went home. That was rather the point of him." She closed the atlas and finally looked up, and Dominic saw what he'd been seeing more of each day: not the prickly stranger from the tavern ambush but someone sharpening herself against a deadline, honed and impatient. "There are three ways up. The mail packet goes as far as Serrano Bend, but it stops running in six weeks and it's syndicate-chartered—the captain will have our descriptions before we board. The mountain road is nine days' walk through country where every toll-keeper sells information to whoever pays last. Or—"

"Or we find someone dishonest," Dominic said.

"Someone *specifically* dishonest." She stood, brushing off her skirt, and nodded upriver toward the timber yards. "There's a barge moored below the tannery. Black hull, no name painted, cargo manifest that says 'agricultural implements' and smells like nothing agricultural at all. The man who runs it is called Teodor. He runs the Reach twice a season. My father used him."

Dominic felt the familiar cold settle behind his ribs. "Your father trusted a smuggler."

"My father *paid* a smuggler. Trust is different." She shouldered her pack. "Come and be charming. You're better at it than you think, and worse at it than you believe."

* * *

Teodor turned out to be a broad, gray-bearded man mending net in the shadow of his own wheelhouse, with hands like knotted rope and eyes that catalogued everything and committed to nothing. He listened to Ilsa's proposal without interrupting, which Dominic learned quickly was not agreement—it was inventory.

"Passage to the head of navigation," she said. "Two berths, discretion, speed."

"Passage I can sell you," Teodor said. "Discretion is included. Speed costs extra, and extra isn't coin."

"We don't have coin worth your trouble," Dominic admitted.

"No," Teodor agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Which is why we're still talking." He set down the net and looked at Dominic directly for the first time. "I charge in secrets. One now, as deposit. One at Serrano Bend. One at journey's end. And they have to be true secrets—yours, not bought or borrowed. I can tell the difference. Everyone who deals in secrets learns to tell the difference."

"That's an absurd price," Dominic said.

"It's the only price I take. Coin gets spent. Secrets keep." Teodor smiled without warmth. "And a secret given freely tells me more about a person than any ledger ever could. Ask yourself what yours will say about you."

Dominic glanced at Ilsa. She gave him nothing—she'd already paid her deposit, apparently, somewhere he hadn't witnessed, and her face said she wasn't going to make it easier for him.

So he sat down on a coil of rope, which surprised all three of them including himself, and gave Teodor the truth he'd told no one: that he hadn't lost his engineer's license to incompetence or drink, whatever the trade journals said, but because he'd signed a safety report condemning a bridge on the Marrow Line, and the consortium had buried the report and buried him with it, and he had let them—had taken the disgrace quietly, taken the small settlements, taken the slow erasure—because fighting meant poverty with witnesses instead of poverty alone.

"I could have burned them down," he finished. "I had the figures. I chose to survive instead. That's my secret. It doesn't flatter me."

Teodor studied him for a long moment. "No," he said. "It doesn't. But it's true, and it's yours, and it tells me you'll pay the rest." He spat over the rail, a formality, a signature. "We cast off at slack water tomorrow. Be here or don't."

* * *

They cast off in fog so thick the banks existed only as sound—the slap of water, the cough of a dog, the far iron groan of the Reach locks waking. Dominic stood at the bow while the black hull slid upstream, and for the first time since hauling the chest from the river he felt something that wasn't dread.

Momentum. It was a dangerous gift. He knew that. But the river was carrying them toward the thing itself now, toward the source of both the water and the debt, and every mile put the collectors' polite questions further astern.

He made the mistake of saying so.

"Don't," Ilsa said, coming to stand beside him. She nodded back at the fog they'd left. "You think we're ahead of them. We're not."

"The collector in Corren's was two days behind us—"

"Was. Past tense. He knew about the dredge contract before we did; I checked with the harbormaster's clerk. Men like that don't chase. They *position*." She tapped the rail. "If I were the syndicate, I wouldn't send anyone after us at all. I'd simply make sure the passes close with us on the wrong side of them. Let the monsoon do the work. Rivers are cheaper than knives."

Dominic stared at her. "Then why book passage at all? Why let us run?"

"Because they want to see where we go. Who we meet. Whether the chest has friends upstream." Her voice dropped. "Or because they want us to lead them to the valley, if there is a valley, so they can finish what the railway started. Either way—" she met his eyes— "we're not fleeing them, Dominic. We're being *escorted*. The only question is whether we're clever enough to use the escort."

The fog thinned as morning came up gray-gold, and the river widened, and the first hills of the middle Vell rose out of the haze like animals deciding whether to stand. Somewhere behind them, Dominic understood, someone was keeping their accounts. Somewhere ahead, the river was keeping its own.

* * *

Teodor found him that evening at the stern, where Dominic was checking their position against the chest-map by lamplight—the old Karveli script along the margins, the double-star crest stamped faintly in the corner, the contour lines drawn by a hand that had clearly walked every yard of them.

"May I?" Teodor didn't wait for permission. He looked at the map for a long time, and something moved through his face that Dominic had never seen there—not greed, not calculation. Recognition. Grief, maybe. The kind that has been carried so long it has become furniture.

"Where did you get this?" Teodor asked quietly.

"The river gave it up."

"The river gives up what it's given." He touched the crest with one blunt finger, careful as a man touching a wound. "My grandmother was Karveli. From the lock towns. She used to sing songs in a language my mother forbade in the house, because speaking it got people disappeared, even after the dissolution. Even after." He straightened. "I run this stretch because of her. I carry things upstream because of her. You'd call it smuggling. I call it mail."

"Mail to whom?"

"To whoever's left to receive it." Teodor looked at him, and the merchant's blankness was gone entirely, and what was underneath was older and much more frightening. "That map says there's still someone home. Do you understand what that means? Forty years, and the world believes that valley is empty rock and bad water. If you're right—if the chest is right—then the dissolution wasn't just a theft. It was a lie with a body count, and the bodies are still breathing."

"And the syndicate?"

"The syndicate writes the lie down fresh every year and calls it history." Teodor gathered himself, and the blankness returned like a shutter closing. "Second secret comes due at Serrano Bend. Think about what you'll pay. Make it count."

He paused at the companionway, silhouetted against the lamp glow.

"One more thing, engineer. The mail packet we passed at dusk—the syndicate-chartered one, the one that shouldn't be running this late in the season." His voice was flat. "It turned upstream half a mile behind us. It's running dark, no lamps. Whatever you and the girl decide you're doing—you're doing it in front of an audience."

Above the black water, the first stars were out, indifferent and cold. Dominic folded the map away and did the arithmetic again, differently now.

Ten weeks to the passes. Eleven to the rains.

And somewhere in the dark behind them—or ahead, which was worse—a ship with no lights, keeping pace.
