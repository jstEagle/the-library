## 5. What the Locks Remember

Ilsa Venn had spent her whole life learning to read paper the way other people read faces, and the map had been lying to her for three days.

Not lying exactly—her father had never lied to her, not even dead—but withholding. The cipher marks along the upper river's course, the little hooked glyphs she'd assumed were surveyor's shorthand for soundings or grade changes, kept repeating in a pattern that made no sense for a working chart. She'd copied them onto a scrap of paper and turned them over and over in her fingers like a coin that wouldn't spend, while Teodor's barge groaned upstream through the narrowing gorge, and Dominic Yarrow stood at the rail pretending he wasn't watching her watch them.

She caught him at it on the fourth morning, when the walls of the gorge rose so high the sun only touched the water for an hour at noon.

"You've figured it out," he said. Not a question.

"I've figured out I don't know what it is." She held up the scrap. "These aren't engineering marks. They're too regular. Too *deliberate*. My father didn't decorate his maps."

Dominic leaned against the rail beside her. Up close he looked like a man who'd been subtracting from himself for years—everything nonessential pared away until what remained was mostly stubbornness and a good coat gone thin at the elbows. "Show me the sequence again."

She laid the scrap flat on a bollard and weighted it with a shackle pin. He studied it the way he studied everything: silently, completely, as if the world might change under sustained attention.

"Repeats every nine," he said finally. "Nine marks, then a break, then nine again. That's not shorthand. That's a verse. Or a prayer." His jaw tightened. "Or a name."

"Whose?"

"Ask the river." He nodded forward. "It's about to tell us."

* * *

The locks appeared around the next bend the way ruins always do—suddenly, and then all at once, as if they'd been waiting behind the rock the entire time.

Two stone gates closed across the Vell where the gorge pinched to less than eighty yards, each gate a wall of fitted blocks taller than Teodor's mast, weathered silver-green with age but still true, still plumb, still holding. Between them lay a chamber the length of a chapel, its floor visible through forty feet of unnaturally clear water, and above it all, cut into the living rock of the gorge wall itself, a stairway of a hundred steps climbing to a gallery where—

"Stop the boat," Ilsa said.

Teodor didn't stop the boat; he brought it alongside a rotting timber quay with the unhurried precision of a man who had done this many times, which was its own kind of answer. "You have two hours," he said. "The current through the old sluice turns at midday. If you're not back aboard, I will assume you've found something worth staying for, and I'll be sorry about it in a professional way."

"The syndicate ship," Dominic said quietly. "Still behind us?"

"Behind. Or ahead. Or both. They know these waters better than you do." Teodor spat over the side, unhurried. "Two hours."

The quay held. Barely. Ilsa went up the stone stair first because going second meant following, and she had followed enough men into enough half-kept promises to last her the rest of her life. The steps were worn hollow in their centers—not by rain, rain doesn't wear stone like that, but by feet. Thousands of them. Decades of them.

At the top, the gallery opened along the face of the upper gate, and there she stopped so abruptly that Dominic nearly walked into her back.

The carvings covered everything. Every block, every lintel, every surface the chisels could reach: the same nine-mark sequence from the map, repeated in bands and borders and great sweeping panels, interspersed with figures—people, hundreds of people, carved in low relief, hauling lines and turning capstans and standing in ranks before the gates with their hands raised. Not worshipping. *Operating.* The whole cliff face was a record of a lock system in use, rendered by hands that had loved it.

And beneath the largest panel, in letters deep enough to hold shadow even at noon, a line of text in no alphabet she knew—except that she did know it, because her father had taught it to her as a game when she was seven, a private cipher between them, and she had thought for twenty years it was just that. A game.

"It's Karveli," she said. Her voice came out strange. "The writing. It's Karveli script."

Dominic looked at her. "You can read it?"

"Some. My father—" She stopped. Started again. "He used it for his field notes. Said it was cleaner than any alphabet on the market. I thought he invented it. I thought it was *ours*." She heard how that sounded and hated it, and said it anyway. "I thought it belonged to us."

"Read the big one," Dominic said. "The dedication."

She stepped closer. The light was hard and white off the water, and the letters were patient, and her father's voice was in her ear the way it always was when she read anything difficult: *Sound it out, Ilsa. Stone keeps its promises better than people do.*

"'We built this river a door,'" she translated slowly, "'so that the water would remember whose hands it passed through. Let no consortium, crown, or company close what the valley opened. Signed, the Lockwrights' Guild of Karvel, in the year—'" She frowned. "There's no year. There's a mark instead. A double star."

Behind her, Dominic made a small sound, like a man taking a blow he'd been expecting.

"The chest," he said. "The crest on the chest. Double star."

"Yes."

"So my grandfather—" He didn't finish it. He put his hand flat against the stone instead, the way you'd check a fever, and Ilsa watched him understand, block by block, that the ledger in his pack wasn't a record of debts owed *to* someone. It was a record of debts owed *by*. And the creditors' names ran down one column, and the Karveli names ran down the other, and somewhere in the middle of that arithmetic was a nation that had built doors for its own river and been told, by men with ledgers, that it had never existed.

"There's more," she said. "Look at the lower panel. The later work."

The lower carvings were cruder—chisel-work in a hurry, cut over the older figures like graffiti over frescoes. But the subject was unmistakable: the same gates, the same gorge, and a new channel blasted through the rock to the east, a bypass, a rerouting. Around the new cut marched figures in a different style, stiff and uniform, wearing what were clearly consortium uniforms, and above them, in Karveli script but a hostile hand, a single repeated word.

"What does it say?" Dominic asked.

"*Sealed*," Ilsa said. "Over and over. Sealed, sealed, sealed." She traced the bypass channel with one finger. "They didn't destroy the locks. They couldn't—the gates are too well built, look at them, they'll outlast the empire that buried them. So they starved them. Cut the river east, dropped the water level, and let the nation that lived on this stretch dry up behind its own beautiful doors." She turned to him. "Your 'dissolved nation,' Yarrow. This is the euphemism. They drowned it in reverse."

* * *

They found Teodor at the foot of the stair, sitting on a bollard with a tin cup of tea and an expression Ilsa could only describe as *waiting*, as though he'd been waiting for years and they were merely the latest excuse.

"You knew," she said.

"I knew there were locks." He blew on his tea. "Everyone who runs this stretch knows there are locks. The charts call them a hazard. Submerged obstruction, old masonry, keep to the east channel." He smiled without any humor in it. "The east channel. You see how thorough they were. Even the hazard is a lie."

"The carvings," Dominic said. "The seal order. Did you know that?"

Teodor drank his tea. Below them, the Vell slid past the derelict gates, thin and clear and obedient, running in its stolen bed.

"I run cargo," he said at last. "Grain, tools, cloth. Downstream mostly, into the hill country east of the gorge, where the maps go blank and the tax collectors don't." He looked up at them both. "And sometimes I carry things the other way. Letters. Medicine. A violin once, wrapped in oilcloth, that took me four months to find a buyer honest enough to deliver it. Goods for people who officially do not exist, ordered from merchants who officially never sold them, paid for in coin that officially was never minted." He shrugged. "Karveli survivors. Their grandchildren. There's a valley upriver where the sealed ones gathered after the water left, and they have been buying back their own history one crate at a time for thirty years, and the syndicate has been burning those crates wherever it finds them, and I have been outrunning the syndicate since before either of you was weaned."

"Why?" Ilsa asked. "What's it to you?"

Teodor considered the question with the seriousness he seemed to bring to everything, even tea.

"My mother was a lockwright's daughter," he said. "From a family that worked the third gate downstream—the one they dynamited in the first year, because it was the weakest and they wanted the rest to understand. She escaped east with nothing but her name, and she gave it to me, and for most of my life I thought it was just a name." He stood, joints cracking, and nodded up at the carved gates. "Then I carried my first crate up here and read the wall, and I understood that names are the only thing they couldn't seize. Everything else—the river, the locks, the nation—they took with paperwork. A dissolution decree. A debt schedule." His eyes went to the pack on Dominic's shoulder, and Ilsa saw him see it, and saw him decide, deliberately, to pretend he hadn't. "Paperwork burns, engineer. Stone doesn't. That's why your grandfather drew his map the way he did. That's why the cipher is cut into the gates. He wasn't hiding the route. He was making sure that anyone who came looking would have to stand here first, and read, and understand what was taken before they understood what was owed."

The gorge was silent except for the water. Ilsa found that her hand had gone to the scrap of paper in her pocket, to her father's cipher, to the game he'd taught her when she was seven, and she understood suddenly, with a pain that was almost physical, why a cartographer with no nation and no funding had spent eleven years and his life chasing the headwaters of a river everyone else had agreed to forget.

He hadn't been mapping terrain. He'd been mapping testimony.

"How far to the source?" Dominic asked.

"Six days, if the weather holds. Four, if it doesn't." Teodor drained his cup and set it upside down on the bollard, a bargeman's habit, keeping the dust out. "And there's the arithmetic you two keep doing in whispers. Ten weeks to the passes. Eleven to the rains. Except—" he pointed downstream, back the way they'd come, toward the dark ship running without lamps, "—they don't need the passes. They need only you. And you are standing on the one structure in fifty miles tall enough to be seen from the east channel."

Ilsa followed his finger. High on the eastern rim of the gorge, where the consortium's bypass cut met the sky, something glinted. Once. Twice. Deliberate.

Glass catching sun. A lens. Someone up there, watching the locks, watching the barge, watching them—and signaling to someone below.

Teodor was already moving. "Aboard," he said. "Both of you. Now. We run the old chamber on the flood pulse or we don't run it at all—and if the syndicate is already at the head of the gorge, we may be about to learn whether these gates your ancestors built can still do the one thing they were made to do."

Below them, faint but unmistakable, carried up the stone throat of the gorge: the sound of an engine, turning upstream.
