## 6. The Collector's Bargain

The trading post at Kessel's Landing was the kind of place that existed because the river slowed there and men had learned to do the same. It sat on a shelf of gravel and rotted pilings where the Vell widened into a brown slack-water mile, all of it built on the bones of an older town whose foundations showed at low water like a drowned man's ribs. There was a customs shed with no customs officer, a chandlery that sold rope by weight and lies by volume, and the Saltcellar, a tavern whose floorboards had absorbed forty years of spilled spirits and were drunk themselves.

Dominic had been in Kessel's Landing two days when he understood he was being watched.

It wasn't the ordinary watching. A man broke and traveling upstream on forged survey papers attracted attention the way an open wound attracts flies; he'd grown used to the sidelong arithmetic of eyes measuring his boots, his coat, his worth. This was different. This was patient. On the first morning, a boy too well-dressed to be a wharf rat had looked at him for exactly three seconds from the end of the dock, then walked away without hurrying. On the second morning, the same boy stood outside the Saltcellar while Dominic ate, and this time he smiled before he left.

Ilsa saw it too. She said nothing until they were back in the room above the chandlery, their gear stacked against the wall like passengers waiting for a boat that hadn't come.

"He's counting us," she said. She was cleaning her father's compass with a rag gone gray from use, though it didn't need cleaning. "Counting how many we are. That's not a thief. Thieves don't count."

"Collectors count," Dominic said.

She set the compass down. "Then we leave tonight."

"We can't." He said it flatly, because saying it any other way would have meant admitting how much it frightened him. "Teodor doesn't run the Serrano narrows after dark, and if we take a different boat we lose him, and without him we don't get past the tolls at Vellmouth. We wait. We watch. If they wanted to take us, they'd have done it already."

"And if they want to *buy* you?"

He didn't have an answer for that, so he went to the window instead and looked down at the street, where the lamplighter was making his rounds and the well-dressed boy was nowhere to be seen, which was somehow worse than seeing him.

* * *

The collector came at dusk on the third day, and he came alone, which was its own kind of message.

Dominic was at the Saltcellar's corner table working through Teodor's manifest—cargo weights, toll bribes, the names of lock-keepers who could be trusted and those who couldn't—when the door opened and let in the river smell and a tall man in a coat cut close enough to be expensive without announcing it. Gray at the temples, hands soft, face arranged around a mildness that had clearly been practiced for years. He crossed the room without looking left or right, as though every other person in it were furniture, and stopped at Dominic's table.

"Engineer Yarrow," he said. Not a question. "May I?"

There was no chair. He pulled one over anyway, from a table of card players who didn't object, and sat, and folded his hands.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Dominic said.

"Merric Solm. I'm the senior collector for the Consolidated Interests of the Lower Basin." The name of the syndicate spoken aloud, plainly, in public—that was a message too. Most people who owed them money never heard the name. Hearing it meant you'd been promoted to a different category of debtor. "I've been looking forward to this conversation for some time. Your grandfather's file is one of the oldest still active. Two hundred and eleven years of accrued interest. Remarkable bookkeeping, really. You should be proud."

"I've read the ledger."

"I know you have. I know everything you've done since you pulled that chest out of the mud, including the part where you told the woman you travel with that you haven't decided what to do with it." Solm smiled gently. "You lie badly, Engineer. It's one of your more charming qualities. Your grandfather lied beautifully. It's why he lived as long as he did."

The tavern noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Dominic kept his hands flat on the manifest. "What do you want?"

"What I've always wanted. What my office has wanted since before I held it." Solm leaned forward, and for just a moment the practiced mildness slipped and something older showed underneath—not cruelty, exactly. Tiredness. The tiredness of a man who had done a long job many times. "The chest. The map. The ledger. All of it, returned intact, and everything downstream of it forgotten. In exchange—" he produced a folded paper from inside his coat and laid it on the table between them, squared to the table's edge with a small, precise motion that made Dominic's skin crawl— "this."

Dominic didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"A discharge. Full forgiveness of the Yarrow debt, principal and interest, sealed and witnessed. And beneath that, letters of introduction under a new name. Your choice of name—we keep a list of men who have died owing nothing, and their identities are clean, unencumbered, available. You could be a shipping clerk in Corvane by autumn. Or a bridge inspector on the coast road, if you miss the work. No creditors. No syndicate. No history." Solm's voice stayed level, almost kind. "You'd be free, Engineer. Genuinely free. Most men never even learn what that costs."

"And Karvel?" Dominic asked quietly. "Does the new name come with amnesia about what happened to Karvel?"

Something moved behind Solm's eyes—the flicker of a man hearing a word he'd been paid to stop hearing. "Karvel is a closed account."

"Nations aren't accounts."

"They are when someone holds the paper on them." For a moment Solm looked out the salt-fogged window toward the darkening river, and his voice dropped into something almost conversational. "Do you know what I found when I took this post? A ledger entry your grandfather made himself, in his own hand, the year before the dissolution. He wrote: *the river is being taken from them and they are signing the receipts.* He knew. He documented it. And then he spent the rest of his life making sure no one else ever read what he'd written, because the alternative was watching his family be collected." Solm turned back. "Your grandfather chose survival, Engineer. I am offering you the same choice, with better terms than he got. He'd have taken it in a heartbeat. I think he'd be ashamed of you for hesitating."

* * *

That was the knife of it. Not the threat—there hadn't even been a threat, not explicitly, and somehow that was worse—but the idea that Aldous Yarrow, the man whose careful hand Dominic had traced page by page through the ledger, might have taken the deal. Might have wanted his grandson to take it.

Dominic sat in his room above the chandlery with the discharge paper unrolled under the lamp, and he read it the way he'd once read load tables, line by line, looking for the flaw. There wasn't one. That was the terrible thing. It was clean. It was generous. Whoever had drafted it had drafted it to be accepted, and the terms were better than anything a disgraced engineer scraping by on closed-line surveys had any right to expect from the world.

A new name. A clean identity. No debt, no syndicate, no collectors' knives waiting in riverports. He thought about the last six years—the cold offices where he'd begged for survey contracts, the way innkeepers' faces changed when they learned his surname, the night in Vellmouth he'd slept in a coal shed because every bed in town belonged, one way or another, to men the syndicate owned. All of it, ended. One signature. One chest, handed over, and whatever was buried upriver stayed buried, and the dead nation stayed dead, tidy and permanent as a closed account.

And Ilsa?

The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. Ilsa, whose father had died following that map. Ilsa, who carried her inheritance in a compass case against her heart. If Dominic signed, she lost the map, the trail, the answer to why her father had drowned in a river that had been moved out from under him. He could give her his share of the passage money and a letter of recommendation and watch her go upriver alone into the monsoon, and the new name he wore would fit so tightly he'd never feel anything again.

*He'd have taken it in a heartbeat.*

"No," Dominic said aloud to the empty room, and heard how much it cost him, how thin his own voice sounded. "No, he wouldn't have."

Because he'd read the ledger. All of it, now, every page, and there was a thing in it he hadn't told anyone—not Ilsa, not Teodor. On the last leaf, in Aldous Yarrow's hand, squeezed into the margin as though written fast, in the dark, in fear: *If anyone of my blood reads this—do not settle. Settling is what they want. The debt is the only copy of the truth they cannot burn.* His grandfather hadn't chosen survival. His grandfather had chosen the trap, and lived inside it for thirty years, and left the door open behind him.

Dominic rolled the discharge paper closed. Then, deliberately, he fed one corner to the lamp flame and watched the fire walk up the page, eating the seals, the names, the generous clean terms, until there was nothing in his hand but heat and ash. It felt less like courage than like cutting off a finger he might someday need. But it was done, and some part of him that had been clenched for six years loosened, and another part that had been asleep woke up screaming.

Below the window, in the street, a lantern bobbed past. The well-dressed boy again—or not the boy; taller, broader, moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who already knew where everyone was. Two more shapes followed at the corner. They didn't look up at Dominic's window. They didn't need to.

They were heading for the docks. Toward Teodor's barge.

Dominic grabbed his coat and the ledger and took the stairs three at a time, and behind him the lamp guttered over the last curl of ash that had been his freedom, settling on the floor like gray snow.
