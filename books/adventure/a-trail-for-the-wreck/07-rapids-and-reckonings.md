## 7. Rapids and Reckonings

I saw them first as a shimmer on the water above the trading post—two longboats sliding out of the morning mist with their oars muffled in rags, the way poachers row, not the way honest men do.

"Teodor," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted it to.

He was already looking. He'd been looking, I think, since we'd tied up at the post the night before; he had a smuggler's instinct for when a river went quiet in the wrong way. The barge's single sail hung slack. Downstream, the Serrano Rapids made their low continuous thunder, like something enormous grinding its teeth beneath the water.

"How many?" Dominic asked, coming up from below decks with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the way he'd taken to carrying it—against his heart, like a shield worn backwards.

"Two boats. Six men, maybe eight." Teodor didn't raise his voice. He never raised his voice; he simply arranged himself so that the world had no choice but to listen. "They've been behind us since the locks. I hoped they'd wait for the collector."

"The collector isn't here," I said.

"No," Teodor said. "Which means he doesn't want you talked to anymore. He wants you emptied."

I watched Dominic's face do the thing it did—that terrible arithmetic, the flicker of the trading post still on him like smoke. Last night, at the table by the window, the syndicate's man had laid out the offer again in his soft voice: *the ledger and the map, and every debt your family carries dies with it. A new name. A new life. No one hunting you across the face of the earth.* And Dominic had sat there with his hands flat on the wood and said nothing, and the nothing had lasted too long, and I had hated him for it—not because he was tempted, but because I understood the temptation down to its bones. I'd have taken it. That was the worst part. I'd have traded my father's map for one clean night of sleep.

Dominic had finally stood up and walked out without answering, which was not refusal, but wasn't acceptance either, and the collector's man had smiled like a man marking a card.

Now the boats were coming, and the bill for that silence was arriving early.

"We can't outrun them," Teodor said. "Barge is heavy, wind's dead." He looked downstream, toward the thunder. "But they can't follow us through Serrano. Not in those boats, not loaded."

"You want to run the rapids," I said. "In this."

"I want to *not be killed*," Teodor said. "The rapids are optional. Those men are not."

* * *

We stripped the barge of everything that mattered in four minutes flat: the ledger, the map case, water, the medicine tin, Teodor's coiled line, half the dried fish, nothing else. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the tin cup I left behind, and how I'd drunk from it every morning for six days, and how you don't know what's yours until you're choosing what to abandon.

Teodor took the tiller. Dominic and I took the sweeps. The first boat was fifty yards off and closing, and a voice carried across the flat water—not shouting, just carrying, the way the collector's people did everything, politely, inevitably: *"Yarrow! Nothing has to be lost today!"*

"That's what worries me," Dominic muttered, and dug his sweep in.

The river bent. The sound rose. And then the world simply fell away beneath us.

I have read a hundred accounts of rapids and none of them were true. There is no sequence to it, no scenery. There is white noise and cold that slaps the breath out of you and the boat going weightless, then slamming, then weightless again, and Teodor's hands moving on the tiller like a pianist's, small corrections in a storm built entirely of wrong answers. We hit the first ledge sideways. Something cracked—I thought it was the hull, and maybe it was—and a wall of water came over the gunwale and took our food bag and half the water skins as if they'd never existed.

"LEFT!" Teodor roared, and I threw my whole body against the sweep, and Dominic did the same on his side, and for one impossible moment the boat stood straight in the chaos, running the tongue of green water between two black teeth of rock, and I felt—God help me—I felt *joy*. Pure, stupid, alive joy, the kind my father used to talk about, reading contour lines aloud at the kitchen table like poetry: *here the river narrows, here it sings.*

Then the second drop came, and joy ended.

We hit the trough wrong. The boat slewed. A rock the size of a chapel rose out of the foam on our right and Teodor did the only thing there was to do—he put the tiller hard over and took the impact on the stern instead of the beam, and the crack of wood was lost inside the crack of water, but I saw him fly forward, saw his shoulder catch the bollard, saw the way he landed, folded, wrong, the way bodies aren't meant to fold.

Then we were through. Just like that. The river spat us into the long green pool below the last cataract, and the noise fell away behind us, and the two longboats turned back upstream above the falls, and we drifted in a silence so complete I could hear my own blood.

"Teodor," I said.

He was trying to sit up. That was the thing about Teodor—he was always trying to sit up. His left arm hung at an angle that made my stomach turn, and there was blood on his lips, and when he spoke his voice was still level, which frightened me more than screaming would have.

"Ribs," he said. "Shoulder. Maybe worse. Don't make faces at me, girl, I can see you making faces."

* * *

We grounded the boat on a shingle bar where the valley narrowed into the gorge country, and I did what I could with the medicine tin and what remained of my father's teaching—pressure, splinting, the willow bark he'd taught me to strip on survey trips when I was nine years old. Teodor bore it the way stone bears weather. But when I finished binding the shoulder he caught my wrist with his good hand, and his grip was still strong enough to hurt.

"Listen to me," he said. "That arm is done for the season. Maybe more. I can hold a tiller on calm water. I cannot haul, I cannot fight, and I cannot run." He said it plainly, the way he said everything, and somehow that made it unbearable. "You two are carrying me now. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means we carry you," Dominic said.

"It means," Teodor said, "that every mile from here is bought twice. You should know the price before you pay it."

Nobody answered. The gorge walls went up and up on either side of us, grey stone streaked with the first brown stains of the coming rains, and somewhere above, birds wheeled in the thermals, indifferent as gods.

It was Dominic who broke. Not loudly—that wasn't his way. He sat down on the shingle with the ledger in his lap and put his head in his hands, and I heard him say, almost to himself: "At the post. If I'd told the collector no—if I'd said the word, one word, instead of standing there like a coward doing sums—they'd have waited. They'd have taken us quietly, or not at all. They came now because I hesitated. This—" and his hand moved, taking in the wrecked boat, the ruined supplies, Teodor grey-faced against the packroll, "—this is mine."

"Don't flatter yourself," I said, sharper than I intended.

His head came up.

"You think hesitation is a sin only you commit?" I knelt down in front of him, close enough to see the exhaustion sitting in the hollows under his eyes. "I have spent six days deciding whether to steal that ledger from you while you slept. Six days. My father died following that map and I have lain awake listening to you breathe, wondering if I could take it and finish his work alone. So don't stand at the front of the church confessing like you're the only sinner in the parish, Dominic Yarrow. We are both of us traitors to something. The only question is what we're loyal to now."

The water slid past the shingle bar, patient, endless. Somewhere in that silence, something between us changed—not softened, exactly. Settled. The way stones settle in a wall, finding the shape that lets them bear weight together.

"He's right about one thing, though," I said, nodding at Teodor. "The price. From here we move light and fast, and we stop apologizing for it."

Teodor laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway. "Look at that. Two days ago she couldn't look at him. Now they gang up on me." He struggled upright against the rocks, breathing carefully around the broken ribs. "There's a path on the east bank, past the narrows. Old Karveli mule road. It climbs toward the headwaters faster than the river does—but it climbs through country the syndicate watches, because it's the only way in." He looked at each of us in turn, and for the first time since I'd known him, Teodor looked tired. "Three days' march. Then the source gorge. Assuming the monsoon waits for us, which it won't."

* * *

We buried what we couldn't carry—no, that's not true. We cached it, weighted under stones, the smuggler's habit even in ruin, because Teodor insisted that runners might come this way behind us and supplies left well are supplies found. He directed the work one-armed from the shingle, cursing with great precision, and there was something in the ordering of it, in the three of us moving around each other with the load shared out—ledger to Dominic, map case to me, line and hooks to Teodor because he refused to be cargo—that felt less like an alliance and more like a household. A poor one. A hunted one. But a household.

My father spent his last years alone in a rented room with his maps pinned to every wall, and I used to think the loneliness was the price of the work. Standing on that shingle bar, lashing a pack with numb fingers while a wounded smuggler insulted my knots and a disgraced engineer checked the ledger for damp for the fourth time, I understood that my father hadn't been lonely because of the work. He'd been lonely because he'd never found his crew.

We started up the mule road as the light went long and coppery behind the western peaks. Teodor set a slow pace and hated every step of it, and Dominic walked beside him without being asked, matching his stride, ready with a hand at the bad moments and pretending not to be.

Near dusk, Teodor stopped at a bend where the road looked out over the whole descending length of the Vell—a silver thread unspooling through darkening country, running down and away toward nations that had never heard of Karvel. He nodded at it, at all of it.

"My grandfather ran goods on this river," he said. "Before the closure. Before the seals on the locks." His good hand rested on the old dry-stone wall beside the road, on carvings worn nearly smooth—the double star, over and over, a litany in rock. "They say the Karveli sent things downstream, after the end. Chests. Messages. Waiting for someone to pull one out of the water someday." He glanced at Dominic, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the smuggler's calculation, but something older and stranger. Reverence, maybe. Or pity. "You know what you pulled out of that river, engineer? You didn't find a treasure. You answered a letter forty years in the mail."

Dominic didn't answer. He was looking up the road, into the gorge country, where the first clouds of the monsoon were building along the peaks—great blue-black towers of them, silent, patient, already on the move.

And then he went very still, and I followed his eyes, and saw what he saw: far up the mule road, a thin line of figures where no figures should be, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned the ground they walked on.

Not following us this time.

Waiting ahead.
