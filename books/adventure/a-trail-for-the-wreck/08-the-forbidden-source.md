## 8. The Forbidden Source

The gorge announced itself before we saw it, as a change in the river's voice. For six days the Vell had run wide and brown and patient, the way a debt runs—always there, always accruing. Then the banks began to close like a hand folding, and the water went from brown to green to a color I had no name for, something between glass and grief, and it started to *sing*, high and thin against the rock, and Teodor pulled the skiff into an eddy below a wall of stone that went up so far my neck ached trying to follow it.

"End of the line for boats," he said. He was still moving stiffly, his bandaged shoulder held tight against his ribs, but his eyes were bright in a way that frightened me more than any fever would have. "From here you walk. And from here—" he spat over the side, ceremonially, "—I go no farther. Not because I'm afraid." A pause. "Because they'd know me."

"They?"

"The people who are about to know you."

Ilsa was already climbing out onto the shelf of wet granite, map case slung across her back, her father's cipher-book strapped to her hip like a weapon. She didn't look back at me. She hadn't looked at me properly since the rapids, since Teodor's blood thinned in the river and she'd stitched him closed by firelight with hands that never shook once—not once—and I understood that this was how she grieved: by becoming absolutely competent.

I climbed out after her. The skiff slid away downstream with Teodor's last words trailing behind it: "The gates open inward. Remember that. Everything Karvel built opens inward."

* * *

The trail up the gorge wall wasn't on Ilsa's map, which unsettled her more than if it had been marked forbidden. It was simply *absent*—a seam in the stone that only revealed itself when you were standing inside it, a slot canyon no wider than my shoulders in places, dripping, dark, smelling of iron and old water. Ilsa navigated by touch, reading chisel marks in the walls with her fingertips the way other people read print.

"These aren't natural," she said, somewhere above me in the dark. Her voice came down strange and flattened. "The whole passage is cut. Dominic—the entire approach is engineered. Who cuts a road and then erases it from every map?"

"A nation that doesn't want to be found," I said, "or a nation that isn't allowed to exist."

The first squall hit us an hour later. It came down the gorge like a living thing—a wall of warm rain with wind behind it, hammering the slot full of white noise, and the trickle under our boots became a stream became a torrent in the space of ten minutes. We climbed with our fingers hooked into chiseled handholds two hundred years old, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that felt obscene, that whoever had cut those holds had cut them for exactly this—for people climbing toward shelter while the sky came apart. They had known the monsoon. They had planned around it. Every step I took had been anticipated by the dead.

We came out of the slot into light.

I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe what we saw, but I owe it a try. The gorge opened like a throat into a valley perhaps three miles long, walled in green stone, terraced from the river to the ridgelines in steps of pale masonry gone soft with moss. Water moved everywhere—in aqueducts along the terraces, in flumes crossing the air on stone piers, in channels beside the footpaths—all of it clear, all of it *ordered*, every drop apparently employed. And dominating the head of the valley, where the river emerged from a cleft in the mountain itself, stood the locks.

Not one lock. Nine of them, ascending the gorge in a staircase of black timber and dressed stone, each gate tall as a cathedral door, each carved—now I could see it plainly—with the double star. The crest from the chest. The crest from the ledger. The crest that appeared on documents filed by a nation that, according to every atlas printed in forty years, had never existed.

Smoke rose from chimneys among the terraces. There were people in the fields. There were children running a race along the top of an aqueduct, and when they saw us they stopped, and stared, and did not run away.

"They're alive," Ilsa said. It wasn't wonder in her voice. It was accusation. "Dominic. They've been *alive* this whole time. My father died following a map to a country everyone said was empty."

Below us, a bell began to ring. Not an alarm—I knew alarms; I'd spent a decade listening for them. This was slower, deliberate, a pattern of three and then a pause and then three again, and down in the valley people began to move with purpose but without panic, setting down tools, closing shutters, walking—not fleeing—toward the lowest lock.

Toward *us*.

"They're not surprised," Ilsa said quietly. "Look at them. Dominic, look at their faces. Nobody is surprised."

* * *

They met us at the ninth gate, the lowest one, and they were led by a woman past seventy with a surveyor's chain coiled over one shoulder like a stole and a face weathered down to its essential architecture. Behind her stood perhaps thirty others, and I searched their faces for hostility and found none, and searched for welcome and found none either, and finally understood that what I was looking at was neither. It was *recognition*. The way you recognize a train arriving on a line you built the platform for decades ago.

"You are Yarrow," the woman said. Not a question.

"How do you know my name?"

"Because we sent for it." She unslung the chain, held it loosely, an old habit rather than a threat. "I am Maret Ostrevich. Keeper of the Ninth Gate. You'll want to sit. No—you won't. Your kind never does, at first. Then walk with me, and I'll tell you why your grandfather's name is written in a book you pulled from a river, and why the book was in the river at all."

She turned and walked through the gate—which opened inward, silent on oiled hinges, exactly as Teodor had said—and we followed because there was nowhere else in the world to follow.

Inside the gatehouse, on a table of black wood, they had laid out documents. I stopped dead in the doorway. Ilsa walked into my back and swore.

There were letters. Survey reports. A photograph, cracked and silvered, of a rail crew posed before a tunnel mouth—Karveli faces, the double star on their caps. And at the center, weighted down with a stone, a single sheet of heavy paper covered in a precise engineer's hand I knew better than my own, because I had grown up tracing it in the margins of my grandfather's textbooks.

"Dominic Yarrow," Maret said, "your grandfather was chief engineer of the Upper Vell Consolidated Line. He did not build these locks. He was ordered to seal them—to reroute the river, drown the lower valley, and file the paperwork that declared Karvel dissolved. And he did it." She let that land. She was not cruel about it; she was simply exact, the way a level is exact. "And then, for the last eleven years of his life, he did everything else. He copied the ledger. He hid the true surveys inside false ones. He taught the lock-keepers' grandchildren the cipher your friend carries in her father's book—did you know that? Your friend's father learned it from *us*. The map in your chest was drawn by three hands across two generations. It was never lost, Mr. Yarrow. It was *mailed*."

"Mailed," I repeated. The word felt wrong in my mouth, too small.

"The chest was sealed and sent downstream deliberately," Maret said. "On a flood year, timed to reach the dredge lines. We have sent four. Three were intercepted by the syndicate's collectors and destroyed, and the men who carried them were never seen again. Yours arrived." Her eyes, gray and steady, did not blink. "Do you understand what I am telling you? The ledger names debts because debts must be named by someone with standing to collect them. Your grandfather signed every page so that someday, somewhere downstream, a Yarrow would pull it from the water and *have to read it*. The debt was engineered, Mr. Yarrow. Like everything else on this river. It was built to find its heir."

I sat down after all. My legs made the decision without consulting me.

Outside, the rain thickened against the gatehouse roof, and I heard the monsoon coming up the valley in long rolling volleys of thunder, and I thought about my grandfather teaching me to read contour lines when I was seven years old, his finger moving over the paper, saying: *the land tells you where it wants the road, boy; your job is only to listen.* All those years I'd believed I was disinherited. Broke, disgraced, nameless on the closed lines. And the whole time I had been a letter in transit, addressed and stamped, waiting for the water to deliver me.

"There's a collector coming," Ilsa said. She said it flatly, to the room. "There's been one ahead of us since the trading post. If they sent four chests and got three back—"

"We know," Maret said. "The bell rang twice today. Once for you." She looked at me, and for the first time something moved behind the exactness—something old and tired and fierce. "Once for him. He reached the seventh gate this morning. He is resting there. He has offered the valley a bargain, and tomorrow he will offer it to you, and I will not tell you what it is, because my father told me what your grandfather told him: *a man must meet his own temptation walking.*"

Thunder came down the gorge, and the nine locks took the sound and gave it back changed, deeper, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"But tonight," Maret Ostrevich said, "you should sleep. Because tomorrow the river rises, and you, Mr. Yarrow—heir to the debt, inheritor of the theft—are going to have to decide what this valley is worth. To you. To the world. Whether it stays buried—" and she nodded toward the ledger lying open on the table, my grandfather's hand marching across the page, naming names, naming sums, naming *everything*—

"—or whether you make it visible."

The rain came harder. Somewhere above us, at the seventh gate, a lamp burned in a window, and a man who could erase my debts waited behind it like a second set of footprints, following mine up the mountain.
