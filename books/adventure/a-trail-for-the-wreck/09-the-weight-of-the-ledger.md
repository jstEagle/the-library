## 9. The Weight of the Ledger

The rain came an hour before the collector did.

Dominic heard it first as a sound like a crowd gathering far upstream—a low, rolling murmur in the gorge that made the old Karveli women pause in their work and look at one another without speaking. Then the first squall hit the valley sideways, hammering the lock gates, flattening the terraced gardens, turning the source falls from white silk to a roaring brown fist. The river that had been rising for two days now rose with intent.

He stood under the stone overhang above the lower lock, the ledger open on his knees, and watched the water climb the gauge marks cut into the gatehouse wall. The marks had names beside them. Old ones. *Spring thaw, year of the double star. Monsoon, year of the sealing.* A nation had kept records here the way other nations kept armies.

"You're going to ruin it," Ilsa said, crouching beside him. She had to raise her voice over the rain. Her father's map was wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and her hair was plastered flat, and she looked, Dominic thought, like someone who had finally arrived at the place she'd been walking toward all her life and found it on fire.

"It's already ruined," he said. "Water-stained through half the pages. That's why it survived, maybe. Nobody thought to steal something the river had already chewed."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know." He turned a page. His grandfather's hand marched down it in columns—names, sums, dates in the Karveli style, the little crossed stars used instead of numerals for the years. *Vell diversion, phase three: 4,100 souls resettled per consortium schedule. Compensation issued: none recorded. Note: resettlement route crosses Serrano Rapids. Recommend delay until dry season. Recommendation overruled by order of Consortium Director Hask.* The same name, over and over. Hask. Hask. Hask. The syndicate's founding charter, Teodor had told him, bore a single signature.

"He'll come today," Ilsa said. "Teodor's runners say riders took the gorge road at dawn. In this?"

"In this," Dominic said. "Because in this, no one else can follow. He wants us sealed in here with the monsoon, the way Karvel was sealed behind its locks. It's how his people work. They don't burn things. They close the doors."

The valley lay below them in the rain: forty roofs, the terraces, the great derelict flight of locks descending the gorge like a staircase built for giants, every gate still greased and functional because the descendants had never stopped maintaining them. Waiting. Sixty years of waiting, and what they'd been waiting for was a book.

An old man climbed the wet stairs to the overhang—Seraf, the keeper of the gates, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had spent his life reading water. "The third mark," he said, nodding at the gauge. "By nightfall we're at the fourth. If your collector means to talk, he should talk soon."

* * *

The collector came on foot, alone, which was somehow worse than riders.

Dominic watched him walk up the towpath through the downpour without hurrying, a lean figure in a gray traveling coat, water streaming off the brim of his hat, and felt the old familiar contraction in his chest—the surveyor's dread, the debtor's flinch, the body remembering every closed door. This was the man who had sat across from him in the trading post with tea and patience and offered him a new life the way you'd offer a drowning man a boat with a hole in it. This was the man whose syndicate had erased a country so cleanly that Dominic had grown up believing Karvel was a spelling error.

The collector stopped at the foot of the lock stairs and removed his hat. Rain ran down his face and he didn't seem to notice it.

"Engineer Yarrow," he called up. "May I climb? The water's rising faster than my manners."

"Come up," Seraf said, before Dominic could answer. "A guest who walks into a flood unarmed is either honest or very confident. Either way, we watch him."

The collector climbed. Up close he looked older than Dominic remembered, or perhaps the rain had washed something away—the polish, the patience worn down to whatever was underneath. He set his hat on the stone and looked out at the valley, the locks, the people standing in doorways watching him with expressions that held sixty years.

"A whole town," he said quietly. "All this time. Do you know what our ledgers say about this valley, Engineer? Nothing. There is no entry. We are meticulous people. We have entries for everything—every debt, every asset, every grave we've paid to dig. And this place simply doesn't exist." He turned to Dominic. "That's what you're holding. Not a map. An omission. And omissions, Mr. Yarrow, are the only things my employers fear."

"Then they should have let me drown in debt," Dominic said. "It would have been cheaper."

"They tried the cheap way first." The collector's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "Your grandfather refused us too, did you know that? Forty years ago. We offered him the same thing I'm offering you. He chose the ledger. He died on a survey line in the rain, alone, unpaid, unmourned by anyone but a nation that no longer existed." He let that sit. "I am offering you his choice again, and I confess I don't understand it. Listen to the terms. The ledger burns. The map burns. The debt—yours, your father's, your grandfather's, the whole poisoned inheritance—is struck from every book we keep. You receive a new name, clean papers, a commission on any railway in the world you care to build. You will never be invisible again, Mr. Yarrow. You'll be *free*. All you have to do is let a dead country stay dead."

Below them, the river hit the third mark and kept climbing.

"And them?" Dominic asked, nodding at the valley.

The collector didn't answer, and the not-answer was the truest thing he'd said.

"There it is," Dominic said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. Inside, terror was doing its old arithmetic—*survival, survival, take the boat, take the name, live*—and he let it finish the sum, because he'd learned in the rapids that you don't beat fear by pretending it isn't speaking. You hear it all the way through, and then you choose. "You want me to trade a living valley for a dead man's peace. My grandfather wouldn't take that bargain and neither will I."

The collector sighed, and picked up his hat, and for just a moment looked tired rather than dangerous. "Then there's nothing more to discuss."

"No," Dominic said. "There's one thing left. The part you came to prevent."

He walked to the edge of the overhang, where the lock gates rose out of the floodwater, and where the Karveli carvings spiraled around the stone—double stars, water-lines, names. Ilsa was already there, unwrapping the oilcloth. Seraf raised a horn to his lips, and the sound that came out was low and brazen and rolled down the gorge like a second thunder, and doors opened along the terraces, and people came out into the rain—not hiding anymore, not sealed, but gathering, climbing the lock stairs, filling the gatehouse gallery, the whole valley coming to listen.

"What are you doing?" the collector asked, and for the first time his voice had an edge of genuine alarm. "Yarrow. The ledger proves nothing legally. Every court on the river is ours. You'll die soaked and unheard."

"Maybe," Dominic said. "But my grandfather wrote this for someone to read. Sixty years in a chest, and the chest went downstream on purpose. It wasn't lost. It was *sent*." He opened the book to the first page, to the name he'd dreaded since the day he pulled it dripping from the Vell. "And I'm done being the last person willing to say these names out loud."

The rain hammered down. The river climbed toward the fourth mark. And Dominic Yarrow, disgraced engineer, debtor, heir to nothing anyone would claim, began to read.

"*Entry the first. Vell diversion, phase one.*" His voice cracked on the first words and he forced it steady, a surveyor's voice, a voice for reading measurements across a noisy worksite. "*Compensation owed to the households of Lower Karvel: eleven thousand crowns. Issued: nothing. Recorded by Aldous Yarrow, chief engineer, who writes this knowing it may be the only copy, and that the men who ordered this will one day send someone to find it.*"

He read the debts. All of them. The diverted river and the drowned farms. The resettlement columns that marched into the Serrano Rapids in flood season because a director wanted the schedule met. The names—hundreds of names, his grandfather's careful hand recording each household, each sum, each theft—and with every name, someone in the crowd below cried out or knelt or simply stood straighter, hearing a grandmother or a village spoken aloud for the first time in sixty years. Ilsa held the oilcloth over the book when the rain gusted. Seraf translated the old Karveli figures into the river-tongue for the eldest listeners. The collector stood at the back of the gallery, hat in his hands, and did not move, and Dominic understood suddenly that he couldn't—that the syndicate's power lived in silence, and a man who interrupted a reading became proof of it.

The water reached the fourth mark as he reached the final page.

"*Final entry,*" Dominic read. "*I leave this ledger to whoever the river chooses. The debt is real. So is the proof of what was taken. Let them weigh each other.*"

He closed the book. The gorge roared. And then, from downstream—from the direction of the sealed gorge road, through the rain—a sound that froze every face in the gallery: the crack of timber, once, twice, and a long groaning sigh of stone.

Seraf went white. "The fifth gate," he breathed. "Someone's forcing the fifth gate. From below."

The collector put on his hat. "My employers," he said, "send more patient men than I am. But even they don't wait out floods."

Dominic clutched the ledger to his chest as the first brown tongue of the flood licked over the lower lock's top step, and somewhere beneath the roar of the water, the valley's oldest door began to break.
