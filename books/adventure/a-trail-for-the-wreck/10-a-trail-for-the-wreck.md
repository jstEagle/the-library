## 10. A Trail for the Wreck

The river took the gorge road on the third day of the flood, and Ilsa Venn stood at the lock gates to watch it go.

It did not go gently. The Vell, swollen fat with the first true week of monsoon, shouldered through the narrows like a debt collector with a writ, peeling the switchback trail off the cliff face in slabs. She heard the rock let go before she saw it—a deep groan under the water's roar, the mountain clearing its throat—and then a whole span of the old Karveli road simply folded into the white water and was gone, as if it had never been built at all.

*As if it had never been built at all.* That was the syndicate's whole art, she thought. That was what they had done to Karvel: not destroyed it, merely unwritten it. Dissolved. A word that sounded like sugar dissolving, not like ten thousand people sealed behind rerouted water while the world's railways ran rich on their labor.

She had spent her life learning to read terrain the way other people read letters—every contour a sentence, every scree slope a warning. And now she stood reading this valley, this hidden green throat of the world, and understood that her father had died trying to read the same page. He had gotten as far as the lower locks. He had died forty miles short of this.

"Dom says the road can be rebuilt." Teodor's voice came from behind her, roughened by the wound in his side that would not stop weeping. He lowered himself onto the gatehouse step with the care of a man carrying something breakable inside him. "Says the Karveli cut stone is still sound under the slide. Says your father would have agreed."

"My father said a lot of things about roads." Ilsa did not turn around. "Most of them were arguments."

Teodor laughed, then winced, then laughed anyway. Below them, in the flooded gorge, the river carried away the last visible trace of the old world's silence.

* * *

The runners had gone out two days before the road died.

Ilsa had watched them from the same spot: six of the valley's young people, lean and quick, each with a satchel of oilcloth-wrapped copies pressed against their spines. Copies of the ledger. Every page, every name, every sum—the debts Dominic's grandfather had recorded in his neat engineer's hand, and beside them, in the valley elders' older script, the record of what had been taken to create those debts. Land. Water. Names struck from registries. A nation filed away under a euphemism.

Six runners, six routes, over passes that would close within the fortnight. To riverports and rail junctions, to newspaper offices and a magistrate in Corven who owed Teodor more than he owed his own conscience. By the time the snows sealed the high country, the ledger would be in fifty hands across three nations.

"You understand what that means," the eldest of the valley council had told Dominic, the night before. "Once it is out, they cannot kill you quietly. Killing you confirms it. Their only weapon left is silence—and silence costs them more than knives now."

Isla had seen Dominic's face when he heard that. She had seen a man stand at the edge of the thing he'd wanted all his life—belonging, purpose, a finished thing—and realize it came with no door marked *exit*. Her father had worn that same expression once, over maps, before the hunger for the upper river ate everything else in him.

But Dominic had nodded. And when the collector's last messenger had come up from the drowned road with one final offer—*a new name, a clean slate, walk away*—Dominic had sent the man back down with a single sheet of survey paper on which was written, in careful block letters, the full text of the ledger's first page.

That, Ilsa thought, was the moment she stopped watching him the way you watch a stranger. That was the moment he became hers to worry about, which was its own kind of inheritance.

* * *

The wet season made surveyors of them both whether they liked it or not.

There was nothing else to do. The valley could not be left; the passes were shut; the syndicate's collectors were stranded on the far side of a drowned road along with everyone else. So the days arranged themselves around rain—hard mornings of work between squalls, long afternoons in the gatehouse with instruments drying by the fire, evenings in the council hall where the valley elders argued about a railway the way other people argued about weather.

Because that was the strange fruit of it all. They wanted the line. Not a consortium's line, not an empire's artery bleeding wealth out of the gorge—they wanted a railway of their own, one that belonged to the valley the way the locks did, built on terms written in the open where anyone could read them.

"They keep asking me if it can be done honestly," Dominic told her one night, mud to the elbows, hair plastered flat, looking younger and more exhausted and more alive than she had ever seen him. "As if honesty were an engineering problem. As if I could just—calculate the load-bearing capacity of decency."

"What do you tell them?"

"That it's not a question of whether a line can be honest. It's who holds the deed." He turned his cup in his hands. "My grandfather drew beautiful surveys for men who owned everything they touched. I used to think the shame was in the drawing. It isn't. It's in the owning."

Ilsa looked at him across the fire and felt the prickly mistrust of that riverport tavern seem like something that had happened to different people entirely. "Then we draw it so the valley owns it," she said. "Every contour. Every culvert. My father's methods, your grandfather's hand, and none of their ledgers."

Dominic smiled—the rare one, the one that reached all the way up. "A trail for the wreck," he said, using the phrase the valley children had already taken up, because the elders had begun calling the proposed line exactly that: the road laid down atop the ruin, the track that followed the disaster home.

"A trail for the wreck," she agreed.

* * *

They worked the valley all season.

Ilsa taught herself the locks until she could read the gates better than the map—her father's cipher finally resolved not as a treasure key but as maintenance notes, a cartographer's love letter to machinery he'd never been allowed to see. She walked the full length of the system twice in breaks between storms, and at the third lock from the source she found the carving her father must have died searching for: the double-star crest, and beneath it, small enough to miss, a date and two initials that matched the ledger's flyleaf.

He had been right. He had simply been born too early by one generation.

Dominic, meanwhile, surveyed. He measured the gorge with a chain and a level and a patience she hadn't known he possessed, and the valley's young people trailed after him learning the trade, and somewhere in the middle of the wet season Ilsa realized she had stopped counting the days toward anything. There was no deadline now. No monsoon arithmetic, no closing window. Just the long work, and the rain, and the growing stack of drawings in the gatehouse that would become, come spring, a real proposal with real signatures.

One evening in the deepest of the rains, Teodor sat propped by the fire with his wound finally knitting, and looked at the two of them bent over the same drafting table, and said, "You know you're both orphans of the same country now. Neither of you was ever going to fit anywhere else."

Neither of them answered. Neither of them needed to.

* * *

On the last night before the deep snows, the valley held a naming.

It was a Karveli custom, the elders explained—one of the few that had survived the sealing, kept alive the way embers are kept, banked and passed hand to hand. When something lost is found again, it is given a new name by the people who found it, so that it belongs to the finding and not only to the loss.

They named the rebuilt road first: *the Yarrow Line*, over Dominic's red-faced protest, because the elders were immovable and because, as the head of the council said, "Your grandfather's name is on every page of that ledger as a debtor. Let it be on our rails as a builder. Names should get to choose their second lives."

And then, to Ilsa's complete surprise, they turned to her.

"The map," the elder said. "The one your father drew, that brought the chest downstream, that brought you both here. It should carry a maker's mark again. What name does it bear?"

Ilsa opened her mouth to say *Venn*, and found she couldn't—not because the name shamed her, but because it suddenly seemed too small. Her father had drawn that map alone, hungry, following a ghost. But the map that mattered now was being redrawn, line by line, by four hands and a valley's worth of voices.

"Ilsa Venn Yarrow," she heard herself say, and felt Dominic go very still beside her, and decided she would not take it back even if the roof fell in. "Let it bear both. We finished each other's inheritances. The map might as well admit it."

The hall laughed, and cheered, and someone began a song that had clearly not been sung aloud in sixty years, and outside the rain went on falling on the locks and the gates and the drowned road and the sleeping mountains, falling on everything the river had buried and everything, at last, that was being dug back out.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned to orange coals and the song had ended, Dominic stood with her at the gatehouse window, looking out at the black shape of the gorge.

"In spring," he said quietly, "when the passes open, the first thing through will either be survey crews and newspapermen—or collectors. We won't know which until they arrive."

Ilsa watched the rain silver the dark water below and found, to her own surprise, that she was not afraid.

"Then we'd better have the rails staked before they come," she said. "Whichever ones they are."

Below them, the Vell ran high and loud and free through the valley that owned it at last—and somewhere beyond the snowbound passes, the world was already beginning to read.
