## 1. Fuel, Ice, and Rumors

The trouble with a Beaver on floats, Dominic Delacroix liked to say, was that it never let you lie to yourself. A car would forgive you. A truck would forgive you for weeks. But an aircraft kept books — honest ones — and every morning she told you exactly what you were worth in pounds of payload and gallons of fuel.

He was doing the arithmetic at six a.m., standing on the dock at Caribou Bend with his breath freezing in the whiskers of his beard, watching the lake steam like a kettle somebody had forgotten about. Forty-one degrees and clear. The water so flat it looked poured rather than fallen. Good flying weather, which meant good working weather, which meant he'd be broke by Thursday instead of Wednesday.

"Delacroix." Old Marta Kowalczyk came down the dock with two mugs of coffee and the particular walk of a woman who had decided decades ago that hurrying was for people with worse knees. She owned the trading post, the fuel cache, and — though she'd deny it under oath — half the gossip in town. "You're taking the medicine run up to Little Otter today?"

"I'm taking whatever pays."

"Mm." She handed him a mug. "Then you're taking the medicine run. Doc Halloran's got insulin for the Kushtaka family, and Mrs. Two Rivers wants her blood pressure pills, and there's a crate of something heavy marked FRAGILE that I suspect is neither."

Dominic sipped. The coffee was terrible in the way that only coffee brewed on a wood stove can be terrible — burnt and bitter and somehow exactly right. "What's in the crate?"

"If I told you, it wouldn't be gossip, it'd be information. Different product entirely." Marta looked out over the water, at the way the morning light was coming down the valley all gold and patient. "Meridian's been buying up diesel again. Third barge this month."

"They're drilling."

"They're *drilling*," she agreed, in a tone that suggested they were also doing several other things she preferred not to name before breakfast. "Town's happy about it, anyway. Half of Caribou Bend eats because of that concession."

"And the other half?"

"The other half works for you." She patted his arm with a hand like a bird's foot and went back up the dock, leaving him with the coffee and the cold and the arithmetic.

That was the thing about Caribou Bend that no tourism brochure would ever say out loud: the whole town hung from a single hook, and the hook was Meridian Mining. The concession paid the wages, bought the groceries, filled the church collection plate, and kept the school's furnace burning. When Meridian sneezed — and Meridian, being a company, sneezed in spreadsheets — Caribou Bend caught pneumonia. Everybody knew it. Nobody said it, the way you don't mention the load-bearing wall while you're hanging pictures on it.

Dominic finished the coffee, loaded the Beaver, and tried not to think about hooks.

* * *

By seven-thirty he was airborne, climbing out of the valley in long lazy circles, the town shrinking beneath him into something almost lovable. From three thousand feet, Caribou Bend lost its rusted trucks and its unpaid invoices and became what it pretended to be: a scatter of rooftops along a silver river, smoke rising straight up from chimneys, a place where people lived simple and true.

From three thousand feet, everything was simple and true. That was the other thing about flying. It was the only altitude at which Dominic's life made sense.

Below him the country unrolled — black spruce marching to the horizon in ranks so vast they stopped looking like trees and started looking like texture, like the fur of some enormous sleeping animal. Rivers braided and unbraided. Lakes lay everywhere, ten thousand of them, each one a mirror catching its own private sky. He'd been flying this country for eleven years and it still did something to his chest every time, some quiet hydraulic pressure behind the sternum, as if the landscape were a hand gently squeezing.

"You're a sentimental fool," he said aloud to the windshield. The Beaver didn't disagree. She just droned her steady drone, four hundred and fifty pounds of radial engine singing the same note it had been singing since before he was born.

The medicine run to Little Otter was ninety minutes of nothing, which was precisely why he loved it. No drama. No weather. Just him and the machine and the country, going where he was pointed. He checked his gauges more often than they needed checking, because checking gauges was a form of prayer, and flew with one hand loose on the yoke and the other holding a thermos lid of coffee.

At Little Otter he set the Beaver down on the lake in a landing so smooth the loons didn't even bother to complain, taxied to the beach, and spent an hour being the entire postal service, pharmacy, and newspaper of a community of fourteen people.

The insulin went to old man Kushtaka, who inspected it, grunted, and said, "You look thin, Delacroix. Your mother would feed you."

"My mother lives in Vancouver and thinks I'm a disappointment."

"So does mine. Eat anyway." There were bannock and smoked fish pressed on him whether he wanted them or not, and he wanted them, because bush pilots learn early that refusing food in the North is a sin somewhere between theft and blasphemy.

Mrs. Two Rivers took her pills and gave him the news, which was the real cargo. This was how it worked: he carried freight north and rumor south, and rumor, properly handled, weighed nothing and paid better than anything else in the hold.

"Big doings at the Meridian camp," she said, settling onto her porch step with the air of a woman opening a ledger. "They've flown in new men. Survey men. Not drillers — surveyors. And not local hires, either. Brought their own from down south."

"Surveying what? They've surveyed that ground twice already."

"That's what my nephew says. He drives the cat train for them. Says the new men ask strange questions. Ask about the old workings, up on the high plateau. The ones that were sealed." She paused, delicately, the way you pause when you're laying a card face-down on the table. "Ask about what happened up there. Ten years back."

Dominic kept his face pleasant and empty, which was a skill, and said, "Ten years back is before my time here."

"Mm," said Mrs. Two Rivers, who had been having conversations longer than he'd been alive and knew exactly what an empty face was worth. "Well. Before your time, then." She smiled at him with all the warmth of a January morning and let it go.

But he turned it over the whole flight home, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath. The old workings. Everyone in the country knew the story the way everyone knows a scar: there'd been an accident underground, years ago, back when the first company held the claim. Men had died. How many depended on who was telling it — three, or five, or nine. The mine had been sealed, the permits had changed hands, Meridian had arrived with money and lawyers and a new name for everything, and Caribou Bend had gotten very busy being grateful and had stopped asking questions. Questions were bad for the economy. Everybody knew that too.

Dominic landed back at the Bend at noon, tied off the Beaver, and went looking for lunch and trouble in roughly that order.

* * *

He found both at the Aurora Café, which was less a café than a room with a coffee urn and opinions. Ray Boudreau was holding court at the corner table, as usual. Ray ran the fuel barge in summer and the snowplow in winter and the rumor mill year-round, and he greeted Dominic with the enthusiasm of a man greeting fresh meat.

"Delacroix! Come sit. Tell me what the lake country's saying."

"The lake country's saying you still owe me for those drums of avgas from June."

"Details, details." Ray waved this away like smoke. "I hear you were up at Little Otter. Hear anything?"

"Hear that you owe me for the avgas."

Ray laughed, delighted, and pushed a chair out with his boot. Dominic sat, mostly because sitting was easier than standing, and accepted a plate of eggs he hadn't ordered and would absolutely pay for.

It went on like that for an hour. That was the rhythm of the town, especially in the shoulder season, when the fishing camps were shut down and the mining crews were rotating and there was nothing to do but talk. Dominic sat in the middle of it, drinking bad coffee, contributing just enough to keep the conversation fed, and carrying away far more than he gave. It was a kind of flying, really. You went up among the currents and came back with a load.

And the load today was heavier than usual. Three separate sources, none of whom knew each other well enough to have coordinated: Meridian had chartered extra flights. Meridian's new survey team was asking about the sealed adit on the high plateau. Meridian's man Vane — Aldous Vane, the fixer, the smooth one in the good boots who appeared whenever anything needed appearing or disappearing — had been seen at the government office in Fort Nelson, going through files with the district recorder.

"Government geology survey's coming through next week, too," Ray said, lowering his voice as if the news might spook. "Four of them. Flying in from the south. Word is they're re-examining the whole northern range. Ore estimates, water rights, the lot."

"Re-examining," Dominic repeated. "That's a word that makes companies nervous."

"Companies around here don't get nervous. Companies around here get generous." Ray grinned. "Vane bought the Legion a new roof last month. Didn't ask anybody. Just sent a crew up."

"Generous," Dominic agreed, and thought: *load-bearing wall.* He thought: *hook.* He thought about the whole town of Caribou Bend hanging from it, swinging gently, everybody pretending the wind wasn't blowing.

He paid for the eggs — Ray protested, meaninglessly — and walked back down to the dock through the late afternoon light. The town smelled the way it always smelled in September: woodsmoke and diesel and the green cold smell coming off the water, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang from the concentrator, the smell of money, which was to say the smell of rock.

His hangar stood at the end of the dock, and inside it his whole world fit in forty feet: the Beaver, a workbench, a cot he slept in more nights than not, a shelf of paperbacks gone soft with damp, and the logbooks. Eleven years of logbooks. Every flight, every hour, every engine cycle, written out in his own cramped hand. People thought pilots kept logs for the regulators. Pilots kept logs because a logbook was proof you'd existed. Proof that the hours had happened, that the weather had been beaten, that the loads had been delivered, that somebody, somewhere, had needed you enough to pay for the fuel.

He ran his thumb along the spines of them and felt the familiar restlessness come up in him like groundwater. It was always there, that itch. The feeling of being ready to go somewhere without knowing where. He'd flown supply runs in three countries and chased season work across half the continent, and every place had eventually revealed itself as a waiting room. The sky never did. The sky was the only address that had ever fit.

The trouble with the sky, of course, was that nobody lived there.

He fired up the hangar stove, put water on for coffee he didn't need, and started the evening inspection of the Beaver out of pure habit, walking the wings, draining the fuel strainer, checking the control cables with fingers that knew the feel of frayed wire the way a doctor's fingers know a bad pulse. The work steadied him. Work usually did. Competence was a kind of company, if you kept it fed.

He was halfway through the inspection when the floatplane traffic advisory crackled out of the radio he'd left monitoring the strip frequency — the scheduled mail plane, southbound from the interior settlements, calling in his position.

"—Caribou Bend, twenty minutes out, got a passenger for you folks, over."

Dominic glanced up, mildly interested. Passengers were rare enough to be weather. "Caribou Bend copies. Who've we got?"

There was a pause on the radio, the particular staticky pause of a pilot consulting a manifest he found interesting.

"Name of Marsh," the mail pilot said finally. "Nadia Marsh. Says she's looking for somebody who flies the plateau." Another pause. "Says her mother was on the government survey team."

Dominic stood there in the empty hangar with the wrench still in his hand, and outside the light was going gold and then copper over the range, and somewhere up on that high plateau the wind was moving through the spruce the way it always moved, indifferent, patient, keeping whatever it kept.

He hadn't met the survey team yet. Their charter wasn't until next week. But the rumors were already stacking up in his hold like freight, and now here was a daughter flying north into all of it, before any of it had even happened.

"Well," Dominic said to the Beaver, to the hangar, to nobody. "That's ahead of schedule."

The engine ticked as it cooled, keeping its own kind of time.
