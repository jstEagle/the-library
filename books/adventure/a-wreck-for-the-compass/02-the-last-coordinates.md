## 2. The Last Coordinates

The morning Elena Marsh walked into my hangar, she was carrying a clipboard like it had personally wronged her.

"Delacroix?" she said, reading the name off the fuselage of the Otter rather than off me, which I respected. People who read the airplane first are usually people you can trust with your life. "Four passengers, survey equipment, and a fuel drum or two. Weight and balance sheet's filled out."

I took the clipboard. Her handwriting was small and ruthless, every number squared away to the decimal, which told me she'd done this before — not the flying part, but the trusting-a-stranger-with-everything-you-own part, in enough remote places that she'd stopped pretending it didn't matter.

"That's a lot of core boxes for a routine mineral assessment," I said.

"It's a lot of mountain," she said back, and almost smiled. Almost. There was something underneath the smile, some weight she was carrying that had nothing to do with cargo. "You've flown up there? The Kettle Plateau?"

"Flew over it more times than I can count. Landed on it twice." I handed the clipboard back. "It doesn't invite you to stay."

"Good," she said. "We're not staying long."

Her team came trailing in behind her like ducklings who hadn't yet decided whether they liked water. There was Okafor, tall and cheerful, already asking if he could sit up front; there was the two of them — Brandt and Liu — arguing quietly about magnetometers the way married couples argue about whose turn it is to do dishes; and there was a kid named Reyes who looked about nineteen and carried a rock hammer on his belt like a sidearm, which, for a geologist, I suppose it is.

"You're the one who found the medicine cache last spring," Okafor said, squinting at me. "When the Hendersons' boy got sick out at Wolf Narrows?"

"Somebody had to fly it in."

"They said you went through weather that grounded everything else."

"The weather wasn't grounded. It was just weather." I started loading gear. "You want the hero version or the true version?"

Okafor grinned. "True version."

"There is no true version. It was Tuesday." I cinched a strap down over the core boxes. "Help me with the drums."

We were wheels-up by nine, into air so clean and cold it felt like drinking glacier water. Caribou Bend fell away beneath us — the town laid out along the river like an afterthought somebody had decided to keep: the airstrip, the mercantile, the school with its hockey rink half-flooded, and then the mine road running east toward the Meridian operation, where the headframe stood against the morning like a church steeple built by people who worshipped ore grades. Half the town drew its paycheck from that concession. The other half drew its paycheck from selling things to the half that did. In a place like Caribou Bend, Meridian Mining wasn't a company. It was gravity.

I climbed south and west, following the river valley until the trees gave up trying, and then the range opened up under me the way it always does — sudden, enormous, indifferent. The Kettle Plateau sat high on the shoulder of it, a broad table of grey scree and old snow ringed by ridgelines, and I brought her down on the strip of gravel and frost-killed grass that passes for a landing site at six thousand feet.

Elena Marsh was quiet most of the flight. But somewhere over the divide she leaned forward between the seats, close enough that I could smell coffee and pencil shavings, and asked her first odd question.

"These old workings," she said. "The underground ones. From before Meridian consolidated the claims. How many adits are still open?"

"Open?"

"Not collapsed. Not sealed. Accessible."

I thought about it. "Two, maybe three, depending on what the spring melt did. Why? You're doing surface geophysics, aren't you? Mag lines and soil samples?"

"We're doing whatever the data tells us to do." She said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the mountains sliding past the window, and she didn't ask anything else until we were on final approach, when she leaned forward again.

"And the shafts themselves. The deep ones. Are they mapped? Officially, I mean. On record."

"Everything's on record somewhere," I said. "Whether the record's true is another question."

That got a look from her. A long one. Like I'd said something in a language she'd been hoping someone else around here spoke.

* * *

I stayed on the plateau an hour while they unloaded, because that's the deal you make with the north: you never leave people standing beside an airplane you could have helped them unload. Okafor and Reyes hauled core boxes. Brandt and Liu assembled the mag line with the smooth choreography of people who'd been doing it for years. And Elena Marsh walked the perimeter of the campsite with her hands jammed in her pockets, looking not at the ground but at the ridgeline to the east — the one with the dark scar of the old haul road cut into it, running up toward workings nobody had touched in fifteen years.

"Beautiful country," I said, coming up beside her.

"It is." She didn't say it the way tourists say it. She said it the way a doctor says it about a patient. "Dominic. Can I ask you something else? Off the record, as it were."

"You've been talking to my ex-wife."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, then a real smile, brief and tired. "Do you know much about the history of these claims? Before Meridian?"

"I know the town would starve without them. I know the old operators pulled out fast, and I know nobody in Caribou Bend asks why, because asking why is bad for business." I shrugged. "I fly. I don't dig."

"No," she said. "But you notice things. I can tell." She turned to face me fully, and for a moment the clipboard-lady armor dropped and I saw how tired she actually was, in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "If we radio in and we're not on schedule — if we miss a pickup — you'll come looking yourself, won't you? Not wait for the official search."

"That's a strange thing to ask a man you met an hour ago."

"It's a strange plateau," she said. "There are numbers that don't add up here, and I intend to find out why. Numbers make people nervous. Nervous people do stupid things." She stuck out her hand. "Just remember what I asked."

I shook it. Her grip was cold from the altitude and hard as a drill core.

"Miss your pickup," I said, "and I'll be the first one up here. Probably before anyone knows you're missing."

"Probably before anyone admits we're missing," she corrected, and turned back to her camp, and that was the last conversation I ever had with Elena Marsh.

* * *

Two days later I came back for them at four in the afternoon, because the pickup was scheduled for four and I am constitutionally incapable of being late to a mountaintop. The weather was turning — a wall of cloud building over the western divide, fat and grey and patient — and I wanted them loaded and gone before it made good on its mood.

The camp was exactly where they'd pitched it. That was the first thing wrong.

From five hundred feet up, a living camp has a texture. Smoke. Movement. Gear scattered in the particular chaos of human beings doing human things. This camp was a photograph of a camp. Tent up, taut, guy-lines dressed. Core boxes stacked in their original grid. The mag line staked out and abandoned mid-survey, the instrument sitting on its tripod like a man waiting for a bus that isn't coming.

No smoke. No movement. No tracks.

I circled once, twice, watching the snowfield below the camp with the kind of attention you give a runway at dusk. Fresh snow had fallen overnight — an inch, maybe two — and it lay unbroken from the tent to the treeline and from the treeline to the ridge and everywhere a person could possibly walk. Four geologists do not evaporate. Four geologists leave tracks going somewhere, even if the somewhere is a crevasse. Especially if the somewhere is a crevasse.

I keyed the radio. "Kettle Plateau party, Kettle Plateau party, Delacroix on station, do you copy?"

Static answered me. Static has a sound, when you listen to it long enough — a live receiver hisses differently than a dead channel, and this was dead. Dead as a stone. Their radio had been working two days ago; Elena had confirmed the pickup window herself, crisp and clear, right down to the minute.

I put the Otter down.

Walking that last hundred yards to the camp, I felt the unease arrive ahead of me like a draft under a door. Everything was intact. That was the second thing wrong. People who walk out of a camp take things — food, parkas, the radio handset, at minimum. People who are taken from a camp leave everything, because they leave in a hurry or they leave unconscious or they don't leave at all. The tent was buttoned. Inside it, sleeping bags rolled, stove packed, food cached under a rock cairn exactly the way careful field people cache food. Elena's field notebook lay on the crate that served as a table, closed, a pencil laid across it square.

Nobody closes a notebook and squares the pencil before walking into the wilderness without their coat.

I stood in the middle of that tidy, silent camp with the wind coming down off the ridge and the storm building behind me, and I did what pilots do: I ran the checklist. Radio — dead, and not broken-dead, removed-dead; the handset was gone from its bracket. Tracks — none, in fresh snow, which was impossible. Personnel — zero. Time elapsed since last contact — forty-one hours.

Forty-one hours. In October. At six thousand feet. With a front moving in that would seal this plateau under a week of cloud.

I checked the cairn. I checked the latrine trench — unused in two days, which meant they hadn't been here either. I climbed the little rise behind the tent and looked east at the old haul road, at the dark mouth of the upper adit maybe three kilometres distant, and I thought about a woman with cold hands and a hard grip saying *numbers make people nervous.*

Then I got back in the airplane, because there was nothing else to do, and the only useful thing left in the world was speed.

Caribou Bend heard about it before I landed — I'd raised the RCMP detachment on the HF set from abeam the divide, and by the time I rolled out on the strip there were trucks and people and that awful small-town electricity that means bad news has arrived and everyone wants to touch it. The Mountie on duty, a decent kid named Prasad, took my statement twice. Meridian's man took it once, wrote very little down, and asked me mostly about weather.

The official search went out the next morning. I flew support for three days, burning fuel I couldn't afford, crisscrossing the plateau and the drainages below it while ground teams beat the timber. We found nothing. No sign, no track, no scrap of orange flagging tape. The weather closed in on day four, the way weather does, and the search coordinator — a polite man whose expenses, someone mentioned, were being handled through the company's community liaison office — called it suspended pending conditions.

Pending conditions. In October, in the northern range, pending conditions means March.

I was the last one to fly the plateau before the clouds ate it. I made one slow pass low over the camp, over the tent still standing, over the notebook still lying closed on the crate with its pencil squared across it like a sentence waiting to be finished, and I thought about Elena Marsh's hand shaking mine in the cold.

*Remember what I asked,* she'd said.

I remembered. I'd come looking. I'd come looking first, exactly like I promised, and I'd found a camp so neat it looked staged, snow with no story written on it, and a silence with four people inside it.

On the way home I flew past the Meridian headframe, because it sits square in the approach path and there's no avoiding it. The floodlights were on in the administration building, burning bright against the early dusk, and smoke stood up straight from the cookhouse chimney, and everything about it looked orderly and prosperous and accounted for.

Everything about it looked like a camp where somebody had tidied up.

I landed at Caribou Bend in the last light, tied the Otter down against the coming blow, and stood in the hangar doorway a long time, listening to the wind start to work on the tin roof. Somewhere up on the Kettle Plateau, under the first snows of winter, four people were missing, and the snow was erasing the only witness that couldn't be paid off.

And I could not stop thinking about a woman who'd asked me, one hour after we met, whether I'd come looking before anyone admitted she was gone.

She'd known. God help me, she had known.
