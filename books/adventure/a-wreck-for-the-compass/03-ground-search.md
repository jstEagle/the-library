## 3. Ground Search

The search plane went up at first light on the third day, and Dominic was not invited to fly it.

This was, he reflected as he watched it lumber off the strip in Caribou Bend — a chartered twin from Fort Nelson, all corporate livery and clean windows — probably just as well. The pilot flying it was a man named Kowalski who navigated by confidence rather than instruments, and whose confidence had never once been tested against the northern range in November. Dominic stood on the frozen mud of his apron with a coffee going cold in his fist and made a private bet with himself about how many passes Kowalski would attempt before the weather turned him around.

He gave it four.

He gave it three and a half, actually, because the last pass would be aborted halfway through when Kowalski saw the cloud deck sitting on the plateau like a lid on a pot.

"You could offer," said Rosie Tremblay, who ran the fuel cache and the town's entire supply of opinions, appearing beside him the way she always did — silently, suddenly, and already mid-conversation. She was seventy-one years old and dressed for the cold like a woman who had personally defeated winter several times and expected to do so again.

"Offer what?"

"Your services. Your plane. You know that plateau better than your own kitchen."

"My kitchen is four feet wide, Rosie."

"Exactly my point." She squinted at the departing twin. "They'll find nothing, you know. Not because there's nothing to find. Because they don't know where to look."

Dominic didn't answer that. He'd been not answering versions of it for two days, ever since he'd flown the grid pattern the RCMP had asked for and come back with nothing but cold hands and a bad feeling that had taken up residence behind his sternum like a tenant who paid no rent.

The official story was settling over Caribou Bend the way snow settles — quietly, evenly, and with the full cooperation of gravity. Four geologists missing. Misadventure presumed. Terrible weather coming. The Meridian Mining company had issued a statement expressing deep concern and offering a reward, which was generous of them, considering the survey team had been up there examining Meridian's own mountain.

"Company sent up sandwiches this morning," Rosie said. "For the searchers' families. Turkey clubs."

"That's kind."

"It's theatre. You don't feed grief, Dominic. You feed searchers." She patted his arm with a mitten that could have tenderized meat. "Go up again. Off the record. You've got the eyes for it."

"I flew the grid twice."

"Flew it. Didn't *look* at it." She turned to go, then stopped, and her voice dropped into something older and more serious than her usual register. "My husband searched for a lost trapper back in '79. Found him in April, sitting under a spruce, perfectly preserved, eleven miles from where everyone swore he must be. The wilderness doesn't hide people, Dominic. It just makes them hard to see. People hiding people — that's different. That leaves edges."

She walked off toward the pumps, and Dominic stood there holding his dead coffee and thinking about edges.

* * *

He went up anyway. Off the record, as instructed, though in Caribou Bend nothing was off the record; by evening everyone would know his fuel burn to the decimal, and Rosie would have opinions about it.

The plateau sat under broken cloud, white and enormous and indifferent. He flew low along the survey camp's coordinates, and there it was below him: the orange tents, the supply crates, the generator sled, everything arranged with the tidy geometry of people who had left in an orderly fashion or been removed in one. No scatter. No distress. From three hundred feet it looked less like an abandoned camp than a museum exhibit titled *Geologists, Mid-Work.*

He put down on the frozen shallows at the lake's edge, a quarter mile out, because the plateau itself had no strip and he'd landed here twice before and knew the ice held past the reed line. The cold hit him the moment he opened the door — the real cold, the kind that made the air feel solid, that made each breath a small financial transaction paid in lung tissue.

He walked in with his rifle slung, not because he expected trouble but because his father had taught him that a man walking alone into country where other men had vanished carried a rifle for the same reason he carried a spare pair of socks: hope for the best, preparation for the personal inconvenience.

The camp was exactly as he'd left it two days ago, except colder, and except for the searchers' tracks.

That was what stopped him. Not the absence of the team — he'd made his peace, provisionally, with the idea that they'd walked out, though every instinct he owned hated the theory. It was the tracks themselves.

There were plenty of them. Boot prints everywhere, crisscrossing, from the RCMP grid search and from Kowalski's ground crew, who'd been ferried in by ski-plane and had spent six hours combing a radius around the camp. But underneath the chaos of the official search, near the tent line, Dominic found something else.

A swept patch.

It wasn't obvious. That was the point of it. Someone had dragged a bough — spruce, by the needles still caught in the frost — across perhaps ten feet of ground between the second tent and the supply crates, erasing whatever prints had been there before. And then, after the sweeping, other boots had crossed the patch, because the searchers had walked right over it without noticing, their own prints stamped cleanly on top of the smoothed snow like signatures on a forged document.

Dominic crouched over it for a long time, so long his knees filed a formal complaint.

Wind did this sometimes. Wind scoured bare patches on ridgelines, polished drifts into strange glassy expanses. But wind didn't sweep in straight lines. Wind didn't work *around* things — and this patch worked neatly around the corner of the supply crate, preserving the crate's own frost shadow while clearing the ground beside it. Wind was an artist. This was a janitor.

Somebody had cleaned up after something.

He straightened slowly and looked at the camp differently now. The tents were intact, guy lines taut. The radio sat on its crate, antenna still erected, and he checked it without much hope — dead, as reported, but dead in the way of a drained battery, not a cut cable. The generator's fuel tank was half full. Four adults did not walk away from shelter, heat, and a working radio into a November range without taking the radio, or the fuel, or — he checked — the food, most of which was still in the crates, though someone had taken the emergency ration tins. All of them. Every one.

People planning to survive took rations. People planning to be found left notes. There was no note.

He spent two hours walking the perimeter in widening spirals, and the country gave him nothing, which was itself becoming informative. No dropped gloves. No torn fabric on the willows. Four people leaving a camp on foot leave a trail a blind man could follow for the first mile, because human beings are terrible walkers — we snag, we stumble, we drop things. This trail simply began, fully formed, forty yards from the tents, heading northeast toward the old mine access road, and even that was generous. It was more suggested than present, a few compressed patches in the snow that could have been boots or could have been wishful thinking.

Then, near the fire pit, he found the paper.

It was mostly ash. A fragment the size of a playing card, half-burned, its corner weighted under a stone as if someone had meant to finish the job and been interrupted — or as if the wind had done the interrupting and nobody had cared enough to check. Dominic picked it up with numb fingers and turned it over.

Printed text, most of it gone. What remained was a fragment of a table — columns of figures, mostly consumed — and, in the surviving corner, a letterhead fragment: a partial logo, geometric, angular, and beneath it the ghost of letters that might have read *—RIDIAN.* Or might have read anything. Ash was an honest medium but a vague one.

He should have left it. Chain of evidence, contamination, all of it — he knew the drill from a misspent youth of watching his uncle handle a dispute over trapline boundaries with the forensic rigor of a man who believed every problem was eventually a courtroom problem. But the paper was burning-fragment fragile, and the next wind would take it, and the next snow after that would bury it, and so Dominic Delacroix folded it into a tin of pipe tobacco he didn't use — the tin was for carrying things that mattered, which said something about him he chose not to examine — and put it in his inside pocket, against his chest, where it rode like a small hot coal of doubt.

* * *

He was back in Caribou Bend by four o'clock, in the blue hour when the town's lights came on one by one like a slow argument being won. He went first to the RCMP detachment, because that was the correct order of operations, and found Corporal Enns doing paperwork with the expression of a man being slowly digested by it.

Enns was young, earnest, and from Saskatoon, and he pronounced the word *plateau* like a man handling live ordnance. He listened to Dominic's report with genuine attention, took actual notes, and then got to the part Dominic had known was coming since the moment he'd walked in.

"The search's been suspended," Enns said. "Weather window's closing. Command made the call this morning."

"Suspended pending what?"

"Spring." Enns said it carefully, watching Dominic's face. "Look, Mr. Delacroix, I pushed for another week. I got told about aircraft costs, about risk to searchers, about—" he paused, choosing his words with visible effort, "—about the *sensitivity* of ongoing commercial operations in the area. Direct quote from a lawyer, that. Sensitivity. Of commercial operations. While four people are missing."

"Who told you?"

"A gentleman from Meridian's head office. Very polite. Very sorry. Brought his own coffee." Enns set down his pen. "I'm telling you this because you flew those people in and I figure you're owed the shape of it. The file stays open. Officially. Unofficially, the file has been advised that further searching would be inconvenient."

"Inconvenient to whom?"

Enns looked at him for a moment, and behind the Saskatoon earnestness Dominic saw something flicker — the look of a young officer realizing that the north operated by different physics than the training depot, and that some of those physics were gravitational in nature, pulling everything toward money.

"To the same people who brought turkey clubs," Enns said. "You found something up there. I can tell. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face my grandmother gets when she's decided not to say something at Christmas dinner." He slid a card across the desk. "If you find anything else — anything — you call me before you call anyone. Not because I can promise anything. Because somebody should write it down who isn't on Meridian's Christmas list."

Dominic took the card. Outside, the temperature was falling through twenty below, and the streetlights of Caribou Bend burned in halos of ice-fog, and somewhere a dog was making its opinions about the moon publicly known.

* * *

Rosie was at the Northern Lights Café, because Rosie was always at the Northern Lights Café, occupying the corner booth with the permanence of a geological feature. Dominic slid in across from her and ordered coffee he didn't want, because the booth required tribute.

"Well?" she said.

"There was a swept patch. Between the tents. Somebody erased boot prints with a spruce bough."

Rosie absorbed this the way she absorbed everything — completely, and without any change of expression that a stranger would notice. But her hands, wrapped around her mug, went very still.

"And?"

"And a burned piece of paper with what might be a Meridian letterhead on it. And the rations gone, everything else left. And a trail that starts forty yards from camp like somebody laid it down with a ruler." He turned the coffee mug in his hands. "Rosie, four people don't vanish from a camp and leave it tidier than they found it. Either they walked out clean as ghosts, or—"

"Or somebody wanted it to look like they walked out clean as ghosts." She nodded slowly. "Edges," she said. "I told you. Edges."

"The corporal says the search is suspended. Company called it. Cited costs and sensitivity."

"Sensitivity." Rosie snorted, a sound like a small avalanche. "I've buried two husbands and a dog in this town, and I have never once heard death described as sensitive." She leaned forward, and the café light caught the frost-white of her hair. "Dominic. Listen to me. I've watched Meridian run this town for fifteen years. They pay for the hockey rink, they pay for the clinic, they pay half the wages in the valley, and everybody knows it and everybody's grateful and nobody asks what's under the mountain, because asking is bad for the gravy train." Her voice dropped. "Elena Marsh asked. I met her, you know. Day you flew them out. Sharp woman. Bought a thermos off me and asked why the old shaft road was gated when the maps showed it open. I told her to ask Meridian. She smiled like I'd confirmed something."

Dominic sat with that. Behind the counter, Walt was arguing amiably with the fryer, and the radio played something country, and outside the window the night came down over the range like a lid.

"If they're alive," he said slowly, "somebody's holding them. If they're dead, somebody's hiding them. Either way—" He stopped, because the sentence ended somewhere he wasn't ready to go.

"Either way," Rosie finished for him, "the person who swept that snow is still walking around on two legs, and probably drives a nice truck." She reached across the table and tapped the pocket where the tobacco tin sat, with unerring accuracy. "And you're carrying the only piece of that mountain that says otherwise. So the question, bush pilot, is what you're going to do with it."

Dominic looked out the dark window, toward the hangar, toward the north, toward the plateau sitting under its snow like a secret under a blanket.

"I'm going to keep it somewhere safer than my shirt pocket," he said. "And then I'm going to figure out who sweeps snow in the middle of nowhere, and why."

Rosie smiled, and raised her coffee an inch. "Attaboy," she said. "Mind the edges."

He was halfway home, boots crunching on the packed snow of the main street, when he noticed the truck.

It was parked across from his hangar, engine idling, exhaust curling up through the cone of the streetlight. Dark paint, government-plain, new tires. Nobody inside that he could see, though the cab's shadow was deep, and the idle was the lazy expensive idle of an engine kept warm for someone planning to sit a while.

Dominic stopped in the middle of the street and looked at it, and it looked — as far as a truck can look — back.

In Caribou Bend, you parked and shut off. Fuel cost too much and idle time was gossip currency. An idling truck outside his hangar at nine o'clock at night was a message, and the message had excellent fuel economy.

He walked past it without hurrying, unlocked the hangar, and stood in the dark among the smell of oil and canvas and cold metal, listening to the truck idle outside until, ten minutes later, it pulled away with unhurried courtesy, headlights sweeping the frost off the fence posts one by one.

Dominic stood a long time in the dark.

Then he took the tobacco tin out of his jacket, considered it, and went looking for a better hiding place — because whatever Elena Marsh had found on that plateau, it appeared she hadn't been the only one asking questions.

And somebody, very politely, very patiently, was keeping track of who.
