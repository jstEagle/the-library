## 4. The Daughter Who Came North

The mail plane came in on a Thursday, which was unusual, because the mail plane usually came on Tuesdays and because it was carrying a passenger, which it almost never did. Dominic Delacroix stood on the dock at Caribou Bend with a crate of engine oil under one arm and watched the little Norseman bump down onto the gray water, and he thought, with the particular dread of a man who has spent three weeks being looked at sideways by an entire town: *that's for me.*

He was right. It was always better to be right about the bad things. At least then you could skip the surprise.

The woman who climbed out onto the float was maybe thirty, wearing a city coat that had already given up on the north — wool gone dark at the shoulders with spray, boots that had never met a trailhead, hair escaping from under a knit hat like she'd been fighting it the whole flight and losing. She carried one bag and a folder of papers clamped under her elbow the way other people carried weapons.

She looked at the town — the single street, the company store, the Meridian banner over the hotel reading *PROUD PARTNER OF THE NORTH* — and her expression suggested she'd been promised a civilization and delivered a rumor of one.

"You're Delacroix," she said. Not a question. She'd picked him out of the two men on the dock correctly, which wasn't hard; old Pete Kowalski was seventy and mostly beard.

"Depends who's asking."

"Nadia Marsh." She let that land and watched his face do what faces did when they heard the name. "Elena Marsh's daughter."

Dominic set down the crate of oil. There were perhaps six people in Caribou Bend who would say Elena Marsh's name out loud anymore, and none of them said it comfortably. The official finding had come down from Yellowknife eleven days ago — *misadventure, exposure, no evidence of foul play* — four words doing the work of a eulogy, and the town had exhaled with a relief so collective you could hear the windows rattle. Meridian Mining had even paid for flowers. Carnations, someone said. The cheapest flower there is.

"I'm sorry about your mother," Dominic said, and meant it, and watched her decide not to accept it.

"Are you." She looked past him at the plane tied up at the dock, the de Havilland Beaver with its patched cowling and its paint gone matte from ten thousand hours of sun. "You flew them in. You were the last person to see them alive. That's what the RCMP report says. Last person to see them alive." She repeated it like she was checking the weight of it. "Do you know how many times I've read that sentence?"

"No idea."

"Forty-one." She adjusted the folder under her arm. "I've read it forty-one times, because I've read the entire report forty-one times, and every time I get to that sentence I think: *someone saw my mother alive and well and standing on a plateau, and then nobody ever saw her again, and everyone agreed to stop asking questions in under three weeks.*"

"The search—"

"The search was called off because of weather," Nadia said, "in September. In the subarctic. Where it's been weather since roughly the Pleistocene." Something moved behind her eyes then, something that wasn't anger, something underneath the anger that was much worse, and for a second Dominic thought she might cry, and thought also that she would rather chew gravel. "I'm sorry. That was — I've been practicing not saying that to anyone official for two weeks and apparently I'm saving it all up for you."

"That's fine," Dominic said. "I'm used to weather complaints."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Gone immediately, but it had been there. He filed it away.

* * *

She wouldn't take a room at the hotel. "Meridian owns the hotel," she said, as if this explained everything, and it did explain everything, so he put her up instead in the bunkroom above his hangar, which had last hosted a fisheries biologist who'd left behind a coffee mug shaped like a fish and a profound sense of abandonment. Nadia surveyed the bunk, the fish mug, the window looking out over the gray lake, and said, "It's perfect," in the tone of a woman who meant *it's a bunker,* and unpacked her folder onto the table like a surgeon laying out instruments.

By evening half the town knew Elena Marsh's daughter was staying at Delacroix's hangar, and by morning the knowledge had acquired the usual embellishments. Pete Kowalski brought them bannock and gossip in equal measure and warned Dominic, sotto voce, that Meridian's man Vane had been seen at the airstrip office twice this week asking after schedules. "Man asks after schedules," Pete said, "like a man asks after your health before he takes your temperature with something unpleasant."

Dominic thanked him for the bannock and the metaphor.

Nadia worked. That was the thing that struck him first, watching her across two days — she didn't weep, didn't drink, didn't pace. She read. She had the coroner's report, the RCMP file obtained through a friend-of-a-friend lawyer, the transport ministry's weather logs for the week of the disappearance, and she cross-referenced them with a patience that reminded him uncomfortably of her mother. Elena Marsh had checked his fuel calculations twice before boarding, politely, thoroughly, the way you check a stranger's arithmetic when lives depend on it. The daughter did the same thing to documents.

On the third evening she cornered him. Literally — he came back from fueling the Beaver and she was standing between him and the hangar door, arms crossed, breath fogging, and he realized with a small internal sigh that he'd been maneuvered, that the casual questions over dinner had been reconnaissance.

"You logged the flight," she said. "September ninth. You have the coordinates in your head if not on paper."

"They're in my head."

"Then give them to me."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to walk into whatever's left of that camp, and I'd rather do it without wandering around the plateau like a tourist."

"You're not walking anywhere." He said it mildly, setting down the fuel hose. "That's a two-day hike from the nearest road through country that kills experienced people. You wore sneakers to the dock."

"These are hiking shoes."

"They're shoes that hiked once, to a parking lot."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her decide, again, not to cry, and he understood suddenly that this was her whole war right now — grief on one side, dignity on the other, and herself in the middle holding both off with her elbows. It was exhausting just to watch. He'd seen it before, in families of the lost, and he knew which side usually won, and it was rarely dignity.

"Mr. Delacroix." Her voice went quiet, which was worse than loud. "My mother and I hadn't spoken properly in six years. Six years, over a stupid fight about — it doesn't matter what it was about. A man, a career, the usual demolition. And I told myself there was time. There's always time, until the Mounties send you a form letter with the word *misadventure* in it and you realize the time ran out while you were waiting for her to call first." She stopped. Breathed. The fog of her breath hung between them like a small ghost. "So I can't bring her back and I can't apologize to her, and I have looked very carefully at every legal avenue available to me, and they are all closed, because everyone signed everything and everyone agrees the wilderness took four people and the wilderness cannot be sued. Which leaves me exactly one thing, Mr. Delacroix, and it's this: somebody erased those people, and I am going to find out who, and I am going to be so thorough about it that whoever did it will dream about me."

There was a silence. Somewhere across the lake a loon made its idiot beautiful sound.

"And you think I can help," Dominic said.

"I think you flew them in. I think you noticed things — pilots notice things, it's your whole job. And I think—" she hesitated, and for the first time since stepping off the mail plane she looked uncertain, young, "—I think you don't believe the verdict either. I've watched you for three days. You flinch when someone says the word *misadventure.* You look northwest, toward the range. Every time."

He hadn't known he was doing that. He did it now, involuntarily, and caught her almost smiling.

"Fine," he said. "Come inside. There's tea. It's terrible. The fisheries guy left it."

* * *

She showed him the letter at the table, under the hanging work lamp, with the fish mug steaming between them.

"My mother wrote to me in August. Before the survey. First letter in two years." Nadia handled the envelope carefully, but her hands were steady now — steadier than they'd been all week, and he understood that too: purpose was a kind of painkiller. "I want you to understand what that means. Two years of silence, and then she writes, and it's not personal. It's not *how are you, I miss you.* It's business. She couldn't even reach across the fight without hiding behind geology." A breath. "But she reached."

The letter itself was short. Elena Marsh had written the way she'd spoken, Dominic remembered — economical, precise, every sentence load-bearing. Most of it was careful small talk with the seams showing. But near the end:

*I shouldn't write this, so I'll write it badly. The numbers for the East Ridge extension don't add. Not slightly — not the way numbers normally fail to add, with an honest error you can chase down. They add wrong. Deliberately wrong, in a direction that benefits exactly one party. I've requested the original assay records from the '14 underground program and been refused twice. When a company refuses a geologist access to its own historical data, it isn't protecting trade secrets. It's protecting a grave.*

*Nadia, if anything happens to me, don't let them call it misadventure.*

*Love, Mom.*

"P.S.," Nadia said, her voice cracking only slightly on the word. "She signed it *Mom.* First time in six years. She hid the important part in the middle and signed it *Mom.*" She pressed the flat of her hand against the paper, not quite touching the ink. "And I sat on it. For five weeks. Because I was still angry, because answering her meant ending the fight and I wasn't ready to lose it, and then the mail plane came with the news, and now I will spend the rest of my life knowing that my mother asked me for help and I let it sit in a drawer in Vancouver next to the takeout menus."

"It was five weeks," Dominic said. "People sit on letters. It doesn't make you—"

"Don't." Gentle, final. "Don't absolve me. I need the guilt. It's the only tool I've got that works." She straightened, and the lamp caught her face, and he saw the resemblance to Elena for the first time clearly — same stubborn architecture around the jaw, same eyes that assessed everything including you. "Here's what matters. *Numbers that don't add.* *Original assay records from the '14 program.* *Protecting a grave.* My mother was a careful writer. She chose those words."

"She was very careful," Dominic said quietly. He thought of the burned fragment he'd found at the edge of the search grid, the corner of a page with a column of figures surviving, the rest charred to lace. He hadn't told anyone about that. He found himself telling her now. "There was a document burned at the camp site. Someone tried to destroy paperwork and didn't finish the job. The Mounties photographed it. It's in the file as *insignificant debris, likely camp waste.*"

"Show me."

He got his own copy of the file — he'd paid a Yellowknife clerk generously for it, a fact he kept to himself — and spread the photograph beside the letter. The surviving corner showed the top of a table: assay values, sample numbers, dates. Dates from 2014.

Nadia stared at it for a long moment. Then she laughed — one short, hard, disbelieving note, nothing like humor.

"They burned fourteen-year-old assay records," she said. "At a fresh campsite. Why would anyone burn *historical* documents at a *current* survey? Unless the current survey had turned up something that connected to the old records. Unless my mother found the new fraud by tripping over the old one." She looked up at him, and the grief in her face had changed state entirely, like water becoming ice — same substance, new structure, capable now of cutting. "This isn't a search anymore, Mr. Delacroix. This is an audit."

"It's dangerous," he said. "Whatever it is. Vane's been asking after schedules. The company called off the search. Somebody burned documents and somebody else decided four missing people were weather."

"Good," Nadia Marsh said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"If it were safe, it would mean I was wrong. If it's dangerous, it means I'm right, and it means they're afraid, and frightened people make mistakes." She began stacking her papers with quick, precise movements, squaring the edges, a woman building an arsenal out of photocopies. "Tomorrow I want to see the permit filings at the territorial office. The East Ridge concession application. Everything public, everything boring, everything nobody reads. Fraud hides in boring places. My mother taught me that, actually. One of the last things she taught me before the six silent years." She paused at the door of the bunkroom, folder under her arm, and for a moment the fury dropped away and she just looked tired, and young, and alone in a town full of strangers who wouldn't meet her eye.

"Thank you," she said. "For the tea. For listening. For not calling me crazy."

"Not yet," Dominic said.

The almost-smile again, there and gone. "Get some sleep, Mr. Delacroix. Tomorrow we find out what the numbers don't add to."

She closed the door. Dominic sat a while longer at the table with the photograph of the burned page, the lamp buzzing, the loon calling across the dark water. Outside, far down the single street, a truck idled a long time outside the Meridian office before driving away.

He looked northwest, toward the range, where the first snows were already whitening the high passes, and thought: *she signs her letters Mom, and she audits her grief, and God help anyone who stands between her and either one.*

Then he got out his logbook — the real one, the one he'd started keeping in pencil after the fire-safety lecture he'd slept through — and under the entry for September ninth, beneath the coordinates of the plateau, he added a single line:

*Passenger's daughter arrived. Asking questions. Good ones.*

And below that, because the sky had taught him honesty: *We're going to need more than good questions.*
