## 5. An Old Warrant

The marten had been dead for three days when Silas Crowe decided to go to town, and the decision had nothing to do with the marten.

It had to do with the paper.

He'd found the fragment on the plateau's southern shoulder, ten days after the searchers had packed out and declared the wilderness guilty of murder by misadventure. Silas didn't search for things anymore. He trapped. But a trapline is a slow argument with the land, and you end up walking places other men don't, and you end up seeing what the land is holding onto. The land holds onto paper the way it holds onto bones — grudgingly, and only until the weather gets bored.

The fragment had been tucked under a stone, which was the first wrong thing. Weather doesn't tuck. Weather throws and scatters and shreds. Something had put that paper under the stone to keep it from burning all the way, or to keep it from blowing away, or because whoever put it there had been interrupted. It was the corner of a page, maybe four inches of it, scorched brown at the edge like toast nobody meant to make. The rest was ash somewhere downwind.

He'd carried it home in his breast pocket, and for ten days it had sat on his kitchen table under a tin cup, and every morning he'd looked at it the way a man looks at a dog he knows is going to bite somebody.

The paper had a letterhead. Most of it was gone, but the bottom right corner had survived, and in the bottom right corner of corporate letterhead — Silas knew this from a decade of warrants, subpoenas, and evidence bags — you find the signature block. This one had a stylized *M*, a mountain peak drawn inside it with two confident strokes, and beneath it, in small clean type: *Meridian Resources Ltd. — Permits & Compliance Division*.

He'd stared at that little mountain for a long time.

Ten years ago, he'd stared at the same little mountain on a different piece of paper, in a different office, in a different life. That paper had been a settlement agreement, and Silas Crowe had been a corporal with the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, and he had signed a receipt for a box of evidence that he was told to lose, and he lost it, and the box had contained photographs.

He hadn't known about the photographs when he signed. That was the story he told himself, anyway, the story he'd told the internal review, the story that had gotten him eighteen months of suspension instead of charges. *I didn't know what was in the box.* True, in the way that a man who closes his eyes doesn't know what's in front of him.

The marten in the trap was incidental. But a man needs a reason to fly to Caribou Bend that isn't *I found a piece of paper and it frightens me*, and fur prices being what they were, the marten would do.

* * *

Dominic Delacroix's hangar smelled the way Silas imagined heaven might, if heaven were run by a practical man: gasoline, hot metal, coffee, and engine oil, all of it warmed by a stove that was working harder than the furnace in any church. The Beaver sat with her cowling open like a patient mid-examination, and Dominic was elbow-deep in her, a wrench in one hand and a look on his face that suggested the engine had said something about his mother.

"Trapper," Dominic said, without pulling his head out of the engine. "Heard your snowmachine from the lake. You're a week early for the mail run."

"Fur's ready when the fur's ready." Silas held up the pelt. "Marten. Prime. You want first look before the buyer comes through?"

Dominic emerged, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag and was now mostly grease. "Always. Though I'll warn you, my opinion of fur is worth exactly what you paid for it." He took the pelt, turned it over, and made a noise that was either admiration or indigestion. "Nice. What'd you want for it?"

"Fair price. And a question."

Dominic's eyebrows went up. "Those usually come bundled?"

"Not from me." Silas looked around the hangar — at the logbooks on the shelf, at the survey maps pinned above the workbench, at the photograph of the plateau with a red circle drawn on it in someone's impatient hand. "Question's about the Marsh woman. The survey team."

The warmth went out of the hangar by a degree or two. Dominic set the pelt down carefully, the way you set down something that might be evidence. "What about her?"

"Heard they found nothing. Heard the company's calling it misadventure. Heard the search got called off early on account of weather." Silas let that sit. "I trapped that country for nine winters, Delacroix. That plateau in October? Weather's a reason, sure. It's also a convenient one. Weather doesn't sign anything."

"You sound like a policeman."

"I sound like a man who used to be one." Silas reached into his coat — slowly, because a big man reaching into his coat in a hangar full of aviation fuel should move slowly — and brought out the tin cup, and set it on the workbench, and lifted it off the paper fragment. "Found this on the south shoulder. Under a rock. Weather doesn't tuck paper under rocks."

Dominic picked it up. Silas watched him read it — watched the young man's eyes go from the scorched edge to the little mountain with its two confident strokes, and watched something happen in his face that Silas recognized, because the same thing had happened in his own face ten days ago and was still happening, off and on, like a bad tooth.

"That's Meridian's letterhead," Dominic said. "That's from the survey team's paperwork. Elena Marsh had permit filings, maps, the whole kit. It burned."

"It burned *partway*. And then somebody hid the part that didn't." Silas took a breath. He'd rehearsed this on the walk in from the lake, and the rehearsal hadn't helped, because the next part was the part he'd been avoiding for ten years. "Delacroix, I need to tell you something, and you're not going to like me much after, and I'd take that as fair."

Dominic set the fragment down. "Go on."

"Ten years back, I was RCMP out of the northern detachment. There was an incident at the old Meridian underground workings — the ones they sealed. Men died down there. More men than the company reported, and the ones they reported died of things they didn't die of. I caught the case. I was good at my job. I had the evidence — statements, photographs, a mine foreman willing to talk if we could keep him breathing long enough to testify." Silas's voice did something he didn't authorize it to do; it went rough. "And then my inspector called me in, and there was a lawyer from a firm I'd never heard of, and a settlement agreement, and a box. I signed a receipt for that box and the box went into a storage locker and the locker was cleaned out two weeks later, and the case died, and the foreman moved to Alberta and stopped answering letters, and I —" He stopped. Started again. "I took the suspension. I didn't fight it. Because fighting it meant explaining what I'd signed, and I couldn't explain it in a way that didn't end with *I knew something was wrong and I signed anyway*."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls of the hangar, the wind found a gap and worried at it.

"They took my badge," Silas said. "And the honest truth, Delacroix, the truth I don't say out loud, is that they should've taken it. I've been out at the trapline nine years telling myself the quiet was penance. It wasn't penance. Penance costs something. The quiet was just hiding with better scenery."

Dominic was quiet a while. He picked up the paper fragment again, turned it toward the light, and Silas saw him do the thing Silas himself had done — hold the little mountain up against the memory of every other little mountain.

"The burn pattern," Silas said. "You want to know why I really came in. It's not the letterhead. Letterheads burn all the time. It's the *way* it burned." He touched the scorched edge, gentle, like touching a scar. "Somebody burned a stack of documents in a coffee can — you can see the curl, the way paper curls when it's burned in a small hot container, fast, one handful at a time. I've seen that curl before. Ten years ago. The box I signed for? Part of what was in it, before it was in the box, had been through a fire. The photographs were prints made from negatives that survived a fire exactly like this one. Same curl. Same fast, hot, careful burn." He looked up. "Same hands, or same instructions, or same company manual. I don't know which. But I know that curl, and it knows me, and it's back."

Dominic sat down on his work stool, which put them nearly eye to eye, which Silas appreciated, because being looked down at was a habit he'd earned.

"There was a woman here last week," Dominic said. "Nadia Marsh. Elena's daughter. She's got a letter from her mother — last letter — talking about numbers that don't add. She and I have been going through Elena's flight notes, what I remember of them, what Nadia's got. And now you're telling me the same company that's sitting on the survey team's disappearance burned the same kind of evidence ten years ago, and you were the policeman who let it happen."

"Who let it happen and then came to your hangar to say so," Silas said. "Which is either the bravest thing I've done in a decade or the stupidest, and I genuinely don't know which. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking you to use me. I know how these people work. I know what a compliance division does when it finds something it doesn't like — it doesn't like it *permanently*. And I know that if Elena Marsh found what I think she found, then somewhere in that range there's a paper trail, and paper trails have two ends, and the company is going to be burning from both ends until the snow seals the passes."

"And you want to be there when they do."

"I want to be there when they *try*." Silas stood, joints announcing themselves the way they did now, each one filing a complaint. "I'm not looking for redemption, Delacroix. I've read enough books to know redemption's a word for people who get to feel better. I'm looking for a debt. There's a difference. Redemption's a gift somebody gives you. A debt is something you pay whether anybody's watching or not, and I've got ten years of interest owing."

Dominic studied him for a long moment. Then he did something Silas hadn't expected: he laughed, short and tired, and rubbed his face with the grease rag, which explained some of the marks on it.

"You know what's funny? Nadia said almost the same thing. Not about debts. About — she said her mother used to tell her that the wilderness forgives nothing, it just keeps records. I thought it was grief talking." He nodded at the fragment. "Turns out it's bookkeeping."

"Everything's bookkeeping," Silas said. "That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anybody believes."

Dominic stood and offered his hand, and Silas took it, and the handshake was firm and brief and businesslike, which was exactly right. No absolution in it. Just an agreement.

"Come by the hotel tomorrow," Dominic said. "Nadia will want to hear this. Fair warning — she's angry in a way that makes weather look forgiving, and she'll take it out on you, because you're here and the people who actually did it aren't."

"I've had ten years of taking it out on myself," Silas said. "It'll be a nice change of pace to have help."

He collected his marten pelt, took his payment, and walked out into the blue cold of the afternoon feeling lighter than he had any right to feel and more frightened than he'd felt in nine years, both at once, which he was beginning to understand was simply what hope felt like when it had been locked in a drawer for a decade.

Behind him, in the hangar, he heard Dominic say something to the airplane — pilots did that, talked to their machines like they were wives or horses — and the words carried on the still air clearly enough:

"Well, girl. Looks like we're getting the band back together."

Silas smiled into his collar and started the long walk back to the lake, and the little mountain with its two confident strokes rode in his breast pocket over his heart, where debts belong.
