## 6. Hangar Fire

The smell woke him before the light did.

Dominic Delacroix had spent eleven years sleeping above his hangar at Caribou Bend, and in that time he had learned to read the night by nose as much as by ear. Jet fuel had a sweetness to it. Hydraulic fluid was sharper, almost medicinal. The cold itself had a smell up here — iron and pine and old snow — and when that smell changed, a man paid attention.

What came through the floorboards now was kerosene and burning varnish.

He was out of bed and down the stairs before his brain finished assembling the thought, bare feet on frozen boards, and he flung open the hangar's side door into a wall of orange.

The fire had started low and aft, where he kept the drums of avgas stacked against the far wall — except the drums were intact, which told him everything he needed to know. Nobody had knocked one over. Somebody had poured something *around* them, along the baseboards, patient as a man laying out a fishing line, and then lit it from the corner nearest the door. His door. The one he'd come through.

Which meant whoever set it had expected him to come through it.

He stood there for exactly two seconds — long enough for the heat to press against his face like an open palm — and then he went back inside instead of forward, because the Otter was in there, and so were his logbooks.

"Delacroix!" That was Pete Kowalczyk from across the field, the fuel-truck man, already running with a extinguisher that was going to be about as useful as a teacup. "Get back, you idiot!"

"The logbooks—"

"They're *wood pulp*, Dom!"

So were airplanes, technically, and nobody ever let that stop anybody. Dominic got three steps into the smoke before the heat shoved him sideways like a bouncer with opinions. He found the shelf by memory, blind, eyes streaming, hands walking along spines — 2014, 2015, 2016 — and pulled what he could carry, which turned out to be four binders and Elena Marsh's flight notes, the copy she'd left with him because she trusted bush pilots the way she didn't trust filing cabinets.

Then the ceiling joist over the Otter's tail let go with a crack like a rifle shot, and Pete got an arm around his collar and hauled him out into the snow, and the two of them sat down hard in a drift and watched eleven years of Dominic Delacroix go up in sparks that rose into the dark like the world's saddest fireworks display.

* * *

By dawn there wasn't much left to argue with. The Otter was a black skeleton, wings folded inward like a bird that had given up mid-thought. The fire marshal — who in Caribou Bend was also the postmaster, the coroner, and the man who ran the hockey pool — walked the ashes with his hands in his pockets and pronounced, eventually, the word everyone already knew.

"Accelerant." Earl Fennick crouched, poked at a charred line in the concrete with a pencil. "Poured, not spilled. Started away from the fuel drums, which any real accident would've gone straight for." He looked up at Dominic over his glasses. "You want me to write 'accidental' anyway? Because I can write 'accidental.' I've written 'accidental' for better men than whoever did this."

"No," Dominic said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Write what it is."

Earl wrote what it was. Then he stood, brushed off his knees, and said quietly, "Meridian's been asking after you, you know. Down at the office. Friendly-like. Twice this week."

"I know."

"Just so we're all reading the same book." Earl tucked his pencil away. "I liked your mother, Dom. She'd have wanted you reading the same book too."

* * *

Nadia Marsh arrived at nine in the morning, which meant she'd heard about the fire by eight, which meant half the town had been awake watching it burn and the other half had been woken by phone calls from the first half. She came up the hangar apron in her dead mother's parka — he recognized it now, the fox-fur ruff, the way she wore it like borrowed armor — carrying two coffees and an expression that could have stripped paint.

"You slept *above* it," she said.

"I sleep above it every night."

"And last night you nearly died above it."

"Last night I nearly died below it, if we're being technical. The dying would've happened downstairs." He took the coffee because refusing seemed like more effort than accepting. His hands weren't quite steady, and he watched her notice, and he watched her decide not to mention it, and he was grateful in a way he didn't have words for yet.

She looked at the wreck for a long moment. The Otter's tail stuck up out of the ash like a question mark.

"My mother kept her notes with you," Nadia said. "Because she trusted you."

"She said filing cabinets burned."

"Filing cabinets do burn." Nadia turned to look at him, and under all that fury there was something rawer, something that had been awake all night too. "Dom. They didn't burn your plane because they hate planes."

"No," he agreed. "They burned my plane because somebody thinks I'm looking."

"*Are* you looking?"

He thought about lying to her, mostly out of some ancient protective instinct that had never once done him any good, and decided against it. "I've been reading her notes. The ones she left. There are numbers in them that don't sit right — tonnage figures, assay values. And there's a page about the old underground workings at the Meridian site, shafts that are supposed to be sealed, with survey dates that don't match anything official."

Nadia set her coffee down on a drum — carefully, deliberately, the way people do when putting it down slowly is the only thing keeping them from throwing it. "Show me," she said.

"There's a problem with showing you."

"What problem?"

"The notes were in the hangar." He nodded at the ash. "Along with my logbooks. Eleven years of flying records. Every hour, every landing, every charter, every medicine run. Gone." He heard his own voice flatten out and didn't much care. "You know what a pilot is without his logbooks? On paper? A guy who claims he can fly."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. Then she picked her coffee back up and drank it, grimly, like medicine.

"So we don't need paper," she said. "My mother used to say the only database worth trusting is the one between your ears. Come on. You're going to sit down and tell me every single flight you can remember, and I'm going to write it down, and we're going to rebuild whatever they think they just erased." A pause. The ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed her face. "They made a mistake, you know."

"How do you figure?"

"They gave us a fire. My mother always said grief is fuel. Turns out so is arson."

* * *

They worked in the bunkhouse above the ruined hangar, because it hadn't burned and because Dominic refused — on principle, and also because his knees hurt — to be anywhere else. Nadia cleared his kitchen table with the efficiency of someone raised by a scientist, stacking his dishes into a tower he privately admired, and laid out a legal pad, three pens, and Elena's surviving letter, which had been in Nadia's coat pocket and therefore still existed.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "First flight you ever logged."

"That'll take all day."

"We have all day. We have until the passes close, which is longer than all day but shorter than you'd think." She clicked a pen. "Go."

And here was the strange thing, the thing Dominic would think about later: it worked. It worked better than it had any right to. Because a logbook isn't really a record of flights — it's a record of *days*, and days turn out to be sticky things once you start pulling at them. He remembered the March morning he'd flown the vet out to the Kowalczyks' line camp because a mare was colicking, and remembering the mare meant remembering the weather, and the weather meant remembering he'd filed a flight plan late because he'd argued with the dispatcher about coffee. One memory led to another like footprints across a lake.

"June fourteenth," he said, eyes closed. "Charter, four souls, Meridian rotation crew. Wind out of the northwest at fifteen gusting twenty-five. Landed at the concession strip at 0940."

"Souls' names?"

"Briggs, Okafor, two guys whose names I never caught. Big fellas. One of them snored the whole way in."

Nadia wrote it all down, including the snoring, because she said details were how you proved a memory was real and not invented. She was good at this. Better than good. She had her mother's habit of asking the question behind the question — not *where did you fly* but *what did you see when you got there*, not *who was aboard* but *why them, why that day, why does your voice change when you say that name*.

It was on the second pot of coffee, somewhere around reconstructing the autumn charters, that she stopped writing.

"Say that again," she said.

"September third. Survey support flight. I flew Elena and three of her team out to the plateau—"

"No, before that. The cargo manifest."

Dominic frowned, reaching back. "Drill cores. Sample crates. Standard."

"How many crates?"

"Six." He could see them, suddenly, vividly — strapped down in the Otter's belly, stenciled with Meridian's logo, heavier than drill cores had any business being. "Except—" He opened his eyes. "Except the weight and balance didn't work out. Six crates of core samples should've come in under four hundred pounds. I remember doing the math on the pad and coming up with six-eighty, and Elena telling me the scale at the concession was miscalibrated, and me deciding it wasn't my job to argue with a geologist about rocks."

Nadia's pen had gone still against the pad. "Two hundred and eighty pounds unaccounted for," she said softly. "On a flight to a survey site. Two weeks before she vanished."

"It could be nothing. Density variance, wet cores—"

"Dom." She looked up at him, and there was no fury in her face anymore, just the thing underneath it, sharp and bright and terribly awake. "My mother spent her whole career weighing things. Rocks, evidence, arguments. She did not miscount two hundred and eighty pounds." She tapped the letter, the line about *numbers that don't add*. "I think this is what she meant. I think she found something in those crates that weighed more than it was supposed to, and I think she knew exactly what it meant, and I think that's why she left a copy of her notes with a bush pilot instead of a filing cabinet."

Outside, the ash of the hangar was still smoking faintly in the cold, little threads of grey rising off the ruin. Dominic looked at it, and felt the fear he'd been carrying since dawn shift its shape — not leaving, nothing ever really left, but changing state, the way water changes to ice. Fear you run from, and fear you load onto a sled and haul.

"Somebody burned eleven years of my life last night," he said. "To stop me from adding up numbers."

"Yes."

"So let's add them up."

* * *

Silas Crowe came at dusk, the way he seemed to do everything — without announcement, without vehicle noise, just suddenly present at the edge of the yard like a thing the forest had decided to return. He stood looking at the wreckage for a long time, hat brim low, breath steaming, and when he finally spoke his voice was quiet as falling snow.

"Burn pattern's wrong for accident," he said. "Poured trail, lit from the exit side. Whoever did it wanted the fire to meet you at the door."

"That's what Earl said."

"Earl's right." Crowe couched by a melted drum, studied something in the ash only he could read. "I've seen this signature before. Ten years back." He didn't say more, and the not-saying had a weight to it, a door held almost open.

Nadia stepped out of the bunkhouse doorway, arms crossed against the cold, and looked the old trapper up and down with frank appraisal. "You knew my mother," she said. Not a question.

"Heard of her," Crowe allowed. "Heard of you too, lately. Heard the three of you are the only people in Caribou Bend still asking questions." He straightened, joints cracking like green wood, and looked at Dominic with eyes that had seen too many winters and one unforgivable spring. "Whoever poured that kerosene is going to come back. Men like that always come back. Question is whether you'll be ready, or whether you'll be another entry in somebody else's ledger."

"We're rebuilding her flight notes," Nadia said. "From memory. Tonight."

Something moved behind Crowe's beard — not quite a smile, but the place where one might live. "From memory," he repeated. "Huh." He glanced back at the treeline, at whatever waited for him there, then at the burned hangar, and Dominic watched a man stand at a crossroads in plain view and pretend he wasn't.

"Well," Crowe said at last, stepping past them toward the bunkhouse light. "If we're doing this, somebody ought to make coffee that isn't an insult. And then you two are going to tell me everything, starting with why Meridian's fixer asked about *me*, by name, at the trading post on Tuesday."

Nadia and Dominic exchanged a look — the first fully shared look between them, the kind that means a crew has formed without anyone signing anything.

"Tuesday," Dominic said. "Before the fire."

"Before the fire," Crowe confirmed, holding the door. "Which means somebody's been making a list. And I'd very much like to know how far down it our names go."

Inside, Nadia flipped to a fresh page on the legal pad, and the lamp burned steady against the dark coming down over the ashes, and the three of them bent together over the arithmetic of the dead woman's last flight — while out beyond the tree line, unseen, something that had been making a list went on making it.
