## 7. What the Numbers Don't Add To

The hangar smelled like burnt pine and someone else's coffee.

Nadia had learned in the last three days that grief kept strange hours. It slept through the morning while she worked, then woke at dusk and sat on her chest like a cat that had decided she was furniture. So she worked mornings. She worked mornings the way her mother used to work — hunched, obsessive, fueled by whatever caffeine was within arm's reach — because it was either that or lie in the bunk above Dominic's office staring at the ceiling beams and cataloguing every conversation she'd never gotten to have.

Elena Marsh's last letter lay flat on a workbench between a torque wrench and a tin of rivets. Nadia had read it forty times. She could recite it. She'd caught herself reciting it in the shower, in the tone of voice her mother used when she was being deliberately infuriating, which was most of her tones.

*"You always said I couldn't do arithmetic without showing my work. Well, sweetheart, I'm showing my work now, and some of these numbers don't add. Ask the registry for the Meridian filings, 2014 through present. Compare tonnage to headcount. You'll see what I mean. Don't trust anyone whose boots are too clean."*

Don't trust anyone whose boots are too clean. Her mother had ended letters that way since Nadia was twelve. It had been a joke once. It wasn't anymore.

"Registry opens at nine," Dominic said from under the Beaver's wing, where he was doing something meditative and greasy to a carburetor. "Town office. Missus Okonkwo runs it. She'll let you photocopy things if you bring your own quarters and don't ask about the weather."

"Why wouldn't I ask about the weather?"

"Because she'll tell you. For an hour. With charts."

Nadia almost smiled. It surprised her, the way a bird surprising you off a fencepost surprises you. Three days ago this man had been a stranger with tired eyes and a hangar full of smoke, and now he was feeding her eggs and lending her his truck and pretending not to notice when she cried into the dishwater.

"I still don't understand what I'm looking for," she admitted. "Tonnage to headcount. That's mining gibberish."

"It means how much rock they claim to pull out of the ground versus how many people they claim to have pulling it out," Dominic said. He emerged wiping his hands on a rag that had been white during the previous administration. "Your mother was a geologist. She'd know what a crew of a given size can actually move in a season. If the filings say they're shipping more ore than any plausible number of miners could dig, blast, and haul — then somebody's inventing ore."

"Inventing *ore*."

"Paper is cheaper than rock. Always has been." He shrugged, and there was something old and tired underneath the shrug, something that had seen paper do terrible things. "A company can't sell shares on rock nobody's weighed. But they can sell them on numbers. Numbers weigh nothing. Numbers fly."

*Numbers fly.* She wrote it down in the margin of the letter, because her mother would have appreciated the poetry even if she'd have rolled her eyes at the sentiment.

* * *

Missus Okonkwo turned out to be a small, magnificent woman with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the air of someone who had personally outlasted three governments and intended to outlast this one too.

"You're Elena Marsh's girl," she said, before Nadia had finished explaining why she was there. "I did the filing intake for twenty years, dear. I remember names. Sit down. Quarters?"

Nadia put six dollars in quarters on the counter like tribute, and Missus Okonkwo nodded as though the gods had been suitably honored, and led her back to a room that smelled of toner and decades, where the public registry lived in gray binders and a computer older than Nadia's grudge against her mother.

Mining claims, it turned out, were public record. That was the beautiful, stupid joke of the whole thing: the evidence had been sitting in a government office in Caribou Bend, population four hundred and eleven, available to anyone with six dollars in quarters and a reason to look. Nobody looked. Nobody ever looked. The wilderness kept its secrets, but so did the paperwork — not by hiding, just by being boring enough that no one bothered.

Nadia pulled the Meridian Mining annual production filings, 2014 to present, and stacked them on the table like bricks.

She started with the current year, because that was where her mother's letter pointed. The Kettle River concession — the operation that paid half the town's wages, sponsored the hockey rink, bought the fire department its new truck — reported, in neat typed columns, an extracted ore tonnage that made her sit back.

Then she pulled the employment figures. Average seasonal workforce: sixty-two.

She didn't know mining. She knew spreadsheets, because she was an accountant, which her mother had once described as "honest work done dishonestly often," and which Nadia had chosen specifically to annoy her. But she knew throughput. She knew what a crew could produce, because she'd audited a gravel company once and spent a miserable summer learning exactly how slowly rock moved through human hands.

Sixty-two men running an open pit and a mill could move a lot of rock. They could not move *this* much rock. Not with the equipment fleet listed in the same filing — she counted the loaders, the haul trucks, did the arithmetic on cycle times the way her mother had taught her over one long, rare, good summer at the kitchen table, back when they were still speaking the same language. Even running twenty hours a day, even assuming no breakdowns, no weather delays, no human beings needing lunch —

The filings claimed forty percent more production than the physical operation could possibly deliver.

Her hands had gone cold. She noticed it distantly, the way you notice a draft.

"Okay," she whispered to the empty records room. "Okay, Mom. Showing my work."

She went backward. 2023: same gap, slightly smaller. 2022: same. 2021, 2020, 2019 — the inflated numbers marched back year after year like soldiers who'd been told the war was over and refused to stop marching. And then, in 2015, the numbers changed character entirely.

In 2014, the Kettle River underground workings — the old mine, the deep one, the one the town called the Undercut — reported a workforce of one hundred and forty and a production figure consistent with it. Modest. Believable. A mine doing honest, unglamorous work.

In 2015, the underground workforce dropped to ninety-one.

And the production figures *doubled.*

Nadia stared at the two columns until they blurred. Forty-nine workers gone overnight. Production up one hundred and two percent. No capital expenditure filed that year — no new equipment purchased, no expansion permit applied for. Nothing that could explain how fewer people with the same machines suddenly produced twice the ore.

Unless the ore wasn't coming from where the filings said it was.

Unless the ore was coming from somewhere that didn't file anything.

She photocopied everything. The machine groaned like a dying animal, and Missus Okonkwo appeared in the doorway with two cups of tea and the expression of a woman who had been waiting thirty years for someone to ask the right question.

"My husband worked the Undercut," Missus Okonkwo said, setting the tea down without being asked. "Before it sealed. He doesn't talk about the last shift. None of them do."

Nadia's mouth was dry. "What happened on the last shift?"

"They came up short," Missus Okonkwo said simply. "Forty-nine men went down. Forty-one came up. And the company said eight men had quit early and driven out the south road, and after a while everybody agreed, because agreeing was easier, and because the alternative was thinking about it." She refilled Nadia's cup though it was full. "My husband signed the statement. We all signed things, those years. It was that or leave, and where would we go? The whole town was the company's payroll." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Nadia had ever seen on a living face. "You'll want the incident annexes, dear. Shelf four. They're supposed to be redacted, but redaction ink fades faster than regret."

* * *

Shelf four gave her the annexes, and the annexes gave her the shape of it, and the shape of it was so much worse than the numbers.

There had been a collapse in the Undercut in March 2015. Not eight men quitting. A stope failure — a chamber of rock letting go — with forty-nine men below. Forty-one came up. Eight did not, and the bodies were never recovered, because the company sealed the adit within seventy-two hours, citing "geological instability," and the coroner's report — she found it cross-referenced, her hands shaking so badly she had to pin the pages flat with the tea cups — listed cause of death for all eight as "presumed, unrecovered."

Presumed. Unrecovered. Filed, stamped, forgotten. The permits for the new open-pit concession were approved eleven months later, on land that included the sealed workings, based on ore estimates that assumed — she checked the technical appendix twice, three times, because she wanted to be wrong — that the underground reserves were accessible and producing.

They weren't producing. They were a grave. The numbers hadn't just been inflated; they'd been *invented*, built on top of eight dead men like a house built on bones, and the whole town — the hockey rink, the fire truck, the school gymnasium with MERIDIAN MINING: PARTNER IN PROGRESS painted on the wall — stood on top of that.

And now her mother had found it happening again. The current filings showed the same disease: production climbing, workforce shrinking, equipment lists unchanged. Whatever was generating the phantom tonnage this time, it wasn't the pit. It was somewhere else. Somewhere unfiled. Somewhere her mother had flown to with three colleagues and a set of questions about old shafts.

Somewhere her mother wasn't coming back from.

Nadia sat in the records room for a long time after that, listening to the furnace tick, holding her mother's letter the way you hold a hand.

*"Some of these numbers don't add."*

They added to eight men in the dark. They added to a town that had agreed, because agreeing was easier. They added to a daughter who'd spent ten years angry at her mother for missing birthdays, never guessing that her mother had spent those same ten years pulling at a thread that led down into the Undercut, alone, patient, showing her work.

She thought about the burned fragment Dominic had pulled from the plateau search site. She thought about clean boots.

When she got back to the hangar, the light was going amber and long, and Dominic was standing outside looking north at the range with the particular stillness he got when the mountains were talking to him. Silas Crowe's truck was parked beside the fuel drums — the trapper had taken to drifting by in the evenings, bringing meat and suspicion in roughly equal quantities.

"We need to talk," Nadia said, and heard her mother's voice come out of her own mouth, steady and terrible. "Both of you. Inside. Bring paper."

Dominic looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him go get the paper without asking questions.

She spread the filings across the workbench — the columns, the annexes, the faded redactions, the coroner's report with its obscene little word *presumed* — and she watched two men read their town's foundation turn into a crime scene. Silas got to the date first. March 2015. She saw him go very still, the way animals go still, and she remembered what he'd said about the case that ended his career, the burn pattern, the corporate signature, ten years ago.

"The Undercut," he said quietly. "It was the Undercut. All this time I thought—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I carried a piece of that. I just never knew how big the piece was."

"Yeah," Nadia said. "Welcome to the family."

She hadn't meant to say it. It just came out, warm and cracked, and neither man laughed, and neither man left, and that was answer enough.

Outside, far off across the darkening flats, a vehicle engine started up — idled a long moment, deliberate, the way a man clears his throat — and then drove slowly past the hangar with its high beams off.

Silas moved to the window without hurrying. "Company truck," he said. "Vane's driver. Just letting us know he knows we're home."

"Good," Nadia said, and was startled to find she meant it. She gathered the photocopies into a folder and held it against her chest like armor, like inheritance. "Let him watch. My mother spent ten years showing her work alone. She's not alone anymore."

Dominic was already reaching for the aviation charts.
