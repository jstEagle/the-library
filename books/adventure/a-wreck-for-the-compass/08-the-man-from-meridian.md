## 8. The Man from Meridian

The knock came at seven in the morning, which told Dominic two things before he even opened the door of his apartment above the hardware store: whoever it was had money, and whoever it was wanted him to know it.

Nobody in Caribou Bend knocked at seven in the morning unless they were delivering fish or bad news, and neither of those came with a knock. They came with a thump and a shout. This was three measured raps, evenly spaced, the kind of knocking that had been rehearsed.

He opened the door on a man in a wool overcoat that cost more than Dominic's annual insurance premium — which, granted, was a low bar, since Dominic paid his premiums the way he paid his bar tabs: late, partially, and with promises. The man was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of face that looked like it had been assembled from a catalogue of trustworthy features by someone who'd only read about trust.

"Mr. Delacroix." He extended a hand. "Aldous Vane. Meridian Mining."

Dominic shook it. The grip was calibrated — firm enough to seem honest, soft enough to remind you he'd never hauled anything heavier than a briefcase. "I know who you are," Dominic said. "You're the reason my fuel bill has a surcharge."

Vane smiled like that was charming. Everything, Dominic suspected, was charming to Aldous Vane, right up until the moment it wasn't.

"I was hoping we could talk business. Over breakfast, if you're free."

"It's seven a.m."

"The diner opens at six-thirty."

"In Caribou Bend, breakfast is what you eat while you're doing something else. What's wrong with here?"

Vane glanced past him into the apartment — the single room, the hot plate, the charts pinned to the wall, the window overlooking the strip where the town's four businesses pretended to be a main street. "Here is fine," he said, in the tone of a man who would rather have eaten off a shovel.

* * *

They ended up at the diner anyway, because Vane insisted, and because Ruthie's coffee was terrible in a way that gave Dominic an unfair advantage in any negotiation. You could watch a man drink it and learn everything about his character. Vane drank it black, without flinching, and complimented it. That told Dominic plenty.

"So," Dominic said, wrapping both hands around his own mug like a man holding onto a railing. "Meridian wants to charter me. Let me save us some time. I don't fly for companies that are currently under investigation by—"

"There's no investigation." Vane said it smoothly, but a half-second too fast, the way a man steps over a hole in the floor he knows is there. "There's a tribunal review. Routine. Paperwork matters. And that's actually why I'm here." He set a leather folder on the table between them, square and heavy as a hymnal. "We'd like to retain you. Exclusively."

"Exclusively."

"All our interior flights. Crew rotations, supply runs, equipment moves. A guaranteed forty hours a month through winter, plus standby pay. Full fuel allowance out of Whitehorse." He slid the folder across. "The numbers are inside."

Dominic didn't touch it. He looked at it the way you'd look at a bear that had sat down at your campfire — politely, and while calculating the distance to a tree.

"That's more than I grossed last year," he said.

"It's less than you're worth. We've done our homework, Mr. Delacroix. You're the best pilot in this valley, possibly the territory. You've landed on lakes I wouldn't land a canoe on. Frankly, the fact that you're still flying a twenty-year-old Beaver out of a rented hangar is an indictment of this region's economy." He smiled again. "We'd like to fix that."

Outside the window, the town went about its morning. The school bus wheezed past. Old Marta Kowalczyk swept her porch with the grim thoroughness of a woman who believed dust was a personal insult. Somebody's dog was barking at the mail plane, as it did every morning, on principle. It was, Dominic thought, a good town. Fragile, poor, held together by gossip and the concession payroll — but good. Which was exactly what made the folder on the table so heavy.

"And in exchange," Dominic said, "I stop asking questions."

Vane's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "In exchange, you fly safe routes on a reliable contract, and you get your nights back. I understand you've been... involved in certain discussions. About the survey team."

"They were my passengers. Four people got on my airplane and never got off it. That makes them mine."

"That's very noble." Vane turned his mug a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the table edge, and Dominic understood he was looking at a man who straightened pictures in other people's houses. "But here's what concerns me. Elena Marsh was a fine geologist — we hired her, after all — but she was also a woman under real personal strain. Estranged daughter. Financial trouble. She'd been drinking more than her file suggested. When she disappeared, well." He spread his hands, an orchestra conductor inviting silence. "Misadventure happens in country like this. It's tragic. But it doesn't require amateur detectives making things harder for everyone."

"She left a note saying she found numbers that didn't add."

"A figure of speech. Geologists talk like that." Vane leaned forward, and for the first time the charm thinned, showing the machinery underneath, all gears and no warmth. "Mr. Delacroix. I'm going to say something plainly, because I respect you, and because men who respect each other shouldn't waste time. Whatever Dr. Marsh thought she found, she found nothing. There is no fraud. There is no cover-up. There is a mining operation that pays the wages of every person in this town, including yours, including Ruthie's, including the schoolteacher who taught your nephew to read. If that operation goes down because of rumors, this town dies with it. Is that the outcome you want? For a feeling?"

"You've got a strange definition of rumor," Dominic said. "Somebody burned my logbooks."

"A fire. Tragic. Old wiring, probably." Vane sipped the terrible coffee. "Hangars burn up here. So do planes. So do pilots, occasionally, when they push weather they shouldn't or land where they've been asked not to. The north is unforgiving that way." He said it gently, almost kindly, the way you'd tell a child not to run with scissors, and the gentleness was the threat. "I'd hate to see you become another item on that list. You strike me as a man with a great deal of flying left in him."

The diner noise filled the pause. The griddle hissed. Two trappers argued amiably about whether a wolverine could beat a lynx, a debate that had been running, in various forms, since roughly 1974.

And here was the thing — the thing Dominic would turn over later, alone, in the dark above the hardware store. Part of him *wanted* to say yes. Not the coward part, either. The tired part. The part that had spent three weeks sleeping badly, jumping at engine sounds, checking the tie-downs twice. The contract was real money. Real safety. He could fix the Beaver properly instead of cannibalizing spare parts. He could stop counting loonies for coffee. He could fly, just fly, the one thing he'd ever been unambiguously good at, and let the world sort out its own paperwork.

It would be so easy. That was the seduction of it — not greed, just rest. Complicity rarely arrives wearing a villain's face. It arrives wearing a fair wage and a full fuel tank and the promise that you can go back to being the man you were before you knew things.

He thought about Elena Marsh's handwriting on that letter Nadia had shown him — quick, slanted, urgent, a woman writing against time. *Numbers that don't add.* He thought about boot prints raked smooth on the plateau. He thought about the smell of his own hangar burning, and how he'd stood in the snow watching thirty years of records go up, and how the strangest part hadn't been fear. It had been clarity.

"No," Dominic said.

Vane sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor who respected his craft. "That's disappointing."

"Yeah, well. I disappoint a lot of people. Ask my ex-wife."

"The offer stands for seventy-two hours." Vane closed the empty folder — Dominic noticed he'd never actually opened it; the numbers had always been beside the point — and stood, dropping a ten on the table for two coffees that cost a dollar eighty. "Take some time. Talk to your friends. The trapper, Mr. Crowe. The daughter." He said their names lightly, idly, and that lightness was the coldest thing in the room. "Everyone makes the sensible choice eventually, Mr. Delacroix. My job is mostly just waiting for people to make it."

He put on his coat, nodded to Ruthie behind the counter — "Excellent coffee, ma'am" — and walked out into the morning like a man who owned it, which in most of the ways that mattered, he did.

* * *

Dominic sat there for a while after he left, watching steam rise off his mug, doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Seventy-two hours meant Vane expected something to happen within seventy-two hours. Either Dominic would come around, or whatever Vane planned to do about it would begin. And Vane knew about Silas. Knew about Nadia. Which meant somebody in town was talking, or somebody was listening, and either way the walls had ears with a company payroll number.

Ruthie refilled his cup without being asked. "That fella leave you a tip?" she asked.

"He left a ten."

"Huh." She squinted at the door. "Men who tip big are either guilty or selling something. Sometimes both." She topped off his coffee. "You look like a man doing math he hates."

"Always, Ruthie."

"Well." She patted his shoulder with a hand floury from pie crust. "Whatever it is — the good Lord, the RCMP, and your mother are all keeping an eye on you, and two of the three think you're an idiot. Try to deserve the third."

He laughed despite himself, and the laugh loosened something in his chest, and he realized he'd already made the decision somewhere between the second coffee and the wolverine argument. You couldn't take Vane's money and ever again look at yourself in the hangar mirror without wondering whose pilot you were.

He pushed open the diner door into the cold and stood a moment on the boardwalk, breath smoking, looking down the street toward the blackened skeleton of his hangar at the end of the strip. Seventy-two hours. Maybe less, knowing Vane.

Which meant no more waiting around to be hunted. If Vane knew about Silas Crowe, then Silas needed to know that too — today, now, before the seventy-two hours started costing more than money. Dominic dug the truck keys out of his pocket and tried to remember which cut-off road led to the old man's trapline cabin, and whether a disgraced Mountie who hadn't trusted anyone in ten years would open his door to a bush pilot with bad news and worse timing.

There was one way to find out. He cranked the engine and pointed the truck north.
