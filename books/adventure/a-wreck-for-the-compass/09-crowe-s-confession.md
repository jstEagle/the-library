## 9. Crowe's Confession

Silas Crowe had spent thirty years learning how to be alone, and he'd gotten good at it. Good enough that when he heard the snowmobile coming up his trapline — a sound no one made, because nobody came up his trapline — his first instinct was to reach for the rifle and his second was to feel, shamefully and unmistakably, glad.

He didn't shoot. He stood in the doorway of his cabin with steam rising off the coffee in his hand and watched Dominic Delacroix and the Marsh girl bounce up the last hundred yards of trail like two sacks of mail thrown off a truck.

"You're a long way from town," Silas said.

"You're a hard man to find," Dominic said, killing the engine.

"That was the general idea."

Nadia Marsh climbed off behind Dominic, stamped her boots, and looked at the cabin — the smoke-blackened tin chimney, the stretched hides, the rack of traps that Silas oiled more faithfully than he'd ever oiled anything for the Force — with an expression he recognized because he'd worn it himself once, forty years ago, standing outside another man's door with questions in his mouth. It was the look of someone who had decided the person inside was going to answer them whether he liked it or not.

"We found something," she said. "A burned document fragment, out on the plateau where my mother's team disappeared. Dominic says you took one look at it and went white as a birch."

"I did not go white."

"Silas." Dominic's voice was gentle in the way that big men are sometimes gentle, which is to say it carried further than shouting. "You looked like you'd seen a ghost wearing a company letterhead."

Silas sighed, opened the door wider, and jerked his chin at the two chairs by the stove. "Coffee's on. It's strong enough to tan hides, so mind your teeth."

* * *

They sat. The stove ticked. Outside, the light was doing that late-October thing where it gave up around three in the afternoon and left the world to the blue shadows. Silas poured two cups, then a third for himself, mostly so his hands would have something honest to do.

"I'll tell you a story," he said. "And I want you to understand before I start that I'm not telling it because it makes me look good. There is no version of this story where I look good."

"Okay," Nadia said.

"Your mother asked some questions about the old workings. About shafts that were sealed in—" he counted back, wincing, "—2014. Eleven years ago now. And eleven years ago, I was still wearing red serge, and I caught a case out of Meridian's exploration camp. A fire. Officially a boiler accident."

He drank. The coffee was terrible. He'd made it himself, which was the whole problem.

"Three men died in that fire. That was the report. Three men, boiler accident, case closed, everyone very sad, very efficient funeral, Meridian sends flowers." He set the cup down. "Except the fire investigator — a good man, better than me, name of Okafor — kept pulling threads. Burn pattern was wrong. Accelerant wrong. And there were four sets of personal effects logged into evidence, not three."

"Four," Nadia said quietly.

"Fourth man wasn't on any crew list. No next of kin filed. A laborer, probably, brought up under the table because he was cheap and because nobody upstream asks questions about men who don't have paperwork." Silas looked into the fire. "Okafor wanted to run it all the way down. Names, permits, the whole rotten string. And somebody at Meridian got nervous, and somebody talked to somebody, and eventually a man in a good coat sat across from me in a diner in Caribou Bend and offered me a number with a lot of zeros in it to lose a file."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, a raven said something rude.

"And you took it," Nadia said. Not cruelly. Just factually, the way you'd say *and then it snowed*.

"I took it." He heard his own voice come out flat and old. "I told myself stories. Told myself the fourth man was probably illegal anyway, that the case would get buried by lawyers regardless, that I had a pension and a mother in a care home in Edmonton and a mortgage shaped like a boat I shouldn't have bought. All true. None of it matters. I took money to bury evidence, and the evidence—" His jaw worked. "The evidence was a human being. Somebody's son, probably. Maybe somebody's father. And I helped make him into nothing."

Dominic said nothing. He had the particular silence of pilots, Silas thought — men who understood that sometimes the most useful thing you can do is hold altitude and wait.

"What happened to Okafor?" Nadia asked.

"He pushed. Alone, after I folded. Kept pulling threads until Meridian's lawyers came down on him like a rockslide. Then they came down on me too — not for taking the payoff, mind, they'd never admit that existed — but they found other things. Things they'd have overlooked if I'd stayed a good boy. An expense discrepancy. A witness I'd interviewed sloppy. Enough to end a career without ever touching the real crime." He almost smiled. "Elegant, really. They ruined me for corruption I half-committed, to protect corruption I fully committed. There's a poetry in it if you're drunk enough."

"So the burn fragment we found," Dominic said slowly. "Same signature?"

"Same accelerant. Same over-burn at the edges, same tidy little fire that destroys exactly one thing and nothing else. I've seen that fire twice now. Once eleven years ago, once on your plateau." He met their eyes. "Whoever lit it learned from the first one. Or is the same hand."

Nadia leaned forward. "My mother's letter said numbers that don't add. Ore estimates. We've been through the permit filings — the tonnage Meridian claims to pull out of that concession doesn't match what the ground could give. Unless—"

"Unless there's ore coming out of workings that officially don't exist," Silas finished. "Sealed adits. Old tunnels from before the modern claim. Men working shifts that aren't on any schedule, ore trucked at night, everything laundered through the legal operation on top." He shook his head. "It's the oldest trick in mining. You don't need to falsify much. You just need a hole the government doesn't know about and men nobody will miss."

The words *men nobody will miss* hung in the cabin air, and Silas watched Nadia Marsh go pale, and hated himself freshly for putting them there.

"My mother found the hole," she whispered.

"Your mother found the hole, or the proof of it, or both. And four people don't vanish off a plateau in October without help." He spread his hands. "I can't tell you who took them. But I can tell you someone who might."

* * *

Dominic sat forward. "You've been sitting on an informant for eleven years?"

"I've been sitting on a ghost for eleven years." Silas got up and went to the trunk at the foot of his bunk — an old ammunition box, paint gone gray, lock long since rusted into decoration. Inside, under a wool sock full of .303 rounds he moved aside with a delicacy that made Dominic raise an eyebrow, was a bundle of letters tied with trap wire.

"Elias Two-Rivers. Cree, seventy-some years old, knew every tunnel in that range because his grandfather helped cut half of them. Worked Meridian's early surveys as a guide. When the boiler fire happened, Elias came to me privately — wouldn't testify, wouldn't sign anything, but he talked. Told me about the fourth man. Told me about a second entrance to the old workings, up past the tree line, that isn't on any map Meridian files." Silas untied the wire carefully, the way you handle something that might still be alive. "After I fell, I warned him they'd come for him next. He went to ground. Way to ground. Out past the far side of the range, an old trapper's cabin his family used before the park boundaries moved. Nobody's been out there in years. Including me."

"Why not?" Nadia asked. "If he knows where the second entrance is—"

"Because I'm a coward, girl. Because every time I thought about walking into that valley, I remembered the diner, and the coat, and the number with the zeros, and I figured a man like me forfeited the right to ask anybody for anything." He laughed, short and ugly. "Turns out cowardice keeps better than pemmican. Eleven years and it's still fresh."

He drew a folded paper from the bundle — a hand-drawn map, grease pencil on waxed paper, mountains rendered in the confident strokes of a man who'd walked them since boyhood. He smoothed it on the table between the coffee cups.

"There. Cabin sits in the bowl below the twin peaks — locals called them the Two Sisters, government calls them something boring. Follow the creek northeast from the frozen falls, third day's travel from where a floatplane can put down on Kettle Lake, weather permitting. Which in November means pray." He tapped a small X. "And here — see this notch? That's the second entrance. Sealed with a steel door since '09, but Elias swore the seal was cosmetic. Company seals doors it wants to keep *other companies* out of, not doors it wants forgotten."

Dominic studied the map the way he studied weather — silently, thoroughly, with his whole body. "Kettle Lake's a rough strip this time of year. Ice forming at the edges, no wind sock, no nothing."

"You've landed worse."

"I've landed worse *in daylight in August*." But he was already folding the map along its creases with the reverence of a man handling a living thing, and Silas saw the pilot in him take over from the sensible man, which was a fight the pilot always won.

Nadia hadn't touched her coffee in ten minutes. She was staring at the stove, and when she spoke her voice was steady in the way of someone who has decided crying is a luxury scheduled for later. "Elias. Is he alive? Still out there?"

"I don't know." Silas said it plainly, because lying to this girl felt like a sin even by his generous standards. "He'd be past eighty. Winters out there kill men half his age. But if anyone could live alone beyond the range for a decade, it's Elias Two-Rivers. He used to say the government lost track of him in 1971 and he worked hard to keep it that way." A ghost of real warmth crossed his face. "Best tracker I ever met. Taught me half of what I know about reading snow, and the half he taught me was the better half."

"Then we go find him," Nadia said. "Before the passes close. Before the permits renew. Before whoever burned that document decides we're worth burning too."

"It's not that simple—"

"It never is." She turned on him, and Silas saw Elena Marsh in her face so strongly it hurt — the same jaw set at the same stubborn angle, though he'd only seen the mother twice and briefly. "You spent eleven years not going. I just spent six years not calling my mother. We're apparently a pair of experts on waiting until it's too late." Her voice cracked on the last word and she shoved through it. "I'm not doing it again. Are you?"

The stove ticked. The raven outside said something else, possibly supportive.

Silas looked at the map. At the X. At eleven years of a debt he'd been carrying the way you carry a bad shoulder — compensating, favoring, pretending it didn't ache.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

He got up, went to the wall, and took down the rifle that had hung there untouched since the day he'd moved in — because a man who'd sold justice didn't deserve a gun, he'd told himself, which was dramatic nonsense, but drama was the only currency guilt paid in. He checked the action anyway. It was clean. He'd been cleaning it for eleven years too.

"If we're doing this," he said, "we do it properly. Fuel cached at Kettle Lake. Food for eight days minimum, ten to be safe. The girl needs proper gear — those boots won't survive the first moraine, and frostbite doesn't negotiate. And Delacroix—" he pointed the charging handle at him like a schoolmaster's finger, "—you'll want to file a flight plan that says medicine run to the outposts, because the moment Meridian hears a plane went toward the Two Sisters, Vane's people will start asking questions with their eyebrows."

"You've met Vane?" Dominic asked.

"Not yet. But I've met his species. They smile with their teeth and they remember everything." Silas began pulling gear from under the bunk — rope, a canvas pack older than Nadia, a coil of snare wire that would serve for a hundred things besides snares. "We leave the day after tomorrow. First hard freeze locks the lake ice enough to land on, and after that every day we wait is a day the snow takes another foot of trail."

Nadia stood too, and something in the cabin had changed — he felt it the way animals feel weather. Three people who had each been alone in their own particular ruin, suddenly standing in the same room facing the same direction.

"Crowe," Dominic said quietly, as Silas handed the map over. "Why now? Really."

Silas considered lying. Considered the easy answer — *because the girl's right, because time's short.* But these two had come up a frozen trapline to hear a confession, and they deserved the true one, and he found, to his surprise, that he had it ready.

"Because I buried a man once," he said, "and I told myself the ground would keep him safe. Ground keeps nothing. Ground forgets. But guilt—" he tapped his own chest, twice, like knocking on a door, "—guilt remembers. It's the only compass I've got left. And it's pointing northeast."

Outside, the first flakes of the season's real snow began to come down, fat and unhurried, already erasing the snowmobile tracks behind them — as if the wilderness itself had decided to give them a head start, or as if something in the trees wanted very much for no one to follow.
