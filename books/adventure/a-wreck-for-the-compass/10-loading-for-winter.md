## 10. Loading for Winter

There is a particular arithmetic to loading a bush plane for winter, and it goes like this: whatever you think you need, double it; whatever you want to bring, leave it behind; and whatever the plane can actually lift, subtract twenty percent and then lie to yourself about the rest.

I spent three days on that arithmetic in the charred skeleton of my hangar, working by lantern light because someone had also taken the generator's fuel line along with everything else worth stealing before they burned the place. The Beaver still flew — I'd checked her over twice, then a third time out of pure superstition — but she sat under an open sky now, frost feathering her struts each morning like the north was already trying to claim her.

"Your insurance man called again," Nadia said, coming around the tail with two thermoses and the expression she wore when she'd been arguing with someone on the telephone. She'd gotten good at that expression. She'd had a lot of practice lately.

"What did you tell him?"

"That you were busy." She handed me a thermos. "He asked if the fire was suspicious. I said all weather in Caribou Bend is suspicious."

"He'll love that."

"He hung up on me." She said it with something close to pride.

We worked through the morning in the cold, laying gear out on tarps in rows so neat it would have made a drill sergeant weep: rope, snowshoes, the primus stove Silas swore by and I swore at, sleeping bags rated for temperatures I preferred not to think about, a canvas tent that smelled of forty years of other people's campfires. Food was the hard part. Calories are weight, weight is lift, and lift in November off short strips at altitude is a currency you spend carefully. Pemmican, pilot biscuits, chocolate frozen into bricks, a slab of salt pork Silas dropped into the pile without comment, as though salt pork were a sacrament.

"You're bringing the rifle," Nadia said. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching Silas oil it on his porch for two days like a man saying goodbye to an old dog.

"I'm bringing the rifle," I confirmed.

"And the maps."

"And the maps."

She nodded once, satisfied, and went back to repacking the medical kit she'd assembled from a list Elena had written her years ago — a list Nadia had kept, folded in a book, through six years of not speaking to her mother. She didn't say that part out loud anymore. She didn't have to. I'd seen the creases in the paper.

* * *

Silas arrived at noon driving a sled behind a dog team that moved with the bored efficiency of professionals, which is more than I could say for any of us. He'd shaved. That was how I knew he'd decided this was real. A man going out to die doesn't shave; a man going out to finish something does.

He climbed down off the runners and stood surveying my tarp inventory with the flat appraisal of a man who'd once inspected crime scenes for a living.

"You're short on candles," he said.

"Candles?"

"For the cabin nights. You can't burn the primus in a closed space without ventilating, and ventilation costs heat. Candles give light, a little warmth, and something to look at when the talking runs out." He paused. "The talking runs out."

So we added candles. And a spare axe head. And the old maps, which he unrolled across the tailgate of my truck with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, given what those maps had cost him.

They were beautiful things, hand-drawn in the 1930s by some government draftsman who had never seen the country he was rendering — contour lines like fingerprints, the old workings marked with a symbol meaning *sealed, do not enter*, which in the north has always meant *enter here*. Silas had annotated them in pencil over the years: traplines, game trails, a spring that ran warm under the ice, a pass he called the Widow's Teeth that he drew a small skull beside, in case the name wasn't clear enough.

"Elena's team went in here," he said, tapping the plateau. "But nobody walks a plateau in October. They walked out. Down here—" his finger traced a drainage, "—or here. The informant's cabin sits on the second route. If they were moving, they were moving toward help or toward proof. With your wife—" he caught himself, the way he did every time, "—with Dr. Marsh, it'd be proof."

"My mother," Nadia said quietly, not looking up from the medical kit.

Silas went still. It was the first time anyone had corrected him on it, and I watched him take the correction the way a man takes a bullet he's been expecting — grateful, almost, that it had finally arrived.

"Your mother," he said. "Then we go the second route."

* * *

Nadia announced she was coming on the second day, formally, standing in front of us both with her arms crossed, which meant she'd rehearsed it.

"I've been coming since day one," she pointed out, when I opened my mouth. "You just haven't been listening."

"Nadia—"

"I've read every survival manual in the library. Twice. I can mend anything with wire. I don't panic — ask Silas, I sat with him through the whole story about the case and I didn't even throw up, and I wanted to." She took a breath. "My mother is either dead in a hole up there or alive in one, and either way I'm not staying in Caribou Bend drinking bad coffee while two men who barely know her go find out which. That's the whole speech. I wrote it last night. There's no rebuttal section."

"There's always a rebuttal section," I said.

"Name one."

I looked at Silas, hoping for backup. Silas examined the horizon with the serenity of a man who had raised no children and regretted nothing about it.

"She's right about the coffee," he offered.

"Thank you," Nadia said.

"It's terrible coffee, Dominic. Tastes like burnt rope."

"You're both fired."

"We're not employed," Nadia said. "That's sort of the point. Nobody pays us, nobody owns us, and nobody gets left behind. I read that on a plaque in your hangar before it burned down."

I hadn't known she'd read the plaque. I hadn't known anyone read the plaque. But she was right, and worse than that, she was ready — I'd watched her learn the radio procedure in four days, watched her memorize the load manifest, watched her hands stop shaking when she talked about Elena and start doing things instead. Grief makes some people fragile and some people load-bearing. She was the second kind.

"Zero field experience," I said finally. "Which means you do exactly what I say, when I say it, even if it sounds stupid. Especially then."

"Especially then," she agreed.

"And if I tell you to turn back—"

"You won't," she said, "because you need me to carry half this gear, and we both know it."

That was true, and we both let it stand there in the cold air between us, honest and unlovely and solid as ballast. Three broken people choosing each other as crew. I'd have laughed at the idea a month ago. Now it felt like the only sound structure in town.

* * *

We loaded the Beaver on the fourth morning, hard frost on the floats' tie-downs, breath hanging like signal smoke. Everything went aboard twice — once to find out it didn't fit, once to make it fit. Nadia invented a new system involving color-coded duffels and a written manifest taped inside the door, and I mocked it thoroughly until it saved us forty minutes, after which I claimed the idea had been mine.

Silas stowed the rifle wrapped in oilcloth behind the cargo net, along with a box of cartridges and, inexplicably, a harmonica.

"You play?" I asked.

"No," he said. "But there might be long evenings."

"That's an argument against the harmonica."

"Everything's an argument against the harmonica," he agreed, and packed it anyway.

Fuel caches were the real work. I'd flown two quiet trips the week prior, dropping sealed drums at the lake below the plateau and at a meadow halfway along the drainage, each cache marked with a blazed spruce and coordinates written three places: my logbook copy, Nadia's manifest, and a note sewn into Silas's coat, because men like Silas trust paper they're wearing more than paper they've filed. If the plane went down, the plan could still walk.

It was while we were cinching the last net that I felt it — that prickle on the neck every bush pilot learns before he learns landings. I looked up the street.

Aldous Vane stood outside the Northern Mercantile with his hands in his coat pockets, watching us load. He wasn't pretending otherwise. That was the thing about Vane; he never pretended. He simply stood where he stood, immaculate in a town of patched canvas and dog hair, and let you understand that being watched was a service the company provided free of charge.

"He's been there twenty minutes," Nadia murmured.

"Longer. His truck's got frost on the windshield. Frost melts from the inside when a truck's been running." I tied off the net. "He wants us to see him."

"Why?"

"So we know the price of coming back empty-handed. Or full-handed." I tested the net one last time. "Vane doesn't threaten, Nadia. He schedules."

She turned to face the street openly, lifted a hand, and waved — a bright, cheerful, entirely hostile wave. Something flickered across Vane's face, there and gone, the smallest recalculation. He touched his hat brim, got into his truck, and drove off unhurried, because hurrying would have admitted the wave had landed.

"I like her," Silas said.

"Don't encourage her."

"Too late."

* * *

By late afternoon the plane sat loaded and lashed, sitting heavy on her floats like a bird that had eaten something ambitious. I walked her a final time — wing bolts, control cables, fuel caps, the little rituals that keep pilots humble and alive — and found the others waiting by the truck with their bags, not talking, just waiting, the way crews do when the talking's done and only the doing remains.

"One more thing," I said. I'd gone back to the hangar ruins that morning and dug it out of the wreckage: the brass plaque, smoke-blackened, screws torn loose. NOBODY FLIES ALONE. My father had hung it there in 1958. I'd always thought it sentimental. I set it on the float between us.

Nobody said anything clever. Nadia put her mitten on it. After a moment Silas did too, with the gravity of a man swearing on something, and maybe he was.

"Dawn tomorrow," I said. "Weather window holds maybe thirty-six hours before the next front. We fly to the lake, cache the drum, walk the drainage, and we're back inside a week with answers or we're not back at all. Questions?"

"Yeah," Silas said. "Who's got the harmonica?"

"You do."

"Right." He patted his coat pocket. "Just checking."

We shook hands all around, which felt formal and foolish and exactly correct, and then I stood alone a moment in the blue dusk looking north, where the range had already put on its white cap and the passes were thinking hard about closing. Somewhere up in all that silence were four people who had asked questions about numbers, and a man named Rook whose job was making sure the questions stayed buried, and a truth with an expiry date.

The compass on my dash pointed north, the way compasses do, indifferent and certain.

Tomorrow we'd follow it.
