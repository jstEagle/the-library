## 11. Over the Range

The lake at dawn was a sheet of hammered tin, and Dominic Delacroix loved it the way you love anything that could kill you.

He stood on the ice beside the Beaver, breathing steam, running his palm along the propeller blades like a man greeting old horses. The engine had been ticking all night in its insulated blanket — he'd heard it in half-sleep, that faint metallic heartbeat, and each time he'd surfaced just enough to think: *still there. Still mine.* Twenty-two years of flying had taught him that an airplane was less a machine than a relationship, and relationships in the North required attention or they left you.

Behind him, the cabin door banged open and Nadia Marsh came out wearing every piece of clothing she owned, which was not enough.

"It's cold," she announced, with the tone of someone delivering breaking news.

"It's November," Dominic said.

"I know what month it is. I'm from Vancouver. We have a different arrangement with November."

Silas Crowe came out behind her without a word, rifle slung, and began kicking snow off the floats' tie-downs. The old man moved like weather — slow to start, impossible to stop once he got going. He'd been up before all of them, as far as Dominic could tell. There were already fresh tracks around the cabin perimeter, and when Dominic had woken at four, Silas's bunk had been empty and the woodstove had been fed and banked so precisely it looked like an apology.

"Weight and balance," Dominic said, pulling his checklist from inside his jacket. "Nadia, you're two hundred pounds of gear lighter than we planned, because I'm leaving the spare tent. Silas, your rifle goes aft, ammo forward, don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were composing the argument. I could see it."

Silas almost smiled. It was a strange thing to watch, like ice shifting on a river — you weren't sure what it meant, but you knew something was moving underneath.

They'd spent the night at the trapper's cabin on Tseetso Lake, the last comfortable roof between them and the plateau. Now there was nothing ahead but forty miles of range country: ridgelines like broken teeth, valleys that funneled wind into howling, and weather that changed its mind the way a cat changes its mind about sitting on your lap.

Dominic did the math one more time while they loaded. Fuel for three hours and change. Weight near max. Cloud base forecast at eight thousand, dropping. Wind out of the northwest, gusting thirty by afternoon. Every line item said *wait*, and every hour they waited was an hour Rook gained, an hour closer to the first real snows sealing the passes, an hour closer to midnight on those permits.

"So we go now," he said aloud, mostly to himself.

"We go now," Nadia agreed, and climbed aboard like she'd been doing it her whole life instead of for six days.

* * *

The first twenty minutes were the good kind of flying, the kind that reminded him why he'd never managed to hold down a life on the ground. The Beaver clawed off the ice with a full load, wallowed, found itself, and climbed. Below, the world unrolled in white and blue-black: frozen creeks stitching the valleys together, spruce stands dark as spilled ink, the long shadow of the range lying across everything like a sleeping animal.

Nadia leaned against the window, breath fogging the glass, and Dominic watched her face do what everyone's face did the first time they saw the interior from altitude. It went quiet. All that fury she carried — at her mother, at Vane, at the whole machinery of men who made numbers lie — it drained out of her expression and left something younger underneath.

"There's no roads," she said. "None. It just goes on."

"That's the part people don't get from maps," Dominic said over the intercom. "The map says empty. It isn't empty. It's full. Just not of us."

Silas grunted from the back seat, which from Silas was a standing ovation.

Dominic set his heading for the pass below Mount Corliss and settled into the rhythm of the work: scan, trim, scan again. Engine gauges. Sky ahead. Sky above. The mountains didn't care about their schedule, and he gave them the respect of constant attention, the way you'd keep an eye on a large dog of uncertain temperament.

By the time they crossed the last foothills, the sky ahead had begun to curdle.

It started as a smudge over Corliss, the kind of cloud that looks harmless from a distance and turns out to be a wall. By the time they were close enough to see detail, the smudge had become a front — a gray avalanche pouring off the summit, spilling down the far side into the valley they needed to cross.

"That doesn't look like eight-thousand-foot cloud base," Nadia said.

"No, it does not."

"Is that a problem?"

Dominic checked the gauges out of habit rather than need. "It's a consideration. There's a low route through the valley south of the peak. We'll be under it. Probably."

"Probably is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Probably always does. That's why they pay me." Nobody paid him, actually, not for this. He let it stand anyway.

He dropped down into the valley, and the world closed in around them like a book. Walls of rock rose on both sides, snow-plastered, streaked with the black ribs of exposed scree. The river below was frozen in terraces, white steps descending toward the plateau country. It was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, and Dominic felt the old double awareness settle over him — half pilot, half animal — every nerve cataloguing the terrain, asking the only question that mattered: *if the engine quits right now, where do I put it?*

There was nowhere. That was the honest answer. For the next fifteen miles there was nowhere, and he flew anyway, because the alternative was going back, and going back meant letting the mountain decide their timeline for them.

The engine coughed.

It was a small sound. A hiccup. Most people wouldn't have noticed it over the drone and the wind. But Dominic felt it come up through the airframe and into his hands on the yoke, and every hair on his arms stood up under his flight jacket.

"What was that?" Nadia asked.

"Nothing," he said, and then, because lying to passengers was a sin worse than crashing: "Maybe something. Give me a second."

He ran the checks. Mixture rich. Carb heat hot. Fuel selector — and there it was. The left tank gauge, which had read a hair over half at departure, was reading a quarter. Either the gauge was lying or the tank was feeding wrong, and either way the engine had just told him it had noticed too. A second cough, longer this time, the propeller faltering like a stumble on stairs.

"Left tank's starving," he said. "Switching to right." He did it, primed, waited. The engine caught, smoothed out, ran clean. His heart was still going like a drum solo. "Right tank's fuller. We're fine. But I'm not crossing the pass on a maybe, so we're putting down at Loon Lake and sorting it out on the ground."

"Is that far?"

"Fifteen minutes. It's frozen. It'll hold us." He glanced back at Silas. "You've landed on Loon Lake before, I'm guessing."

"Nineteen eighty-one," Silas said. "Different plane. Same pilot error."

"That's reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

* * *

Loon Lake appeared out of the valley mouth like a held breath — a long oval of white lying in a bowl of timber, smooth as poured cream. Dominic flew the length of it twice, low, reading the surface the way other men read faces. No pressure ridges. No dark patches where current had thinned the ice. Wind-blown snow lay in gentle sastrugi, softening everything.

"Landing on a frozen lake in a floatplane," Nadia said. "Explain to me why this is fine."

"The ice is two feet thick if it's an inch. Floats spread the load. We touch down smooth, we stay smooth, we stop, we get out, we have a very frustrating afternoon." He lined up. "If it goes badly, you'll know immediately, and then none of it will matter, so try to enjoy the view."

"You are *terrible* at reassurance."

"I'm excellent at it. I just reserve it for people who deserve it."

He flared, held the Beaver off a foot above the snow, and let it settle. The floats touched with a long hiss, a shudder, a spray of powder — and then they were sliding, decelerating, the lake reaching up to hold them like a hand. The airplane rocked once, twice, and went still.

Silence. The particular silence of the deep interior, which isn't really silence at all: wind worrying the ridge tops, a raven somewhere talking to itself, the tick of cooling metal. Dominic sat with his hands on the yoke for a moment and let his pulse come down.

"Well," Nadia said finally. "That was horrifying and also completely fine."

"Welcome to bush flying. That's the entire job description."

They unloaded onto the ice, and here was the thing Dominic hadn't fully prepared himself for: the weight of what they were doing, standing out there in the open. From the air, the wilderness was scenery. Standing on it, it was a presence. The bowl of the lake held them the way a stage holds actors, and he couldn't shake the feeling of audience — the ridgelines watching, the timber watching, the raven making its slow circuits overhead with what he could have sworn was professional interest.

Silas stood at the edge of the trees for a long time, reading the ground. Then he crouched, brushed snow from something, and beckoned them over.

Tracks. Old ones, weeks old by the drift-fill in them, but unmistakable: boot prints, several sets, heading north along the lakeshore toward the plateau country. And beside them, smaller and shallower, the prints of someone limping — a favored left foot, over and over, mile after mile if these prints went where they seemed to go.

"Elena?" Nadia whispered.

"Could be anyone," Silas said. "Could be nobody. Could be trappers." But he didn't say it like he believed it, and nobody pretended otherwise.

Dominic opened the cowling and got to work, and the work was bad news wearing coveralls. The left tank's outlet was choked with ice crystals — water in the fuel, frozen at the pickup, throttling the flow. He could clear it, but it would refill with doubt every cold night from now until spring. Worse, draining the tanks showed him water in both. Somewhere, at some fuel stop he couldn't even identify, someone had sold him — or handed him — wet gas. Or had done worse than sell it.

He didn't say that part out loud. He watched Silas thinking the same thought, and neither of them said it, and the not-saying sat between them like a third passenger.

"Can you fix it?" Nadia asked.

"I can make it fly tomorrow, maybe. Today, no. We drain, we dry, we filter every drop through a chamois, and we pray the right tank behaves." He wiped his hands on his thighs and looked north, where the tracks went. The plateau was twelve miles beyond the lake's head, over a divide that would take a fit person a day and a half in good conditions. Conditions were not good. The front over Corliss was sliding east toward them like a lid closing on a pot.

"So we walk," Nadia said. It wasn't a question, and there was something in her voice that scared him a little — not fear, exactly. Readiness. She'd been a woman arriving six days ago; whatever she was now, the wilderness had already started building it.

"We walk," Dominic agreed. "We cache the heavy gear with the plane, take tents, stove, four days of food. We follow the lake to the head, cross the divide at the low shoulder, and come down on the plateau from the west. If the weather holds off."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we get very well acquainted with a snow cave, and I teach you the difference between discomfort and dying. It's a shorter lecture than you'd think."

Silas shouldered his rifle and pack without ceremony. "I'll break trail. You two walk in my steps. Saves energy."

"You don't have to—" Nadia started.

"Boy's right, though," Silas said, nodding at Dominic. "Fresh trail through knee-deep is double the work. And I've been walking away from things my whole life. Time I walked toward one."

They cached the survival kit under the floats, weighted and flagged. Dominic took one last look at the Beaver sitting on the white lake — his whole livelihood, his whole history, parked alone in a bowl of watching timber — and patted the cowling the way you'd pat a dog you had to leave behind.

"Don't let anyone touch her," he told the airplane, and then, quieter, to the treeline, to the ridges, to whatever was listening: "Or else."

They walked.

* * *

The lake narrowed as they went north, the forest leaning in, and by mid-afternoon they reached the head of it where the creek came down from the divide in a staircase of blue ice. Silas set a pace that was patient and merciless, and Dominic kept Nadia on his heels, calling breaks before she needed them, watching her hands. Her fingers had gone stiff and clumsy by the second hour, and he made her strip her mitts and put them inside his jacket against his chest while they rested, and she looked at him with an expression he decided not to interpret.

"This is how you lose fingers," she said.

"This is how you keep them. There's a difference and it's embarrassing to explain at the hospital."

The divide, when they climbed it, gave them the plateau — and stopped Nadia mid-step. It lay below them under late light, vast and white and utterly indifferent, a country with no edges, going on past sight. Somewhere out there was a sealed adit, a dead informant's cabin, Elena's team, Rook, and the truth, buried under a decade of snow and money.

"It's so big," Nadia said. "How does anything hide out here?"

"Nothing hides," Silas said. "Things just wait. Snow waits. Ground waits. Bodies wait." He resettled his rifle. "Question is whether we find them before somebody finds us."

Dominic scanned the slope below, the way they'd come, the dark seam of the timber. For a moment — just a moment — he thought he saw movement down by the lakeshore, far back the way they'd traveled. Something small and dark against all that white. A bird. Almost certainly a bird.

Except birds didn't stand still and watch for that long.

"Move," he said quietly. "Off the skyline. Now."

And the three of them dropped below the crest of the divide as the light failed, and behind them, on the shore of Loon Lake, small and patient in the gathering dark, something followed.
