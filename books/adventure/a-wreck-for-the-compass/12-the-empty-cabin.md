## 12. The Empty Cabin

The cabin appeared the way all good things had appeared for three days: suddenly, and only after Dominic insisted it wouldn't.

"There," he said, pointing his chin at a gray smudge between two black spruces. "Crowe's map says a trapper named Ouellette worked this line until the nineties. If the roof held, we're rich."

"Rich in what?" I asked. My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to. Three days of snowshoeing will do that to a person's vocabulary. Mine had shrunk to roughly eleven words, four of which were variations of *ow*.

"Rich in walls," Silas said from behind me. "Rich in a stove. Rich in not sleeping in a hole you dug yourself."

I had to admit the appeal. We'd spent the previous night in just such a hole, under a spruce bough lean-to that Dominic had constructed with the cheerful efficiency of a man who found shelter-building fun, which I considered evidence of something slightly wrong with him. He'd called it "cozy." I had called it several things I won't repeat here, though my mother — wherever she was — would have been proud of at least two of them.

The cabin was small, maybe twelve feet by fourteen, built of peeled logs gone silver with age. The roof had held. The door had not quite: it hung on one leather hinge, breathing open and shut in the wind like the house itself was deciding whether to let us in.

Dominic went first, because he was the kind of man who went first, and because Silas had his rifle up and was scanning the treeline with an expression I'd learned to read as *professional*. I stood in the snow and tried to look useful by holding the map, which was upside down.

Inside: one bunk frame, one stove with a pipe still seated through the roof, one table, one chair, and a smell of old woodsmoke and mouse that was, after three days of our own smells, frankly luxurious.

"We're rich," Dominic announced.

I set down my pack and my shoulders made a sound like green ice cracking. "If there's a mattress made of money, don't tell me. I'll cry."

There was no mattress made of money. There was, however, half a bundle of cedar shakes stacked against the wall, a rusted pot, a tin of matches so old they crumbled when you looked at them, and — this was the part that stopped all three of us mid-motion — signs that someone else had been here recently.

Not decades ago. Recently.

The stove was cold, but the ash inside wasn't old ash. It was fine and pale and undisturbed, but when Silas crouched and put his nose nearly into the firebox, he grunted.

"Spruce and birch," he said. "Burned within the last month. Maybe six weeks." He looked up at us, and the lamplight — no, the daylight through the one window — caught the scar tissue along his jaw. "Somebody wintered here or sheltered here. Not Ouellette. Ouellette's been dead longer than I've been disgraced."

"Somebody," I said. "Or somebodies."

"Four somebodies," Dominic said quietly.

He was standing at the far wall, where somebody had scratched marks into the log with a knife or an awl. Four sets of tally marks, grouped in fives, marching across the wood in a careful column. Below them, in small, cramped letters that I recognized before my brain finished processing them — recognized the way you recognize your own name shouted across a parking lot — someone had written:

**E.M. — DAY 9 — ALL WELL — HEADING NNE**

My mother's handwriting. I'd know it anywhere. She wrote her E's like little combs, three teeth exactly, always. When I was eight she'd labeled all the jam jars in the kitchen that way, and I'd spent a summer eating raspberry preserves straight from the jar with a teaspoon, and when she caught me she didn't yell, she just relabeled the jar **N.M. — DAY 1 — ALL WELL**, and I'd laughed so hard I cried, and that was the last year before everything went wrong between us, and now here were her comb-teeth E's scratched into a wall in the middle of nowhere, nine days into whatever walk she and her team had made out of a camp that officially contained no people.

Nobody said anything for a while. Dominic put his hand on my shoulder, briefly, the way you'd steady a ladder. Silas studied the tallies like they might testify.

"Nine days," I managed. "They walked nine days' worth of days. And then they kept going northeast." I traced the direction on Crowe's map, which I had finally turned right-side up. "Toward the old workings."

"The adit," Silas said. "Where your informant's cache is." He said *your informant* the way other people say *my shame*, which is to say constantly, and with feeling.

"So we're on their trail." I heard my own voice harden and was grateful for it. Grief is a terrible fuel but it burns long. "Weeks behind, but on it."

"Trail's trail," Silas said. But he hadn't straightened up from the stove. He was looking at the floorboards near the door, and his face had done something complicated.

"What," Dominic said.

Silas didn't answer right away. He crossed to the window instead, moving with a low, deliberate economy that still surprised me every time — a big man who moved like a thought rather than a body. He looked out at the clearing, then at the treeline, then back at the floor.

"Come look at this," he said. "And then nobody step near the door again."

* * *

What he showed us was boot prints. Or rather, the ghost of boot prints — the snow inside the doorway had melted and refrozen in the changing temperatures, preserving impressions the way amber preserves insects. Two sets. One larger, one smaller, both newer than anything my mother's team would have left. Both coming in, and only one going out.

Wait. No. I looked again, and my stomach performed a maneuver I did not authorize.

Both coming in. Neither going out.

"They walked in," I said, "and then?"

"Then they walked out after the crust softened," Silas said. "Or they left another way. Or—" He stopped himself, which was its own kind of answer.

"Or they're in the ground behind the cabin," Dominic finished, flat. "Thanks, Silas. Really keeping the mood buoyant."

"I'm not paid to keep the mood buoyant."

"You're not paid at all."

"That's why the quality suffers."

It was gallows humor and we all clung to it like men clinging to a rail in weather, because the alternative was admitting what the prints meant, which was this: whoever burned our hangar, whoever erased the tracks on the plateau, whoever had turned four living geologists into a paperwork problem — he had stood in this room. He had stood in this room weeks ago, following the same trail we were following, and then he had walked out into the trees and kept walking, northeast, the same direction we intended to walk tomorrow.

We weren't following Elena's trail.

We were following Rook's.

* * *

We spent the rest of the light doing the things you do when you've realized you're prey: pretending to be hunters.

Dominic checked the roofline and the chimney and pronounced the cabin defensible, "in the sense that a teacup is defensible if the bear is polite." Silas walked a slow circuit fifty yards out, reading the snow around the clearing the way I read a letter twice — once for content, once for what it was hiding. I organized our gear, which mostly meant touching each item and putting it back, but organizedly.

It was during this that I found the tin.

It was behind the loose board under the bunk — a biscuit tin, rusted at the corners, and inside, wrapped in waxed paper gone brittle as autumn leaves: a surveyor's notebook. Not my mother's. This one belonged to Ouellette, according to the flyleaf, and it was mostly trapline records — marten counts, fur prices, a long-running feud with a lynx he referred to only as "the gentleman" — but tucked into the back pages was a hand-drawn sketch of the surrounding country with distances marked in chains, and one annotation in pencil that made the hair on my arms stand up:

*Company men came through '89 asking about the north line. Told them nothing. They don't ask twice. The gentleman got one of their dogs.*

Gentleman: 1. Meridian Mining: 0. I decided instantly that I loved this dead trapper and his cat-shaped nemesis, and I put the notebook in my jacket, against my chest, where the important things rode.

"Find anything?" Dominic called from the stove, where he was performing alchemy on our remaining food: rice, a strip of smoked whitefish, snowmelt, hope.

"A dead man who hated the mining company and kept score against a lynx."

"Sounds like Silas."

"I heard that," Silas said, from outside, through the wall, without raising his voice, which somehow made it funnier and more unsettling in equal measure.

* * *

Night came down the way it does up there — not falling so much as being poured, thick and total, over everything. We ate our rice around the stove with the shuttered lantern turned low, and we argued about watches.

"Three shifts," Silas said. "Two hours each. I'll take first."

"You took first last night," I said.

"And I'll take it tonight. I sleep worse than either of you anyway. Consider it charity."

"It's not charity if you want it," Dominic said. "That's just scheduling."

"Nadia takes midnight, I take two, she gets dawn." Silas settled against the wall with the rifle across his knees, and something in the set of him closed like a shop gate. Conversation over.

But I wasn't ready for conversation to be over, because conversation was the only thing holding the fear down, and the moment it stopped, the fear came up through the floorboards like cold.

"Tell me about him," I said. "Rook. You've both been circling it for days."

Dominic and Silas exchanged a look — the particular look of two men deciding who has to say the unpleasant thing, a negotiation conducted entirely in eyebrows.

"He's professional," Silas said finally. "That's the whole report. Ten years ago, working a case out of Fort Nelson, I heard the name attached to three separate incidents that all looked like misadventure. A fall. A drowning. A fire. Different companies, different towns, same shape underneath. Like footprints." He turned his hands palm-up. "You learn to recognize a print. Doesn't matter whose boot made it."

"And you never caught him."

"I never looked very hard," Silas said, "because by then I'd already been bought, and bought men don't go digging in other men's graves. They're afraid of finding their own." He said it without drama, the way you'd recite a license number, and somehow that was worse. Then, because he was Silas, he added: "He'll be patient. That's the thing to understand. He doesn't need to be clever. The country does his work for him. All he has to do is wait for one of us to make the mistake the wilderness is already suggesting."

"Cheerful," said Dominic.

"I'm saving my cheer for when we're out."

"Is there a specific date?"

"Spring, if we're lucky. Never, if we're honest."

I pulled my mother's letter from my inner pocket — the real one, the one that had started all this, soft as cloth now from handling — and read it again by the coal-glow of the stove. *Numbers that don't add*, she'd written. *If anything happens to me, don't trust the company, don't trust the tribunal filings, trust the rock.* Trust the rock. She'd been a geologist to the marrow; even her paranoia had a methodology.

"She knew," I said. "Out here, nine days walking, writing tallies on a wall — she knew someone might come behind her. Why else leave a message? She was leaving a trail for whoever cared enough to follow."

"Then she left it for you," Dominic said. He said it simply, without ceremony, the way he said most true things, and I had to turn toward the wall and pretend to check the shutter for a while.

* * *

My watch. Midnight to two.

The others slept — Dominic curled like a comma, breathing slow; Silas apparently asleep but I had begun to suspect Silas slept the way a rifle sleeps, i.e., technically at rest, functionally loaded. I sat by the window gap with the blanket around my shoulders and watched the dark.

Here is what nobody tells you about the northern night: it isn't empty. It's full. Every sound has a source and none of the sources are visible. The trees ticked and groaned as the temperature fell. Somewhere down the creek, ice was forming with a sound like distant applause. Once, close — too close — something moved through the snow with a soft, rhythmic crunch-crunch-pause, crunch-crunch-pause, and my heart went off like a flare before my brain supplied the obvious: a fox, hunting mice under the crust. The gentleman's smaller cousin, perhaps, keeping the family business going.

I made myself catalog the sounds. Naming things was my mother's trick for fear — she used to do it with thunderstorms when I was small. *That's just the sky rearranging its furniture, Nadia.* So: wind, ice, trees, fox. Wind, ice, trees, fox. And then, at some hour I couldn't name, a sound that didn't fit the list.

A sound like a boot. One boot. Far off, southeast, in the timber. Then nothing.

I sat absolutely still for a full minute, then two, listening so hard I could hear my own blood. Nothing came again. Foxes don't make boot sounds. But wind moves limbs, and limbs fall, and a falling limb can sound like almost anything when you're alone in a dead trapper's cabin with your mother's handwriting on the wall and a killer somewhere behind you on the same trail.

I woke Silas. I hadn't planned to — my hand just did it, shaking his shoulder, and his eyes opened all the way at once, no fog, no confusion, which told me everything about how he actually slept.

"Sound," I whispered. "Southeast. One step. Maybe twenty minutes ago."

He listened. The cabin breathed. The stove ticked as it cooled.

"Could be a tree," he murmured.

"Could be."

"Could be him."

"Could be."

He rose without a sound, took the rifle, and stood at the window gap for a long time, a silhouette cut out of the fainter darkness beyond. When he finally turned back, his face was unreadable, which from Silas was a full paragraph.

"Dawn's in four hours," he said. "We'll know more in the light. Sleep if you can."

I couldn't. I lay on the bunk frame with my jacket balled under my head and stared at the roof beams and did arithmetic I hated: if Rook was behind us, he was closer than we'd believed, because we'd believed we had days. If he was ahead of us — if he'd passed us in the storm, circled wide, was waiting at the adit or the cache or any of the dozen places we had to go — then the trail we were following wasn't a trail at all. It was a corridor. And corridors have two ends.

At some point Dominic stirred and, without opening his eyes, reached up from the floor and hooked one finger around my ankle, the way you'd check a mooring line. Held it a moment. Let go. Said nothing.

I slept after that. A little.

* * *

Dawn came gray and grainy, and Silas was already outside when I crawled down from the bunk. He was crouched at the treeline to the southeast, and when Dominic and I joined him, he didn't point or speak. He just stepped aside.

There, pressed into the crusted snow at the edge of the trees, half-filled with last night's powder but unmistakable: a boot print. Deep-heeled, long-strided, facing north.

Facing the same direction we were going.

"He passed in the night," Silas said quietly. "While we sat in here arguing about shifts, he walked past this cabin close enough to count the boards." He straightened, and there was something new in his face — not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man meeting an old debt come due. "He knows we're behind him. He's known since the hangar. He's not running."

"Then what's he doing?" I asked, though I already knew. I think I'd known since the ash in the stove.

Silas shouldered the rifle and looked north, toward the adit, toward my mother, toward whatever waited at the end of the corridor.

"He's choosing the ground," he said. "Let's make sure we choose it first."
