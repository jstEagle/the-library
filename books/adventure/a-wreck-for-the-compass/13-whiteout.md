## 13. Whiteout

The storm announced itself the way a debt collector does — politely at first, then all at once.

We'd made maybe nine miles out of the informant's cabin when the sky went the color of dishwater and the wind came down off the peaks like it had a personal grudge. Dominic had been watching that ridge line all morning, reading it the way Silas read tracks, and when the first spindrift hissed across the snow like sand through an hourglass, he called the halt without ceremony.

"There," he said, pointing at a wind-scoured hollow beneath a granite shoulder. "We dig in."

"Dig in where?" Nadia asked. There was nothing there. That was the whole problem with whiteouts — they didn't just take away the horizon, they took away your faith in the ground.

"Under it," Dominic said, and started shoveling with the aluminum scoop from his pack.

They cut blocks of snow with the saw and built a wall, then hollowed a chamber into the drift behind it, big enough for three people who liked each other and barely adequate for three people who were about to spend two days breathing each other's air. Silas rigged the tarp as a roof membrane and packed snow over it until the whole thing looked like a whale carcass that had died of ambition. By the time they finished, the world outside had ceased to exist entirely. No trees. No ridgeline. No up or down, just a howling gray nothing pressing against the entrance like a thumb over a hole in a dike.

Inside, by candle stub, Dominic did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since morning.

Two days pinned. Maybe three, if the front stalled the way fronts sometimes did on this side of the range. They'd left the informant's cache with food for six days at full rations; at half rations they could stretch to ten, but half rations meant cold bodies, and cold bodies made bad decisions. He counted the fuel tablets twice, hoping the number would improve on the second count. It didn't.

"Well," Silas said, settling back against his pack with the sigh of a man who had done this before and hadn't enjoyed it then either. "Anyone know any card games?"

"I know one," Nadia said. "It's called *we should have turned back at the lake*."

Dominic winced. She wasn't wrong, exactly. The sensible move — the move every survival manual ever written would endorse — was to sit out the storm and then retrace their steps to the frozen lake, wait out the weather window, fly home, come back in spring with proper gear and proper numbers. Every hour they spent pushing toward the adit was an hour borrowed against their margin for error, and the margin was already thinner than the ice under a late-season moose.

But she'd said it looking straight at him, and he understood it wasn't really about the lake.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble often does, with hands.

Nadia's mittens had been wrong from the start — city mittens, beautiful wool things her mother had knitted her twenty years ago, which is exactly the kind of object that carries you north and then betrays you. Somewhere in the second day's march, before the storm, she'd taken them off to work the map, and the cold had gotten into her fingers the way water gets into a hull: quietly, and then catastrophically.

By candlelight, Dominic peeled the wet wool back and felt his stomach drop. Her fingertips were waxy, bloodless, the color of candle tallow. Two of them on the right hand, three on the left. Not black yet. Not gone. But the kind of white that doesn't come back on its own.

"How long have they been like this?"

"Like what?" She tried to flex her fingers and couldn't quite. Her face did something complicated — fear trying on anger as a disguise. "They're fine. They're cold. Everyone's cold."

"Nadia." He kept his voice level, the voice he used on passengers who'd just discovered what turbulence actually was. "Look at them. Really look."

She looked. And he watched the fight go out of her shoulders all at once, watched her understand in a single terrible second what her body had been trying to tell her for hours and she'd been refusing to hear because hearing it meant stopping, and stopping meant losing the trail, and losing the trail meant losing her mother.

"They'll come back," she said. "Right? You warm them up and they come back."

"If we warm them now, properly, and keep them warm? Probably." He said *probably* and hated himself for it, but he wasn't going to lie to her. Lying to her would cost more later. "If we keep marching on them in this cold, no. Frostbite that goes deep doesn't ask politely. It takes."

Silas said nothing, but he'd stopped unpacking, and his silence had a shape to it. Dominic knew that shape. It was the shape of a man doing the same arithmetic and arriving at the same answer: *turn back.*

"So we turn back," Dominic said gently. "Storm breaks tomorrow, we walk south to the lake, I get us airborne, and we come back in February with a doctor's clearance and gear that fits. Nadia—"

"No."

The word came out small and absolute, a stone dropped down a well.

"Nadia—"

"No." Louder now, cracking in the middle. "You don't understand. My mother has been *out here* for weeks. Weeks! And every day we turn around is a day she—" Her breath hitched. "I spent eleven years not calling her back. Eleven years of being too proud, too busy, too *something*, and now the only thing I have left is finding her, and you want me to trade her fingers for mine? To go home and *wait*?"

"It's not trading," Dominic said. "It's coming back equipped. There's a difference between retreat and resupply."

"Not if she dies while we're resupplying!"

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind found some new register of fury, something between a freight train and a choir. In the little dome of packed snow, the three of them sat with the truth between them like a fourth passenger nobody wanted to look at.

It was Silas who finally spoke.

"My first winter on the trapline," he said, "I lost two toes because I wouldn't stop for a blister. You know why? Pride? No. Worse. I'd convinced myself that turning around was the same thing as failing, and I'd rather lose parts of myself than fail." He held up his boot, waggled it. "Forty years old and I still can't feel my little toe. You know what I got for my stubbornness? Nothing. The trapline didn't care. The mountain didn't care. The only person impressed by my suffering was me, and believe me, I'm a cheap audience."

"This isn't a blister," Nadia said.

"No. It's worse. Which is why the lesson scales." He leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the seams of his face. "Your mother walked out of Caribou Bend knowing the odds. Elena Marsh struck me as a woman who did her sums. If she could see those hands, what do you think she'd say?"

Nadia's jaw worked. "She'd say put on better mittens and stop whining."

"She'd say exactly that," Dominic agreed, "and then she'd say *go back and come again smarter*, because that's what surveyors do. You don't run a traverse off a bad fix. You re-shoot it."

For a long moment Nadia stared at her own ruined-white fingers as if they belonged to someone else, someone careless, someone she was furious at. Then, so quietly the wind nearly ate it:

"I can't go back to empty."

And there it was. Not stubbornness. Not stupidity. Grief, wearing a parka, walking north.

Dominic reached over and took her wrist — carefully, above the damage. "Nobody said anything about empty. We go back, we regroup, we come back. But we do it in an order that keeps everyone alive, including you. Because here's the thing you haven't figured out yet, Nadia Marsh: you're not cargo on this expedition. You're crew. And I don't leave crew behind, and I don't let crew destroy themselves either. That's the deal. That's been the deal since the hangar fire."

She looked at him. Something moved behind her eyes — exhaustion, mostly, and under it a grudging, unwilling tenderness, the way ice shifts before it accepts the sun.

"Half rations," she said finally. "We stay put through the storm, and if it clears, we reassess. I'm not promising anything."

"That's all I'm asking. Reassessment." He exhaled. "Welcome to aviation. Everything is reassessment."

* * *

The storm owned the next thirty-six hours completely.

They ran the stove low, melting snow in shifts, and Silas produced, from some bottomless pocket of his pack, a greasy deck of cards missing the seven of hearts and bearing the cigarette burn of some previous owner's despair. He taught them a trapping-camp game called Screw Your Neighbor that appeared to have no strategy whatsoever and therefore suited all three of them perfectly.

"You're cheating," Nadia informed him after the third hand.

"I am absolutely cheating," Silas agreed serenely. "It's the only skill the Force never put on my resume."

"You were Mountie."

"I was a Mountie the way a shipwreck is a boat."

Dominic laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the plateau, since the erased boot prints, since the whole rotten business began — and heard the sound bounce around the snow cave and come back changed. When had this happened? When had these two become the people whose voices he needed to hear? He'd spent forty-one years belonging to the sky precisely because the sky asked nothing of him except competence. Now here he was in a hole in a snowdrift, down to his last twelve fuel tablets, responsible for a grieving cartographer and a disgraced lawman, and instead of feeling trapped he felt — he searched for the word and was embarrassed by it — *held.*

The tenderness crept in sideways, the way it always does. He noticed Nadia had started sleeping with her hands tucked inside Silas's spare sweater, which Silas had donated without comment and defended fiercely whenever she tried to give it back. He noticed Silas had begun saving Nadia the first pour of the tea without anyone asking him to. He noticed that when Nadia slept, she'd migrated in the night until her shoulder rested against his, and that he'd adjusted his own position so she wouldn't have to wake up embarrassed about it.

None of them mentioned any of it. That was the family part, he supposed. Families were just people who'd stopped keeping score.

On the second evening, warming her hands against his chest through two layers of shirt — doctor's orders, though neither of them said so aloud — Nadia spoke into the dark.

"When we find her," she said, "she's going to be so angry with me."

"For coming?"

"For everything. For the eleven years. For showing up furious. For probably getting frostbite because I wouldn't wear the right gloves, which is *exactly* the thing she'd—" A wet laugh. "She used to say I inherited her stubbornness and none of her judgment."

"Sounds like you inherited both," Dominic said. "The judgment's just running a few years behind schedule. Mine did too."

"What was yours?"

He was quiet a moment. The wind screamed its one long note outside.

"A woman in Yellowknife," he said finally. "Ten years ago. I was flying hard, building hours, building a name. She asked me to stay. I told her the sky was home." He shrugged in the dark, uselessly. "Turns out a home you can't land in isn't a home. It's just a very large way of being alone."

"And now?"

"Now I'm in a snow cave playing cards with a cheat and a mule." He felt her smile against his shoulder. "And it's the least alone I've been in a decade. Draw whatever conclusion you want."

* * *

The storm broke on the third morning the way a fever breaks — suddenly, and leaving everything weak and glittering.

Dominic crawled out into a world remade. The storm had scoured the sky down to a blue so hard it rang, and the sun lay along the snowfields in long gold bars, and the peaks stood up sharp and clean as if freshly carved. It was, objectively, the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and he hated it, because beauty like that was a liar. It made you want to push on. It made you forget what it had just spent two days trying to do to you.

He checked Nadia's hands first thing. Color was back in four of the five damaged fingertips — mottled, tender, blistered at the worst one, but alive. The fifth, the left index finger, stayed stubbornly pale and numb.

"That one worries me," he admitted.

"It can worry you while we're walking," Nadia said. She was already packing, moving with the brisk economy of someone who had won an argument in absentia and knew it. "Because I've been thinking. While you two snored."

"We don't snore," Silas said.

"You both snore in harmony. It's very touching." She spread the map on a pack lid, weighting the corners with stones, and her good hand traced the line north. "Here's my reassessment. My hands are bad enough that another storm ends the expedition — fine, granted, conceded, witnessed. So we don't get caught by another storm. The front passed. High pressure behind it means three clear days, maybe four, before the next system. The adit is eighteen miles. We march hard, we reach it in two, we're inside the mountain — *inside*, Dominic, underground, where the weather can't touch us — and we hold up there until the window opens for the walk back. The mine is the safest place in the entire range. It's the one place the sky can't follow us."

Dominic stared at the map. He wanted to find the flaw. He genuinely, professionally wanted to find the flaw, because the alternative was admitting that the grieving daughter with the city mittens had just out-navigated him.

"The timing works," Silas said slowly. "If the high holds."

"If the high holds," Dominic echoed. "And if it doesn't?"

"Then we're in a mine with supplies and shelter instead of on a glacier with neither." Nadia looked up at him, and her chapped face was set in an expression he recognized from photographs — the same jaw, the same unblinking dark eyes. Elena's eyes, aimed at him across twenty years and a fold in a map. "You said I was crew. Crew gets a vote."

"Crew gets a vote," he agreed. "Captain gets the last word." He looked at the clean blue sky, at the gold light lying on the snow, at the two people waiting on his answer as if his answer were a load-bearing wall. His hands, he noticed, weren't shaking. Whatever else the last weeks had cost him, they'd given him back his nerve.

"Then here's the last word," he said. "We break camp in twenty minutes. Eighteen miles, hard pace, and if anyone's hands go white again, we stop and I make the call and nobody argues — including you. Deal?"

"Deal," Nadia said, and stuck out her bandaged hand, and they shook on it, gently, like people handling something fragile that they intended, somehow, to keep whole.

Silas was already kicking snow over the stove's ashes. Then he stopped, head cocked, facing north into the blinding glare, and Dominic watched the ease drain out of the old man's face the way color drains from a sunset.

"What?" Dominic said.

"Tracks," Silas said quietly. "Along our backtrail. Fresh ones. Somebody's been following us through the storm."

Dominic turned. Far to the south, at the edge of seeing, a single dark figure stood motionless on a rise against the burning snow — watching them, unhurried, patient as winter itself.

Then it stepped backward over the ridge and was gone.
