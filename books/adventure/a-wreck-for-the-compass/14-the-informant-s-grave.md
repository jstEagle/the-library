## 14. The Informant's Grave

The cabin sat in a fold of the valley like something the mountain had tried to swallow and half succeeded.

Silas Crowe saw it first from two hundred yards out — a dark notch of timber roofline against the white, one corner sagging where the snow load had been winning an argument with the joists for a decade or more. He stopped and held up a fist, the old habit, and behind him Dominic and Nadia came to a halt without being told. Three days on the trail had taught them his hand signals if not yet his silences.

"That's it," Silas said, and heard how strange his own voice sounded saying it. "That's Amos's place."

"Amos," Nadia said. "Your informant."

"My friend, near the end. Informant sounds too much like business." He worked his jaw under the frost-crusted scarf. "He was a trapper before he was anything to me. Forty years on this line. He knew every game trail, every creek, every lie anybody ever told within fifty miles of here, because nothing stays secret in the bush — it just goes quiet for a while."

Dominic shifted the weight of his pack and looked at the cabin the way pilots look at weather building over a pass. "And you're sure he kept what you sent him?"

"I'm sure he was the most stubborn man I ever met, and I once arrested a mule that wouldn't cross a bridge." Silas started forward again, breaking trail. "I'm also sure that when I asked him to hold onto those photographs, he said, 'Crowe, I'll keep your paper safe longer than you'll keep your badge.' Which at the time stung like a slap. Now it sounds like prophecy."

They came down through the trees single file, boots crunching the crust that had formed overnight, breath hanging in little flags behind them. The cold had settled into its winter manners now — not the dramatic killer of the first days but the patient kind, the kind that just quietly took inventory of everything you owned and started charging interest.

Up close, the cabin was worse and better than it had looked. Worse: the door hung open on a hinge gone to rust lace, snowdrifted halfway up the interior wall. Better: someone had built it to last. Amos Teller had been many things, but sloppy with timber wasn't one of them. The walls were spruce logs adzed flat, chinked with moss, and even after all these years they stood straighter than Silas felt.

He stood at the threshold and didn't go in.

It was foolish. He'd walked away from worse scenes than this — he'd walked away from a scene very much like this, ten years ago, carrying an envelope of cash and a career's worth of shame. But standing there, looking at the grey light falling through the broken door onto a table still set with a tin plate and a mug fossilized to its surface, he felt the years collapse like a snow bridge.

"Crowe?" Dominic said softly.

"He died in here." Silas cleared his throat. "Winter before last, we figure. The trapline association sent a man up in the spring. Found him in the bunk."

Nadia stepped past him, careful, her bandaged hands tucked into her armpits the way she carried them now since the whiteout. She'd refused to turn back then, and she hadn't complained about the fingers since, which worried Silas more than complaining would have. She looked around the cabin with the expression of someone reading a letter.

"It's tidy," she said. "For a man who died alone."

"He was tidy. Drove me crazy. I'd come up for a visit and he'd be re-hanging his door because it stuck by a sixteenth of an inch." Silas finally crossed the threshold, ducking under the lintel out of habit though he'd have fit fine. "Everything in its place. Except the thing I gave him, which is why we're here."

* * *

They searched the way Silas imagined archaeologists must search — slowly, respectfully, touching everything twice. Dominic took the walls and floorboards; Nadia took the shelves and the cold stove; Silas took the places he already knew and checked anyway, because hope makes you superstitious.

The cabin gave up its ordinary history in small confessions. A rack of traps, oiled and wrapped in canvas, ready for a season that never came. A shelf of books — Zane Grey, a Bible, a surveyor's almanac going back twenty years, each one annotated in a pencil hand so small and precise it looked printed. On the wall above the bunk, a photograph in a birchbark frame: a young woman squinting into summer sun beside a string of fish, and beneath it, in that same tiny hand, *Martha, 1961.*

"His wife?" Nadia asked.

"Daughter. Moved south when she was nineteen. He talked about her the way other men talk about weather — constantly, and mostly about how much of it there was." Silas touched the frame's edge. "He never went down to see her. Said the road only ran one direction as far as he was concerned."

"And you?" Dominic had paused by the stove, one hand resting on the blackened iron. "How did a Mountie and a trapper end up friends?"

"The usual way. I arrested him."

Nadia laughed — a short, surprised sound, the first one in days. "You did not."

"I did. Poaching, 1979. He'd taken a moose out of season, and he didn't run, didn't argue. Just said, 'You'll want to write down that my freezer's empty and my daughter's birthday's Thursday, so weigh that against your regulation book however you like.'" Silas found himself smiling at the memory, which surprised him; most of his memories from those years had gone sour in the jar. "I wrote it up. Judge fined him forty dollars. He mailed me half the moose as a thank-you, which was technically evidence tampering, and I ate it, which made me an accessory. After that we were done pretending we were anything else."

The light through the doorway was starting to lean amber. They had maybe three hours of usable daylight, and Rook was somewhere behind them — or ahead of them, or both; the man moved through country like smoke moving through a house. They couldn't afford sentiment. Silas knew that. He searched anyway.

It was Nadia who found it, because of course it was. She had a surveyor's eye, Elena's eye, and she noticed things that added up wrong.

"This stove pipe," she said. "It's new."

Silas came over. The stove itself was ancient cast iron, but the pipe running up through the ceiling was bright galvanized steel, factory-clean, utterly wrong against everything else in the room.

"He replaced it," Dominic said. "Before he died?"

"No." Silas crouched, heart beginning a slow, heavy drumming. "Amos never replaced anything until it failed. And this pipe doesn't show stove use — no creosote, no heat discoloration. It was put in to look like a stove pipe." He reached up along the seam, fingers numb, working the joint where the sections joined. It didn't turn. It didn't need to. The whole pipe lifted free of the collar at the ceiling like a key sliding out of a lock.

Behind it, mortared into the chimney gap, was a tin box the size of a bread loaf, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with what had once been tar.

Nobody spoke. The wind moved through the broken door and stirred the dust, and Silas thought he could almost hear Amos Teller saying, *Well? You going to stand there all day, Crowe?*

He brought the box down and set it on the table, next to the dead man's plate and mug, which felt right somehow. His hands shook getting the oilcloth off — cold, age, and something else — and when the lid came up, the smell of old paper rose out of it like a ghost clearing its throat.

Photographs. A thick stack of them, rubber-banded, plus a folded oilskin packet and a map.

"Oh," Nadia breathed.

* * *

They laid the photographs out on the table in the grey-gold light, and Silas found he couldn't touch them at first. He knew what they were. He'd seen them once, eleven years ago, in an envelope he was supposed to destroy, and instead of destroying them he'd driven them north and handed them to a stubborn old man and told himself that was enough. That keeping them alive somewhere was the same as doing something.

It hadn't been. He knew that now. But they were alive.

The first photograph showed the old workings' lower camp, taken from the ridge above — Meridian's exploration camp, a decade ago, but with far more vehicles than any exploration camp needed. The second showed men loading something onto a truck bed under tarps, at night, the flash freezing them mid-motion. The third—

The third was the one that had ended Silas's career, or rather the one that should have. It showed the sealed ventilation adit of the underground workings, and in front of it, a row of stretchers. Eight of them. Men under blankets. Company men standing around with their hats off, which was the detail that had haunted Silas longest — the hats off, the strange formality of it, men observing the courtesies of a funeral nobody was supposed to know was happening.

"A cave-in," Nadia said quietly. "The report said a cave-in with no casualties."

"There was no cave-in." Silas's voice came out flat as lake ice. "There was a fire in the lower workings. Bad ground, cheap ventilation, and a company that didn't want to stop production for repairs. Eight men went down on shift and eight men came up dead, and Meridian's solution was to seal the level, pay off the families, and file a report saying the section had been played out and closed for economic reasons." He tapped the photograph of the stretchers. "These men were listed in company records as having quit. Resigned. Walked away from good wages in the middle of a boom. Every one of them."

"Where did these come from?" Dominic asked. "Who took them?"

"A safety inspector named Gagnon. Company-contracted, which is how he got access, and honest, which is how he got fired. He shot that roll the night it happened — he'd been on site doing air quality tests and stayed late. He tried for months to get anyone official to look at them." Silas's jaw tightened. "Anyone official included me. I was the investigating officer when Gagnon finally went outside the company. And my commanding officer at the time was already having his mortgage paid by somebody with a Meridian logo on their Christmas cards."

"You took the payoff," Nadia said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table alongside the photographs, where it belonged.

"I took the envelope. I told myself I'd find another way to bury Gagnon's evidence properly, some legal channel, later." Silas looked at his own hands, scarred and cracked and steady now only because he willed them to be. "Then I drove north and gave the photographs to Amos, and reported Gagnon as a disgruntled contractor spreading fabrication claims, and let the case die. My CO got promoted. I got transferred to a posting so small it didn't appear on some maps, and eventually I resigned before they could finish finding a reason to fire me. And Amos—" he gestured at the cabin, at the plate, at the grave of a friendship he'd neglected for years out of shame— "Amos kept faith better than I deserved. For eleven years. In a tin box behind a stove pipe. Waiting for me to come back and be the man he thought I was."

The silence held for a moment. Then Dominic said, in the mild tone he used for delivering hard truths, "He waited because he knew you'd come back. Men like that don't keep boxes for people they've given up on."

Silas didn't answer. He unfolded the map instead, because his eyes were doing something embarrassing and the map gave them somewhere else to be.

* * *

The map was a topographic sheet of the high plateau country, the same series Elena Marsh would have used, and it was covered in Amos's tiny pencil handwriting — decades of it, notes in margins like a diary written in geography. *Moose yarded here, Nov '84. Creek changed course after flood, '91.* And then, fresher, the graphite barely faded:

A circle, drawn around a feature on the eastern shoulder of the plateau. Beside it, in letters pressed deep enough to read by fingertip alone:

*Marsh party came through Oct 3. Asked about the old adit. Told them what I knew. Woman — the leader — she understood. Said the numbers were wrong and she meant to prove how. Went east toward the seal. Watched them go. God forgive us all, I think she found it.*

Below that, a second line, added in a different sitting — the pencil shakier:

*Man asking questions after them. Not company. Worse. Turned back at the pass when the storm came. Will try the line again when it clears.*

Silas read it twice. Then he laid it flat where the others could see, and watched Nadia's face as she read it — watched her mother come alive for her in a dead trapper's handwriting, four weeks gone and still moving people across a mountain.

"She was headed for the adit," Nadia said. "The sealed level. Where they—" She stopped, steadied herself. "She figured it out. From the numbers. The ore estimates required the lower workings to have produced tonnage that could only have come out of a section the company claimed was closed. So she went to see for herself."

"And she found it," Dominic said. "Or she's near it. If she's alive."

"If," Nadia said, and the word landed between them like an axe.

Silas turned the map toward the light and studied the circle again. The eastern shoulder. High ground, exposed, a day and a half from here in good conditions — and conditions were not good, and tomorrow they'd be worse, and somewhere on this mountain a man named Rook was reading the same country they were.

"Gagnon died before any of this could matter," Silas said slowly. "Heart attack, two winters after. Never saw a day of justice. Amos died holding the proof, and it did him no good either. Eleven years, two graves, and the company's still filing permits." He rolled the photographs carefully, the way you handle something that has survived entirely by being treated gently. "But here's the thing about paper, and about patience. Power can buy sheriffs and editors and coroners. It can't buy every trapper in the range. It can't make a man throw out a box he promised to keep. Elena Marsh walked into that country with nothing but a hunch about arithmetic, and she walked toward the one place the truth was physically buried, because she trusted that buried isn't the same as gone."

He looked up at them — the pilot who'd lost his hangar and flown anyway, the daughter who'd lost her mother and walked anyway.

"We're the second expedition to this grave," Silas said. "Let's make sure we're the last ones who leave without answers."

Dominic was already studying the map, tracing the route east with one finger, lips moving as he calculated fuel and daylight and the mood of the sky. Nadia was copying Amos's notes into her notebook with her stiff, careful hands, refusing help, refusing rest.

Outside, the light was failing, and far off across the valley — so faint it might have been imagination, might have been wind, might have been neither — came a sound none of them wanted to name.

A rifle shot. One. Deliberate. Like a man marking a map of his own.
