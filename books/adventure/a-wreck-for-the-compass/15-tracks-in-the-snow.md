## 15. Tracks in the Snow

The morning after the informant's grave, we woke to a silence that had teeth in it.

I'd been flying the northern range for nineteen years, and I could tell you that snow has a hundred different silences — the soft one after a gentle fall, the ringing one when cold clamps down like a vise, the hollow one of wind moving through dead timber. This was none of those. This was the silence of a forest holding its breath around something it had seen and didn't want to talk about.

I stood at the edge of the old man's clearing with a mug of snow-water going cold in my hands and looked at the tree line until my eyes watered from the effort of seeing nothing.

"You feel it too," Silas said behind me. Not a question. He was breaking down his bedroll with the economy of a man who had slept outdoors for ten years on purpose, and he didn't look up while he said it. "Rook's not following our trail anymore. He's ahead of it."

"How can you know that?"

"Because if I were him, that's what I'd do." He cinched the last strap and straightened, rolling his shoulders the way an old dog does before deciding whether to bother getting up. "We've been reading his sign for two days. He knows we know. Smart money says he cuts the angle through the timber and picks his ground."

"And you'd pick?"

Silas squinted down the valley, where the creek dropped through a narrow throat of spruce and alder toward the lake country below. "There," he said, with no enthusiasm whatsoever. "Where the trees pinch in. If I were a man who got paid to make people disappear, that's where I'd wait."

Nadia came out of the cabin with Elena's map folded into her coat pocket and her jaw set in the particular way she had — the way her mother had, I realized, though I'd only met Elena Marsh across a radio and a campfire, briefly, two years ago. Genetics is a stubborn thing. It hands down cheekbones and stubbornness and apparently the inability to hear the word *dangerous* as anything other than *interesting*.

"So we go around," she said.

"We go around," I agreed.

"We go through," Silas said, at exactly the same moment.

We both looked at him.

"The detour adds half a day over open muskeg," he said. "No cover, no fuel wood, and if the weather turns — and it will turn; my knee's been singing hymns since dawn — we're caught out in it with nothing to burn. Through the pinch is faster, and it's bad ground for him too. Narrow. He gets one angle, one shot." He paused. "And I'll be between him and you."

"No," Nadia said flatly.

"Yes," Silas said, equally flatly, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before — not the dry self-mockery he wore like an old coat, but something underneath it, worn smooth by years of handling. "Nadia. Girl. Let me tell you how this works, because your mother never let me finish telling her, either."

That stopped her. She hadn't known, I think, that Silas had known Elena — or rather, she'd known they'd crossed paths a decade ago, in the case that broke him, but knowing a fact and hearing it spoken are different animals.

"I took money once to make evidence disappear," Silas said quietly. "People died because of it, and more people lived badly because of it, and I have spent ten years out here talking to martens about it because martens don't ask follow-up questions." He shouldered his pack. "This isn't redemption. Redemption's a word preachers use to sell tickets. This is arithmetic. I owe a debt, and today I get to pay some of it down. That's all. Don't make it prettier than it is."

"It's not your debt alone," Nadia said. "It's mine too. My mother walked into this alone because everyone who could have helped her didn't."

"And look how that went," Silas said gently. "Which is my point."

I cleared my throat, because somebody had to be the adult, and God knew it wasn't going to be either of them. "Nobody's dying today. We go through the pinch fast and quiet, eyes up, and nobody plays hero. Agreed? All three of us walk out of that timber, or none of us do."

Silas looked at me for a long moment, and then he did something I'd never seen him do: he nodded first. "Agreed," he said. "But Dominic — if it goes wrong, you take her and run. You don't come back for me. Swear it."

"I don't swear things I intend to break."

"That's the most honest thing a pilot's ever said to me." And for just a second, the old crooked smile came back, and I understood why, ten years ago, people had trusted this man. Trusted him right off a cliff, some of them. But trusted him.

* * *

The pinch was everything Silas had promised and less than we feared, for the first mile.

The creek ran under shelf ice, black and quick in its channel, and the trail along it was packed enough that we made good time. The spruce crowded close on both sides, old growth, their lower branches dead and gray, so that walking through them felt like moving down the aisle of a ruined cathedral. Nadia kept pace without complaint, which worried me more than complaining would have. Her hands were still wrapped against the frostbite from the whiteout, clumsy in her mitts, and every time she stumbled she corrected herself faster, angrier, as if the mountain were watching and taking notes.

It was. Of course it was. The wilderness always watches. It just doesn't usually hire help.

I heard the shot before I understood it — a flat crack that the canyon threw back and forth until it seemed to come from everywhere — and then the world became very simple and very loud.

"Down!" Silas roared, and shoved Nadia behind a fallen spruce with a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere, and the second shot came and took a fist-sized chunk of bark off the trunk a hand's width from my ear. Splinters stung my cheek. Somewhere upslope, maybe eighty yards, maybe a hundred, Rook was lying in the rocks with a rifle, and he was good, and he was patient, and we were pinned in a slot canyon with one way forward and one way back.

"Third shot's yours, Delacroix!" a voice called down, unhurried, almost friendly. Rook had a voice like gravel poured slowly. "You want to count the math on that?"

I counted the math. The math was bad.

Silas had gone very still beside the log, and I saw him doing his own arithmetic — lips moving slightly, eyes tracking the slope, reading the terrain the way I read weather. Then he looked at me, and I saw the decision arrive in his face before he said a word, the way a storm front shows on the horizon before the wind arrives.

"Creek ice," he murmured. "Under the shelf, thirty yards down, there's a cutbank where the water's undercut the bank. You can crawl it. It comes out below his position."

"You're coming with us."

"Somebody's got to give him something to shoot at while you crawl." He was already pulling the rifle off his shoulder, checking it with fingers that didn't shake, and I hated him a little for that. "He's expecting three targets huddled behind one log. Give him a fourth thing to think about and he'll hesitate. Hesitation's worth thirty yards."

"Silas—"

"Dominic." He gripped my arm, hard, through both sleeves. "Ten years ago I sat in an office and signed a piece of paper and four men went into a hole in the ground and stayed there. I have thought about that paper every single day for ten years, and I am telling you, as a man who knows the difference: this is not that. This is the other side of the ledger. Let me have it."

Above us, Rook shifted — I heard the scrape of a boot on rock, repositioning, patient as winter.

"He's going to flank," Silas said. "Thirty seconds, maybe less. Go."

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, and I've replayed it so many times since that the memory has worn grooves.

Silas stepped out from behind the log — stepped *out*, upright, into the open, firing twice upslope at nothing in particular, making noise, making himself a target, shouting something I couldn't parse over the gunfire — and I grabbed Nadia's collar and hauled her down the bank into the black water and the freezing dark under the shelf ice.

We crawled. The water was waist-deep and moving like a living thing that wanted us gone, and the ice above scraped my pack, and Nadia's breath came in ragged white gasps that echoed strangely under the shelf. Behind us, above us, muffled by ice and water and distance, the rifle fire continued — Silas's shots, then Rook's answer, then Silas again, moving, drawing him along the slope away from the cutbank.

Then a shot that sounded different. Lower. Closer to the ground.

Then nothing.

"Nadia, keep moving." My voice didn't sound like mine.

"He's hit—"

"*Keep moving.*"

She kept moving. God forgive me, I made her keep moving, because if I'd stopped I would have turned around, and turning around would have killed all three of us instead of possibly one. We came out of the cutbank into grey daylight, soaked to the ribs, and ran — not walked, *ran* — down the trail, through the timber, no direction in particular except away, until the cathedral spruce closed behind us and the sound of the creek faded and my lungs burned like I'd swallowed stove coals.

We collapsed against a blowdown maybe two miles on. Nadia was shaking — partly cold, mostly not. I wrung out my mitts uselessly and stared back the way we'd come.

Nothing followed. That was either very good or very bad.

* * *

We found Silas an hour before dark, and I will tell you honestly that when I saw the blood on the snow my heart fell out of my body and lay in the trail beating.

He'd dragged himself off the main track — of course he had, the stubborn old mule, even shot he wouldn't leave sign for Rook to read — into a stand of young pines, and he was propped against one of them with his rifle across his lap and his face grey as porridge, and when he saw us his first words were:

"Took you long enough. Thought I'd have to send a postcard."

The bullet had gone through the meat of his left shoulder, high, missing anything that mattered by what he later described, with typical understatement, as "a whisker and a prayer." He'd field-dressed it himself with a strip of shirt and a quantity of swearing. His pack was gone — dropped somewhere on the slope, along with, I discovered with a lurch, most of ours. In the scramble at the ambush, in the creek, in the blind running afterward, we had shed food the way a fleeing bird sheds feathers. When we inventoried what remained, crouched in the blue dusk of the pines, it came to this: one bag of bannock mix, half a pound of salt pork, a tin of tea, six bouillon cubes, and Nadia's emergency chocolate bar, which she surrendered to the common pot without being asked, which tells you more about her than any speech.

"Three days' food for three people," I said. "Two days' travel to the adit, minimum, and Silas can't move fast."

"I can move," Silas said.

"You'll move at the speed I say," I told him, and something in my voice must have surprised him, because he shut up. Fury does that. I was furious — at Rook, at Vane, at the whole rotten concession of it, but mostly at Silas, for stepping into the open, for deciding his ledger balanced better with a hole in him, for making me complicit in a sacrifice I hadn't agreed to. "You want to pay a debt? Fine. Pay it alive. Dead men's payments bounce."

That got a wheeze out of him that might have been a laugh. "Preacher Delacroix."

"Somebody has to be, out here."

Nadia worked in silence, cleaning the wound properly with boiled water and the last of our whiskey — medicinal, she said, daring anyone to object — while Silas watched her with an expression I couldn't read until I could. It was the look of a man watching someone do for him what nobody had done in ten years. Not the wound care. The *bothering*.

"My informant," he said suddenly, quietly, while she tied off the bandage. "Old Peter. You remember what was in his cache. The photographs. The map."

"Elena's team was headed for the sealed adit," Nadia said. "We know."

"And Rook knows we know." Silas flexed the fingers of his wounded arm experimentally, winced, and kept going anyway. "So here's the arithmetic now. He doesn't need to chase us anymore. He needs to be at the adit when we arrive. He can move faster than us — no wounded man, no green girl—" 

"I'm not green," Nadia said dangerously.

"You're green as June, girl, and it's not an insult, it's a measurement." He looked at me. "He'll circle ahead tonight. Which means the trail behind us is clear, and the ground ahead is not, and the smart play — the play I'd advise, if anyone were fool enough to take my advice — is to not play smart. Walk straight at him. Tired, hungry, short-handed." The crooked smile again, thinner now. "Because he's counting on us being careful, and careful is a luxury we just lost in a creek."

I sat with that, chewing a strip of salt pork that tasted mostly of salt and decision. Above the pines, the sky had cleared to that hard diamond cold that means the temperature's going to fall through the floor of the thermometer, and somewhere north, past the black ranks of the range, the sealed adit was waiting with Elena's report inside it, and Rook was walking toward it too, and midnight on permit day was coming down on all of us like an axe.

"All right," I said finally. "Straight at him. But we do it my way — slow march tonight while the cold keeps him in his bag, big miles at dawn, and nobody—" I pointed at Silas, then at Nadia, then back at Silas, "—*nobody* steps into the open again. We're a crew. Crews don't spend members. Understood?"

Silas held my gaze for a long moment. Then he reached over with his good hand and picked up the bannock mix, and began measuring out tomorrow's breakfast with the meticulousness of a man balancing books.

"Understood," he said. "But Dominic — one correction. For the record." He poured the flour carefully, level, exact. "A crew spends what it has to. The trick — the whole trick, the only trick — is making sure what you spend buys something worth the price." He glanced up, and the firelight caught the grey in his beard. "Elena Marsh is worth the price. Always was. I just took ten years to do the sums right."

Nadia's hands went still over the bandage roll. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Some debts get acknowledged in silence, and some get paid in blood on snow, and the northern range takes both currencies and gives no receipts.

We slept in shifts. I took first watch, sitting with the rifle across my knees, watching the timber breathe, and somewhere in the small hours I heard — or thought I heard, or maybe wanted to hear — a single shot far off to the northeast, toward the adit.

Rook, signaling someone. Or Rook, arriving early.

Either way, the message was the same: whatever window we'd had was closing, and the road ahead had gotten shorter and darker, and dawn was going to find us walking straight into the mouth of it.
