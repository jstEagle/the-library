## 16. The Sealed Adit

The map from the informant's cache had been drawn in pencil on the back of a 1979 hunting license, and it was wrong in exactly the way old men's maps are wrong — which is to say, confidently.

"Here," Silas said, tapping a crease where the paper had been folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. "The adit's above the creek fork. Maybe two hours if the snow holds."

"The snow will not hold," Dominic said. "The snow never holds. The snow is contractually obligated to betray us."

We'd been walking since dawn, and by noon the light had gone flat and pearl-colored, the kind of light that erases distance and makes every tree look like the next tree and every hour feel like the same hour wearing different coats. My hands were still tender inside their mitts — the frostbite from the whiteout had healed into a permanent faint ache, like my fingers were remembering something I wanted them to forget. I flexed them anyway. Elena Marsh's daughter did not get to have delicate hands. Not anymore.

"You're quiet," Dominic said, falling into step beside me. He said it the way he checked his fuel gauges — routinely, without drama, but you knew he'd notice if the needle moved.

"I'm calculating."

"Dangerous."

"My mother used to say that about me. That I was always calculating instead of feeling." I watched my boots break the crust. "She wasn't wrong. But she also left me a letter full of numbers that didn't add up, so maybe we're both calculators."

Dominic made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been wind through his beard. With him it was hard to tell. "For what it's worth," he said, "I think she'd be proud of how you're doing this. The stubborn part, I mean. Not the walking-through-a-blizzard-toward-a-haunted-mine part."

"That's my favorite part."

"I know. It's very you."

* * *

We heard the adit before we saw it.

Not sound, exactly — absence. The creek fork Silas had promised appeared right where the map said it wouldn't, and above it the mountainside changed character: the timber thinned, then quit, and the slope became raw rock furred with old tailings, gray and sterile as ash. And there, cut into the face of it, a black rectangle perhaps four meters high, framed in rotting timber, breathing cold air at us like the mouth of something sleeping.

The Meridian Number Two workings. Sealed for eleven years. Except someone had pried the boards off the left side, recently enough that the splinters were pale.

"She went in," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended.

"Elena?" Silas crouched at the opening, reading the ground the way other people read newspapers. "Three sets of prints going in. None coming out." He looked up at us, and for a moment the shame that usually lived behind his eyes was crowded out by something older and harder. "Nobody walks out of a mine they've found what they're looking for in. They walk out when they're running."

"Cheerful," Dominic said.

"I'm a trapper. Cheerfulness scares off the game."

We went in with headlamps, single file, because the tunnel was barely wide enough for two and because none of us wanted to be the one touching the walls. The air tasted of iron and stone and time. Twenty meters in, the daylight died behind us, and then we were three small lights swimming in a black ocean, and I understood why people wrote ghost stories about mines. The dark down here wasn't empty. It was *full*. Full of everything that had ever been buried in it.

Then my lamp caught fabric, and my heart stopped, restarted, and stopped again.

A tent. A real dome tent, pitched inside the mine mouth itself, in the wide chamber where the tunnel opened into the first gallery. Beside it, a camp stove, its fuel canister nearly empty. A crate of core sample boxes, stacked with an engineer's precision. And pinned to the tent wall with a carabiner, a clipboard.

"Oh God," I whispered. "Oh God, Mom."

"They camped *inside*," Dominic said softly. "Smart. Out of the wind. Hidden from the air." He paused. "Hidden from everyone."

Silas stood watch at the entrance while Dominic and I pulled the camp apart with the care of archaeologists and the urgency of thieves. Core boxes first. I lifted a lid and there they were: cylinders of stone in waxed cardboard sleeves, labeled in my mother's block capitals — *HOLE 14, 22.4 M, SULPHIDES* — and beside each label, assay values written in red ink that did not match the company's published figures. Did not match them by orders of magnitude. The ore body Elena had documented was a fraction of what Meridian's permits claimed, and the grades were wrong, and the depths were wrong, and every falsification was initialed and dated in a hand I'd known since childhood.

"She proved it," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it. "Dominic. She *proved* it. Down to the meter."

"There's more." He was holding a bound report, thick as a Bible, the cover page typed and titled: *FINAL SURVEY REPORT — MARSH GEOSURVEY LTD. — RE: MERIDIAN CONCESSION 44-B.* Underneath, in handwriting: *Complete. Do not trust Vane. Do not fly direct.*

"Your mother," Dominic said, "was not a woman who got caught twice."

I took the report and held it against my chest like a child, which is embarrassing, but the dark was total and nobody could see me, so I allowed myself ten seconds of being somebody's daughter before I was again somebody's witness.

It was the diary that undid me.

It was a plain surveyor's logbook, and her entries ran backward through the weeks — the plateau, the radio dying, the decision to move rather than wait for a pickup that Elena had apparently already decided would never come. She'd figured it out early. Of course she had. She'd looked at the boot prints around their camp, the ones the searchers never found because they weren't looking for erasure, and she'd understood that the wilderness hadn't taken them. Someone had been sent to make them disappear, and Elena Marsh had done the single most dangerous thing possible: she'd taken her team and walked into the one place in the range no searcher would think to look, and she'd kept working.

The last entry was dated nine days ago. I read it aloud, because some things need air:

*"Report complete. Samples catalogued. We are low on food but high on spite, which Burns says is a renewable resource. I have sent a duplicate of the report and selected samples ahead with the weekly mail run from the exploration camp — the pilot owes me a favor and doesn't ask questions. Destination: I'm not writing it here. If this book is found, let them sweat. N., if it's you reading this — and I don't know why it would be, but your mother raised a stubborn calculator — check the return address. I taught you to read labels. Love, Mom."*

I sat down on a core box and laughed until it turned into something else, and Dominic put a hand on my shoulder and pretended to study the tunnel wall, which was the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me.

"Ahead," he said finally. "With the mail run. Nadia — do you know who flies mail into the exploration camp?"

And I did. Of course I did. Everyone in Caribou Bend knew old Tom Beaudry flew that route, and everyone knew Tom Beaudry kept a hangar full of parcels he'd forgotten to deliver, and everyone knew Tom Beaudry owed my mother money from a card game in 1998 that he swore she'd cheated at and she swore she hadn't, though knowing her, she absolutely had.

"It's in Caribou Bend," I said. "It's been sitting in Caribou Bend the whole time. In a hangar. Next to a canoe and somebody's taxidermied lynx."

"We go home," Dominic said, and there was a lightness in his voice I hadn't heard since the hangar burned, "we collect a parcel, and we make a tribunal very unhappy with Meridian Mining."

"Assuming," Silas called from the entrance, his voice carefully flat, "that we are all still alive to—"

He stopped.

I want to record exactly what happened next, because later, at the hearing, lawyers would ask me to describe it and I found I couldn't, not in their language. What happened was this: the daylight at the mouth of the adit changed. A shape crossed it. Not an animal shape — animals move in curves, in hesitation. This shape moved in lines. It stopped. It stayed.

Silas stepped back from the entrance, slow as syrup, and put one finger to his lips.

Rook.

We'd lost him in the timber six days ago, after he'd shot Silas through the shoulder and scattered our food into a creek, and for six days we'd told ourselves the story you tell yourself in the bush: *he gave up, we outran him, the mountain ate our tracks.* The mountain had eaten nothing. He'd simply been patient. He'd read us the way Silas read game, and he'd known that eventually we'd lead him to whatever we'd walked all this way to find.

And now we had. Now we were standing in a hole in the ground with the only proof in the world, and the man who'd been paid to bury it was standing in the daylight at the only door.

Dominic killed his lamp. Then mine. Then Silas's, already dark. The black closed over us like water.

In the silence I could hear everything: my own pulse, the tick of the cooling stove, the far-off drip of meltwater somewhere deeper in the mine. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the crunch of boots on frozen tailings. One set. Unhurried. Circling toward the gap in the boards where the seal had been pried open — the gap we'd come through, the gap that was now, from our side of it, the whole width of the world.

"He knows we're in here," Dominic breathed, so close to my ear it was barely sound. "He watched us go in."

"Then why hasn't he fired?" I whispered back.

Beside us in the dark, Silas answered, and his voice had gone quiet and terrible, the voice of a man meeting an old acquaintance. "Because he wants to see our faces when he takes it. Because men like Rook don't shoot evidence." A pause. The boots crunched closer. "They shoot witnesses."

Outside, the daylight at the mouth of the adit began, slowly, to darken — a shadow settling across it, patient as winter, as the silhouette of a man filled the doorway and blocked out the sun.
