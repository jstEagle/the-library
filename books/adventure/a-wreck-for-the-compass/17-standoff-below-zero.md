## 17. Standoff Below Zero

The dark inside the adit was a different animal than the dark outside.

Outside, the dark of the north was honest — it took everything and gave you back the wind, the cold, your own small heartbeat. But in here, in the mine mouth where Elena Marsh had spent her last weeks alive, the dark was old. It had been sitting in these tunnels for ten years, patient as a debt, and it did not care who came to visit.

Dominic crouched behind an ore cart with its wheels rusted solid, one hand on Nadia's shoulder to keep her still, and listened.

Rook's breathing. That was the thing about hired men like Rook — they were quiet, but nobody was quiet forever. Somewhere ahead, past the survey camp's ghost of tarps and crates, past the core sample boxes stacked like grave goods, there was the soft rasp of a man controlling his breath. And beneath it, fainter, the drip of water finding its way through rock that hadn't been disturbed since the company poured concrete over its sins.

"Delacroix." Rook's voice came out of the blackness, flat and unhurried, the voice of a man reading a menu. "You're down to a pistol with maybe six rounds, a flashlight you've been rationing for two days, and two civilians. I've got a rifle and no reason to miss."

"That's a fair summary," Dominic said. His voice echoed off the tunnel walls and came back sounding braver than he felt. "You forgot the weather. It's coming down sideways out there. Whatever happens in here, neither of us is walking to anywhere tonight."

"So we wait each other out."

"We could also talk."

A dry sound from the darkness — not quite a laugh. "Vane said you'd want to talk. Said you were the sentimental type. The kind of pilot who names his aircraft."

"I named her after my mother. You'd be sentimental too if you'd met her."

Silence. Then, closer than Dominic liked: "Here's how it goes. You put the film and the report on the cart, you walk out slow, and I let the cold do the paperwork. Vane's instructions were specific about the evidence. About you, he said use judgment."

"Generous of him."

"He pays for generosity." A pause, and when Rook spoke again his voice had shifted half a register, and Dominic understood with a cold drop in his stomach that the man had moved. "Your ex-Mountie isn't in here with you. I counted three sets of tracks at the treeline and only two sets coming in. He's outside somewhere, or he's gone, and either way it's just you, the girl, and me."

*He's in the ventilation shaft,* Dominic thought. *Or he's dead in it.* Silas Crowe was sixty-one years old and carrying a wound in his side from the ambush on the timber, and he'd looked at the rusted ladder bolted into the rock fifty yards back and said, in the tone of a man ordering coffee, "I'll go up and over. Give me twenty minutes and keep him talking. Talking's the one thing you're good at, Delacroix."

That had been fifteen minutes ago.

"He's not gone," Nadia said suddenly, and her voice cracked on it, young and furious in the dark. "None of us are gone. My mother is *alive*, you son of a bitch. We read her diary. We know she sent word out. So whatever story Vane told you about misadventure, about accidents, you can save it—"

"Nadia." Gentle. *Easy.*

But Rook made a small sound, almost thoughtful. "She told you that? The Marsh woman?"

"She didn't have to," Dominic said carefully. "We found her camp. Her notes. Her samples."

Another silence, longer this time, and when Rook finally spoke, something had come loose in his voice — not remorse, nothing so useful as remorse, but the flatness of a man setting down a weight he'd been told was lighter than it was. "Vane told me they were witnesses. Willing ones. In protective custody until the permit review cleared, then relocated, new identities, the whole arrangement. He showed me papers." A breath. "I've done things for money I'm not proud of, Delacroix. But I don't dig graves for living people."

"You dug them anyway," Nadia said. "Four people. My mother—"

"They're not dead." Rook said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "They're at the Kettle River camp. The old exploration site on the far lake. Alive. Fed, mostly. Cold, probably. Vane's holding them until midnight tomorrow — permits expire, concession transfers, and then—" He stopped himself.

"And then what?" Dominic asked quietly.

The silence answered him. And in that silence, Dominic felt something move through him that wasn't fear and wasn't quite anger either — it was colder than both, and steadier. Rage with a flight plan. Because he'd spent two days hating this man in the dark, and the whole time, Elena Marsh had been thirty miles away eating bad food under guard, waiting for a daughter who was right now standing ten feet from her captor with frostbite scars on her hands and murder in her heart.

*Midnight tomorrow.* The permits expired at midnight. Which meant Vane needed the evidence destroyed and the witnesses — no. He couldn't think that through yet. One problem at a time. First problem was currently holding a rifle somewhere in the black ahead of him.

Above them, faint as a mouse in a wall, something shifted in the ventilation shaft.

Rook heard it too. Dominic saw the shape of him go rigid against the grey seam of the tunnel mouth — a big man, moving wrong, too fast—

"Down!" Dominic grabbed Nadia's collar and hauled her flat as the rifle barked once, twice, muzzle flash strobing the tunnel like lightning in a bottle. Rounds sparked off the ore cart. Then a third sound, heavier, wetter — the sound of sixty-one years of guilt landing on a man from eight feet up in the dark.

They went down together in a tangle of limbs and curses and old rope. Silas got a knee across Rook's back, wrenched the rifle away, and pressed the man's face into the frozen mud with a gentleness that was somehow worse than violence.

"Hold still," Silas rasped. Blood sheeted down his own side where the wound had torn open on the ladder rungs. "Hold still, son. I've wanted to say that to somebody for ten years."

* * *

They bound him with the same rope Rook had used on the supply cache at the lake, which Nadia insisted was justice of a kind, and sat him against the tunnel wall where the grey light of the storm outside just barely reached. Dominic kept the pistol. Silas kept the rifle across his knees, though his hands shook now that the adrenaline was draining, and Nadia stood over all of them with a headlamp beam that made everyone look like ghosts at their own funeral.

"Kettle River camp," Dominic said. "How many guards?"

Rook worked his jaw. Spat blood into the mud. "Two. Part-timers. Company men, not like me. They think it's a security detail." He looked up, and in the lamplight his face was older than it had sounded in the dark, tired around the eyes in a way that hadn't been there a week ago. "Marsh's team is in the bunkhouse. They get walks. They get fed. I checked every day myself." He met Nadia's stare and didn't flinch from it, which Dominic gave him credit for. "I told you. Not graves for living people. I made Vane swear it."

"And you believed him," Nadia said. "A man who falsifies permits. Who burned bodies into statistics."

"Yes." Simple. Ugly. True. "Because believing him was cheaper than the alternative."

Silas made a low sound. "That's the whole trade, son. Everybody in it learns the price late."

"The storm breaks tomorrow afternoon," Dominic said, pacing now because sitting still felt like drowning. "Window's maybe six hours before the next front. Permits expire at midnight. If we can reach the far lake, signal my floatplane — Marta's got her fueled and staged at the fishing lodge since Tuesday — we fly them all out and land the evidence on a tribunal's doorstep with hours to spare."

"It's thirty miles," Silas said. "Over the divide. With a wounded old man, a prisoner, four starved geologists, and whatever the sky decides to do."

"Twenty-eight," Dominic said. "I flew it enough times to shave the miles off."

Nadia crouched in front of Rook. Up close, in the flat light, she had her mother's chin and none of her mother's patience. "Why should we take you instead of leaving you zip-tied to a support beam with a granola bar and an apology?"

"Because I know the guard rotation at Kettle River. Because I know which bunkhouse window sticks. And because—" he paused, and something honest finally surfaced through the flat water of his voice, "—because I'd rather walk into that tribunal in cuffs than live another year being the man Vane thinks I am."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, the storm hammered the mountainside like it had opinions about their plan.

"He could be lying," Nadia said.

"He could," Dominic agreed. "But he told us they were alive when lying would've been easier. That buys him a rope, not a bullet." He looked at Silas. "Can you walk twenty-eight miles?"

Silas tried to stand, failed on the first attempt, succeeded on the second, and waved off Nadia's hand with tremendous dignity. "I've walked farther for worse reasons. Usually women. Once a hockey game."

"We rest four hours," Dominic decided. "Then we move. Nadia, take first watch on our guest. Silas, let her look at that side. And somebody eat something, because I refuse to explain to Elena Marsh that we rescued her and then starved on the doorstep."

As the others settled into the small motions of survival — rope adjusted, bandage unwound, the last of the pemmican divided with a fairness that bordered on sacred — Dominic walked to the tunnel mouth and looked out at the white roar of the world.

Somewhere out there, thirty miles of storm away, a woman he'd flown into these mountains two months ago was waiting in a bunkhouse, counting days. She'd written *the numbers don't add* in a letter to a daughter who hated her, and her daughter had crossed a country to prove her right.

Tomorrow he'd put them all in the air. Tomorrow the truth would weigh more than his plane was rated for, and he'd coax it off the ice anyway, because that was the job, that had always been the job.

Behind him, Nadia's voice, low and hard: "One move, Rook. Give me one reason."

And Rook, weary as winter: "Girl, the only move I'm making is toward the light."

Dominic pulled his collar up against the wind and let himself, for exactly four seconds, believe him.
