## 18. Prisoners of the Concession

The camp at Meridian's old exploration site was the kind of place that looked abandoned even when it wasn't — which, I suppose, was the whole point. Three Quonset huts gone to rust, a collapsed drill shed, a radio mast leaning like a drunk against a fence post. Snow had drifted over the machinery until it looked like the bones of some prehistoric animal. And in the middle hut, behind a door Rook's colleagues had locked with a padlock that probably cost more than my mother's annual salary, four people were waiting to find out if anyone was coming for them.

I knew before we opened the door. I heard her voice through the metal — thin, cracked, furious — telling someone that if they were going to hold her hostage they could at least return her pencil.

"Mom," I said, and then couldn't say anything else, because the padlock came off and the door swung open and there she was.

Elena Marsh stood in the middle of a room that smelled of diesel and old canvas, thinner than I remembered, one wrist wrapped in a strip of blanket, hair gray where I'd never seen it gray before. She looked at me the way you look at a math problem that has suddenly come out right. Then she crossed the room in three steps and hit me — actually hit me, a closed-fist thump against my shoulder blade that would have been a hug if either of us had ever learned how.

"You absolute idiot," she said into my collar. "You came up here. In November."

"You sent me a letter with numbers in it," I said. "What did you think I'd do, frame it?"

She laughed, and then she cried, and then she laughed again while crying, which is a sound I hope I never have to hear from anyone else as long as I live, because it undid something in me that had been holding me upright since the mail plane landed. Behind her, three geologists were getting to their feet off cots and bedrolls: Priya Nair, small and sharp-eyed, already doing inventory of our faces like we might be useful data; Tomás Reyes, who had a beard that suggested he'd been held captive long enough to make peace with it; and young Callum Bright, who was twenty-six and had been the junior member of the team and who now looked about fifty.

"Is that Delacroix?" Tomás asked, peering past us at Dominic, who filled the doorway with snowmelt dripping off his parka. "Tell him the landing on that plateau was the worst flying I've ever survived and I've flown with him twice."

"He can hear you," Dominic said.

"Good. It was worse the second time."

Priya pushed past all of us to grip Silas Crowe by both hands. "You're the trapper. The one Elena said — you found the burn pattern." Silas nodded, uncomfortable, a man being thanked for something he still considered a debt. "We owe you our lives," Priya said.

"No you don't," Silas said. "Not yet. Ask me again tomorrow."

* * *

They told us what happened in pieces, over the next hour, while we shared out the food from our packs — freeze-dried stew that tasted like salted cardboard and was received like a state banquet. The survey team had found what Elena's letter hinted at: core samples showing the ore body was a fraction of what Meridian's filings claimed, and beneath it, in the sealed workings, evidence of the older fraud. Bones, actually. Eleven men listed as "relocated" in 2013 who had never been relocated anywhere but down.

"We documented everything," Elena said. She'd spread her materials on a cot: the final report, wrapped in oilcloth; sample bags labeled in her cramped hand; a diary. "Then Vane's men came up the trail two days early. They knew our schedule better than we did. They took the report, took the samples—" she tapped the oilcloth bundle, "—but I'd made copies. Sent one ahead with the resupply flight. They don't know that. Or they didn't, until recently, which is why we're guests instead of corpses."

"Where did you send it?" I asked.

"I'll get to that." My mother has always had a gift for making information feel like a hostage negotiation. "First: how much time do we have?"

Dominic unfolded onto an overturned crate, and the room got smaller. "Permits expire at midnight tonight. That's not rumor — Vane told me himself, thinking he was buying me. After midnight, the concession lapses unless the tribunal renews it, and the tribunal sits in Caribou Bend tomorrow morning. Which means whatever proof exists has to be in that hearing room by nine a.m., or this all resets and they get another ten years to bury it deeper."

"And the weather?" Priya asked.

"Storm window closes in—" Dominic checked his watch, an old thing with a cracked face he refused to replace. "Six hours, maybe less. There's a floatplane on the far lake. My backup. I filed it as a medicine run so nobody flagged it. It's fueled, it's warm-blooded enough to start, and it will carry six people badly or five people well."

"Seven," Callum said quietly. "You're forgetting Rook."

Everyone looked at him. Callum shrugged, embarrassed. "He's tied up in the drill shed. We're not leaving a man to die in a snowstorm, even him. Elena raised us better than that."

"She did raise you better than that," Elena said, and there was something in her face — pride and exhaustion braided together — that made me look away, because watching my mother love other people's children had always been easier than wondering why she'd been so bad at loving her own.

"Rook comes," Silas said flatly. "Bound, gagged, and sitting where I can see him. But he comes."

* * *

The plan assembled itself out of arguments, which is how all good plans are born. The far lake was eleven kilometers east, mostly downhill through timber, doable in four hours if nobody's legs gave out. The storm would arrive around the same time we did — which was terrible for flying and perfect for cover, because Vane's men at the main camp wouldn't expect anyone to move in whiteout conditions.

"They won't expect it because it's insane," Tomás pointed out.

"That's generally why it works," Dominic said.

Elena laid out the last piece. "The copy I sent ahead went to Marcus Idowu. He ran the assay office here ten years ago. He kept his own records — I'm nearly certain of it, because he's the one who tried to blow the whistle back then and got run out for it. He lives in Whitehorse now. If the tribunal needs corroboration beyond our testimony, he's it. And Nadia—" she turned to me, and I braced myself, because I knew that tone, the one she used when handing someone a live wire, "—the letter you decoded. The numbers. You worked it out faster than I expected anyone to."

"It wasn't hard once I stopped assuming you were bad at arithmetic."

"Gentle," said Priya.

"I'm being gentle. I've been practicing for years."

My mother smiled, and for a second the room was just a room full of tired people laughing, and I let myself have that second, hoarded it like fuel. Because underneath it, all of us could hear the clock. Midnight. Six hours. A storm eating the sky from the west.

* * *

We moved out at 2:40 in the afternoon, seven of us plus one prisoner. Rook walked with his wrists lashed in front of him and a rope from his wrists to Silas's fist, and he walked well, which somehow made it worse — steady, uncomplaining, eyes doing quiet arithmetic of his own. Once, passing through a stand of black spruce, he said, without turning his head: "Vane has men on the lake road."

"Noted," Silas said.

"There's a cutline north of the lake. Old seismic line. Trucks can reach the shore."

"Noted," Silas said again, and yanked the rope, and Rook shut up, but I saw Dominic's jaw tighten and knew the note had been taken.

The timber slowed us. Snow came up past our knees in the drifts, and Callum — weakened worst of the four captives, he'd given most of his rations to the others, Elena admitted later, in the tone of a woman confessing a crime — began to fall behind. Tomás carried his pack. Then Priya carried his pack too. Then Dominic simply picked the boy up fireman-style and kept walking, breathing through his nose like a man hauling furniture, and when Callum protested that this was humiliating, Dominic said, "It is. Humiliation builds character. Ask anyone who's flown with me."

By the time the lake showed through the trees — a vast white plain under a sky the color of wet slate — we had forty minutes of margin and no more. The wind had arrived ahead of the storm, combing spindrift off the ice in long smoke-colored banners.

And then we saw the lights.

Two vehicles, far across the lake, parked near the point where the shore narrowed. Headlights burning. Between them, figures moving — setting something up, or waiting, patient as traps.

"The cutline," Silas said grimly. "Told you."

Dominic crouched in the trees, counting. "Three men. Maybe four. They're between us and the plane."

"Then we go around," Elena said. "The shoreline loops south—"

"In this light, on this ice, with him?" I jerked my chin at Rook. "That's two extra hours we don't have."

Nobody answered, because I was right, and everyone hated it. The wind gusted, and somewhere out on the white plain the storm's vanguard arrived — a first soft curtain of snow sliding across the lake like a stagehand pulling a sheet.

Silas Crowe stood up slowly, joints cracking, and looked at the rope in his hand, and then at the lights across the ice, and I watched him arrive at the same idea I was arriving at, and I watched him decide it before I could say a word.

"Well," he said, almost cheerfully, unwinding the rope from his fist. "Somebody ought to go make some noise."

* * *

He explained it fast, in the clipped way of a man who knows that if he pauses he'll lose his nerve. The vehicles were watching the direct route. If one man crossed the open ice toward them — visible, loud, firing a couple of rounds into the air — every gun on that shore would chase him, because one man alone on a frozen lake in a storm is either a lunatic or a decoy, and soldiers shoot at both. While they chased him, the rest of us would swing wide along the southern shore, through the trees, and come at the plane from the blind side.

"And then what happens to you?" Elena demanded.

"Then I know this country better than they do, and I disappear into it. I've been disappearing into things for ten years. I'm very good at it." He handed the rope to Dominic. "Get them home. All of them. Even him." He nodded at Rook, who was watching Silas with an expression I couldn't read — something almost like respect, which frightened me more than the headlights did.

"This isn't your debt," Dominic said. "Whatever you think you're paying—"

"It's exactly my debt." For the first time since I'd met him, Silas Crowe smiled, and it transformed his whole ruined face. "Ten years ago I buried the truth to save my own skin. Tonight I dig it up. That's not redemption. That's just arithmetic." He shouldered his rifle. "And I've always been good at numbers."

He stepped out onto the ice.

For a moment he was just a dark figure on a white plain, walking into the rising wind, and then he fired twice into the air — two cracks that rolled across the lake like the world clearing its throat — and the headlights swung, and shouts carried thin across the ice, and figures began to move.

"Go," Dominic said, his voice breaking on it. "Go, go, *go*."

We ran south along the treeline, seven of us stumbling through the drifts with the storm coming down and the shots cracking behind us — one, two, then silence, then more, farther away, drawing the hunters off into the white. I didn't look back. Looking back costs time, and time was the only currency any of us had left, and we were spending it all.

The plane appeared out of the snow like a miracle with dents in it — Dominic's backup Beaver, tied down at the southern shore, wings already furred with rime. Dominic tore the covers off, cleared the control surfaces with his bare hands, swearing gently and continuously in French, and got the engine coughing, then roaring, just as the storm arrived in earnest and the world turned to milk.

We loaded fast — the weak ones inside, the evidence wrapped in oilcloth and strapped down like a passenger, Rook bound to the cargo netting. Doors slammed. The floats rocked on black water between shelves of ice.

Dominic turned around in his seat, and through the frosted windshield I saw him look back at the empty shoreline, at the storm swallowing the lake, at the place where a man with a rifle had walked out onto the ice to buy us thirty minutes with his life.

"He'll make it," I said, though I had no way of knowing it.

"He'd better," Dominic said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the Beaver began to move across the darkening water toward the wind.
