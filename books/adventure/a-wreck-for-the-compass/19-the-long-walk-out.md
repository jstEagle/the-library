## 19. The Long Walk Out

The storm had spent itself in the night, and what it left behind was a world remade in white — soft, silent, and treacherous as a liar's handshake.

Dominic stood at the tree line above the derelict exploration camp and counted heads the way he counted everything: twice, then once more for luck. Elena Marsh, moving on stubbornness and half a ration. Her three surveyors — Okafor limping, the Reyes twins pale but upright. Nadia beside him, her bandaged hands tucked into her armpits like a woman holding a secret she refused to spill. And Silas Crowe, standing slightly apart, reading the snow the way other men read newspapers.

"Six hours of light," Silas said. "Maybe five. Snow's knee-deep in the open, waist-deep where it drifted against the timber. We make the far lake by dark or we don't make it."

"And Rook?" Dominic asked.

Silas didn't answer right away. He crouched, touched two marks in the crust near the camp's collapsed fence line — boot prints, long-strided, unhurried. A man who knew exactly where he was going.

"He slipped his ropes sometime before midnight," Silas said. "Took nothing. Didn't even take his gloves." He straightened, and there was something old and tired in his face, something that had been waiting thirty years to be proven right about the world. "A man who leaves his gloves behind isn't planning to be out long. He's planning to be done."

Nadia made a small sound — not quite a word. Dominic put his shoulder against hers, just enough pressure to say *I'm here* without saying anything at all.

"Then we don't give him a trail to follow," Dominic said. "We walk in the creek bed until it freezes up, we cut through the burn scar where the ground's bare, and we come at the lake from the north instead of the east."

"That adds an hour," Okafor said.

"It subtracts a bullet," Dominic said. "I know which one I'd rather spend."

Elena laughed — a dry, cracked sound, but a laugh. "Your mother raised you right, Delacroix."

"My mother raised me to argue with people who were wrong. Same thing, most days."

They moved out.

* * *

The first hour was almost kind. The creek bed gave them hard ice underfoot and walls of willow on either side, and for a while the march felt almost like a picnic gone strange — six exhausted people shuffling north through a white corridor while the sun climbed thin and yellow over the range. The Reyes twins played a whispered game of naming birds they saw, which was mostly a game of inventing birds, since nothing with sense stayed this far north after the first real cold.

"There," Marta Reyes said, pointing. "Gray jay."

"That's a spruce grouse."

"That's *clearly* a gray jay."

"I'll bet you my socks."

"You're not wearing socks."

"Then I can't lose, can I?"

Dominic let the sound of it wash over him. This was the trick of the north that no map ever showed you: terror and laughter lived in the same house, shared the same stove, and some mornings you couldn't tell which one had gotten up first.

Nadia fell into step beside him. Her hands were wrapped in strips of blanket now, because mittens wouldn't fit over the frostbite, and she'd learned to do everything — eat, zip, grip a ski pole — with the awkward patience of someone relearning her own body.

"How are they?" he asked quietly.

"Liar's answer: fine." She glanced at him sideways. "Honest answer: they hurt like hell and I'm terrified I've done permanent damage, and if I think about it too long I'll sit down in the snow and refuse to get up. So I'm not thinking about it."

"That's the healthiest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"What about you? How's the pilot without his plane?"

Dominic considered the question honestly, which was a novelty. "Like a fish that found out it could walk. Technically possible. Deeply undignified."

She smiled, and for three steps the march was just a walk, and the fear was just weather.

Then Silas, fifty yards ahead, stopped and raised a fist, and the whole column froze the way water freezes — all at once, everywhere.

He pointed back the way they'd come. Far off, at the mouth of the creek, something small and dark moved between the willows. Then again, closer to their own track. Not following their prints. Cutting the angle.

"He's not tracking us," Silas breathed. "He's herding. He knows where we have to go, same as we do."

"The lake," Nadia said.

"Everything comes down to the lake," Dominic said. "The plane, Vane's trucks, the permits at midnight. It's the only door out of this whole country, and every man in these hills is walking toward it."

* * *

They cut cross-country through the burn scar after that, moving fast over bare black ground where no tracks would show, and the gray sky pressed down close enough to taste. Twice Silas ranged ahead and came back shaking his head. The third time he came back different — slower, and with a look on his face that Dominic had learned, over the past weeks, to dread more than gunfire.

"Smoke," Silas said. "Woodsmoke. From the direction of the lake."

"Vane's men?"

"Vane's men don't build fires. They run generators." He worked his jaw. "That's a cook fire. Somebody's settled in comfortable, like they've got all the time in the world. Which they do. It's us that doesn't."

Dominic did the arithmetic and hated every number. Five hours of light. Three hours to the lake at their best pace, which nobody present had. Between them and the water: whatever waited at the shoreline, plus Rook somewhere in the timber behind them, patient as winter itself.

And the permits expired at midnight. After midnight, Meridian's lawyers would file paper so thick it could stop a bullet, and everything Elena had carried out of that mine — the report, the core samples, the photographs, the diary — would become a curiosity instead of a verdict.

"We can't outrun them," Elena said flatly. She'd been listening, of course. She always listened. "And we can't go around. So what's left?"

Nobody answered. The wind moved through the dead snags of the burn scar with a sound like distant applause, mocking them.

It was Silas who broke the silence.

"There's a cutline," he said. "Old seismic road, forty years grown in, runs west from here along the ridge and then hooks down to the lake from the south shore. Wide enough for a truck. If Vane's got men on the north shore watching the obvious approaches—" he tapped the map in his head, the one he'd been carrying since before any of them met him, "—then the south is thin. Maybe one man. Maybe none."

"So we go south," Nadia said.

"You go south." Silas looked at each of them in turn, and Dominic felt the shape of the sentence coming before it arrived, the way you feel a storm front in your bad knee. "All of you. But somebody has to make sure the north shore stays interested in the north shore. Somebody loud. Somebody slow enough to catch."

"No," Nadia said immediately.

"Somebody," Silas went on, as if she hadn't spoken, "who knows this country better than any man Vane can hire. Somebody whose legs still work even if his reputation never did." He pulled the rifle off his shoulder and checked it with hands that didn't shake, and Dominic understood suddenly that the steadiness wasn't calm. It was decision. It had been decided for days, maybe weeks, and today it was simply being announced. "I took money once to make evidence disappear. People died because I kept quiet, and I told myself for ten years that the debt was paid because I suffered. Suffering isn't payment. It's just interest." He slung the rifle. "This is payment."

"Silas." Dominic stepped into his path. "You crawl into one trapline cabin and come out alive, and now you're immortal? You're sixty years old and you're proposing to play decoy against a professional killer in broken country."

"I'm proposing to make noise in the woods," Silas said. "Which I've been doing successfully since before you were born. And I'm proposing it because if I don't, we all stand here debating until dark, and dark is the one thing we cannot afford." His voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "Dominic. You built a family out of wreckage this month. I know you did it on purpose, even if you won't say it. Let me do one thing worth being in it for."

"That's not how families work," Nadia said fiercely. Her ruined hands were fists at her sides. "You don't buy your ticket with blood. You just show up. That's the whole job. Showing up."

"Then I'll show up," Silas said. "At the lake. Tomorrow. Or the day after. I've survived worse plans than this one — I married one, for eleven years."

The laugh that went through the group was ragged and unwilling, and it broke something loose. Elena touched his arm. Okafor nodded slowly, the nod of a man who had run surveys in worse places than this and knew when the numbers only worked one way.

Dominic looked at the old man for a long moment. Everything in him wanted to argue, and everything in him knew the argument was already lost, had been lost the day Silas Crowe handed them his confession like a man handing over a loaded gun.

"South shore," Dominic said finally. "You draw them west along the ridge, make them chase you until full dark, then you lose them in the timber and come around. You hit the lake by midmorning. If you're not there by noon—"

"If I'm not there by noon," Silas said, "you fly out anyway, and you tell the tribunal every word I ever confessed to you, and you spell my name right." He grinned, sudden and crooked, the first true grin Dominic had ever seen on him. "Crowe. C-R-O-W-E. There's an E on the end that everybody forgets."

"Not me," Nadia said. Her voice cracked clean through the middle. "Not on the end and not anywhere else."

He hugged her then, careful of her hands, and she pressed her face into his coat, and for a moment the whole frozen world held still around the two of them.

Then he turned and walked west into the trees, and within twenty steps the snow had taken him the way the north takes everything — completely, silently, as if he'd never been there at all.

* * *

They heard him start his performance an hour later, and it was, Dominic had to admit, magnificent.

A shot — high, wild, deliberately terrible. Then another. Then Silas's voice, carrying across the ridgeline with the practiced bellow of a man who'd spent decades shouting at dogs and deputies: *"I SEE YOU! COME AND GET ME, YOU COMPANY BOYS — I'VE GOT YOUR PAPERS RIGHT HERE!"*

From the north shore of the distant lake, faint but unmistakable, came answering shouts. An engine turned over. Another.

"They bit," Okafor whispered.

"Hook, line, and snowmobile," Dominic breathed.

They pushed south, fast now, the decoy's noise drawing every eye and engine away from them, and Dominic ran on legs that burned and lungs that ached and a heart doing two things at once — hammering with hope and breaking a little with every shot that echoed off the ridge, each one a reminder that somewhere behind them an old man was spending himself like the last of a fuel load, buying altitude they all needed, with no guarantee of landing.

Twice more the rifle spoke. Once, much closer to the ridge crest, a burst of automatic fire answered it, and Nadia stumbled and nearly went down, and Marta Reyes caught her arm and held her up, and nobody said anything, because saying things would have made them true.

The light went gold, then copper, then gray. They came down through the last timber to the south shore in the blue hour, breath smoking, legs gone, and there it was — the lake, vast and black under its skin of new ice, and at the far northern end, tiny with distance, the lights of Vane's camp. And moored at a rickety dock two hundred yards south, riding low and patient on the dark water—

His floatplane. His beautiful, battered, borrowed-time Beaver, exactly where he'd cached it, untouched.

"Oh," Nadia said, and it wasn't a word, it was a prayer.

They were fifty feet from the dock when Dominic stopped, because Silas's voice was still going in his head — *count everything twice* — and the second count had come up wrong.

The dock was empty. But the snow on the shore path, the snow that should have shown only their own tracks, showed others. Fresh ones. One set, long-strided, unhurried, leading not toward the plane but away from it — toward the tree line, toward them, circling wide.

Rook hadn't chased Silas. Rook had never intended to chase anyone. While the whole north shore emptied itself westward after an old man's noise, Rook had walked around the lake, quiet as falling snow, and taken up the one position that mattered.

Dominic put out an arm and stopped the whole column.

"Everyone in the trees," he said softly. "Now."

Out on the black water, the ice groaned and shifted, settling into winter. Somewhere behind them, faint and far, a rifle fired one last time — and then, terribly, didn't fire again.

And in the tree line ahead, deliberate as a signature, a match flared: one small flame, cupped in a gloved hand, lighting a face they all knew.

Rook let them see him. Then he smiled, and blew the match out.
