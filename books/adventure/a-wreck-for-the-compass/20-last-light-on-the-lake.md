## 20. Last Light on the Lake

The lake had a name on the old survey maps — Lac du Corbeau, some long-dead cartographer's joke — but everyone who flew this country called it what it was: a landing strip God built and then forgot to maintain.

Dominic stood at the shoreline with his hands jammed in his armpits and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since dawn, the kind of arithmetic that didn't care about your feelings. The Beaver was moored at the far end of the ice, two miles across open wind-scoured surface. The sun was already leaning hard toward the western peaks, which meant he had maybe forty minutes of usable light before the shadows in the valley went from blue to black to nothing at all. And somewhere behind them — back up the trail, back through the timber — Rook was loose, and Vane's men were coming down the old haul road that ran along the north shore like a scar.

"Tell me we're not walking," Nadia said beside him. Her hands were wrapped in Silas's spare mittens, two sizes too big, making her look like a child playing dress-up in her dead grandfather's clothes. She'd refused to take them off since the cabin, frostbite or no frostbite. Pride, probably. Or maybe she'd just learned, out here, that you don't give back warmth once someone's handed it to you.

"We're flying," Dominic said. "Walking's the backup plan, and the backup plan is terrible."

"How terrible?"

"Terrible enough that I'm going to pretend I didn't think about it."

Elena Marsh came up behind them, moving slow, one of her surveyors — pale, hollow-eyed Prewitt — leaning on her shoulder. Four days in a derelict exploration camp on half-rations had carved years off all of them. Elena looked like a woman who'd been through a wringer and come out the other side folded differently, but her eyes were the same eyes that had asked him pointed questions about old mine shafts from the right-hand seat two months ago. Sharp. Unfinished with the world.

"The plane," she said. "How overloaded?"

Dominic winced. Trust a geologist to go straight for the ugly number.

"Beaver's rated for nine hundred pounds of useful load with full fuel," he said. "I've got six of you, me, survival gear, and roughly forty pounds of film, core samples, and paperwork that's worth more per ounce than gold right now. So call it eleven hundred, maybe twelve."

"And?"

"And," he said, "the ice is smooth, the wind's calm, and I've taken off out of worse with less reason to live." He grinned at her, because somebody had to, and because grinning was free. "We'll fly. It'll be ugly. Ugly and airborne beats pretty and parked."

Behind them, Silas Crowe came down the trail at his own pace, rifle slung, scanning the treeline with the unhurried attention of a man who'd spent thirty years learning how much country a pair of eyes could actually cover. He'd barely slept since the decoy run — none of them had — and there was something new in his face, something Dominic couldn't quite name. Not peace. Closer to a man who'd finally set down a bag he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it had a strap.

"Truck lights on the haul road," Silas said without preamble. "Two sets. Maybe three miles back, moving fast for that road. Twenty minutes if they push it."

The temperature in Dominic's chest dropped ten degrees.

"Then we're done talking," he said.

* * *

Crossing the lake was the longest two miles of Dominic's life, and he'd once spent four hours in a Cessna 150 with a fuel gauge that lied to him the whole way.

They moved as a group across the wind-polished ice, six survivors and three rescuers, boots crunching on snow that squeaked like packing foam in the deepening cold. The light was doing that northern thing it did at the end of a short day — stretching every shadow into something long and accusatory, gilding the ridgelines, turning the world briefly, cruelly beautiful. Dominic kept glancing over his shoulder at the north shore, where the haul road emerged from the trees, and every time he looked, the dark line of the forest seemed closer to producing headlights.

Nadia walked beside him, carrying the evidence satchel strapped across her chest like a bandolier. Inside it: Elena's final report, the core sample logs, the photographs from the informant's cache, the diary, the map. Everything Meridian Mining had killed for, wrapped in a canvas mail bag that had seen more honest service carrying catalogues to trapline cabins.

"You know what my mother said to me," Nadia said quietly, "when we got them out of that camp? First thing, before anything else?"

"What?"

"She looked at me like she wasn't sure I was real, and she said, 'You got tall.'" Nadia huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Ten years of estrangement. A letter that took me apart. And the first thing out of her mouth is *you got tall*."

"That sounds about right for mothers," Dominic said. "Mine used to greet me off the mail plane by asking if I'd eaten. Like I might have forgotten food existed between stops."

"She's been trying to apologize ever since. In her way. Which means she keeps telling me facts about ore density."

"Love language of geologists."

That got an actual laugh out of her, short and surprised, and Dominic felt absurdly proud of it, like he'd coaxed a rough engine into turning over on a cold morning.

Then the north shore lit up.

Headlights. Two vehicles, bouncing down the haul road, throwing wild beams through the timber. Someone in the group made a sound — Prewitt, maybe, or one of the other surveyors — and the whole column's pace broke from walking into something uglier.

"Don't run!" Dominic barked. "Running on lake ice is how you find out about pressure cracks the hard way. Fast walk. Everybody. Go!"

Silas was already peeling off toward the treeline with the rifle, and Dominic grabbed his sleeve. "Crowe—"

"I'm not doing the hero bit," Silas said. "I'm doing the *cover the crossing* bit. There's a difference. The hero bit killed better men than me." He looked at Dominic, and for a second the old shame and the new resolve were both visible, layered like ice over moving water. "Get them off my lake, Delacroix. I'll be along."

He melted into the trees, and Dominic turned and ran — fast-walked, goddammit, *fast-walked* — for the Beaver.

* * *

The plane sat where he'd left her, tied down at the south end of the lake, frost furring her struts, looking exactly like what she was: a 1952 de Havilland Beaver that had been loved hard and maintained harder and was now being asked to do something her designer never imagined.

Dominic worked the tie-downs with fingers gone clumsy inside his mitts, talking to her under his breath the way he always did, the way his father had taught him without ever admitting that's what he was doing. "Easy, girl. I know. I know it's a lot. We're all carrying too much these days."

Loading was chaos conducted at speed. Surveyors into the bench seats, Prewitt wedged in front, Elena up front beside Dominic because she'd earned the right seat and because if they went in, he wanted the woman who found the truth to see it coming. Nadia shoved the satchel into the baggage area and then climbed in after the others, and the Beaver settled on her gear — actually *settled*, floats' suspension compressing, tail sitting heavy — in a way that made Dominic's stomach tighten.

"Everyone buckled. Everyone breathing?" He counted heads twice, because stress math was lying math. Nine souls. Eleven hundred pounds over gross, give or take. "Good. Here's the part where nobody asks questions."

He climbed in, slammed the door, and the cabin went suddenly quiet — that particular airplane quiet, nine people holding nine breaths while a man who belonged to the sky prepared to ask it for one more favor.

Engine start. The old R-985 coughed, caught, coughed again — cold-soaked, furious about it — and then roared to life with a sound like the whole valley clearing its throat. Oil pressure climbing. Temps coming up. Dominic ran the checks faster than he liked and slower than he needed, splitting the difference between hurry and haste, because haste was how bush pilots became statistics.

He taxied to the south end of the lake and stopped, and looked down the two-mile runway of glass and wind-packed snow ahead of him, and did the last of the arithmetic.

Full throttle. Flaps one notch. Into whatever wind there was, which was nearly nothing, which was bad — he wanted a headwind, wanted the airspeed over the wings before the groundspeed under the tires got stupid. On this load, on this ice, he'd eat up most of the lake getting airborne, and the far shore was going to arrive like an opinion he hadn't asked for.

"Everybody," he said over the intercom, "this is your captain speaking. In about ninety seconds you're going to be absolutely certain we're going to hit those trees. We are not. Probably. Buckle tight."

He pushed the throttle to the stop.

* * *

The Beaver gathered herself the way old women rise from deep chairs — slowly, with dignity, with visible effort. Snow spooled off the skis. The airframe began to shudder and sing. Forty knots. Fifty. The ice below strobing past, and the far shore growing from a gray suggestion into a very committed wall of spruce.

Sixty knots. The tail came up. The horizon tilted wrong — nose-heavy, this load, everything nose-heavy — and Dominic hauled the yoke into his lap with both arms and felt the airplane say *no* in every rivet.

Seventy knots. The trees were close enough now that he could pick individual ones, could see the snow on their shoulders, and behind him somebody — Prewitt, he thought — was saying a wordless prayer in the tone of a man renegotiating with God.

"Come *on*," Dominic told her, teeth gritted, arms shaking. "You've never quit on me. Don't you dare start with witnesses."

And then — the small miracle, the ordinary miracle, the one he'd been granted ten thousand times and never once gotten used to — the ice let go. The wheels stopped being wheels. The shudder smoothed into air, and the Beaver climbed, shallow and groaning and magnificent, dragging herself skyward with all the grace of a loaded mule and all the stubbornness of a machine whose pilot had never once given her permission to fail.

They cleared the treeline by what Nadia would later insist was four feet and Dominic would insist, with a straight face, was "at least six."

The cabin erupted. Whooping, sobbing, Prewitt laughing the unhinged laugh of a man recently certain he was going to die. Elena put her hand on Dominic's shoulder and squeezed once and said nothing, which from Elena Marsh was a standing ovation.

Dominic leveled off, nursed the nose down to build speed, and let himself breathe for exactly three seconds.

Then he looked at the lake receding behind them, and stopped breathing again.

* * *

The trucks had reached the south shore. They were racing along it now, parallel to the lake, kicking up rooster tails of snow — and one of them had swung out onto the ice itself.

"He's on the lake," Nadia said over the intercom, her voice flat with disbelief. "He's driving on the *lake*."

"Vane," Elena said. "It'll be Vane. He doesn't delegate the things that matter."

The truck was a quarter mile behind and closing, its headlights strobing across the ice, and Dominic understood with cold clarity what the man was doing: not trying to catch them — nobody catches an airplane in a truck — but trying to be *seen*. Trying to make the last image of this day a warning. Or worse, checking the wind, checking the light, marking them for whatever came next.

Except.

Except the truck was hitting drift ridges now, the wind-carved sastrugi that laced the middle of the lake, and each one hammered the vehicle sideways. Dominic watched it fishtail, recover, fishtail again, the driver pushing too hard because Aldous Vane was in the passenger seat and Aldous Vane did not accept weather, physics, or refusal.

"He's going to roll it," Nadia said.

"He's going to roll it," Dominic agreed.

For one strange, suspended moment, the whole cabin watched the truck below them — the man who had burned Dominic's hangar, disappeared a survey team, bought a Mountie's silence a decade ago, and put a killer named Rook in the woods — spinning and sliding across the ice like a toy, undone at last by the only authority out here that had never taken his money.

The truck hit a drift ridge broadside, went up on two wheels, hung there for a heartbeat like a coin deciding, and came down sliding in a great white detonation of snow. It slewed to a stop, steaming, canted, alive but finished.

Nobody cheered. That was the strange part. Nine people in a rattling, overloaded airplane, watching the man who'd hunted them spin out beneath them, and the feeling that came through the intercom wasn't triumph. It was something quieter. Something like watching a storm finally spend itself.

"Vane gets a tribunal instead of a glacier," Elena said at last. "Some men would call that mercy."

"Some men would call it the law working," Dominic said. "Which is rarer, and worth more."

He banked south, gentle, easy, the Beaver finally answering like herself now that the air was under her, and the range fell away behind them — the plateau, the sealed adit, the lake, the whole white country that had tried to kill them and failed and would try again tomorrow, indifferent as gravity.

Ahead, the sky over the southern passes was burning down to embers. Last light. He had maybe twenty minutes of it, and Caribou Bend was fifty minutes away, and the forecast was calling for the front to move in overnight — the front that would seal the passes, bury the trails, and close the window on the permits at midnight tomorrow.

The evidence rode in the baggage compartment behind him. The survivors rode in the seats. Somewhere behind, in the darkening timber, Silas Crowe was either following their track or writing his own, and Dominic's chest ached around that fact like a splinter too deep to dig.

"Hangar's burned," Nadia said quietly beside him. "Logbooks gone. Your livelihood's ash. You know that, right? Whatever happens at the tribunal — you come home to nothing."

Dominic looked out at the last light lying gold along the wing, at the country unrolling beneath him, at the compass needle steady in its bowl pointing the way it always pointed, whether anyone was watching or not.

"Nadia," he said, "I've been coming home to nothing my whole life. Turns out it was just waiting for the right passengers."

She laughed — wet-eyed, exhausted, real — and beside her Elena Marsh opened the flight log she'd salvaged from her coat and began, in a geologist's precise hand, to write down everything, so that when they landed there would be no gap between the truth and the telling.

Ahead, the first star came out over Caribou Bend, and below it, impossibly far and impossibly near, the town lights flickered on one by one — the town that lived on Meridian's money, that was about to learn what its money was made of.

Dominic eased the throttle forward and flew into the dark, carrying everybody.
