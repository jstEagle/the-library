## 21. Testimony

The tribunal convened in the community hall of Caribou Bend, because the territorial capital was nine hundred kilometers south and nobody with any sense wanted to fly there in December. This meant that justice, for one week, shared a building with the curling club, and the exhibit tables — core samples in labeled tubes, photographs of a sealed adit, Elena's final report in its waterproof sleeve — were set up each morning on folding tables that still smelled faintly of chili from the fall fundraiser.

Nadia thought it was fitting, actually. Her mother had spent her whole career insisting that geology belonged to everyone, that a map was a promise made in public. It seemed right that the reckoning should happen under a banner reading *BONSPIEL 50th ANNIVERSARY*, slightly crooked.

She had not slept properly in eleven days. She suspected she would not sleep properly for another eleven. But her hands were healing — Dominic had been relentless about the ointment, chasing her around the hangar with it like a man administering medicine to an uncooperative mule — and when she stood at the witness table on the first morning and swore to tell the truth, her voice did not shake.

Mostly.

* * *

The tribunal's chair was a woman named Justice Adeyemi, who had flown up from Yellowknife with two clerks and an expression suggesting she had read every page of the evidence twice and found all of it worse on the second read. She ran the hearings with a patience that Nadia came to understand was a weapon. When Meridian's lawyers tried to object that the film — Silas's informant's photographs, plus Elena's own documentation — had been obtained improperly, Adeyemi let them talk for four full minutes and then said, mildly:

"Counsel, the permits expire Friday at midnight. I suggest we spend our remaining time on facts rather than adjectives."

The hall laughed. It was not a big laugh, but it was unanimous, and Nadia felt something in her chest loosen by a degree. The town had come out in force. Half of Caribou Bend worked for Meridian directly or indirectly, which meant half the town had spent three weeks pretending not to have opinions, and now here they all were, filling the curling-club bleachers, wearing their opinions openly for the first time.

Dominic sat beside her at the applicants' table, in the one good shirt he owned, which Nadia had learned was reserved for funerals and aircraft inspections. He kept fidgeting with his collar like it was a harness strap he couldn't quite cinch.

"You're going to be fine," she murmured.

"I've landed on ice in a crosswind," he whispered back. "This is different."

"How?"

"There's no checklist for this."

There was, as it turned out. It just took five days.

* * *

Elena testified first.

She had lost weight in the company camp — they all had; the survivors looked like pencil sketches of themselves — but she walked to the witness table on her own feet, declined the offered chair until the second hour, and answered every question in the flat, precise voice Nadia had grown up hearing through the wall of a dozen rented houses, explaining isotope ratios over dinner while Nadia sulked about wanting a normal mother.

Nadia had wanted a normal mother for twenty years. Watching her now, she understood she'd never had one and never would, and that this was possibly the best luck of her life.

Elena described the survey. The odd readings. The old shafts that appeared on no current map but showed up in her mother's — Nadia's grandmother's — field notes from 1971. The night her junior geologist, Okafor, pulled core samples from the archive shed at the exploration camp and found the assay numbers had been transposed. Not misread. Transposed. Deliberately, repeatedly, across a decade of filings.

"And what did you conclude?" asked the tribunal counsel.

"That the concession's ore estimates were fabricated," Elena said. "And that someone had gone to considerable trouble to make sure no one ever compared them against the ground truth." A pause. "We were comparing them against the ground truth."

"What happened then?"

"Then our radio died, our truck's fuel line was cut, and four people arrived at our camp who said they were from Meridian safety services and asked us to come with them." Elena's voice stayed level. "We went, because the alternative was the plateau in November without a radio. In hindsight I'm told we should have fought. I maintain we made the correct call. Dead geologists file no reports."

Justice Adeyemi leaned forward. "Dr. Marsh. You were held for how long?"

"Nineteen days. At the derelict exploration camp on Kettle Lake. We were fed. We were not harmed. We were told that if we cooperated, we'd be released after the permit renewal cleared." Elena finally took the chair. "I want the record to reflect that the food was terrible and the coffee was an abomination, and that Mr. Rook — their man — apologized for both, sincerely, several times, which somehow made it worse."

Someone in the bleachers snorted. Even Adeyemi's mouth twitched.

* * *

The photographs went in on the third day.

Silas Crowe testified sitting down, because his leg was still held together with more hardware than a floatplane. He wore a clean flannel shirt and his hair had been cut by someone — Nadia suspected Dominic, judging by the slightly alarmed topography of it — and he spoke the way Nadia imagined he must have once spoken as a Mountie: careful, complete, and with the weary authority of a man who has already cross-examined himself for ten years and lost.

He told them about the case. Ten years ago. Three men dead in a collapse at the old underground workings, written up as two, written up as an accident, written up and buried — and Silas, young and broke and flattered that someone important finally noticed him, had taken the envelope and closed the file.

"I told myself it was paperwork," he said. "That the truth was somewhere else, in some other office, and my signature didn't matter. That's the lie you tell yourself. The envelope wasn't a bribe. It was permission. Permission to stop looking." He looked at the bleachers, at the miners and pilots and storekeepers of Caribou Bend. "I stopped looking for ten years. I'd like the record to show what that costs. Not me. Them." He nodded toward Elena's team. "And the families of the men in the photographs, who got a death certificate that named the wrong number and the wrong cause, and no grave worth visiting."

The photographs themselves were almost unbearable in their ordinariness: a portal frame, a collapsed stope, a clipboard with names. Taken by Silas's informant — a Meridian warehouse man named Gus Therrien, who had hidden the film in a trapline cabin and waited a decade for someone to come asking, and died eight months before anyone did.

"Gus kept faith longer than I did," Silas said. "I'd like that noted too."

Adeyemi noted it. Nadia watched the clerk write it down and thought: *there. Somebody wrote it down.* It seemed like such a small thing. It was the whole thing.

* * *

Vane attended every session.

He sat at the respondent's table in a charcoal coat that probably cost more than Dominic's annual fuel budget, taking notes he never consulted, and he listened to the testimony about falsified assays and cut fuel lines and nineteen days of captivity with the expression of a man reviewing a contractor's invoice — professionally interested, personally untroubled.

On the fourth day, the tribunal counsel read into the record the statement Rook had given after his capture — given freely, or as freely as a man gives anything while zip-tied in a mine shaft with three exhausted people deciding whether to be judges or executioners. Rook had named Vane. Had named the order, the date, the method. Had named, with a kind of professional disgust, the instruction to make it look like weather.

Vane's lawyer objected that Rook was a confessed felon seeking leniency. Adeyemi observed that Rook was also the only person who had been present for the relevant conversations, and that the tribunal would weigh his statement accordingly, alongside — she tapped the folder — the flight logs reconstructed from memory, the permit filings, the assay comparisons, the photographs, and the sworn statements of four surviving geologists.

"The weight of paper," Adeyemi said, "is considerable, even in this territory."

Vane did not flinch. But Nadia, watching him from three meters away, saw something else: the small recalculation behind his eyes, the arithmetic of a man discovering that the numbers, for once, did not add up in his favor.

It was, she reflected, the only thing she and Aldous Vane had ever had in common. They had both spent weeks doing math. Hers had saved lives. His had cost them.

At 4:40 on Thursday afternoon, as the session recessed, two officers from the territorial police — who had been sitting quietly in the back row since Tuesday, in jackets that fooled absolutely no one in a town where everyone knew everyone's truck — crossed the hall and spoke to Vane briefly.

He stood. He straightened his coat. He looked, for one moment, directly at Nadia, and whatever he intended to communicate — contempt, warning, apology, nothing — she found she couldn't read it, and decided she didn't need to.

They walked him out past the curling trophies. Nobody clapped. That wasn't the town's way. But nobody looked away either, and the silence he walked through was heavier than any jeering could have been.

* * *

Friday. The last day. Midnight deadline.

Adeyemi delivered the decision at six in the evening, standing, in a hall so quiet Nadia could hear the woodstove ticking.

She revoked the concession. All of it. Citing fraudulent filings, suppressed casualty records, obstruction, and the unlawful detention of a scientific survey team — "conduct," she said, "that disgraces not only the permit holder but the very idea that a piece of paper signed in bad faith deserves the protection of law." The reclamation bond would be released for remediation of the old workings, including proper memorialization of the men whose deaths had been filed away. Criminal referrals would go forward. And the tribunal's findings would be transmitted to every regulatory body with jurisdiction over Meridian's other holdings, "so that the burden of looking does not fall, next time, on a bush pilot and a trapper and a surveyor's daughter."

Nadia heard the words. She understood them. What she remembered afterward, though, was smaller: the sound the hall made. Not cheering. Something better — a long collective exhale, like a town setting down a weight it had carried so long it had forgotten it was heavy. Old Pete Andersson, who had run the fuel cache for thirty years and had spent the week looking like a man watching his house burn slowly, stood up, straightened his suspenders, and said loudly, "Well. I suppose somebody ought to put the coffee on," and that was the moment Nadia started crying, quietly, into her sleeve, while Dominic pretended very hard to study the ceiling.

"It's the ointment fumes," he said to no one.

"Shut up, Dominic."

"Yes ma'am."

* * *

They walked home along the frozen river road, the three of them, under a sky doing its northern trick of being enormous and close at once. Elena walked between them, moving slowly, her breath hanging in the cold.

"So," Elena said, after a kilometer of comfortable silence. "Your letter said 'numbers that don't add.' My letter. The one I mailed before—" She stopped. Started again. "I've been meaning to ask. Why did you come? We hadn't spoken in two years. I sent that letter half hoping you'd throw it out."

Nadia considered a dozen answers. Most of them were true. None of them were the true one.

"Because you're impossible," she said finally. "Because you taught me that if the data contradicts the story, you throw out the story. Because I was furious at you, and fury is just grief that hasn't found its job yet." She shrugged. "And because somebody had to tell you your coffee order was pretentious, and apparently I flew nine hundred kilometers to do it."

Elena laughed — a real laugh, rusty, startled out of her — and then stopped walking, and then, awkwardly, fiercely, hugged her daughter in the middle of the river road while the temperature dropped toward minus thirty and neither of them cared.

"We're going to be bad at this," Elena said into her shoulder.

"Terrible," Nadia agreed. "Years of practice being strangers. We'll have to unlearn it slowly."

"Spring," Elena said. "Come to the plateau with me. There's a memorial to build. Four names that were never carved anywhere. Five, if we count Gus."

"Six," Dominic said quietly, ahead of them, not turning around. "When we find him."

Silas said nothing at all. He was looking north, up the river, toward the range, toward a trapline cabin and a decoy run through deep snow and a debt he believed he was still paying. Nadia watched him watch the horizon and thought: *not yet. But soon.*

Behind them, the community hall lights went off section by section. The curling club would have the building back tomorrow. The chili smell would win in the end, as chili always does.

But somewhere in a clerk's filing cabinet, in ink that would outlast everyone in that room, it was written down: the wrong number corrected, the right names recorded, the paper signed in bad faith torn up at last.

Nadia tucked her frost-scarred hands into her pockets, matched her mother's slow pace, and let the town carry them home.

Tomorrow there would be hearings and appeals and the long unglamorous work of making it stick. Tonight there was coffee — terrible coffee, someone would apologize for it, sincerely, which would somehow make it better.

And far to the north, under the first real snow, a wreck waited for the compass. But that, Nadia was learning, was spring's business.
