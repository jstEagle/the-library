## 22. A Wreck for the Compass

The hangar went up in stages, the way most honest things do.

By the first week of May, when the ice on Caribou Bend started groaning and shifting like an old man getting out of a chair, Dominic had the frame standing again. He'd salvaged what the fire hadn't eaten — three good trusses, the workbench with its burn-scarred leg, and the coffee tin of assorted bolts that had somehow survived everything, including, apparently, fire itself. He'd nailed a sign over the new door before there was even a door to hang it on: DELACROIX AIR — EST. TWICE.

Nadia laughed at that for a full minute when she came by with coffee and a box of nails she'd bought in Fort Nelson without being asked. She had a way of showing up with exactly the thing he hadn't said he needed. He was starting to suspect she kept a list.

"You know," she said, handing him a mug, "most people put up a sign after the building exists."

"Most people don't get their hangar torched by a mining company's errand boy."

"Fair." She looked up at the skeleton of rafters against the blue morning sky. "It's going to be bigger than the old one."

"Everything's bigger when you rebuild it," he said. "That's not construction, that's spite."

She smiled, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the river ice boomed somewhere down the bend like distant artillery, and Dominic thought: this is what it sounds like when winter lets go. He'd heard it every spring of his life and never once noticed until he had someone standing next to him to hear it with.

Elena Marsh arrived the following week, thinner than the mountains had left her but walking on her own legs, which was more than anyone had a right to expect after four months as Meridian's prisoner. The tribunal had gone the way the tribunal was always going to go once the photographs surfaced — Vane arrested at the airport trying to leave with a briefcase full of other people's money, the concession revoked at 11:47 p.m., thirteen minutes before the permits would have renewed themselves like a bad dream. There were hearings still pending, lawyers still circling, but the town had already made its verdict at the Legion hall, where somebody bought Elena a beer and nobody asked her to pay for it ever again.

She stood in the half-built hangar with her hands in her pockets, looking at the frame the way she probably looked at core samples.

"You're rebuilding," she said.

"I'm rebuilding."

"After everything."

"Especially after everything." He set down his hammer. "A man who quits flying because somebody burned his airplane is a man who never really flew. He just owned a machine."

Elena nodded slowly. Then she said the thing she'd clearly come to say, the thing she'd been carrying since the rescue, maybe since the plateau:

"The team wants to go back. To the workings. Properly this time — survey equipment, a memorial marker for the men who died in '09, the ones under the tailings. And for the ones from ten years before that nobody ever counted." She met his eyes. "We need a pilot who won't ask too many questions."

"Ma'am," Dominic said, "I'm the wrong pilot for that job. I ask all of them."

"That's why we want you."

* * *

They went north on the twenty-third of May, when the passes were open and the lakes were still solid enough at the edges to trust and not solid enough anywhere else to lie about.

Dominic took the Beaver up with Elena and two of her geologists, Priya and Okafor, and Nadia rode along in the second seat with a camera and a notebook and the particular expression of a woman who had learned the hard way that going along was the only way to know. The weather was the kind pilots write poems about and then deny writing — high broken cloud, ten miles' visibility, air smooth as poured cream. The range rolled out ahead of them white and enormous, and Dominic felt the old familiar loosening in his chest, the one that had been missing all spring while he swung a hammer and pretended he wasn't grieving a trapper who was almost certainly dead.

Because that was the arithmetic he'd never managed to make come out right. Silas Crowe had walked into the trees on the last night out, drawing Rook's men off with a decoy lantern and a rifle and fifty years of bushcraft, and he had not come back. They'd searched for six days in weather that got worse by the hour. They'd found his rifle, empty, propped against a spruce with almost ceremonial neatness. They'd found blood in the snow, not enough of it, and tracks that ended at a creek running fast with meltwater.

The Mounties had called it. Missing, presumed dead. Nadia had cried in a way that scared Dominic more than the gunfire had, and he had held her and said nothing, because there was nothing true to say except *he knew the odds when he stepped out*, and Silas Crowe had spent his whole life knowing the odds.

Now they crossed the plateau and Dominic brought the Beaver down on the frozen lake below it one last careful time, and they hiked up to the adit with packs and tools and a bronze plaque wrapped in a blanket like something newborn.

The mine mouth looked smaller than it had in November. That was the trick of places like that, Dominic thought — terror inflates geography. In daylight, with the snow retreating into grey rags and the willows budding green along the creek, the sealed adit was just a hole in a hillside, boarded over decades ago, its timber frame silvered by wind. Ordinary. Almost gentle.

Priya and Okafor set the marker while Elena read measurements off her clipboard, and Nadia took photographs, and Dominic did what he always did on the ground, which was stand slightly apart and watch the treeline out of habit that might never fully leave him.

The plaque listed eleven names. Four from the collapse of 2009 that the company had reported as "equipment losses." Seven from the earlier workings, men whose deaths existed only in a dead informant's photograph cache and a widow's pension file nobody had ever opened. Elena had tracked down two of the families over the winter. One of them had sent a letter to be buried with the marker, folded into oilcloth. Okafor read it aloud, quietly, and the wind took the words out over the valley, and nobody minded.

It was Nadia who spoke last. She didn't have a speech prepared; you could tell because she started twice and threw both beginnings away.

"My mother wrote me a letter I never answered," she said. "I answered it here instead. It took me a year and a mountain range, but — answer your letters, everybody. That's the whole lesson. That's it."

Elena put her arm through her daughter's, and Dominic looked away to give them the privacy of it, and that was when he saw the smoke.

Thin. Grey. Rising from the direction of the old trapline cabin, two miles east along the creek bottom, the one marked on Silas's maps as Marten Creek. A thread of woodsmoke in cold clean air, unmistakable, impossible.

Nobody lived at Marten Creek. Nobody had lived at Marten Creek since Silas had trapped it alone, and Silas was dead, and dead men did not stoke stoves.

"Elena," Dominic said, very calmly, in the voice he used for engine trouble. "How long would it take you to finish here?"

* * *

He flew the short hop east alone. Some things a man does solo or not at all.

The cabin sat in its clearing exactly as he remembered it — low log walls, sod roof going green, the woodpile Silas had stacked with geometric obsession still half-eaten by winter. Smoke curled from the stove pipe in a lazy ribbon. A pair of snowshoes leaned against the doorframe, and beside them, hung on a peg, a parka that Dominic recognized the way you recognize a voice.

His hands were shaking on the controls of nothing. He tied off the plane at the creek edge and walked up through the mud and the first wild crocuses, and at the door he stopped, because through the window he could see a figure moving inside, slow, favoring one side, and the figure turned, and it was not a ghost. Ghosts didn't have beards going grey at the chin. Ghosts didn't look like they'd lost forty pounds and won a war anyway.

Silas Crowe opened the door before Dominic could knock.

"Took you long enough," Silas said. His voice was gravel that had been rained on. "I been eating ptarmigan since February. You know what ptarmigan tastes like?"

"Like ptarmigan."

"Like punishment. Get in here before I change my mind about being found."

The cabin smelled of woodsmoke and spruce pitch and something healing. Silas lowered himself onto a stool by the stove with the care of a man whose left leg had opinions, and Dominic stood in the doorway a moment too long, taking inventory: the crutch by the bunk, the bandage showing above a mended boot, the rifle — a different rifle — cleaned and oiled and hanging on pegs, and on the table, laid out flat and squared to the edges, a stack of papers. Affidavits. Written testimony, dated and signed, in a hand like fence wire.

"What are those?" Dominic asked, though he knew.

"Everything I remember," Silas said. "Every name, every date, every payment I took and every document I buried. Ten years of it. Been writing since my leg let me hold a pen." He tapped the stack. "Rook's bullet went through the meat of my thigh and out the other side. Missed the bone by a prayer. I crawled here — four days, maybe five, I quit counting — and I decided that if I was going to die in this cabin, I was going to die useful. Then I didn't die. Kept meaning to mention it to somebody, but the radio's shot and my leg wasn't keen on the walk."

"You let us search for six days."

"I let you search for six days because if I'd been found, I'd have been found as a witness, and witnesses get lawyers, and lawyers get delayed, and Vane's people were still in the field." Silas's eyes, pale and steady as winter water, held Dominic's. "I figured the evidence in your hands was worth more than my hide in their reach. Was I wrong?"

Dominic thought about it honestly, the way Silas deserved. "No," he said finally. "But you could've left a note."

"Left a note." Silas snorted. "On what? Write it in the snow? 'Gone crawling, back whenever'? Man my age doesn't leave notes. Man my age leaves affidavits." He pushed the stack across the table. "Take these to the tribunal. Every word's true, which is more than I could say for anything I said for ten years before it."

Dominic picked up the pages. They were heavy in a way paper shouldn't be.

"They'll want you to testify in person," he said. "Read it aloud. On the record."

Something moved behind Silas's face — the old flinch, the shame that had driven him into the bush a decade ago and nearly kept him there forever. Dominic watched him wrestle it, watched him lose, watched him — deliberately, visibly, like a man hauling on a rope — win.

"Yeah," Silas said. "I figured they would." He reached for the crutch. "Get the door, would you? Leg's stiff. And Delacroix—" He paused, halfway upright, and for a second he looked every one of his years and also, somehow, lighter than Dominic had ever seen him. "There's a woman in Caribou Bend who thinks I'm dead. Marsh's girl. Cried over me, I'm told."

"She did."

"Well." Silas cleared his throat and looked ferociously at the stove. "Best not keep her waiting, then. Help me down to the plane. And Dominic — bring the ptarmigan. Waste not."

* * *

They came into Caribou Bend at six in the evening, into light the color of honey, and Dominic made no announcement on the radio because some landings should be witnessed rather than broadcast.

The whole town seemed to be at the dock anyway — it was that hour when the floatplanes come home and the day's gossip gets sorted — and so there were plenty of eyes when Dominic taxied in, plenty of waves, and then a spreading silence as people registered the second figure in the aircraft, hunched and bearded and leaning on a crutch and a younger man's shoulder.

Nadia got there first. She always did.

She ran the length of the dock and stopped short, three feet away, staring, and Silas stared back, and the whole town held its breath around them like a held chord.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said.

"Been informed," Silas said. "Repeatedly. Never took to it."

And Nadia Marsh, who had crossed a mountain range on grief and fury, who had faced down a killer in a mine mouth and a corporation in a hearing room, sat down right there on the wet planks of the dock and laughed and cried at the same time, and Silas Crowe lowered himself beside her, awkward as a foal, and let her take his arm, and did not pull away.

Dominic stood at the plane's strut and watched, and felt something settle in his chest that had been loose since November.

He'd spent his whole life believing the compass pointed north — that a man's true heading was always away, always out, always over the next ridge where nobody knew him and nobody needed him. He'd built a whole self on that bearing. It had kept him aloft and it had kept him alone, and he'd told himself those were the same thing.

But compasses don't point north, Elena had told him once, over bad coffee in a hospital corridor. They point at magnetic north, which moves. Which wanders. Which has to be re-charted every few years because the pole itself won't sit still. A compass isn't pointing at a place, Dominic. It's pointing at a correction. You navigate by what's true *now*.

The truth now was a rebuilt hangar with a sign that said EST. TWICE. It was a woman on a dock wiping her eyes and laughing. It was an old sinner come home to testify. It was a town that had nearly sold itself and chosen, at the last possible minute, not to.

Dominic Delacroix climbed out of his plane, and did not look north.

He looked at the hangar, and the lights coming on along the shore, and the people walking toward him across the boards, and he thought: *there*. That's the heading. That's the fix.

Then Nadia waved him over, and Silas shouted something unrepeatable about supper, and Dominic went, leaving the compass in the plane where it belonged — a good instrument, but useless on the ground, and no use at all to a man who had finally landed.
