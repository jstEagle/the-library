## 1. The Weight of Names

The name on the work board at dawn was *Corben, T.* — trail crew, upper switchbacks, two days' pay if the weather held. Gideon Nystrom read it twice, memorized it, and let it go. By the time the foreman called roll he would answer to Corben the way other men answered to their own names: without flinching, without a half-second's hesitation that might give him away.

Six years of practice. He could have done it in his sleep, and some mornings he suspected he did.

"Corben." The foreman, a squat man named Dree with a voice like gravel in a bucket, ran his thumb down the list. "You're on lopping today. Take the east face."

"East face," Gideon said. "Rope's frayed on the third anchor. I checked last night."

Dree looked up. Most men on the crew didn't check anchors after hours; most men didn't notice frayed rope at all. But Dree had learned not to ask questions about Corben, because Corben was the only man on the payroll who made the dangerous jobs boring. "Take the new coil from the shed, then."

Gideon took the new coil. He always took the new coil. Old rope killed people, and he had spent six years trying to be a man to whom nothing bad happened — no accidents, no attention, no names in anyone's mouth longer than a shift.

Kestrel's Landing woke around him as he walked to the east face: fish-smoke and diesel, gulls arguing over the offal barges, the first ferry horn of the morning rolling upriver like something clearing its throat. The town sat where the Vane River widened into its estuary, all pilings and rust and salt-bleached wood, a place built for cargo and transients and nobody's permanent home. Half the population was hiding from something. The other half was paid not to notice.

Gideon fit perfectly.

He climbed the east face by mid-morning, working alone above the switchback road, sawing deadfall that threatened the trail below. The work was beneath him — that was the point of it. A man who had once guided expeditions across the Corren Pass icefields, who had been written up in three alpine journals before he turned thirty, now cut brush for coin wages and was grateful for it. Gratitude was part of the choreography. You didn't survive invisibility by resenting it. You survived by making it feel like a choice.

Below him, the registry office opened its doors at ten. He could see it from the east face — a narrow brick building with a green door, wedged between a chandler and a pawnshop, the kind of building you'd walk past a thousand times without seeing. Every worker in Kestrel's Landing passed through that green door eventually. Licensing, permits, labor registration. One form, one fingerprint, one clerk cross-checking your name against the provincial rolls.

One name against the blacklist.

Gideon had arrived in the landing six years ago with a duffel bag, a forged journeyman card, and a rule he had never broken since: *never touch the registry*. It meant he couldn't hold licensed work. It meant he was paid off the books, under rates, first fired. It meant every winter was lean and every spring was a negotiation. It also meant he was nobody. The consortium's reach was long, but it moved through paper — registries, guilds, licensing boards, the quiet machinery of a company that owned the rivers and most of the men who worked them. Paper needed a name. Gideon Nystrom had stopped existing on paper the day he'd walked out of the inquiry into the Corren Pass avalanche, and as long as he stayed off the page, he stayed free.

Or free enough. Free the way a man is free who checks the street behind him out of habit, who sits facing doors, who knows which clerks gossip and which ferries run spot checks.

At noon he came down for the bread and dried fish Dree handed out, and ate sitting on the seawall with his back to the water. That was another rule: never sit with your back to a crowd. The habit had cost him friendships — men didn't warm to someone who watched them like weather — but habits were load-bearing. Remove one and the whole structure came down.

"You eat like a man expecting the meal to be confiscated," said a voice beside him.

Gideon glanced over. A woman he knew by sight but not by name — one of the river pilots, dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, a canvas jacket with salt stains mapping her shoulders like continents. She worked the freight runs up the lower Vane. He'd seen her handling a boat through the estuary shallows in fog so thick the channel buoys vanished, and she'd brought it through without touching bottom, without raising her voice. He kept track of competent people the way other men kept track of debts.

"I eat like a man who's hungry," Gideon said.

"Mm." She bit into her own bread and looked out at the water with him. "Dree says you flagged a frayed anchor line nobody else caught. Says you do that kind of thing regular."

"Dree talks too much."

"Dree's scared of you a little, I think. Won't say why." She shrugged. "I'm Sera. Okonkwo. I pilot the *Marrow*."

"Gideon—" He caught it a half-second too late, the old reflex reaching for the wrong syllable, and course-corrected. "Tom. Tom Corben."

Something flickered behind her eyes — quick, amused, gone. She'd heard the hitch. She filed it away; he could see her filing it away, neat as a ledger entry. "Well, Tom Corben," she said, standing and brushing crumbs from her trousers. "Try not to die on the east face. It's bad for Dree's paperwork."

She walked off toward the docks, and Gideon sat very still for a moment, watching her go, doing the arithmetic he hated doing. *How much had she noticed? How much would she remember?* Pilots remembered faces; it was trade craft. And she had heard the stumble — *Gideon* — heard it and smiled at it, and smiles like that meant she'd turn it over later, alone, in the wheelhouse, the way she probably turned over everything.

He finished his bread, went back up the east face, and put the woman out of his mind as best he could. Some threats you couldn't plan around. You just kept moving and hoped the current carried them past.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually did in Kestrel's Landing, at the ferry house.

Gideon came down at dusk to collect his pay from Dree and found a knot of dockworkers gathered at the freight slip, talking low and fast. A barge had come in on the evening tide from downriver — a prospector's barge, patched and filthy, riding high because whatever it had carried out had been sold or lost long ago. And standing at its rail, refusing the gangplank hand's shoulder, was a man who looked like death had already drafted the paperwork and was waiting on a signature.

He was gaunt. Yellowed. His coat hung off him like it belonged to a bigger man, and maybe it had once. He gripped the rail with both hands and lowered himself down the plank one step at a time, and the whole dock went quiet the way docks do when everyone realizes at once that they're watching the end of something.

"He came up from the delta," someone murmured. "Been out past the checkpoint weirs. Nobody goes out there."

"Past the weirs?" Another voice, disbelieving. "Consortium seals that whole stretch. What's out there worth dying for?"

The old man reached the dock. Stood swaying. Then he raised his head and looked at the crowd with eyes that were still burning even though everything else about him was going out, and he said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"I'm looking for a guide."

Nobody moved. Dockworkers looked at their boots. The gangplank hand studied the sky. In Kestrel's Landing, *guide* was a word with teeth in it — the guild had been gutted years ago, licenses revoked, the trade strangled by consortium restrictions until the only guides left were unlicensed ghosts. Nobody admitted to being one. Nobody wanted the questions that followed the word.

"I need a guide," the old man said again, louder, turning slowly. "Upstream. All the way. I'll pay—"

"You need a doctor," said the harbormaster's man, stepping forward with the weary authority of a man whose job was making problems go away. "Come inside. There's a cot—"

"I don't need a cot." The old man coughed, doubled, and the cough went on long enough that several people looked away from the sound of it. When he straightened, there was blood on his knuckles where he'd wiped his mouth. "I need someone who knows the Vane. The upper Vane. Who isn't afraid of—"

His legs went.

It happened fast — one moment upright, the next folding, and the crowd stepped back as one body, the way crowds do, opening a ring of empty planking around him. The harbormaster's man shouted for someone to fetch the medic, and nobody moved, because moving meant involvement, and involvement in Kestrel's Landing was a currency people hoarded.

Gideon didn't remember deciding to move. Later, examining it, he would understand that his feet had decided before the rest of him caught up — six years of careful choreography undone in the time it took to cross a dock. He knelt beside the old man, got an arm under his shoulders, and felt how light he was, how the bones moved under the skin like loose cargo.

"Easy," Gideon said. "I've got you."

The old man's head rolled against his arm. His eyes found Gideon's face and fixed there with sudden, terrible focus, the way dying men fix on things — as if the whole world had narrowed to a single point of light and he intended to look at it while it lasted.

"There you are," the old man whispered. "There you are. I've been looking for you a long time."

"You've got the wrong man," Gideon said quietly. "I'm nobody."

"No." A smile cracked the old man's cracked lips. "No, you're exactly right. I can tell. The hands." His gaze dropped to Gideon's grip on his arm — the rope-callused hands, the mountaineer's hands, hands that had held nine families' worth of trust once upon a time and been blamed for losing it. "A guide's hands. I'd know them anywhere."

"Medic's coming," Gideon said. "Save your breath."

"Don't want the medic." The old man's fingers closed around Gideon's wrist with a strength that startled him, that had no business living in that ruined body. "Want a name. Yours. Your real one."

The dock had gone silent again. Too many ears. Gideon leaned close, lowering his voice to almost nothing. "Whatever you think I am, I'm not. Let the medic look at you."

"Halloway," the old man breathed. "My name's Halloway. Remember it. You'll want to know whose promise you're keeping." His grip tightened, trembled, tightened again. "Upstream, guide. Before the dry season ends. Before they seal it forever. Promise me you'll carry it—"

"Carry what?"

But Halloway's eyes had gone past him, unfocusing, and the medic arrived then — a tired woman with a canvas kit — and the moment broke apart into noise and motion. They got the old man onto a stretcher, into the ferry house's back room, and Gideon stood in the gathering dark of the dock with Halloway's grip still ghosting his wrist, telling himself the arithmetic that had kept him alive for six years.

*You're nobody. You're Tom Corben. You walk away. You always walk away.*

Behind him, at the edge of the lamplight, the river pilot — Sera Okonkwo — stood with her arms crossed, watching him with an expression he couldn't read. Not suspicion exactly. Curiosity, maybe. The look of a woman adding a column of figures and getting a number she didn't expect.

Gideon pulled his collar up against the river wind and walked home the long way, checking the street behind him, sitting nowhere, speaking to no one.

By midnight he had almost convinced himself the old man was just a dying fool who'd grabbed the wrong stranger.

Almost.

* * *

He couldn't sleep. That was the truth of it, stripped of all choreography. He lay in his rented room above the chandler's shop — a narrow bed, a narrow window, the smell of tar and hemp rising through the floorboards — and listened to the landing settle into night, and told himself every reasonable thing there was to tell.

*He's delirious. Dying men grab strangers. By morning he'll be dead or gone, and either way it won't be yours.*

But the words kept circling back: *before they seal it forever.* And underneath the words, older and more dangerous, a feeling he hadn't allowed himself in six years — the pull of the upper country. The high valleys. The source of the Vane, which every map in the province marked with a blank space and a printed warning, and which no licensed soul had entered since the consortium closed it. Gideon had grown up in mountains like those. Had loved them before they took everything from him. Some part of him had never stopped listening for them, the way an exiled man listens for his own language in a crowd.

Toward dawn he gave up on sleep, dressed, and told himself he was only going to check — only going to confirm the old man was dead or gone, so his mind would stop gnawing it. The ferry house was cold and smelled of lamp oil. The medic's cot in the back room held a shape under blankets, breathing shallow and fast.

Halloway's eyes opened as Gideon approached. Clearer now, horribly clear, as if dying had burned away everything except the one thing left to do.

"You came back," Halloway whispered.

"I was passing."

"Liar." No anger in it. Almost fond. "Guide. Listen. I don't have long, and they'll come looking — they always come looking. So listen." His hand fumbled at his coat, at some inner pocket, and Gideon caught the wrist again, gently this time.

"Don't. Whatever it is—"

"A map." The word came out cracked and reverent. "Hand-drawn. To the source of the Vane. The true source — not what their maps say, not their fenced springs. The real one. Sixty years I worked the delta claims, and twenty I worked upstream, and I found it, guide. I *found* it." His chest heaved. "And they know. They've known for years, and they'll bury it deeper than they buried me, unless someone carries it out. Unless someone carries it *up*. Before the dry season ends and the river closes."

"Why me? You don't know me."

"I know hands." Halloway's mouth twisted into something like a grin. "And I know a man hiding under a borrowed name when I see one. Takes one to know one, guide. I've been invisible thirty years myself. Invisible men find each other. It's the only way we recognize our own kind."

Gideon stood very still. The room was gray with coming dawn. Somewhere outside, the first ferry horn sounded, and the sound went through him like a blade finding a seam.

"I'm not a guide anymore," he said.

"You're exactly a guide. That's the tragedy of you." Halloway's breath rattled. "Promise me. Carry it upstream. That's all. Don't even open it if you don't want to. Just carry it. A dying man's asking. It's the only currency I've got left."

"I can't." Gideon heard his own voice go flat, final, the voice he used on clients who wanted to push past safe turnaround times. "I'm sorry. Find someone else."

"There is no one else." For a moment the fire in the old man's eyes guttered, and Gideon saw the fear underneath — not fear of dying, but fear of failing, of the map dying with him in a rented cot in a town that forgot names by lunchtime. "They sealed my village's wells, guide. Upstream. Did you know that? Whole valleys drinking from ditches while the consortium pipes the good water south. My wife died of what came out of those wells. So no — there's no one else. There's just you, and me, and whatever you're running from that's bigger than a promise."

Gideon stepped back. His heart was hammering in a way it hadn't hammered since Corren Pass, and that frightened him more than anything the old man had said.

"I'll send the medic back," he said. "Rest."

He walked out of the ferry house into the blue-gray morning, and the cold air hit him like a slap, and he made himself walk past the chandler's, past his room, down to the seawall where the tide was turning. He stood there watching the Vane move — brown and broad and patient, carrying everything downstream and giving nothing back — and he repeated his rules to himself like a litany.

*Never touch the registry. Never give a real name. Never take the dangerous job.*

*Never go back to the mountains.*

The sun came up over the estuary, and the fishing boats went out, and Kestrel's Landing began its ordinary day around a man who had spent six years learning to be ordinary, and who understood, with a certainty that sat in his stomach like ballast stone, that the ordinary days were numbered now. The old man had seen his hands. The pilot had heard his name. Two threads, pulled loose from the weave.

He should leave the landing tonight. Take the coastal packet south. Start over somewhere the threads couldn't follow.

That was the smart play. That was the play he'd run six times in six years, and it had kept him alive.

Gideon turned from the water and went to pack his duffel.

He was halfway through folding his spare shirt when the knock came at the chandler's door below — heavy, official, three strikes — and a voice he didn't know called up the stairwell, flat and procedural:

"Registry office. We're conducting a labor census. All workers will present identification this morning. No exceptions."

Gideon stood in the gray light of his narrow room, one shirt folded, one still in his hands, and listened to the census takers begin at the far end of the street, working their way door to door toward him like a slow tide.

*No exceptions.*

Downstairs, the chandler called up cheerfully, "Corben! You up there? They'll want your papers!"

Gideon set the shirt down. He looked at the window — the drop to the alley, the roofline to the next building, the route he'd mapped the day he rented the room and had never needed until now. Then he looked at the door.

Somewhere across town, in a cot in the ferry house, a dying man was holding a map to the source of the Vane, waiting on a promise Gideon hadn't made yet.

The knock came at the next door down.
