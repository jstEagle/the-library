## 2. A Stranger at the Landing

The *Marta June* came in on the morning tide with her hold riding low and her crew riding lower, and Sera Okonkwo read the barge the way other people read weather. Something was wrong aboard. She could see it from the pilot house of her own boat, the *Tern*, moored two slips down: the deckhand working the bow line moved like a man who hadn't slept, and the mate stood at the rail scanning the landing with the flat, deliberate sweep of someone looking for a specific face.

Sera finished her tea, set the cup down, and went to watch properly.

Kestrel's Landing woke the way it always did—slowly, and then all at once. The fish sheds opened first, then the fuel dock, then the chandlery with its owner, a wide woman named Petra, sweeping her step like the step had personally offended her. By the time the *Marta June* bumped her tires against the pilings, the boardwalk had its morning population: gulls, longshoremen, two customs men who were mostly decorative, and Sera, leaning against a mooring post with her arms crossed, doing nothing that anyone could object to and missing nothing at all.

She'd been running the Vane delta for eleven years. She knew every pilot, every smuggler, every inspector on the take, and every inspector who wasn't, which was a shorter list. She knew which barges carried ore and which carried ore-shaped crates full of river stones, and she knew that the *Marta June* had come down from the upper Vane three days late with no radio explanation, which on this river meant one of two things: money trouble, or the kind of trouble that money couldn't fix.

The gangway came down. The mate went straight to the harbormaster's office without looking left or right. And then, last off the boat, moving like his legs were borrowed and the owner wanted them back, came a prospector.

Sera had seen a thousand prospectors come down the Vane. They had a walk, a particular walk, a roll of the shoulders that said *I've carried more than you've ever lifted and I'm not impressed by your dock*. This man had lost the walk. He came down the gangway in a series of controlled collapses, one hand on the rail, one hand on nothing, his pack riding him like a saddle on a horse that hated him. His coat was good coat—oiled canvas, brass hardware, the kind that cost a season's wages—and it was ruined, stained from collar to hem with something dark that Sera's river eye identified before her brain agreed to: dried mud, engine oil, and under both, blood. Old blood, mostly. Mostly.

He reached the bottom of the gangway, took three steps onto the boards, and stopped. Just stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-motion, mid-breath, and Sera saw the deckhand at the bow line look away very quickly, and she understood that the crew had been carrying this man the last hundred miles the way you carry a thing you're afraid to be found holding.

The prospector stood on the dock of Kestrel's Landing in the morning light and looked around like a man trying to remember why he'd come.

Then his legs went out from under him.

He didn't fall, exactly. He descended, in stages, catching himself on a bollard and ending in a crouch that wasn't going to end anywhere good. People noticed the way dock people notice—sideways, without stopping, each of them deciding with great speed that it wasn't their problem. The customs men discovered a fascinating piece of paperwork. Petra swept harder.

Sera swore under her breath and started toward him, because that was the other thing about her: she'd never once in her life managed to mind her own business when it mattered, and she'd stopped pretending she was going to start.

She didn't get there first.

* * *

The man who got there first came out of the alley between the chandlery and the net shed, and Sera knew him the way you know a dog that never barks—by absence, by the shape of quiet he left in a street. Gideon. Just Gideon. He'd been in Kestrel's Landing eight months, maybe nine, long enough to have a reputation and not long enough to have a history, and the reputation was simple: he did trail work, he did it better than anyone, he charged fairly, he spoke maybe forty words a week, and you did not ask him anything.

Sera had hired him once, in the spring, to haul a failed engine up from the lower rapids. Three men's work, done by one, over ground that would have broken the three men. He'd taken his pay, nodded once, and vanished back into whatever country he lived in when he wasn't carrying things. She'd heard the dock talk since: *cold, that one; not right in the head; you don't want him knowing your business*. Dock talk was mostly weather with opinions, but enough of it agreed that Sera had filed him under *competent and closed*, which was where she filed most people.

Now the closed man crossed the dock in six strides and went down on one knee beside the collapsing prospector like he'd been waiting for him.

Sera slowed. She hadn't meant to slow. She slowed anyway.

"Hey." Gideon's voice carried just far enough. "Hey. Look at me. Not the dock. Me."

The prospector's eyes were the problem—rolling, unfocused, hunting for something that wasn't in front of him. Fever, Sera thought. Or worse. The man's lips were cracked gray and his breathing had a whistle in it, a small wet sound at the top of each breath that she'd heard before, once, in a deckhand she'd carried sixty miles downstream to a doctor who'd arrived too late.

"Can you hear me?" Gideon had the man's wrist now, two fingers on the pulse, his other hand braced flat against the man's sternum—not grabbing, bracing, the way you'd steady a mast. "Squeeze my hand if you can hear me."

The prospector's fingers twitched. Then, with what looked like enormous effort, they closed around Gideon's hand and held on.

"All right." Gideon said it quietly, and something in the quiet of it made the prospector's rolling eyes settle. "All right. You're down. You're on the dock, and you're down, and nobody's going anywhere. Stay with me."

Sera arrived at the edge of the scene and stopped, because there was a geometry to it that she hadn't expected. Gideon had positioned himself between the sick man and the foot traffic without appearing to move, a wall made of a kneeling body, and his face—she'd seen his face maybe ten times in eight months, always in passing, always set like a locked door—was doing something she had no file for. It wasn't soft. It was *attentive*, the whole face aimed at the prospector like a lamp, and behind the attention was something older and more careful, the look of a man handling a thing he already knew how to lose.

"Petra," Sera called, not turning her head. "Water. And your blanket, the ugly one."

"I've got customers—"

"Petra."

There was a pause, and then the sound of the ugly blanket's hook scraping free of its nail. Sera crouched down on the prospector's other side. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and sickness and the particular mineral stink of the upper river, the iron-and-stone smell of water that had come a long way fast.

"When did he board?" she asked Gideon.

"Doesn't matter." He was peeling back the man's collar with two careful fingers, looking at the neck, the jaw. "He's past when. Look at his hands."

She looked. The prospector's hands were a topography of his trade—scarred, cracked, nails black—and the fingertips were dusky, the nail beds wrong, cyanotic in a way that Sera's limited medical vocabulary translated as *bad, and getting badder*.

"He came down the Vane," she said. "In this condition."

"He came down the Vane," Gideon agreed, "in this condition."

Which meant the crew had let him. Which meant the crew had been told to. Sera turned her head and looked at the *Marta June*, and found the mate still standing at the rail, still watching, and when their eyes met the mate turned away with the smoothness of a man who had practiced turning away.

Petra arrived with the water and the blanket, looked at the prospector, and said, "That's Halloway."

Sera glanced up. "You know him?"

"Everybody knows Halloway. Been working the headwaters twenty years. Comes down every dry season, sells his color, drinks half of it, goes back up." Petra was already kneeling, tucking the blanket around the man with a briskness that was its own kind of gentleness. "He's early. Dry season's got a month left in it, easy."

"He's early," Gideon repeated. He said it the way Sera would have said *the water's rising*—a fact with weather in it.

The prospector—Halloway—stirred under the blanket. His eyes found focus for the first time, and the focus went to Gideon's face and stayed there, and his cracked lips moved.

Gideon bent closer. So did Sera, against her better judgment, and heard it: a thread of a voice, sanded down to almost nothing.

"—didn't make the landing office. Couldn't— they'd see. Had to come to the dock. Had to find—" A cough took him, a long tearing one that bent him double against Gideon's bracing arm, and when it released him he was smaller, somehow, inside his ruined coat. "—find someone going up."

"Nobody's going up," Gideon said. "You're going to the doctor's."

"No." It came out flat and immediate, a door closing, and Sera blinked at the strength in it. Halloway's hand came up from under the blanket—shaking, filthy, determined—and closed on Gideon's jacket front, and he pulled himself up off the boards an inch, two inches, close enough that Sera could see his eyes properly for the first time. They were yellow. Not sick-yellow. *Determined*-yellow, the yellow of a man who had decided something a long time ago and was only waiting for the rest of him to catch up. "Listen to me. Listen. Not the doctor. Not the office. Not the—" another cough, shorter, fought down, "—not the registry. You understand me? Nobody in the registry."

Sera felt the sentence land somewhere below her ribs, because everyone on this river understood the registry, and everyone on this river understood why a man half dead would crawl off a barge on his hands and knees rather than have his name entered in it. The registry was where the consortium looked. The registry was where men became findable.

"You're dying," Gideon said. Not cruelly. Just laying it on the boards between them, the way you'd lay down a heavy pack before a portage.

"I'm aware." Halloway almost smiled. It was a terrible, wonderful thing to watch. "Been aware for some weeks. That's why I'm early." His grip on Gideon's jacket tightened. "That's why I need—"

He went out. Not a collapse—a shutdown, like a lamp turned off, mid-word, his weight swinging into Gideon's arm and Gideon catching him without a stumble, lowering him back onto the blanket with a control that Sera, who had dropped a crate of glass floats in her day and would again, found frankly offensive.

For a moment nobody moved. The gulls argued about something. Down the dock, the *Marta June*'s mate was gone from the rail.

"He's breathing," Gideon said. His fingers were at Halloway's throat again, reading the pulse like a pilot read water. "Shallow. Fast. He needs a doctor."

"He said no doctor," Petra said.

"He's dying. He doesn't get a vote." But Gideon said it slowly, and Sera watched his eyes move—dock, alley, the *Marta June*, the harbormaster's office, the alley again—mapping, the way she mapped channels. Weighing. She'd seen that exact look on men deciding whether to run a weir at flood stage, and she knew what it cost, and she knew that whatever he was weighing, it wasn't medicine.

"The doctor's office shares a wall with the registry," she said.

Gideon looked at her. It was the longest look he'd ever given her, maybe four seconds, and it had the quality of a man re-reading a chart he thought he'd memorized.

"I know," he said.

"So does half the dock," Sera said. "Including whoever put him on that barge in this condition and told the crew to keep him breathing until the Landing. Somebody wanted him delivered here, Gideon. Delivered. You don't deliver a man to a dock unless the dock is the address."

Something moved behind his face—there and gone, a fish under a boat, no shape you could name. "Then somebody's going to come looking for him," he said.

"Yes," Sera said. "So. The question is whether he's here when they come."

* * *

They got him to the net shed. It was Sera's idea, or half hers—Petra's shed was on the main street and the net shed was Sera's, technically, in the way that half of everything at Kestrel's Landing was technically hers or technically nobody's, a lean-roofed space where she stored gear and mended nets and conducted the small private business of not being watched. Gideon carried Halloway. He carried him the way he'd done everything else that morning, without wasted motion and without softness that showed, but Sera walked beside him with a lantern even though it was full morning, and she watched the set of his shoulders under the weight, and she thought: *he's carried men before. More than once. He knows exactly how a dying man's weight changes.*

They laid Halloway on her mending table, which was the only flat thing in the shed, and Gideon did things with a competence that kept surprising her in small, accumulating ways—propped him on his side so the whistle in his breathing eased, got water into him a spoonful at a time without flooding him, wrapped his blackened fingers in a wet cloth without once asking Sera where anything was, because he'd already seen where everything was. He was the kind of man who saw where everything was. She'd known that from the engine haul. She was only now beginning to wonder what else he saw.

Halloway surfaced twice in the next hour. The first time he surfaced confused, fighting the blanket, and Gideon put one hand flat on his chest and said, "Net shed. Landing. Safe," and the man stilled like a horse hearing a familiar voice. The second time he surfaced clear, eyes finding Gideon in the lantern light, and he said, in a voice with more gravel in it than strength:

"You're the one. Off the Corren trails. Before."

Sera, mending a net she did not need to mend, felt her shuttle stop.

Gideon didn't move. "You've got me wrong."

"I don't." Halloway's laugh was a ruin. "Twenty years in the high country, boy. I forget faces. I don't forget hands. I watched you rig a litter on the Corren switchbacks in a whiteout, the year before— " He stopped himself, delicately, a man stepping around a grave. "Before everything. You were the best of them. They said otherwise. They were wrong, and I knew it then, and I know it now."

The shed was very quiet. Outside, the gulls had gone off somewhere, and the tide turned with its small sound against the pilings, and Sera sat with a net needle in her hand and a man's whole hidden history sitting in the room like a third person.

Gideon said, "You're feverish."

"I'm dying," Halloway said, with the same flat patience as before. "Feverish is underneath it. Don't change the subject." He worked one hand free of the blanket and caught Gideon's sleeve—not gripping this time, just holding, a man holding on to a railing. "I've got something for you. Not for the office. Not for the registry. Not for the consortium's little gray men with their little gray ledgers. For you. Because you're the only man on this river who ever carried what he was told to carry all the way up the mountain, and I've got one more thing that needs carrying up."

"Halloway." Gideon's voice was very even. "Whatever it is—"

"It's the source." The words came out of the old man like water out of a cracked dam, all at once, unstoppable, years of pressure behind them. "The Vane's source. The real one, not the one on the charts. I found it. Twenty years I looked and three years ago I found it, and I've been sitting on it because sitting on it was the only way to stay alive, and now I'm not staying alive, so it needs to go up before the dry season ends, and it can't go up in any hands but yours."

Sera set down the net needle. She was aware that she had stopped pretending to mend the net. She was aware that Gideon had gone very still, the stillness of a man standing on ice and listening to it speak.

"Halloway," Gideon said quietly, "I don't go upriver."

"Everyone goes upriver," the old man whispered. His eyes were closing, fighting it, losing. "That's what a river's for."

He slept then, or went under—Sera couldn't tell the difference and suspected, from Gideon's face, that neither could he. The whistle in his breathing had thickened. The wet cloth had fallen from his fingers, and Gideon picked it up and rewet it and laid it back with a gentleness that Sera watched the way you watch something you're not supposed to be watching.

"You should get out of here," she said, finally, because somebody had to say something. "Whatever he's tangled in. You should walk out that door, go take a trail job in the hills, and let this be my problem. I'm good at problems that are officially not my problem."

Gideon looked at the old man on the table. The lantern threw his shadow up the net-hung wall, and the shadow looked like a bigger man than the one casting it, the way shadows do.

"He knew my hands," Gideon said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. He wrung out the cloth, folded it, set it down with a precision that had nothing to do with cloth. "It's not."

Sera studied him—the locked door of him, the closed country of him, and the one small window now standing open onto a man kneeling beside a stranger, keeping him alive with his hands. Eight months she'd filed him under *competent and closed*. She was beginning to suspect the file was wrong, or the man was, or both.

"Whoever put him on that barge," she said, "will come for whatever he's carrying. Tonight, probably. The *Marta June*'s mate has been walking past this shed twice an hour since we brought him in, and he's bad at it."

"I know," Gideon said. "I've been counting."

Of course he had. Sera almost laughed. She looked at the dying man, and the closed man, and the open window, and the tide going out of the landing with the whole day still ahead of it, and she heard herself say, in the tone she used for quoting prices she already knew were too low:

"I keep a boat two slips down. In case you decide the answer isn't *no*."

Gideon didn't look up. But after a moment he said, "I know which boat," and Sera went back to her net, and mended nothing, and listened to the old man breathe.
