## 3. The Dying Trade

The man from the barge was still alive when I found him.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one currency I have left and I've spent most of my life hoarding even that. He was alive. Breathing in shallow pulls, the way a bellows breathes when the leather's cracked. His eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling beam of my rented room at the Ferry House, tracking something that wasn't there.

I hadn't invited him up. That matters too, or I keep telling myself it does. He'd collapsed against the woodpile behind Corrag's stable an hour after sundown, and old Corrag—whose charity extends exactly as far as a full purse—had looked at me across the yard with the flat expectation of a woman who knows which drifter in town will do the dirty work for free. She knew about me. Everyone in Kestrel's Landing knew something about me, and none of them knew the right thing, and that was the arrangement I'd spent four years building: a reputation cold enough to keep questions away, useless enough to keep ambition away, and just competent enough to stay employed.

So I carried him. Two hundred yards through the mud, his weight wrong in my arms—not heavy so much as *loose*, the weight of a body already negotiating its surrender—and I told myself the whole time that I was doing it because leaving a corpse by the woodpile would bring the constable, and the constable would bring the registry clerk, and the registry clerk had a ledger with a name in it I could not afford to have spoken aloud in this town.

That was the story I told myself. It was even mostly true.

* * *

He was maybe sixty, though prospectors age like driftwood, weathered past their years until you're guessing between forty-five and seventy. His hands were the tell: knuckles swollen to twice their size, nails blackened, the fingertips of a man who'd spent decades reading stone by touch. His clothes were river clothes gone past worn into ruin—a canvas coat stiff with dried silt, boots held together with wire and hope. And under the filth, stitched into the lining where no one would look, there were places where the fabric bulged wrong. Flat, rectangular wrong.

I noticed. Of course I noticed. Four years of staying invisible teaches you to read what people carry the way other men read faces.

I set him on the cot and he grabbed my wrist with a grip that had no business surviving in that body. Strong fingers. A climber's grip, or a miner's—the kind of hold a man develops hanging over drops.

"Nystrom," he said.

The room went very quiet. The rain on the roof, the wind off the landing, the creak of the Ferry House settling—all of it dropped away, and there was just that word sitting in the air between us like a lit fuse.

"That's not my name," I said.

"It is." His voice was gravel dragged through water. "I watched you carry me. Nobody carries a stranger like that unless they've buried friends wrong. You lift from the knees, you cradle the head. Mountain rescue habit." A wet sound that might have been a laugh. "And you flinched."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You're Nystrom. The guide. Corren Pass." His eyes came off the ceiling beam and found mine, and they were clear—terribly clear, the clarity of a man who has burned through everything else and kept only what mattered. "Don't waste both our time lying. I haven't got the hours for it."

I should have walked out. I want that on the record too. Every instinct I'd honed since the Pass said walk out, let the man die of whatever was killing him, let the river take his secrets the way it took everything else. The smart move. The safe move. The move that had kept me breathing and anonymous through four years of trail work and false names and never once sleeping two nights in the same bed if I could help it.

Instead I sat down on the stool by the window, where the lamplight wouldn't reach my face, and said, "You're dying."

"Water in the lungs. Started three days ago, downstream of the Grinder." He said it the way another man might report the weather. "Swallowed half the Vane getting past the weirs. They've got the middle river locked down tight as a drum these days. Had to run it dark."

"The consortium checkpoints don't stop prospectors. There's nothing up there worth—" I stopped. Because that was the official line, the line posted on every notice board from Kestrel's Landing to the coast: *UPPER VANE CLOSED — SURVEY HAZARD — NO MINERAL VALUE*, and everyone in the trade repeated it with the flat obedience of people who had learned not to ask why a worthless valley needed armed guards.

Halloway—I learned his name later, but I'll use it here because he earned it before I ever heard it—watched me stop, and something in his face suggested he'd expected the sentence to die right there.

"There's nothing worth guarding," he agreed. "That's the point. You can't guard nothing. Costs money to station men at every weir, bribe every pilot, buy every magistrate from here to the headwaters. Somebody's spending a fortune protecting an empty valley." His chest hitched; he coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when he brought his fist away from his mouth there was pink froth on his knuckles. He wiped it on the coat without looking at it. "You worked the high country. Before."

"Yes."

"Before they took your name."

I stood up. "I'm going to get you water."

"Sit down, Nystrom."

And here is the thing I still can't fully explain, not to Sera, not to the inquiry boards, not to myself on bad nights: I sat down. A dying stranger in a borrowed room ordered me to sit and I sat, because his voice had the particular authority of a man who has already settled all his accounts except one, and I recognized it. I'd heard it before, on the mountain, in the hours after the Pass, in the mouth of a man named Ellery Vance who held my hand while the snow covered us and made me promise things I couldn't keep.

Dying men make promises out of other people. It's how they spend what they can't take with them.

* * *

He talked for most of the night. I'll give the shape of it here, stripped of the coughing and the pauses and the long stretches where his eyes drifted back to the ceiling beam and I thought we'd lost him, only for him to surface again with more words.

He'd prospected the upper Vane for eleven years. Not gold—he'd chased gold first, like everyone, and found traces, colors in the pans, enough to keep a man hungry and no more. But Halloway was the kind of prospector who read water the way I read snowpack, and the water kept telling him things the official surveys said weren't true. Springs flowing when they shouldn't. Aquifer levels that rose instead of fell. A watershed behaving like a reservoir with a gate on it.

"So I stopped looking down and started looking up," he said. "Spent six years mapping the source country. Every spring, every seep, every gauge the consortium didn't know I'd found. And what's up there, Nystrom—" he lifted one ruined hand and pressed it flat against his own sternum, as if holding something in— "is the whole region's water. Three provinces drink out of that valley whether they know it or not. The Vane isn't a river. It's a tap. And somebody owns the tap."

"The filings say the upper basin is dry."

"The filings are the product. The guards are the packaging." His breath whistled. "They falsified the hydrology surveys twenty years back. Declared the source springs seasonal, unreliable, negligible. Then they bought the water rights for pennies from settlements that believed the paperwork. Now they sell it back downstream at whatever price the market will bleed. Every drought, every rationing order, every well that fails in a dry year—that's not weather. That's inventory management."

I'd heard corruption described before. Everyone in the border towns had; it was the local folklore, half complaint and half superstition. But Halloway spoke like a man reading instruments, and instruments don't care what you want to believe.

"Why me?" I asked. "You watched me carry you twenty feet and decided I'm your courier?"

"Because you flinched at your name." He smiled, and it cracked his lips. "Because you're a mountain man and the last thirty miles is climbing, not boating. Because you're already dead to the registries—they can't threaten you with anything worse than you've got." The smile faded. "And because I've been watching this landing for two weeks waiting for somebody who might say yes, and you're the only one left."

"Two weeks. Watching."

"A dying man has time. Living men don't notice being watched." He fumbled at his coat, at those wrong-shaped bulges in the lining, and I saw then that his hands were shaking—not from weakness, or not only from weakness, but from the effort of deciding. "There's a map. Hand-drawn. Six years of work. Springs, gauges, the fence lines, the stations, the names of the men running them. And survey data underneath—readings, measurements, the real hydrology. Enough to prove the filings are forged. Enough to break them open in any court that isn't already bought."

"And you want me to carry it upstream."

"To the source. Before the dry season ends." His eyes fixed on mine with that terrible clarity. "Thirty-one days, maybe less. After the channels drop, the upper river's unnavigable and the portages turn to killing ground. It has to be now. It has to be someone who can climb, and row, and lie, and disappear. That's you. That's been you since Corren Pass, whatever they wrote in the reports."

"No," I said.

It came out faster than I meant it to, and harder. He didn't blink.

"They killed my partner in March," he said, conversational, as if we were discussing freight rates. "Found him floating below the second weir. Official ruling: misadventure. He'd been asking the same questions I'd been asking. I've been running since spring. Running and coughing." He gestured at his chest. "This is what running gets you. So understand me, Nystrom: I'm not asking you to save my life. I'm asking you to finish mine."

"I bury promises along with everything else," I said. "Ask anyone."

"Nobody in this town knows anything about you. That's rather the point of you." He tried to laugh and it turned into the cough again, longer this time, and I did stand then, and got the water, and held the cup while he drank because his hands had passed beyond shaking into something worse. When he finished, he lay back and looked at the ceiling and said, quietly, "You'll say yes tomorrow."

"I won't."

"You will. Not because you're good—I don't think you believe you're good anymore, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You'll say yes because you looked at the official story of Corren Pass and it didn't match what your eyes told you, and you've spent four years swallowing that mismatch, and a man can only swallow a thing like that for so long before it starts eating him from inside." He closed his eyes. "I know the taste. Go to sleep. One of us should."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat by the window with the lamp out and watched the rain come off the landing in gray sheets, and I ran the arithmetic of my life the way I always ran it, the nightly audit: name false, trail clean, ledger balanced, no debts, no ties, no promises outstanding. Four years of careful nothing. And underneath the arithmetic, Halloway's voice, saying *you'll say yes tomorrow*, and underneath that, older and colder, the memory of snow sliding on Corren Pass and nine people who trusted me with their lives and a report that called it my fault.

If I took the map, I'd be moving toward the thing that destroyed me. Toward the high country, toward the consortium's reach, toward every record and registry and reward notice I'd spent four years hiding from. If I refused, a man would die in my rented room with six years of truth sewn into his coat lining, and the truth would die with him, and the tap would stay owned, and the wells downstream would keep failing on schedule, and I would go on being nobody, safely, forever.

Both options cost exactly what I had. That's how I knew one of them was right.

Near dawn the rain stopped. The light came up the color of tin. Behind me, the breathing changed—not louder, not weaker, just *finished*, the way a rope goes slack when the load comes off it.

I crossed the room. I put two fingers against his throat because that's what you do, because the body demands the ritual even when it already knows. Nothing. His face in the gray light had relaxed into something almost peaceful, the ceiling beam finally released from duty, and his right hand was curled loosely around the edge of the coat, over the lining, over the map, the way a man holds a door shut.

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd talked all night not because he believed he'd convince me but because he was leaving the argument where I'd find it, the way you cache supplies on a route you won't survive yourself.

I sat on the floor beside the cot until the light strengthened. I told myself I hadn't promised anything. I told myself refusal was final, that dead men's requests expire with them, that the map wasn't mine and the debt wasn't mine and the name he'd spoken into the dark wasn't mine either.

Then I opened the coat lining, and took out the oilcloth packet, and weighed it in my hand—lighter than a promise should be, heavier than I could put down.

And downstairs, boots on the stairs. More than one pair. Heavy, unhurried, the tread of men who go where they like and expect the world to arrange itself around them.

I blew out the lamp that was already out, which tells you the state of me, and moved to the wall with the packet inside my shirt, listening to whoever it was climb toward the room where a prospector lay dead and a blacklisted guide stood holding the reason men like them existed.

The door handle began to turn.
