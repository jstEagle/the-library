## 4. What the Map Costs

Sera Okonkwo had a rule about strangers: watch their hands, not their faces. Faces lied for a living. Hands only knew how to tell the truth—whether they were soft from ledgers or scarred from lines, whether they reached for a knife or a coin purse, whether they shook when they said *I'm fine*.

The drifter's hands had been steady when he'd caught the dying prospector off the barge. Steadier than hers would have been.

She thought about that all through the morning while she re-caulked a seam on the *Maggie V*, working by feel more than sight because her attention kept sliding across the yard to the ferry house where the drifter—Nystrom, he'd said, though it sat wrong in her mouth like a borrowed coat—had carried Halloway two days ago.

The prospector was dead now. Buried fast and shallow behind the chandlery because nobody wanted to spend registry money on a man with no papers. Sera had watched them shovel the dirt. She'd also watched Nystrom stand at the edge of that hole longer than any stranger should have, his jaw working like he was chewing on something too big to swallow.

Now there were four men in gray walking up the landing road, and every hand in Kestrel's Landing stopped moving at once.

* * *

She knew the type before she knew the livery. Consortium men didn't dress like soldiers—they dressed like clerks who'd been given permission to be cruel. Clean boots, clean jackets, badges laminated in plastic that caught the light like fish scales. The one in front was broad through the shoulders with a face like a closed ledger, and he walked the boardwalk the way a man walks a room he already owns.

"Vane Consolidated," he announced to no one and everyone, holding up a folded paper. "Looking for property of a deceased party. Halloway, T. Prospecting license, survey materials, personal effects. Anyone holding such property is obligated under river statute eleven-four to surrender it immediately."

River statute eleven-four. Sera had run the Vane for eleven years and had never once heard of it. Neither, judging by the blank looks along the boardwalk, had anyone else. That was how the consortium worked: they made up laws faster than anyone could check them, and by the time you checked, you were already in violation.

The clerk's men started at the top of the landing and worked down. She watched them go into the ferry house first—of course the ferry house—and heard drawers bang, floorboards lifted, the registry clerk stammering about proper warrants while someone laughed without humor.

Sera went back to her caulking. She kept her head down and her iron moving and she listened with the whole surface of her skin, the way you do when the river goes quiet before a squall.

They were efficient. That was the thing that raised the hair on her arms. These weren't drunk enforcers kicking over market stalls for show. They worked in pairs, one talking, one searching, polite as customs officers, and everything they touched went back exactly where it had been. They were looking for paper. Specifically paper. And they were looking like men who knew precisely what the paper looked like.

Which meant somebody upstream knew Halloway had carried something worth killing a search warrant over.

* * *

They reached the chandlery mid-morning. Sera could see the whole exchange from where she knelt on the *Maggie V*'s deck: the clerk-in-charge asking after Halloway's effects, the chandler shrugging, the second man drifting toward the back of the shop where the dead man had been laid out before burial.

And then Nystrom came around the corner of the net shed carrying a coil of rope he clearly had no use for, and Sera's stomach dropped, because she saw what he saw—the open back door of the chandlery, the gray jacket moving inside—and instead of turning around, which is what any sensible invisible man would do, he slowed down.

He slowed down and set the rope down and stood there looking at the chandlery like a man deciding something.

Sera had spent her life reading water. Rapids told you what they were going to do to you three seconds before they did it, if you knew where to look—a seam in the current, a change in the sound, a line of foam bending wrong. People were harder. But she read Nystrom the way she read water, and what she read was this: he had something on him. Something flat and important, carried against his chest the way you carry a chart in rain. And those men were ten steps from finding out.

The clerk came out of the chandlery wiping his hands, satisfied or not, Sera couldn't tell. He spotted Nystrom immediately. Of course he did—Nystrom was the last person seen handling Halloway alive, and even a clerk could do that arithmetic.

"You. You're the one caught him off the barge."

"I caught him," Nystrom said. Flat voice. Nothing in it.

"Where are his effects?"

"What effects? He had a jacket full of holes and forty cents in tin."

The clerk smiled the way ice smiles. "Statute eleven-four requires—"

"Requires whoever holds his property to surrender it." Nystrom shrugged. "I don't hold his property. Search me if you want. I'd hate to keep you from your schedule."

It was a gamble and Sera watched him make it, standing loose-limbed in the road with his arms half away from his body, daring them. And here was the thing that decided her—not his courage, which might just be stupidity, but the clerk's hesitation. The clerk looked at Nystrom's face a beat too long, like a man trying to place a name, and then visibly decided not to touch him. Decided it wasn't worth the paperwork.

"Name?" the clerk said.

"Nystrom."

Something flickered. Sera saw it even at fifty yards—the clerk's pen pausing above his little book, the way his eyes narrowed, some private calculation running behind his face. Then he wrote it down anyway and moved on, and Nystrom picked up his useless rope and walked away, and Sera exhaled a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

She should have gone back to work. That was the smart move. The *only* move. A pilot who made trouble with Vane Consolidated didn't run the middle river anymore; didn't run anywhere anymore; found her permits mysteriously expired and her moorage reassigned and her name quietly struck from the salvage rolls until she starved or left. Everybody on the landing knew the rules. You stayed small. You stayed boring. You let the gray jackets walk past you like weather.

Sera put down her caulking iron.

* * *

She caught up with him at the water stairs below the net shed, where he stood looking at the river like it owed him an answer.

"Nystrom." He turned. Up close his face was worse than she'd thought—gray under the tan, cheekbones standing out like he'd been rationing himself for weeks. "You've got it, don't you. Whatever they're looking for."

His eyes came to hers and held, and there was a long moment where she watched him decide whether to lie to her. She watched him decide *not* to, and wondered why.

"If I did," he said, "what would you do about it?"

"That depends. What is it?"

"A map."

The word landed between them and neither of them picked it up. A map. Men in gray jackets tearing apart a town over a map. She'd heard rumors her whole career about the upper Vane—closed since before she'd been born, sealed by order of the consortium, *hazardous conditions, restricted watershed*, the signs rotting on the fences at the weirs. Every pilot had a theory. Gold, mostly. Silver, sometimes. One old man swore it was a meteorite.

"They'll come back," she said. "That clerk wrote your name down like he recognized it. They'll pull the harbor records, they'll find out where you sleep, and they'll come back at night when there's no audience. That's how they do it. I've watched them do it to better men than either of us."

"Then I won't be sleeping where I usually do."

"That's not a plan, that's a postponement."

He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "You offering a better one?"

No. She wasn't. She was offering nothing. She was a river pilot with a boat, a reputation, and a healthy animal instinct for staying out of other people's avalanches, and everything she knew about survival said turn around, go back to the caulking, let this hollow-eyed stranger drown in whatever he'd fished out of a dead man's pockets.

"Halloway," she said instead. "The night he died. I brought him brandy from the chandlery—you were sitting with him. I heard him talking. Half of it was fever nonsense, but he kept saying *upstream*. Kept saying *before the dry season*. Like he was giving orders to somebody."

"He was delirious."

"He was specific. Delirious men repeat themselves. Specific men mean it." She stepped closer, close enough to smell the river on him and something else underneath, woodsmoke and old sweat, the smell of a man who'd been sleeping rough long before this town. "So either you promised a dying stranger something, or you robbed a corpse. And I watched you sit with him for six hours holding a bowl when he couldn't lift his own hands, so I know which one my money's on."

"You don't know anything about me."

"No," she agreed. "That's the interesting part. Everything about you is wrong. Your boots are mountain boots with the hobnails ground off. Your rope is climbing line dressed up as dock line. You handle a corpse like it's cargo you're responsible for delivering. And when a consortium clerk asks your name, you give it easy as breathing—which no guilty man does, and no innocent man does either, unless the name itself is the lie." She let that sit. "Whoever you were before Kestrel's Landing, you paid a lot to stop being him."

For a moment she thought he'd walk away. His weight shifted toward the stairs, toward the river, toward anywhere else. Then he stopped, and when he spoke his voice had dropped into something rawer.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because those men are going to tear this landing apart until they find that map, and when they don't find it on the landing, they're going to start asking who else talked to Halloway. And I talked to Halloway. I handed him brandy. Which means I'm already in your story whether I like it or not, and I would very much like to know what story I'm in."

The river slapped the pilings below them. Somewhere upstream a weir gate groaned through its cycle, the consortium's machinery chewing the water on schedule, same as every day, same as forever.

"It's a map of the source," Nystrom said finally. "The actual source of the Vane. Above the restricted zone." He said it quickly, like paying out a rope hand over hand, wanting it done. "Halloway walked out of there. Whatever he saw, the consortium wants it buried, and they want buried anyone who knows it exists. That's the story you're in. There's no version of it that ends well for bystanders."

Sera looked at him for a long time.

She thought about her father, who had piloted freight up the middle river until the consortium's new license regime priced him out, and who had died still owing money to the company store. She thought about the villages upstream that appeared on no charts, whose fuel she sold at cost because nobody else would carry to them. She thought about the sealed signs on the weirs and the eleven years of pilots' gossip and the way the whole river bent around one forbidden stretch like water bending around a sunken hull.

She thought: *this is the moment. Right here. You can still walk back to the boat.*

"Sera Okonkwo," she said, and stuck out her hand. "I run the *Maggie V*. Freight, salvage, passengers, if the price is right." His grip was dry and hard and careful, a climber's grip. "I'm not promising you anything, Nystrom. I'm telling you that if you need to move something—or someone—upstream past the weirs, quietly, I know the channels better than the men who patrol them. And I know what their checkpoints actually check. That's not complicity. That's commerce. We can call it commerce."

"We can," he said. Something moved behind his eyes—relief, maybe, or its opposite, grief that she'd joined him in this. "Why?"

Because you caught a dying man like he mattered. Because you stood in the road and dared them. Because my father died owing money to these people and I have been small and boring for eleven years and it has bought me nothing but smallness.

"Call it curiosity," she said. "It's killed better pilots than me."

* * *

They came back at dusk, the way she'd predicted. Two of them this time, plus a local deputy rented for legitimacy, working the waterfront end of the landing while the clerk's voice drifted from the ferry house. Sera sat on the *Maggie V*'s gunwale in plain view, mending a net she didn't need mended, and watched them work down the moored boats one by one.

When they reached her, the taller one flashed his laminated badge. "Vane Consolidated. Searching for property of a deceased party. You seen anything? Papers, charts, a leather case?"

"On my boat?" She gestured at the *Maggie V*'s interior with pride, letting them look at the honest chaos of a working vessel—tar pots, chain, bait barrels, her father's old chart tubes stacked in the corner. The tall one poked at two of the tubes before his partner called him onward, impatient, the light going.

"Man who caught the prospector," the tall one said, lingering. "Nystrom. You know him?"

"Drifter," Sera said. "Took a trail job off Marta Voss last week. Keeps to himself." All true. She'd learned young that the best lies are inventory. "Why? He in trouble?"

"Not yet." The tall one smiled and moved on down the dock.

Sera sat with her useless net and listened to their footsteps fade, and felt the shape of what she'd just done settle over her like wet canvas. She'd lied to Vane Consolidated. Not by omission—by construction, cold and clean, with a straight face and a steady voice. Eleven years of staying small, spent in one sentence.

Below the dock, in the crawl space under the *Maggie V*'s stern where she kept spare lines wrapped in oilcloth, a flat leather case sat against the hull stringer, right where Nystrom had stowed it an hour ago with hands that shook only after it was hidden. She'd seen him do it. She'd stood watch while he did it, scanning the boardwalk, and neither of them had discussed it beforehand, and that frightened her more than the gray jackets did—how easily the two of them had fallen into it, like a crew that had sailed together for years instead of strangers who'd shared one conversation.

Complicity, she thought. Chosen in a heartbeat, before the heart even finished beating.

She coiled her net and looked upstream, where the last light was dying on the water and the sealed weirs ticked and groaned in the dark like something sleeping. Somewhere up there was a source the most powerful company in three provinces would burn a town to protect. And somewhere on her boat was the map to it, and somewhere in her chest was the beginning of a decision she hadn't officially made yet.

In the morning, she told herself, she'd think it through properly. Weigh the risk. Count the cost.

But she already knew she wouldn't. Because at dawn, when she went to check the bilge, Nystrom was crouched at the dock edge waiting for her, and the first thing he said was:

"The dry season ends in nineteen days. I need to be on the water tomorrow."
