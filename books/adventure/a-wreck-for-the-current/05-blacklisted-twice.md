## 5. Blacklisted Twice

The map stayed under the floorboard all day while Gideon worked, and still he felt it against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

He spent the morning hauling gravel for a widow named Prentiss who was rebuilding her walkway after the spring melt had chewed it apart. She paid him in coin and bread and didn't ask his name, which was the arrangement he preferred. By noon he'd moved to the tannery yard, stacking hides that stank of lime and old mistakes, and by late afternoon he was on the breakwater repairing storm damage to the mooring cleats because the harbormaster paid cash and never looked at your face longer than courtesy required.

That was the whole art of it. Never let anyone look too long.

He was good at it now. Six years of practice. He knew how to stand so that lantern light didn't catch the scar over his left eyebrow. He knew which questions to answer with a shrug and which to answer with a lie so boring no one bothered to test it. He had been Tam Voss, then Merrick, then just *the big one who works the docks*, and each name had worn smooth as river stone from use.

But tonight, back in his room above the chandlery with the lamp turned low, he pulled Halloway's map out from under the floorboard anyway, and the moment he unrolled it across the table, the old choreography failed him. Because the map was drawn in a hand that shook, and it led up the Vane River into country where no alias would hold, and somewhere between the landing and the source there would be checkpoints, and registries, and men whose job was to look at faces carefully.

Men like the ones who had ended his first life.

Gideon sat down slowly on the stool and let the memory come, because it was going to come whether he invited it or not. It always did this time of year—late summer, when the high passes were still holding their snow and the meltwater ran loud in every gully, when the mountains started talking again in the language that had ruined him.

* * *

Corren Pass, six years ago. He could still draw it blind: the saddle between the two massifs, the approach couloir, the serac field hanging off the eastern shoulder like a held breath. Nine clients. A support guide named Brandt. Perfect weather window, the kind you waited three seasons for.

Gideon had been twenty-six and already known as the best high-route man on that side of the range. He'd run Corren eleven times without losing so much as a finger. The consortium's expedition arm—the Vane Consolidated Mining & Water Company, though nobody called them that in those days; they were just *the Company*—had contracted him personally. Big fee. Big names on the client list. Investors and their sons, nine of them, flown in to stand on the shoulder glacier and toast the opening of the new survey road.

He remembered the morning of it with terrible clarity. The way the snowpack had sounded under his probe that dawn—hollow, a drum note where there should have been resistance. He remembered digging the pit and reading the layers and not liking what he read: a wind slab sitting on facets, loaded overnight, the whole slope cocked like a trigger.

He remembered calling it. Standing in the cold with his breath smoking and telling the expedition lead, a Company man named Rusk, that they were turning around. That the route through the serac field was dead until the pack settled or the temperature dropped.

And he remembered Rusk checking a watch that cost more than Gideon made in a year, and saying, "The investors have a schedule."

"The mountain doesn't," Gideon had said.

"Then find another way down, guide. That's what we pay you for."

That was the sentence. That was the sentence he'd carried for six years, turning it over at night like a stone with a sharp edge. *Find another way.* As if the mountain were a locked door and expertise a key, rather than a set of conditions you either respected or died inside of.

So he'd found another way. A lower line, longer, through the timbered bench below the couloir—slower, but out from under the seracs. He'd put himself at point and spaced the rope teams wide. And it might have worked. He believed, some nights, that it would have worked.

Except for the blast.

It came at 9:40 in the morning—he knew the time because he'd checked his watch when the sound reached them, a flat concussive *whump* rolling up the valley from the east, from the direction of the survey road. Not thunder. Thunder has a texture. This was powder charge, shaped and deliberate, the sound of men doing work.

He'd stopped dead on the bench and turned and looked up-slope, and what he saw in the four seconds before the world came apart was this: the crown of the slab fracturing above the couloir, a clean vertical seam ripping open along exactly the line his pit had warned him about, triggered not by their weight—they were two hundred meters lateral and forty below—but by the shockwave of the charge propagating upslope through the packed snow of the road cut.

The avalanche took the middle of the column. Teams two, three, and four. Six clients and Brandt, swallowed in the time it takes to say a name. Three more clients died in the debris field over the following hour despite everything Gideon and the survivors could do—buried too deep, buried wrong, buried in terrain so steep that probing it was its own hazard.

Nine dead. And Gideon Nystrom, who had called the turn, who had rerouted the column, who had put the weakest climbers in the safest positions available on a mountain that offered none—Gideon Nystrom stood in the wreckage holding a broken rope end and understood, even before anyone said anything, that someone was going to be blamed.

He'd assumed it would be Rusk. Rusk had overridden the safety call. Rusk had ordered the column onto the mountain against the guide's judgment. There were witnesses. There was Gideon's own written route assessment, filed two days prior, flagging the slab.

But when the inquiry report came down eight months later, it read like it described a different mountain on a different morning. In the official account, Gideon Nystrom had assessed the slope and cleared it. Had ignored standard protocol. Had routed the column beneath the serac field—*beneath the serac field*, the very thing he'd refused to do—for reasons of schedule pressure attributed to *the lead guide's eagerness to please his employers*. His written assessment was referenced once, in passing, as having been *amended verbally on the morning of the incident*. Amended. By whom, the report did not say. Rusk, when Gideon finally cornered him outside the inquiry office, had looked at him with something that wasn't quite apology and wasn't quite contempt and said, "You understand how these things have to go, son. The Company can't be liable. You're the licensed professional on site. It's unfortunate, but it's the shape of it."

"And the blast?" Gideon had asked. "There was a blast. Ask anyone who lived. Ask the road crew."

"There was no blast," Rusk said, mildly, the way a man corrects a child's arithmetic. "Weather event. Spontaneous release. It's all in the report."

Two weeks later Gideon's guiding license was revoked in his home country. A month after that, the neighboring republic—whose mining interests were entangled with the Company's in ways the newspapers only ever hinted at—banned him from entry entirely. Blacklisted twice, a dockhand in Kestrel's Landing had joked once, years later, not knowing how close to the bone the joke landed. Two countries, one profession, gone.

His father hadn't come to the inquiry. Hadn't answered the letters. Aldous Nystrom was already ten years gone by then into the upper levels of the Company, a man Gideon saw mostly in photographs—cutting ribbons, signing water compacts, standing beside governors with his hand extended and his face arranged into benevolence. Gideon had grown up in that house learning that his father's silences had architecture, that they were load-bearing, that you built your life around them the way a town builds around a fortress. But even so, even knowing all that, some part of him had waited for a letter. One letter. *I know what happened. I'm working on it.*

What came instead, eighteen months after the funerals, was a notice that his family name had been removed from the Company's employee benefit rolls at his own request—a request Gideon had never made—and after that, nothing. Silence with architecture. He'd stopped writing then. He'd taken the ferry to Kestrel's Landing with a duffel bag and a fake reference letter and become Tam Voss, and he had told himself the story was closed.

* * *

Except it wasn't closed. It had never been closed, and lying on his narrow bed with the lamp guttering, Gideon admitted it to himself plainly for the first time in years: the official story had never matched what he saw, and he had let it stand anyway, because fighting it meant becoming visible, and visibility meant the blacklist followed him everywhere, and survival had seemed like the only victory available.

Six years of letting it stand.

He got up and crossed to the window. Below, the landing lay quiet, lamplit along the waterfront, the Vane sliding past dark and heavy toward the sea. Somewhere upstream—days upstream, past the checkpoint weirs the Company kept manned and the stretches of river marked on no chart a pilot would sell you—was the source Halloway had bled to describe. And somewhere in the machinery of the Company, presumably, were the same men who had written the Corren report. Maybe Rusk himself, older now, better paid.

Here was the thought that had been circling him since dawn, and he finally let it land:

Halloway had come off a Company freight barge. Halloway had been prospecting in sealed country—Company country, patrolled, bought, closed. Halloway had been dying of something done to him, not something caught, if the burns on his hands meant anything, and Gideon had seen enough industrial accidents among dockworkers to make a rough guess. And the Company's men had torn through the landing looking for his effects within hours of his death, with a thoroughness that suggested orders from far upstream.

Which meant whatever Halloway had found, the Company wanted it badly enough to hunt a dying man's pockets across half a province.

And Gideon had it. Under his floorboard. Drawn in a shaking hand, with a promise attached.

He thought about the promise the way he thought about the pass: as terrain to be assessed honestly. He could stay invisible. He was good at it; it had kept him alive. He could sell the map, even—there were buyers downstream who paid for secrets, men with no love for the Company, and no one would ever trace it to Tam Voss the dockhand. Or he could burn it, and let the river keep its secret, and go on stacking hides and hauling gravel until his hands gave out.

Or he could go upstream, into the teeth of the registry system, carrying the one name in two countries he'd spent six years burying.

He pressed his palm flat against the floorboard, over the gap where the map slept.

"You asked the wrong man," he said aloud to the dead prospector, and heard how hollow it sounded, and hated that he couldn't tell whether it was a refusal or an excuse.

Outside, boots on the boardwalk—two sets, unhurried, stopping near the chandlery door. Gideon killed the lamp without thinking about it, an old reflex, and stood in the dark listening. Voices, low. Then a knock, not at his door but at the chandlery below, and the muffled exchange of men asking questions, and the specific apologetic tone of his landlord answering them.

They moved on. But they'd been asking about strangers, he was almost sure of it. About newcomers. About a sick man who'd come off the barge.

Gideon stood a long time in the dark with his hand on the floorboard, and the old injustice sat in his chest reopened like a wound that had never really closed, and he understood that the choice he'd been refusing since dawn was not actually a choice at all—it was just a question of how much it would cost, and when he'd pay.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he'd go find the river pilot with the scarred boat and the level eyes, and he'd ask her the question he'd sworn not to ask anyone.

Upstream. Past the weirs. All the way to the source—if she'd take him, and if the price wasn't his neck.

He lay back down and did not sleep, and above the chandlery the map waited under the floorboard like a debt accruing interest, and the Vane ran on in the dark toward the sea, patient as everything else that intended to have its way with him.
