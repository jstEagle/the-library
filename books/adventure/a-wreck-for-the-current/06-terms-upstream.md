## 6. Terms Upstream

Sera Okonkwo kept a ledger of debts, and she kept it in her head, which meant it was accurate in the way that mattered and merciless in the way that didn't. The ferry house owed her three days of engine work she'd done herself because the mechanic drank. The fishmonger owed her for the ice she'd fronted him through the warm week. And the man who called himself Corren—tall, quiet, hands like he'd once held something heavier than rope—owed her nothing yet, but had stood at her dock two mornings running, looking at her boat like a man pricing his own funeral.

She'd been watching him since Halloway died.

That was the thing about Kestrel's Landing: you learned people by what they did when nobody was arranging to be seen. Sera ran cargo up and down the Vane twelve years, and she could read a riverbank the way other women read faces. The landing was her face. She knew which men drank to forget and which drank to rehearse. She knew that the one called Corren never drank at all, that he walked the high bank every evening at dusk like a man checking escape routes, and that when the consortium's men came through tearing open floorboards looking for a dead prospector's effects, this drifter with no history had looked at her—one look, flat and measuring—and she had lied for him without knowing why.

*He's not staying with me,* she'd told them. *Haven't seen him.* And the whole time her eyes had gone, against her own will, to the woodpile behind the ferry house where a canvas roll sat wrapped in oilcloth, and the consortium man had followed her eyes exactly as far as the woodpile and no further, because she'd put just enough boredom in her voice to make lying seem like a chore rather than a craft.

She still didn't know why she'd done it. That bothered her more than the lie itself.

Now it was the third morning, and the fog hadn't burned off the Vane yet, and Corren was standing at the foot of her gangplank with his hat in his hands like a man about to ask a father for a daughter.

"Okonkwo." He said her name carefully, like he'd practiced it. Most people got it wrong on purpose or didn't bother.

"Corren." She stayed on the deck of the *Marrow*, coiling a line that didn't need coiling. "You want something. Men who want nothing don't stand in fog."

"I want to go upstream."

"Everyone wants to go upstream. Nobody pays to go upstream. Upstream is where the money comes from, not goes."

"Past the weirs."

The coil stopped moving. Sera set it down and gave him her full attention, which she rationed like fuel. Past the weirs meant past the checkpoint at Miller's Bend, past the second one at the old toll island, into water the consortium had closed to licensed traffic two years back—closed with paper and patrols and, according to the stories that came downstream with the log drives, less polite methods. Her license covered the lower river. Her life expectancy covered slightly more.

"You're asking me to lose my ticket," she said. "My ticket is my name on this river. You know what happens to pilots who run closed water?"

"They stop being pilots."

"They stop being anything. They get a hearing they're not allowed to attend, and then their name goes in a book, and after that no dock will take their line and no insurer will write their hull." She leaned on the rail. "I've heard of a guide like that, actually. Mountain man. Corren Pass. You'd maybe know the story, being a drifter type. They say he killed nine people with his own ambition."

Something moved behind his eyes—not flinching, she decided later. More like a door closing very quietly so as not to wake the house.

"I've heard the story," he said.

"Everybody has. Consortium made sure of it. Funny how their version always travels fastest." She let that sit. "So. Past the weirs. How far?"

"The source of the Vane."

Sera laughed. It came out harder than she intended. "Nobody knows the source of the Vane. That's the point of the source of the Vane. That's why there are fences."

"Halloway knew."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. She watched him decide not to pick it up.

"Halloway's dead," she said.

"Yes."

"And whatever he knew went into the ground with him, or into somebody's pocket, and either way it's not worth my ticket."

"It's worth more than your ticket." He said it without heat, which was worse. "It's worth more than this landing, probably. I can't tell you what it is. I'll tell you this much: Halloway spent eleven years walking toward it, and the consortium spent eleven years making sure the walking was hard, and neither of them did all that for gravel."

Sera looked past him at the fog peeling off the water. Twelve years on this river. She knew its moods the way she knew her own pulse—the brown sulks of spring, the glassy lies of August, the way the middle reaches narrowed at the narrows and the current went from friend to interrogator in the space of a bend. She'd told herself a hundred times that the river was neutral ground, that a pilot ferries whoever pays and asks nothing else. But the consortium's men had come through her landing with crowbars, and she'd lied, and the lie had felt less like a choice than like a reflex—a body remembering something the mind hadn't agreed to yet.

"What's your real name?" she asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters whether you have one. Men with no names are either running from something or toward something, and the second kind pay better but die louder."

He almost smiled. It was the first time she'd seen anything on his face that wasn't weather. "Running," he said. "Toward now, maybe. Both."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They negotiated on the deck of the *Marrow* while the fog finished dying, and Sera discovered that bargaining with Corren was like poling through deadwater—every push met a push back, and nothing gave until suddenly everything did.

Her first number was designed to end the conversation. Passage past both weirs to the head of navigation, plus the portage fees, plus hazard pay for a hull she might lose to patrol rammers or rocks or the simple bad luck of being noticed. She named a figure that would buy her boat twice over and a new life somewhere the consortium's books didn't reach.

He didn't blink. "Half now."

"There's no half. It's—"

"Half now, half at the source. If we don't make the source, you keep the half and I walk home." He set a canvas bag on the hatch cover. It landed with the specific dense sound of coin, not paper. "Count it if you want."

She wanted to. She made herself not want to, and then counted it anyway, because trust was for people with margin. The coins were old mint, mixed provinces, the kind of money a man accumulates when he's been paid in cash under names that aren't his. There was enough in the bag to make her first number look reasonable and her second number look greedy, and she understood, watching him watch her count, that he'd known roughly what she'd ask and had brought enough to make the haggling short.

"Where does a trail hand get this kind of money?" she asked.

"Trail hands get paid when the job finishes. This job finished badly, years ago. Somebody finally settled up."

"That's not an answer either."

"You keep asking questions I'm not going to answer. We should establish that now, so it doesn't feel personal later."

Sera sat down on the hatch cover next to the money, which was a power move and also because her knees wanted it. "Here's what I need to hear before I say yes," she said. "Not what you're carrying. Not who you are. Just this: if the consortium comes alongside us midriver and puts a gun on my gunwale, do I hand them you and go home whole? Or am I already in it past that?"

He considered the question with the seriousness she was starting to suspect he brought to everything, including tying his boots.

"If they come alongside," he said, "they won't be asking for me. They'll be asking for what Halloway carried. And if you give them that, they'll let you go, and then they'll burn the record of your license anyway, because you'll have seen their faces doing a thing they don't want written down." He paused. "You're already in it. You were in it the morning you lied about the woodpile. I'm sorry. I watched you do it and I didn't stop you, and that's the worst thing I've done this month."

It was the closest thing to an apology she'd get, and the fact that he knew it was the worst thing he'd done this month—that he ranked his sins, that this small complicity of hers weighed on him—did more to move her than the money had.

"One condition," she said. "My boat, my rules on the water. When I say tie off, you tie off. When I say jump, you're already wet. I've run this river longer than you've been whatever you used to be, and I will not drown for a dead man's errand because my passenger thinks mountains qualify him for water."

"Agreed."

"And another condition. Whatever's in that map—and it's a map, don't bother denying it, Halloway talked in his fever at the ferry house and I heard the word 'source' twice—if it turns out to be gold, or a claim, or anything you mean to keep for yourself, you tell me now, because I'll help you steal it but I won't help you lie about stealing it. There's a difference and I live on it."

He looked at her for a long moment. Fog light, gray eyes, a face built for not being remembered.

"It's not gold," he said. "I'd tell you if it were. What it is—I haven't fully read it myself yet. But Halloway believed it belonged to everyone the river touches, and I promised him I'd carry it upstream, and I intend to keep that promise even if keeping it costs more than either of us has priced."

"A promise to a corpse."

"The only kind that holds," he said. "A living man renegotiates."

* * *

They cast off at noon, because Sera refused to leave before noon on principle—morning departures were for smugglers and fools, and she was licensed for one of those at most. The *Marrow* took the current with her usual heavy grace, forty feet of patched steel and stubbornness, and Kestrel's Landing fell away behind them boardwalk by boardwalk.

At the last bend, where the town showed itself as a smear of roofs and smoke, Gideon Nystrom—for she had decided privately to think of him by no name at all, since the ones on offer were clearly rented—stood at the bow and did not look back at the landing. She noted that. People who don't look back are either free or hunted, and the money in her strongbox argued for hunted.

"Miller's Bend by dark," she called forward. "Weirs tomorrow. Sleep now, because after the first checkpoint you'll want to be awake for everything, and I mean everything. The guards count gulls. If our gull count is wrong, they'll know we're carrying more than ballast."

"How do you know the gull count?"

"Because I invented it. Three years ago I started reporting gulls in my manifests. Nobody corrected me. Now it's tradition." She permitted herself the day's first real smile. "The consortium is very good at violence and very bad at attention. We're going to spend the next hundred miles living inside that difference."

He came aft and sat across from her, and for a while neither spoke, and the river did the talking—water over gravel, the tick of the current finding the channel markers, the far-off complaint of some machine upstream that might have been a dredge or might have been a warning.

"Okonkwo," he said finally. "Why did you lie for me? At the landing. You said yourself you didn't know why."

She watched the water a while before answering, because the honest answer was embarrassing and the dishonest answer was easy, and she'd promised herself on the dock that this arrangement would run on a different fuel than most.

"Because when they were tearing up the floorboards," she said, "you were the only man on the landing who looked afraid for someone besides himself. Everyone else was counting their own boards. You were watching Halloway's room like the room still had a tenant." She shrugged. "I've piloted liars, thieves, one cannibal, and two tax collectors. I know what fear looks like when it's about other people. It's rare. It's usually load-bearing. I figured a man built like that was worth more upright than broken."

He absorbed that the way he seemed to absorb everything—completely, silently, with some internal accounting she couldn't see.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I lied to you too. At the negotiation. Not about the money or the map. About how far past the source this goes. It goes further. For both of us, it goes further, and I knew that when I asked, and I asked anyway."

"I know," Sera said. "I priced it anyway too."

The fog had long since burned away, but the river ahead bent north into country neither of them pretended to know, and somewhere up that bend, at Miller's Bend specifically, a weir waited with a guard who liked to study faces, and a manifest waiting with three gulls on it, and a story waiting to hold together for one more day.

Two liars, one boat, and a hundred miles of borrowed truth.

Sera put the wheel over and drove them into the bend.
