## 7. The First Weir

The permits were Sera's idea, but the forgery was mine.

I'd learned the trade in another life, back when I was still someone whose signature opened doors. A mountain guide's paperwork is a small bureaucracy of its own—route filings, insurance bonds, client waivers, salvage claims for gear abandoned on the ice—and I had filled out thousands of them in a hand so clean that registrars used to compliment it. That was before my name became a thing I couldn't put on paper at all.

So I sat in the stern of Sera's boat, moored in the reeds two miles below the first weir, and built us a company out of nothing.

"Name it something dull," she said, watching me work with her arms crossed and one boot up on the gunwale. "Dull is armor. If you write *Vane Recovery Syndicate* they'll want to see a charter. Write *Harrow & Sons Salvage* and they'll want to go back to sleep."

"Harrow," I said. "After Halloway?"

"After nothing. It's a boring word." She paused. "But sure. Let's make it a memorial. He'd have laughed."

I wrote *Harrow & Sons, Licensed Salvage and Recovery, Kestrel's Landing*, and beneath it the permit number of a real outfit that had gone under three years ago when its owner drank himself into the Vane during spring melt. The registry office never purged lapsed numbers—nobody downstream ever checked, because nobody upstream was supposed to be going there anyway. That was the whole architecture of the consortium's river: not walls, but the assumption that no one would bother.

I forged the countersignature of a licensing clerk whose hand I remembered from better days, dated it eleven months back so it looked like a renewal, and added the one detail that mattered: a salvage claim on "derelict barge wreckage, upper middle Vane, above Gravel Bar Weir." Salvage men going up after scrap was the oldest story on the river. Nobody liked them. Everybody believed in them, because everybody had seen a derelict half-sunk somewhere and wondered who'd profit off it.

Sera read it over twice. "Your clerk writes a very confident R."

"He was paid to."

"You've done this before."

"I've done paperwork before. This is paperwork with ambition." I handed it across. "You're Harrow. I'm your hired muscle. Muscle doesn't talk."

"And what does muscle do if they ask it questions?"

"Muscle looks bored."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, because we both heard it at the same time—the low industrial groan of the weir's winch machinery carrying down the water, faint as weather. Two miles away and we could hear it working. Whatever stood at Gravel Bar wasn't a checkpoint. It was an installation.

We cast off in the last grey hour before dawn, running dark, engine at a murmur, because Sera wanted to arrive rather than sneak. "Boats that run dark get boarded," she said. "Boats that come in slow with their lights on get waved through. Fear smells different from boredom. Give them boredom."

The river narrowed as we went up. The banks stepped out of marsh into gravel bar, gravel bar into cutbank, cutbank into the first shoulders of real hills, and the water went from brown to a hard, cold green that I hadn't seen since Corren Pass. My hands remembered that color. I kept them busy coiling line.

Then the lights came around the bend and there it was.

Gravel Bar Weir was a steel comb laid across the whole width of the Vane—a gate of vertical bars set in a concrete spine, with a navigable gap in the middle span just wide enough for one hull, controlled by a chain hoist. Behind it, floodlights on poles turned the water white. A low blockhouse squatted on the left bank with a radio mast and a fuel drum, and two men stood on the walkway over the gap, rifles slung loose the way men carry them when they're allowed to use them.

Sera killed our headlamp and lit the single stern lantern instead, the old pilot's courtesy signal, and brought us in toward the gap at a crawl.

"Evening," she called up, before they could call down. Voice flat, tired, mildly annoyed—the voice of a woman who had done this forty times and found it tedious thirty-nine of those times. "Harrow and Sons, salvage. Got a claim on some wrecked barge sections above you. Permit's current, if your boys downstream filed it right, which—" she let it hang, let them fill in the complaint themselves.

The younger guard leaned over the rail. The older one didn't move. He stood back from the light with his hood up, and I understood immediately that he was the one who mattered.

"Papers," the older man said.

Sera held up the folder. The younger guard came down the ladder to take it, boots ringing on the wet steel, and carried it back up, and the older guard read it under a lamp while the river pushed gently against the closed gate and the floodlights made everything look staged, like a play about a river instead of a river.

I stood in the stern with my arms crossed and my face arranged into the expression of a man being paid by the hour. It's a real skill, that arrangement. You let your jaw go slack. You let your eyes settle on nothing—the middle distance, the water, the rivet pattern on the gate. You do not look at the rifles. You do not look at the faces. Boredom is armor; Sera was right about that too.

The older guard read the permit twice. Then he did the thing I was afraid of, the thing I'd been afraid of since Kestrel's Landing, since the day I took Halloway's weight off that dock.

He looked up from the paper and studied my face.

Not a glance. Not the flick every guard gives every passenger. He looked at me the way a man reads a document—top to bottom, features in order, hairline to jaw, the way you'd compare a face to a description or a photograph. The floodlight was full on me. There was nowhere to put my face except where it was.

"Who's this?" he said.

"Crew," Sera said. "Rigger. Doesn't talk much."

"They usually don't." The guard came along the walkway toward the ladder, unhurried, and the younger one drifted after him, and I felt Sera shift her weight at the wheel—a small movement, nothing, a woman adjusting her stance—but I knew what the adjustment meant. She was putting herself between the throttle and the question.

The guard stopped at the bottom of the ladder, one rung above the deck, which put him looking slightly down at me, and I gave him the bored stare and he gave me the reading stare and between us the river ran cold and green through the bars of the gate.

"Where'd you work before this?" he asked me directly.

I shrugged. It's amazing how much a shrug can hold. "Around."

"Around where?"

"Wherever the wrecks are." I let a little irritation into it, just enough. "Look, you want to check my references, talk to Okonkwo. I haul what she points at. That's the job."

Something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly. Recognition trying to find its footing. I watched him reach for it, watched him turn the problem over: where do I know this face from? And I knew exactly where he could know it from. Every consortium office from here to the coast had a wall with notices on it, and somewhere on those walls, ten years old now, sun-faded, corners curling, was a photograph of a younger man with the same jaw and the same eyes, taken the week after Corren Pass, when Aldous Nystrom needed a face to attach to the blame.

The photograph was old. The notice had probably been reprinted a dozen times. But faces are patient things. They wait inside other faces.

"Name?" the guard said.

And here was the moment the whole plan balanced on, because Sera's forgery listed crew as *T. Harrow, rigger*, and if I said Harrow in a certain way, carelessly, the way a man says his own fake name when he's worn it long enough to stop flinching at it—

"Harrow," I said. "Like the boat."

"Like the boat," he repeated.

"Family outfit," Sera said from the wheel, dry as gravel. "His father runs it. You know how it is—you hire one relative, suddenly the whole payroll's cousins."

That landed. Guards are men with families; family jokes are the softest spot on any uniform. The younger one snorted. The older one didn't laugh, but the recognition in his face lost its footing, slid sideways, became something smaller: the vague sense of having met a thousand men who all looked like other men.

He handed the folder down to me instead of Sera, which I took as a test, and I took it without hurrying, without gratitude, the way muscle takes paper.

"Gate opens at first light for commercial traffic," he said. "You'll wait."

"We'll wait," Sera said.

"There's a fee. Salvage class, that's eight marks a head plus cargo declaration."

Sera counted coins out of her jacket with theatrical slowness, and the younger guard collected them, and the older one went back to the blockhouse, and once—he did it only once, at the door, before the light took him—he glanced back at me across the water.

I gave him nothing. I gave him the whole empty harbor of my face, and he went inside, and the door shut, and I let my breath out so slowly it made no sound at all.

* * *

We tied up below the weir to wait out the night, because running the gate at first light among a queue of real barges was safer than being the only hull in the floodlights at three in the morning. Sera rigged a tarp over the cockpit and heated coffee on the single burner, and for a long time neither of us spoke. The weir hummed. Somewhere upstream, water fell through the gate bars in a sound like slow applause.

"He knew me," I said finally. "Or he almost did."

"Almost is a country you don't want to live in."

"I've lived there ten years."

She poured the coffee and handed my cup across without comment, and then, because she was Sera, she skipped straight past comfort to tactics. "If he radios ahead, what do they have? Description?"

"Height, build, maybe the jaw. Maybe nothing. He didn't radio anything while we sat there—I watched the mast. If he sends anything tonight it'll be a routine traffic log. *Salvage vessel, two crew, cleared for morning.*" I turned the cup in my hands. "Unless the face comes back to him at two in the morning and sits up in his bunk."

"Then we're through the gate before he's through his coffee."

"That's the plan."

"It's a bad plan."

"It's the only plan shaped like a plan."

She looked at me across the burner flame, and the light did something honest to her face—showed the tiredness under the competence, the calculation under the calm. "You understand what happens if that man remembers. It's not a chase. They don't chase people on this river. They close it. Gate behind us, gate ahead of us, and we're cargo."

"I know."

"Do you? Because back at the landing you talked like a man delivering a package. Now you're talking like a man carrying one. Those are different weights, Gideon."

It was the first time she'd said my name since the landing—my real one, given in the reeds two nights ago when the terms were struck, spoken now like a tool she was testing the edge of. I hadn't decided yet whether I trusted her with it. I had decided I didn't have a choice, and sometimes those are the same thing.

"The weight's the same," I said. "I'm just done pretending it isn't heavy."

She accepted that, or appeared to, and we split the watch. I took midnight to four. The weir lights burned all night, white and sourceless, and the queue of morning barges began assembling below us around three—fuel scows, a gravel barge, a fish carrier with nets drying on its rails—all of them waiting for the gate like cattle at a fence, all of them bored, all of them invisible. I looked at them and understood Sera's whole doctrine in one image: the river doesn't hide you. Crowds hide you. Be one more dull thing.

At first light the winch started, and the gate rose out of the water dripping, and the queue began to move.

* * *

Going through the gap was ninety seconds of engineered humiliation. A guard on the walkway—not the older one, mercifully, a third man I hadn't seen—checked our papers again against a clipboard, waved us into the slot, and the current grabbed the hull and shouldered us through the bars while Sera feathered the throttle and I fended off the steel with a boathook, the metal humming under my hands. On the far side the river opened out and the light changed and the weir dropped behind us into its own noise.

For six minutes I let myself believe it.

Six minutes of green water and opening hills, of Sera easing the throttle up until the bow lifted, of the checkpoint shrinking in our wake until it was just a smudge of floodlight drowned in morning. Six minutes of the specific relief that comes when tension lets go all at once—the body's stupid, dangerous gift, telling you the danger is over because the danger has passed out of sight.

Then Sera said, quietly, "Don't relax."

"Why?"

"Because he's on the bank."

I turned. Far downstream, tiny with distance, a figure stood on the cutbank above the weir—the older guard, hood down now, watching us go. Not doing anything. Not signaling, not raising a rifle, not running for the radio hut. Just standing there in the early light, a small dark shape against the gravel, and even at that distance I could feel the attention coming off him like heat off a stove.

Watching us the way you watch a face you haven't finished reading.

"He's not calling it in," Sera said. "Why isn't he calling it in?"

"Because he's not sure." I kept my eyes on the figure until the river bent and took it away. "A wrong report costs a man his post. He's gambling that if he's right, we'll give him proof."

"What kind of proof?"

"The kind where we run." I looked upstream, where the Vane threaded north between hills that were getting higher and harder, toward rapids I couldn't see yet and hunters I could imagine perfectly. "So we don't run. We're salvage men. We putter. We stop and inspect imaginary wreckage. We are the most boring thing this river has ever carried."

Sera pushed the throttle up anyway—not fleeing speed, just working speed, a hull with a schedule—and the banks began to slide.

"Grinder's a day and a half up," she said. "After that the water gets serious. If your friend on the bank makes his mind up, he won't come alone and he won't come slow."

"Then we make the Grinder before anyone makes up their mind."

She glanced at me, and for the first time since the landing there was no calculation in it at all—just the flat appraisal of one professional measuring another. "You've run whitewater, rigger?"

"I've run icefalls," I said. "Water's just rock that learned to move."

Ahead of us the river bent north into the hills, and somewhere on the bank behind, a man who had almost placed my face was reaching for a telephone.
