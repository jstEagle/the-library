## 8. Rapids Called the Grinder

I heard it before I saw it.

That's the thing about the Vane that nobody who's only read about her understands. The bad water announces itself a full bend ahead of time—a low grinding hum that comes up through the hull and into your boots and settles somewhere behind your sternum. The rivermen call it the Grinder's voice. The old pilots used to say if you could hear the Grinder singing, you were already late.

I'd run this stretch twice before, years ago, back when the consortium still let licensed traffic through the middle river. Both times I'd portaged around it. The Grinder wasn't a rapid you ran. It was a rapid you respected from a distance, the way you respect a grizzly sow with cubs or a widow with a shotgun.

Now I was running it in a patched salvage boat with a forged permit, a passenger who claimed to be a trail hand, and half a ton of cargo that had no business being on this river at all.

"You hear that?" I called over my shoulder.

Gideon Nystrom—or whatever his name was—stood amidships with one hand on the cargo netting. He'd been quiet most of the morning, watching the banks with that flat attention of his, cataloguing things. He'd been quiet all night too, taking second watch without being asked, which I'd noted and filed under *suspicious* along with everything else about him: the way he moved like his joints had been reassembled by someone in a hurry, the rope burns scarred into both palms, the fact that he'd paid my outrageous price in cash without blinking and then haggled me down on the price of coffee like a man who understood exactly what money was worth because he'd once had none.

"I hear it," he said.

"Then you know we're committed. There's no eddy big enough to pull out below that bluff." I jerked my chin at the rock wall sliding past on our left—black basalt, forty feet of it, streaked with the high-water marks of springs gone by. "We're in the throat in about four minutes."

"What do you need from me?"

The question surprised me. Most passengers asked *what should I do*, which was a way of saying *please take care of this for me*. Gideon asked what I needed, which was a way of saying *give me a job and get out of my way while I do it.*

"Sit down," I said. "Strap yourself to something solid. And whatever happens, don't touch the wheel."

"And the cargo?"

"The cargo is the whole reason we're alive to have this conversation." Half the value of Halloway's map was the paper it was drawn on; the other half was the survey instruments and sealed samples riding in those crates, and I'd promised myself when I took this job that I'd deliver both the man and the manifest or neither. "If she swamps, the crates float longer than we will. They're cork-lined."

"They'll also scatter."

"They'll also scatter," I agreed. "So let's not swamp."

He sat down against the main thwart, clipped himself in with a carabiner I hadn't known he was carrying, and went still. Not tense-still. Ready-still. I'd seen that quality before, in old pilots and in soldiers on their way home, and every time I'd seen it, it had meant the same thing: this person has been in disaster before and has made peace with the possibility of another one.

It did not make me feel better. It made me feel like I'd hired a man who expected my boat to sink.

* * *

The Grinder earned its name honestly. The river narrowed there to less than half its width, funneled between two shelves of submerged rock that nobody had ever mapped properly because mapping them would require standing in the middle of them, and the whole volume of the upper Vane came through that gap at once, confused and furious. The surface didn't have waves so much as it had arguments—water shoving against water, rising up in pyramids that collapsed sideways, holes opening and closing like mouths. And underneath all of it, the sound: stone rolling against stone, the river chewing its own bed.

I'd scouted it from the bluff on my last trip through, six years ago. The line, as best anyone had ever worked one out, was left of center, threading between the first shelf and the bank, then a hard ferry right across the boil to avoid the second shelf, then pray.

Six years was a long time for a river to rearrange its furniture.

"Left of center," I said, more to myself than to him. "Then right. Hard right."

"Copy," Gideon said, and then, because he was apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone: "Your bow's sitting heavy. You trim it?"

I glanced down. He was right—the nose was riding two inches low, the crates stacked forward where I'd loaded them for balance on flat water. In the Grinder, a heavy bow meant the river would grab it and steer for me, and the river's idea of steering was straight into the biggest hole available.

"It'll hold."

"It'll pivot." He said it without heat, the way you'd tell someone their fly was undone. "Move two crates aft and you'll buy yourself a boat that answers instead of argues."

Every instinct I had as a pilot bristled. This was my boat, my river, my four minutes of dying if it went wrong. But the hum was building behind my sternum, and the mathematics of it were simple: a heavy bow in that water was a coin flip, and I hadn't survived twenty years on the Vane by liking coin flips.

I killed the throttle, dropped the nose into the eddy behind the bluff's last spur, and moved two crates aft. It cost us ninety seconds. The Grinder's voice got louder the whole time, patient as tax.

"Better?" I said, when we came back into the current.

"Better," he said. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just be right." I fed the throttle back in. "Because in about three minutes, either you are or we're both going to find out what the inside of this river looks like."

* * *

The entry, when it came, came fast.

One moment we were in green moving water with the black bluff sliding past; the next the river simply gave up pretending to be navigable. The surface broke apart into chaos. My hands were moving before my mind caught up—left stroke, feather, throttle down to half, thread the gap—and the first shelf rose off our port side like the back of a sleeping animal, brown water peeling over it in a smooth glassy curve that would have swallowed us without a burp.

Through the gap. The hull slammed a trough, jumped, slammed again. Spray came over the gunwale in sheets. Something in the engine compartment rattled loose and I made a note to hate it later, if there was a later.

Then the boil.

This was the part I'd remembered wrong, or the part the river had rebuilt. The crossing to starboard was supposed to be a hard ferry across churning but buoyant water. What was actually there was a seam—a visible line where two currents met and sheared against each other, and along that seam the water stood up in a ragged wall maybe three feet high, folding over on itself, and beyond the seam the downstream shelf waited with its white tongue licking out into the channel.

If we hit the seam wrong, the river would pick a side for us. The wrong side was the shelf.

"Ferry right!" I shouted, though he hadn't asked, though he was strapped to a thwart with nothing to do but watch. Some things you shout anyway. I cranked the wheel and brought the nose up into the current and felt the boat start to slide sideways across the boil—

And felt the river take the bow.

It happened the way these things always happen: too fast to think, slow enough to see every piece of it. A pyramid of water rose under the starboard bow—I'd moved the crates aft, bought us a responsive boat, and the river responded by lifting our nose like a dog picking up a stick—and slewed us thirty degrees off my line, and suddenly we weren't crossing the seam, we were running *down* it, parallel, being carried toward the shelf with all the unhurried certainty of a conveyor belt.

"No no no—" I had the throttle wide open and it wasn't enough. Power doesn't beat mass. The Vane had more mass than anything I'd ever argue with.

The shelf was fifty yards away. Then forty. The white tongue of it curled and smacked, curling and smacking, and I knew exactly what it would do to us: broach us, roll us, fold the hull around its own spine, and feed the pieces to the hole below.

Thirty yards.

"Sera." Gideon's voice, calm as a weather report. "Rope bag. Where?"

"Bow locker! Why—"

"Throw me a line. Now."

There was no time to ask questions, which was fortunate, because I didn't have any good ones. I wrenched the wheel to keep our angle honest, ducked a wall of spray, and clawed the bow locker open one-handed. The throw bag came out trailing its coil. I slung it aft and he caught it out of the air without looking, the way you catch something you've caught ten thousand times.

What he did next, I'm going to describe as best I can, because I've told this story a hundred times since and people always think I'm embellishing.

He clipped the bag's rope to his carabiner, unclipped himself from the thwart, and went forward on his belly along the gunwale like a man crawling out onto a ledge. The boat was pitching hard enough to break teeth. He moved across it the way water moves—finding the low spots, flowing into them, never fighting the motion, only using it. At the bow he wrapped the rope twice around the forward cleat, bit the tag end free, and stood up.

Stood up. In the Grinder. On the deck of a thirty-foot salvage boat being carried sideways toward a rock shelf, and the man stood up like he was stepping off a porch.

"Angle her bow-left!" he shouted. "Two points! Trust the rope!"

I didn't understand it. I want to be honest about that. Two points bow-left pointed us *at* the seam, at the worst water in the whole rapid, and every cell in my body screamed that the line was dead ahead into the fold. But he had a rope cleated to my bow and a stance like he'd grown out of the deck, and there was no version of continuing straight that ended anywhere except the shelf.

I trusted him. God help me, with thirty yards of white tongue coming up, I turned the wheel left.

The seam hit us.

The boat bucked, buried its nose, and for one endless second we were submarine—green water over the windshield, my hands frozen on a wheel that had gone weightless—and then the rope came tight.

That's the part that saved us. Not power, not luck, not the river's mercy, because the river doesn't have any. Rope. When the bow punched through the seam and the current grabbed the whole hull to sweep it down onto the shelf, Gideon's line—anchored to the cleat, anchored to his harness, anchored to a man whose center of gravity sat about a foot above the deck and who leaned back into the load with his whole body, knees bent, hips low, the exact posture of someone who has held falling climbers on the end of a rope and knows precisely how much a human frame can take—when that rope came tight, it checked the swing. Not stopped it. Checked it. Took the energy of the river's shove and bled it off in a long controlled arc, swinging our bow through the seam instead of along it, converting momentum into rotation the way a pendulum converts a fall into a swing.

The boat came around. The shelf slid past our starboard side close enough that I could have spat on it, close enough that the backwash sucked at the hull and made the whole boat groan, and then we were through—through the seam, through the boil, into the churning but honest water below, and the Grinder's voice fell away behind us like a door closing.

I ran us out into the pool below before I let go of the wheel. My hands were shaking so badly I had to peel them off the spokes finger by finger.

Gideon coiled the rope with quick, economical turns, stowed the bag in the locker, clipped himself back into the thwart, and sat down.

"That," I said, when I could produce words, "is not trail-hand rope-work."

"No," he agreed.

"That is *alpine* rope-work. That is belay-stance, load-transfer, dynamic-catch rope-work. That is the kind of thing they teach on glaciers." I throttled down and turned to look at him properly for the first time since the entry. He was soaked to the skin, gray-faced, breathing carefully through his nose the way people breathe when they're managing pain or fear or both, and his hands—the ones with the old rope burns—were perfectly steady. "Who are you?"

"A man who owes you a clean run to the source."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got today." He nodded at the crates. "Check your cargo. If the seam shifted anything, better to know now."

I wanted to press. Every instinct I had said press, now, while his guard was down and his pulse was still up. But he was right about the cargo, and the river had just taught me the day's lesson: trust the rope in front of you, ask questions at the eddy.

The crates were intact. One had shifted and cracked a corner against the thwart; I'd deal with that at camp. The bilge had taken maybe eight inches of water. I ran the pump and watched the level drop and let the adrenaline drain out of me the slow way, through my hands, which wouldn't stop trembling for a quarter mile.

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar below the rapid, in the lee of a driftwood pile big enough to be a landmark. I built the fire because it was my boat and my firewood, and he cooked because I ordered him to, and somewhere between the beans and the second cup of coffee I found myself talking, which I almost never do.

"I portaged the Grinder twice," I said. "Both times I had crews. Good ones. Licensed men with ten seasons apiece. And both times I looked at that water and thought, no line through there is worth the gamble." I poked the fire. "Today I ran it with one passenger and a rope trick I've never seen, and we came through cleaner than I've ever come through anything. So either the river's changed, or I have, or you're not what you say you are. Those are the options."

"Could be all three."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Be reasonable at me. It's infuriating." I threw a stick at him. He caught it, which was somehow worse. "You climbed onto the deck of my boat in the worst rapid on the middle Vane and performed a maneuver I couldn't name if I had a week and a textbook. Men don't learn that on trails. Men learn that tied to a glacier with somebody's life hanging off their harness. So here's what I know: you were a guide. A real one. Mountain work. And something ended it, and you paid cash for this trip because you can't use your real credentials anymore, and you flinched at the checkpoint guard like a man who's been looked at too closely before." I leaned forward. "I'm not asking for the story. I'm telling you I've noticed there is one."

For a long moment he watched the fire. The light moved on his face and made him look younger and older at once, the way fire does.

"Halloway picked me because of it," he said finally. "Whatever I was. He said I was the only man at the Landing who'd ever get that map upstream, and he wasn't talking about my charm." He looked up at me. "You're right. There's a story. It's long, and it isn't mine alone to tell, and parts of it would change how you see this trip. When I can give it to you, I will. That's the best trade I can make tonight."

Most men would have given me a lie. Lies are cheap and smooth and easy to swallow on a gravel bar after a near-drowning. He'd given me a debt instead, marked and dated, and something in me—the part that had spent twenty years reading water and men and knowing the difference between a rock that looks solid and one that holds—recognized the currency.

"All right," I said. "But I'm keeping a ledger."

"I'd expect nothing less."

I rolled into my bedroll and stared at the stars through the driftwood branches and listened to the Grinder humming faintly upstream, still chewing its rocks, indifferent to all of us. Somewhere in the dark, Gideon turned over, and I heard him check the rope bag's position by touch—an unconscious little movement, a man confirming his tools were where his hands expected them.

A guide. A real one. Broken somehow, and paying cash.

I revised my estimate of my passenger upward by a wide margin, and then, because I was raised honest even when I wasn't, I revised my estimate of my own luck downward. Men with stories like his didn't pay outrageous prices for river runs. They paid outrageous prices because the price was the cheapest thing about the trip.

I was almost asleep when I heard it—a sound from the far bank, thin across the water. Not an animal. Animals don't move in rhythm.

A lantern flickered once among the trees on the eastern shore, maybe half a mile down, and went dark again. Deliberately. The way a man dims a light when he realizes he's showing his position.

I lay very still and listened to the river, and for the first time since the Landing, I wondered whether the hardest water on this trip would turn out to be the kind you couldn't run.
