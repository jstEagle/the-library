## 9. Hunters on the Bank

The river changed its mind about us an hour past the Grinder.

I'd been dozing against the cargo netting, one hand on Halloway's map where it rode flat against my chest inside the jacket, when Sera's voice cut through the engine drone—not loud, but pitched in a way that made my eyes open before my brain caught up.

"Gideon. Left bank. Don't turn your head."

I didn't. I kept my gaze on the water ahead, on the brown seam where current met current, and let my peripheral vision do the work. The left bank rose in a shelf of grey shale above a gravel bar, and along the top of that shelf, three figures were moving.

Not walking. *Tracking*—the way I'd once moved across a glacier face with a client's life hanging off my rope. Heads down, spaced wide, reading ground. One of them stopped and crouched, and even at two hundred meters I knew what he was doing: studying a patch of mud where our last portage had churned the grass to pulp.

"They've been there a while," Sera said. Her hands never left the wheel. "Since before the narrows, maybe. I thought I saw dust on the ridge back at the second bend and told myself it was cattle."

"Cattle don't read sign."

"No," she said. "They don't."

* * *

We ran another six miles before we spoke about it properly. Sera found a side slough behind a screen of willows and killed the engine, and the silence that came down over the boat was heavier than any I'd felt since Corren Pass. Water lapped the hull. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge complaining.

"Three of them," she said, sitting on the gunwale, forearms on her knees. "Maybe four. They had pack frames. That's not a patrol—that's an expedition."

"Consortium trackers are contract men," I said. "Vess runs them, if the rumors hold. Ex-military, paid by the day, equipped like they're going into the high country for a month." I pulled the map out just far enough to touch it, then pushed it back. "Somebody tipped the checkpoints. First weir, that guard looked at me too long. Now this. Word's moving faster than we are."

"Or it was moving before we ever launched." Sera's eyes were steady on mine. "Halloway didn't die quietly. People saw him come off that barge. People saw you carry him. If the consortium has ears at the landing—and they have ears everywhere—then they've known since before we cast off."

I'd considered that. I'd considered it every night since the first checkpoint, lying awake in the boat while Sera slept, listening to the river and doing arithmetic I didn't like. But hearing it said aloud was different. Hearing it said aloud made it real in the way that a distant avalanche report is different from feeling the slope shudder under your boots.

"So the river isn't neutral anymore," I said.

"It never was." She almost smiled. "You just hadn't noticed yet."

* * *

We traveled the rest of the day like men under sentence. Sera ran the channel fast where it was safe and slow where it wasn't, hugging the right bank where the cliffs gave cover, using every bend as a blind. I stood at the bow with binoculars and did what I used to do on approach marches: broke the country into segments, cleared each one, moved on.

At the third bend I caught them again. Two figures this time, higher up, moving parallel through a stand of dead pine. They weren't hurrying. That was the part that sat wrong in my stomach. Trackers who've lost you hurry. These men moved like people who knew exactly where we were and were content to keep us in view, herding us the way you'd herd stock toward a gate.

"They're not trying to catch us," I told Sera. "Not here. Not on open water where we could run or wreck and scatter the evidence. They're pushing us downstream toward something."

"Toward the next weir," she said. "Which is fortified. Which we cannot bluff past twice."

"Then we don't go past it on their schedule."

She looked at me then, really looked, and I watched her weigh me—the disgraced guide, the drifter she'd named an outrageous price because she assumed I'd haggle and I hadn't—and whatever she saw seemed to settle something.

"Night run," she said. "There's a stretch below the old ferry crossing where the channel braids. Moonless until late. If we run dark, no lights, no engine noise above idle, we might slide the weir before their pickets are set."

"Might."

"You want guarantees, hire a consortium pilot." She turned the wheel and the bow swung toward the deepening channel. "Get some sleep. You'll want your hands steady tonight."

I didn't sleep. I lay in the bottom of the boat with the map against my chest and listened to the water, and somewhere in the middle of the night I heard, faint and far off across the water, a sound I recognized from another life: the thin metallic note of a carabiner clipping onto wire. Someone on the bank, rigging lines.

The hunt was real now. And the river, which had carried us kindly through rapids and checkpoints alike, had picked its side.

It hadn't picked ours.
