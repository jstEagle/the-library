## 10. The Village That Isn't on Maps

The trackers stayed on the east bank all morning.

I kept them in the corner of my eye while I worked the wheel—two figures moving through the alder scrub with the patient, unhurried gait of men who didn't need to hurry. They weren't following us. Not exactly. They were pacing us, holding their line on shore while we held ours on water, like two hands closing around the same stretch of river from opposite sides. Every few minutes one of them would stop and raise something to his face. Binoculars, or a rangefinder, or worse.

"They're not even trying to hide," Gideon said from behind me. He'd been quiet since dawn, coiled in the stern with Halloway's map spread across his knees, and when he spoke his voice had that flat quality I was starting to recognize as his version of alarm.

"That's what worries you?"

"What worries me is that they don't need to." He folded the map along its worn creases. "They know where we're going. There's only one channel up here. They can walk faster than we can run this current against the weir gates. By tomorrow they'll be ahead of us."

"Then tonight we won't be where they expect."

"And where's that?"

I nodded upstream, to where the Vane bent hard to starboard around a shoulder of black rock, and beyond it, invisible unless you knew to look for it, a thread of woodsmoke rose thin and straight into the cold air. "There's a village up that slough. It won't be on your map."

"Every village is on a map."

"Not this one."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic—fuel, daylight, two men on the bank who'd report our heading by nightfall if they had a radio, which of course they did. Then he stowed the map inside his jacket, against his chest, where he'd taken to keeping it like a second heartbeat, and said, "Show me."

* * *

The slough mouth was a lie of geography, a gap in the reeds no wider than the boat, choked with deadfall that looked impassable until you saw the cut marks—fresh, deliberate, arranged so a hull could slide through if it came in at exactly the right angle. Which meant someone maintained it. Which meant someone wanted it passable for friends and impossible for anyone else.

"Kill your engine," I said. "We pole."

The channel behind the reeds opened like a held breath let out. Willows closed over our heads. The water went shallow and tea-dark and utterly silent except for the drip off the blades and the small sounds of birds resettling. Behind us, the main channel of the Vane went on talking to itself, and then even that faded, and we were inside a place that made its own weather.

Gideon poled with the economy of a man who'd hauled sledges over ice—I noticed that, filed it away with everything else I was noticing about him—and after twenty minutes the willows parted and there it was.

A village. If you wanted to be generous. Thirty-odd structures on stilts above the flood line, built of driftwood and salvaged tin and woven reed, connected by walkways that zigzagged without any plan at all. Drying racks hung with fish. A net strung across a side channel. Children on the walkways who stopped still when they saw us—not frightened, just watching, the way herons watch—and then scattered wordlessly into the maze of planks.

"No name," Gideon said slowly. "No dock. No—" He stopped. I knew what he wasn't saying. No registry marker, no tax post, no consortium sign nailed to anything. In a region where the Vane River Company had stamped its crest on every landing, every weir, every fuel drum from the delta to the high country, this place wore nothing at all.

"The surveyors call it a seasonal fishing camp," I said. "It's on the consortium maps as uninhabited. The census skips it. The mail boat passes twice a year and doesn't stop."

"How does a whole village stay off the books?"

"By being worth nothing," said a voice below us, "until someone decides otherwise."

An old woman stood at the base of the ladder, and I use *old* the way you'd use it about a river—something that had been there so long it had stopped being a thing with an age and become a feature. She was small and square through the shoulders, with hands like knotted rope and eyes the color of the water under cloud. She looked at me first, and something in her face eased.

"Sera Okonkwo," she said. "Your mother ran barges past here before you were born. Before the company bought the delta pilots' guild and made them all sign loyalty papers. She never signed."

"She never told me that."

"There's a lot she never told you. Come up. Bring your man and whatever you're carrying, because you're carrying something—I can see it in how he sits."

Her name was Maren, though half the village called her Grandmother and the other half called her nothing at all because you didn't need a name for a thing that permanent. She fed us smoked whitefish and barley soup in a long house that smelled of resin and woodsmoke, and while we ate she talked—or rather, she drew talk out of us, the way you draw water, one measured pull at a time. She asked about the checkpoint at Sorrel Weir and I told her about the forged permits. She asked about the men on the bank and Gideon described them—their boots, their spacing, the way the lead one carried his rifle slung forward instead of back—and she listened to that description with a stillness that made the hair rise on my arms.

"Vess's people," she said finally.

"You know the name?" Gideon asked.

"Everyone on this river knows the name eventually." She refilled his bowl without asking. "She worked the upper settlements three winters ago. Marking wells. Taking samples. Writing things in a book." Maren's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Then the book went downstream and the wells started failing, and nobody ever came back to explain why."

I felt Gideon go very still beside me. He'd gone still like that once before, back at the Landing, when Halloway had gripped his wrist and whispered the word *source*, and I'd learned to read that stillness as a door opening somewhere inside him onto a room he kept locked.

"Maren," I said carefully, "we're headed upriver. To the headwaters."

"I know."

"We need to be gone before light. And we need—"

"You need nothing," she said. "You need to eat, sleep, and listen. In that order. The rest is already decided; it was decided the day you were born on this water, Sera Okonkwo, and it was decided longer ago than that for him." Her eyes moved to Gideon, and there was something in them I couldn't parse—a weighing, a recognition, a grief held in reserve. "Longer ago than either of you knows."

* * *

They gave us a guest platform at the edge of the village, a plank room over the water with a reed screen for a door, and by full dark the settlement had settled into the particular quiet of a place that has practiced being unnoticed for generations. No dogs—that was the first thing I'd noticed, coming in. No dogs to bark at strangers. No lamps burning past necessity. Even the children moved quietly, as though sound itself were a debt they couldn't afford.

I sat on the platform edge with my feet over the black water and listened to the village breathe. Gideon came out and sat beside me after a while. He didn't say anything for a long time. That was another thing I was learning about him: the silence wasn't emptiness. It was load-bearing.

"Your mother," he said finally. "Maren knew her."

"Everyone's mother knew everyone's mother, on this river. It used to be one long neighborhood before the company put fences in it."

"Fences." He turned the word over like a stone he didn't like the weight of. "Halloway talked about fences. At the end, when he could barely talk at all. He said, *they fenced the water.* I thought he was raving."

"Were you close to him? Before?"

"I'd never met him." Gideon's jaw worked. "He picked me out of a crowd at the Landing because I was the only person who didn't look away from him. That's the whole story. A dying man saw a stranger who wouldn't flinch, and he bet his life's work on it." He laughed, short and bitter. "Worst investment anyone ever made."

"You believe that?"

"I know it. Everything that man touched turned bad. His map got him killed, probably. Now it's getting hunted." He looked down at the water, where our reflections had come apart in the current. "Whoever carries that map next is going to end up the same way."

"Then it's good you're not carrying it for yourself."

He turned his head. In the dark I couldn't read his expression, but I heard the breath he took, and it wasn't steady.

"Why do you say that?"

"Because a man running for himself would have sold that map the night Halloway died and been on a freighter south by now. You paid me an obscene amount of money instead." I shrugged. "I've piloted liars, smugglers, and one actual pirate. I know the difference between cargo a man protects and cargo a man is protecting himself from. You hold that map like it's not yours. Like you're just the courier."

"Maybe I am."

"Maybe." I let it sit. Downstream, far off, I thought I heard the main channel talking, and beneath it, fainter, the memory of carabiners on wire. "Get some sleep, Nystrom."

I said the name casually, the way you'd toss a fish back, and I was watching him when I said it, and I saw it land. Just a flicker—shoulders tightening a quarter inch, breath catching on something—but enough. Enough to tell me the name meant something to him beyond the ordinary weight of a name. Enough to make me wonder why a trail hand named Gideon Nystrom flinched at his own surname in the dark.

He didn't answer. After a moment he went inside the platform room, and I heard him lie down, and I heard him not sleep.

* * *

I woke before dawn to voices.

Not alarmed voices—worse, in a way. Measured ones. I slipped out through the reed screen and followed the walkway toward the long house, where lamplight showed amber through the wall seams, and found Maren standing outside in the cold with Gideon beside her. Between them, wrapped in oilcloth, lay something long and flat that I recognized with a lurch: Halloway's jacket, the one Gideon hadn't let out of his sight since the Landing.

"—came to the boy in the night," Maren was saying, low. "Old Toma. He doesn't sleep much anymore, and he walks the walkways, and last night he walked past your platform and heard you talking in your sleep."

Gideon had gone the color of ash. "What did I say?"

"A name. Your own name, over and over, the way a man says it when he's trying to remember who he used to be." Maren lifted her eyes to his face, and in the lamplight spilling from the long house I saw her expression fully for the first time, and it stopped my breath. It wasn't curiosity. It wasn't suspicion. It was recognition, old and deep and sorrowful, like a woman looking at a ghost she'd buried decades ago. "Nystrom," she said softly. "That was the name of a guide. Corren Pass. Nine souls. My sister's son was one of them."

The world went very narrow and very loud. I heard my own pulse. I heard the water under the walkway. I watched Gideon stand absolutely motionless, the way a man stands when the ground has opened under him and movement would mean falling.

"He was cleared of nothing and blamed for everything," Maren went on, and her voice didn't rise or sharpen; it just stated, the way the river states. "The company's inquiry. Both governments. All of it built on paper that never matched what the mountain actually did. I have spent eleven years knowing that, because grief makes you read everything, Mr. Nystrom, and I read every page of that inquiry until I could recite it. And every page had fingerprints on it. Big ones. Upstream ones."

"Maren." Gideon's voice came out sanded down to the grain. "Whatever you think I—"

"I think nothing. Thinking is for people with incomplete information." She stepped closer, and she was so small and he was so tall and somehow she was the larger of them. "I know what I know: that the avalanche reports were sealed by order of the Vane River Company. That the sealing order carried a signature. And that signature belongs to a man whose son walked into my village last night wearing a dead prospector's jacket and a borrowed name, carrying a map to the water that man has spent thirty years fencing off from the world."

I watched Gideon's face do something I had no words for yet. Somewhere behind his eyes a door I'd only seen flickers of—the locked room, the stillness, the flinch at his own name—swung open, and what was inside was too large and too dark for me to see from where I stood.

"You knew," he said hoarsely. "Last night. When you looked at me. You knew."

"I suspected. Toma confirmed. And I said nothing, because a debt of nine lives isn't collected in a doorway at midnight." Maren reached out and took his hand—his big guide's hand, scarred across the knuckles—and folded it around something small that glinted dully in the lamplight. A ring of braided metal, river-worn. "My sister's. She asked me to give it to whoever finally carried the truth down off that mountain. I've waited eleven years to find out it would be the man the truth was stolen from."

Gideon's fist closed around the ring. He was shaking. I'd seen him steady on the Grinder, steady under the guard's stare at Sorrel Weir, steady through all of it—and now he was shaking like a man standing in a doorway of a burning house he'd thought was long cold.

"Why tell me now?" he managed. "Why not let us pass?"

"Because the men on the bank reached the slough mouth an hour ago," Maren said, "and they turned back. All of them. Which means Vess knows where you are and has chosen not to come in—which means she's waiting for you somewhere ahead, where the river narrows and there's nowhere left to run." She released his hand and stepped back, and the warmth in her face banked itself down to embers. "So eat, take what stores you need, and go. And Mr. Nystrom—when you reach the source, and you will reach it—you tell the water who you are. It's been waiting a long time to hear."

Behind us, down the dark slough, the first grey light was coming up over the willows, and somewhere out on the main channel, faint but unmistakable, an engine turned over and began to move upstream.
