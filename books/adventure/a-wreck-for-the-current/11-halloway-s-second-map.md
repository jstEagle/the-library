## 11. Halloway's Second Map

The rain came at dusk, the kind that made the whole landing smell of tar and rot, and Gideon sat in the back of Sera's boat shed with Halloway's jacket spread across his knees like something he was afraid of.

He'd been meaning to bury the jacket. That was the honest truth of it. Three days now since the old man died in the cot behind the ferry house, and Gideon had kept the map rolled in oilcloth inside his own shirt, but the jacket he hadn't touched. There was something obscene about going through a dead man's pockets. He'd watched enough families do it at the base of mountains, picking through packs for identification, and he'd always sworn he'd never be the one doing the picking.

But the jacket had to go somewhere, and the ground outside was already soft, and he told himself he was only checking the pockets one last time so nothing of value went into the mud.

The coat itself was a marvel of endurance. Canvas so old it had gone the color of tea, patched at both elbows with different cloth, the collar worn to fuzz. It smelled of pipe smoke and river water and something faintly chemical—sample vials, Gideon guessed, carried against the chest for years. Halloway had been a surveyor before he was a prospector. That much Gideon had learned in the last three nights of the man's dying, in fragments delivered between coughs that seemed to come from somewhere below the lungs. Halloway had worked the upper Vane before the consortium sealed it. He'd been one of the last licensed men inside. And then he'd seen something in the filings that didn't match what he'd measured with his own instruments, and he'd spent eleven years being quietly ruined for it.

*They didn't seal the valley because of gold,* he'd said, the second night, when the fever had burned low and his voice was almost steady. *Gold's a curtain. Nobody hangs a curtain unless there's a window behind it.*

Gideon hadn't understood. He'd nodded the way you nod at a dying man, giving him the comfort of being heard without committing to anything. He'd been careful about that his whole exile—careful about promises, careful about knowledge, careful about letting anyone's weight rest on him. Weight was how men got buried.

Now he turned the jacket over and his thumb caught on something wrong at the left seam.

It was the stitching. He felt it before he saw it—a ridge under the canvas where there should have been flatness, a line of thread too regular, too tight, too new. Someone had opened this seam and closed it again, and they'd done it in a hurry, or in fear, or both, and they'd used a different color thread because beggars on the river don't get to choose.

Gideon sat very still for a moment, listening to the rain on the shed roof and Sera moving around on the deck above, her footsteps a rhythm he'd already learned to read without meaning to. Then he got Sera's smallest knife from the tool board—he wasn't going to use his own, some superstition about that—and picked the seam open the way you'd open a wound, slow, with the blade angled away from what was underneath.

The canvas parted. Inside the lining, folded into a square no bigger than a prayer book, was a second document.

Not a map. He knew that in the first second, from the weight and the stiffness of the paper. He set the knife down and drew it out with both hands, the way you'd draw a splinter from a wound, and unfolded it on his knee under the lantern.

It was a survey log. Halloway's own hand, the same cramped, patient lettering that covered the first map's margins, but this was different in kind. This was numbers. Dates going back eleven years, arranged in columns. Gauge readings. Flow measurements. Chemical assays of water from a dozen points along the upper Vane and its feeder streams. And across the top, underlined twice in ink gone brown with age, a title in Halloway's careful capitals:

*HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY — VANE HEADWATERS & CONNECTED AQUIFERS — PERSONAL COPY — NOT FOR FILING.*

Gideon read the first page three times. He was good with numbers—mountain guides have to be, load calculations, anchor angles, weather math—but this wasn't his language and he had to work at it. What he understood, slowly, the way cold works into a boot, was this: the numbers didn't describe a river. They described a system. The Vane's source wasn't one spring in one valley. It was the pressure head for a web of aquifers running under three provinces, feeding wells in towns Halloway had listed by name—towns Gideon had passed through in his years of drifting, towns where he'd drunk from taps without once wondering where the water came from.

*Eleven years of measurements,* Halloway had told him, the last night, when the words came slow and each one cost. *Every season. I went back when they weren't looking. Old man with a fishing rod, nobody watches an old man fishing. And every year the filings got worse. They report the aquifer at sixty percent. It's at forty-one and falling. They report recharge from the source valley at baseline. It's been dropping since they fenced it. They're drawing it down, boy. They've got gauging stations up there and they're drawing the whole watershed down like a well, and when it's gone they'll own the only water left, and they'll sell it back to the provinces that used to get it free out of the ground.*

Gideon had heard that. He'd nodded at that, too. But hearing a dying man's theory and reading eleven years of his own instrument data were different things entirely. The numbers had a terrible patience to them. They climbed and fell in clean curves, annotated in Halloway's small hand—*dry season, no snowmelt contribution, anomalous; fenced section, no access, estimate only; well at Marrow's Bend, depth to water table increased 2.3 meters year over year.* Page after page. Towns after towns. A slow, documented theft, measured with a fisherman's patience by a man nobody had thought worth stopping.

Gideon set the pages down on his knee and found that his hands weren't quite steady.

The first map had been a debt. A promise made over a corpse-to-be, one man's dying wish, a thing you could carry out of respect and grief and the simple fact that a dead man had asked. Gideon had been able to hold that in his mind without it changing the shape of his life. Run the map upstream, keep a promise, stay anonymous. A errand, even a dangerous one. Men did errands.

But this wasn't an errand. This was evidence. Eleven years of evidence, gathered by a man the consortium had already ruined once, hidden in a jacket seam because Halloway had known—had *known*, all those years, carrying it against his chest through every checkpoint and every lean winter—that someday he'd either find a way to use it or die trying to get it to someone who would.

*Not for filing.* Gideon almost laughed at that, a short, airless sound. Halloway had filed his surveys, once. That was how it started. He'd filed honest numbers and the consortium had filed dishonest ones over them, and when Halloway had objected, the machinery had turned on him—license revoked, credit called in, name quietly passed around every registry office in two countries until a licensed surveyor became an unlicensed prospector became a half-dead man staggering off a freight barge into a stranger's path.

Gideon knew how that machinery worked. He'd been fed into it himself, nine years ago, at Corren Pass.

The thought arrived and he made himself sit with it, because sitting with uncomfortable things was the discipline his exile had taught him. Halloway and I, he thought. Same machine. Different gears. The consortium had taken his name and Halloway's livelihood with the same motion, the same indifference, and here they both were—Halloway in the ground behind the ferry house, Gideon under a false name in a boat shed, holding the only honest numbers in three provinces.

Rain drummed. Above him, Sera's footsteps crossed the deck and stopped. He heard her pause at the head of the ladder, and he knew she was deciding whether to come down, and he knew she'd decide not to, because she'd decided not to every night for three days, and he understood that too. She was giving him room the way you give a climber room on bad ground. Watching without hovering. He hadn't asked her to. He suspected she'd deny doing it.

He looked back down at the survey.

There was a final page, and it was different from the rest. The columns stopped. In their place, Halloway had written a letter—not finished, never finished, the last lines tapering off into the weakness of his final days—but a letter, addressed to no one and everyone:

*To whoever carries this. I was a licensed man for thirty years. I believed in filings. I believed the numbers would speak for themselves. They spoke, and the men who own the paper said they'd heard something else. So now the numbers need a voice, and legs, and someone stubborn enough to walk them up a river in dry season. I can't pay you. There's no reward coming. What there is, is this: three provinces drink from what's behind that fence, and they don't know it's being sold out from under them. That's not a secret anymore once you've read these pages. I'm sorry for that. Knowledge is a load. But you picked it up.*

*You picked it up.*

Gideon read that last line four times.

He thought about the promise. He'd made it carefully, the way he made all his promises now, with limits built in like relief valves: *I'll carry the map upstream. I didn't say I'd fight anyone. I didn't say I'd be anyone.* A courier's promise. A ghost's errand.

But Halloway hadn't asked for a courier. Halloway had asked for a voice. And the second document—the one hidden deeper, the one sewn into the lining like a heart inside a chest—wasn't a map to a source. It was a case against everything that had been built over the source, and it was written in numbers so patient and so plain that no filing office, no bought magistrate, no consortium lawyer could call it a madman's scribbling. This was the thing the checkpoints were really for. This was what the hunters would be looking for, if they knew it existed.

Which meant, Gideon thought, that he wasn't carrying a dead man's wish anymore. He was carrying a loaded weapon, and everyone downstream was standing in the room.

He folded the pages along Halloway's own creases, precisely, and sat for a long moment with the square of paper in his hands. The lantern hissed. Somewhere out on the river, water moved in the dark, indifferent, carrying everything downstream eventually.

He thought about the aquifer towns. Marrow's Bend. He'd drunk beer in Marrow's Bend. There'd been a girl at the ferry crossing there with a laugh like a hinge, and a well behind the tavern with a bucket chain that groaned. He'd stood next to that well and never once thought about what fed it, the way no one thinks about their own heartbeat. That was the trick of it. That was the whole architecture of the thing—you didn't have to steal from people who didn't know what they owned.

Halloway had known. Halloway had spent eleven years being nobody so he could keep count.

Gideon got up, took a length of waxed linen from the supply shelf, and wrapped the survey the way he'd wrap a map he meant to take across a glacier—tight, dry, redundant. He sewed the jacket seam closed again with thread from Sera's kit, clumsy stitches, and put the jacket on a hook by the door, because it had earned a better end than the mud, and because tomorrow he'd bury it properly with the rest of Halloway's things.

Then he sat back down and did the thing he'd been avoiding for three days. He took out the first map, unrolled it beside the survey, and actually read it—not as a courier reads a route, but as a guide reads ground. The source valley. The feeder streams with Halloway's flow annotations. The aquifer recharge zones marked in a surveyor's shorthand. And there, at the head of it all, a small symbol Halloway had drawn and redrawn until the paper wore thin: a circle with a line through it, and beside it, in letters pressed so hard they'd nearly torn the page:

*GAUGE STATION 1. THEY WATCH IT. IT WATCHES THEM.*

Gideon stared at that for a long time.

A gauge station at the source. Manned. Which meant records, which meant filings, which meant the consortium's own instruments sitting a stone's throw from the springs, logging the true numbers day after day into whatever archive Aldous Nystrom's people kept. Halloway's survey proved the theft. The consortium's own gauges would prove it better. The evidence wasn't just in his shirt—it was waiting at the end of the river, in a fenced valley, in a building with a roof, kept in perfect condition by the very people it would destroy.

All they had to do was get there before the dry season ended, past the weirs, past the hunters, past whatever waited at the head of the water.

*They,* he noted, and stopped, and looked at the word in his own head. Not *I*. Somewhere in the last hour, without asking her, without paying her, without telling her what he'd found under a dead man's seam, he'd made Sera Okonkwo part of this.

That wasn't fair. That was exactly the kind of thing that got people killed, deciding for them what they were carrying. He'd been on the other end of that decision. He knew what it cost.

He rolled the map, stowed the survey inside his shirt against his skin where Halloway had kept it, and climbed the ladder into the rain.

Sera was under the deck awning, mending a net by lantern light, and she looked up when he came out, and he saw her take his face in with one long pilot's look—the kind of look that reads water before deciding whether to run it.

"You've been down there three hours with a dead man's coat," she said.

"He had more than a map sewn into the lining." Gideon crouched across from her, rain running off the awning edge between them. "Survey data. Eleven years of it. Flow readings, water table depths, well measurements from towns all down the basin." He kept his voice level, kept it in the register of a man discussing cargo. "The source valley feeds the aquifers under three provinces. The consortium's sealed it off and they're drawing the whole watershed down. Wells are failing. They're going to own the only water left, and they're filing false numbers so nobody notices until it's done."

Sera's needle stopped moving.

She didn't say anything for a while. He watched her process it the way he'd watched her read rapids—fast, total, no wasted motion. He saw the moment it stopped being a stranger's story and became a story about her river, her landing, her water.

"Marrow's Bend," she said finally. "My cousin's wells went dry two springs back. Everyone said it was the drought."

"It's not the drought."

"No," she said. "I don't suppose it is." She set the net down, and when she looked at him again her face had changed—not softer, exactly, but aimed. "And you're still planning to run this up the river alone, under a name that isn't yours, carrying the one document the consortium would burn a town to get back."

"I'm planning to keep a promise."

"You're planning to get us both killed over a dead man's paperwork." She said it flat, without heat, and then, before he could answer: "I didn't say no. I said what it is. There's a difference, and you'd better learn it before the first weir."

Gideon almost smiled. "Why didn't you say no?"

Sera picked the net back up. The needle flashed. "Because my cousin's well is dry," she said, "and because you sewed that seam back up with my thread and your hands were shaking, and a man who shakes over a dead stranger's stitching is a man worth running water with." She glanced up. "Get some sleep. We cast off at first light, before the checkpoint shift changes."

He went down the ladder and lay in the dark of the boat shed with the survey against his chest, rising and falling with his breath, and understood that the promise had changed shape while he wasn't looking. It had been a debt to one man. Now it was a debt to every town downstream that drew water without knowing what fed it, and debts like that didn't have relief valves.

He thought about the gauge station at the head of the river. *They watch it. It watches them.*

He thought about how the consortium's own instruments were keeping the true record, all these years, in a fenced valley at the top of the world.

And he thought, as sleep finally took him, that somewhere in a high office downstream, a man who shared his blood was signing orders to find a map that had already slipped past him—and that the first checkpoint weir was a half day's run upstream, and the water was already falling.
