## 12. Sabotage at Night

Sera woke to the sound of the river lying to her.

That was the thing about anchoring in a backwater eddy instead of running night water—the silence got inside your head and started making promises. *All clear. All still. Sleep.* The Vane didn't do silence, not really; it just dropped its voice low enough that a pilot could forget it was always moving, always taking something with it.

She'd anchored below the willow bar an hour past dusk, nose into the current, engine ticking as it cooled. Gideon had taken first watch without being asked, which she'd noted and filed under things she'd decide whether to trust later. She'd slept in her clothes, boots laced, knife under the coil of her hair the way her mother had taught her before she could swim.

The sound that woke her wasn't a sound. It was the absence of one—the engine's tick gone, replaced by a soft rhythmic knock against the hull that didn't match the water.

She came up off the bunk with the knife already in her hand.

The cabin was dark. The lamp over the chart table was out, though she'd left it burning low. That was the second wrong thing. She stood in the black and listened, counting heartbeats the way you counted seconds between lightning and thunder, except there was no storm, only the knock-knock-knock against the hull, patient as a carpenter.

"Gideon." She said it barely above breath.

Nothing from the bow where he'd bedded down on the cargo netting. Her stomach dropped through the deck plates. She moved to the companionway, knife low, and looked out.

Moonlight, half a moon, silvering the willows. The river sliding past dark and smooth. And Gideon—Gideon was there, at the stern, crouched by the fuel line where it ran from the tank through the transom fitting, holding something up to the light.

He turned when he heard her. His face did something complicated.

"You should see this," he said. "Before you touch anything."

* * *

The fuel line had been cut.

Not slashed—Sera saw that once she knelt and put her own eyes on it. Slashing was rage or haste, a single stroke, messy. This was two clean parallel cuts an inch apart, then the section lifted out whole, neat as a surgeon taking a vein. Whoever did it had brought a replacement length of hose in their pocket and forgotten it, or lost their nerve, or wanted the boat crippled rather than dead—a boat that couldn't run but could be found intact told a story a burned hulk didn't.

"They were going to fix it," Sera said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Cut this out, let us drift or stall somewhere downstream, patch it with their own line so we wouldn't know until they wanted us to know."

"Or they got interrupted," Gideon said.

"By what?"

He didn't answer. He held up what he'd been looking at when she woke: a scrap of rubber, torn from a boot heel or a knee pad, snagged on the transom fitting's rough edge. Black rubber, good quality, and pressed into it where it had caught, a partial impression.

Sera took it from him. Turned it toward the moon.

A brand. Not all of it—the heel had torn away mid-stamp—but enough. A circle, and within the circle the suggestion of a V laid over a vertical stroke, like a gauge needle pinned hard right.

Vane Consortium. She'd seen the mark a thousand times on freight seals and permit stamps and the crates of cheap tools the company stores sold at prices that kept everyone owing. She'd never seen it on a man's skin before, but she'd heard of it. Pilots talked. Hunters who worked retainer for the consortium took the brand on the inside of the forearm, an old practice from the days when the company paid in scrip and marked its men like cattle so no other outfit would hire them. A lifetime contract, burned in.

"He was on the boat," she said. "While we slept."

"Yes."

"While *you* were on watch."

Gideon's jaw worked. In the half-light his face looked older, the lines around his eyes cut deep. "I watched the bank," he said. "The willows. The channel. I watched everywhere a man would come from if he came by land or water." He paused. "I didn't watch the river itself. Not underneath."

Sera looked at the black water sliding past the hull and felt the hair rise on her arms. A swimmer, coming down the current with the debris, no wake, no paddle-splash, breathing through a reed or not at all. Hooking a hand over the gunwale while the boat's own motion covered him. Doing his work in the time it took a kettle to boil, and slipping away downstream into the dark the same way he'd come.

"We anchor again, we die," she said. "Or worse. We run nights, we die tired. They've got us between—"

"No." Gideon said it quietly, and something in the quiet made her stop. "They've had us since the first weir. Someone read my face and made a radio call, and everything since has been them deciding how they want us. This—" he nodded at the cut line, "—this is them deciding they're done being patient."

* * *

They didn't light the lamp. They worked by moonlight and by feel, Sera splicing in the spare hose she carried because a pilot who trusted other people's maintenance deserved whatever she got, Gideon holding the line steady while she cut and clamped. Her hands knew the work. Her mind kept circling back to the same image: a hand hooking over the gunwale, inches from where her head had lain.

"How long?" Gideon asked.

"An hour's work done properly. Twenty minutes done fast and ugly." She tightened the last clamp and sat back on her heels. "We can't test it till morning. If I start the engine now, every ear on this stretch of river knows we found it."

"So we sit," he said, "and pretend we don't know."

"And they sit on the bank and pretend they aren't watching to see if we know." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "Welcome to my river, Nystrom. It's not the water that kills you. It's the arithmetic."

He almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "What's the arithmetic?"

"Two of us, one boat, and a hunter who can swim cold water at night, which means he's not alone, because nobody swims a support position solo. So call it three, maybe four, spread along the banks, leapfrogging us downstream every time we move. They don't need to catch us. They need to slow us. Every hour we spend repairing, hiding, doubling back, the dry season drops another inch off the upper river." She looked at him. "You've seen the bars coming up since the weir. Another week of this and there are channels I can't run at any price."

Gideon was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had that careful evenness she'd learned to distrust, the tone of a man arranging words like stones across a creek. "Then we don't give them another night at anchor."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning we leave now. Drift down on the current, silent, past wherever they're watching, and take on fuel at the next settlement."

"The village." She'd been thinking about it herself, hating it. The unrecorded community, the one that survived by never appearing on charts. "You want to bring this to their doorstep."

"I want to bring *us* to their doorstep," Gideon said. "Whatever follows follows."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. There it was again—that thing underneath the drifter's flatness, the thing she'd first glimpsed when he'd caught the cargo line in the Grinder without being asked, rope-work too clean for a man who claimed to be nobody. He said *whatever follows follows* the way other men said *I'll be right behind you*, and she understood suddenly that he'd already decided the cost was acceptable, that he'd priced himself in from the start.

"That's a hell of a thing to spend other people's homes on," she said.

"It's a hell of a thing to ask." He met her eyes. "Which is why I'm asking, not deciding. You know these waters. You know these people better than I ever will. If you say run for the landing and give it up, I'll run."

And there it was, the trap inside the trap: he'd handed her the choice, which meant handing her the blame, which meant she couldn't hate him cleanly afterward no matter how it went. She should have resented that. What frightened her was how much she respected it.

"Drift past the willow bar," she said finally. "There's a moon-shadowed stretch below the point where the current does the work. We pole through the shallows after that, no engine till we're two miles clear. Then we pray the splice holds."

* * *

They drifted.

No lights, no engine, the boat riding the current's slow fist down the center of the channel while Sera stood at the helm reading the water by feel, by the pull of the current against the rudder, by the small cold sounds of the river sorting itself around bars she couldn't see. Gideon sat in the bow with a boathook across his knees, watching the banks, and Sera watched him watching and thought: *he looks like a man walking back into a house that burned down.*

Twice she saw movement on the left bank—a shape detaching from the willows, keeping pace, then falling behind as the current carried them past whatever watching post the hunters had established. Neither shape entered the water. Either they hadn't finished the job and were waiting for the boat to stall, or—

Or they'd done exactly what they meant to do, and the cut fuel line was only the message, and the message was *we are here, we are patient, and the river is ours.*

The splice held. Two miles below the point, in a stretch of dead water behind a gravel bar, Sera risked thirty seconds of engine—just long enough to confirm fuel pressure, just long enough to hate the noise—and then killed it again and poled through the shallows with Gideon pushing from the bow, the two of them working in a rhythm neither of them commented on.

Dawn found them tied up in a back slough a mile short of the village, mist standing on the water in columns, fish rising in rings. Sera sat on the gunwale with cold tea and looked at the cut section of fuel line she'd kept, coiled in her palm like a shed snake skin. Evidence, Gideon had called it. She was starting to keep a collection without meaning to: a torn boot-heel print, a length of sabotaged hose, a forged permit, a map stitched into a dead man's jacket. Little pieces of a truth that added up to something she wasn't sure she wanted to be holding.

Gideon came aft and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"When we get to the village," he said at last, "there'll be an elder. Old woman, maybe, or a man with river-clay tattoos. Someone who decides things." He hesitated in a way she'd never seen him hesitate. "If they ask my name—"

"They'll ask your name," Sera said. River people always asked names; it was how they weighed you.

"If they ask," he said again, "let me answer."

She turned and looked at him properly. Mist beaded in his hair. He looked exhausted and resolved and afraid, all three at once, and none of those was the thing that made her chest go tight. The thing that made her chest go tight was that he was offering her honesty the way a drowning man offers a rope—*hold this end, I'll hold mine, and maybe neither of us goes under.*

"Fine," she said. "But Nystrom—if there's more to that name than a blacklist, I want to hear it before some elder hears it first. Whatever it is. Whatever it costs."

Something moved behind his eyes, quick and submerged, like a fish turning deep in green water.

"You will," he said. "Sooner than I'd like."

Downstream, around the bend, smoke was rising from the village fires—thin, domestic, ordinary. But beyond it, far upstream where the gorge narrowed to a throat, Sera thought she heard it: a sound too regular for rapids, too distant for engines.

A drum. Or a signal. Or a countdown.

She drained her tea and went to raise the anchor.
