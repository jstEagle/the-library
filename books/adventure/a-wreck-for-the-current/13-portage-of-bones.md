## 13. Portage of Bones

The gorge announced itself an hour before we saw it—a low grinding under the river's voice, like millstones turning somewhere beneath the world. Sera heard it first. She always heard things first.

"Deadwater," she said, and cut the engine.

The Vane narrowed ahead of us into a slot of gray-green water that dropped away in a series of ledges no sane pilot would attempt. The walls rose sheer on both sides, black rock streaked with mineral stain, and the sound coming up out of that slot was the sound the river made when it stopped being a river and became a weapon.

"How far around?" I asked.

Sera stood at the wheel with her arms crossed, reading the gorge the way I used to read a ridgeline—looking for the line through it, knowing there wasn't one. "Two miles of rock on the east side, maybe three on the west. East is scree. West is cliff." She looked back at me. "So we take the scree."

I looked at the boat. The *Marrow* was thirty feet of riveted steel and stubbornness, loaded with fuel drums, spare parts, our supplies, Halloway's map sewn into my jacket, and everything else two people needed to survive a river that wanted them dead. Thirty feet of steel does not go over scree. It goes over scree in pieces, or it goes over scree because somebody refuses to accept what steel can't do.

"We could run it," I said, mostly to hear her say no.

Sera laughed once, without any humor in it. "You see that second ledge? Water goes under it. Not over—under. There's a cave in there eating half the flow. You'd come out the other side as driftwood, if you came out at all." She killed the engine fully and the silence that followed was enormous, filled only by the grind of the gorge. "No. We carry her."

"You've done this before."

"Once. Smaller boat. Younger back." She flexed her hands against the wheel. "Get the lines out. We'll need every rope you brought, mountain man, and then we'll need more."

* * *

We beached below the last eddy before the drop, nosed the bow into gravel, and began the work of making thirty feet of steel portable.

The method, when Sera explained it, was simple in the way that terrible things are simple. We'd build a sledge from the boat's spare planking and the broken oar frames. We'd rig the *Marrow*'s lifting lines to a harness system—two long haul ropes running forward, two drag lines aft, a bridle across the keel rollers. Then we'd pull. And pull. And pull. Two miles of scree, gaining perhaps four hundred feet of elevation before descending again to the quiet water below the gorge.

"Four days," Sera said. "If nothing breaks. If nobody's shoulder gives out. If the weather holds."

"And if something breaks?"

"Then we're standing in the middle of nowhere with half a boat and a consortium hunting party somewhere behind us." She said it lightly, but her eyes went upstream first, checking, the way they'd checked every morning since the slashed fuel line. "Let's not break anything."

I built the sledge while she stripped weight from the boat. Every nonessential thing came off: the anchor chain coiled and buried, the cabin door unshipped and lashed flat, even the stove's iron plate. She worked fast and silent, and I noticed she kept touching certain objects as she moved them—the brass compass bolted by the wheel, a chipped mug hanging from a hook—and setting them down gently, apart from the rest, like things that had earned their place.

By late afternoon the sledge was ready and we moved the *Marrow* onto it for the first time, using a deadman anchor I dug into the gravel and a three-to-one purchase that screamed under load. The hull settled onto the rollers with a groan, and when we finally got her level, Sera sat down hard on a rock and looked at the whole ridiculous arrangement—boat on sled on gravel at the foot of a gorge—and shook her head.

"My father would say this is where I explain to him why I never took the safe job at the cannery," she said.

"He sounds like he'd understand anyway."

"He'd understand. He'd also laugh until he cried." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and picked up a haul rope. "Come on. Daylight's wasting."

* * *

The first mile taught me what my body had become in six years of hiding.

There is a particular dishonesty in recovery. You heal enough to function, enough to work, enough to convince yourself the damage is behind you—and then some load comes along that reaches past all your careful compensations and finds the original break, and you learn that healed is not the same as whole.

My left knee went first. It had been wrong since Corren Pass, since the day the cornice let go above us and I rode forty meters of avalanche debris down into the trees with a man's ice axe handle jammed through my thigh and my leg folded underneath me at an angle legs don't fold. The surgeons in the border province had rebuilt it well enough to walk on, badly enough to remind me. On flat ground I managed it fine. But scree doesn't care about compensation. Scree shifts under every step, and by the end of the first day every step sent a bright wire of pain from kneecap to hip, and I was favoring the leg so heavily that my right hip had begun its own complaint, a deep ache like a bruise in the bone.

I said nothing. I shortened my stride, changed how I leaned into the haul rope, found angles that hid the limp. Six years of practice at being unreadable, and here was the payoff: Sera Okonkwo, who missed nothing about rivers, saw nothing about me.

Or so I believed for most of that first day.

The second day was worse. We gained the shoulder of the scree field proper—a vast slope of shattered slate, ankle-deep loose over house-sized slabs, the whole hillside ticking and settling in the heat like something alive deciding whether to move. We hauled in relays: fifty meters of rope paid out, both of us leaning into the harness until the webbing bit our shoulders, walking the load forward hand over hand, resetting the deadmen, doing it again. Fifty meters an hour, on a good stretch. Less when the sled hung up on a slab edge and we had to lever it free with the spare oar and a prayer.

Somewhere in the afternoon of that second day, hauling uphill through loose stone with the sun hammering the slope and the gorge roaring below us like a trapped animal, my knee simply quit. Mid-stride. The joint locked, the leg buckled, and I went down on the scree with the rope still in my hands, dragged a body-length before I could get my feet under me again.

Sera had the load snubbed to a deadman within seconds—she'd learned the system fast—but she was beside me almost as fast, and I was still on one knee in the shale trying to stand up like nothing happened when she crouched in front of me.

"Let me see it."

"It's fine. Slipped, that's all."

"Gideon." She said my name—not the alias, the name I'd given her at the landing, which was itself a lie wearing a true shape—and waited. "I watched you favor that leg for two days. You think I hired a cripple upriver and didn't notice his stride? Let me see it."

I should have lied better. Instead I sat down on a slab and pulled up my trouser leg, and she looked at the knee—at the surgical scar, long and ugly, and the way the joint sat slightly wrong even at rest—and she didn't flinch and she didn't fuss. She just studied it the way she studied water, looking for the line through.

"Avalanche?" she asked.

"Rockfall." Another lie, smaller, reflexive. She looked at me for a moment too long, and I saw her decide not to press it, and I understood that she was keeping a ledger. That she'd been keeping it since the landing. That someday she'd read it aloud to me.

"Can you haul on it?"

"Yes."

"Then we rig you differently." She was already moving, pulling the spare webbing from the kit. "You take point position on the forward line—shorter pulls, more of them, less sustained load. I'll take the heavy drag. And you tell me before it locks next time, not after. If you go down under load again with the sled on a slope, we lose the boat. Understood?"

"Understood."

She knotted the new harness herself, cinching the webbing high on my thigh to take strain off the joint, and her hands were quick and impersonal and careful all at once, and something about that combination undid me more than pity would have. Pity I knew how to refuse. Carefulness I had no defense against.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Haul." But she was almost smiling when she said it.

* * *

The third night the wind came down the gorge and made sleep impossible, so we took turns keeping the fire alive in a hollow of stone, and it was my watch past midnight when I heard her talking in her sleep.

Not words, exactly. Fragments. A name—I caught *Emeka*, twice—and a phrase in a language I didn't know, spoken with such gentleness that I stopped feeding the fire and just listened, feeling like a thief. Then, clearer, in plain speech: *"I've got the line. I've got you. Hold on."*

And then silence, and her breathing evened out, and I sat with the fire and thought about the way she'd said *hold on* to someone who wasn't there, and I understood that the ledger ran both directions. That she carried her own gorge, her own scree field, her own names she didn't say in daylight. That whatever had put a river woman alone on the upper Vane with a chipped mug she touched like a relic, it wasn't nothing, and she hadn't confessed it either.

Endurance, I was learning, was a kind of intimacy. Not the intimacy of telling—you could tell a stranger your whole history in an evening and remain strangers. It was the intimacy of watching someone suffer well. Of seeing what a person did at the edge of their strength, hour after hour, when there was no performance left in them and no reason left to pretend. Sera hauling the drag line with blood blisters split open on both palms, wrapping them with tape and picking the rope back up without comment. Sera counting cadence under her breath on the worst pitch, a low steady rhythm, giving us both something to hang on. Sera, at the top of the scree on the third day, when the sled slipped sideways toward a drop and I threw myself against the tail rope and held it with my bad leg screaming and my boots plowing furrows in the shale—Sera reaching me in four strides, taking the load without a word, holding it while I got my feet back, and afterward just nodding once, as if to say: *there you are. That's who you are.*

I had spent six years making sure nobody saw who I was. On the scree above the gorge, it turned out, I couldn't help showing her.

* * *

The descent nearly killed us.

Going up, the sled wanted to stay put; going down, it wanted to leave without us. The eastern side of the gorge fell away in a long ramp of broken stone toward the quiet green water below the rapids, and we controlled the *Marrow*'s weight with drag lines snubbed around boulders, paying out inches at a time, both of us walking backward down a slope that shed stones constantly, small ones hissing away into the void, big ones booming.

Halfway down, a slab the size of a table came loose above us.

I heard it before I saw it—that deep click of stone letting go—and I had time to shout and grab Sera's harness and throw us both sideways behind a rib of bedrock, and the slab went past close enough that I felt the wind of it, and it hit the slope below and exploded into shrapnel that rang off the rocks around us like gunfire.

For a moment neither of us moved. Then Sera started laughing—a shaky, unhinged, magnificent laugh, pressed against my shoulder, her fist still knotted in my jacket.

"That," she said, "was closer than the Grinder."

"Noted. Next time I'll schedule it further away."

She pulled back and looked at me, and there was shale dust in her hair and a cut on her cheekbone she didn't know about yet, and she looked at me the way she'd looked at the river after the Grinder—with revised estimates. With something warmer than respect.

"You held the tail rope yesterday," she said. "On that leg. You shouldn't have been able to."

"Adrenaline."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "That wasn't adrenaline. Adrenaline is cheap. That was—" She searched for the word, gave up, and patted my chest once, hard, like sealing a hatch. "Never mind. Come on. Water's down there, and I'd trade this whole boat for a drink of it."

* * *

We floated the *Marrow* off the sledge into the pool below the gorge near dusk on the fourth day, and the relief of it was physical, like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying. The hull had taken scrapes that would need patching. One roller had cracked clean through. My hands were ruined, my knee was swollen to twice its size inside my boot, and Sera moved like a woman assembled from wire and stubbornness.

But we were around it. Below the gorge the Vane ran wide and slow and green, gathering itself for whatever came next, and the evening light lay along it like a promise.

Sera fired the stove and cooked the last of the rice with dried fish, and we ate in the boat's cabin because the wind had turned cold, sitting close in the narrow space, our shoulders touching, neither of us moving away.

"The village," she said, after a while. "Halloway's map shows one past the next bend. Off every chart."

"I remember."

"People live up here. Whole lives, whole histories, and the consortium's maps pretend they don't exist." She turned her mug in her hands—the chipped one, rescued from the stripped boat and carried over two miles of scree. "My grandfather piloted freight on the lower Okavango channels. Waterways that weren't on any colonial map either. He used to say the maps weren't mistakes. They were arguments." She glanced at me. "Somebody draws the map to win the argument. Everybody living outside the lines just loses quietly."

I thought of Halloway dying on a pallet in a room that smelled of kerosene, spending his last strength on a document the powerful had decided shouldn't exist. I thought of the survey data hidden in his jacket stitching, which I hadn't yet told Sera about—hadn't told anyone, hadn't fully read myself, because part of me understood that once I read it, I would stop being able to turn back.

"Sera," I said. "When we make the next anchorage, there's something in Halloway's jacket you need to see."

She looked at me, and I watched her decide—again—not to ask why I hadn't shown her sooner, why I kept rationing the truth out of myself like a man hoarding coins. She just nodded and drank her tea.

Outside, full dark had come down, and far upstream—or downstream, it was impossible to say—a light appeared on the water. Small, steady, moving. Too deliberate for a fisherman. Too patient for a drifter.

It held our bearing for a long moment. Then it went dark.

Sera set down her mug without a sound. "Tell me that's a village lamp."

I watched the black water where the light had been, and my knee throbbed in time with my pulse, and somewhere behind us, over two miles of scree we'd taken four days to cross, I knew the hunters would find our track in the morning—sled runners, deadman holes, boot prints in shale that would hold for weeks.

"No," I said. "I don't think it is."
