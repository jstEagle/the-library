## 14. The Founder's Face

The trading post sat where the Vane bent east and the old pack trail climbed out of the river valley toward the plateau—a squat stone building with a tin roof gone the color of old pennies, a dock of split pilings, and a hand-painted sign that read MARLOW & SONS, PROVISIONS, which was a lie twice over, because there was no Marlow anymore and there had never been any sons.

Sera tied off at the dock with the particular care she gave to unfamiliar water, testing the pilings with her boot before trusting them with a line. Three days since the village that wasn't on maps. Two days since she'd found the slashed fuel line and the brand burned into the tracker's forearm, and she still caught herself checking the bilge every morning like a woman checking a child's forehead for fever. The boat was patched. The fuel was rationed. Her nerves were another matter.

Gideon stood at the bow coiling the stern line, and she watched him do it, because she'd been watching him for days now and had mostly stopped pretending she wasn't. He coiled rope like a man who'd been taught by someone patient—over, under, over, the loops falling into each other's arms—and there was a steadiness to his hands that she'd come to trust the way you trust a rail in the dark. Which was exactly the problem. She'd trusted a rail once, on a barge out of Corren Landing, and the rail had been rotten through, and she'd gone into the water with three hundred pounds of cargo and learned that trust was just a bet you made before you had all the information.

"Two hours," she said. "Fuel, food, and I want to ask about water levels above the gorge. The old man at the village said the drop's coming early this year."

Gideon nodded. He'd been quiet since the village—quieter than his baseline, which was already a low bar. The elder woman, Nadi, had said something to him on the last morning, something Sera hadn't caught, and whatever it was, it had gone into Gideon like a splinter. He kept working at it with his thumb. She'd seen the gesture three times since: that absent press of his thumb against his forefinger, worrying at nothing.

They climbed the dock steps together. The trading post's front room smelled of kerosene, cured fish, and the particular dust of places where money changed hands without anyone being glad about it. A fat man behind the counter—Marlow's widow's brother, maybe, or just a man who'd inherited the inventory—looked up from a ledger and took their measure with the speed of long practice.

"Fuel," Sera said. "Diesel. Forty liters if you have it."

"I have it." He named a price that was robbery with a receipt, and Sera paid it without blinking, because the alternative was rowing, and she'd done enough rowing in her life to know exactly what it was worth.

While the man went back for the drums, she drifted along the shelves, pricing what they could afford and mourning what they couldn't: salt pork, hardtack, a coil of tarred twine, a tin of matches that had probably been damp since before the war. Gideon took over the negotiation for the food, and she let him, because he was better at it than she was—softer, somehow, in a way that made hard bargains feel like conversations. Another thing she'd noticed. Another thing she'd filed.

The poster was on the post's back door, half-covered by a notice about a lost mule.

She wouldn't have seen it at all except that the mule notice had come loose at one corner and swung in the draft, and behind it, the paper beneath caught the light from the kerosene lamp, and the light caught the consortium's seal—that interlocking V and C, the mark she'd grown up seeing on everything from medicine tins to arrest warrants.

She pulled the mule notice aside.

The paper was a standard consortium circular, the kind that went up in every post, every ferry house, every checkpoint from the delta to the head of navigation. REWARD, it said, in letters sized for men who read slowly. And beneath it, a face rendered in the smudged lithography of a thousand-copy print run—a face younger than the one she knew, smoother, the jaw less set, the eyes carrying none of the wariness that had become, over these weeks on the river, almost a kind of beauty in him. But the same face. She knew it the way you know your own hands.

*WANTED FOR QUESTIONING,* the text ran. *GIDEON NYSTROM. ALSO KNOWN AS "GIDEON COLE," "N. GIDEON," AND OTHER ALIASES. WANTED IN CONNECTION WITH THE CORREN PASS DISASTER. NINE COUNTS OF MANSLAUGHTER. LAST SEEN—* and the date was eleven years old, and the location was a border crossing she'd run cargo through a hundred times.

*THE CONSORTIUM OFFERS A SUBSTANTIAL REWARD FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO THE APPREHENSION OF THE ABOVE-NAMED INDIVIDUAL. THE FOUNDER'S OFFICE DIRECTS THAT ALL CITIZENS REMAIN VIGILANT.*

The Founder's Office.

Sera stood very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere behind her, Gideon was haggling over the price of hardtack, his voice easy, his hands—she knew without looking—moving in that patient rhythm. The fat man laughed at something he'd said.

She'd known there was something. Of course she'd known. You didn't run rivers with a man for this long without accumulating the evidence: the way he flinched from registry offices, the way he handled rope like a certified guide and then lied about where he'd learned it, the way he knew consortium procedures—the permit formats, the checkpoint rotations—better than a salvage man ever should. She'd catalogued it all and told herself a story about it. Deserted from the river militia, maybe. Skipped out on a debt. Ran a boat aground with cargo aboard and let someone else's name take the blame. Small crimes. Understandable ones. The kind of history a person carries the way you carry ballast—ugly, but it keeps you upright.

She had not let herself imagine this.

Corren Pass. Nine counts. She'd been eleven years old when the pass went, but she remembered it anyway, because everyone remembered it, the way everyone remembered the flood of '09 and the burning of the delta warehouses. The mountain came down on a survey team and a road crew and took nine people with it, and the story that came downstream afterward was a story about a guide—a young guide, barely certified—who'd led them onto the slope in bad season, who'd ignored the warnings, who'd walked away while the mountain buried them. She remembered her father saying the name at their kitchen table. *Nystrom.* Said it like a curse. Said it the way you said the names of men who got people killed.

And here he was, three steps behind her, buying hardtack.

"Sera." Gideon's voice, close now. "I got us the twine and the salt. We should—"

She turned.

She didn't plan what her face did. Later, replaying it—and she would replay it, in the dark of the boat, in the days after, turning it over like a stone in her pocket—she would understand that what he saw was not anger. It was the arithmetic. He saw her doing the math, all of it at once: the rope-work, the permit forgeries, the flinch at the registry, the way he'd known the checkpoint commander's rank at the first weir without being told, the way Nadi the elder had looked at him and said something and gone quiet, the way he'd gone quiet after. Nine people. Corren Pass. The name her father had cursed.

"You," she said. It came out strange, almost a whisper, and she hated it, and made the next one come out level. "Gideon Nystrom."

Behind him, the fat man had stopped moving. Sera saw it in the edge of her vision—the stillness of a man who'd just understood what was standing in his shop, and what it might be worth.

"We're leaving," she said. "Now."

"Sera—"

"Now, or I walk out alone and you explain to the consortium why their poster is three days late."

That landed. She watched it land—watched something go out of his face, not fear exactly, but a kind of bleak confirmation, the look of a man who'd been waiting years for exactly this and had hoped, against all evidence, it would come later. He set the hardtack on the counter. He paid the fat man with exact coins, not shorting him, not overpaying, and Sera understood that too, suddenly: he was making sure there'd be no grievance, no story to tell, nothing to sharpen the man's memory. It was the most damning thing he could have done. Innocent men didn't think that way.

They walked out. The daylight was hard and white off the river. Gideon carried the drums; she carried the food. Neither of them spoke until the dock, until the boat, until the lines were cast and the current had them and the trading post was a brown smudge behind them with its tin roof and its poster and its fat man who was probably, even now, reaching for a radio.

Sera took them into the channel and held the tiller and said nothing for four miles.

It was Gideon who broke first, which surprised her. She'd have bet on herself to crack.

"It's not what the poster says."

"No?" She kept her eyes on the water. The channel bent, and she bent it with them. "Which part? The name? Because I've got to tell you, the name fits. I've seen your face for six weeks, Gideon, whoever you are, and that lithograph is you. Younger. Before." Before whatever had carved him down to this. "The aliases fit. The registry flinch fits. The way you forged those salvage permits like you'd been born holding a consortium stamp—that fits. What doesn't fit is me. Standing on that dock next to a man with nine counts of manslaughter, finding out from a poster."

"You think I did it."

"I think nine people are dead and the whole river country spent a decade cursing your name, and I found out from a *mule notice.*" Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. Let him hear it. "I lied for you. At the landing, with Halloway's effects—I stood in front of consortium men and lied, and I didn't know why, I did it because you asked with your whole face, and now it turns out the men I lied to were hunting *you*, and the reward on your head would have bought my boat twice over and paid for a new engine besides."

"I know."

"You *know*." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You knew, and you let me do it. You watched me lie for you and you said nothing. Every day on this river, every rapid, every checkpoint—you let me walk into it blind."

"Yes," he said. "I did."

The plainness of it stopped her. She'd braced for argument, for the smooth deflection she'd half-suspected he was capable of, for some version of the truth arranged to flatter him. Instead he just took it, standing in the stern with the fuel drums lashed at his feet and the current pushing them both toward the gorge, and he took it like a man accepting a sentence.

"Say something," she said.

"I'm going to. All of it. Tonight, at camp, I swear it on—" He stopped. She watched him reach for something to swear on and find nothing, because he'd spent eleven years burning everything a man could swear on. "On Halloway," he finished. "On his grave, wherever it ends up being. But not on moving water. I've seen what happens when a man talks on moving water. He says the wrong thing and the river takes it and it never comes back."

"That's not a river rule. That's an excuse."

"It's both," he said, and there was something in his voice then, something raw under the steadiness, that made her look at him despite herself. He was watching the banks. Both banks, the way he always did now, since the trackers. But his eyes came back to her, and held. "Sera. Whatever you decide tonight, after I tell it—you're right to decide it however you do. I bought your silence with your ignorance, and that's the worst trade there is. I know that. I've known it since the first weir, when the guard looked at me too long and you covered for me without even knowing what you were covering."

"I covered because I thought you were a debt-dodger," she said. "I could have lived with a debt-dodger."

The current took them around a bluff and the wind changed and for a while there was only the work of the boat, the tiller's live pull, the sound of the water going shallow over the gravel bars. She ran the boat. It was easier than running the conversation. The boat never lied to her; the boat just told her the truth in a language she was fluent in.

But the poster was in her now. She could feel it like a splinter of its own. *The Founder's Office directs that all citizens remain vigilant.* She'd read those circulars her whole life, in ferry houses and customs sheds, and she'd always read them the way you read weather warnings—distant, administrative, about other people. Now she knew the face on one. Now she'd eaten hardtack with him. Now she'd watched him coil a line and thought, *I could trust this man with my life,* which was a thought she'd had maybe three times in her whole life and never once about a man with nine counts against his name.

Unless the counts were wrong.

The thought arrived uninvited and she pushed it away, and it came back, and she pushed it again, and by the fifth time it returned she realized she was no longer pushing it away because it was wrong. She was pushing it because she wanted it to be true, and wanting a thing to be true was exactly how her father had lost a barge in the Narrows, trusting a channel he liked over a channel that was real.

*The official story never matched the water.* That was what Nadi's grandson had said, back in the village, one night by the fire—some old river-country grievance, secondhand, half-remembered. *They said the guide ignored the warnings. But the warnings came from downstream, and the mountain came from upstream, and any fool who knows snow knows you can't read a slope from below it.* She'd barely listened. It had been firelight and fish and a boy showing off for strangers.

Now it sat in her chest like a second splinter, right next to the poster.

* * *

They made camp that night in a cutbank willow thicket, cold camp, no fire—she'd insisted on no fire, and Gideon hadn't argued, which she noted, and resented herself for noting. Everything he didn't argue with now looked like guilt. Everything he did argue with looked like performance. There was no move left that read innocent, and she understood, with a tired kind of clarity, that this was how suspicion worked: it wasn't a conclusion, it was a lens, and once it was fitted you saw through nothing else.

They ate in silence. Above them the plateau stars came out, hard and white as the daylight had been. The river talked to itself over the shallows. Somewhere upstream, a long way off, an animal screamed once and stopped.

Gideon sat with his back against a willow trunk, the map—the dead man's map, Halloway's map—wrapped in oilcloth in his lap, and he didn't open it, and she realized he was holding it the way you hold a thing you're about to give away.

"Corren Pass," he said.

"Don't." She held up a hand. Then she lowered it, because she'd asked for this, and she had. "No. Go on."

He told it in a flat voice, a voice stripped down to the joists, and she listened the way she listened to water—reading the surface for what ran beneath. He told her about the survey contract, the consortium's road crew, the season pushed early because the Founder's office wanted the pass road open before the fiscal year turned. He told her about the warnings—the downstream readings, the warm week, the slope that had been weeping water for three days. He told her he'd filed a hold order, in writing, and the hold order had come back countersigned *overruled* by an office he hadn't known then was his own father's. He told her about the blast—unsanctioned, off the survey schedule, set at dawn by a consortium crew to clear a shelf of rock, because blasting in the cool of morning was cheaper than blasting in the heat of afternoon. And he told her about the sound the mountain made when it let go, and how it didn't sound like thunder, it sounded like a door.

Nine people. He said the names. All nine, from memory, unhurried, the way a man says the only prayer he still believes in.

"And then," Gideon said, and here his voice did change, went quieter, went somewhere she hadn't heard him go, "my father came to the tent. Aldous Nystrom. The Founder. And he told me that the blast record was already destroyed, and the hold order was already destroyed, and that there were two ways this could go. The consortium could take the loss honestly and lose the pass contract and the water concessions and everything the road was for. Or one young guide could have made a series of errors. Errors happen. Errors are survivable. Errors have a name, and the name can carry it." He looked at his hands. "He said it would break my mother's heart either way, but this way it wouldn't break her life. And I was twenty-three years old, and I believed him, for about a day, and then I understood what he'd done, and by then the story was in every paper in two countries and my name was the story."

Sera sat very still. The river talked. The stars burned.

"Why didn't you fight it?" she asked. It wasn't an accusation. She genuinely needed to know. "You had the hold order. Countersigned. Paper survives."

"It survived for eleven years," he said. "In a place he never found. And fighting it meant a hearing, and a hearing meant consortium lawyers, and consortium lawyers meant the blast record got examined in a room where the consortium owned the doors. And if I lost the hearing, the evidence died with it—sealed, destroyed, whatever they wanted. If I ran, the evidence lived." He almost smiled. It was the worst thing she'd ever seen him do with his face. "So I ran. Eleven years. I let the whole river country curse me because a curse is at least a true thing, and the alternative was a clean name built on a lie. Halloway was the first person in a decade who guessed what I was carrying. He didn't even guess right. He thought I was hiding from the consortium. I was hiding *inside* their lie, because it was the only place the evidence was safe."

Sera looked at him for a long time.

She wanted to say she believed him. She also wanted, with a smaller and uglier voice, to say she didn't—because not believing him would have been simpler, would have let her drop him at the next landing and wash her hands and go back to being a woman who ran cargo and trusted no one and lost nothing. Believing him meant the poster was a lie. Believing him meant the Founder of the consortium had fed his own son to the river country to bury a blast record. Believing him meant the thing they were carrying upstream wasn't a dead man's map anymore—it was a live round, and every mile they gained was a mile closer to a detonation.

"The boy in the village," she said. "Nadi's grandson. He said the warnings came from downstream and the mountain came from upstream." She watched Gideon's head come up. "I didn't know what he was talking about until now. It's still out there, Gideon. Eleven years, and the river country never believed the poster. Not all of it. Not deep down. Stories like yours don't die—they just go quiet and wait for the water level to drop."

He was looking at her like a man looking at dry land after a long time at sea, and she had to look away from it, because she hadn't decided yet. That was the truth of it, the truth under the truth: the story fit, the evidence fit, the man fit—but trust wasn't a verdict, trust was a channel, and you didn't run a channel on one reading. You ran it on what the water did next.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we run the gorge approach, and you tell me the rest. All of it. The second map, whatever Halloway stitched into that jacket, why the source matters, why your father's face is on the founder's seal—and why a man who's spent eleven years being no one suddenly can't stop being someone." She stood, and brushed off her trousers, and looked down at him sitting against the willow with the dead man's map in his lap. "And Gideon? The next time you let me walk into something blind, I'll put you off the boat myself, poster or no poster. Are we clear?"

"Clear," he said.

She rolled into her bedroll with her back to him and did not sleep, and somewhere past the middle of the night she heard him move—quiet, careful, the way he did everything—and when she cracked her eyes he was sitting up, awake, staring upstream at the black shape of the gorge with the map across his knees, and in the starlight his face was the face from the poster and not the face from the poster at all, and she understood that whatever he was staring at up that river was bigger than the both of them, and that they were going to see it in two days, and that nothing—no poster, no lie, no name—was going to stop him.

The sound reached them an hour before dawn: an engine, far off, on the water behind them.

Not one engine. Two.
