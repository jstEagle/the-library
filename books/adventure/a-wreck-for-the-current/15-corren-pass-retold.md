## 15. Corren Pass, Retold

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sera asked the question.

She didn't ask it the way I expected. She'd had the poster for two days, folded into her jacket pocket like something she was afraid of, and she'd said almost nothing since the trading post. She'd cooked dinner, checked the lines, patched a chafe in the mooring rope—all the small work of a river pilot keeping her hands busy so her mouth wouldn't start. But when the light finally went out of the sky and there was nothing left between us but fire and dark water, she sat down across from me with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, and she looked at me the way you look at a rapid before you commit to running it.

"Tell me about Corren Pass," she said. "Not the version in the newspapers. The one that's true."

I fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch. There are moments when you know a thing is going to cost you everything, and you do it anyway, because the alternative is carrying it another mile, and then another, until the weight kills you somewhere alone where nobody will even know what broke. Halloway had died with his truth unspoken for years. I'd watched what that did to a man's face at the end.

"I need you to understand something first," I said. "Whatever I tell you tonight, you can't unknow it. And if the consortium ever learns you know it, they'll come for you the way they came for Halloway."

"They're already coming for us," Sera said. "We've got their trackers on our bank and their brand on my fuel line. I'd rather know what I'm bleeding for."

So I told her.

* * *

"Nine years ago I was twenty-four years old," I began. "And I was, by every measure that mattered, the best mountain guide working the northern ranges. That's not vanity—it's just fact. I'd put forty-one parties over Corren Pass without an incident. Forty-one. I knew that mountain the way you know the Vane—every seam, every choke point, every place where the snowpack went rotten in a warm spell. Clients used to request me by name. My father used to say—" I stopped. Started again. "My father used to introduce me as *my son, who reads stone better than any man alive.*"

Sera didn't move. The fire cracked and settled.

"That spring, the consortium booked the pass. Not for mining—they had no claims up there. For a survey. They wanted to run seismic charges through the headwall to map what was underneath, because there were rumors of a deep aquifer feeding down toward the Vane basin. Same water we're chasing now. It always comes back to the same water." I turned the stick in my fingers. "The survey team needed a guide certified for high-altitude work, and the consortium's own people weren't rated for it. So they hired me. Through my father's company. Nystrom Expeditions. He brokered the contract himself."

"You didn't know what they intended?"

"I knew they intended charges. What I didn't know was the schedule." I could feel it all rising again—the taste of that morning, cold and mineral, the way the light had come off the icefield flat and white. "There was a warm front moving in. I'd flagged it three days running. The snowpack on the eastern face was loaded—two meters of fresh sitting on a crust of old melt, the whole slope hanging like a held breath. Standard protocol says no blasting within seventy-two hours of a load event like that. It's not a suggestion. It's the law up there, because the mountain enforces it whether you obey or not."

"And they blasted anyway."

"At dawn on the fourth day. Before I was even awake." My voice came out flatter than I meant it to. "They set the charges unsanctioned—no permit amendment, no safety review, no notification to me or my second. Nine of my clients were camped directly below the headwall. A school group's chaperone, two geologists, a honeymoon couple, four students from the technical college. Good people. People who trusted me because my name was on the permit and my father's company stood behind it."

Sera's breath caught audibly. "Gideon—"

"The blast itself wouldn't have done it. It was a small charge, poorly placed, into a slope I'd have refused outright if anyone had asked me. But it didn't need to be big. It just needed to be the wrong sound at the wrong time in the wrong snowpack." I watched the fire instead of her. "I heard it from the lower camp. One crack, deep, like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the slope let go."

I had never said this part aloud. Not to investigators, not to the inquiry board, not to the priest who'd tried to talk to me afterward. Saying it now, to a woman I'd known six weeks, on a riverbank nine hundred kilometers from that mountain, felt like opening a vein.

"I got to them in under twenty minutes. Twenty minutes is a miracle, up there. I dug four of them out alive. Four. I had two breathing when the rescue teams arrived." My hands had gone still around the stick. "One of them was a girl named Petra Voss. Nineteen years old. She held my hand while they worked on her, and she kept asking me if the others were okay, and I lied to her, and then she stopped asking, and then she stopped everything."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the bank, an animal moved through the reeds and went quiet.

"The inquiry took eleven weeks," I went on, because stopping would have meant never starting again. "And in those eleven weeks, I learned how a story gets built. The consortium produced blasting records showing a permitted charge on a different day—a forged log, timestamped clean. Their survey chief testified he'd consulted me about conditions and I'd approved. Three of their employees swore to it, one after another, rehearsed, consistent. And my father—" Here my voice did break, just slightly, and I hated it. "My father sat in that hearing room every single day. Front row. He wore his best coat. And when they asked him whether his son had signed off on the charge schedule, he looked the panel in the eye and said, *My son has always been careless with other people's lives.*"

Sera made a sound low in her throat—not quite a word.

"He didn't hesitate," I said. "That's the part I've never been able to get past. Not the lie itself. Men lie under pressure; I understand pressure. It's that he'd had time to prepare it. He'd sat with that sentence, polished it, chosen the words. *Careless with other people's lives.* Nine dead, and he handed them a phrase that fit in a headline." I finally looked at her. "He traded my name to bury the evidence. Because if the unsanctioned blast came out—if anyone traced the aquifer survey to what was under that pass—the consortium's whole water play downstream would have unraveled. Corren wasn't about mining. It was about finding the Vane's source and locking it away before anyone else knew it existed. The avalanche wasn't the crime. The avalanche was the cleanup."

The silence after that went on a long time. The river ran. I let it.

"So they blacklisted you," Sera said at last. "Two countries. No guiding certification anywhere in the territory. Every registry office in the region flags your name."

"Every one. Which is why I've spent nine years being no one. Trail work under aliases. Dock labor. Whatever pays cash and doesn't ask for papers." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I showed. "It turns out a man can live a long time without a name. You just stop expecting anyone to say it kindly."

* * *

Sera didn't speak for a long while after that. She poked the fire once, twice, and I understood she was giving herself time, the same way I'd given myself time before starting. When she finally looked up, her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady.

"The girl. Petra. Did they ever find out?"

"No one found out anything. That's the architecture of it. The inquiry closed. The families got settlements with confidentiality clauses. The consortium's survey data from Corren went into a sealed file—which is probably the same file Halloway's second map belongs to—and Aldous Nystrom got promoted to regional director inside the year." I shook my head slowly. "I used to think I'd feel better if I could prove it. Now I'm not sure proving it is survivable. Every time I've gotten close—every time someone leaked me a document, every time an old colleague offered to testify—they ended up ruined or gone. Halloway is the most recent. He carried a piece of this for thirty years, and look where carrying it got him."

"But you're carrying it upstream anyway."

"Halloway made me promise."

"You told me you refused him."

"I did refuse him. And then he died with my hand on his chest, and the promise stopped being his and started being mine." I met her eyes across the fire. "You should know what you're tied to now, Sera. If we're caught, they won't treat you as an accomplice. They'll treat you as leverage against me. Everything I just told you makes you more valuable to them, not less. You could still turn back at the next trading post. Take the boat, take your cut, and I'll walk the rest alone. Nobody would blame you."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket and pulled out the poster—the one with my face on it, the one offering a reward for *the Nystrom boy*—and she held it over the fire until the corner caught. She let it burn halfway down before she dropped it into the flames and watched it curl to ash.

"I don't turn back," she said simply. "Not for clients, not for rivers, not for men with famous fathers. You should know that about me by now."

Something in my chest loosened that I hadn't realized was tight. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and the firelight caught the hard line of her jaw. "Because here's what I believe, Gideon, and I want you to hear it plainly. I believe every word you said. I believed it before you said it—I watched you carry Halloway off that barge like he was made of glass, and men don't handle strangers that gently unless they know exactly how heavy a body can get. I believe your father buried nine people under a lie to protect a water monopoly. And I believe that if we make it to the source, whatever's in Halloway's map is going to crack this whole rotten thing open like ice going out in spring."

She paused, and her voice dropped.

"But I also believe your father knows exactly where we are right now. And I believe that man doesn't send posters and radio messages unless he's already planned what happens when they fail."

Downriver, faint through the dark, came a sound neither of us wanted to hear: the low diesel throb of an engine, running without lights, somewhere below the bend.

Sera was on her feet before I'd finished standing. "Get the map," she said. "We're moving tonight."
