## 16. Running the Checkpoint

The weir announced itself before we saw it—a gray smudge of light where the middle river should have been black, and beneath the engine's drone, a sound I'd only heard once before in my life: the flat, patient roar of water going over concrete.

I cut the throttle and let us drift. Gideon came forward without being called. He'd learned that much about boats in the last week, that silence traveled faster than orders.

"Checkpoint," I said.

"How many lights?"

I counted. "Four on the barrier. Two on the bank. Searchlight's sweeping the upstream eddy." I killed the running lamps and let my eyes adjust to the dark. "That's not a toll station, Gideon. That's a wall with men on it."

The Vane was in flood stage. Three days of rain in the headwaters, and the river had come down brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways, the banks gone soft and drowned, the channel markers either missing or lying about where the deep water ran. Nobody ran the middle river in this. Nobody ran it at all anymore, not past the second weir, not since the consortium had decided the river was theirs the way they'd decided everything else was theirs—by being the first to write their name on it.

Gideon crouched at the bow, one hand on the gunwale, watching the lights. In the dark his face was all angles and shadow, and I thought, not for the first time, that I was running a river with a man who was mostly river himself now—something that had learned to move fast and take no permanent shape.

"They'll want the boat," he said.

"They'll want the map."

"Same thing."

"Same thing," I agreed.

We'd talked about this at the last anchorage, or rather we'd talked around it, the way we talked around most things. Surrender the boat and we were done. The map might survive in Gideon's jacket, might survive in the stitching of Halloway's jacket inside Gideon's jacket, but we'd be on foot, in consortium country, with hunters on the bank and the dry season eating the river a foot a day. Every hour we lost was an hour the upper Vane came closer to becoming a staircase of dry rock we couldn't climb.

Or we ran it.

I'd been turning the run over in my head since sundown, the way you turn a stone over, looking for what's underneath. The weir was a low barrage—maybe four meters of concrete with a gated spill on the left and a fish ladder nobody had cleaned since the consortium bought the water rights. Below it, a chute where the river came off the apron in a standing wave, then a rock garden I couldn't see from here but remembered from the old pilot charts my mother kept rolled in oilcloth under her bunk. Charts from before the closures. Charts from when the river belonged to the river.

"Fish ladder's on the far side," I said. "Too narrow. We'd wrap."

"The spill gate's open."

"The spill gate's a meat grinder."

"The searchlight's on the eddy," Gideon said. "They're watching the place where a sensible boat would stop."

"And we are not a sensible boat."

"We are not a sensible boat," he agreed, and there was something in his voice I hadn't heard before—almost a laugh, worn thin, but real. "Sera. Your boat. Your call."

That was the thing about Gideon Nystrom, or whatever his name was. He never pushed. He laid the facts out like tools on a bench and stepped back, and made you own the decision, and I was starting to understand that this wasn't coldness. It was penance. A man who'd once made a call on a mountain and watched it bury nine people doesn't push anymore. He waits. He lets the river decide, and then he lives with what it decides.

But it wasn't his boat.

I looked at the water coming down at us—brown, fast, freighted with debris—and at the lights on the concrete, and I did the arithmetic I'd been doing all evening and kept getting the same answer, which was that the numbers were terrible and there was only one column worth adding.

"They'll board us if we drift in," I said. "They'll find the map. They'll find us. And whatever they do to us, they'll do it slow, because we've embarrassed them past the point where fast is enough." I put my hand on the throttle. "So we go over the spill. Port side, tight to the gate, where the water's still stacking before it breaks. We hit it square, we punch through the curl, and we pray the apron below doesn't have a hole in it the charts are too old to show."

"And if there's a hole in it?"

"Then we find out very quickly," I said, "and I'll apologize to you at the bottom."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment. The searchlight swept past a hundred meters downstream and its glow grazed the edge of his face, and I saw that he was afraid—actually afraid, not performing calm over it, just carrying it, the way he carried everything.

"Square," he said. "Port side. I'll hold the cargo."

* * *

We backed water first, drifting down the true channel with our engine idling to a whisper, letting the flood do the work. The weir grew out of the dark by degrees: the concrete lip shining wet, the spill gate a black rectangle of falling water on the left abutment, the guard platform above it a lit box where I could see the shapes of two men, one of them smoking, the smoke curling up into the searchlight beam like it was showing off.

The river wanted the gate. That was the thing about flood stage—every drop of the Vane was trying to get through that opening, and the water going in was smooth and glassy and utterly silent for the last three meters, and then it was thunder. A boat caught crosswise in that glass would roll before the crew finished screaming. A boat caught bow-on would go down clean and come up—maybe—forty meters later, if the apron was kind.

I'd run water like this twice in my life. Once at nineteen, showing off, and once at twenty-four, showing off for a man who didn't deserve it. Both times I'd been young enough to think the river would forgive me because I was having fun.

"Debris," Gideon said quietly.

I saw it. A whole tree, roots first, rotating slowly in the glass above the gate, its crown just breaking the surface like a drowned thing reaching up. If it went through ahead of us, fine. If it went through with us—

"We wait for it," I said.

"Searchlight's coming around."

It was. The beam was sweeping upstream now, patient as a lighthouse, walking the water toward us. In maybe forty seconds it would find a boat with no lights sitting in the current above a checkpoint weir, and then the night would fill up with rifles and loudspeakers and every choice would be made for us.

The tree went over the lip. It stood vertical for one impossible instant—roots skyward, trunk a black column against the falling water—and then the river took it under and the roar swallowed whatever sound it made.

"Now," I said, and opened the throttle.

The boat leapt. I brought her around in a hard arc that put the gate dead ahead, port gunwale kissing the edge of the glass, and the world went simple. There was the gate. There was the smooth water. There was the moment when the bow dropped and my stomach rose and the searchlight found us all at once, a white fist of light, and I heard a voice on a loudspeaker, tinny and furious, saying something that began with *halt* and never got to finish.

Then we were in it.

The falling water hit the deck like a building. The boat went under—I knew it went under, because the world stopped being air and became brown thunder, and I was thrown against the wheel, and the engine screamed a note engines are not built to scream, and then we were through, out the other side of the curtain, into the boiling trough below the apron, and the boat came up like something born.

I've been thrown by rivers before. I've been flipped, wrapped, and once, on the Sert, held under long enough to learn what my own panic sounds like. Nothing had ever hit me like that curtain. It was as if the river had picked the boat up in both hands and thrown her at the downstream water, and the only reason we weren't matchwood was that Gideon had lashed the cargo down with a mountaineer's rigging and was lying across the bow crate with his arms locked around it, taking the impact through his own spine.

"Apron!" he shouted, and I saw it—too late to avoid, barely in time to steer—the second drop where the concrete apron ended and the river fell again into the rock garden below. The charts had shown one drop. The flood had built another.

I threw my weight against the wheel. The bow came around two degrees, maybe three, and that was enough, because the boat went off the apron's edge slightly left of square and the second fall, though it hit us hard enough to crack a plank somewhere aft, put us into the chute instead of onto the rocks flanking it.

Then the rock garden.

I won't pretend I remember it clearly. What I remember is noise and water and Gideon's voice calling rocks from the bow—*left, hard left, dead ahead, right*—his eyes working in the dark better than mine, a man who'd spent his life reading terrain finding that white water is just terrain that moves. What I remember is a boulder the size of a chapel sliding past my elbow close enough to touch. What I remember is the boat grinding something beneath the hull, a long horrible shudder, and then clearing it, and then the river widening, and the lights of the weir shrinking behind us into a small furious constellation, and the loudspeaker's voice thinning to nothing, swallowed by the roar we'd already survived.

Then quiet. Real quiet, the kind that comes after. We were a kilometer downstream, maybe more, drifting in an eddy behind a gravel bar, the engine ticking as it cooled, and somewhere aft water was coming in through the plank we'd cracked, slow and steady, a sound like a small patient clock.

I let go of the wheel. My hands didn't want to open. I had to peel them off finger by finger.

"Well," I said.

Gideon sat up from the cargo crate. In the starlight I could see him moving each limb deliberately, inventorying himself, and I understood he was checking for what I was checking for, and that we were both doing it silently because neither of us wanted to hear the other's list.

"You're bleeding," I said.

He touched his eyebrow, looked at his fingers. "Head wound. Bleeds a lot, means nothing." He looked aft, at the water creeping across the deck boards. "She's taking it slow. We can patch that at dawn."

"We can patch it now."

"We can patch it now," he agreed, and climbed back to the stern without another word, and we bailed and patched by starlight, passing the pitch and the hammer back and forth in a rhythm that didn't need talking, and the whole time I kept waiting for the shaking to start and it never did, because there was too much still to do, and because some part of me had understood, going over that curtain, that I had stopped being a woman who ran rivers for money and become something else, something the consortium's checkpoints and loudspeakers and searchlights had made without meaning to.

They'd built a wall across the river. And the river had taught me, over thirty years, exactly one lesson: water always finds the way through, and once it's through, it doesn't apologize.

* * *

We made camp at dawn in a willow brake above a side channel, hull patched, cargo dried, the two of us sitting on the upturned spare fuel drum with a pot of coffee between us, watching the mist burn off the water. My hands had finally started shaking. Gideon pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"They'll have boats behind us by midday," he said. "They'll have radios on every bank above the gorge."

"They'll have us on posters by the weekend," I said. "I'll want a good likeness. Tell them I'm handsomer."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't, because his eye had gone to something downriver, and I followed it, and the coffee went cold in my mouth.

Smoke. Not cookfire smoke—too much of it, too black, too far upstream but closing, a smudge standing over the water where the weir was. And beneath it, faint on the flood-stage wind, a sound I'd grown up knowing and had spent the last week hoping not to hear again: the flat slap-and-throb of a consortium patrol boat's hull, running fast, coming downriver after us.

Gideon stood, and I saw him go very still, the way he did before hard ground.

"They're not waiting for the checkpoints anymore," he said. "They're hunting."
