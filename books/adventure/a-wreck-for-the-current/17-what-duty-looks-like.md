## 17. What Duty Looks Like

The cave sat forty feet above the river on the true left bank, a seam in the limestone that the flood seasons had hollowed out over centuries. Sera found it the way she found everything—by reading water. *See how the current kinks there,* she'd said, pointing at a smooth tongue of green sliding past a rock shelf. *Something's pushing it. Something that isn't the rock you can see.* She'd been right, as she had been right about the eddy behind the drowned willow, and the channel through the gravel bar, and a dozen other small mercies since the first weir.

Now Gideon stood at the cave mouth with his arms crossed against the evening chill, watching the Vane slide past in the last copper light, and tried to remember what it felt like to be a man whose name cost nothing to say.

Behind him, Sera had gotten a fire going—small, screened by a slab of stone so its glow wouldn't reach the water. She'd cooked rice with dried fish and some bitter green she'd gathered from the bank without asking his opinion, because she never asked his opinion about food or boats or anything else she considered hers. The smell filled the cave and made it, briefly, something other than a hole in a mountain.

"Eat," she said. "You've been standing there like a fence post for an hour."

"I'm thinking."

"You're brooding. There's a difference. Thinking has an end point." She held out the pot anyway, and he took it, because refusing her twice in one day had proven to be beyond him.

They'd run the middle checkpoint at flood stage two nights ago, in the dark, with the river carrying more water than any sane pilot would trust to a boat this size. He could still feel it in his hands—the rope burns across both palms where he'd held the bow line while Sera fought the wheel, the whole hull shuddering under him like a living thing trying to buck them off. They'd come through the weir gap with inches on either side. Inches. He'd heard the concrete scrape along the starboard strake like a knife drawn slowly from a sheath.

Afterward neither of them had spoken for an hour. Some things were too big for talk until they'd shrunk a little.

Now they'd shrunk enough, and what was left was this: a cave, a fire, a woman who kept looking at him when she thought he wasn't looking back, and a map stitched into a dead man's jacket lining that was going to take everything Gideon had left and burn it for fuel.

"Halloway," he said.

Sera looked up. "What about him?"

"I keep trying to remember his face. I got him a blanket off the barge deck. I gave him water. And I can't—" He set the pot down. "I looked at him for maybe four minutes total, Sera. Four minutes, and now he's the reason I'm running rapids at night past men with rifles. You'd think a person would leave a bigger impression than that."

"He left a map."

"He left a job." Gideon heard the flatness in his own voice and didn't like it. "That's all a promise is, once the person's dead. A job nobody can relieve you of."

Sera fed a stick to the fire. "You don't believe that."

"No," he admitted. "I don't."

* * *

He told her the parts he hadn't told anyone. Not the Corren Pass story—that would come later, or never; he still couldn't say which—but the smaller arithmetic underneath it. What invisibility actually cost, itemized daily, the way a pilot might itemize fuel.

"Every morning for six years I've woken up and done an inventory," he said. "Not of gear. Of exposure. Did I sign anything yesterday? Did anyone ask my surname? Is there a new man at the registry office who might pull the old files out of boredom? I know which clerks are lazy and which ones are thorough. I know which taverns water their beer and which ones gossip. I have a whole geography of this town built out of things to avoid." He turned his hands over, palms up, the rope burns scabbing dark. "It's not a life. It's a holding pattern. But it's mine, and it works, and every year it works it gets harder to imagine doing anything else."

"And now?"

"Now I'm carrying a dead prospector's survey data upstream to a valley the consortium kills people to keep closed. If I finish this, I can't go back to the holding pattern. The moment I hand that document to anyone who matters, I'm a name again. *Nystrom.* And Nystrom comes with nine graves attached and a blacklist in two countries and whatever else my father buried under it." He laughed, short and joyless. "You see the joke? The only way to keep myself safe is to fail. To lose the map, or sink the boat, or just stop—one hard day's walk back downriver and I could be nobody again by the end of the month."

Sera was quiet for a long time. The fire popped. Down below, the river made its endless argument with the rocks.

"Is that what you want?" she asked finally. "To be nobody?"

"It's what I chose."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at her. Firelight moved on her face, and he realized he'd been cataloguing her the way he catalogued everything—the set of her jaw, the scar through one eyebrow, the way she listened with her whole body turned toward you like a helm answering wind—and that somewhere between the Grinder and the portage and the night run through the checkpoint, the cataloguing had stopped being reconnaissance and become something else. Something he had no alias for.

"No," he said. "It's not what I want."

"Then say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that I want to stay the man you think I am. The one who handles a dying stranger gently. That man doesn't exist anywhere except here, in this boat, going the wrong direction for his own survival. And finishing Halloway's promise means walking out of that and into the open, where everyone who ever believed the official story gets to decide what I am all over again." His voice caught and he pushed through it. "I'm not afraid of dying on this river, Sera. I'm afraid of surviving it and having it not matter. Of trading the only self I've got left for a truth people read over breakfast and forget by noon."

She reached over and took one of his burned hands. Turned it over. Examined the scabs with a pilot's unsentimental attention.

"These'll heal stiff if you don't work them," she said. "Same as anything else." Then, before he could decide whether that was a metaphor: "Halloway picked you. Out of everyone on that dock, half-dead, he walked straight to you. People do that. They find the one person who'll carry it." She let go of his hand. "You think your father made you invisible. Maybe. But you're the one who's been guarding the door of that prison for six years, and you're the one holding the key now, and it looks to me like a rolled-up piece of jacket lining."

* * *

He slept badly, which was normal, but the dreams were new. In them he stood at the registry office window in Kestrel's Landing—the one he'd avoided for six years—and the clerk behind the glass was Halloway, gray-faced and smiling, sliding a form across the counter. *Name?* the dead man asked. And Gideon opened his mouth and produced nothing, because the alias dissolved on his tongue like sugar in the rain, and behind the clerk the shelves of files stretched back into darkness, every one of them labeled NYSTROM, every one of them burning.

He woke before dawn with his heart hammering and lay listening to the river. Sera slept curled near the fire pit, one arm under her head, breathing slow. Even asleep she looked like someone steering.

He got up quietly and took Halloway's jacket from the cargo bundle. Sat cross-legged with it across his knees and unpicked the stitching again, though he already knew what was inside. The second map. The survey data. Aquifer lines running three provinces deep, springs gauged and fenced, the source of the Vane not a mystery but a *holding*—somebody's property, somebody's leverage, sealed behind bribes and checkpoints and a forbidden-zone legend that travelers repeated like weather lore.

And beneath the data, in Halloway's cramped hand, a note Gideon had read a dozen times now:

*They bought the water before anyone knew they could. By the time the wells fail downstream, the paper will be twenty years old and untouchable. Someone has to carry this while it can still break them. I carried it thirty years. I'm tired.*

*I'm tired.* Two words, and the whole weight of a man's life in them.

Gideon folded the document along its creases and put it back inside the jacket, and sat there as the cave filled with gray light, and did the thing he'd been circling for days: he counted the cost honestly, like a guide counting rope lengths before a descent.

If he went forward, he lost the alias. Lost the low-paying trail jobs that asked no questions. Lost the choreography of avoidance that had become, without his noticing, the entire architecture of his days. Lost the safety of being no one worth hunting. Possibly lost his freedom—blacklists came with warrants in some jurisdictions, and Aldous Nystrom's reach had never stopped at borders. Possibly lost Sera, too, though he didn't let himself look directly at that one; it was too bright, like staring at sun on water.

And if he turned back—if he walked away, sank the papers, reclaimed the holding pattern—he kept all of that. Kept the alias like a coat that fit worse every winter. Kept his silence, which was the consortium's favorite sound.

Kept, also, the knowledge that Halloway had died ten feet from him, having asked one thing of one man, and that the man had said no with his feet even if his mouth had said nothing at all.

Duty, he thought, was a word guides used for clients they didn't like. But this wasn't duty. Duty was external, a harness someone else buckled on you. This was older and simpler: a dying man's hand closing around yours in the dark, and the unshakable animal knowledge that what passes between two people in that moment doesn't belong to either of them afterward. It belongs to the crossing.

He'd spent six years keeping his name locked in a drawer. But a name wasn't the same as a self. The self—the one who'd checked Halloway's pulse, who'd hauled line through the Grinder, who'd watched Sera sleep and felt something in his chest he refused to name yet—had survived the avalanche, the trial, the blacklist, the long gray years of Kestrel's Landing. It had been waiting. Halloway's map was just the door it had been waiting behind.

"All right," he said aloud, very quietly, so as not to wake her. "All right. Upstream, then. All the way."

The word *all* did most of the work. Not partway. Not as far as convenient, then vanish down some tributary of a new life. All the way—to the source, to the gauging stations, to whatever confrontation waited at the headwaters with the man who had traded his son's name for a company's secrets. If the truth was going to cost him his anonymity, then the truth was going to get its full value. He would spend himself completely or not at all.

Behind him, Sera stirred. "Talking to yourself is the first sign," she mumbled into her arm.

"Of what?"

"Dramatic temperament. Very common in passengers." She sat up, hair flattened on one side, eyes already sharp. "So. Have you decided whatever it was you decided at three in the morning, loudly, in whispers?"

"Yes."

She waited. She was good at waiting; it was half her trade.

"We go to the source," he said. "Both maps. Everything. And when we get there, I use my real name."

Something moved through her face—relief, and under it a flicker of fear she didn't bother hiding, because hiding things wasn't how they operated anymore, not since the checkpoint, not since the night the river nearly ate them. "You understand what that means."

"I understand exactly what it means."

"Do you?" She crawled over to the fire pit and started coaxing the coals. "Because the man on that poster at the trading post—the reward, the *Nystrom boy*—that's not a wanted notice, Gideon. That's a leash. The moment you claim the name, Aldous knows where you are every hour of every day. He knew about the checkpoints. He knew about the hunters. He'll know about the cave." She struck a match and touched it to kindling. "There's no version of this where you hand over the data and slip away clean. You know that, yes?"

"I've known it since the portage."

"And you're choosing it anyway."

"I'm choosing it anyway."

She looked at him across the small flame, and whatever she saw seemed to settle something in her, because she nodded once, the way she nodded when she committed the boat to a channel—no second-guessing, no looking back at the safer water. "Then we'd better move. Dry season's eating the river a foot a day. Another week and even I can't get us to the headwaters."

They broke camp fast, the practiced choreography of two people who'd learned each other's rhythms on the water. Gideon stowed the jacket with the documents double-wrapped in oilcloth. Sera doused the fire and scattered the ashes. At the cave mouth he paused once, looking down at the Vane running low and clear over its stones, glittering like spilled coins in the early light.

Somewhere up there, past the narrows and the gorges and whatever the consortium had left between here and the springs, was a valley full of fenced water and a father with a helicopter. Somewhere behind him was a town where a man named Marlow Voss could live out his whole unremarkable life and die unremarkable and free.

He turned his back on that and climbed down to the boat.

They'd been on the water less than an hour when Sera, scanning the far bank with her field glasses, went very still.

"Gideon." Her voice had gone flat, which he'd learned meant trouble. "Smoke. Thin, black, deliberate—a signal fire. And there's a boat pulled up under the alders." She passed him the glasses. "Three men. One of them is watching us through optics of her own."

He found her through the lenses instantly: a lean figure on the bank, rifle slung, making no attempt at concealment. A tracker who wanted to be seen. Who wanted *him* to see her.

"She's not shooting," Gideon said slowly.

"No," Sera agreed, easing the throttle. "Which worries me a lot more than if she were."
