## 18. The Tracker's Mercy

Sera heard the dogs before she saw anything else—a thin belling sound off the portside bank, carried flat across the water the way sound travels at dawn when the river is holding its breath.

She killed the engine mid-stroke and let the boat drift.

"Gideon." She said it once, low, and he was already awake. He'd been sleeping badly since the checkpoint, curled in the bow with Halloway's jacket bundled under his head like a pillow he didn't trust anyone else to hold. Now he came up on one elbow without a word, eyes going to the bank, reading it the way she read water.

"Two hundred meters back," he said. "The bend."

"I know."

They'd made nine miles before first light, running dark with a single running lamp hooded by a tin plate Sera had riveted over it years ago for exactly this kind of morning. The plan had been to push through the Narrows before the consortium's people got organized, to put distance between themselves and the wreckage of the fuel line, the slashed hose, the tattooed wrist she'd seen in the lantern glow three nights ago. They'd almost done it. Almost was a word the river liked to punish.

The bend behind them produced a boat.

It came around the cutbank fast—aluminum, shallow-draft, twin outboards, the kind of rig consortium money bought when it wanted something caught. A man stood at the bow with a rifle slung loose across his chest, not aimed, which was its own kind of message: *we don't need to aim yet.* Behind him, two more boats. Behind those, somewhere up on the ridge, the dogs.

"Vess," Gideon said quietly.

"You know that?"

"I've been listening to how they move." He sat up fully now, and Sera watched him do the thing he did—go still, go economical, shed everything unnecessary until what remained was just a man watching a problem approach. "They don't run their engines wide open like hired muscle does. She's pacing us. She's been pacing us since the fuel line."

Sera's stomach dropped. "You think she cut it?"

"I think whoever cut it wanted us slowed down enough to be found tired." He looked at her. "Not dead."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No," Gideon said. "I think it's supposed to make you think."

* * *

There was nowhere to run and both of them knew it. Ahead, the river braided out into gravel shoals where the dropping water would ground them inside an hour. Behind, three aluminum hulls eating the current. The banks were scree and jackpine, no cover worth the name, and the dogs would find them in any of it. Sera had spent eleven years learning every way a river could kill you, and none of them were faster than a bullet.

So she did the only thing left. She turned the boat broadside to the current, let it swing like a compass needle, and waited.

Vess's boat came alongside with a bump of rubber fender against hull, gentle as a dockhand. Up close, the lead hunter was smaller than Sera had imagined—she'd built her into something out of the stories, six feet of consortium iron—and younger, too, maybe thirty, with a sun-cracked face and gray coming in early at her temples. Her rifle stayed slung. Her hands stayed visible on the gunwale, which Sera recognized as deliberate, a language spoken between people who both carried weapons and both knew better than to reach.

"Sera Okonkwo," Vess said. "Pilot's license out of Marrow Bend, suspended twice, reinstated twice. You run cargo nobody asks about."

"And you're the one who's been chasing us up half a province," Sera said. "We should trade dossiers. Mine's shorter."

Something moved at the corner of Vess's mouth—not quite a smile, more like the place a smile would live if it had ever been allowed to build a house there. Then her eyes went past Sera to Gideon, and the warmth, such as it was, closed like a door.

"The map," Vess said. "And whatever else Halloway was carrying. Hand it across and you two walk out of this valley. I'll log you as drowned at the Grinder. It happens. People drown."

"And if we don't?"

"Then my orders change." She said it without theater, without relish, and somehow that was worse. "I've got twenty men on this river who get paid whether or not I like my orders. I'd rather not find out which of them enjoy the work."

Gideon shifted in the bow. Sera put a hand back without looking, found his forearm, pressed once. *Wait.*

"Your orders," Sera said. "From whom? The consortium's land office? Because here's what I know about your employer, Vess. They sealed a river. Not a mine—a *river*. Whole watershed, fenced and gauged and written off the maps, and every man they send upstream gets told it's about claim-jumping or smuggling or some other story that doesn't survive contact with the water itself. So before you take anything off this boat, ask yourself why it takes an army to guard a spring."

Vess's jaw tightened. "You don't know what you're carrying."

"Neither do you," Sera said. "That's my point."

For a long moment the only sounds were water working against the hulls and the far-off dogs, patient on their ridge. Then Gideon moved—not toward Vess, but down, into the bottom of the boat, and came up with Halloway's jacket.

Sera's hand closed on his sleeve. "What are you doing?"

"What she asked for," Gideon said. "Sort of."

He held the jacket out—not the map, Sera saw, not the folded oilskin packet with the hand-drawn source route that rode wrapped in waxcloth inside his own shirt. Just the jacket, the dead man's coat, salt-stiff and patched at the elbows, with its hidden stitching already split from the night they'd found the second document.

"This is his survey book," Gideon said. "Halloway spent four seasons taking readings most of the way up this river before the consortium ran him out. Flow rates. Aquifer levels. Well depths in every settlement downstream of the middle gorge. It's not a treasure map, Vess. It's a ledger. Read it before you decide what your orders are worth."

Vess didn't move. "That's a trick."

"It's a dead man's homework," Gideon said. "He died on a freight barge dock with it under his coat, coughing up blood, asking a stranger to carry it upstream because nobody downstream would listen to him anymore. I made him a promise over his body. I'd rather keep it than use it as a shield. But if you're going to take it either way, then take it with your eyes open."

The dogs were closer now. Sera could hear the handlers' whistles threading through the belling, calling them in, holding them at the ridgeline. Everything about the morning felt like a held breath.

Vess reached out and took the jacket.

* * *

She read standing up, one boot braced on her own gunwale, the survey book balanced on her forearm the way soldiers read field orders. At first it was fast—flipping, scanning, the impatient page-work of someone confirming a suspicion. Then it slowed.

Sera watched it happen. Watched the flipping stop. Watched Vess go back three pages, then forward two, then back again, her thumb pinning a page while her eyes ran the columns of figures. Halloway's handwriting had been small and exact, a draftsman's script, numbers stacked like masonry. Static levels. Draw-down curves. Dates going back four years.

"Where did he sample these wells?" Vess asked. Her voice had changed. Flatter. Careful.

"He marked coordinates," Gideon said. "Cross-reference them yourself."

Vess didn't need to. Sera watched her lips move faintly, matching numbers to places she clearly knew by heart, and understood before Vess said it.

"Kettle Row," Vess said. "These are Kettle Row wells. And Harrow Flats. And—" She stopped. Turned a page. Stopped again.

"How deep are your wells supposed to be?" Gideon asked quietly.

"Fifty feet." Vess's voice was mechanical now, reciting. "Fifty feet and you hit good water. My grandmother drew from the same well her whole life. Last summer we drilled to ninety. This spring, eighty-five." She looked up, and her eyes were doing something complicated—anger and arithmetic fighting for the same space. "The company told us the aquifer was seasonal. Told us it was drought. Paid for the drilling and called it community investment."

"Halloway's numbers say the draw started the year the weirs went in," Gideon said. "The year the source was fenced. He charted it settlement by settlement. The further downstream, the worse the curve—but it reaches everywhere. Three provinces drink out of this watershed, Vess. The consortium isn't hoarding gold up that valley. They're hoarding the tap."

"They wouldn't." Vess said it automatically, reflexively, the way a person says *the floor is solid* while standing on a bridge. But her thumb was still pinned to the page, and she wasn't looking at Gideon anymore. She was looking at the numbers, at the neat descending columns, at four years of a dead man's patience laid out in ink.

"My sister's kids bathe out of a cistern truck now," she said, mostly to herself. "Twice a week. The company brings it. Everyone says how generous."

Nobody answered her. There wasn't anything to say that the page hadn't already said.

One of her men called out from the trailing boat—something about the shoals, about losing the light, about wanting orders. Vess raised a hand without turning around. The voice shut up.

She read for another five minutes. Sera let the boat drift, kept her hands off the ignition, and studied the woman the way she'd study an unfamiliar rapid: looking for the line, the channel through, the rock that looked soft and wasn't. Vess had come upriver on consortium wages for however many years, chasing smugglers and claim-jumpers and probably a few genuinely bad men, and she had done it believing—or needing to believe—that the fence around the source guarded something that deserved guarding. Sera knew that kind of belief. She'd carried her own version of it for years after Marrow Bend, after the accident, after the license board took two years of her life and gave back a stamp and a shrug. Belief in the system was just ballast. It kept you upright right up until the moment it dragged you under.

Vess closed the book.

For a moment Sera genuinely thought she might hand it back and take them all prisoner anyway, because orders were orders and a paycheck was a paycheck and a sister's kids needed cistern trucks regardless of whose signature was on the weir permits. That was how the machine worked—it didn't need villains, just people too busy to look down.

Instead, Vess opened her mouth, closed it, and then did something Sera had never seen a consortium hunter do.

She laughed. One short, ugly syllable, with no humor anywhere in it.

"I led forty men up this river," she said. "Burned a fish camp at Second Bend because the elder wouldn't tell me who was moving cargo. Put a boy in irons for three days for selling fuel without a chit." She looked at the book in her hand like it had teeth. "All of it to protect a fence around the wellhead of every drop of water my family will ever drink."

"The numbers could be wrong," one of her men offered from the second boat—hopeful, loyal, reaching for the rope.

"They're not wrong." Vess said it with finality, the certainty of someone who had grown up on that water and could feel the truth of it in her own lowered bucket. She turned back to Gideon, and Sera watched her weigh him—the blacklisted guide, the drifter with the careful hands, the man the posters called dangerous—and whatever equation she ran, it came out different than the consortium's version.

"Where are you taking it?" she asked. "The data."

"Downstream," Gideon said. "Every radio that'll carry it. Every settlement office. Publicly, under my own name—which, fair warning, is a name your employers have spent money burying."

"Nystrom," Vess said. Not a question. "The Corren Pass guide."

"You've seen the poster."

"I wrote the requisition for the printing." Something flickered across her face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "My handlers believe you're a saboteur. They believe it very firmly, and they pay me to believe it with them."

"And now?"

Vess looked down at the survey book. Out at the braided shoals ahead, where the falling water was laying the river's bones bare a little more each day. Back at Sera, at the boat, at the whole improbable wreck of their expedition.

"Now I'm going to log that I cornered a derelict boat at dawn and found nothing but salvage gear and two ghosts," she said. "I'm going to report that the trail goes cold above the Grinder, and I'm going to recommend pulling the shore teams back to the middle weir because the upper river's dropping and it's a waste of payroll." She handed the book back to Gideon, handle-first, formal as a sword returned. "That buys you maybe four days. Maybe five, if the dogs come up lame."

"Why?" Sera asked. It came out harder than she meant. "Why turn?"

Vess stowed her rifle across her knees and looked at Sera with an expression that had finally, at long last, found room for something like honesty.

"Because I've been a captive longer than you have," she said. "You two still thought you were free. I grew up drinking that water." She stepped back onto her own boat, and the fender bumped free, and the current took up the small gap between the hulls and widened it. "Kettle Row's cistern runs Tuesdays and Fridays. If the company ever stops sending the truck, there won't be anyone left who remembers why."

She raised two fingers off her gunwale—not quite a salute, not quite a wave—and her boats swung away downstream, engines throttled low, professional to the last, a hunting party converting itself back into a patrol in front of witnesses.

The dogs on the ridge went quiet, whistled home.

* * *

They didn't speak for a mile. Sera ran the channel slow through the braid, picking her way between gravel spits the dropping water was exposing hour by hour, and Gideon sat in the bow with the survey book open on his knees, not reading it, just resting his hand on it like a man resting his hand on a shoulder.

Finally he said, "You covered for me back there. With the map. You didn't know what I was going to do."

"I never know what you're going to do," Sera said. "It's becoming a hobby."

"She could have taken everything. We'd be in irons at the middle weir right now."

"She could have." Sera eased the hull past a snagged sweeper, close enough to hear the wood groan against the current. "But she didn't, and you want to know why, and I'll tell you, because I watched her face while she read. It wasn't mercy, Gideon. Mercy is giving something you owe. What she did was arithmetic. She finally got shown the full bill for a thing she'd been paying on her whole life, and she couldn't unsee it." She glanced at him. "People like us, we chose our exile. She was born inside hers. That's not the same wound, but it bleeds the same direction."

Gideon was quiet a moment. "Four days," he said. "Maybe five."

"Less." Sera nodded at the water sliding past the hull, clearer now, thinner, running with a high bright urgency that any pilot learned to dread. "Look at it. That's snowmelt gone and dry season settling in. Four days is what she's buying us on paper. The river's only promising three."

Ahead, the valley walls began to lean together, and the light in the gorge went from gold to copper, and somewhere beyond the next bend the upper river waited with its knife rocks and its narrowing sky.

Gideon closed the survey book and tucked it inside his shirt beside the map, next to his heartbeat.

"Three days, then," he said. "Let's not waste the dog's lameness."
