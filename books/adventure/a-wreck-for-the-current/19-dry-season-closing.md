## 19. Dry Season Closing

The river told us before the gauges did.

I woke on the third morning after Vess let us go and knew something was wrong by the sound—or rather, by the absence of it. For two weeks the Vane had been a constant voice beside us, that low gravel mutter of a big river working its bed. Now it had gone quiet in the shallows, reduced to a whisper over the gravel bar where we'd tied up, and the bar itself had grown overnight, pale stones surfacing like bones through skin.

"Look at that," I said.

Sera was already crouched at the waterline, forearms on her knees, reading the current the way other people read letters. She didn't answer right away. She never did when the river was talking to her; she said it was rude to interrupt.

"Down eight inches since yesterday," she said finally. "Maybe ten." She stood and wiped her hands on her thighs. "Dry season's closing early this year. Snowpack up top must have failed."

"How long?"

She looked upstream, toward the canyon country we still had to cross, and I watched her do arithmetic she didn't like.

"Four days," she said. "Five if we're lucky. After that, the upper Vane isn't a river anymore. It's a staircase with puddles."

* * *

We spent the morning measuring what we'd already lost.

The boat—Halloway's old freight skiff, patched twice since the narrows, scarred along her port side where the gunboat had clipped us—drew less water than most, but not none. Sera sounded the channel below camp with a broken oar marked off in hand-spans, calling depths back to me while I wrote them in the margin of Halloway's map, which by now was soft as cloth from handling, its ink lines ghosted with sweat and spray.

"Two spans," she called. "One and a half. One—" A pause. "Gideon, it's one span here. That's six inches over the guard rail of the prop shaft."

I wrote it down anyway. Numbers were numbers, even when they were bad news. Especially then.

We spread everything out on the tarp: Halloway's first map, the hand-drawn one he'd pressed into my hands over his own dying body; the survey sheets from the hidden stitching, covered in aquifer data and elevation figures in his cramped, patient hand; and Sera's own pilot charts of the middle Vane, annotated in pencil with every hazard she'd memorized in eleven years of running cargo past checkpoints that weren't supposed to exist.

The three documents didn't agree. That was the problem.

"Halloway drew this from the bank," Sera said, tapping the upper gorge on his map. "He walked it. Look—he marks a channel through the Split, here, the east fork. But my charts say the east fork doesn't carry enough water to float a canoe even in spring flood."

"Your charts are consortium surveys."

"My charts are *accurate* consortium surveys," she said. "They lie about who owns things, not about how deep the water is. They want pilots to trust them. Dead pilots don't pay fees."

She had a point. The consortium's power rested on controlling the river, and you couldn't control a river people were afraid of. So their charts were good—the best on the water—and they said the east fork of the Split was a rock garden with a trickle down the middle.

"And the west fork?" I asked.

"Runs deep. Runs clean. And runs directly under the guns of the last fortified weir before the headwaters." She sat back on her heels. "That's not an accident, Gideon. They built the weir where the only safe channel goes. Force of geography and force of arms, all in one place."

I looked at the maps until my eyes burned. Somewhere in the overlap between a dead man's observations and a monopolist's hydrography there had to be a line we could follow. There always was. That was the whole discipline of guiding—not finding the easy way, but finding the way that existed whether you liked it or not.

* * *

By noon the temperature had climbed into the high nineties and the river kept dropping as if someone downstream had pulled a plug.

We passed the day doing the work that couldn't wait. I recaulked the seams Sera pointed out, working pitch into the hull with a heated knife while sweat ran into my eyes. She stripped and cleaned the engine, which had been running rough since the chase, and came up from the bilge with grease to her elbows and a verdict: "She'll run. Not fast, and not for long if we ask her to fight current."

Above us, the canyon walls threw heat like ovens. The air smelled of dust and pine resin and the particular mineral stink of exposed riverbed. Twice I saw fish stranded in pools cut off from the main channel, circling in water going warm and thin. By evening those pools would be gone.

It's a strange thing, watching a river die. You'd think water would be the one thing you could count on. But rivers are promises made fresh every day, and the dry season breaks them on a schedule nobody controls.

Sera cooked rice and dried fish over a smokeless fire and ate looking upstream, jaw working, thinking. I knew better than to interrupt that either.

Finally she said, "Say your piece. You've been winding up to it since breakfast."

"The east fork," I said.

"It's suicide."

"It's four days of suicide or five days of nothing. Those aren't the choices. The choice is which kind of dead we'd rather be."

She set down her tin plate. In the last light her face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment I saw her the way the river must see her—as something carved to fit exactly this life, no wasted material anywhere.

"Walk me through it," she said.

So I did. I laid Halloway's map flat and put my finger on the line the old man had drawn, and I talked the way I used to talk to clients before Corren Pass, before the blacklist, back when my judgment was worth money instead of shame. The east fork dropped faster but ran narrower, which meant the water it still carried would be concentrated, not scattered across a wide bed. Narrow channels hold depth longer in a falling river. It was the same physics that let a mountain stream stay runnable in August while the broad valley rivers went to gravel.

"Concentrated flow," Sera repeated. "Through a rock garden."

"Through a rock garden Halloway walked in a dry year. Look at his notes." I turned the map so she could read the marginal scrawl: *E fork low but live — 3 chutes, all runnable R bank line — portage 200 yds at falls.* "He wasn't a pilot. He was a walker. But he was a careful walker, and he drew what he saw."

"He drew it nine years ago."

"Rivers change slow in canyons. Canyons protect them." I tapped the walls on either side of the drawn channel. "No floodplain here to shift the bed. What he saw is mostly what's there."

"*Mostly,*" she said. But she was leaning over the map now, and I could feel her resistance changing state, solid melting toward liquid. That was the thing about Sera Okonkwo: she fought an idea until it beat her, and then she committed to it completely, and God help anything standing in its path.

"There's one more thing," I said, and hated myself a little for saving it, because it was the argument that mattered and I'd wanted her to arrive at the rest on her own. "The west fork means the weir. We'd be running straight at the last fortress on the river with a patched hull and a tired engine, in daylight, in a drought, with every man on that wall knowing we're coming because there's nowhere else to go. Vess let us go once. Her handlers won't let her do it twice. The west fork is where they'll be waiting, because the west fork is the only sane route."

"And the east fork is insane."

"The east fork is *unwatched*. Nobody guards a channel that doesn't exist."

Sera was quiet a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere above us a hawk screamed once and gave up, too hot even for hunting.

"You understand what you're asking," she said at last. "If your dead prospector misjudged one chute—if one of those three drops is two inches shallower than he measured—we hit rock at speed in a canyon with no eddy to catch us. No rescue. No portage option. The walls are sheer for miles."

"I know."

"And if we take the west fork instead, we get shot, boarded, and the map ends up in Aldous Nystrom's safe by Friday." She exhaled through her nose. "You know what the worst part is? Your logic is sound. Concentrated flow, protected channel, unguarded approach. It's the kind of route I'd design if I were building a trap."

"Or a door."

"That depends entirely on which side of it you're standing." She picked up her plate again, then didn't eat, just held it. "Give me until dawn. I want to dream about it. Sometimes the river tells me things asleep that it won't say awake."

* * *

She dreamed. I kept watch.

There wasn't much to watch for—Vess was a day behind us at best, and the hunters' boats couldn't follow us into water this thin—but I sat up anyway, because sleep had gone somewhere else. I sat with Halloway's jacket across my knees, the one with the hidden stitching, and turned the survey sheets over and over, though I had them memorized down to the smudges.

Aquifer recharge zones. Three provinces. Forty settlements downstream whose wells drank from whatever fell as rain in the valley we were trying to reach. The consortium hadn't fenced off gold—they'd fenced off the region's lungs, and filed papers saying the springs were seasonal seeps, negligible, unworthy of public claim. Halloway had spent years walking the headwaters with a surveyor's eye and a smuggler's patience, proving the opposite, and died in a border town full of strangers rather than let the proof die with him.

And I had almost refused him. I lay awake remembering that—remembering saying *find someone else*, remembering the relief I'd felt at refusing, the clean feeling of a man protecting his small invisible life. Then dawn had come with him cold in the alley behind the ferry house, and guilt had arrived ahead of consent, exactly the way it always does, exactly the way it had at Corren Pass when I'd signed the report my father put in front of me because signing was easier than the alternative.

Every cowardice in my life had been a shortcut. Every one of them had cost more than the honest road would have.

The stars wheeled. The river got quieter. Around two in the morning I heard a boulder shift somewhere upstream, a deep grinding knock, the sound of a channel rearranging itself while we weren't looking, and I thought: *even the river is voting.*

* * *

At gray light Sera sat up without a word, walked to the waterline, and stood there for the length of time it takes to boil tea. Then she came back and started loading the boat.

"East fork," she said.

"You slept on it."

"I slept on it and the river answered." She handed me the bow line. "I dreamed about my father. He taught me to read water, back on the delta. He used to say there are two kinds of pilots: the ones who ask the river's permission and the ones who ask its forgiveness. He said permission is cheaper." She cinched down a crate. "But he also said the river only respects you when you ask for something hard. Easy requests, it forgets you by lunchtime."

"Is that approval?"

"That's a woman who's made her decision and doesn't want to discuss it further." She swung aboard and took the tiller. "Get in, Gideon. We've got maybe ninety miles of impossible left and the water's leaving faster than we are."

I cast off. The skiff slid out over gravel that should have been three feet under, and her keel whispered against stone for the first hundred yards, a sound like a file drawn slowly across bone, and then the channel deepened just enough and we were moving.

By midmorning the banks had risen into canyon walls, red and black banded stone, and the heat came down the gorge like a lid closing. The river shrank as we climbed—braids collapsing into single threads, sandbars joining into bars, the current quickening as the same volume of water crowded into less room. Sera read it aloud as we went, half to herself: "Drop's accelerating. Two feet since yesterday, I'd swear it. At this rate the east fork entrance will be barely wet by tomorrow night."

"Then we get there tonight."

"We get there tonight," she agreed, "and pray your dead man could measure."

We ran all day, engine at a laboring pitch, past country that grew stranger and more beautiful the higher we went—hanging gardens in the shade seams, bighorn watching us from ledges, ancient flood debris lodged in cracks forty feet above the present water, evidence of a younger, fatter river that had made all these promises long ago and now, in the dry season's end, was taking nearly all of them back.

Toward late afternoon I took the depth soundings again and didn't like any of them, and neither did she, and we didn't say so, because some knowledge is a weight you only have to carry if you speak it.

It was almost dusk when the canyon split ahead of us.

I heard it before I saw it—a widening of echoes, the river's voice doubling—and then the walls parted around a great island of stone, and the Vane divided into its two throats. To the west, the water ran broad and dark and smooth, sliding obediently toward the distant silhouette of the weir tower, toward the guns and the floodlights and the men paid to make sure nothing like us ever reached the source. To the east, the channel narrowed to a slot between ribs of rock, and what water remained went through it in white braided ropes, loud and angry and alive.

Sera brought us into the slack water below the island and held us there, engine idling, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. The east fork roared. The west fork waited.

"Last chance for sanity," she said quietly.

I looked at the east fork and tried to see it the way Halloway had seen it, nine years ago, an old man with a notebook standing on these same stones in another dry autumn. *Three chutes, all runnable R bank line.* Right bank line. Keep right, keep right, keep right.

"Sera," I said. "The map says—"

"I know what the map says." She pushed the throttle forward and swung the bow toward the roaring slot, and the last light caught her face, and she was smiling, which frightened me more than the rapids did. "Map says impossible. River says tonight or never. Hold on to something, Gideon."

The east fork took us.
