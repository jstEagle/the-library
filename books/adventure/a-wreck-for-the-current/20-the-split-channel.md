## 20. The Split Channel

The river forked at dawn, and the map lied about it.

Sera stood in the bow with Halloway's map flattened against her thigh, one thumb pinning the corner against the wind, and stared at the thing she'd been dreading since the Grinder: a clean split in the Vane, two channels peeling apart around a gravel island half a mile long and furred with deadfall. The left channel ran wide and shallow, braided silver over sandbars, easy to read from here. The right channel dove between rock walls, dark water moving fast, no visible bars.

The map showed one channel. One. A single blue line drawn confident and thick up the center of the valley, as if the river had never once considered disagreeing with itself.

"Tell me you're seeing what I'm seeing," Gideon said behind her.

"I'm seeing that Halloway drew this from memory, or from someone else's memory, or forty years ago when the river had a different idea." She turned the map over, checked the back, checked it again out of pure spite. Nothing. No annotation, no warning, no little skull-and-crossbones sketch to mark the spot where the cartography gave up. "The dry season's dropped us another foot overnight. Look at the left channel—you can see the bottom of it from here."

"So we take the right."

"So I don't know." She said it flatly, because lying to him about this would be worse than useless. "Right channel's deeper, probably. Deeper means runnable. It also means I can't see what's in it until I'm committed, and by then I'm committed. Left channel I can read like a book, but every page of it says *ground*."

Gideon came forward and stood beside her, close enough that she could feel the heat coming off his shoulder. He'd slept badly—she'd heard him turning on the boards most of the night—and there was a rawness around his eyes that hadn't been there before the canyon, before the loudspeakers started offering them amnesty for a piece of paper. He studied the fork the way he studied everything now, like a man doing arithmetic with his own life.

"How much time do we have?" he asked.

"If we mean to beat the dry season to the source? We needed three days ago." She folded the map along its worn creases. "But for this decision specifically? About ten minutes. That fog bank upstream is going to burn off, and once it does, anyone with glass on those ridgelines can see exactly which channel we take."

"You think they're watching."

"I think Vess is smart enough to watch, and I think her handlers are dumb enough to have given her a gunboat with a hole in it instead of letting her do her job properly. Which buys us maybe a day. Maybe less." Sera looked at the left channel again, really looked, reading the water the way she'd been reading water since she was nine years old standing on her father's barge deck with a pole too big for her. The braids shifted even as she watched—a bar migrating, a thread of current cutting loose and re-forming. Shifting bars meant unstable bottom, meant the channel that looked passable at dawn could be a gravel garden by noon. "Left is a gamble against the river changing its mind. Right is a gamble against my own eyes."

"Which gamble do you trust?"

And there it was—the question, laid down plain, the way Gideon had learned to lay things down somewhere between the portage and the cave. Not *what do you think*. Not *are you sure*. Just: which one do you trust. As if the answer were already his and he only needed her to say it out loud so he could follow her into it.

Sera had run rivers for eleven years. She'd taken paying passengers through the Grinder eleven seasons running without losing so much as a crate of dried fish. She knew her boat the way she knew her own hands, knew how much she could ask of the hull and how much the hull would ask back. And in all that time, she had built her reputation on one principle, repeated to every green deckhand who ever worked for her: *never run water you can't see*.

The right channel was water she couldn't see.

"Left," she said, and heard her own voice come out wrong—careful, hedged, the voice of a woman managing risk instead of reading a river. She stopped. Made herself look again. The left channel glittered, inviting, obvious, the kind of water that flattered you right up until it ate your keel. Every instinct she owned was screaming the same word at her, and the word was *trap*, and she couldn't have defended the feeling in front of a tribunal if her life depended on it.

Except she could defend it, actually. That was the thing. Eleven years of reading water wasn't superstition; it was data, just data that lived below language. The left channel was too smooth. Rivers that shallow, that braided, didn't stay smooth—they churned, they stuttered, they broke white over every bar. Smooth shallow water meant deep uniform silt over a hardpan shelf, and silt over hardpan meant the whole bed could be lying about its depth the way a mirror lies about a room. And the right channel—

The right channel moved like the main stem above the fork. Same cadence. Same muscular roll under the surface. The river was telling her, in the only language it had, which arm was still its own.

"Right," she said again, and this time it came out level. "We take the right channel."

*

Gideon didn't argue. That was the strange part, the part she kept turning over while she rigged the spare line and stowed everything that couldn't swim. He just nodded, once, and went aft to shift weight, moving crates toward the stern with the economy of a man who'd spent a season learning her boat by feel. Somewhere in the last hundred miles he had stopped being a passenger and become crew, and neither of them had marked the moment, and she found she didn't want to examine that too closely with a decision like this sitting in front of her.

They passed the island's head at speed. The current grabbed them the moment they crossed the seam—the visible line where the two channels' waters met, a long ragged scar of confused surface—and pulled them right with a hand like a gate swinging shut. Sera felt the boat heel, corrected, felt the bow swing true, and then the rock walls were sliding past on either side, close enough to touch, and there was no more deciding anything.

The channel was narrow. Narrower than it had looked from the fork, because distance lied about width the way maps lied about channels. The walls rose sheer on both sides, gray stone streaked black with old high-water marks, and the river inside them ran deep and fast and utterly opaque. She couldn't see bottom. She couldn't see rocks. She was steering by surface texture alone, reading the boils and the seams and the glassy V-marks that meant something submerged was parting the flow, calling commands over her shoulder—*weight left, weight left, hard left*—and feeling Gideon move before she finished saying it.

A quarter mile in, the first real drop appeared. Not big—eighteen inches, maybe two feet—but the water below it boiled back on itself in a hole wide enough to swallow their bow, and beyond the hole the channel bent hard around an unseeable corner.

"Sera." Gideon's voice, low. Not scared. Reporting.

"I see it."

She lined them up on the tongue of clean water feeding the drop, the one thread of green in all that brown chaos, and drove them in. The bow dropped. The hole took the front third of the boat and held it—one full second, two, the hull shuddering, spray coming over the gunwale in sheets—and then the current underneath did what current underneath always does if you've read it right, rolled them up and out and through, and the bend was on them and she threw her weight against the tiller and the boat carved around it with six inches to spare off a rock she never saw until they were past it.

Then open water. Then quiet, except for the hammering of her own blood.

Gideon was soaked to the waist and grinning—not the cold half-smile she'd catalogued in the early days but an actual grin, brief and unguarded, gone almost before it arrived. "Eleven years," he said. "You've done that eleven years?"

"Ten thousand times worse than that." She wrung out her sleeve. Her hands weren't steady yet; she kept them busy coiling the throw line so he wouldn't notice, or so she could pretend he wouldn't. "Don't get sentimental. We're not through."

He wasn't, though. Sentimental. He went straight to the bilge pump and started clearing the spray, and she let herself have ten seconds of looking at the channel ahead—wide again now, the walls falling back, the river reassembling itself into something civilized—and that was when she saw the problem.

The problem was floating.

Their spare barrel—the sealed drum with the reserve fuel, the backup food, the tools, the spare line, everything they'd need if the primary supplies got wet or lost or stolen—was supposed to be lashed amidships. It was not lashed amidships. It was fifty yards behind them, bobbing in the eddy below the drop, spinning slowly in the boil, riding high and cheerful as a festival lantern.

The lashings. The hole had taken the bow and the shock had taken the knots—or her knots had been bad, and she made herself consider that possibility honestly, because a pilot who blamed the river for her own rigging was a pilot who'd die of her own confidence someday. Either way: the barrel was gone, adrift in the worst possible place, caught in the recirculating eddy below the drop that had nearly eaten their bow.

"We go back for it," Gideon said, already reaching for the oars.

"No."

"It has the fuel, Sera. Half our food. Without it—"

"Without it we're lighter, hungrier, and alive." She pointed at the eddy with her chin. "Look at it. That's a keeper hole. Anything that goes in that recirculation stays in it—we'd be rowing into the exact hydraulic that almost flipped us, in a loaded boat, to chase a barrel that will dodge us every time the boil cycles. People die doing exactly that. Experienced people. People who thought they were just going to pop in and grab something."

"So we leave it."

"We leave it." The words tasted like ash. She'd never abandoned cargo in her life. Her father had a saying about that too—*the boat keeps what the boat keeps*—and she'd always thought it was fatalism dressed up as wisdom, and here she was living it, and it turned out it was neither. It was just arithmetic. A barrel against two lives. "I'm sorry. I know what's in it."

Gideon stood at the stern rail and watched the barrel spin in the eddy for a long moment. She expected anger, or at least the tight-jawed silence he did instead of anger. Instead he said, "Halloway carried that map through thirty years of claims offices and consortium thugs and it never once got wet. You got the map and yourself and me through a channel nobody's run in a generation. The barrel's the cheapest thing on this boat."

"The fuel isn't cheap."

"The fuel is replaceable. Downstream, at the trading post, from Vess's own people if it comes to that." He turned from the rail. "You made the call in about four seconds and it was the right call. Both calls. The channel and the barrel."

She wanted to argue—to point out that one good call didn't balance one bad knot, that expertise that stranded your own supplies was expertise with an asterisk—but the current was carrying them and the day was burning and there wasn't time for the argument, which was probably why she never had it. Some debts you just carry.

*

They made camp that night on a gravel bench below the second bend, and Sera sat up after Gideon turned in, working the numbers by lamplight.

Fuel first. They had perhaps a third of a tank. Enough for two days of running, three if she babied it, and the source country ahead was all gradient—all drops, all fast water, all throttle. She could row some of it. Rowing cost food, and food was the other column: with the barrel gone they had nine days of rations for two people, seven if she counted honestly, five if the weather turned or the route went long. The math did not close. She turned the figures over and over and they refused, politely and completely, to work.

Which meant the plan had to change, and the new plan had a shape she didn't like: they'd have to put in somewhere. Take help. The unrecorded village was two days downstream of here—no. Upstream. Behind them now, past the checkpoint they'd run at night in flood stage, in country the consortium watched. There was nothing ahead but the trading post at Mile Forty, and Mile Forty was consortium ground, and walking into consortium ground to buy fuel was a special category of foolishness she'd so far managed to avoid.

Unless.

She got out Halloway's map and spread it across her knees and looked at it properly for the first time since the fork—not hunting for channels this time but for the small marks in the margins, the ones she'd taken for decoration. A circle here. A triangle there. Little glyphs inked in faded brown along the upper river, spaced at intervals that were too regular to be random.

"Gideon," she said quietly.

Nothing. He slept like a man who rationed sleep and took only his share.

She traced the glyphs with one finger. Circles at the bends. Triangles at the tributary mouths. And at one spot, roughly a day upstream, a mark she'd have sworn was a smudge until she looked at it upside down in the lamplight and saw it for what it was: a tiny drawing of a barrel. A cache. Halloway had cached supplies along this river—of course he had, thirty years of running it, a man that careful would salt the route like a trail-blazer salting a path—and he'd written the locations into the margins in a code so modest it had hidden in plain sight through checkpoints, searches, and two weeks of her own inattention.

Her expertise had read the river right and the map wrong, both in the same morning. Humility arrived on schedule, right on time, wearing its usual expression.

She looked at the sleeping shape of him across the camp, at the rope and the piton hammer stowed within reach of his hand even in sleep, and felt the thing she'd been refusing to name since the cave settle another inch deeper into its place. Trust. Not the blind kind—the earned kind, the kind you build out of watching a man keep a promise over a corpse for weeks without once asking what he'd get. She trusted him with the boat now. She trusted him with the truth. Tomorrow she'd find out whether she could trust him with a margin glyph and a detour.

Because that was the hook in it, and she saw it clearly as she folded the map away: the nearest cache mark sat not on the main channel but up a side slough—a narrow, winding offshoot that the map showed connecting back to the Vane three miles on, through country marked with two triangles and no circles. Tributary mouths. Rapids, probably. Or worse.

The river had forked once today and she'd guessed right. Tomorrow it would fork again, and this time the map wouldn't even pretend to have an opinion.

She banked the lamp and lay down listening to the water, and somewhere in the dark upstream, faint and rhythmic, she heard a sound she told herself was wind in the canyon walls.

It wasn't wind. Wind didn't keep time.

Engines. Far off, but closing—someone running the river behind them in the night, and running it fast.
