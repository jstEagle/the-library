## 21. Voices in the Canyon

The voice found us before the light did.

I woke in the grey hour, before dawn had committed to anything, and for a moment I thought it was a dream—some memory of a rescue briefing, a ranger's bullhorn on a mountainside. Then Sera's hand closed on my shoulder in the dark of the boat's cabin, and I understood she'd been awake longer than I had.

"Listen," she said.

I listened.

*"...amnesty is extended to all persons currently within the Vane Gorge corridor..."*

The words came down the canyon walls the way water does—bouncing, splitting, arriving from everywhere at once. A man's voice, calm and reasonable, the kind of voice they use at airports and funerals. It echoed off the rock above us, doubled itself, faded, came again.

*"You are trespassing in a restricted watershed under emergency quarantine provisions. Lay down any materials in your possession. Remain where you are. Assistance will reach you within the hour. No charges will be filed."*

Sera was already dressed, already moving to the porthole. "How long has it been going?"

"I don't know. I woke to it."

"They've got speakers on the rim." She said it flatly, the way she said things that were bad news delivered early. "Big ones. That's not a patrol boat with a megaphone. That's infrastructure."

Infrastructure. The word sat wrong in my chest. You don't build infrastructure overnight. You don't mount loudspeakers along forty miles of canyon wall unless you planned, years ago, for someone to be down here someday, hearing them.

Unless you built the whole gorge into your security plan from the start.

"Amnesty," I said.

"For the map." She turned back to look at me. In the dark her face was just an outline, but I could feel her watching me the way she watched white water—reading it, waiting for it to show its line. "They're not even pretending it's about trespassing anymore, Gideon. They want what Halloway gave you."

I reached under the floor panel where we'd hidden it days ago, wrapped in oilcloth, and put my hand flat on it. Just to feel it there. Paper and thread and a dead man's promise.

"They can't know exactly where we are," I said. "Or they'd have stopped talking and started shooting."

"Not yet." Sera pulled the curtain aside a finger's width. Grey light was beginning to gather in the top of the gorge, the first pale suggestion of the cliffs. "But they know the stretch. We're between the narrows and the old gauging station, and they know it, because those speakers didn't get hauled up there this morning."

She was right, and we both knew it. This wasn't pursuit anymore. This was a net being described aloud, patiently, by a voice that never tired.

---

We ran the boat through the morning on Sera's terms—short stretches, eyes on the water, ears on the echoes. The voice followed us. Sometimes it was behind us, sometimes ahead, sometimes both at once, so that the canyon seemed full of men saying the same gentle thing from every direction. *No charges will be filed. Your safety is our priority. Amnesty.*

By midday we tied up in a side slot—a fold in the rock where a tributary trickled down over black stone and the echo died. Sera killed the engine and we ate cold rice and tinned fish and listened to the silence, which felt borrowed.

"They'll escalate," she said finally. "That voice is the polite version. There's always a polite version first. My father worked the coastal freight runs—he used to say you could tell how bad a situation was by how courteous the radio got."

"What comes after courtesy?"

"Blockades. Then force, and no more talking." She looked at me across the little cabin. "You've been quiet since dawn. Too quiet, even for you."

I almost told her then. Almost said: *I'm not quiet, I'm working.* But the thought hadn't finished forming yet, and half-formed thoughts are dangerous things to hand another person. So instead I said, "Play it back for me. In your head. Everything the voice said since dawn."

She frowned. "Why?"

"Humor me."

She recited it—she had that kind of memory, a pilot's memory, everything logged and timestamped. *Amnesty. Restricted watershed. Emergency quarantine provisions. Lay down any materials. Remain where you are. Assistance within the hour. No charges will be filed.* Twice more through the day, word for word identical. She caught herself on that. "Identical," she said slowly. "Every time. Scripted."

"Scripted and recorded," I said. "You notice they never say what the materials are? Never say *map*, never say *documents*. 'Materials in your possession.' And they never say what the amnesty is *for*. Amnesty implies a crime. What crime? Trespassing doesn't need amnesty; trespassing needs a fine and a ride downstream."

Sera set down her cup. "Quarantine," she said. "They keep using that word."

"They keep using that word because it's the only one that lets them do whatever they want next. Quarantine means disease. Disease means the public should be grateful for a sealed valley. It means if villages have to burn—" I stopped myself, but the shape of the thought was already out in the air between us, and Sera's face went still.

"You think they'd actually—"

"I think a company that falsifies water surveys for three provinces will say the word *quarantine* out loud and let everyone else imagine the rest." I heard my own voice come out level and cold, the guide's voice, the one I used on bad mountains. "Halloway knew. That's why he was dying in a freight hold instead of a hospital. That's why they hunted him across two hundred miles of river. Not for gold. Nobody runs loudspeakers along a canyon for gold."

The silence stretched. Downstream, faint as breath, the voice began again—*amnesty... amnesty...*—and something in me shifted, the way a snowpack shifts before it decides.

"Sera. Do you still have the recorder? The one you use for logging run notes?"

Her eyebrows went up. "The dictaphone? It's junk. Half the buttons stick."

"It records?"

"If you hold the button right."

"Then we're going to make a log entry."

---

It took two hours to set up, and it was Sera who made it work, because Sera can rig anything to anything—that's what twenty years on the river teaches you, that every problem is a fastening problem. She wired the little recorder to the boat's spare battery so it wouldn't die mid-sentence, taped it inside a tin to cut wind noise, and rigged a parabolic reflector out of a cooking wok that she refused to explain and I refused to ask about.

Then we waited for the voice.

It came at dusk, right on schedule—the schedule being the thing that told us most. Every four hours, like a tide table. Whoever ran this operation ran it by the clock, from a room somewhere, comfortable, certain.

The voice rolled down the canyon, and I held the wok up to the cliff face and held the button down, and Sera stood beside me with her watch open, timing it, mouthing the words along with the speaker because she had them memorized now too.

*"...amnesty is extended... lay down any materials in your possession... emergency quarantine provisions remain in effect... no charges will be filed..."*

When it finished, I lowered the wok. My arms were shaking—not from the weight. From something else.

"Play it back," I said.

Sera did. The tin-muffled voice filled the cabin, thin and hissy and unmistakably real. A corporation, speaking into a canyon, offering freedom to two people it intended to bury—and naming its own crime in the vocabulary it thought nobody would parse.

"Now the second part," I said.

I took the recorder in my hands. My hands were steadier now. Strange, how that works—how the shaking stops the moment you stop being the hunted thing and start being the witness.

I pressed record.

"This is a log entry," I said. "Date as marked. Location: middle Vane Gorge, approximately eleven miles below the upper gauging station. Present: Gideon Nystrom and Sera Okonkwo." 

Sera's head snapped toward me. I kept talking.

"At oh-six-hundred and at intervals of approximately four hours thereafter, loudspeaker broadcasts were audible throughout the gorge, originating from the canyon rims. The broadcasts offered 'amnesty' in exchange for 'materials in our possession,' cited 'emergency quarantine provisions,' and promised 'no charges.' The broadcasts were scripted, repeated verbatim, and timed at fixed intervals, indicating centralized coordination. We have recorded the broadcast in full. Note for the record: no quarantine order for the Vane watershed exists in any public registry. Note further: parties offering amnesty for documents are thereby declaring, on the record, that the documents contain matter worth amnesty. The consortium knows what Halloway carried. This recording is evidence of that knowledge."

I stopped the recording. The cabin was very quiet. The river talked to itself below us, patient, indifferent.

"Gideon Nystrom," Sera said. "You said your name. On a recording. Into a canyon full of their speakers."

"I did."

"You spent seven years making sure that name never touched paper. You flinched when the guard at the first weir looked at you too long. And now you've said it into a machine that could end up in a courtroom, or in Aldous Nystrom's hands, or at the bottom of this river."

"All of those are possible." I wound the wire carefully around the recorder, the way Halloway must have wound his maps, the way you secure anything you intend to survive the journey. "But here's what seven years taught me, Sera. Anonymity only protects you while you have nothing anyone wants. The moment you carry something worth killing for, invisibility stops being armor and starts being a grave. If they take this from us—if we go into the water—I want the recording to go with us or ahead of us, either way attached to a name. Because a dead anonymous drifter is a footnote. A dead named witness is a scandal."

She stared at me for a long moment. Outside, the dusk was turning to true dark, and far upstream, right on schedule, the voice began its evening round—*amnesty, amnesty*—rolling down the canyon like a tide that never reaches shore.

And then Sera did something I didn't expect. She smiled. Small, sharp, the smile she wore running rapids she hadn't scouted.

"There it is," she said.

"There what is?"

"The pilot. Under the drifter. You've been navigating by fear this whole trip—every decision defensive, every move about staying hidden. And now you've just read the water." She tapped the recorder in my hands. "That's a route choice, Gideon. That's you looking at the whole rapid and picking your line. About time."

I wanted to tell her it didn't feel like mastery. It felt like standing on a ledge I couldn't climb back up to. But she was right in the way that mattered: the fear was still there, and I had simply decided what it would steer for.

"We copy it," I said. "Tonight. Two copies—one stays hidden in the boat, one goes in your jacket, against your body. If we get separated, yours travels downstream however it has to. Mail it. Give it to a child. Throw it in the river in a sealed tin if you have to. But it moves."

"And the original map?"

"Stays split. Half on each of us. Like Halloway left it—no single point of failure." I looked at the dark mouth of the canyon, where the voice was fading into its own echoes. "They think they're herding us. Two exhausted trespassers listening to a kind voice, getting scared, getting ready to give up."

"Aren't we?"

"No." I tested the word in my mouth and found it true. "We're documenting. There's a difference. They've spent years making sure nothing in this valley leaves it except water they bill for. Well—something else is leaving. Us, and this."

Sera took the second recorder copy and sewed it into her jacket lining with sail twine, quick fingers, no wasted stitches, while I split Halloway's map along the old creases and tucked the halves into separate oilcloth sleeves. When she finished, she held the jacket up, considered, and put it on.

"You realize what happens tomorrow," she said.

"Tell me."

"That voice gets tired of being polite. Courtesy was phase one. Tomorrow they close a stretch of river ahead of us, or they put bodies on the bank, or both. We just told them, with our route and our patience, that we're not stopping. Polite men don't like being ignored."

"Then we don't sleep past first light."

We didn't. And it's just as well, because at some black hour before dawn, when the voice had gone silent and the canyon held nothing but river-sound, Sera shook me awake with a grip that meant business, and pointed upstream to where the darkness had grown a new feature: a line of lights strung across the water, low and even, like a bridge that led nowhere.

A boom. Across the entire channel.

Beyond it, faint but distinct, a new sound joined the night: engines. More than one. Idling. Waiting.

The polite voice, when it came again at oh-six-hundred, had changed its script.

*"...the amnesty period closes at noon today..."*

* * *

Sera read the new wording once and handed me the watch. "Twelve hours," she said. "Then they stop asking."

I stood at the rail with the recorder in my pocket and the map halves against my ribs, and I looked at that string of lights across the black water, and I understood that the canyon had just stopped being a corridor and become a trap with a deadline.

But traps have lines through them. Every trap does. And I had spent my whole life reading lines through impossible ground.

"Sera," I said, "how much floodwater is still coming down from the melt?"

She followed my gaze past the boom, past the idling engines, to the narrow dark throat of the gorge beyond—and I saw the exact moment she saw what I saw, the same line, the same impossible route, and her grin came back sharp as a knife.

"Oh," she said, "you're going to make me run it, aren't you?"
