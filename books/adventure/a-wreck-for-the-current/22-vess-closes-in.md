## 22. Vess Closes In

I heard the gunboat before I saw it—a low diesel throb rolling up the canyon like a fist knocking on a door from the inside. Sera Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning what every engine on the Vane sounded like: the lazy knock of a fisherman's two-stroke, the cough of an irrigation pump, the flat drone of consortium patrol skiffs running their scheduled loops. This was none of those. This was a military hull with a commercial registration, and it was coming fast.

"Company," I said.

Gideon looked up from the chart he'd been studying across my knees, Halloway's second map weighted flat with a coil of rope. He didn't ask how I knew. Two days past the narrows where we'd traded paint with Vess's patrol craft, he'd stopped asking me to justify my ears.

"How far?"

"Behind us. Around the bend at the talus slide." I throttled down and let the boat drift, listening. "Maybe four kilometers. Maybe less if they're not being polite about it."

"They were polite last time," Gideon said. "Last time she had orders she could pretend were suggestions."

"And this time?"

He was quiet for a moment. The river talked instead—the Vane's middle reach ran shallow and braided here, water braiding over gravel bars in a sound like someone whispering through their teeth. Then he said, "Aldous threatened to burn villages. Vess read Halloway's data. She knows her own wells are dying." His jaw worked. "She let us go once."

"People who let you go once get called back to the office," I said. "And offices don't accept mercy as an excuse. They accept results."

The diesel throb swelled. Around the bend, still hidden by the shoulder of the canyon, but no longer abstract.

I'd been running rivers since I was nine years old, when my father put a tiller extension in my hands on the lower Crosswater and told me the water would always tell me the truth if I listened longer than I was scared. Eleven years on the Vane specifically. I knew its moods the way you know a lover's—every shallow that lied about its depth in certain light, every eddy that would grab a hull and hold it, every channel that existed only in high water and would eat a propeller in low.

And I knew what a gunboat could do to my boat, which was everything, and what my boat could do to a gunboat in these waters, which was also something.

"Gideon," I said. "How do you feel about not winning?"

* * *

We couldn't outrun them. That was arithmetic, not pessimism. My boat was a converted cargo skiff with a sixty-horse outboard and a hull designed to take groundings gracefully; the gunboat behind us was aluminum, shallow-drafted, and probably pushing three hundred horses. In open water it would run us down inside of twenty minutes.

But the Vane above the Split Channel wasn't open water. It was forty kilometers of narrows where the canyon pinched down and the river dropped through a staircase of chutes and pools, water accelerating between walls close enough to spit across. Water that fast didn't care about horsepower. It cared about displacement, draft, and nerve—and about whether the person holding the wheel had memorized the line.

I had memorized the line. Mostly.

"There's a chute eight kilometers up," I told him, spreading the chart on the deck plates while the diesel grew behind us. "The locals call it the Needle's Eye. Single thread, maybe three meters wide, drops two meters into a pool with a rock garden on the far side. A gunboat can't run it loaded. Even unloaded, the pilot would have to be insane or local."

"You're going to run it."

"I'm going to try to run it. There's a difference, and the difference is usually the part where I scream."

He almost smiled. It was thin, stretched over something else. "Sera. If we wreck in there—"

"Then we wreck ahead of them, which is the whole point of racing." I started repacking gear, lashing anything loose, moving the fuel cans forward to shift our trim. "They can't arrest us if they're swimming."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "Vess isn't stupid. She'll know what we're doing. She might back off rather than follow us into that."

"She might." I cinched the last strap. "But her handlers are watching. There'll be a spotter plane overhead within the hour, maybe already. If she backs off twice, she stops being the hunter and starts being the hunted. I've seen how organizations like hers treat people who come back empty-handed." I met his eyes. "You of all people know how that works."

Something moved behind his face—the old flinch, the one I'd seen when the poster went up at the trading post, when his father's voice came crackling out of the radio offering him his name back like it was a gift and not a leash. He knew exactly how organizations treated failure. He'd been wearing the consequences for nine years.

"Then we make sure she has nothing to bring back," he said. "Not even us."

"That's the spirit. Take the bow. When I yell left, throw your weight left, and don't ask why."

* * *

The gunboat came around the talus slide at 10:40 by my father's battered wristwatch, and it was worse than I'd guessed. Not the standard patrol skiff but a riverine interceptor, armored wheelhouse, a pintle mount on the bow with a crewman standing behind it—not firing, not yet, but present the way a raised voice is present. Grey hull, consortium markings freshly painted over older ones, and riding light enough that they'd stripped it for speed.

Through my binoculars I found the figure at the helm. Short hair, set shoulders, a stance I recognized from two days ago on the bank of the narrows: Vess, flying her own boat, which told me either her pilot was dead or she trusted no one else's hands. Behind the wheelhouse glass, another figure—I'd have bet money it was her handler, some grey man sent upstream to make sure the mercy didn't happen twice.

"They see us," Gideon reported from the bow. "Someone's pointing."

"Pointing or shooting?"

"Pointing. For now."

"For now is my favorite kind of now. Hold on."

I opened the throttle and the Vane took us.

The first six kilometers were a blur of decisions made faster than thought—threading braid channels I'd only ever run at half speed, kissing gravel bars I could feel through the hull like a held breath, using every meter of river the gunboat's deeper draft wouldn't forgive. We gained maybe four hundred meters. Maybe five hundred. Behind us the diesel note climbed and climbed, patient as arithmetic, eating our lead in bites.

A loudspeaker crackled across the water, distorted by canyon echo into something almost human: *"Skiff on the Vane. Heave to. This is a consortium safety interdiction. Heave to or be boarded by force."*

Safety interdiction. I'd heard that phrase used to stop smugglers, poachers, and once, memorably, a floating clinic. Now it was aimed at a man carrying a dead prospector's map and a woman carrying both of them.

"They're gaining," Gideon called. "Two hundred meters. Maybe less."

"Tell me something new!"

The canyon walls closed. The light went from noon-bright to a greenish dusk in the space of a minute, the river compressing, speeding up, the whisper turning to a hiss. Ahead, I could hear it before I could see it—the Needle's Eye announcing itself as a long drawn roar, water funneling toward a gap I remembered as a nightmare and hoped was still only that.

I killed the throttle and let the current carry us the last stretch, because the Eye demanded finesse, not power, and because I wanted the gunboat to see us slow. Wanted Vess to watch us commit.

The gap opened ahead: two walls of granite leaning together like conspirators, the entire river squeezed through a slot barely wider than my boat, dropping away into white chaos beyond. At this year's water level there was a tongue of smooth green feeding straight into it, and on the right side of the tongue, just visible under the surface, the fang that had opened a friend's hull in '19.

"Sera." Gideon's voice came flat and careful. "That's—"

"I know what it is."

"It's narrower than we are."

"It's exactly as wide as we are. That's different." I lined us up on the green tongue, hands light on the tiller, heart doing something arrhythmic. "Left in three seconds. Weight left, hard."

The roar swallowed everything. The walls rushed at us. And then—

*"LEFT!"*

He threw himself portside and the boat rolled under me, gunwale kissing the black water, and I steered with my wrists more than my arms, threading the slot by feel and memory and pure animal refusal to die. Granite scraped past both sides close enough to leave paint. The fang hissed under the starboard sponson—I felt it through my boots, a shudder like a snake striking and missing—and then the world dropped two meters and we were through, slamming into the pool below in a curtain of spray that hit like a thrown bucket.

The boat wallowed, shipped half a ton of water, and steadied. Alive. Both of us alive. The engine, God bless its cheap Japanese heart, caught on the second pull.

Behind us, the roar of the Eye continued uninterrupted, and in it I could hear the gunboat's diesel falter, throttle down, die to an idle.

I turned and looked back through the spray.

Vess had brought her interceptor to a stop above the chute. Through the binoculars I watched her stand at her rail, looking down at the slot, then at us, then at the grey man beside her. The loudspeaker crackled again, but the words dissolved in the roar—we were too far downstream now, too deep in the pool-and-chute country where sound went to die.

"She's not following," Gideon said.

"She can't. Not that boat, not that load." I kept us moving anyway, because backing off and giving up are different animals and I wanted distance between them. "But she's not leaving. She knows this river too. She'll run the bank trail, portage the Eye, and be waiting for us below the gorge. Or her handlers will send word ahead to the next weir and we'll trade a gunboat for a fortress."

"Then we've bought hours, not freedom."

"We're on a river, Gideon. Hours are all anyone buys." I checked the sky—clear, blue, and empty, which meant the spotter plane hadn't found us yet or hadn't been launched. "Hours are enough to reach the cave. Enough to dry out, patch what needs patching, and look at the route past the middle weir."

He came aft and sat across from me, dripping, and for a moment neither of us said anything. The gorge carried us along, green water sliding over drowned boulders, the walls opening by degrees back toward daylight.

"That was the worst driving I've ever survived," he said finally.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

"It absolutely was."

* * *

It was the starboard sponson that betrayed us.

The fang hadn't missed after all—or rather, it had missed fatally and grazed honestly. Below the fourth chute, with the gorge widening and my pulse finally settling somewhere near human, I noticed the bilge pump cycling more often than it should. Then more often than it could. I pulled the floor plate and looked down into eight centimeters of cold green water and a seam along the sponson weld that had opened like a bad smile.

"We're taking water," I said, and heard how calm I made it sound, and hated how much practice that calm had taken.

Gideon was already moving—found the bilge sponge, the spare epoxy, the canvas patch kit I kept for hull wounds. "Can we beach her?"

"Next gravel bar. There's one below the bend, big enough." I eased the throttle back and brought us in sideways, the way you land a wounded thing, gentle as I could, and we grounded on clean shingle with the stern in the current and the wounded sponson high and dry.

The damage was worse out of the water. The graze had flexed the weld, cracked the sealant, and started a fracture line running aft along the seam—nothing catastrophic today, but a day of pounding rapids would finish it. I sat back on my heels and did the math I didn't want to do.

"Twelve hours minimum to patch this properly. Epoxy has to cure. And even then, I don't trust it above seventy percent throttle."

"So we go slow."

"We go slow, and Vess goes fast, and the math gets ugly." I looked up at him. He was crouched over the hull with the epoxy in his hands, and there was something in his face I'd learned to read by now—not fear, exactly. Reckoning. The face of a man counting what he had left.

"We could cache the boat," he said slowly. "Portage the supplies over the ridge to the upper valley trail, finish on foot. Halloway's map shows a track."

"On foot we're slower than any boat they have, and exposed on open ground, and we abandon the only thing keeping us mobile." I shook my head. "No. We patch, we run careful, we stay ahead. The river's still ours. It's the only thing in this whole country that hasn't been bought."

He started sanding the seam without arguing, and I went to check the rest of the hull, and that was when I saw it.

Scratched into the transom, low near the waterline where no one would look unless they were sitting exactly where I was sitting: fresh marks, bright metal under old paint. Letters. I leaned close.

**VESS WAS HERE — NEXT TIME I WON'T MISS THE BOAT**

Below it, smaller, almost apologetic, as if added by a different hand or a later mood:

**CHECK YOUR FUEL LINES. THEY DIDN'T SEND ME ALONE.**

I sat very still. The gorge murmured. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, a diesel engine turned and headed back the way it had come.

"Gideon," I said. "You need to see this."

He came around the transom, read it twice, and his face did something complicated—the hunter's message resolving into something closer to a warning shot, a debt acknowledged, a line drawn that wasn't quite the line her handlers thought they'd ordered.

"She's telling us the truth?" he asked.

"She's telling us something. Question is what it costs her to say it, and what happens to her if we believe it." I stood, wiped my hands, and looked downriver toward the middle weir, invisible beyond the bends but heavy in my mind as ballast. "Check the fuel lines."

He did. The reserve tank's valve had been opened. Not slashed—opened, quietly, so we'd run dry ten kilometers from anywhere and be found sitting ducks. Sabotage dressed as bad luck.

We drained, resealed, and cursed, and by the time the epoxy was curing under a tarp and the sun was leaning west, I'd made a decision I didn't fully understand yet.

"When we meet her again," I said, "and we will—we don't run first. We talk first. One minute of talk. If she takes it, we learn something worth more than an hour's head start. If she doesn't—" I shrugged. "Then we run, and I drive like I drove through the Eye."

Gideon looked at me for a long moment. "You think she's salvageable."

"I think everyone on this river is somebody's captive," I said. "Including, lately, us. The question is who figures it out first."

Downstream, around the last bend of the gorge, the water flattened and widened toward the middle weir, and the evening came on gold and quiet, and I tried not to count how few hours of quiet we had left.
