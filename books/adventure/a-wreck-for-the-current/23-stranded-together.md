## 23. Stranded Together

The gunboat came to rest fifty meters downstream of us, half-sunk on a gravel bar, its bow driven into the shallows like a fence post somebody had hammered in drunk. Steam curled off its engine housing. One of the crew was already wading ashore, and the others were bailing with their hats.

Our own hull was taking water through a seam that hadn't been there an hour ago. Sera ran us onto the same bar—deliberately, I'd give her that, no panic in it—and we stepped off into knee-deep water while the Vane tried to pull the boat back out of our hands.

For a long moment nobody did anything but breathe.

Then Vess stood up from where she'd braced herself against her boat's gunwale, wrung out one sleeve, and looked at me across forty meters of gravel and wrecked machinery. Her rifle was still slung. She didn't reach for it. That was either mercy or arithmetic—she had three crewmen soaked to the ribs and a dead engine, and I had a river pilot who could thread a needle at flood stage and a passenger who'd spent ten years learning how to survive country that wanted him dead.

"Nobody shoot," she called out, to her people more than to us. Then, louder: "Nystrom."

"Not my name today."

"It's your name every day. You just don't like paying for it." She started walking toward us. Slow. Hands visible. A soldier's theater, and she knew I'd read it as theater, and she did it anyway because it was the only language we shared.

Sera moved up beside me, hammer still in her hand from the last repair job, and said quietly, "I can put this boat back together in four hours if nobody's shooting at us. Can you make that true?"

I watched Vess come. Watched her crew watch me. Two of them were young—the kind of young the consortium hired because they hadn't yet learned what the work cost other people. The third was older, gray at the temples, and he was looking at the wreck of his own boat with an expression I recognized. It wasn't anger. It was the look of a man doing sums about his pension.

"I'll try," I said.

* * *

We dragged both boats above the waterline while the light went copper. The Vane was dropping fast—you could see it, the wet line on the stones retreating an inch an hour, the dry season closing its hand around the whole upper river. In three days, maybe four, this stretch would be a staircase of rocks and neither boat would float again anywhere above the gorge.

Vess's engineer—a squat woman named Brakka with burn scars up both forearms—pulled the housing off the gunboat's engine and swore at it in three languages. The impeller was shredded. Something we'd hit, or something she'd hit, or the river itself, which didn't care whose fault anything was.

"We're not going anywhere," Brakka announced, to no one in particular.

"Neither are they," Vess said. "Not until morning." She glanced at our hull, at the seam Sera was already chalking. "And you're not going anywhere until that's patched. So."

"So," I said.

She sat down on a drift log, laid her rifle across her knees—not threatening, exactly, but present, the way a man lays a bill on a table—and took out a tin of tea leaves and a battered pot. "There's a spring back in those willows. Good water. We might as well drink it before the consortium fences it too."

That landed harder than she probably intended. Or maybe she intended it exactly.

Her two young crewmen built a fire with the practiced economy of people who'd done fieldwork together a long time. The older one, Gray-Temples, whose name turned out to be Oduya, kept glancing at me. Not hostile. Curious, the way you'd look at a story you'd only heard told badly.

"You're really him," he said finally. "Corren Pass."

"Oduya," Vess said, a warning note.

"No, I want to know. My cousin was a guide out of Corren. He always said the official report was garbage. He said no guide alive would have led nine clients under that cornice after the warm front came through." He looked at me. "He said whoever took the blame either died or got destroyed."

"Both, almost," I said.

Vess poured the tea. Four cups for her crew, then, after a pause that cost her something, two more, which she held out without ceremony. Sera took hers. I took mine. The metal was hot enough to hurt and I was grateful for it.

"I read the file," Vess said, staring at the fire. "All of it. Part of the job—they give you the dossier on whoever you're hunting, so you know what leverage to use." She sipped. "You know what struck me? Not the avalanche. The timing. The consortium filed its mineral claim amendments for the Corren drainage eleven days *after* the slide. Eleven days. Like they were waiting to see what the mountain uncovered before they drew the map."

The fire popped. Downstream, the gunboat ticked and settled as it cooled.

"What are you telling me?" I asked. Though I knew. I'd known since chapter fifteen of my own life, since the night I'd finally said Aldous Nystrom's name out loud to Sera and watched her decide to believe me anyway.

"I'm telling you I've been wondering for two years why a company that owns every gauge on the Vane needs hunters with rifles. Companies with clean hands hire lawyers." She turned and looked at me straight on. "So here's where we are. My boat is dead. Your boat is leaking. The river is dying under us by the hour. And my handlers expect me to radio in that I have you cornered."

"And instead?"

"Instead I'm going to sit here and drink bad tea and think very carefully about what I actually know." She fed a stick to the fire. "You should think about what you know too. All of it. Halloway's papers. Whatever your father buried at Corren. Because when we leave this bar tomorrow, one of two things happens: I take you in, or I stop being able to pretend I don't understand what I'm part of. And I'd like to know which man I'm sitting across from before I choose."

* * *

Sera worked on the hull through the last of the light, and I hauled for her, holding planking while she drove caulking into the seam, and the rhythm of it was old and good—two people making a thing hold against the water. Behind us, Vess's crew rigged tarps over their dead engine, and the two camps existed ten meters apart like neighboring households that hadn't decided whether to be friendly.

"She's going to flip," Sera murmured, driving another caulk home. "You can see it working in her. The question is which way."

"She read Halloway's data once already. On the bank, at the narrows. She let us go."

"People let you go once for sentiment. Twice costs them their livelihood."

"Three times costs them their soul," I said. "She's deciding which she can afford to lose."

Sera sat back on her heels and studied the seam, running her thumb along it. "Four hours' work left. Maybe five. We launch at first light, ahead of them, and pray Brakka works miracles overnight."

"Brakka doesn't do miracles."

"Everyone does miracles when the alternative is walking out of the Vane headwaters on foot." She wiped her hands on her thighs and looked over at the other fire, where Vess sat alone now, her crew rolled in tarps, turning something over in her fingers. A photograph, maybe. Or a badge. "Go talk to her. You're better at this than you think."

"I'm not good at anything but mountains."

"You survived being a mountain for ten years. Go."

* * *

I crossed the gravel with two cups of Sera's coffee, which was terrible, and sat down on the far end of Vess's log without asking. She didn't tell me to move. She was turning a flat brass object in her fingers, and in the firelight I saw what it was: a well cap plate, the kind stamped with a settlement's name and parcel number, pried off somewhere and carried as a keepsake or a wound.

"Ferrin Crossing," she said, before I asked. "My village. Up on the Lasseter fork. Population four hundred when I left." She turned the plate so the firelight caught the stamp. "Population question mark now. The wells started failing six years ago. Nobody could say why. The consortium sent surveyors, and the surveyors said the aquifer was 'naturally declining,' and then the consortium offered to sell us water shipped in by rail at a price nobody could pay." She laughed, short and ugly. "They were selling us our own water back. We just couldn't prove it was ours."

"Halloway could have."

"Halloway." She said the name like a debt. "I pulled his file too. Forty years prospecting. Blacklisted from three claims he'd legally staked. Died in your arms at a ferry landing, and the last thing he did was hand a disgraced guide a map." She finally looked at me. "Why you? Out of everyone on that river, why hand it to a man with no boat, no name, and no future?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I'd asked myself the same question every night since.

"Because I was the only one who didn't want anything," I said. "Every other soul at the landing wanted gold, or revenge, or a cut. I just wanted to stay invisible. He needed someone with nothing to lose except the promise." I turned the cup in my hands. "Turns out I had one thing to lose. Turns out it was the only thing worth losing."

"The anonymity."

"The lie. Anonymity was just the shape of it." I nodded toward the dark upstream, where the source waited behind its fences and gauges. "Ten years ago my father chose the company over his own son's name, and I built a life inside the hole that left. Tomorrow I walk into the valley he built the hole to protect. And I'm going to stand in it and say my name out loud, and everything he buried comes up with me."

Vess was quiet a long time. She weighed the brass plate in her palm like a stone she was deciding whether to throw or keep.

"If I let you pass tomorrow," she said slowly, "I don't come back from this. You understand that. There's no version where Vess of Ferrin Crossing delivers Nystrom to the source and gets a pension."

"There's a version where you deliver testimony instead of a prisoner. There's a version where your village gets its water back and its proof."

"And there's a version where Aldous Nystrom burns the valley and buries all of us in the paperwork." She looked at the fire. "I've met men like him. They don't lose gracefully."

"No," I agreed. "They don't. But they lose."

Something moved through her face then—not surrender, nothing so clean, but a settling, like scree finding its angle of rest. She pocketed the plate.

"Terms," she said. "First light, we launch together. My crew walks the boats down the shallow stretches—no point pretending we're racing anymore. If there's fighting, my rifle points at whatever shoots at you first. And when we get to the source—" she held my eyes— "you say Halloway's name before yours. He paid more than either of us."

"He did," I said. "Agreed."

We shook on it, her grip dry and hard, a hunter's grip, and it was strange how much it felt like every rope-end handshake I'd ever trusted my life to.

* * *

I went back to our fire. Sera was asleep sitting up, or nearly, her head tipping forward and catching itself, and I eased down beside her without waking her. Across the gravel, the gunboat's crew slept in a row under tarps, and Brakka's silhouette bent over the open engine housing even now, swearing softly, working by lantern.

Above us the stars came out hard and cold, the way they do at altitude, and the river muttered over its shrinking bed, and I sat with the strangest feeling I'd had in ten years: that I was not alone in the dark, and that the people around me were not enemies, and that tomorrow the current would carry all of us—willing and unwilling, guilty and owed—toward the one place on earth my father would pay anything to keep me from reaching.

Sera opened one eye. "Well?"

"She's coming with us."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "Then we'd better launch early. Because if Vess has flipped, her handlers already know she's missed her check-ins." She sat up, all sleep gone from her voice. "Gideon. Look upstream."

I looked. Far up the black throat of the canyon, where the gorge narrowed to a knife-cut of sky, a light was moving. Not a star. Not a campfire.

A searchlight, sweeping the water, coming downriver toward us—and behind it, faint on the wind, the thump of rotors.
