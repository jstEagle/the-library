## 24. Aldous on the Radio

The storm that had chased them through the narrows spent itself by midnight, and what it left behind was a silence so complete Sera could hear the river thinking. They'd tied up in a side slough two bends past the wreck of Vess's gunboat, wedged between deadfalls where the water ran brown and slow and the mosquitoes owned the air. The *Magpie* sat low in the water with her patched hull weeping a slow thread into the bilge, and Sera had been bailing by lamplight for an hour when the radio woke up.

Not their radio. The valley's.

It came out of the dark the way weather does—first a pressure change, then a sound. A carrier wave rolling down the gorge from somewhere upstream, bouncing off canyon walls, finding every receiver within forty miles whether they wanted to be found or not. Their set was a cheap salvaged unit bolted under the dash, and it crackled, hissed, and then resolved into a voice.

Sera's hands went still on the bail bucket.

"Vess." The voice was calm. Cultured. The kind of voice that had never once needed to shout to be obeyed. "Report your position."

Static. Then, faintly, Vess's reply—clipped, careful, and lying. "Systems failure at the narrows. Hull breach. We're holed up for repairs. No contact with the targets."

A pause long enough to hold an entire childhood of disappointment.

"I didn't ask about your boat, Ms. Vess."

Sera looked across the cockpit. Gideon sat against the gunwale with his knees drawn up, hat off, hair still damp from the storm. He hadn't moved when the radio came alive. He'd gone very still instead—the stillness of a man hearing a sound he'd spent years teaching himself not to flinch from.

"They're damaged," Vess said. "Both vessels. I have two men needing stitches and no working radio on the secondary set. I'll resume at first light."

"You'll resume now."

Another silence. Sera found herself holding her breath on Vess's behalf, which was absurd—Vess had chased them three hundred miles, shot at them twice, cornered them once and let them go only after reading Halloway's survey data with her own failing village's wells reflected in her eyes. But there was something in the pause before Vess answered that made Sera's chest ache anyway.

"With respect," Vess said finally, "the river doesn't resume at anyone's convenience. First light."

The click of the channel closing was almost inaudible. Then the voice came back alone, unmoderated now, filling the slough and the deadfalls and the dripping dark.

"Gideon."

Sera watched Gideon's jaw tighten. Watched him not look at her.

"I know you can hear this. I know the boat you're in—I signed off on its salvage registration myself, years ago, when I was buying up everything Okonkwo ever floated. You always did gravitate toward my property, son. Even when you were pretending it wasn't mine."

*Son.* The word dropped into the cockpit like a stone into still water. Sera had known since the trading post—since she'd shoved the reward poster under his nose and watched four years of trust crack down the middle like river ice. She knew the name Nystrom meant founder, meant consortium, meant the man whose face was stamped on every freight manifest from Kestrel's Landing to the headwaters. But knowing a thing and hearing it were different animals. Hearing it, spoken in that voice, warm as banked coals—

"This is Aldous Nystrom," the voice continued, as if introducing himself at a dinner party. "And I'm going to speak plainly, because you inherited my dislike of wasted words, whatever else you may have inherited or refused."

Gideon moved then. Slowly, deliberately, he reached under the dash and turned the radio's volume knob all the way down until the voice became a murmur, a ghost talking through cotton.

"We don't have to listen," Sera said quietly.

"He knows we're listening either way." Gideon's voice was flat. "Turning it off is for us, not him." He rubbed a hand over his face. "He'll talk for twenty minutes. He always talks for twenty minutes. It's how he wins—he makes the other person feel like the silence is theirs to fill."

Sera settled back against the thwart, the bail bucket forgotten, water sloshing gently around her boots. "Then let's hear how he loses."

For a moment—one flickering moment—something that might have been gratitude crossed Gideon's face. Then he turned the volume back up, and the father's voice poured into the boat again, mid-sentence, seamless, as if it had never stopped.

"—and I want to be clear that no one is angry. Anger is for people who've lost control of a situation. I haven't lost control of anything. What I've lost is patience, and those are different losses with very different consequences."

The river chuckled over its stones. Somewhere in the deadfalls a night bird called, indifferent.

"Here is what I'm offering, Gideon. Full reinstatement. Your guide's certification restored in both countries—the Corren sanction dissolved, the blacklist struck, the record corrected. Every door that closed on you six years ago opens again tonight. Not just opened—held open. I'll put you back on the mountain register under your own name. I'll fund your own outfit if you want one. You could be running expeditions on Corren Pass itself within the season, the pass they took from you, leading parties up routes you drew when you were nineteen years old."

Sera heard it happen in Gideon's breathing—a hitch, barely there, the sound of a man being offered exactly the thing he'd been starved of. Six years of trail jobs under borrowed names. Six years of ducking registry offices, of watching other guides' names go up on the boards while his went nowhere, of being the best mountaineer on three rivers and legally nobody at all. And here it was, gift-wrapped, delivered in a voice that remembered how he liked his coffee.

"And in exchange," the voice said, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, "you give me the map, and you give me your silence, and you get on with your life."

* * *

The rain started again around the time Aldous began describing the terms—or maybe Sera just noticed it then, a fine mist ticking against the canopy overhead, running down the leaves in threads. She'd pulled her jacket tight and was watching Gideon the way she watched water: for the thing underneath the surface.

Because the voice wasn't finished. It was doing something worse than threatening now. It was remembering.

"I know what you think happened at Corren," Aldous said. "I know what you've carried. And I want you to understand that I have never once blamed you for it. Not for a single day. You were twenty-four years old and the mountain did what mountains do, and every man who died up there died doing work they chose. That's not on you, son. It was never on you. Whatever you believe I believe—that file I kept sealed, the inquiry I steered, all of it—I did it to protect *you*. Do you understand? Everything I did, I did so that you could walk away from that mountain and become someone new."

Sera saw Gideon's knuckles whiten on the gunwale.

"You built yourself a life downstream. I let you keep it. Six years, Gideon. Six years I let the world forget you, because forgetting was mercy. And now you're dragging that name back up my river, tied to a dead prospector's fantasies, and every mile you climb you make the forgetting harder—and the consequences larger. Not for me. For you. For the woman piloting that boat, who is guilty of nothing but greed and poor judgment. For every settlement along the Vane that depends on things staying calm."

There it was. Sera felt it land even though it wasn't aimed at her: the pivot from offer to warning, done so smoothly you could miss the seam. *I'm giving you everything. Also, I know where she sleeps.*

"The map is a fiction," Aldous went on. "Halloway was a drunk and a fraud who spent thirty years chasing a source that belongs, by every lawful instrument, to the consortium. Those springs are gauged, licensed, and managed. Three provinces drink from that water because we keep it managed. You think tearing the lid off helps anyone? You think the villages downstream are ready for what happens when the filings come apart? There will be injunctions, seizures, panic buying, wells drilled illegally into aquifers that take decades to recharge. You wouldn't be freeing that water, Gideon. You'd be starting a war over it, and the people who bleed first will be the ones Halloway claimed to care about."

A pause. When the voice resumed it had dropped lower, and Sera understood with a cold slide in her stomach that this part wasn't performance. This part was the true thing underneath, the thing Aldous Nystrom had probably never said to another living soul.

"I'm sixty-eight years old. I built all of this with my hands. Not the money—the money came later, the money was easy. I mean the *work*. I surveyed the Vane when there were no charts. I sank the first gauge at the headwaters myself, in water so cold it took feeling out of my fingers for a winter. Everything upstream of the second weir, I found before any government cared it existed. And they let me believe it was mine. They sold me the paper and shook my hand and told me the river was a commodity, and I believed them because believing them made me great." A breath, static-frayed. "You want to know the truth about the source, Gideon? It stopped being water a long time ago. It's power. It's the only power in this region that can't be voted away or bought out, and I am not going to be the man who let it slip. Not even for you."

The mist thickened. In the bilge, the slow leak kept its patient time.

"So. One more time, and then I'll stop insulting us both. The map. The documents. Bring them to the second weir within the week, walk in under escort, no charges, no hunters, no Vess. Reinstatement. Certification. Your name cleared at Corren—not buried, *cleared*, publicly, with my signature on the correction. Or keep going, and I will do what I should have done at the first checkpoint, and neither of us will enjoy it. I have helicopters, Gideon. I have men who are paid to be thorough. And I have—" the smallest hesitation, the closest thing to humanity the voice would permit itself, "—a son who used to sit on my knee while I read survey notes aloud, who once asked me why the river sang, and who deserves better than to die in a gorge over a dead man's paperwork."

The channel held open. Ten seconds. Twenty. Long enough for the offer to breathe, for the silence to do its work, for some younger version of Gideon Nystrom to crawl up out of six drowned years and say *yes, Papa, I'm coming home.*

Then the carrier wave folded, and the voice was gone, and the slough was just a slough again—rain and river and mosquitoes and the tick of the cooling radio.

* * *

Neither of them spoke for a while.

Sera bailed. It gave her hands something honest to do. The bucket went down, came up heavy, went over the side; down, up, over. The level in the bilge dropped by inches and her mind raced the whole time, turning the broadcast over like a stone looking for what lived under it.

Reinstatement. Her mind snagged on it again and again. She'd watched Gideon flinch at registry offices. She'd watched him sign manifests with his left hand when he was right-handed, some old habit of erasure. She'd seen the way he looked at other men's guide certifications pinned to ferry-house boards—hungry and hating the hunger. And Aldous had reached straight into that wound with a surgeon's knowledge of the anatomy. Of course he had. He'd helped carve it.

"That was quite a speech," she said at last.

Gideon didn't answer immediately. He was staring at the radio like it might grow teeth. "He quoted the survey notes," he said finally. His voice came out rough. "Reading them to me. I was five. I'd forgotten that. Twenty-nine years, and I'd forgotten it, and he reaches into a microphone and hands it back to me like—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Like it was a kindness."

"Was it?"

"No." Flat, immediate, and then less flat. "Maybe. That's what makes it filthy. He means it. Every word of the soft parts, he means. He genuinely believes he protected me at Corren. He genuinely believes managing that water is stewardship. He's not lying to me, Sera. He's worse than lying. He's *sincere*."

She set the bucket down. "And the rest of it? The threats?"

"The threats are real too. 'What I should have done at the first checkpoint'—that's helicopters and a cordon on the upper gorge. He's telling me he's been gentle so far." Gideon laughed, short and without any humor in it. "Gentle. Nine deaths at Corren Pass, a blacklisted boy, a burned reputation, and that was the *gentle* version."

Sera climbed forward to check the patch. The hull seam they'd caulked at the wreck site was holding, mostly, a bead of water seeping through one thumb-width of the seal that she'd re-tighten at dawn. She worked the caulking iron along it by feel more than sight, and talked with her back to him, because some conversations went easier without eye contact.

"He offered to clear Corren," she said. "Publicly. His signature on the correction."

"Yes."

"That's the thing you've wanted for six years. That's the thing you won't even say out loud that you want."

The caulking iron paused. Behind her, the river murmured its one long sentence.

"It is," Gideon said. "And he knows it. That's the whole design, Sera. He didn't build that offer to fail. He built it to be *almost enough*. Enough truth mixed into the lie that a tired man could swallow it and call it medicine." She heard him shift, heard wet rope creak. "If I take it, Halloway rots in an unmarked grave and three provinces keep drinking from a lie. If I refuse it, I stay nobody forever, and he knows I know that too. Either way he wins something. That's how he negotiates. He taught me that over chess when I was eight. Never make an offer with only one outcome."

She turned around. In the shielded glow of the lamp his face was all planes and shadow, older than his years, and she thought—not for the first time—that she was looking at a man who had been carrying a weight so long he'd mistaken it for a spine.

"What are you going to do?" she asked.

Gideon was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked on the canopy. Downstream, miles back, Vess and her broken gunboat were presumably limping through the dark, and upstream, somewhere past the second weir, a helicopter pad was probably warming its engines.

"He said bring the documents to the second weir within the week," Gideon said slowly. "Which means he thinks I still have them all. Both maps. The survey data. Which means he doesn't know about the copies."

Sera went still. "What copies?"

But Gideon was already moving, pulling the waterproof satchel from beneath the thwart, and the look on his face wasn't the look of a man deciding whether to surrender. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic.

"He wants an answer," Gideon said. "He left the channel note. Standard consortium practice—they monitor the return band for twelve hours after a personal broadcast." He glanced up at her, and in the lamplight his eyes had gone bright and hard and terribly awake. "Sera, how far are we from the gauging relay at Mile Ninety?"

She blinked. "Four hours' run, if the slough connects to the main channel. Why—"

"Because he offered me a deal in front of witnesses," Gideon said, reaching for the radio's transmit key, "and I'm going to give him my answer while the whole valley's still listening."

His hand closed over the switch.
