## 25. The Offer Refused

The transmit key was cold under Gideon's thumb, colder than it had any right to be in the close heat of the cabin.

Sera hadn't moved from the doorway. She'd gone very still in the way she did before running water she didn't trust—weight low, eyes reading everything, committing nothing. Behind her shoulder, through the salt-fogged window, the slough lay black and motionless under a sky still bruised from sunset. Somewhere out there, Vess's gunboat sat dead in the reeds with its engine torn open, and somewhere farther out than that, a relay tower at Mile Ninety was already listening.

"You understand what you're about to do," Sera said. It wasn't a question. She'd learned that from him, maybe—hearing the shape of a decision before it finished forming.

"I'm going to say my name on an open channel." His voice came out level, which surprised him. Inside, something was moving like floodwater under ice. "Every settlement from here to the delta has a set tuned to the return band tonight. Consortium men. River families. The unrecorded village, if their elder keeps the old shortwave working like I think he does."

"And him."

"And him." Gideon looked down at the radio as if he could see through its casing to the towers and repeaters strung along the river's spine, all of them owned, all of them paid for, all of them listening for one voice. "Twelve hours, he said. He wants his answer while the channel's hot."

Sera stepped fully into the cabin and pulled the door shut behind her. The lamp swung, threw their shadows long across the chart table, steadied. "Then let me say this first, because after tonight I don't know when we'll get a quiet minute again." She crossed her arms. "Once you speak, there's no version of this where you go back to being nobody. No alias, no trail jobs, no registry office you can walk past. He'll have your voiceprint inside the hour. Every checkpoint from here to the sea will have your name attached to a face."

"I know."

"He offered you reinstatement, Gideon. Full pardon. Your name cleared in both countries. That's not nothing—that's the life they took from you, handed back with a bow on it."

"That's exactly why it's nothing." He heard the edge come into his own voice and didn't file it down. "He's not offering me my life back, Sera. He's offering to sell it back to me at the price of Halloway's map and every village that drinks from the Vane. My father doesn't give gifts. He makes purchases. And what he's buying is silence."

She held his gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded once, slow, the way she nodded when she'd read a channel and found it runnable—barely, against sense, but runnable. "All right. Say your piece. But say it clean. He'll be listening for anger. Anger sounds like guilt to men like Aldous Nystrom."

Gideon almost laughed. "You've met him."

"I've met his kind on every river I've ever worked. They're all the same man wearing different watches." She reached past him and flipped the radio from receive to standby, then checked the frequency dial twice, because checking things twice was how Sera Okonkwo stayed alive. "Channel's clear. Return band's open. Twelve hours starts whenever you touch that key."

Gideon stood over the radio and did not touch it yet.

Because here was the thing he hadn't said aloud, the thing sitting in his chest like ballast: the last time he'd spoken to his father's voice over distance, he'd been nineteen years old, calling down from Corren Pass on a field line crackling with static, telling Aldous Nystrom that the blast charges had gone off wrong, that the snowfield above the work camp had moved, that people were buried. And the voice that had come back to him—not his father's warmth, never that, but his father's *calm*, which was worse—had said: *Say nothing, Gideon. Come down the south route. We'll sort out the story together.*

*We'll sort out the story together.* Nine dead. Two countries' worth of blacklisting. Fourteen years of alias and ash, and the whole architecture of it had been sorted out in a boardroom before Gideon's boots had even left the pass.

His hand was shaking. He watched it shake, clinically, the way he used to watch his hands on bad rope-anchors—acknowledging the tremor without obeying it.

"Gideon." Sera's voice, quieter now. "Whatever you're going to say—say it to him, not to the ghost of Corren Pass. He gets the man who's standing here. Not the boy he buried."

That landed. That landed hard enough that he had to look away from her for a second, out the dark window, at the river he couldn't see but could hear—the Vane, patient, carrying everything downstream whether anyone confessed or not.

"All right," he said.

He touched the key.

* * *

"This is the return band, Mile Ninety relay, open channel." His own voice sounded strange to him, flattened by the transmitter, stripped of everything but words. "This message is for Aldous Nystrom, founder and chairman of the Vane Consolidated Mining and Water Consortium. My name is Gideon Nystrom."

He let that sit for three full seconds. Let the name travel. Let every operator on the relay chain write it down.

"You addressed me by name last night, so I'll extend you the same courtesy, though God knows you haven't earned it. You offered me a pardon. Reinstatement. My mother's ring, according to your people at the trading post—you should know your posters reach further than your discretion does. In exchange you want Halloway's survey data and my silence, and you want them wrapped up neat before the dry season ends."

He glanced at Sera. She'd pulled Halloway's jacket from the oilskin bag and laid it flat on the chart table, the hidden stitching slit open, the second map—pages of aquifer readings, flow gauges, dates and initials going back nine years—spread out under the lamp where the camera of his memory could hold every line of it. She caught him looking and gave him a small, hard nod. *Keep going.*

"Here is my answer. I'm not trading the truth for anything. I'm trading the truth *for itself*. Free of charge. To everyone."

His thumb pressed the key harder, as if pressure could carry conviction down the wire.

"Do you understand what that means, Father? It means that by dawn, three provinces are going to know that the headwaters of the Vane feed the aquifers under Harrow Flats, the Tessel basin, and the delta settlements. It means they're going to know your consortium has known it for nine years—Halloway's gauges don't lie, and neither do your own falsified filings, which I have copies of. It means they're going to know why you sealed the valley. Not for gold. For water. For the monopoly. For the right to turn a public river into a private tap and charge every soul downstream for the privilege of drinking."

Sera had gone to the window, watching the dark. But her shoulders were set in a way he recognized from the Grinder, from the checkpoint run—from every moment she'd steered straight at something that should have made her flinch.

"You called it quarantine in your broadcast," Gideon went on. "'Restricted watershed, health and safety measures.' I grew up learning your vocabulary, so let me translate it for the valley: quarantine means fences. Health and safety means armed gauging crews. Restricted watershed means that when the dry season bottoms out and the wells start failing—and they are failing, Aldous, Halloway proved it with numbers you signed off on burying—the only people with water will be the people who can pay you for it."

He paused. Breathed. This was the part he'd rehearsed in his head a hundred times since the lamplight, and it came out steadier than anything before it.

"So no. I decline the pardon. I decline the ring. I decline the family name restored by the man who sold it in the first place. You want to know what I'll accept? Here's my counteroffer, and I'll leave it on this channel until it expires: tear down the fences at the source. Open the gauging records. Rescind the closure orders on the upper Vane. Do that, publicly, and I'll consider whether the truth needs my voice at all—because it won't. The documents will speak fine on their own."

He released the key. Counted five seconds of dead air, letting the offer land in whatever office or helicopter or fortified parlor his father occupied tonight. Then he keyed it again, and the last of the ice in his chest cracked and let the flood through—not rage anymore, or not only rage. Something forged out of it. Something with edges.

"But hear me clearly, because I know how you operate, and I know what you do when purchases fail. If one fence goes up instead of down—if one crew moves against the villages on this river, if one 'quarantine measure' burns so much as a smokehouse—then the broadcast goes out anyway, tonight, complete, with your name attached to every page. There will be no deal left to make and no channel quiet enough to bury it. You taught me that a man's word is collateral, Father. Consider this mine, posted in full view of the valley."

He let go of the switch.

The radio hissed back at him, empty, indifferent—a machine that didn't care what had just passed through it. The lamp buzzed. The river murmured against the hull. Sera turned from the window slowly, and her face in the lamplight was unreadable for a moment, and then it wasn't: it was the face she wore after threading a rapid that had no business being survivable. Wound tight. Awake. Alive.

"Well," she said. "That's done."

"That's done."

"You realize he's going to answer."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

They didn't wait idle. Waiting was a luxury the river had stopped selling them around Mile Sixty.

Sera broke out the charts while Gideon fed wire and scavenged battery cells into the transmitter's backup rig, splicing connections with the same deliberate hands that had once rigged anchors above four-hundred-foot faces. The plan was simple and therefore fragile: the return-band broadcast would propagate on its own, but the *documents* needed a stronger relay than their little boat rig could push. Mile Ninety's gauging station had a mast and a booster. It also had a consortium technician, which meant it had a lock, which meant it had a problem—but problems were the only currency they'd been rich in for weeks.

They were forty minutes into the work when the radio clicked.

Not the return band. A different frequency—one of the consortium's operational channels, weakly audible on their receiver's scan, the kind of traffic Sera monitored out of habit and paranoia in equal measure. A dispatcher's voice, clipped and bored, reading coordinates.

"—repeat, directive from Chairman's office, priority seal. Upper Vane settlements designated noncompliant under watershed statute eleven. Sanitation enforcement teams mobilizing from Fort Calloway. Three targets: the ferry hamlet at Mile One-Ten, the stilt village—" a pause, paper shuffling, "—and the unrecorded settlement, coordinates per appendix C. Methods at team lead's discretion. Containment language applies. Acknowledge."

A pause. Then two voices acknowledging, flat as stones dropped in wells.

Gideon's hands stopped moving over the splice.

*Sanitation enforcement.* *Methods at team lead's discretion.* *Containment language applies.*

He knew that vocabulary the way he knew rope grades. He'd grown up at his father's elbow learning it, learning how the consortium wrote atrocities in the passive voice so that no hand ever appeared holding the match. *Sanitation* meant fire. *Discretion* meant deniability. *Containment* meant that whatever happened at those villages would be reported, if it was reported at all, as an unfortunate consequence of disease control among noncompliant populations.

"They're not even waiting for morning," Sera said quietly. She'd heard it too. Of course she had; she missed nothing on any frequency. "Fort Calloway's six hours downstream of the stilt village by fast boat. Less if they run the night channels."

"The unrecorded village." Gideon heard his own voice come out wrong—scraped, too low. "They can't put it on a map, so they're going to erase it off the earth instead. An entire community that survived fourteen years by being invisible, and my father's answer is to make them *permanently* invisible."

He stood up so fast the lamp rocked. The splice wire slipped from his fingers. For one raw moment he was nineteen again on a frozen pass, screaming into a field line while his father's calm voice sorted out the story—and the rage came up through him like magma, huge and blinding, wanting to break things, wanting to break *him*, wanting—

Sera's hand closed on his wrist. Not gentle. Firm. Pilot's grip.

"Breathe," she said. "Rage is fuel, Gideon. Burn it in the engine, not the cabin."

He breathed. Once. Twice. The cabin came back into focus: the charts, the lamp, the slit-open jacket, the radio still murmuring its flat acknowledgments. The flood inside him didn't recede—it *channeled*, cut itself a bed, started turning a wheel. He could feel it happening, feel the fury becoming something with direction and torque.

"Appendix C," he said. "They have coordinates for a village that isn't on maps. Which means someone surveyed it. Which means someone in that enforcement chain knows exactly where the elder's people sleep tonight." He pulled his wrist free of Sera's grip—not pulling away, just freeing his hands for work. "How fast can we warn them?"

Sera was already at the charts, finger tracing channels by lamplight, lips moving through distances and current speeds. "Main channel to the village cutoff: seven hours at night pace, and we'd pass within sight of Fort Calloway's patrol water doing it. Slough route adds two hours but keeps us behind the reeds." Her finger stopped. Tapped once. "But the gunboat's dead, our spare fuel went down with the supplies in the split channel, and we're running on what's in the tank plus the jerry cans. Enough for the slough route. Barely. No margin for weather, no margin for mistakes."

"We've been running without margin since the Landing."

"Yes," she said. "And I'd like to keep it that way, which is why we leave *now*, before the enforcement teams clear Fort Calloway, and why you finish that splice while I warm the engine." She was already moving, shrugging into her river coat, and at the door she paused and looked back at him with an expression he was beginning to know well—the one that meant she'd seen all the way through him and decided to steer alongside him anyway. "For what it's worth. What you said on that channel? About burning?" A grim half-smile. "He heard you. Men like him always hear the threats. What they never hear coming is the rescue."

She went out. The engine coughed, caught, settled into its workmanlike idle.

Gideon looked down at the radio, at the splice half-finished in his hands, and finished it with steady fingers. Then he keyed the return band one more time—not for his father this time, but for anyone else tuned in, any operator, any family, any elder with an old shortwave in a stilt house.

"This is Gideon Nystrom again. To every settlement on the Vane: enforcement teams are moving on the upper villages tonight under sanitation orders. You heard the dispatch; some of you have your own sets. Get your people to high ground. Get your boats in the water. And get your stories ready—because in a few days, the whole country is going to want to hear them."

He released the key, killed the lamp, and stepped out into the dark where the river waited, black and swift and finally, unmistakably, on their side of the fight.

Ahead, upstream, the night smelled faintly of woodsmoke—or perhaps he only imagined it. Perhaps. They cast off anyway, and ran for the reeds.
