## 26. Warning Downstream

The diversion cost us four hours we didn't have.

I said it out loud, once, standing at the tiller with the current pulling us backward toward the fork that led to the village, and Gideon didn't argue. He just looked at the smoke on the horizon—thin still, gray as old rope, but rising—and started coiling line. That was his answer. Four hours, spent without a word of debate, because the alternative was arriving at the source with clean hands and a drowned valley behind us.

We ran the fork fast. Sera's river, I'd have called it once, back when I thought rivers could belong to anyone. The channel narrowed and shallowed and I read it by feel now, by the color of the water over the bars, by the way the current leaned against the outside bends. Three days ago I'd have been white-knuckled. Now my hands moved on their own and some quiet part of me noted, with something like grief, how quickly a person learns a thing they'll only need for a little while longer.

Gideon stood at the bow with Halloway's jacket across his knees, the second map's stitching opened, the survey pages sleeved in oilcloth against spray. He'd barely slept since the radio. None of us had. Vess sat amidships with her wrists loose on her knees and her sidearm holstered and her eyes doing what her eyes always did—counting exits, counting us, counting the miles of water between here and whatever orders were waiting at the end of them.

"You could still turn me in," she'd said that morning, not looking at either of us. "Salvage rights on your own boat. It would buy you a day's head start."

"And you'd log it," Gideon said.

"I'd log it."

"Then we're not turning you in." He'd said it like arithmetic, no warmth in it, and Vess had nodded slowly, like a woman confirming a sum she already suspected.

Now the smoke thickened off our port bow, and beneath it the river bent toward the hidden landing—the one that wasn't on charts, the one whose people survived by being unrecorded, which meant there was no registry to warn, no official channel, no siren. There was only us, arriving by water, carrying the news the way news used to travel: mouth to ear, boat to bank, fast enough or not at all.

* * *

They saw us coming before we saw them.

A boy on a rock shelf stood up and ran inland, and by the time I brought the boat alongside the willow-staked landing, two dozen people had gathered—not armed, exactly, but holding things that could become weapons if the morning required it. Nets, adzes, a fish spear. The elder was at the front, as he'd been the first time, small and straight-backed, his hands folded on the head of a walking stick worn smooth as driftwood.

"We're not staying," Gideon called before the hull even touched. "There's no time. The consortium is burning the upper villages. Quarantine measures—they announced it on the radio band last night. They'll come downstream. You need to move. Today."

Murmuring. A man spat. Someone laughed, short and ugly, the laugh of people who've heard too many warnings from too many directions.

"The water is fine," a woman said. "The fish are running. We've heard nothing."

"You won't," Gideon said. "That's the point of you. That's always been the point of you. Nobody tells you anything because officially you don't exist." He held up the oilcloth sleeve. "But this does exist. Survey data from the source of the Vane. Springs fenced, gauging stations manned, aquifer readings three provinces drink from. They sealed this valley so hard they can't let anyone near it now—not us, not you, not the truth. And when they can't hide a thing, they burn around it."

The elder raised his stick, and the murmuring stopped the way water stops when a stone drops into it.

"You," he said to Gideon. Not *you people*. Not *pilot*. Just *you*, and his eyes had that same terrible recognition in them from the first night, the look of a man reading a name off a page that hadn't been shown to anyone. "Come. Walk with me. The rest of you—" he swept the stick toward the crowd, toward Sera and me, "—begin."

And they did. That was the part I'll carry longest, I think. No vote. No council. A woman dropped her net where she stood and walked to the drying racks and started pulling fish down. Two men went for the dugouts. Children were collected like scattered tools, gathered up under arms without ceremony. An entire community folding itself into motion inside ten minutes, because an old man with a stick decided to believe a stranger with a dead man's map.

Sera found me at the landing, watching the first dugout push off loaded with grain jars and a grandmother who refused to sit down.

"They've done this before," she said quietly.

"Everyone on this river has done this before," I said. "We just never noticed."

* * *

The elder walked Gideon up past the gardens to a flat stone above the flood line, and there he stopped, and there—for the first time since the landing—he spoke plainly.

"Nystrom," he said.

Gideon went very still. "You knew."

"I knew the name before you gave it. Your father's company surveyed these springs thirty years ago. Men came with instruments and left with numbers, and the numbers said the water under this valley feeds wells in three provinces. My father watched them take those readings. He told me: one day someone will come back for the water itself, and they will call it law." The elder turned. "Then came Corren Pass. Then came a blacklisted guide with a different name and his father's jaw. Rivers remember faces longer than names, boy. I remembered both."

"Why say nothing?"

"Because a man running from his name cannot be trusted with it. But a man who carries a dead stranger's promise upstream through checkpoints and gunboats—" the elder tapped Gideon's chest with two fingers, once, "—that man's name has changed shape. So now I give it back, with interest."

He pressed his palm flat against Gideon's sternum, and spoke words in the river tongue, low and rhythmic, and though I understood almost none of it, I understood the shape of it: a blessing. When he finished he said, in trade speech:

"A warning, to go with it. The source is not a place. Everyone who goes there wants to own it, measure it, fence it, name it. But water is not property. Water is a debt the mountain pays forward. If you stand at the headwaters and speak your father's crimes, speak also the debt. Speak what everyone downstream is owed. Do that, and the blessing holds. Forget it, and you are only another Nystrom with a better story."

Gideon's throat worked. "And if I can't tell the difference anymore?"

"Then stay at the foot of the mountain," the elder said, not unkindly, "and let the water pass you by."

* * *

By midday the evacuation was a fact you could see from the water.

Dugouts and rafts moving downriver in a strung-out line, heavy-laden, the old and the young aboard, the strong walking the banks beside them with poles. Smoke on the horizon had thickened by then—two columns now, maybe three, hard to count through heat shimmer—and the wind had shifted enough that we could smell it: green wood, thatch, something chemical underneath that none of us wanted to name.

Vess stood at the landing with her radio handset in her hand, not transmitting, just holding it, like a woman deciding whether a thing was a tool or a wound.

"If I call this in," she said, "I report an unauthorized evacuation interfering with declared quarantine operations. That's the order. That's what it says, word for word." She looked down the line of boats. "My handlers wrote those words sitting somewhere with clean air."

"So don't call it in," Sera said.

"And when they ask why the village wasn't logged as dispersed?"

"Tell them the river took it." Sera shrugged. "Rivers do that."

Vess almost smiled. Almost. She clipped the handset back to her belt, silent, and that silence was the loudest testimony yet.

Gideon came down from the stone alone. He looked older—not tired, exactly; emptied out and refilled with something heavier. He stopped in front of me and handed me the oilcloth sleeve without being asked, the way you hand someone a thing you've decided they'll be carrying the rest of the way.

"He knew my name the whole time," Gideon said. "First night. Knew it and fed us anyway."

"Elder's like that," Sera said. "Whole village is like that. It's why they're not on maps. Maps are for people who want to be found."

Gideon watched the last raft clear the bend, the polemen leaning into it, the grandmother still refusing to sit. Behind them the smoke stood up straighter, closer, and the wind carried that chemical edge again, sharper now, undeniable.

"Four hours," he said quietly. "It cost us four hours, and I'd spend forty."

"You'd spend everything," Sera said. "That's the problem with you."

"That's the only thing about me that ever worked."

We pushed off within the hour, running light and fast with the current finally in our favor, the village emptying behind us and the source waiting ahead—two days' hard water, maybe less, with the dry season closing its fist around the upper river a little more each afternoon.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The engine drummed. The banks slid past, staked landings and garden terraces going gray with distance, and ahead of us the gorge walls rose higher and higher until they met the sky in a seam of blue.

It was Vess who broke the silence, at dusk, reading the last light off the water.

"There's a gunboat anchorage below the final weir," she said. "Two boats, full crews, floodlights. Aldous will have pulled everything he has to the mouth of the source valley by tomorrow night." She turned in the thwart, and in the failing light her face was unreadable and completely honest at once. "You should know what's waiting. All of it. Because after tonight, there's no more running this river blind."

Gideon looked upstream, at the darkening seam of sky between the canyon walls, where the water we'd been following all this way began.

"Good," he said. "I'm done running blind."

The current carried us on into the gorge, and somewhere far above us, past the rapids and the fences and the gauging stations, the source waited—fenced, measured, guarded, and about to be spoken to by name.
