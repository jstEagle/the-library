## 27. The Last Rapid

The gorge announced itself before we saw it—a sound like the river grinding its teeth, carried up through the rock and into our boots.

Sera killed the engine and let us drift, and we both stood there listening. Below us, maybe a kilometer down, the Vane funneled into a slot no wider than a wagon road, and what was left of the dry season's water went through it in a single furious ribbon. Everything else—the wide braided channels we'd been threading for two days—simply stopped. The map showed one line through the gorge. The river showed stone.

"That's it," Sera said. Not a question. She'd known since dawn; I think she'd known since the village, when the elder pressed his hand to her shoulder and told her the water would not carry her much farther. "That's the last of it."

I hauled out Halloway's map and held it against the light. The old man had drawn the gorge in a different ink than the rest—a darker line, pressed hard enough to tear the paper in places. Along its length he'd written a single word, over and over, faint as breath: *walk, walk, walk.* And then at the head of the slot, in that same dark ink: *or don't.*

"He ran it," I said. "Halloway. He came down this way."

"Twenty years ago, with snowmelt." Sera leaned on the rail beside me, watching the white noise of the gorge. "We've got a third of that."

I didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer. We'd done the arithmetic the night before, lying awake in shifts while the river dropped audibly around us—you could hear it, a slow sighing retreat, the water giving up ground hour by hour. By tomorrow evening the upper gorge would be a staircase of dry stone. By the day after, even the slot would be wading depth, which sounded like mercy until you understood that wading depth meant the boat couldn't float at all, and everything we carried—Halloway's survey, the transmitter parts, three weeks of evidence—would have to go overland through country where Vess's handlers were already regrouping.

Today was the day. Today or the portage from hell.

* * *

We spent the morning doing what I knew how to do, which was rope.

The plan was mine, and I'll be honest—it was a mountaineer's plan imposed on a river problem, and Sera looked at me like I'd suggested we fly. But she heard me out, because that's what she did by then. Somewhere between the Grinder and the checkpoint we'd stopped arguing about whose element this was. The truth was uglier and better: neither of us owned this river anymore. It belonged to the drought, and we were just asking permission.

Four hundred meters of gorge. I'd climbed down along the rim at first light, testing anchors, and found what I'd hoped for and feared: the old consortium survey bolts, still in place from whenever they'd first come up here with instruments and ambitions. Rusted, but sound. Whoever sealed this valley had anchored their own claim to it, bolt by bolt, and never imagined those bolts would hold lines for thieves.

I rigged six stations down the left wall—two ropes each, a working line and a backup, staggered so we could lower the boat in stages, swing her across the worst shelves, and catch her at the bottom. It took four hours. My hands were raw by the third station and my shoulder—the one the avalanche gave me, the one that ached every cold morning since Corren—had gone from ache to argument. I said nothing. Sera saw anyway. She'd gotten good at reading the things I didn't say, the way you learn to read water: not the surface but the pull underneath.

At noon we ate hardtack and dried fish on the rim, legs hanging over four hundred meters of noise, and Sera pointed her chin at the slot below.

"I'll take her through," she said. "You work the lines from the banks. That's the trade."

"You could portage with me. We both could. Haul her over the scree like the Bones."

"The Bones nearly killed you." She said it flatly, without cruelty. "And that was with water in the river and two days of light. This portage would be three days, loaded, in the open, with a gunboat somewhere behind us and the dry season closing like a door. No. We run it. Half-run it. Whatever you want to call lowering a boat down a waterfall on ropes—but we do it today, fast, and we're through before anyone figures out where we went."

"And if a line parts?"

She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but a kind of settled clarity, like water finding its level. "Then I'll be very annoyed," she said, "for about a second."

* * *

We launched at the head of the slot at half past one.

The plan was simple in the way plans are simple right up until they meet the world. Sera would take the boat down the throat of the cascade alone—no passenger weight, no second opinion—and I'd control her descent from above and beside, paying out the working lines, checking her swing at each shelf, hauling her off the rocks when the current tried to keep her. Four stations. Four chances to kill her.

She stripped the boat to the frame first. Fuel drums lashed low and centered, the waterproofed survey canister double-bagged and strapped inside the hull's strongest bay, everything else cached on the rim with the spare lines for me to carry down after. When she was done she stood in the stern in her patched jacket, hair tied back tight, and looked smaller and harder than any person I'd ever seen.

"Gideon." She said my name—my real name, the one only she used now, the one I was still learning to wear. "If I go in at the third shelf, don't come down after me. You carry the canister out. That's the promise now. Not Halloway's. Ours."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I carry it out."

She nodded once, and pushed off.

I'll tell it straight, because she deserves it told straight, and because I've run it in my head every night since and it doesn't get easier—it gets more precise, the way a scar gets more precise as it settles.

The first shelf took her almost gently. The boat dropped two meters into a churning pocket, buried her bow, and Sera brought her up with two strokes and a lean that looked like nothing and was everything. I paid out line from my anchor above, feeling the load come on smooth, and swung her left around a fang of granite that would have opened the hull like a tin lid. She read my tension on the line—I swear she felt it through the rope, the way I eased her left—and she took the angle without being told.

The second shelf was worse. The channel narrowed to less than the boat's length, and the whole remaining river stood up in it—a single green tongue of water, maybe forty centimeters deep and moving faster than anything I'd ever put a rope on. Sera didn't fight it. She shipped her oars, grabbed the bow line I'd pre-rigged along the wall, and rode it down standing, letting the current do the steering while I took her weight at the next station and slowed her just enough that she could pick her line among the rocks. Knife rock, the pilots called this—stone sheared off vertical by ten thousand years of flood, edges sharp enough to shave with. One touch at that speed and the boat was done, and probably Sera with it.

For eleven seconds she was perfect. I watched her choose three lines in succession, each one impossible, each one made possible by the half-second decision before it, and I understood suddenly why she'd named her price so high back at the landing, why she'd run the checkpoint weir at night in flood stage, why she was standing in a dying river running a cascade that had no business being run. It wasn't courage. Courage is deciding. This was past deciding. This was a woman doing the one thing in the world she was built for, with total attention and no room left for anything else—not fear, not me, not death. Mastery looks like that from the outside. It looks like calm.

Then the third shelf took the choice away from her.

A lateral wave—water rebounding off the far wall, the kind of thing that doesn't exist when a river has volume, but a thin river is all edges—caught the stern and slewed her sideways. I saw it happen and I was already moving, dropping down my own line to the third station, taking the working rope around my back and my bad shoulder and leaning into the drop. The load hit me like a falling tree. The shoulder tore something—I heard it, or felt it, a wet click deep in the joint—and I held anyway, boots braced against a bolt hole, lowering her by pure refusal, meter by meter, keeping her hull square to the current while the river tried to turn her broadside onto the knives.

She got an oar into the water. Just once—one stroke, planted against the seam behind the shelf—and the boat answered, swinging true, and my line caught her fall, and she dropped into the fourth pocket upright and alive and screaming something at me that the gorge ate whole.

I couldn't feel my arm. I lowered myself down the wall anyway, hand over hand, because the last station was mine too and there was no one else.

* * *

The final drop was five meters into the pool at the gorge's mouth, and it should have been the easy part, and it nearly wasn't.

My line fouled. A kink in the pay-out, a loop thrown wrong in my numb-handed hurry, and it jammed the descender just as Sera lined up for the drop. She hung there above the falls, boat wallowing in the entry current, and I saw her look up at me—saw her understand in one glance what had happened and what it meant: thirty seconds of hanging in the jaws, or cut loose and take the drop uncontrolled.

She cut loose.

No hesitation. Her knife came out, the working line parted, and the boat went over the edge sideways.

What happened next took less time than telling it. She threw her whole body against the high side—against the rock side, the wrong side, the side that should have pinned her—and the hull kissed the lip, pivoted on a fang I will see in my dreams for the rest of my life, and fell the last three meters flat, slapping into the pool with a crack I felt through the soles of my boots. The hull held. The canister held. Sera surfaced alongside, spat out a mouthful of the Vane, and hauled herself aboard before the boat finished settling.

By the time I down-climbed the last pitch—one-armed, furious, shaking—the boat was eddied out below the gorge and Sera was sitting in the stern wringing out her jacket, and when she saw me she started laughing. Not hysteria. Something older than that. The laugh of a person who has been to the exact edge of the world and come back with her pockets full.

"You fouled your line," she called up.

"I noticed."

"Third shelf. You held four hundred meters of boat on a shoulder that's supposed to be held together with scar tissue."

"I noticed that too."

She waited while I came down the last of it and collapsed into the bilge, arm dead at my side, and then she did something I didn't expect. She took my ruined hand in both of hers, turned it over, and looked at the rope burns crossing the palm like a signature.

"Halloway wrote *walk*," she said. "Six times."

"I remember."

"We didn't walk." She tied off my hand with a strip of her sleeve, quick and competent, her fingers warm against mine. "First thing anyone's ever run with me who wasn't afraid of it. You know what that makes you?"

"Tired."

"It makes you crew." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Gideon Nystrom, crew of one. Don't let it go to your head."

* * *

We made camp that night below the gorge, on a gravel bar that hadn't existed a month ago, and the silence was the strangest part. Above us the Vane had been roaring for three days. Here it moved shallow and quiet over the stones, barely knee-deep in the channel, a ghost of itself going home to the springs we were finally close to claiming.

I couldn't sleep. The shoulder throbbed in time with my pulse, and every time I closed my eyes I saw the boat pivot on that fang of rock, balanced for one impossible instant between two deaths, and come down flat because a woman trusted her own hands more than the river's math.

Near midnight Sera sat up across the coals. She didn't ask how I was. She looked north instead, up the valley, where the land rose in black folds toward the headwaters, and her face in the firelight was unreadable.

"Lights," she said.

I followed her gaze. Far up the canyon, miles off, a string of lights burned steady against the stars—too regular for camps, too bright for homes. Floodlights. Fences. The gauging stations, maybe, or the compound at the source itself.

"They know we're coming," I said.

"Maybe." She fed a stick to the fire. "Or maybe they've always been there, and nobody downstream ever thought to look up. Either way—" she lay back down, arms behind her head, eyes on that distant constellation of other people's power— "we're through the last hard water, Gideon. From here it's climbing."

She was quiet a moment, and then, softer, almost to herself:

"So why does it feel like we just entered the trap?"

Above us the stars kept their distance, and the lights in the valley burned on, patient as a held breath, waiting for whatever we would bring them.
