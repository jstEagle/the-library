## 28. Foot of the Source

The gorge spat us out at dawn into a bowl of gray water, and the river simply stopped being a river.

I'd been running water since I was nine years old—my father's ferry, the delta channels, the long brown reaches of the lower Vane—and I had never once seen a river end. Rivers narrowed, rivers braided, rivers vanished into marsh or split into a hundred threads you had to guess between. This one just quit. One moment there was current enough to lean on, the next the *Marrow* ground her keel on gravel and the water ahead of her was a sheet of braided shallows fanning out toward a wall of mountain that had no business being that close, that fast.

I killed the engine and the silence came down on us like a dropped tarpaulin.

"End of the line," Gideon said from the bow.

He didn't sound disappointed. That was the strange thing. For three weeks he had been a man running—from the checkpoints, from the name on the posters, from the voice of his father crackling out of every radio we passed—and now, at the moment the running should have gotten harder, something in his shoulders had come down out of his ears. He looked at that wall of stone the way other men look at a door they've been locked out of for years.

I looked at the same wall and saw a different door. One that only opened by climbing.

"Boat's done," I said. "She won't float above here even in spring flood. Not that we'll see spring flood if the dry season has its way."

"She got us here." Gideon ran his palm along the gunwale, and I understood he was saying goodbye. To the boat, maybe. To the version of himself who'd been a passenger in someone else's hull. "She got us here with a cracked manifold, a patched hull, and no fuel worth mentioning. Sera Okonkwo, you are the finest pilot I have ever paid an outrageous sum to."

"You never paid the second half."

"I'm about to die on a mountain. Consider it settled."

Vess laughed. It was a small, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been used in years, and I caught Gideon glancing at her with the same surprise I felt. Three days since the gunboat wreck. Three days since we'd pulled her half-drowned out of the narrows and she'd stood on the gravel with her service pistol still in its holster and made a choice neither of us had asked her to make. She hadn't gone back to it. She'd helped haul the *Marrow* off the rocks instead, and she'd been hauling things ever since, as if the only way she knew to process what she'd done was to keep her hands moving.

"Split the load," she said now, all business, crouching at the stern hatch. "What do we carry, what do we cache?"

"Everything matters," Gideon said. "But we carry light. Rope, the survey, water, food for four days. The tools."

"The transmitter parts," I said, and both of them looked at me. "The gauging station will have its own equipment. But if it doesn't—if they've stripped it, if Aldous has already ordered it gutted—we'll wish we'd carried our own." I'd been salvaging pieces since the checkpoint run: a hand-crank dynamo off the gunboat wreck, coil wire, a signal key I'd built myself from a door hinge and copper salvaged from the *Marrow*'s dead radio. It all went into a waterproof roll I'd slept with for a week like a child with a stuffed animal.

Gideon nodded slowly. "You've been planning the end of this since the middle of it."

"Somebody had to."

We worked through the morning. I cached what the *Marrow* couldn't give up—her spare lines, the tarp, half the food—under a cairn we built at the high-water mark, stones stacked in the old ferryman's pattern my father taught me, three and two and one, so any river soul passing would know someone meant to come back. Then we took what we could carry and stood looking up.

The headwaters wall rose maybe four thousand feet above us, folded and gray-green, streaked with white where springs bled out of the rock. Somewhere up there, water was coming out of the ground. Somewhere up there was the reason for every checkpoint, every hunter, every burned village and blacklisted name between us and this gravel bar. Halloway had walked down out of that wall with a map stitched into his jacket and a hole in him that wouldn't close. The least we could do was walk up.

"Trail's on the north side," Vess said. She said it flatly, the way she said everything, but I saw her jaw work before the words came. "I've serviced the gauging line twice a year. There's a maintenance path. Switchbacks. It's steep, but it's graded."

"You've been up there," Gideon said. Not a question.

"I've serviced equipment. I've never been past the fence line at the springs. Nobody below station chief has." She shouldered her pack and didn't look at either of us. "You should know what you're walking into. The station at the foot of the springs is manned year-round. Four technicians, a station chief, a radio. They'll have been told you're coming since the narrows. They'll have orders."

"What orders?"

Vess looked at him then, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before—not the flat professional mask, not the guilt. Something older and wearier underneath, like water under ice. "Whatever orders keep the valley quiet. I signed the same orders for six years, Nystrom. I know how they're written. I know how they're followed." She adjusted her pack strap. "I also know that the people who follow them are not monsters. They're technicians. They read gauges. Most of them have families in towns that drink this water without knowing where it comes from."

"And when they're told to do something that isn't reading gauges?" I asked.

Vess was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence—that thin, exhausted trickle of it, anyway.

"Then we find out," she said. "That's what we're walking up there to find out."

* * *

The maintenance path was exactly what she'd promised: graded, switchbacked, and brutal. The consortium had built it well enough to get equipment up and ore samples down, and had maintained it just well enough to be passable, which meant it was steep in the way that steals the breath and the argument out of you at the same time. Nobody talked for the first hour. There was only the crunch of boots on scree, the creak of pack straps, and below us the Vane shrinking to a silver thread in its gray bed, the whole journey we'd survived laid out like a map we'd already used up.

Gideon climbed like what he was—a mountain man with his mountain taken away. I watched him read the slope the way I read water: the places where the scree would run, the places where the frost heave had loosened the grade, the places where you cut across instead of up. Twice he called a rest stop before any of us knew we needed one. Twice he redistributed weight without asking permission, taking the heaviest coil of rope off Vess's pack while she was drinking and saying nothing about it, and her letting him, and both of them pretending it hadn't happened.

It was on the third switchback, where the path crossed a spring-fed seep and the rock wept cold water down a face of black stone, that Vess stopped and pointed.

"There. That's the fence."

I'd expected wire. What I saw instead made my stomach turn in a way the climb hadn't. The fence was chain-link, eight feet, topped with coil, running along a contour line maybe two hundred feet above us—and beyond it, the valley opened up into a hanging bowl of green, and the springs came out of the mountain in a dozen bright threads that braided together into the first true water of the Vane. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I'd seen since the delta, maybe since ever. And it was fenced. Gauging stations stood at intervals along the watercourse like little white teeth, instrument sheds and solar arrays, and a gravel service road switchbacked down the valley wall to the south where a helicopter pad sat empty and waiting.

They had fenced a miracle and put a lock on it.

"Six years," Vess said quietly. "I've been within sight of that for six years, and I never once asked what it cost the towns downstream. You stop seeing it. That's the trick of it. You service the gauges, you file the readings, you go home. The fence is just... weather."

"It's not weather," Gideon said. There was no heat in his voice, which somehow made it worse. "It's a claim. Every foot of that wire says *mine* to every person downstream who's ever been thirsty." He looked at Vess. "You know what the filings say this water is?"

"Industrial reserve. Non-potable classification pending survey."

"And Halloway's survey says?"

"Potable. Artesian. Feeding three aquifers." She recited it like a confession. "I read the pages you showed me. Twice. I checked them against my own gauge data from six seasons." Her voice did something complicated. "They match. They've always matched. The consortium's own instruments have been telling them the truth for years, and they filed the opposite."

Above us, the wind came down the valley carrying the smell of cold water and green things, and none of us moved for a long moment. Then Gideon shouldered his pack again.

"Then let's go introduce the truth to its owners."

* * *

We came at the fence from the north, where the maintenance path crossed it through a vehicle gate, because Vess knew the gate code and said so without being asked, and I watched that small act land on her like a stone—giving up the last piece of the machinery she'd been part of. The lock clicked open under her hand and she stood there a moment with her palm flat against the chain-link, and then she pushed through and didn't look back.

The valley floor was soft underfoot. Grass, actual grass, in a region where the dry season had burned everything downstream to the color of old bone. Water ran in channels everywhere, bright and cold and loud, and I kept thinking of Kestrel's Landing in late summer, the river so low the ferry ran on half-load, the wells at the river villages going brackish one by one while this water sat behind wire, classified as *pending*, year after year after year.

The foot station sat at the base of the main spring, a low prefab building with an instrument tower and a radio mast, and as we came down toward it I saw the door open and figures step out—four of them, then a fifth, standing in the sun in consortium gray, watching us come.

Nobody ran. Nobody shouted. Vess had been right about that too: they were technicians, not soldiers. I counted rifles on two of them, slung, hands not on them, and I watched Gideon walk toward the group with his hands visible and his chin up and Halloway's map rolled in its oilcloth tube across his back like a sword, and I thought: *this is the man they blacklisted. This is the man who was so dangerous they had to take his name.*

He stopped ten paces out. The wind moved the grass between them.

"I'm looking for whoever holds the station log," he said. "And whoever holds the transmitter key."

The station chief was a woman in her fifties, sunburned, gray at the temples, with the flat competent look of someone who had run a lonely post for a long time. She looked at Gideon for a long moment, then at Vess, and something passed between them that I couldn't read.

"Vess," the chief said. "You're supposed to be dead in the narrows."

"I'm supposed to be a lot of things, Chief Ibarra."

"You're supposed to report the map recovered and the carriers contained." Ibarra's eyes went to Gideon, then to me, then to the oilcloth tube. "That was the order as of last night. I have it in writing."

"And what did you tell them?" Gideon asked.

Ibarra didn't answer right away. She looked past us, down the valley, at the thread of the river we'd climbed beside, running away toward all those towns and wells and ferry landings, and when she spoke her voice was quiet enough that the water almost covered it.

"I told them the transmission was garbled. I told them I needed another day to confirm." She looked back at Gideon. "That was this morning. I don't have another day in me, Nystrom. I've got maybe an hour before they call again, and then I have to be either the woman who followed orders or the woman who didn't. So whatever it is you climbed four thousand feet to do—"

Gideon slid the oilcloth tube off his shoulder.

"—you'd best start doing it."

* * *

Inside, the station smelled of coffee and machine oil and paper. Ibarra cleared her two technicians out of the instrument room with a jerk of her head, and the younger one—the one with the rifle—hesitated at the door, looking at Vess, and Vess nodded once, and he went. The older one lingered, and Ibarra said, "Marta. Go," and Marta went, but I noticed she left the door half open behind her, and I noticed she stayed within earshot, folding filter papers at the bench in the next room with great concentration and not very clean hands.

Gideon unrolled Halloway's survey on Ibarra's chart table and weighted the corners with coffee mugs, and I watched her read it the way Vess had read it—fast at first, then slower, then going back to the beginning with her finger under the lines.

"These are artesian pressures," she said. "These are aquifer boundaries. This is—" She stopped. Her finger had landed on the correlation tables, and I saw her go still the way water goes still before it freezes. "This is the Corren Pass blast record."

"Seismic signatures," Gideon said. "Unsanctioned. The consortium's own blasting crew, three years before the avalanche, weakening a slope they'd already been told was unstable. My father signed the cover-up. He signed it with my name."

Ibarra looked up at him. "You're the guide. The one from the inquiry."

"I'm the guide."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she did something I didn't expect: she reached across the table and turned the survey pages over, one by one, checking the back sides, checking margins, checking the chain of initials down each sheet's footer. Surveyor's marks. Halloway's initials, and beside them, on the oldest pages, a second set of initials I didn't recognize.

"Whose are these?" she asked.

Gideon leaned in. I watched him read the initials—*A.N.*—and I watched his face do something I had never seen it do in all the weeks I'd known him. Not grief, exactly. Not rage. Something more like a man finding his own handwriting in a dead man's notebook.

"That's my father's," he said. "Aldous Nystrom countersigned the original survey. Twenty-two years ago. Before the fence. Before the falsified filings." His voice came out level and terrible. "He knew. He's known from the beginning. He signed the truth, and then he buried it, and then he buried me to keep it buried."

The room was very quiet. In the next room, Marta had stopped pretending to fold filter papers.

Ibarra straightened up. She looked at the survey, and at the transmitter tower visible through the window, its guy wires humming faintly in the valley wind, and at Vess, and last at me.

"The transmitter's rated for regional broadcast," she said. "It reaches every settlement radio on the river. It's maintained weekly. It's—" she exhaled— "it's been used twice in six years, both times for flood warnings, both times signed by me." She looked at Gideon. "You understand what you're asking. The moment that key goes down, there's no version of this where I keep this station."

"I know," Gideon said.

"And you understand they'll come. Not hunters this time. Everything."

"I know."

Ibarra nodded slowly, and then, to my lasting surprise, she smiled—a small, tired, almost relieved thing, like a woman setting down a load she'd been carrying so long she'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Then we'd best test the transmitter before your father calls back," she said. "Marta! Bring me the logbook and the key. And Marta—" a pause, and I saw the older technician appear in the doorway with her sleeves rolled and her chin set— "bring the good antenna wire. The one we're not supposed to use."

I turned to Gideon. He was standing over the survey with his hand flat on the page, on his father's initials, on twenty-two years of buried truth, and he looked up at me and there was nothing left in his face of the man who'd flinched at the registry office window in Kestrel's Landing.

"Helicopter," I said. "That pad down the valley isn't empty because they forgot about us. He's coming, Gideon. He's coming to see it for himself."

Gideon looked out the window at the bright threads of the springs, at the water running free over the grass toward the fence line, and his hand closed slowly into a fist on the table.

"Then we broadcast before he lands."
