## 29. The Sealed Valley

The last of the trees gave out an hour after dawn, and then there was nothing between us and the sky but a slope of wet granite and the sound of water coming from everywhere at once.

I had spent three weeks imagining what the source of the Vane would look like. In my head it was always small—a seep in a rock face, a thread of silver no wider than my hand, something you could cover with a boot and feel foolish for having crossed a country to find. That's what sources were supposed to be. That's what Halloway's map had promised: a mark like a starburst, *the head*, and beneath it in his cramped hand, *you'll know when you see it*.

I didn't know. I stood on the shoulder of the valley with Sera breathing hard beside me and Vess a dozen paces back with her rifle slung and her hands empty, and I did not know what I was looking at, because nothing in my life had prepared me for a river being born inside a fence.

There were springs—dozens of them, maybe hundreds, weeping out of the whole eastern wall of the valley in braided ribbons that caught the early light and threw it back white. The water gathered in terraces cut into the hillside, channelled, measured, directed. Concrete weirs crossed each ribbon at intervals. Pipes ran from pool to pool. And around all of it, following the contours of the land for as far as I could see in either direction, ran a fence.

Not a boundary fence. Not a rancher's wire to keep cattle honest. This was chain-link, two meters high, topped with three strands of barbed, set on steel posts sunk into poured footings, and hung every fifty meters with signs the size of dinner plates:

**VANE WATERSHED AUTHORITY — RESTRICTED CATCHMENT — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY — VIOLATORS PROSECUTED UNDER CONSORTIUM STATUTE 11**

Sera said a word in her mother's language that I didn't understand and understood completely anyway.

"That's not a source," she said. "That's a factory."

"It's a tap," I said.

We came down off the granite slope in a long traverse, staying in the shadow of the western ridge, and the closer we got the worse it looked. The springs weren't just fenced—they were plumbed. Gauging stations stood at the junctions of the channels, little prefabricated huts on stilts with solar arrays and antenna masts, one after another down the valley like beads on a string. I counted eleven before I stopped counting. Each one would have a technician or two inside it, logging flow rates, transmitting them downstream to some office where men in clean shirts decided, hour by hour, how much river the rest of the world was going to get.

The dry season had stripped everything else bare. The lower Vane was down to its bones—we'd run channels last week that Sera swore had been rapids in her father's time. Every settlement on the river was rationing. Kestrel's Landing had tanks on carts going street to street. And here, at the top of it all, the water came out of the mountain in abundance, hit a concrete weir, and went into a pipe.

"Look at the overflow," Sera said quietly.

I looked. Below the lowest gauging station, the natural channel of the Vane proper began—and it was a trickle. A sad, gravel-choked trickle wandering through a canyon that had been carved by a river ten times its width. Meanwhile the pipes, thick as a man's torso, ran off westward over a saddle in the ridge, toward the consortium's mining country, toward the company towns, toward wherever the money was.

"They're not even using most of it," Vess said behind us. Her voice had a strange flatness. "They're storing it. There are reservoirs over the saddle—I've seen the supply manifests. They hold the water back so the price stays up. My handlers call it *inventory management*."

She'd been walking with us for two days now, ever since the gunboat wreck, and she still hadn't decided what she was. Neither had I. But she knew this valley. She'd guarded its secret without ever seeing it, which was, I was beginning to understand, how the consortium preferred things done.

* * *

We moved along the fence line until we found what Halloway's map had marked with a second symbol: a gap. Not a gate—a gap, where a section of fencing had been washed out by a flood years ago and replaced with a single strand of wire strung between two leaning posts, the kind of repair that gets approved on paper and never inspected in person. Halloway had known about it. Halloway had probably used it.

I went through first, holding the wire up with a stick while Sera ducked under with her pack, then Vess. On the inside, the grass grew longer. Nobody had walked here in a while except maintenance crews, and they rode machines.

The nearest gauging station sat two hundred meters downstream of the main spring complex, close enough that we could hear the hum of its electronics over the water. Through its window, lit by a desk lamp against the grey morning, I could see a young man in a consortium-grey jacket writing in a logbook. He looked bored. He looked like he was twenty-two years old and counting the days on his contract. He looked, more than anything, like me at twenty-two—somebody who'd taken a job because it came with a bed and a wage and hadn't asked yet what the job was *for*.

"We could take the station," Sera said. It wasn't eagerness in her voice; it was arithmetic. "Cut the feed, take the transmitter—"

"Not yet." I crouched in the long grass and unrolled Halloway's map on a flat stone, weighting the corners. "Look at the layout. Eleven stations, one line running down-valley to a relay mast on that knoll. If we hit one station, they know in minutes and they seal the relay before we reach it. We get one move in this valley, and it has to be the right one."

Vess crouched beside me. Up close, in the flat light, I could see how tired she was—the same exhaustion we carried, plus the particular exhaustion of a person whose map of the world has been torn up and not yet redrawn. She pointed at the knoll.

"The relay is manned too. Four technicians, a site supervisor, no armed guard that I know of. Why would there be? Nobody's supposed to know this valley exists." She paused. "Nobody's *supposed* to include me."

"You tipped off the checkpoints," I said. It wasn't an accusation. It was just the record, getting itself straight.

"I did what I was paid for. Then I read a dead man's survey data and found my own village's wells on his list of aquifers scheduled for drawdown." She looked at the fenced springs with an expression I couldn't name. "So now I'm doing what nobody's paying me for. Don't make it heavier than it is."

Sera snorted softly. "You two deserve each other," she said, and started moving down the grass line toward the station, low and quick, a woman who had spent her whole life reading water and had apparently decided that buildings obeyed the same rules—find the current, ride it, don't fight it head-on.

* * *

We watched the station for an hour.

It was Sera's discipline, learned on the river: never enter water you haven't watched turn over. We lay in the wet grass with the smell of it in our noses and catalogued everything. The technician wrote in his logbook at quarter past and quarter to, every hour, regular as a heartbeat. He stepped outside once to smoke, standing on the station's little deck with his back to us, looking out at the fenced springs the way a man looks at anything he sees every day—which is to say, not looking at all. He never once turned his eyes uphill to the ridge where three people lay in the grass watching him guard the biggest secret in the province.

That was the thing that curdled in me as we waited. Not the fence, not the pipes, not even the theft of it—though God knows the theft was vast enough, a river stolen at its birth, every farm and ferry town downstream paying rent on water that fell as rain on public mountains. What got me was the boredom. The whole machine of it—the bribes, the blacklists, the hunters, the burned villages' worth of menace radiating out from this quiet place—all of it existed to protect a secret whose guardians couldn't be bothered to look up from their logbooks.

They didn't need vigilance. They needed paperwork. The consortium had learned that a lie enforced by statute is cheaper than a lie enforced by guns, and they'd built a fortress out of filing cabinets. Statute 11. Restricted catchment. Authorized personnel only. Somewhere in a registry office, my own name sat in a file with a red band across it, and I had spent seven years treating that file like a force of nature, like weather, like gravity—when it was just ink. Just a man named Aldous Nystrom deciding what the paper would say, and everyone downstream believing the paper because believing the paper was easier.

"Gideon." Sera touched my arm. "Two more coming up the path. Maintenance."

Two men in grey coveralls, wheeling a cart of tools, came along a gravel track from the direction of the relay knoll. They passed within thirty meters of our hiding place. One of them was complaining about his knees; the other was laughing at him. They let themselves into the station through a door whose lock hung open on a hasp, and their voices mingled with the technician's, and somebody put a kettle on, because five minutes later there was steam on the window.

A kettle. Steam rising off a kettle inside the fortress at the source of the Vane, while downstream the wells failed and the villages queued for tank carts and a dead prospector's map had cost three weeks of blood and white water to carry home.

I felt awe die in my chest—not fade, *die*, the way a fire dies when you realize the room it's warming belongs to someone who locked you out of it on purpose. What replaced it was colder and much more useful. Clarity. I could see the whole architecture now, the way you see a rapid whole from the scout's ledge before you commit to running it: where the water went, where the power came from, where the weak point was.

The weak point was the truth. It was always the truth. A secret this big, guarded this cheaply, was a dam with a crack in it. All it needed was pressure applied at exactly the right place, publicly, undeniably, in a voice nobody could dismiss.

And there was only one voice left in the world that the consortium couldn't buy, couldn't blacklist, couldn't bury in an avalanche report—because they'd already spent that voice once, seven years ago, and you can't discredit a man twice if the second thing he says proves the first thing they buried.

They should have let me stay invisible. That was the thought that kept arriving, patient as water working at stone. Every hunter, every checkpoint, every forged permit and slashed fuel line—it had all been the consortium spending enormous effort to drag me, specifically, out of the shadows and into their valley. Aldous Nystrom had spent seven years making sure no one believed a word his son said. And then he'd sent guns to make sure his son never got anywhere he might say it.

You can't have both, old man. You can't erase a man and fear him at the same time.

* * *

At noon we pulled back to the tree line above the spring complex and ate cold rice, and I took out both maps.

Halloway's original—the one I'd promised to carry upstream—was soft as cloth now from handling, its ink lines gone brown. I spread it beside the second document, the survey data we'd found stitched into his jacket lining, and for the hundredth time I traced the correspondence between them. The springs. The gauging stations, drawn in Halloway's hand years before the concrete was poured—he'd surveyed this valley for the consortium in his younger days, before he'd understood what the numbers meant, before he'd spent his last years and his last strength carrying the correction of his own work upstream. The aquifer figures. Three provinces. Forty settlements. Everything downstream of this fence, drinking on sufferance.

"There," Sera said, tapping the map. "The transmitter building. That's your right place."

Below us, past the springs, past the stations, on the knoll at the valley's mouth, stood the relay mast—a lattice tower sixty feet tall, bristling with antennas, wired to a blockhouse at its base. From that tower, every gauge reading in the sealed valley flowed downstream to the consortium's offices. From that tower, if we took it, the current could flow the other way.

Vess finished her rice and stood, brushing off her trousers, and looked down at the tower for a long moment.

"When you do this," she said, "when you broadcast—I need to log my testimony first. Before you go live. Names, dates, orders received. Once it starts, there'll be no orderly record, just noise." She glanced at me sideways. "And I want it on the record that I'm not doing it for you, Nystrom. I'm doing it for the wells."

"Wouldn't dream of arguing," I said.

Sera was already packing. "Then we go tonight," she said. "Quarter-hour shift change at the station means the relay runs a skeleton crew after dark. And Gideon—" she paused, and the afternoon light caught the grey starting in her cropped hair, and for a second she looked exactly like the woman who'd named me an outrageous price at a dock in Kestrel's Landing and taken my money with a liar's smile. "When you speak on that radio tomorrow, you'll be saying your name out loud for the first time in seven years. You sure you remember how?"

I looked down the valley—at the fenced springs throwing back the light, at the bored men guarding them, at the thin criminal trickle that was all the Vane was allowed to be—and I found that my hands were steady.

"I've been practicing," I said.

We started down toward the knoll as the sun dropped behind the western ridge, and somewhere far below us, faint on the wind, I heard a sound I hadn't heard since the middle river: an engine. Not a boat engine. Bigger. Rhythmic. Coming from downstream, climbing toward us through the gorge.

Rotor blades.

"He's coming," Vess said quietly. She'd gone pale. "That's a consortium transport. He doesn't send those for technicians."

I stood in the long grass at the top of the sealed valley, holding a dead man's map, and watched the helicopter rise out of the gorge like something the river had finally decided to give back.

My father was coming to the source.
