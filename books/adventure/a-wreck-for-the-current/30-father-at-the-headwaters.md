## 30. Father at the Headwaters

The helicopter came up the valley at midmorning, and Sera heard it before she saw it—a sound like a giant tearing cloth, bouncing off the canyon walls behind them, filling the whole sealed basin with noise.

They were halfway up the last slope to the springs, Gideon ahead of her on the switchback trail, Vess a few paces behind with her hands loose at her sides in that way she had that meant she could be reaching for a weapon in half a second or offering to help carry a pack. Sera had learned to read the ambiguity and let it be. Three days since the gunboat wreck. Two since Vess had started carrying Halloway's survey data in her own breast pocket, against her own chest, like a woman protecting a wound.

The helicopter swung into view over the eastern rim, black and government-plain, no consortium markings at all, which Sera understood instantly to be its own kind of marking. It circled once over the fenced springs below—the gauging station's tin roof flashing in the sun, the technicians inside presumably watching it the way everyone in the valley was watching it now—and then it came down on the flat bench of gravel where the trail widened, fifty meters below them, rotors hammering the dust into a rolling brown cloud.

Gideon stopped on the trail. He didn't turn around. Sera watched his shoulders do something she had only seen once before, on the night he'd answered the radio—go very still, the way a man goes still when a sound from his childhood comes back to him.

"That's not a seizure party," Vess said quietly. "Too few guards. And he'd have brought the station crew up to do it, not flown them in."

"You know who it is," Sera said. It wasn't a question. Vess had worked for the consortium long enough to know what an unmarked helicopter on a private bench meant.

"I know who pays for fuel like that," Vess said.

The rotors wound down. The dust drifted. A man stepped out from under the blade, ducking, and even at fifty meters, even through the settling haze, Sera knew him on sight—not because she'd ever met Aldous Nystrom, but because she'd spent three weeks watching his son move through the world like a man trying to take up less space than he occupied. The father took up all of it. He was broad where Gideon was lean, silver where Gideon was dark, and he stood on the gravel with his boots planted like the valley belonged to him, which, Sera supposed, in every way that mattered on paper, it did.

Two guards stayed by the aircraft. Aldous came up the trail alone.

"Go down to the station," Vess said. "Both of you. I'll—"

"No," Sera said. She heard her own voice come out flat and final and didn't soften it. "I've watched him run from this man for three weeks. I'm not watching him meet him alone."

Gideon hadn't moved. Now he did—came down the switchbacks slow, each step placed like the scree might betray him, and Sera followed, and Vess, after a moment, followed her. Aldous Nystrom watched all three of them descend and did not interrupt, did not hurry them, and that patience frightened Sera more than any show of force would have. Men who rushed you wanted something small. Men who waited wanted everything.

* * *

Up close, the resemblance was a physical blow.

Sera had known it intellectually—the poster at the trading post, the reward for "the Nystrom boy," the jaw and the brow and the way both men stood with their weight slightly back, watching before committing. But standing ten feet away she could see the thing underneath the resemblance, the thing that made her chest ache: Gideon had spent his whole life, she realized, editing himself away from this face. The lean frame, the dark beard, the stoop he fell into when tired—all of it was a sentence written against his father, a lifelong argument conducted in flesh.

Aldous looked at his son the way Sera had seen men look at rivers they'd dammed—possessive, satisfied, and faintly bewildered that the water kept moving anyway.

"Gideon." Just the name. He said it like a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying a long way.

"You're trespassing," Gideon said. "This valley belongs to three provinces' worth of people who don't know it yet."

"It belongs to whoever can hold it," Aldous said, "until someone changes that. Which is why I'm here." His eyes went to Sera, then Vess, cataloguing them without rudeness, the way a surveyor reads terrain. "Miss Okonkwo. The pilot who ran the Grinder with a passenger manifest that didn't exist. And Captain Vess, whose gunboat I am told is now a reef. I'd ask what my son is paying you, but I suspect I already know the answer."

"You don't," Sera said. "Nobody could afford it."

Something flickered across Aldous's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would have gone if it had been built in. "May we speak privately? My son and I. Five minutes. You have my word no one touches you, the data, or the transmitter. I give it in front of a witness who logs testimony for a living."

Vess said, "That's true. He does keep his word about process. It's everything else—" She stopped herself.

"Captain," Aldous said, with something almost like warmth, "I've read your file. We'll speak later, you and I, if you're willing. There are things about your village's water filings I think you should see, whichever way this morning ends."

Sera felt Gideon tense beside her. She put her hand on his forearm—not holding him back, just telling him where she was—and he looked at her, and she said, "Five minutes. I'll be counting."

"I know," he said. And then, quieter, in the voice he used for rope-work and confessions: "Stay where I can see you."

* * *

They walked up the trail together, father and son, and Sera stood at the bend with Vess and watched the two silhouettes shrink against the white scree below the springs, and she hated every step of it and understood every step of it. Some doors a man has to walk through himself. Her job was to make sure the door didn't close behind him.

"What's he doing?" Vess asked. "Really. You've been on the river with him longer than anyone."

Sera thought about it. "He's not arresting us, and he's not buying us. I've watched consortium men try both. This is the third thing. This is—" She searched for it. "This is a man who's about to offer his son the family business."

"That's worse," Vess said.

"It's worse," Sera agreed. "Because you can fight money and you can fight guns. It's very hard to fight a father."

* * *

She couldn't hear them. The wind came down the valley off the ice fields and took their voices, and all she had was the language of bodies, which she'd spent her life reading on the water—current, load, trim, the way a boat tells you the truth before the crew does.

Aldous talked first, and for a long time. Gideon stood with his arms at his sides and let him. Twice Aldous gestured at the valley below—the fences, the gauging stations, the whole privatized watershed laid out beneath them like a model—and Sera could imagine the speech because she'd heard its cousins all her life in port offices: *the region needs stability, the aquifers need management, better us than chaos.* The architecture of every monopoly ever built, delivered as a gift.

Then Gideon said something short. Aldous's posture changed—the surveyor's calm cracking along a fault line Sera couldn't see—and he stepped closer, and for a moment the two men stood nearly chest to chest on the narrow trail, the same height after all, Sera saw, or near enough that it didn't matter, and the resemblance that had been a blow at fifty meters was unbearable at two.

Aldous reached out—Sera saw it clearly, saw his hand rise toward his son's shoulder—and Gideon stepped back.

The hand hung in the air a moment. Then Aldous lowered it, and his shoulders came down, and when he turned and started back along the trail toward them, he moved like a man who had lost an argument he'd been having for twenty years and had only just located the other party.

* * *

He stopped three meters from Sera and Vess. Up close his face was older than the posters, lined around the eyes in a way no portrait had permitted, and Sera understood with a small cold shock that the man was tired. Not performing tired. Actually, structurally, at the bottom of his reserves tired, in the way of a man who has been holding a door shut against a flood for decades and has just heard the hinges go.

"You'll be transmitting within the hour," he said. Not a question. Vess's hand had drifted near her jacket and he noted it without alarm. "I won't stop you. I want you to understand that. I could have brought forty men. I brought two, and told them to stay with the aircraft."

"Why?" Sera said.

"Because my son doesn't believe me when I send other people." He said it simply, and it landed on the gravel between them like something dropped from a height. "I asked him to come home. Not to surrender—to come home. The house on the Corren side is still standing. His mother's things are still in it. I told him the consortium can survive this broadcast. It can't survive what happens after, and neither, I told him, can he—not out here, not as a fugitive, not as a name on a blacklist that I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "That I built. Yes. He knows. He's known since the radio. I won't insult either of you by pretending otherwise."

"You blacklisted your own son," Vess said, "to cover a blast that killed nine people."

"I buried a decision," Aldous said, "that I believed would keep two hundred thousand people drinking water they'd otherwise lose to three companies worse than mine. You've read his data. You know what the other bids on this valley looked like. I was not the worst thing that could have happened to this river. I have told myself that every day for twenty years, and every day it has become less true, and I am standing here anyway, telling you both, because my son is about to do the one thing I never had the courage to do, which is to find out." He looked back up the trail, at the small figure still standing where Gideon stood, against the snowfield. "I offered him everything. The pardon, the partnership, the pass. I offered to reopen Corren Pass under his name, his findings, his terms. He said—" Aldous's voice did something Sera had not expected, something small and cracked. "He said he'd spent his whole life learning to live without a name, and he wasn't going to take one back as a gift from the man who took it."

Sera said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a knife, and she found—surprisingly—that she didn't want to use one. Not here. Not like this.

"He asked me one question," Aldous went on. "He asked if Halloway had ever written to me. Twenty-two years, the man surveyed my watershed, and I never once answered his letters." He looked at Sera directly then, and his eyes were his son's eyes, and it was the worst thing about him. "You're going to broadcast. When you do—when the provinces come up this valley, when the filings are opened, when everything I built is taken apart rivet by rivet in public—there will be a moment, Miss Okonkwo, when they need someone to run the transition. Someone who knows where every gauge is, every easement, every village intake I quietly never charged. My son will refuse it. He'll refuse it because it comes from me. So I'm asking you. Not as consortium. As a father who has run out of doors to knock on."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. The rotors ticked as they cooled. Below them, the springs ran silver through the fences, indifferent, the way water is, the way it always wins eventually.

"I'll tell him what you said," she said. "Every word. And then he'll decide, and it won't be because of you, and it won't be for you. If he takes anything from this valley, it'll be because the river gave it to him. That's the only inheritance he's interested in. I'd stop trying to leave him a bigger one."

Aldous Nystrom held her gaze, and then, slowly, he nodded—once, like a man accepting terms on a contract he'd have drafted differently—and walked back to his helicopter, and did not look up the trail again.

* * *

Gideon came down while the rotors were spinning up. He stood beside Sera in the rotor wash, dust and grit stinging their faces, and watched the black machine lift, swing, and shrink over the eastern rim until the tearing-cloth sound faded and the valley went quiet enough to hear the springs.

"He offered you the transition," Sera said.

"He offered me everything." Gideon's voice was scraped raw, level underneath. "He offered me my mother's ring, Sera. He had it in his pocket. He's carried it up a mountain in a helicopter to put it in my hand." He laughed, once, with nothing in it. "Twenty years ago he couldn't put my name on a rescue report. Today he can't put a ring in my hand. We're both of us exactly as good at this as we've always been."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the ring stays with the house. And the house stays with the past." He turned to look up at the springs, at the gauging station where the technicians were, right now, deciding whether to obey the order that might or might not come, and Vess was already climbing toward them with the survey data against her chest. "Come on. The dry season's closing and we've got a transmitter to find and a name to say out loud."

He started up the trail. Sera followed, and at the bend she glanced back once at the empty gravel bench, at the place where a father had landed with everything and left with nothing, and then she looked up at Gideon's back, climbing toward the water, and put the image away where she kept the things the river had taught her: that nothing holds forever, and that some floods are a mercy.
