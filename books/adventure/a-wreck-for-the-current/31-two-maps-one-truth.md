## 31. Two Maps, One Truth

The gauging station smelled of dust and diesel and old paper. That last one surprised me most. I had expected the machinery of the consortium's secret to be cold—terminals, sealed ledgers, the sterile hum of a lie maintained by men in clean boots. Instead the back room of Station One smelled like my father's study had when I was twelve: lamp oil, pipe smoke soaked into wood decades ago, and filing cabinets full of documents nobody expected anyone to read.

Vess stood at the door with her sidearm holstered and her arms crossed, which I had learned by now was her way of standing guard for someone rather than against them. Sera was on the roof with the station's senior technician, a hollow-cheeked man named Ibarra who had spent nineteen years recording water levels he'd been ordered never to question. The transmitter hummed overhead through the floorboards, already warming for what we would do with it.

But that came next. First there was this—the table, the two sets of papers, and the long slow work of seeing.

I laid Halloway's survey out on the left. It was the worse document: hand-ruled columns on butcher's paper, figures corrected in pencil, margins crowded with his cramped annotations in three different inks as the work had stretched across years. He had carried it stitched into his jacket lining against his ribs until the paper wore soft as cloth. The springs of the Vane headwaters, measured season by season. Flow rates. Aquifer recharge lines drawn in red, branching south and east like the roots of something enormous. Three provinces drank from what came out of this valley, and Halloway had proven it one careful number at a time.

On the right I spread the consortium's filings—copies Vess had pulled from the station's own records without being asked, which told me something about how far she had already traveled from the woman who'd hunted us down the narrows. These were the beautiful documents. Typeset, stamped, certified by hydrologists whose names meant nothing and whose signatures were immaculate. Filed with the provincial water authority eleven years ago. *Survey of the Upper Vane Basin: Findings Negative.* No significant aquifer connection. No downstream dependency. The source waters classified as marginal, seasonal, commercially negligible—a valley worth fencing only because the consortium fenced everything it touched.

Two maps of the same ground. One true. One bought.

I had come up the river expecting to find greed. What I found on that table, as the afternoon light moved across the papers, was architecture.

It started with the dates. Halloway's earliest measurements predated the consortium's filing by four years—which meant the consortium's surveyors had been working in this valley while an old prospector with a hand level and a rain gauge was quietly proving them wrong. And their filing hadn't just ignored his findings. It had been built to contradict them. Every figure in the official survey sat precisely where it needed to sit to make the aquifer vanish. Not sloppy. Not lazy. Engineered. Someone had taken the truth and inverted it, column by column, the way you'd build a dam—not to hold water but to hold a fact underwater.

I turned to the appendix of the official filing, the section listing prior claims and encumbrances on the basin. There it was, item fourteen, in the flat language of land registries: *Corren Pass development corridor, lapsed.*

I sat very still.

I had spent nine years telling myself the avalanche was weather. Nine years of waking at three in the morning with the sound of it in my ears—the crack, not loud, more like a door closing somewhere above us—and nine years of being told by every inquiry, every lawyer, every bitter survivor who spat at me in ferry houses, that a guide of my experience should have read the slope, should have known, should have waited. Nine years of half-believing it myself, because the alternative was believing something worse.

But I had seen the blast scar. High on the shoulder of the pass, two hundred meters above the fracture line, a black crescent in the snow where no crew of ours had set a charge. I had said so at the first inquiry. I had been shown a permit—signed, stamped, legitimate—for seismic testing along the corridor, dated three days before our crossing. Testing that was supposed to happen after we passed. Testing that, per the permit, used charges too small to move anything.

Halloway's margin notes answered the permit.

He'd written about Corren Pass twice. The first mention was dry, professional: observed detonations on the upper shoulder, estimated yield inconsistent with filed permits by a factor of thirty. The second mention, dated six weeks later, was different. His handwriting had changed—smaller, pressed harder into the paper. *Spoke to a driller out of Meridian camp. Says the corridor survey was a fiction. They wanted the pass road built before the water filings went public, because the road is worthless if the basin is declared protected. The blast wasn't testing. It was the road. And the guide's boy who saw the scar—they've made him the reason there was no inquiry.*

*The guide's boy.*

My hands were not steady. I put them flat on the table anyway and kept reading.

Because here was the shape of it now, whole, the way you finally see a mountain's full form only from a distance you've suffered to reach. Aldous Nystrom needed the Corren Pass road. The road required a stable slope or the appearance of one. The blast saved months and buried the instability it created under nine climbers and a blame assigned in advance to the one witness young enough, grieving enough, and—this was the part that took the air out of me—*disposable enough* to carry it. My father had not merely failed to protect me. He had selected me. A son's disgrace was the cheapest seal available for the whole structure, and once it held, everything else could be stacked on top of it: the falsified water survey, the closed valley, the bribed registries, the hunters on the banks, the villages kept off the maps so no one would wonder why they clustered so faithfully around water the filings called nonexistent.

Corren Pass wasn't beside the cover-up. It was its foundation stone. Prove the pass was murder and the water filings became a fraud you could prosecute. Leave the pass alone and the filings were just aggressive business. That was why my name had stayed blacklisted across two countries for nine years—not punishment. *Maintenance.* I was load-bearing.

Sera came down the ladder while I was still staring at item fourteen. She had grease on her knuckles from the transmitter housing and she read the table the way she read water—from the edges inward, fast, finding the current before trusting the surface.

"That's your pass," she said. Not a question. She'd heard enough of my silences over a thousand miles of river to know its geography.

"Halloway measured the yield of the charges." My voice came out level, which cost me. "Thirty times the permitted size. He wrote it down six weeks after they buried nine people under it and blamed me."

She pulled out the crate opposite and sat, forearms on her knees. "And the filings?"

"Built backwards. Look." I turned the official survey toward her and put Halloway's red aquifer lines beside it. "Every measurement here is chosen so the connection disappears. You don't get this from bad science. Bad science scatters. This converges. Somebody handed the surveyors the conclusion and paid them to walk it backward to the data."

"Your father signed off."

"My father *commissioned* it." I tapped the letterhead. "Filed under his personal office, not the consortium's engineering division. He kept this one close. Eleven years, and the only copies outside the registry are here, at the source, where he figured no one would ever be foolish enough to come read them."

Vess shifted at the door. "There's more," she said. "If you're going to do this, you should see all of it."

She crossed the room and opened the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet—the one with a lock whose key hung openly on a hook beside it, security as ritual rather than intent—and brought out a thin folder that had clearly been moved often. Its edges were soft. Inside were correspondence copies, and I watched Vess's face as she laid them out, watched her jaw tighten at whatever it cost her to handle them.

They were memos. Internal. Twelve years of them, and they read like the minutes of a slow crime.

A directive on "registry management" listing the names of guides and pilots whose certifications should lapse—mine at the top, Sera's mentor among them, a dozen others scattered across the watershed like nails pulled from a boat. A payment schedule for the editors of the provincial gazetteer, itemized by map sheet, each entry corresponding to a village that would simply stop appearing. A memo marked *personal attention* concerning a prospector named T. Halloway, described as a persistent nuisance, with instructions that he be denied supply contracts, credit, and eventually—there was a chilling gentleness in the phrasing—*encouraged toward other districts.*

"He wouldn't go," Vess said quietly. "Nineteen years I worked for these people. We chased smugglers, claim-jumpers, men with guns. Nobody ever told me what the fences were for. I thought it was ore. Everybody thinks it's ore." She looked at the memos the way you look at a face you realize you've misread for years. "It was always the water."

"And Corren Pass," I said. "Is it in there?"

She hesitated, then drew out the last page. It was brief. A confirmation of expenditure, authorized by A. Nystrom, for "slope modification services, Corren corridor," dated nine days before the avalanche. Below it, in a clerk's hand: *witness disposition handled per your instruction.*

Witness disposition. Handled. Per your instruction.

I had imagined this moment for nine years without knowing it—inquiry rooms, midnight arguments with lawyers, the fantasy of some document falling into my lap that would make the world say *oh.* I had imagined vindication feeling like warmth. It didn't. It felt like standing on a summit you'd bled toward and finding the view was of everything else that had fallen with you. Nine dead climbers. A prospector hounded to death in a border town. Villages erased from paper so their thirst could be owned. A dozen careers broken to keep the story smooth. All of it resting on a boy who loved mountains because his father had taught him to, ruined on a slope his father had blown open, so that the ruin itself would plug the hole in the story.

Understanding completed itself, and it was not relief. It was weight—but weight I could finally carry correctly, because now I knew where its center was.

Sera was watching me. "Say it," she said. "Out loud. All of it. Before you broadcast it, you should hear how it sounds in your own voice."

So I did. I stood at that table between the butcher's paper and the beautiful filings and I said it plainly, the way I'd testify if I ever got the chance: that the Upper Vane feeds the aquifers of three provinces; that the consortium knew it and filed proof of the opposite; that the falsification required the closure of the valley, the erasure of its people, the ruin of anyone who measured honestly; and that it began with Corren Pass, where Aldous Nystrom destroyed a slope, sacrificed nine lives, and spent his own son's name to buy silence—because a disgraced witness asks fewer questions than a dead one, and draws less attention.

When I finished, the room was quiet except for the transmitter's hum overhead, patient as a held breath.

Vess spoke first. "The technicians will need to see it themselves. They'll want to check the registry numbers against the station logs—Ibarra's kept unofficial readings for two decades. They'll match." She almost smiled. "Aldous Nystrom's great mistake was hiring honest people to run his gauging stations. Honest people write things down."

"And the filings' certification signatures?" Sera asked.

"Three of the hydrologists are dead. One retired to the coast. One—" Vess paused. "One still works for the consortium. Deputy director of compliance now. He'll fold the moment this goes out. Men who sign lies for money keep a list of everyone else who did, purely for self-defense. He's been waiting eleven years for someone to give him permission."

Outside, the light was going amber behind the western fence line, and somewhere beyond it the springs ran on, indifferent, doing the enormous quiet work they had done forever while men argued over owning them. I gathered the papers into two stacks—truth left, lie right—and found my hands had gone steady again. That was the strange gift of it. For nine years my name had been a wound I dressed daily in aliases and lowered eyes. Now it was evidence. Evidence doesn't hide. Evidence stands up.

Sera touched the edge of Halloway's survey, gentle, the way she'd touched the dying man himself on the dock at Kestrel's Landing. "He carried this against his ribs for twenty years," she said. "Longer than you carried yours."

"Then we're even," I said. "Time to set them both down."

Above us, the transmitter finished warming. Down the ladder came Ibarra, wiping his hands, and behind him the two junior technicians, drawn by the smell of old paper and the gravity of whatever was happening in a room none of them had been permitted to enter with questions. Ibarra looked at the table, at the two stacks, at me—at my face, which he must have studied for a resemblance I couldn't see—and he nodded slowly, like a man confirming a reading he'd suspected for years.

"The tower's got range clear to the delta," he said. "But once you press that switch, there's no taking it back. Not for any of us." He glanced at the window, toward the east, where the evening sky was empty of helicopters—for now. "You should know he's coming. The relay picked up flight traffic out of the capital an hour ago. Corporate tail number. He'll be here by midmorning."

Sera met my eyes. "Then we broadcast tonight."

I looked down at the two maps one last time—the soft-worn truth and the immaculate lie—and understood that everything in my life had narrowed to the space between them.

"Yes," I said. "Tonight. Under my real name."
