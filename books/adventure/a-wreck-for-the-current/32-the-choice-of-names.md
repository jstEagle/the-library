## 32. The Choice of Names

The helicopter's rotors died to a ticking silence, and the valley came back the way it always did up here—water first. The sound of it, everywhere at once, seeping and spilling and running through the fenced channels like blood through a body that belonged to someone else.

Aldous Nystrom stood ten paces from me on the gravel flat below the gauging station, and for a long moment neither of us moved. His guards had hung back by the aircraft, close enough to matter, far enough to pretend they didn't. Vess stood off to my left with her arms crossed, watching everything. Sera was still up at the station with the transmitter, and I was glad of it. This part I wanted to do alone.

Ten paces and thirty years.

He'd aged the way stone weathers—not weaker, just more shaped by what had run over him. Silver at the temples now, deeper lines around the mouth, but the eyes were the same eyes from the kitchen table in Corren, the ones that could make you feel like an unpaid debt. He wore field clothes instead of a suit, which was its own kind of message: *I came to you as a father, not a chairman.* Both were lies. Both had always been lies.

"Gideon." He said my name carefully, like a man stepping onto ice he'd been told would hold.

"You said it on the radio," I told him. "No point being careful with it now."

"I wasn't sure you'd hear it as your name anymore."

"It's the only one I've got. You made sure of that."

Something flickered across his face—pain, or the memory of pain, filed away so long it had gone smooth. He glanced at the springs behind me, the chain-link glittering with morning frost, the gauging stations humming their quiet theft. "You've seen it, then."

"I've seen it."

"Then you understand why it can't be in the papers." He took a step closer, hands open, palms out—a negotiator's posture, a father's posture, and I hated how well I knew the difference only because he'd taught it to me. "Three provinces drink from this valley whether they know it or not. If the filings are wrong—if people learn the consortium has been managing their water without mandate—the courts freeze the assets. The pumps stop. The treaties lapse. Do you know what happens to a city of two hundred thousand when the aquifer management stops for even one season?"

"I know what happened to nine families on Corren Pass when management stopped for one afternoon."

The words landed. I watched them land. He didn't flinch—that was the discipline in him—but his jaw worked once, like a man swallowing something that hadn't gone down in twenty years.

"That was a tragedy," he said quietly.

"That was a Tuesday." My voice came out flat and cold and I let it. "An unsanctioned blast on a slope your own engineers flagged twice. A crew sent up anyway because the quarterly numbers mattered more than the snowpack. And when it came down, you didn't call the coroner. You called a lawyer. And then you called me into your office and explained, very patiently, that if I told anyone what I'd seen on that ridge, the company would survive and I wouldn't. That I'd be a disgraced guide who panicked. That nine dead men needed someone to blame, and better a Nystrom ruined than a Nystrom indicted."

"You were nineteen."

"I was the cheapest name you had."

* * *

For a while there was only the water. It never stopped up here; that was the whole obscene truth of the place. Somewhere downstream, towns were rationing. Here, the springs ran over their concrete lips and into flumes and meters and pipes with consortium stencils on them, and the surplus—God, the sheer waste of it—pooled in holding basins and evaporated in the dry-season sun because selling scarcity was worth more than the water itself.

Aldous walked to the fence and put his hand on it. Not leaning. Resting, the way a man rests a hand on a horse he's about to sell.

"I'm not here to threaten you," he said.

"No. You did that on the radio. This is the part where you buy."

"Yes." He turned back to face me, and I'll give him this—he didn't dress it up. "Here is what walking away looks like. Full pardon, sealed records. Your guiding certification reinstated in both countries, backdated, clean. A partnership stake in the northern operations—real authority, not ornament. Enough money that you never take another low-paying trail job under another man's name as long as you live." He paused, and then he reached into his coat, and my whole body went tight before I saw what it was.

The ring.

My mother's ring. Thin gold band, small opal worn nearly smooth, the setting she'd bent herself fixing rather than pay someone because there was never quite enough, not even in the years when there was. She'd been dead eleven years, and I had assumed it was sold, or locked in a vault, or lost in the general erosion of everything she'd touched. He'd kept it. All this time, he'd kept it, and I couldn't decide if that made him more human or less.

"She asked me to give it to you," Aldous said. "Before the end. I told her I would. Then things—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm giving it to you now. That counts for something. Maybe not enough. But something."

I looked at the ring sitting in his palm, catching the flat white light off the glacier, and I felt the pull of it all the way down in my chest. That was the thing nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't come dressed as evil. It comes dressed as your mother's ring. As your name spoken kindly. As a door opening onto a life where you stop flinching every time a clerk asks who you are.

"You want me to walk away," I said. "From what, exactly? Name it plainly."

"The map. The survey data. The broadcast you're planning." He nodded toward the station where Sera was working. "Halloway's evidence goes in a drawer, or a fire, and in exchange you get a life. Not an apology—I know better than to offer you that, you'd throw it back. A life. Which is more than you've had since Corren."

"And the villages upstream. The ones you threatened to quarantine."

His silence answered me.

"And Halloway. The man who crawled off a barge dying because your checkpoints sealed the river so tight he couldn't get his own survey out any other way. What does he get?"

"Halloway," Aldous said, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse than anger, "was an old man chasing a grievance into country that killed him. Nobody made him come up here."

"He made it because you made it illegal to tell the truth about water. He carried forty pounds of survey instruments through the Grinder at seventy years old because the legal route was bought and closed. Don't you dare stand on this gravel and tell me he chose his death. You built the road he died on."

"You don't know what's at stake—"

"I know exactly what's at stake. That's the difference between us. You've spent thirty years making sure no one else does."

* * *

He came closer then. Close enough that I could see the tiredness under the command, the ordinary human exhaustion of a man who had been holding a lid on a boiling thing for decades. Up close he smelled of coffee and helicopter fuel and, faintly, of the same soap he'd used in Corren, and that detail nearly undid me more than the ring had.

"I'm asking you," he said. "Not ordering. Not negotiating. Asking. As your father." His voice dropped. "Come home, Gideon. Whatever that means now. Let this valley stay quiet. Let me fix it slowly, the way it has to be fixed, without burning down the only structure keeping three provinces supplied. Give me time. Give me—"

"Give you what you gave me?" I heard my own voice crack and didn't care. "Time? Silence? You had my whole life. You had my name, and you spent it like small change to cover a blast report. Every job I couldn't get. Every border that closed to me. Every night in Kestrel's Landing signing someone else's name because *Nystrom* meant avalanche and cowardice and nine coffins—and you knew. You knew the whole time, and you let me carry it, because carrying it was cheaper than confessing it."

"I was protecting—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't say you were protecting me. You weren't protecting me. You were protecting the filing date on the water rights. I was a line item. So was Halloway. So were those nine men on the Pass. Everything you love, you hold the way you hold this valley—fenced, metered, and drained."

The guards by the helicopter had shifted. Vess had shifted too, angling herself, and I raised a hand slightly—*easy*—without taking my eyes off him.

Aldous looked at the ring in his palm for a long moment. When he spoke again, the negotiator was back, but quieter, and underneath it, for the first time in my life, I heard something that might have been fear.

"If you broadcast that data, I can't protect you from what follows. Neither of us knows what follows. Courts, collapse, panic—it won't be clean, Gideon. Truths like this don't set anyone free. They just change who's drowning."

"Then we teach people to swim," I said. "That's the job. That was always the job—you just thought it meant deciding who goes under."

I reached out and took the ring.

He exhaled—relief, maybe, thinking I'd taken the whole offer—and I closed my fingers around the opal and stepped back.

"I'll take this," I said. "Because it was hers, and she wanted me to have it, and you don't get to keep her anymore. You lost the right to keep things the day you started keeping secrets instead." I put the ring in my breast pocket, over my heart, where Halloway's jacket had carried his second map. "But the answer is no. No pardon. No partnership. No drawer, no fire. The data goes out today, on the station transmitter, under my full name—which, I'm told, is also your name. You should think about how that sounds, coming out of a public frequency. *Gideon Nystrom, son of Aldous Nystrom, reporting from the source of the Vane.*"

"You'd burn yourself with me."

"I'd finally stop smoldering alone."

* * *

He stared at me, and I watched the last negotiation die in his face—not the offer, but the assumption beneath it, the one he'd carried for thirty years: that everyone had a price, and his son's was simply waiting to be found. I watched him understand that mine had been paid already, long ago, on a gravel bar in Kestrel's Landing, by a dying man who'd asked for nothing except that the truth go upstream.

"You're going to do it regardless," Aldous said.

"I promised a corpse. You should try it sometime. Promising corpses—they hold you to it better than the living do."

"They'll come for you. Not my men. The board. The shareholders. People with more to lose than I have."

"Let them come up the river," I said. "It's been a good teacher."

He stood there a moment longer, and I want to record honestly what I saw, because I've had years to decide and I still don't know. I saw hatred. I saw grief. And underneath both, briefly, terribly, I saw envy—the look of a man staring at a freedom he'd sold before I was born and could never buy back at any price.

He turned and walked toward the helicopter. At the cabin door he stopped, half-turned, and said, without looking at me:

"Your mother used to say you were the stubborn one. She said it like it was a virtue."

"It is," I said. "You'd know, if you'd ever tried being it."

The rotors spun up. The wash flattened the grass along the fence line and scattered frost off the chain-link like the valley shaking itself awake. The helicopter lifted, tilted, and shrank southward toward the world of filings and fences, and I stood on the gravel with my mother's ring against my chest and my father's shadow lifting off me like weather clearing.

Vess came and stood beside me. She didn't say anything for a while. Then: "The technicians are asking if the transmitter schedule holds."

"It holds."

"There'll be consequences."

"There always were," I said. "Now they'll at least be mine."

I started up the trail toward the station, toward Sera and the microphone and the moment I'd been walking away from for twenty years. Behind me the springs ran on, unmetered for the first hour of their new life, and ahead of us the whole river waited to hear a name it had never once been given:

the truth, spoken plain, signed in full.
