## 33. Broadcast from the Source

The gauging station sat on a shelf of granite above the springs, a squat concrete box with a corrugated roof and an antenna mast guyed against the wind. Consortium gray. Consortium bolts. Everything in this valley wore the consortium's stamp, right down to the padlock on the transmitter room door, which Vess opened with a key from her own belt.

"You could have cut it," I said.

"I could have shot you both three days ago, too." She held the door. "Some things you do properly or not at all."

Inside smelled of dust and machine oil and cold coffee gone stale in a mug some technician had abandoned mid-shift. A bank of dials. A reel-to-reel recorder. The transmitter itself, a heavy black cabinet with a single red lamp above its main switch, dark now, waiting. Sera ran her hand along the frequency dial like she was greeting a horse.

"Shortwave. Good reach." She glanced at me. "You know how many settlements hang off this band?"

"No."

"Every one. Every landing, every village, every floating camp between here and the delta. When the weather's clear at night, they pick up stations two provinces over. They'll hear us." She said it flatly, but her fingers had gone still on the dial. "They'll also hear anyone who wants to answer. Including Aldous Nystrom."

"He already had his turn."

"On his equipment, on his terms." She turned to face me fully. "This is yours. So before I throw that switch, I need to hear it from you. Not the plan. You."

I looked at the microphone sitting in its cradle. It was nothing—a dented steel cylinder on a coiled cord—but my mouth went dry looking at it. Twenty years of practicing silence, and all of it came down to speaking into a dented cylinder while a red lamp burned.

"Gideon Nystrom," I said. Out loud. To another person, meaning it. "That's who's going to sign it."

Sera nodded once, like a pilot confirming a heading. Then she got to work, and I understood for the first time how much of her life had been preparation for exactly this kind of moment—hands moving without wasted motion, checking power draw, testing the antenna ground, threading Halloway's survey pages onto the document camera stand we'd rigged from a lamp arm and a board. Vess posted herself at the window with binoculars, watching the compound below, where the technicians were doing something very quiet and very deliberate: nothing. No sabotage. No alarm. They'd heard the broadcast schedule read aloud by their own lead hunter, and they were choosing, minute by minute, where they stood.

* * *

We tested at half past four. Sera keyed the mic and called a carrier wave out over the valley, and somewhere downstream a hundred radios hissed to life on kitchen tables and pilothouses and porch rails. She killed the carrier after ten seconds.

"That's them listening," she said. "Word travels faster than water. By full dark every settlement on the Vane will know something's coming from the source."

"Then let's not make them wait for dark."

"We wait for the evening propagation window. Trust me—if we push now, we're a whisper. At dusk we're a shout." She checked her watch, then did something I hadn't seen her do before: she took my hand. Her grip was rope-callused and warm. "While we wait. Say it to me. All of it. Start to finish. If you stumble here, you'll stumble there."

So I said it. Standing in a concrete room above the privatized headwaters of the Vane, I told the river country everything I'd told no one: Halloway dying in Gideon's Landing with a map stitched into his jacket. The promise made over a corpse. The weirs, the Grinder, the portage, the village that wasn't on any map. And then Corren Pass—the blast that came from below us, unsanctioned, unscheduled, the mountain shrugging nine climbers into the void because a consortium survey crew wanted their seismic data a season early. My father signing the report that blamed route-finding error. Route-finding error. Me. His son, fed to the inquiry like meat to keep the dogs quiet.

I didn't stumble. My voice went strange in places, thin as wire, but it held, and when I finished Sera didn't comfort me. She just said, "Good. Now say it like that tonight, only shorter. Grief is true but it's slow. Lead with the data. Let the grief be the signature, not the letter."

It was the best editorial advice I'd ever been given, and I'd spent years around writers and worse men.

* * *

Dusk came down the valley like a lid closing. The springs below caught the last light and threw it back copper. Vess reported movement at the compound—one helicopter lifting, circling wide, holding position at the valley mouth like a dog told to stay. Aldous, keeping his distance, keeping his options open. He'd had his chance to plead his case privately; now he would listen to it die in public, or try to drown it out.

At 6:47 by the station clock, Sera threw the main switch.

The red lamp lit. The transmitter drew power with a low hum I felt in my teeth. She adjusted the frequency with two fingers, found the band, and nodded at me.

"Carrier's up. River's yours."

I picked up the microphone. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything.

"This is the gauging station at the source of the Vane River," I said. "My name is Gideon Nystrom. Some of you will know that name. Some of you knew it twenty years ago, before the consortium taught you to forget it. I'm going to tell you why."

Beside me, Sera fed the first page of Halloway's survey onto the document stand and keyed the recorder. I read the numbers out loud—flow rates, aquifer recharge figures, seasonal yield projections—Halloway's cramped handwriting translated into voice, into signal, into every kitchen and wheelhouse downstream. The data that proved the Vane's source fed aquifers three provinces depended on. The data the consortium had classified, fenced, and falsified so they could sell the same water twice—once to the cities as rights, once to the villages as charity.

"For eleven years this valley has been listed as forbidden terrain. Geological hazard, the filings say. Unstable aquifer, the filings say. I am standing in it now. There is no hazard here. There is a harvest. Your water, measured at the spring, priced at the fence, sold back to you downstream. And the man who built this—" I paused, and Sera's eyes came up to mine, steady, giving me nothing but permission, "—is Aldous Nystrom. My father. The same man who buried the truth of Corren Pass under my name. Nine dead. One name sacrificed. Twenty years of silence bought with my exile. I'm done paying for his ledger."

I kept talking. The amnesty offers the loudspeakers had shouted into the gorge—I played the recording Sera had made, let the consortium's own voice testify to what it feared enough to buy. Vess gave her name, rank, and employer, and stated for the record that she had verified the survey data against the failing wells of her own home settlement. The technicians downstairs sent up a signed statement attesting that the falsified flow reports had been filed quarterly for eleven years, under orders none of them had dared question until tonight.

Courage is supposed to feel like fire. It felt like spending. Like drawing down an account I'd hoarded my whole adult life, coin by coin, and knowing there would be no refilling it. Every sentence stripped away another layer of the alias, the alias of the alias, the careful choreography of a man staying invisible—and underneath all of it there was just Gideon Nystrom, standing in the light of a red lamp, audible from the delta to the divide.

When I finished, Sera leaned to the mic and gave the coordinates, the repeat-broadcast schedule, and the landing where testimony would be taken. Then she killed the carrier, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Down in the compound, lights were coming on. On the far ridge, the helicopter's running lamps swung, held, then began a slow descent toward the valley floor.

Vess lowered her binoculars. "He's coming up."

Sera exhaled slowly and put her hand flat on the transmitter cabinet, as if steadying it—or herself. "Then he heard it."

"He heard all of it," Vess said. "And so did everyone else. There's no taking it back now. Not with money. Not with blood."

Outside, the first stars were out over the sealed valley, and the springs ran silver in the dark, unmetered, unowned, saying the only thing water has ever said:

*I'm still here. Come and see what you've done.*

The helicopter touched down below the station, and a lone figure stepped out into its own floodlights, walking toward us through the fences his fortune built.
