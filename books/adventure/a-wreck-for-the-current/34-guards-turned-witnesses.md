## 34. Guards Turned Witnesses

The transmitter's echo died against the canyon walls, and for a long moment nobody in the gauging station moved.

I stood at the console with Halloway's data still cycling through my hands—paper copies now, run off the station's own duplicator, because a broadcast can be denied but paper is stubborn. Sera was at the door with her hand resting on the frame, not blocking it, just claiming it. Vess had taken up a position near the window, arms crossed, watching the yard below the way a hawk watches a field it has already decided to hunt.

And the technicians—four of them, the men and women who kept the consortium's gauges wound and its ledgers clean—stood in the doorway of the instrument room looking at me as if I were a weather event they hadn't been briefed on.

The youngest one, a wiry man with a burn scar on his forearm and a name tag that said OKELLO, spoke first.

"You said Nystrom." His voice was careful, like a man testing ice. "On the air. You gave your full name."

"I did."

"Corren Pass Nystrom?"

"That one."

He looked at the older woman beside him—a heavyset technician with grey in her braid and a consortium pin still fastened to her collar, though her hand kept drifting up to touch it like she was checking whether it was still there. She didn't say anything. Neither did the other two. But something passed among the four of them, some current that had been running under the station's floorboards for years and had just, finally, broken the surface.

"You understand," the grey-braided woman said, "what happens next. There's a protocol. When an unauthorized broadcast originates from a station, the standing order is equipment destruction. We pull the transmitter's core, we wipe the logs, we file a report saying the station experienced a catastrophic failure. I've done it twice." She said it flatly, without apology. "Both times it was to hide low water readings. Both times the downstream settlements were told the aquifer levels were stable when they weren't."

"Your name?" I asked.

"Marguerite Deng. Senior technician, nineteen years." She unfastened the consortium pin, looked at it, and set it on the console with a small, precise click. "And you should know the seizure order is already moving. Aldous Nystrom doesn't negotiate. He transmits, and then he arrives, and then men with rifles do what the transmission said."

"He'll be here by morning," Vess said from the window. "Sooner, if he diverted the helicopter at the last fuel cache. I know his logistics better than he'd like."

"Then we have until morning," Sera said, "to decide what kind of morning it's going to be."

* * *

The order came at dusk, as Vess had predicted it would. The station's receiver crackled to life with a voice I didn't recognize—a consortium operations officer reading from what was obviously a prepared script. *All personnel at Station Nine are instructed to decommission transmitting equipment immediately. Disable the core. Purge all data archives. This is a mandatory directive under Section Eleven of your employment terms. Acknowledge.*

Okello looked at me. Marguerite Deng looked at the receiver. The other two—a quiet pair named Ferro and Ansel, who I learned had been manning the station's remote gauges for six years—looked at each other.

Then Deng reached over and switched the receiver off.

"You're supposed to acknowledge," I said.

"I know what I'm supposed to do." She folded her hands on the console. "I've been acknowledging things for nineteen years. I acknowledged the falsified spring reports in Year Four. I acknowledged the removal of the public access gauges in Year Nine. I acknowledged a directive to reclassify the upper Vane as 'non-potable' so the consortium could sell filtered water to the very villages whose wells it had cut off." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "I have a daughter in Merrow's Bend. Merrow's Bend draws from the lower aquifer. The aquifer your dead prospector's survey says has been dropping four percent annually while the filings say stable." She looked at me. "I read the data while you were printing it. I checked his figures against our own internal logs. They match. They've matched for years. We all knew. We just didn't know we all knew."

That was the sentence, I think. *We just didn't know we all knew.* Every technician in that room had been keeping a private ledger of small wrongs, each one small enough to file away, none of them adding up to anything they could name—until a stranger's voice on their own transmitter read the whole ledger aloud in one broadcast.

Okello sat down heavily on a supply crate. "They'll prosecute us. Section Eleven. Aiding unauthorized transmission. They'll blacklist us the way they blacklisted you."

"They might," I said. "I won't pretend otherwise."

"That's not much of an argument."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. The argument is the data. It's in the archive, it's duplicated, and as of an hour ago it's in the ears of every settlement radio operator on the river who stayed up past midnight. Destroying the equipment now doesn't destroy that. It only destroys your chance to be on the right side of the record."

Ferro, the quiet one, spoke for the first time. He had a gauge-tech's flat, unhurried voice, the voice of a man who read water levels for a living and had stopped being surprised by them. "The core's old anyway. Pulling it would take four hours and probably flood the coolant line. I'm not doing four hours of work to help them lie." He shrugged. "Also, I'd like to be able to look at my hands."

Nobody laughed, but something in the room loosened.

Vess came away from the window. "You should all understand exactly what you're refusing," she said. "I ran hunts for this consortium for eleven years. I've seen what happens to employees who become inconvenient. It isn't prosecution. Prosecution is for people they want to make an example of. Inconvenient people just have accidents—bad weather, bad footing, equipment failures in remote country." She let that sit. "I'm telling you because you deserve to decide with your eyes open. And I'm telling you because I'm about to make myself inconvenient too, and I'd rather do it beside people who chose it."

"Vess," I said. "You don't have to—"

"Shut up, Nystrom." But there was no edge in it. She pulled a battered logbook from her jacket—her own hunt log, the one she'd kept in violation of protocol because a good tracker documents everything—and laid it on the console next to Deng's discarded pin. "Eleven years of movement orders, payment authorizations, and instructions I was ordered to destroy after reading. Including two sabotage operations on civilian river craft, including a directive regarding the Corren Pass survey team that I was too junior to understand at the time and have had eleven years to understand since." She looked at me. "I never knew the boy they blamed was you. I knew there was a boy. Everyone in the field knew the story they fed us. You were a cautionary tale. *This is what happens to guides who cut corners.*" Her jaw worked. "They used your name to train us, Nystrom. To teach us that the official version is the safe version. And I repeated it. To new hires. For years."

"You didn't know."

"Knowing wouldn't have changed what I did. Doing this changes what I did." She opened the logbook to a fresh page. "Deng. You're a certified station recorder. Can you log testimony?"

"I can witness, stamp, and archive. Anything entered here becomes part of the station's official record—which, until someone physically seizes the archive, is legally discoverable in three jurisdictions."

"Then witness this." Vess uncapped her pen, and her hand, which I had never once seen unsteady on a rifle or a rope, shook slightly over the page. She noticed me noticing and gave me a hard look that said *comment and die.* Then she wrote.

She wrote for a long time. The station settled around us—generators humming, the Vane muttering in its channel below, the first stars coming out over the fence line that had no business fencing a spring. Sera made tea on the station's hotplate and handed it around without asking, and people drank it, and the ordinary domesticity of it sat strangely against the fact that four of the six people in the room were committing career-ending, possibly life-ending defiance with a pen and a cup of tea.

When Vess finished, she read it aloud. Her testimony, in her own words: the sabotage orders, the payment trails, the hunters' standing instructions to treat river communities as hostile terrain, and the directive she'd received three days ago—*recover the map by any means; witnesses at the source are to be handled as a containment problem.* She signed it. Then she turned the logbook toward the technicians.

"I need witnesses, not agreement. You don't have to sign if—"

Deng signed first. Then Okello. Then, after a moment, Ferro. Ansel hesitated, pen hovering, and Vess said, "Don't sign it if you can't live under it. A half-hearted witness is worse than none."

Ansel signed.

* * *

Later, near midnight, I found Okello on the station's catwalk, looking down at the fenced springs. The floodlights lit the water up from below, and even now, even knowing everything, it was beautiful—the headwaters coming up out of the dark earth like something alive, which, I suppose, it was.

"You grew up on this river?" I asked.

"Downstream. Kessrin flats. Our well went brackish when I was fourteen. My mother boiled everything for six years until the consortium started selling us 'certified clean' water at a price that took half her wages." He turned a stone over in his fingers, dropped it through the railing, watched it fall. "I took this job because it was steady and because I told myself I'd learn how the system worked from the inside. Then I learned how it worked, and I told myself I'd stay because at least I had a view of the water." He looked at me. "That's the trick of it, isn't it? They don't need to threaten most people. They just need to give you a small good thing and a long staircase of small compromises, and you'll walk down it forever."

I thought of ten years of aliases. Of trail jobs under borrowed names. Of the careful choreography of a man staying invisible, and how every year the staircase had gotten easier to walk.

"Yes," I said. "That's the trick."

"Your broadcast," Okello said. "People downstream heard it. My sister runs a radio relay at Kessrin. She'll have heard it twice by now." He almost smiled. "You have no idea what you started."

I had some idea. What I didn't have was a way to know what was coming down the valley at us in answer.

I got my answer at 3 a.m., when the receiver—which Ferro had turned back on, because he said a station that stops listening isn't a station—picked up traffic on the consortium's command band. Deng monitored it with headphones and relayed in a low voice.

Two helicopters out of the southern depot. A seizure team, twelve men. And a third transmission, encrypted, that Deng couldn't crack—but its routing prefix was one she recognized, and her face went grey when she said it.

"That prefix," she said, "is the executive channel. That's Aldous Nystrom's personal authorization code. He's not sending men to arrest you, Gideon." She pulled off the headphones. "He's coming himself."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old tin, and we spent it preparing—not for a fight, none of us were fools enough to prepare for a fight against twelve rifles—but for a record. Deng duplicated the archive again and sealed copies in the station's emergency cache, the one built for flood survival, rated to withstand anything short of a direct hit. Okello photographed every page of Vess's log. Ferro, with a craftsman's stubbornness, serviced the transmitter until it purred, on the theory that whatever happened next, the machine would be ready to speak.

Sera stood beside me at the window as the first rotor-thump came up the valley, faint and getting less faint.

"Any regrets?" she asked.

I thought about it honestly. "Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow you'll either be a free man with his name back or a prisoner with it. Either way you'll be Gideon Nystrom out loud." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "For what it's worth—I liked the alias. But I like this better."

The helicopter cleared the southern ridge, and behind it, catching the early light, came a second one, and I understood that whatever Aldous Nystrom had come to do, he had not come alone, and he had not come to lose.

Below us, in the yard, Okello raised the station's flag—the consortium's grey-and-blue—because protocol required it, and because, he said, let them see exactly whose colors we're flying when we refuse them.

The rotors grew louder.

The machinery of power had arrived at the head of the Vane. And for the first time in its history, the machinery was refusing to answer.
