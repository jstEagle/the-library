## 35. Aldous Unraveling

The second helicopter did not land.

It circled once over the fenced springs, low enough that the downdraft flattened the gauging station's antenna guy-wires into shivering lines, and then it climbed away south with the unhurried confidence of a thing that has never been told no. A show of force, I thought. Or a man making sure his exit was already arranged before he committed to the ground.

The first one set down in the flat below the springs, where Okello's technicians had once surveyed a pump house that was never built. Four guards stepped out first, rifles slung, faces doing their best to be bored. Then Aldous Nystrom.

I had seen his face on posters and permits and the water bills every settlement downstream paid without question. I had heard his voice through a radio speaker promising my partner reinstatement in exchange for silence. None of it prepared me for how ordinary he looked climbing out of the helicopter—a broad, careful man in a grey field coat, silver at the temples, moving like someone who had spent thirty years walking through rooms that rearranged themselves around him. He paused at the bottom of the skid and looked up at the valley as if checking it against a memory.

Beside me on the transmitter platform, Gideon went very still. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of a man watching weather he'd been predicting all his life finally arrive.

"There he is," Vess said quietly. She stood at the platform rail with her testimony log closed under her arm, and there was something in her voice I couldn't name—pity, maybe, or its colder cousin.

"He came to plead," Gideon said. "Chapter and verse."

"He came with four riflemen."

"He came with four riflemen because he can't imagine arriving anywhere without them." Gideon's hands were steady on the rail. "Watch. He'll send them ahead. He always sends other people ahead."

He was right. Aldous spoke briefly to the senior guard, and the four started up the switchback toward the station, spreading out with drilled ease—one to the fence line, two on the path, one swinging wide toward the spring enclosures. Securing the objective. Treating the headwaters of a river three provinces drank from like a warehouse full of stolen goods.

Okello met them at the yard gate.

We were too far up to hear what passed between them, but we could see it: the guard presenting papers, Okello not taking them, Okello pointing—not at the guards, not at the helicopters, but at the transmitter dish above us, and then downstream, toward everything the signal had reached in the night. His posture never changed. He was a small round man who moved like a man half his size, and he stood in front of four armed professionals the way a lock stands in front of a door.

The guard said something else. Okello shook his head once.

And then, from the helicopter pad, Aldous Nystrom raised his voice, and the valley carried it the way valleys carry everything.

"Seize the equipment!"

It rang off the canyon walls, doubled itself, came back thinner. *Seize the equipment.* The same words, probably, he had used in boardrooms and courtrooms and whatever room it was where a man decided his son's name was cheaper than an investigation. Gideon flinched—not at the volume. At the familiarity.

The four guards looked at each other.

That was the moment. I want to be precise about it, because later, when people asked—and everyone asked—I wanted to describe it exactly: the gap between the order and the obedience, which had been zero for thirty years, suddenly became visible. Became a thing you could stand inside. The senior guard looked back down the hill at Aldous. He looked at Okello. He looked at the transmitter dish, still warm from the broadcast, and somewhere beyond it, invisible, at every settlement radio from here to the delta that had played Halloway's survey data and Gideon Nystrom's voice through the night.

Then he looked at the rifle in his own hands, as if noticing it belonged to him.

"We have no seizure order," he called down. Loudly. So the valley would hear it too.

"You have my authority," Aldous said. "That is the order."

"The consortium's filings list this station as decommissioned, sir." The guard's voice was flat, procedural, merciless in the way only procedure can be. "A decommissioned site falls outside operational jurisdiction. If you want a seizure, you need a magistrate's writ, and the nearest magistrate is in Kestrel's Landing." A pause, exactly long enough. "Where they're holding hearings."

I heard Gideon exhale slowly through his nose. Not quite a laugh. Close kin to one.

* * *

Aldous climbed the switchback alone.

That was the detail that undid me, more than anything the guards did or didn't do. He dismissed them with a gesture—he actually waved them off, back toward the aircraft, the way you wave off a waiter whose timing has embarrassed you—and then he walked up the hill by himself, field coat buttoned, hands loose at his sides, a man ascending to meet his son with nothing left between them but altitude.

Gideon met him at the top of the stairs.

I stayed on the platform. So did Vess. We were close enough to hear most of it—the valley gave us the words whether we wanted them or not—but I watched it mostly as shapes and posture, the way you watch a storm from behind glass.

They stood about two meters apart. Neither offered a hand. For a long moment neither spoke, and the sound of the springs filled the space between them—that constant cold whisper the whole journey had been aimed at, the sound of the thing being fought over.

"You broadcast under your own name," Aldous said at last. I'll give him this: he didn't waste time on weather.

"You heard it, then."

"I heard it." Something moved across his face that I refused, then and ever after, to call tenderness. "You always did have your mother's delivery. She read the news like she was handing you something fragile."

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it landed harder than any shout would have. "Don't do that. Don't put her between us now. You've spent twenty years keeping her out of every conversation we never had."

Aldous took it. I watched him take it, the way a man takes a blow he agrees he deserves, and I understood that whatever he had climbed this hill to attempt, he had already lost it somewhere below the tree line.

"I can fix this," he said. "You don't see it yet, but I can. The filings get amended, quietly. Corren Pass gets reopened as a cold case with new evidence—your evidence, credited to you. The blacklist dissolves. There are people downstream right now calling you a hero, Gideon, and heroes are useful, and I am very good at using things. In five years you could have everything. The name. The range. Your old commission, if you want it. All of it, if you just—" his hand made a small closing motion, gathering air, "—let the structure hold. It holds millions of people. Break it carelessly and the aquifer contracts go to courts for a decade while the wells run dry. Is that what Halloway died for? A decade of litigation?"

"No," Gideon said. "Halloway died for the truth getting out before the dry season ended. Which it has." He turned slightly, and I realized he was including us—Vess and me, the technicians, the whole listening valley—in the conversation deliberately, converting a private negotiation into a public record. "And the structure doesn't hold millions of people, Father. It holds millions of people *down*. That's different. You built a dam and called it a foundation."

"The alternative is chaos."

"The alternative is water belonging to the people who drink it." Gideon's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. "You taught me that, actually. On the mountain, when I was twelve. You said a guide doesn't own the route. He serves it. I believed you. I built my whole life on it. And then Corren Pass happened, and I learned what you really serve."

For the first time, Aldous Nystrom looked older than his years. He looked at his son for a long moment, and whatever he saw there—the map, the promise, the name spoken plainly into a thousand radios—it seemed to settle something in him, the way sediment settles when the current finally stops fighting.

"You were the best of them," he said. Almost inaudibly. "Of all the guides. I knew it when I signed the finding. That's why it had to be you. Anyone else's ruin wouldn't have held."

There it was. The confession, delivered not as confession but as accounting, as though he were reading a ledger aloud. Nine dead on Corren Pass, one name destroyed, and the arithmetic done in a father's hand because the son's name was the only figure strong enough to carry the fraud.

Gideon didn't move. But I saw his jaw work once, and I saw him plant his feet the way he planted them on scree, weight low, ready for anything except retreat.

"I know," he said. "Vess logged your surveyor's blast report last night. Timestamps, charge weights, the unsanctioned permit. It's in the broadcast archive now. Three copies. You can't buy it back, and you can't bury it, because the people who have it drink from this river." He let that sit. "I'm not here to ruin you, Father. I'm here to return your property." He held out Halloway's jacket—the stitched lining empty now, the second map surrendered to the record. "Everything else was never yours to lose."

* * *

What happened next took less than ten minutes, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to explain how ten minutes can dismantle thirty years.

Aldous turned from his son and descended the switchback, and I watched him do the thing powerful men do when power drains out from under them mid-stride: he kept walking at exactly the same pace, as if the ground were still arranging itself around his feet. At the helicopter pad he spoke to the senior guard. We caught fragments on the wind.

"—arrest them, that's an instruction—"

"On what charge, sir?" The guard again, patient as stone. "Broadcasting unlicensed? The station's license is posted in the yard, sir. It's valid. Signed by your own operations director in March." A pause. "Trespass? They were invited by the site supervisor." Another pause, and I swear the man almost smiled. "Sir, I've been standing here listening to the same radio they have. Half my company drinks from the Vane. I'm not arresting anyone today."

Behind him, Okello folded his arms. The other three guards had drifted into a loose line that was not quite a formation and not quite an accident—facing the pad, facing their employer, facing the fact of him.

Aldous looked at his men. Really looked, perhaps for the first time in years, past rank and payroll into the plain fact of four human beings declining to be the instrument. And I watched the understanding arrive in him the way floodwater arrives in a dry channel—all at once, from every direction, undeniable.

His authority hadn't been defeated. It had been revealed as conditional. It had always been conditional—built on silence, purchased with paychecks, mortared together with names like mine and Gideon's and Halloway's, crushed quietly whenever they got in the way. And the condition had failed. The silence was over. The river had carried the truth to every ear that mattered, and no order he could shout would climb back upstream.

"Mr. Nystrom," Okello said, not unkindly, "there's coffee in the station if you want it. We're not barbarians. We just stopped being yours."

Aldous stood very still on the pad. The rotors idled behind him, throwing his shadow long across the gravel, and for a moment he looked like a man deciding whether the machine behind him was an exit or an accusation.

Then he straightened his coat, and some last fragment of the old architecture reassembled itself around his spine, and he climbed aboard without looking at any of us. Without arresting anyone. Without even trying. The door slid shut, and the helicopter lifted, and instead of turning south toward the consortium's territory it banked west, out over the ridge, toward the empty country beyond the maps—as if he could not bear to fly home over the places that had stopped obeying him.

The downdraft flattened the grass, and then the grass stood back up, and the valley was ours.

Nobody cheered. That surprised me at the time. Later I understood: you don't cheer the end of a fever. You just notice, carefully, that you're sweating less.

* * *

Gideon came up the stairs slowly, and for a few steps he looked like a man carrying something heavier than he'd started the day with. Victory, it turns out, weighs something. Especially when it arrives wearing your father's face.

"Well," I said.

"Well," he agreed.

He stood beside me at the rail, looking down at the yard where Okello was already directing the technicians back to work—gauges checked, transmitters tested, the mundane machinery of a watershed resuming its actual job. Vess had opened her log and was writing, pen moving steadily, recording times and names and the exact wording of an order that four men declined. History being filed in real time, in a neat hand, by a woman who'd spent a month hunting us.

"Your father," I said. "In the end. He just—left."

"He did what he's always done." Gideon's eyes stayed on the southern ridge, where the second helicopter had gone. "He calculated. And the calculation came back wrong, so he cut the loss." A breath. "Twenty years ago the loss was me. Today it was everything. He's consistent, at least. I'll give him that much."

"Do you feel it?" I asked. "Being free of him?"

He considered that honestly, the way he considers ice slopes and river currents—testing the load before trusting it.

"Not yet," he said. "Maybe when we're off this mountain. Maybe when the hearings start and I say his name out loud in a room full of strangers and the sky doesn't fall." He glanced at me then, and something in his face eased, the way it does at the end of a hard portage when the boat touches water again. "But I feel lighter. Ask me again downstream."

Downstream. The word did what it always did between us—it opened like a door.

Because that was the thing none of us had said yet, standing in the bright cold morning at the top of the world: the source was won, but the source wasn't the destination. Everything that mattered was below us. The hearings in Kestrel's Landing. Halloway's grave, waiting for its true name. The villages with their failing wells, waiting to learn what the water rights meant for them. The whole long river of consequences, all of it waiting on us to come down and finish what a dying prospector had started on a freight barge deck.

Okello appeared at the top of the stairs, wiping his hands on his coveralls, and he was smiling, but it was a working smile, a smile with a task inside it.

"Begging your pardons," he said, "but you should both come look at the gauge readings. Dry season's peaking, the upper channel's dropping fast—half a meter since yesterday—and if you mean to get that boat of yours back down the gorge, you've got a window of about three days." He tipped his head toward the springs, glittering in the sun like scattered coins. "River's telling you something. River's always telling you something. Question is whether you listen while it's still speaking kindly."

Gideon and I looked at each other.

Three days. Down the whole length of everything we'd fought our way up.

I picked up my pack.
