## 36. Descent

The river had changed its mind about us.

I noticed it the first morning below the source valley, watching the current take the patched hull without argument. For three weeks the Vane had fought us—shoved us against rocks, stolen our fuel, split into channels that lied. Now it ran smooth and brown-green and steady, and when Sera set the tiller she barely touched it. The boat seemed to know where it was going.

"Word travels faster than water," Vess said from the bow. She'd said little since we left the gauging station, but she watched the banks with a different face than she'd worn as a hunter. Less like someone tracking prey. More like someone reading weather.

She was right about the word. We heard it before we saw any proof of it. At the first bend below the fenced springs, a fisherman stood up in his punt and just—looked at us. Not the way bank watchers had looked at us for a month, all calculation and threat. He looked the way people look at a funeral procession they've decided to respect. Then he raised one hand off his knee, palm out, and held it there until we'd passed.

"What was that?" I asked.

Sera shrugged, but her mouth was doing something complicated. "Downriver villages have radios. The broadcast went out on every band Halloway's data would carry. You think news stays in the canyon?"

I thought about what we'd sent downstream two nights ago: aquifer surveys, falsified filings, my own voice saying *my name is Gideon Nystrom* into a transmitter that reached three provinces. I'd spent nine years making sure no radio anywhere could say that name without consequences attached. Now it was floating ahead of us like flotsam, washing up on every landing between here and the sea.

I kept waiting for it to feel like a mistake.

* * *

The first checkpoint opened before we reached it.

We'd run checkpoints twice before—the first with forged permits and my heart in my teeth, the second at night in flood stage with Sera laughing like a woman possessed while searchlights swept the water. So when the middle-river weir came into view around noon, boom chains stretched across the channel, floodlights dark, I did the arithmetic out of habit: current speed, distance to the chain, whether we could beach upstream and portage if they didn't open.

We didn't need to. As we closed to two hundred meters, a siren hooted twice—not the alarm tone but the short courtesy blast—and the boom swung aside on greased cables. A man in consortium gray stood on the walkway above the gate. He wasn't aiming anything. He took off his cap as we passed under him.

Sera glanced back at me. "Did you bribe someone while I was sleeping?"

"I've been asleep next to you for two nights. You snore."

"I do not."

"You do," Vess said, not turning around. "Like a winch under load."

But the question underneath the joke stayed with me through the afternoon. Nobody had opened those gates unasked. Those gates were Aldous Nystrom's gates, paid for with the same money that bought Halloway's silence and my blacklisting and whatever quiet arrangement kept the source valley off every map a settler could buy. Men didn't take their caps off for the people who'd broken their employer's system. They took their caps off for something else—for the end of a thing, maybe, or the start of one.

By evening we reached the trading post where Sera had found the reward poster with my face on it. THE NYSTROM BOY, it had said. FIVE THOUSAND FOR INFORMATION LEADING TO—

The board by the post door was bare. Someone had pulled the staples carefully, all of them, and left the wood clean. In the corner of the frame, in chalk, someone had written a single word: *heard*.

Sera tied off at the dock anyway. Old habits. But the postmaster came out before we'd finished securing lines, an old man with a ledger under his arm, and he looked at me the way you look at a photograph you've studied and then met in person.

"You're the guide," he said. "Corren Pass."

"That's not how I'd introduce myself."

"It's how everyone downriver is introducing you." He set the ledger on the dock rail and opened it to a page dense with his own handwriting. "Radio said nine dead at Corren because a consortium blast went unsanctioned and got blamed on the route-finder. Radio said the route-finder is coming down the Vane with proof." He tapped the page. "I've been writing down everything the broadcasts say. Forty-one years keeping this post's accounts. This is the first entry I've made that isn't about freight."

He turned the ledger so I could see. Dates, frequencies, paraphrased testimony—Halloway's survey numbers copied out in careful columns, my statement about the avalanche transcribed word for word. At the bottom, a line of names. Settlers who'd witnessed the entry, signed as if it were a will.

"Why?" I asked. It came out rougher than I meant it.

"Because paper burns," he said, "but forty signatures don't." He looked at Vess then, taking in the consortium tattoo at her wrist, and something moved across his face—not fear, exactly. Recognition of a category. "You're the hunter."

"I was."

"And now?"

Vess was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence. "Now I'm a witness," she said finally. "It turns out the pay is worse but the work is honest."

The postmaster nodded once, satisfied, and closed the ledger. Then he went aboard our boat without asking and pressed a sack into Sera's hands—bread, dried fish, a jar of honey. When she tried to pay him he stepped back like she'd offered him an insult.

"You carried the truth up," he said. "Carrying bread down is nothing."

* * *

That night we anchored in a backwater I remembered from the trip up—a cut behind a gravel bar where the current pooled and the mosquitoes owned the air. Sera cooked. Vess cleaned the boat's wound, re-caulking the seam the gunboat's wake had torn open, working by lantern light with the patience of someone who'd finally found labor she believed in.

I sat on the gravel and couldn't sit still.

Nine years I'd come down rivers like this one alone or in hired company, moving at night, answering to names that weren't mine. Every landing was a risk. Every face was a registry clerk or a bounty man or worse. I'd built the whole architecture of my life on the assumption that the world downstream wanted me caught.

Now the world downstream was feeding us honey.

"Gideon." Sera sat down beside me, handed me a tin cup of tea she'd made strong enough to strip paint. "You've been chewing on something since the weir. Spit it out."

"The gates opened."

"They did."

"Nobody opens those gates. I spent a month learning exactly how much nobody opens those gates." I turned the cup in my hands. "Either the whole system just collapsed overnight, which doesn't happen, or—" I stopped, because saying the rest aloud gave it weight.

"Or your father let them open," Vess said from the boat, not looking up from her caulking. She'd gotten good at finishing my sentences, which I both appreciated and resented. "Aldous Nystrom is many things, but he isn't careless. If he can't stop you, he'll want to control the terms of your arrival. Open gates make you look legitimate. Closed gates make you look like what you are—which is a man running contraband truth."

"So we should be suspicious of kindness."

"We should be suspicious of *convenient* kindness." She bit a thread clean. "There's a difference. The postmaster's honey was real. The gates might be theater. Keep both thoughts."

Sera leaned her shoulder into mine. "And which thought are you keeping tonight?"

I watched the water slide past the gravel bar, carrying moonlight downstream toward towns full of people who knew my name now and had decided, provisionally, to sign ledgers about it instead of selling me.

"Both," I said. "But mostly I keep thinking about Halloway. He crawled off a barge half-dead to put this map in my hands, and he never got to see a single gate swing open for it." I drank the terrible tea. "I want to get him home. That's the rest of the promise. Not just the map. Him."

Sera was quiet a moment. "His people. Downriver."

"He mentioned a sister once. Near the delta. Ferrow Landing, he said. Said it like a man saying a prayer he'd stopped believing in."

"Then that's our next heading." She said it simply, the way she named channels—no drama, just water and direction. It's one of the reasons I trusted her more than I've ever trusted anyone. Sera didn't make promises bigger than the river required. She just read the current and told you where it went.

* * *

The second day down, the river gave us back the village.

Not ours—the unrecorded one was far upstream, beyond the gorge, beyond reach of anything but memory. But word of it had traveled, the way Vess said it would, and at midday we rounded a bend to find thirty or forty boats gathered at a confluence landing: fishing punts, freight skiffs, families standing in them, all facing upstream. Waiting.

For us. I understood it a heartbeat before Sera killed the engine, and my stomach dropped, because crowds had only ever meant one thing in my life, and it was never good.

An old woman stood in the nearest punt, holding a woven basket. As we drifted in she called out—not to me, oddly, but to Vess.

"You're the one from the station logs. The hunter who testified."

"I am."

"My son works a gauging station on the Sorrel fork. Consortium-run, eleven years. He listened to your testimony on the family band and walked out that same afternoon." The old woman's voice didn't rise; it didn't need to. Every boat was listening. "First time in eleven years he's missed a shift. My daughter-in-law says he laughed about it."

Vess's jaw worked. "I didn't do it for—"

"No," the old woman agreed. "You did it because your own wells were failing. That's what makes it worth believing." She handed the basket across to Sera—fruit, eggs, a cloth bundle that smelled of smoked meat. Then she turned to me, and her eyes were sharp and kind at once, the way eyes get in country where kindness has to be earned twice.

"You're the Nystrom boy."

"I'm Gideon Nystrom," I said. It still cost something to say it. I suspected it always would, and I was starting to suspect that was fine—that a name should cost what it's worth.

"The radio says your father built the fences at the source."

"The radio has it right."

"And you broke them anyway."

"I carried a dead man's map. The breaking was incidental."

She smiled at that, toothless and fierce. "Incidental." She looked around at the assembled boats, at the children perched on gunwales, at the men who'd stopped fishing to be here. "Half this confluence drinks from the Vane. Half of what's left drinks from aquifers your dead man's map proved were fed by the headwaters. We didn't know why the water table dropped for twenty years. We thought it was weather." Her voice hardened. "It was a fence."

Nobody spoke. The river moved under all of us, patient, going where it went.

"So we're here," the old woman finished, "to watch you pass. That's all. Some things you have to see with your own eyes or you don't believe the radio." She raised her hand, palm out, the same gesture as the fisherman, and one by one the boats did the same until the whole confluence was a field of open palms, and we slid through the middle of it like a bride down an aisle, and I had to look at the water because looking at the people was more than I could hold.

Sera's hand found mine on the tiller. She didn't say anything. She didn't need to. Nine years of invisibility, and I'd assumed that when visibility came—if it ever came—it would arrive as a noose. Instead it arrived as open palms, and I didn't have a shelf inside me built to hold that.

* * *

We made the lower gorge on the third day, and there the river remembered its temper.

The narrows where Vess's gunboat had nearly run us down were running high with snowmelt from peaks we'd left behind, and Sera worked the tiller like a surgeon, reading the standing waves, calling the water before it came. Vess handled lines. I ran the bow, calling depth over the engine noise, and for an hour none of us were anything but the job—three people and a wounded boat threading rock, no past and no future, just the next ten meters of river, forever.

Then it opened out, and it was done, and the Vane went wide and slow again, and the tension drained out of the deck like water through scree.

"Checkpoint at Marrow Bend," Vess said, consulting the chart she'd taken from the gunboat's wreck. "Fortified. Garrison of twelve. Or it was." She lowered the chart. "Listen."

We listened. Above the engine, faint but unmistakable, came music—a fiddle, badly played, drifting downriver from around the bend.

The garrison at Marrow Bend was playing music.

We came around the bend slow, hands visible, ready for anything. What we found was a fortified weir with its boom chained open and padlocked back, its floodlights off, its guardhouse door propped wide. Six men in consortium gray sat on the walkway sharing a cookfire with a dozen settlers from the near shore, and a seventh guard was murdering the fiddle while everyone sang worse. A banner had been strung across the gate arch—somebody's bedsheet, letters in tar:

**THE RIVER IS OPEN**

They waved us through. One of the guards raised a bottle to us. A settler woman shouted something about the broadcast, about her brother in the delta, about *they can't stop it now, they can't unfire a bullet*, and then we were past and the music faded behind us and Sera started to laugh—low at first, then helplessly, tiller loose in her grip, tears in her eyes.

"A bedsheet," she managed. "They hung a bedsheet on a quarter-million-credit gate."

"Tar letters," Vess said gravely. "Very official."

I laughed too. I felt it come up from somewhere deep and long-sealed, and it hurt a little, like weight-bearing after a bone knits. Nine years I hadn't laughed on a river. There hadn't been anything to laugh about, or rather, I hadn't permitted myself the equipment. Laughter requires a future. I'd lived without one so long I'd forgotten the sensation of it arriving.

But it arrived. And the river carried it downstream along with everything else.

* * *

We anchored that night within sight of the delta's first lights—a scatter of lamps along the far shore where the Vane began braiding itself into the sea. Tomorrow, Ferrow Landing. Tomorrow, Halloway's sister, and the last delivery of a dead man's cargo.

I couldn't sleep. I climbed out onto the gravel and found Vess already there, sitting with her boots off, her tattooed wrist resting on her knee, watching the lights.

"Can't either?" I asked.

"I'm trying to decide what I go back to," she said. "Consortium's finished—or it's not, and the parts that survive will remember I logged testimony against it. Either way, my old life's a burned bridge." She glanced at me. "You know something about burned bridges."

"I wrote the book."

"What I can't figure," she went on, "is that you seem calm about it. Your name's everywhere. Your father's ruined. You've got nothing waiting downstream except a grave to dig and a sister to console. And you're calm."

I considered the lights across the water. "I spent nine years being nobody, and it was the most expensive thing I ever bought," I said. "Calm isn't the right word. It's more like—I put the cost down. Whatever comes next, at least I'm paying in my own name."

Vess nodded slowly, the way people nod at truths they're still testing. "Ferrow Landing tomorrow."

"Ferrow Landing tomorrow."

"And after that? Kestrel's Landing. The hearings. All of it." She pulled her boots back on. "You understand they're going to want you to stand up in front of everyone and say it all again. The avalanche. Corren Pass. Him."

"I know."

"Does that frighten you?"

I looked upstream, into the dark where the river came from—from the fenced springs, from the sealed valley, from the whole hidden architecture of water and lies we'd climbed through to get here. Somewhere up there, Aldous Nystrom was living inside the wreckage of his own design. Somewhere up there, technicians were refusing orders and a ledger was filling with signatures and a bedsheet banner was flapping over a gate that would never close again.

"Not anymore," I said. And found that it was true.

Behind us, Sera stirred in the boat and called my name softly, and I turned toward the sound, toward the lantern glow, toward the narrow bunk and the woman in it and the last hundred miles of river between us and everything we'd promised.

Tomorrow we'd find Halloway's sister. Tomorrow the story would stop being ours and start being everyone's.

Tonight, for one more night, the river carried us—and asked nothing, and hid nothing, and ran clear all the way down to the sea.
