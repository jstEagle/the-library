## 37. Kestrel's Landing Remembers

The river told them before the town did.

We were still two bends downstream when the first skiff came out to meet us—a boy no older than fourteen, poling hard against the current, standing up in his boat to shout before the sound could carry. "They know!" he yelled. "The whole landing knows! They've been listening to the relay since dawn!"

I had slept maybe three hours in the last four days, and for a moment I thought I'd dreamed him. Then came a second skiff, and a third, and by the time we rounded the last bend into the long sightline of Kestrel's Landing, the water between us and the ferry docks was crowded with small craft—fishermen, bargemen, women standing in punts with babies strapped to their backs, all of them come out to look at us the way you'd look at a comet. Something that arrives once and changes the sky.

Gideon sat in the bow with his hands loose on his knees. He hadn't spoken since the gauging station. Not on the descent, not through the checkpoints that opened for us like doors held by strangers, not when Vess's gunboat—what was left of it—was towed past us by a consortium salvage crew whose men refused to meet anyone's eyes. He'd spoken exactly twice since the broadcast. Once to tell me which channel to take around the shoal below the old mill. Once, quietly, in the dark, to say: "It's going to be louder than either of us wants."

He was right. It was.

The docks at Kestrel's Landing were built for freight barges and fish carts, not crowds, but the crowds had come anyway, spilling off the planks and onto the shingle, up the rope stairs, along the sea wall. Somebody had rigged a banner between two pilings. It was crooked and hand-painted and it said WELCOME HOME in letters that dripped, and beneath it, smaller, as if the painter had lost his nerve: *GIDEON NYSTROM*.

I watched him read it. I watched the name land on him like weather.

"They spelled it right," he said.

"Small mercies," I said.

Vess stood at the stern beside our patched hull, arms crossed, in a uniform she'd stopped pretending was clean. She had logged her testimony at the source with the same flat voice she'd used to hunt us, and she'd ridden downriver with us because, as she put it, "If I go back through consortium territory alone, someone decides I'm a loose end." She looked at the crowd now with the expression of a woman counting exits. "There are officials on that dock," she said. "Three colors of sash. That's a tribunal party, or the start of one."

"Good," Gideon said.

"It's not good. Tribunals eat witnesses."

"I'm not going to be eaten." He said it without heat, the way you'd say *the river flows downhill*. "I'm going to say what happened. All of it. Then I'm going to ask for one thing, and it isn't for me."

I knew what the thing was. I'd known since the night below the source, when he'd sat with Halloway's map unrolled on his knees for the last time, tracing the dead man's pencil lines with one finger, and told me what he meant to do with his testimony. I'd argued with him about it, briefly, somewhere around the second checkpoint on the way down—argued that a man who'd spent seven years buying his invisibility with other people's small kindnesses shouldn't spend his first public hour on a grave. He'd looked at me with that patience that used to infuriate me and said, "Sera. Everything else they can take or fake. A name on a stone is the only thing Halloway ever asked anyone for."

So I didn't argue now. I just brought us in.

The crowd made a sound when the boat touched the dock—a low, collective exhale, the sound a crowd makes when a story it has only heard on cheap radios turns out to have a body, a face, a man stepping onto wet planks with river mud to his thighs. Somebody started clapping. It spread the way fire spreads through dry grass, and then, horribly for a man like Gideon, someone began to cheer.

He stopped at the top of the gangway and turned and faced it, and I saw him do the thing I'd seen him do on the Grinder, in the narrows, on the last rapid: plant himself, take the full weight, and refuse to flinch. He raised one hand—not a wave, more like asking quiet from a room—and the crowd, impossibly, gave it to him.

"I'm not a hero," he said. His voice carried because the dock carried it, because the water carried it, because everyone had leaned in. "I'm a guide who broke his country's trust once and spent seven years paying for it. A man named Elias Halloway died so that what's in that valley would see daylight. Everything you heard on the relay—every number, every filing, every name—came off his back and out of his pack. Remember him. I'll ask you all to remember him properly before this is done."

Then he saw the sashes and walked toward them, and the crowd parted like it had rehearsed.

* * *

They put us in the ferry house, which was the only building in the landing big enough and neutral enough, and which still had Halloway's original map framed on its wall where we'd left it—the first thing we'd done on arrival, before anyone official arrived, was hang it where the whole town could see it. Gideon had insisted. *Let it hang where he can watch,* he'd said, and I'd understood he meant it exactly as strangely as it sounded.

The tribunal party turned out to be three people and a stenographer. A magistrate named Corvel, gray and precise, with the sash of the provincial court. A water commissioner whose name I never caught, sweating through his collar in the way of a man who has just realized his signature is on some of the falsified filings. And a thin woman in the dark green of the Records Office, who introduced herself as Undersecretary Ilsa Vane.

I watched Gideon hear that name. I watched him decide, in the space of a breath, that it was a coincidence and to proceed as if it were, because the alternative was a fight he didn't have the hours for. Later I learned it wasn't a coincidence at all—the Vane family had leased the river's naming rights back to the province for a hundred years, one more quiet theft folded into the landscape. But that was a later war.

Corvel opened the proceedings by reading the charges that had been hanging over Gideon for seven years. The Corren Pass avalanche. Nine dead, including two licensed guides and a party of five paying clients. Gross negligence, falsified route assessment, flight from jurisdiction. He read them in a flat administrative voice, and the ferry house was so quiet I could hear the river under the floorboards.

"Do you dispute the findings?" Corvel asked.

"No," Gideon said. "I dispute the record."

He told it plainly. That was the thing that undid the room, I think—there was no performance in it. He'd had seven years to polish this story in the dark and he brought none of the polish. He talked like a man giving a route briefing: here is the terrain, here is the weather, here is what the blast sounded like at 4:40 in the morning on a slope that had no sanctioned charges on it, here is where the survey markers were pulled afterward, here is who signed the removal order. He named Aldous Nystrom three times, without ceremony, the way you'd name a coordinate. He laid Halloway's survey data on the table beside the consortium's filings and let the two documents accuse each other.

The stenographer's keys never stopped. The water commissioner stopped sweating and started a different kind of sweating. Undersecretary Vane took no notes at all; she just watched Gideon with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

It took four hours. Nobody left. The crowd outside didn't disperse either—we could hear them through the walls, a low murmur that rose whenever Gideon's voice rose, and once, when he described pulling the fifth body out of the snow, the murmur became something else, a sound like a wave deciding.

Near the end, Corvel removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"Mr. Nystrom. The province will reopen the Corren Pass inquiry. The consortium's charter is suspended pending investigation—that was done by emergency order this morning, before you arrived, if it matters to you. There will be criminal referrals. Some of them may reach very high." He paused. "What I am authorized to ask is: what do you want? Reinstatement of your guide's license is on the table. Full pardon. Compensation for seven years of—"

"No," Gideon said.

Corvel blinked. "No?"

"Not yet. Not like that. A pardon implies the conviction was a mistake. It wasn't a mistake. It was a product. Somebody manufactured it, and it sold, and it kept selling for seven years. I don't want it pardoned. I want it investigated, publicly, with the same attention you'd give a collapsed mine." He leaned forward. "What I want is small. It fits on a stone."

The room waited.

"There's a man buried in the potter's field behind the chandlery," Gideon said. "Buried under the name *John Doe, cause of death: exposure*. He died in a rooming house on Rope Street on the fourth of the month, and nobody came for him because he had no name anyone would honor. His name was Elias Halloway. He was a surveyor. He walked three hundred miles of the worst river on this coast to bring the province the truth that is sitting on your table right now, and he died of the injuries he took doing it, in a town that was too busy to ask his name twice." His voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. "I want the province to exhume him, record him properly, and rebury him under his own name with the words *surveyor* on the stone. That's my whole petition. That's everything I'm asking for."

The silence went on long enough that I heard a gull land on the roof.

Undersecretary Vane spoke first. "The Records Office can have the correction filed by tonight," she said. "The stone will take longer. Stone always takes longer." She looked at Gideon, and something in her face shifted. "Mr. Halloway's name will be in the provincial register as of this evening. All of it. Birth, trade, death, and what he found."

Gideon nodded once, and sat back, and I watched seven years come off his shoulders—not all at once, not all the way, but visibly, the way ice goes off a river in the first warm week: not melting so much as deciding.

* * *

They buried Halloway properly nine days later, because stone takes longer, but they didn't wait for the stone.

It was Gideon's doing, though he'd deny it. Word had gone out through the ferry house, and through the radios, and through the particular telegraphy of river towns where everything is known by nightfall: *the Doe in the potter's field was Halloway, and Halloway was the man who broke the consortium*. On the morning of the ninth day, half the landing walked up the hill behind the chandlery. Barge captains came off their boats. The fishwives closed their stalls. The boy who'd pole-shouted us the news stood at the front holding his hat. Even the consortium's local factor came, standing at the edge of the crowd in his good coat, saying nothing, which from a man like that was a kind of testimony.

There was no clergy that any of us recognized, so the ferry master read the provincial rite, and then Gideon stepped up before the temporary marker—a plank, planed smooth, with the name burned into it by the shipwright who'd done the work for free—and spoke.

He didn't tell the crowd what Halloway had found. They knew. He didn't tell them what it had cost. They could see the cost standing in front of them, a man with a guide's scarred hands and a name seven years in exile, just given back. What he said was simpler.

"He asked me for a promise over a dying fire, and I said no," Gideon told them. "I want that on the record too. I refused him. I walked out of that room and told myself the map wasn't mine to carry and the dead man's errand wasn't my debt. And then I came back at dawn and he was dead, and I understood that a promise doesn't wait for your consent. It just waits." He put his hand flat on the plank. "So this isn't a eulogy for a hero. It's a receipt. Elias Halloway paid. The province is holding the change. Make them count it."

I stood at the back with Vess, who had put on her uniform for the first time since the source—clean, pressed, the consortium brand on her collar covered by a strip of black cloth she'd cut from Halloway's old jacket lining, which Gideon had given her without a word. She watched the crowd, and I watched her watch it, and at some point she said, quietly, "My village is going to want its wells tested before the dry season ends. The data says our aquifer's third on his list." She paused. "I'm going to be the one who asks. Formally. On the record."

"Good," I said.

"They'll call me a traitor."

"They'll call you a lot of things. The river keeps what's true."

She almost smiled. Almost.

After the burial, the crowd did what crowds do—it wanted to touch the story. People came up to Gideon in ones and twos all afternoon, on the dock, outside the chandlery, in the street outside the rooming house where Halloway had died, which someone had already tied with a ribbon. They wanted to shake his hand. Some of them wanted to tell him their own grievances, the small ones the consortium had ground into them for years—the water fees, the sealed tributaries, the cousin who'd been run off a claim. He listened to every one. That was the thing that surprised me most, after everything: not the testimony, not the broadcast, not standing up to Aldous at the headwaters with a gunship's shadow on the water. It was this—the patience, hour after hour, of a man who had spent seven years avoiding exactly this and now stood in the middle of it and refused to hurry a single soul.

I brought him food at dusk and found him on the dock, alone at last, looking down the river the way he used to look up it.

"You could rest now," I said. "They'd let you. You've earned a week of being nobody again, if you wanted it."

"I tried being nobody." He didn't look at me. "Turns out I was bad at it. It kept costing other people." He turned then, and in the last light his face was the one I'd first seen on the dock seven years' worth of river ago—wary, closed, a man practicing invisibility—and it wasn't that face anymore, and he knew it, and I think that was the strangest gift of the whole journey: not vindication, just the end of the practice. "Corvel says the inquiry will want me in the provincial capital within the month. Vane says the water commission's filings will take a year to unwind. And there's the pass."

"The pass?"

"Corren." He said it the way you'd say the name of a person. "They're going to reopen the inquiry. Which means going back up there. Standing on the slope. Saying it again, to the people whose dead are actually in the ground." He looked downriver, north, toward the mountains that had taken his name from him. "I told them I'd go."

I sat down next to him on the dock boards. Below us the river moved, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea the way it always had, indifferent to what we'd done and somehow, in my heart anyway, changed by it.

"You won't be alone on that slope," I said.

"No," he said. "I know."

And that was when the runner came up the dock—young, breathless, one of the relay station's kids, with a flimsy telegram in his fist. He looked at Gideon, then at me, and held it out to me, because I was the one who looked like she'd read it calmly.

I read it twice.

It was from the provincial capital. Official seal. It said that the Corren Pass inquiry had been scheduled, and gave a date, and then it said a thing that made the dock tilt under me:

*Inquiry chair: Hon. A. Nystrom, recused. Replacement chair appointed: Hon. I. Vane.*

The undersecretary. The woman who'd filed Halloway's name by nightfall. The woman with the river's own name, who had sat through four hours of testimony saying nothing, taking no notes, watching Gideon like she was memorizing him.

Gideon read it over my shoulder. I felt him go very still.

"She asked for it," he said quietly. "Or she was given it. Either way—" He stopped. He was doing the arithmetic I was doing, and arriving where I was arriving: that the machinery was moving now on its own, that the story had left their hands entirely, that the next chapter would be written in a hearing room a thousand miles from this river by people none of us had chosen.

He folded the telegram along its crease, once, precisely, and put it in his pocket next to where Halloway's map used to ride.

"Pack light," he said. "We leave for the capital on the morning boat."
