## 38. What the River Keeps

The hearings were held in the customs hall at Kestrel's Landing, a room built for weighing cargo and now pressed into service weighing testimony. They'd cleared out the scales and brought in folding chairs, a long table for the tribunal, a clerk with a recording drum that turned and turned like something patient. Gideon sat through four days of it in a borrowed coat that fit him wrong, answering questions in a flat voice while the drum recorded every word.

He had expected it to feel like Corren Pass all over again—men in good wool deciding what version of him would be entered into the record. It didn't. That was the strange part. The difference wasn't in the room; it was in what he carried. At Corren he had been defending himself against a story someone else had written. Here he was only delivering one Halloway had already finished, stitched into a jacket lining, drawn in a dying man's hand across three provinces of water.

The tribunal asked him to walk them through the survey data again. He did. The gauging stations, the falsified flow filings, the aquifer maps that showed the Vane's headwaters feeding wells in settlements the consortium had spent thirty years pretending were empty. He watched their faces when he laid the two documents side by side—the consortium's filings on the left, Halloway's measurements on the right—and saw the moment arithmetic did what argument never could.

One of the three commissioners, an older woman named Ferro who had come up from the delta, kept asking him questions no one else thought to ask. How many men had Vess commanded. What the tattoo looked like. Whether the loudspeaker amnesty offer had been recorded before or after Aldous Nystrom's personal broadcast. She understood, Gideon realized, that the case wouldn't be won on water rights alone. It would be won on the machinery—the bribes, the sealed checkpoints, the quarantine orders drafted for villages that existed only because they weren't supposed to exist.

On the fifth day they released him without conditions. No travel restrictions, no bond, nothing. Commissioner Ferro shook his hand at the door and said, "You understand we'll need you again."

"I'll be here," Gideon said, and meant it, and was surprised by how easily the words came. For seven years *here* had been the thing he couldn't afford to say.

* * *

The offers started arriving before the hearings ended.

They came by courier, by letter, once by a man who waited outside the ferry house in the rain for two hours until Gideon agreed to speak with him. Mountain guide associations from two countries whose bans had been quietly rescinded—"reinstatement pending your acceptance," the letters said, as if the ban had been a clerical error rather than a life. An expedition company in the capital offered him a lead position on routes he'd dreamed about at nineteen, when his name still opened doors. A university wanted him to lecture on high-altitude rescue. One letter, on heavy cream stock with a wax seal, simply said: *The profession awaits your return.*

Sera read them over his shoulder at the ferry house table, eating bread with one hand and holding the pages with the other. "You could take any of these," she said. "You know that. You could be Gideon Nystrom, celebrated guide, by spring."

"I was Gideon Nystrom, celebrated guide, at twenty-six." He stacked the letters into a neat pile. "Look how that went."

"That wasn't your name's fault."

"No." He turned the pile face-down. "But the name came with a father attached, and a profession that let him use me as ballast. I'm not going back to be forgiven by the same people who blacklisted me on his word. They didn't fight for me then. Why would I want their approval now?"

Sera chewed her bread thoughtfully. "So what do you want?"

It was a question he'd been circling for weeks, ever since the broadcast, since the valley, since standing at the headwaters with water moving cold and constant under his hands while everything he'd been running from caught up with him and turned out to be survivable. He'd assumed the answer was nothing—that after this much motion he'd want stillness, a room somewhere, work that asked nothing of him.

But standing at the hearing rail on the third day, watching Sera give her testimony—she'd described the Grinder rapid with such precision that the clerk stopped turning the drum to listen—he'd felt something open in his chest that wasn't relief. It was appetite. He wanted the river. Not the mountains, not the old routes, not the profession that had made him and unmade him. The river, which had nearly killed him a dozen times and never once lied to him.

"Work," he said. "Real work. Upstream."

* * *

Sera's outfit didn't have a name yet, which struck Gideon as the last unfinished business of the whole journey. She had the boat—patched, recaulked, the hull scarred where the gunboat had clipped it but sound beneath. She had the route knowledge no one else alive possessed: every weir, every channel, the split where the map and the river disagreed and instinct had to carry the day. She had Vess's written statement attesting that she'd run the middle checkpoint at flood stage in the dark, which Vess had delivered to the ferry house personally, standing very straight, not quite meeting anyone's eyes.

What she didn't have was certification. The river trade ran on papers—pilot's tickets, cargo endorsements, safety inspections—and hers had lapsed years ago under a consortium rule that only licensed consortium affiliates could run the upper Vane. The rule had died with the monopoly, but the paperwork hadn't caught up. She needed a certified mountain-and-water guide to countersign her outfit's qualification for the upper-river license. There were perhaps six men in the region with the standing to do it. Five of them had spent the week writing letters offering Gideon jobs.

"You see the joke," Sera said, when he laid it out for her. "The name they took from you is the only one worth anything on that form."

"It's not a joke. It's arithmetic." He smoothed the form flat on the table. "I'm not taking their reinstatement. I'm spending my name where it does something. There's a difference, and I want you to see it before I sign, because once I do, I'm attached—to the outfit, to the route, to whatever you decide to build up there."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the landing sounds drifted in: gulls, a winch, someone arguing about freight rates. "You're sure? You could have the capital. Lectures. Dry socks."

"I've had seven years of dry socks." He picked up the pen. "They were terrible."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound—and then went serious again in the way she did when something mattered. "Then we do it properly. No shortcuts. I want the inspection, the full route survey, all of it. If we're putting paying passengers above the first weir, I want to know we can bring them back down."

"Obviously," Gideon said. "You think I'd certify anything less?"

"I think you're a man who spent seven years being careful about nothing," Sera said. "I want to watch what you're careful about when it finally counts."

He signed. His name went onto the line in his own handwriting—Gideon Aldous Nystrom, though he'd have to talk to someone about dropping the middle one—and the clerk at the registry office stamped it without blinking, because the world had moved on faster than Gideon's nerves had. Walking out, he passed the window where, years ago, he'd seen his own name posted on the blacklist and turned away before anyone could mark his face. The glass was just glass now. Someone had put a geranium in the sill.

* * *

The inspection took eleven days, and Gideon loved every hour of it in a way that embarrassed him.

They ran the lower river first, then the Grinder, then portaged the scree above the gorge with hired loaders who complained magnificently and earned double pay. Gideon tested her lines, her anchors, her spare rigging; Sera tested him back, running drills he invented and then improving on them, because that was who she was. They rebuilt the cargo tie-downs to a standard neither of them strictly needed, because the standard itself was the point—it was the opposite of everything the consortium had stood for, which was minimum effort behind maximum fence.

At the first weir, now unmanned, its gate rusted half-open, Sera idled the boat while Gideon climbed the catwalk and nailed up a board he'd painted in the ferry house yard. It read:

**UPPER VANE RIVER OUTFITTING — LICENSED PASSAGE — INQUIRE AT KESTREL'S LANDING**

Below it, smaller: *All waters open. All names welcome.*

Sera looked at the sign for a while. "The second line too?"

"Halloway's idea," Gideon said. "He just never got around to mentioning it."

* * *

The first paying clients came in late summer: a hydrologist and two students from the delta, going up to verify the aquifer readings for the restoration commission. Gideon guided them himself, rope and rigging, while Sera ran the boat. On the portage above the gorge, the oldest student asked him if it was true he'd been blacklisted from two countries, and Gideon said yes, and the student waited for more, and Gideon found there wasn't any more that needed saying. The record spoke. The river spoke. He hauled the line and the boy learned knots, and that was the whole of it.

In the evenings at the landing, he wrote letters. To the families of the nine from Corren Pass—not the official apology the tribunal had extracted from the consortium's estate, but his own, each one different, each one naming what he'd seen on the mountain and what he'd carried since. Some wrote back. One woman sent him a photograph of her brother taken the week before the pass, laughing, and asked Gideon to keep it, and he did, pinned above the ferry house table where the cream-stock letters used to sit.

Aldous wrote once, from wherever he'd gone—the investigation had scattered the consortium's leadership like chaff, some to hearings, some to exile, Aldous to a place no one would specify. The letter was four sentences. It offered nothing and apologized for less and asked, at the end, whether Gideon was well. Gideon read it twice, burned it in the stove, and told no one. Some weights you put down. Some you just stop carrying up the hill.

* * *

On the last evening of the season, he and Sera walked the landing from end to end. The ferry house had Halloway's original map framed behind glass now, the coffee-stained paper and the dying man's careful hand, and travelers paused in front of it without always knowing why. The village upstream—the one that wasn't on maps—had sent word that they were putting themselves on the next survey, voluntarily, on their own terms. The elder had included a note for Gideon in a language he couldn't read, which the hydrologist translated for him: *The river remembers who carried it.*

"She's right," Sera said, when he told her. "Rivers keep everything. That's the whole trouble with them." She stopped at the rail where the current ran dark and quick below. "Season's over. Boat's hauled out. So—what does a man do with his first winter under his own name in seven years?"

Gideon considered it. Below them, the Vane slid past, headed for the delta, carrying snowmelt and silt and everything it had witnessed toward the sea.

"I hear there's a wreck downstream," he said, "that the current gave back. Thought I might go look at it."

Sera turned. "That's not a wreck. That's a boat."

"That's what I said."

She was quiet a moment, working it through, and then she smiled—the slow one, the one he'd first seen in white water and had been chasing, in the most patient way, ever since. "Spring launch," she said. "We'll need a new hull. Bigger. Certified for passengers and cargo both."

"Already priced the lumber," Gideon said.

And far upstream, past the rusted weirs and the empty checkpoints, the source ran on in the dark of the sealed valley—unfenced now, ungated, pouring itself downhill toward everyone, keeping what it kept, giving away the rest.
