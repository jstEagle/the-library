## 39. A Wreck for the Current

**Gideon**

The boat came back to Kestrel's Landing on a flatbed truck three weeks after the hearings began, and Gideon stood in the ferry house yard at first light to watch them unload it.

She was a wreck when they'd abandoned her above the last gorge—hull stove in on the port side, transom cracked, half the deck planking sprung from the frame where the gunboat's wake had hammered her against the narrows wall. Sera had run a hand along the damage and said, "She's dead," and he'd believed her. He'd believed everything she said by then. That was the strange arithmetic of the river: it took a month of white water and gunfire to teach him to trust one person completely, after nine years of learning not to trust anyone at all.

But the boatwrights of the Landing were a stubborn guild, and the Landing owed Sera Okonkwo more than any of them would say out loud. The consortium's collapse had opened every ledger in the valley, and among the falsified filings and the bribed inspectors had been pages and pages of river fees collected illegally at the weirs—fees that should have gone to the pilots who worked the water. Sera's name appeared in those ledgers as a creditor. So did the names of forty other pilots, most of them dead or scattered, but their families came to the hearings too, and sat in the back rows with their arms folded, and listened.

So the boatwrights rebuilt her for cost. They steamed new oak into the cracked frames, replaced eleven planks, refastened the whole hull with copper, and when Gideon asked what to put on the invoice they told him to stop asking questions and bring coffee.

Now she sat on the truck bed under canvas, and when they pulled the canvas off, Gideon felt something move in his chest that he hadn't let himself feel since Corren Pass.

"She kept her lines," Sera said beside him. She'd come up quietly; she always did. "Even stove in. Look at the sheer."

"Is that what you call it when a boat looks like it's smiling?"

"That's exactly what I call it." She walked the length of the truck bed, checking the work with her fingertips the way a doctor checks a healed bone. "They even matched the grain. Old Man Ferro did the stem himself. He says a stem is where a boat keeps its name."

Gideon looked at the stem—the curved timber rising at the bow, pale new wood against the weathered gray of the original—and thought about names, which was a thing he did now without flinching. That was new. That was still new every morning.

"You ready?" Sera asked.

"For the launch?"

"For the register."

He wasn't. He said he was anyway, because some lies you keep out of habit even after they stop being necessary, and then he corrected himself, out loud, which was also new. "No. But I will be in ten minutes."

Sera studied him for a moment. The morning light came up the river behind her, gold on the water, and she wore the expression she'd worn at the foot of the source when the fences came into view—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation. She'd spent her life reading water, and somewhere in the last two months she'd learned to read him just as well.

"Ten minutes," she agreed. "I'll get the coffee."

* * *

**Sera**

The ferry house register was a ledger older than the town, or close enough. It lived on a stand inside the door, under glass during the day, and every pilot, guide, freighter, and drifter who worked out of Kestrel's Landing signed it going downstream and signed again coming home. Sera had signed it four hundred times. Her father had signed it before her, in ink gone brown, his letters tall and careful: *Okonkwo, T., pilot, licensed, Vane River, all seasons.*

She'd read the old entries a hundred times over the years, waiting out weather in the ferry house. Names were the whole history of the place. Some men signed with marks instead of letters. One entry from sixty years back just said *the twins* and nothing else, and no one living knew who they were. There were gaps, too—years where the same hand wrote *no traffic, high water* week after week, wars and collapses recorded in a clerk's tired shorthand.

And there was a gap she'd noticed only recently, once she knew what to look for. Nine years ago, spring. A name crossed out so thoroughly the paper had thinned, struck through with such force the pen had torn clean through to the board beneath. Beside it, in the margin, someone had written *struck from rolls — see registry office,* and the registry office record, she now knew, had been altered on instructions that traced back through three shell companies to Aldous Nystrom's desk.

Gideon Nystrom had been erased from this book before Sera ever met him. She'd poled past his blank space for years without knowing it was a grave.

Now he stood in front of the open register with a pen in his hand and didn't write.

The ferry house was full. Word traveled fast in a town built on a landing, and half the Landing had turned out—boatwrights still in their aprons, the elder's granddaughter down from the unrecorded village with a basket of smoked fish for Sera, Vess standing awkwardly at the back in civilian clothes like a woman who'd forgotten how to be off duty. Even Magistrate Corrigan had come, the same magistrate who'd have arrested Gideon on sight eight months ago, wearing the carefully neutral face of an official attending something historic and hoping not to be blamed for it.

Nobody rushed him. That was the thing about the Landing. They'd watched a man carry a dying stranger's map up a river that ate boats and guides both, watched him give his name over a radio to every settlement in three provinces knowing what it would cost him, watched him testify for six days without asking for anything. They could wait ninety seconds while he held a pen.

Sera moved to stand beside him at the stand. Not touching. Just there, the way she'd been there in the gorge when the line went taut, the way he'd been there on the scree portage when her hands were bleeding and he took the heavier end without being asked and without comment.

"The date first," she murmured. "You're overthinking the date."

"I'm not overthinking anything. I'm remembering how to spell my own name under pressure."

"It's seven letters, Gideon."

"Seven letters I haven't written in nine years."

He wrote the date. Then he set the pen down, flexed his hand, picked it up again. Behind them somebody shifted, and Vess said, quietly, "Take your time," in the voice of a woman who had once put a rifle down on a gravel bar because a piece of paper told her the truth about her own wells.

Gideon wrote:

*Nystrom, Gideon. Guide. Vane River, source to mouth.*

Then, after a moment, smaller, underneath:

*Came back.*

Sera read it twice. The second time her eyes blurred, and she didn't bother hiding it, because hiding things was over—for him, and she was starting to believe it might be over for her too, the habit of keeping the important parts below the waterline.

"Came back," she repeated. "That's going to start a tradition. In fifty years every pilot will sign it. *Came back.* Kids will think it means something simple."

"It does mean something simple."

"No," she said. "It doesn't. That's why it'll last."

He capped the pen. The clerk—a boy of maybe nineteen who'd been born after the erasure and had to be told the significance of what he'd just witnessed—entered the counter-signature and closed the ledger with the reverence of someone handling scripture.

Outside, the crowd parted toward the water, and the morning opened up in front of them like a page.

* * *

**Gideon**

They launched her at slack water, because Sera insisted, and because Gideon had learned that when Sera insisted about water, you obeyed the way you'd obey gravity.

The new boat went down the ways smooth and true, took the river without a shudder, and swung at her mooring line looking—there was no other word for it—happy. The crowd made the noise crowds make, and Ferro the boatwright wept openly and claimed it was sawdust in his eye, and the elder's granddaughter sang something low in her grandmother's language that Gideon didn't understand and didn't need to.

Halloway's map hung in the ferry house now, framed under glass on the wall opposite the register, where everyone who signed could see it. The real map—the hand-drawn one, grease-stained, bloodstained at one corner, the river rendered in a dead man's confident strokes all the way up to the springs marked with a single word: *HERE.* The survey data had gone to the tribunals, copied a hundred times over, but the map itself stayed at the Landing, because Halloway had started here and the Landing had finished his errand, and towns keep what they earn.

Gideon had stood in front of it the night before, alone, for a long time. He'd promised a dying man a delivery, and the delivery had turned into a war, and the war had turned into a reckoning, and none of it—not one day of it—had gone the way he'd have drawn it if you'd handed him the pencil. But the promise was kept. The water was spoken for. Three provinces' aquifers now had legal protection moving through the courts, slow as sediment but moving, and the gauging stations on the source were staffed by technicians who answered to the settlements instead of the consortium, because the technicians had refused, on the record, to do otherwise.

And Aldous Nystrom was gone. Not arrested—the lawyers were still arguing about what, precisely, could be proven against a man who'd built his crimes out of signatures rather than guns—but gone, upstream, out of the valley, his helicopter lifting off from the headwaters meadow while his own guards stood in a row and watched it shrink. Gideon had expected to feel triumph. What he'd felt instead was a kind of enormous tiredness, and under the tiredness, unexpectedly, grief—not for the man his father was, but for the man Gideon had spent nine years believing he might have been. You can't mourn a fiction forever. But you're allowed to mourn it once.

This morning, watching the rebuilt boat swing on her line, he found the grief had quieted to something he could carry. Like ballast. Placed low, where it kept you steady instead of pulling you under.

Sera came down the ways and stepped aboard, sure-footed, testing the deck with two stamps of her heel. "She doesn't leak."

"Boats always leak a little."

"This boat doesn't. Ferro would die of shame." She looked up at him across the gunwale. "Well, guide. Cargo's aboard. Pilot's aboard. River's down to a decent level and the forecast is clear for four days. Where are we going?"

It wasn't really a question. They'd been circling it for three weeks in the way of two people who've been through too much together to waste words: the upper river, the stretches above the old checkpoints, the water that had been closed to licensed pilots for a decade and was now, page by page, permit by permit, opening again. Someone had to run it first and chart it honestly—depths, hazards, channels, the truth of it, written down where no one could buy the ink. Sera had applied for the survey charter herself. The magistrate had approved it in an afternoon, almost giddy to sign something uncontaminated.

And she'd listed Gideon Nystrom as her second guide. His name, on a license application, in triplicate, filed with the territory. He'd signed it without his hand shaking, which had surprised him more than anything else that season.

"Upstream," he said. "All the way up. And then we come back."

"Came back," she agreed, and cast off the line.

* * *

**Sera**

The Landing fell away astern in the gold light, and Sera stood at the sweep and let the new boat tell her what she was.

Every boat has a voice. The old one had been a mutterer—complaining in the hull, grumbling in the rudder, talking all night at anchor. This one was quieter, younger, but she had the same bones, the same sheer, the same opinion about crosswinds, and by the third bend Sera had stopped comparing and started listening. Ferro had been right about the stem. A boat keeps its name there, whether the name is painted on or not.

They hadn't painted one yet. That was deliberate. Naming a boat on the Vane was a small ceremony, done at the head of your home water with people watching, and they'd agreed—without ever quite saying it aloud—that the naming belonged to this morning, out on the river, with the Landing in sight behind them and the current taking them forward.

"Gideon," she said. "Come take the bow line. Tradition."

He came forward, easy in the way he'd gotten easy over the months—the mountaineer's balance finally at home on a deck instead of enduring one. He took the line and waited.

"We should name her," Sera said. "And I've been thinking, and I have a candidate, and I want you to hear it before you argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were absolutely going to argue. You argue with your eyebrows."

He glanced down at her, and the corner of his mouth moved—that rare, unguarded motion she'd catalogued like a rare bird over the course of a season. "What's the candidate?"

"*Halloway's Promise.*"

She watched him take it. Watched it land—not as a blow but as a weight set down in the right place, low in the hull, where it would steady him. He looked back at the Landing, at the ferry house where the framed map hung behind glass, at the register with his name drying in the ink.

"He never saw the source," Gideon said. "He carried that map twenty years and never got within a hundred miles of it."

"Now everybody's seen it. Every radio in three provinces heard the numbers read out loud." She let the boat drift a moment, hands light on the sweep, the current doing the honest work. "A promise isn't a promise until somebody keeps it. He kept his. We kept ours. Seems right that the boat carries the proof."

Gideon crouched at the bow, laid his palm flat on the new stem wood, and stayed there long enough that Sera looked away to give him the privacy of it. When he straightened, his eyes were wet and he made no effort whatsoever to hide it, and she understood that this, too, was part of the ceremony—maybe the most important part. A man signing his own name. A man letting himself be seen.

"*Halloway's Promise*," he said. "Yes."

"Then she's named. Somebody should paint it before we hit salt water, or Ferro will do it himself in letters a foot high."

"I'll paint it tonight. My handwriting's better than yours."

"My handwriting is legendary."

"Your handwriting is a hazard to navigation."

She laughed—out loud, full, the sound carrying off the water—and steered them into the main channel as the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the whole Vane at once, bank to bank, all the way up into the hills where the springs ran free now, fenced by law instead of wire.

Behind them, faint across the water, a bell at the ferry house rang the hour. Ahead, the river ran wide and clean and open, and for the first time in nine years, Gideon Nystrom rode it under his own name, going wherever the current allowed and a little beyond.

At the first bend, Sera spotted something on the far bank—a figure standing at the old first weir, the checkpoint that had once demanded forged permits and taken faces too seriously. The guard hut stood empty, its gate swung wide, and the figure raised one arm in a salute that wasn't military at all. It was the boy clerk from the ferry house, off shift, fishing pole in hand, waving them through a checkpoint that would never close again.

Sera waved back. Gideon stood at the bow with his hand still resting on the stem, on the name, on the whole long distance between a man erased and a man returned.

"Four days of clear weather," she called forward. "Where do we sleep tomorrow night?"

Gideon considered the river ahead, the country opening above it, the maps waiting to be redrawn in honest ink.

"Somewhere new," he said.
