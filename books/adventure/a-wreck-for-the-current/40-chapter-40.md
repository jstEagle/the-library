## 40. Chapter 40

**Gideon**

The river took three days to forgive us.

Not that it had ever been angry—rivers don't hold grudges, Sera liked to say, they only keep accounts. And the Vane's account with us had been settled somewhere in the last gorge, paid out in scraped hull and rope-burn and the two of us laughing like idiots on a gravel bar because we were still alive and the water was still going where it wanted. After that, the current simply carried us. It felt less like mercy than like indifference, and after everything, I found I preferred it that way. The river didn't care what my name was. It never had.

We passed the first checkpoint at midmorning on the second day. The weir gates stood open. A young guard in consortium gray stepped out of his shack, looked at our boat, looked at me, and then—this was the part I kept turning over afterward—he put his hand flat against his chest. Not a salute. Not quite. Something older than either. Then he waved us through without asking for papers.

"Word travels faster than water," Sera said, not looking back at him.

"Faster than truth, sometimes."

"No." She trimmed the tiller and the boat swung wide around a snag. "Truth's just slow getting started. Once it's moving, nothing outruns it."

I didn't argue. I'd spent six years believing the opposite—that a lie, properly buried, could hold a mountain shut. Corren Pass had taught me that lesson so thoroughly I'd built a life inside it: an alias, a rented room above the ferry house, trail jobs paid under the table, a face turned from every registry window in Kestrel's Landing. I had been a man shaped entirely by the space around another man's secret. Aldous Nystrom's secret. My father's.

And now the secret was out, broadcast from the headwaters on the gauging station's own transmitter, read into every settlement radio downstream in a voice I hadn't used publicly since I was twenty-four years old. *This is Gideon Nystrom.* Six words. They had cost me more than the whole journey upstream, and they had bought back something I hadn't known I was missing.

We made camp that night below the second weir, on a shelf of sand the village boats used in high water. Sera built the fire while I checked the lines, and neither of us talked much, which was its own kind of talk by then. Around midnight she said, without preamble:

"You're going to turn them down."

"The reinstatement offers. Yes."

"You've already decided."

"I decided at the source." I fed a stick to the fire. "Maybe before. Maybe at Corren Pass, if I'd been honest enough with myself to know it."

She nodded slowly, watching the flames. "The guild would take you back. Full certification. They're practically begging—Vess told me there are three provinces want your name on their avalanche boards."

"I know."

"And you'd rather certify river pilots."

"I'd rather do work that doesn't require me to be someone else to do it." I looked at her across the fire. "You're building an outfit. You said so yourself, back when you thought I was just a drifter with money and a death wish. You need a lead guide who knows the upper Vane now better than any living person. And I need—" I stopped, restarted. "I need to hand something forward instead of burying it. Halloway gave me a map. This is the closest thing I have to giving one back."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Partnership terms. Fifty-fifty. You don't get to hide behind 'employee' anymore, Nystrom."

"That's the idea."

* * *

**Sera**

Kestrel's Landing smelled the same as it always had—tar, woodsmoke, fish guts, the green rot of the pilings—and it had never smelled so much like home. We came downriver on the last day of the dry season with the water so low the barge channel markers stood in mud, and the whole town seemed to have arranged itself along the dock to watch us arrive, which was absurd, because half of them had heard the broadcast weeks ago and the other half had heard about it from the ones who had.

Gideon went stiff when he saw the crowd. I watched him do it—the old choreography, shoulders dropping, eyes finding the exits, the whole practiced grammar of a man preparing to be invisible. Six years of habit doesn't burn off in one valley. But then an old woman near the fish stalls called his name—not *Nystrom*, but *Gideon*, plain, the way you'd call a neighbor—and something in him unlocked. He lifted a hand. Just that. Just a hand. And the town roared like we'd won a war instead of delivered a filing cabinet's worth of survey data.

The hearings ran eleven days in the ferry house, which had been cleared of barges and filled with folding chairs and a magistrate sent down from the provincial capital, a tired woman named Oduya who asked questions like she was cutting cloth—one clean line at a time. Gideon testified twice. I sat behind him both days and listened to him lay out Corren Pass in a level voice: the unsanctioned blast, the timing, the weather record Halloway had helped him reconstruct, the way the official report had moved the trigger point four hundred meters uphill to land on Gideon's route plan instead of the consortium's quarry operation. He did not raise his voice once. When Oduya asked him why he hadn't come forward at the time, he said, "Because the man who buried the evidence was my father, and I was twenty-four, and I believed him when he said no one would listen." A pause. "I was wrong about the listening. I should have given people the chance."

Aldous Nystrom did not appear. His lawyers appeared, in gray suits, and were dismantled over the course of a week with the patience of people unwrapping something fragile they intended to break. On the ninth day the provincial water authority suspended every Vane Consortium filing pending review. On the tenth, two of Aldous's board members resigned by letter. On the eleventh, Oduya closed the session by saying the matter would go to trial in the spring, and that Mr. Nystrom's testimony had been, in her phrase, "the spine of the record."

Afterward, outside, Gideon asked me to walk with him to the registry office. I thought he wanted company for the paperwork—the guiding certification, the outfit license, all the dull scaffolding of a legitimate business. Instead he stopped at the clerk's window and asked for something else.

"Halloway," he said. "Prospector. Came off a freight barge about two months back, dying. Buried in the potter's row up the hill." His voice stayed even, but I saw his knuckles whiten on the counter. "I'm here to claim him. For the record. His name was Elias Halloway, and he deserves a stone with it on."

The clerk, a young man who'd clearly been briefed on who this was, pulled the ledger without being asked. There it was: *Unnamed male, approx. 60, cause: exposure and cardiac failure, interred potter's row, plot 14.*

"We can amend the entry," the clerk said. "It'll need your statement as claiming party."

"I'll give it." Gideon signed. I watched him write *Gideon Nystrom* in a steady hand, and I thought about the first time I'd seen him sign anything—chapter six of this whole disaster, the salvage permits, the forged name he'd written without a flicker. He'd had a liar's penmanship then, smooth and fast. This was slower. This was a man carving.

They set the stone in November, before the ice came. Grey granite, because Halloway had struck Gideon as a grey-granite sort of man. The inscription was short:

*ELIAS HALLOWAY — PROSPECTOR — HE FOUND THE WATER AND GAVE IT AWAY.*

Gideon stood at the grave a long time in the cold. I waited by the gate, because some debts you witness and some you pay alone, and I'd learned which was which.

When he finally came down the hill, he said, "He told me to carry it upstream before the dry season ended. I keep thinking—I barely made it. Days. If we'd lost the boat at the split channel—"

"But you didn't."

"If we hadn't." He shook his head. "I'd have failed a dead man's last request because I was too proud to ask for help sooner."

"You asked me," I said. "Chapter six. Outrageous price, remember? I named it because I figured you'd flinch."

"I never flinched."

"No," I agreed. "That was the first thing I learned about you. Well. Second. The first was how gently you held a dying stranger." I bumped his shoulder with mine. "Come on. The boat's launched at dawn, and if you're late for your own launch, I'm renaming her after you, and nobody wants to holler *Nystrom* across a rapid."

* * *

**Gideon**

We named her the *Elias*. Sera fought me on it for exactly one evening and then pretended the argument had never happened, which is how I knew she'd wanted it too.

The rebuild had taken most of the autumn—new planking below the waterline where the gunboat chase had opened her up, a rebuilt transom, new fuel lines Sera inspected personally every morning with the vigilance of a woman who'd once caught a tracker's sabotage by smell. Vess helped for two of those weeks, between depositions. She was being formally reassigned—her testimony had gutted the hunters' contract structure, and the consortium's security arm was dissolving into something that would eventually be investigated itself. She'd come down to the boatyard with a crate of good rope, dropped it on the sawhorses, and said, "I owe the river a debt and I don't know how else to pay it," and Sera had handed her a plane without another word. That was Sera's whole diplomacy, in miniature.

Dawn on launch day came up clear and hard, frost on the dock ropes, breath standing in the air. The dry season was breaking early this year—the rains due within the week, the first real wet season in three years that the upper aquifers would actually catch, now that the springs weren't fenced and siphoned. Downstream, three provinces were waiting on that water. Upstream, the valley stood open for the first time since the fences went up, and the first survey teams were already poling their way toward it—not consortium men, but the water authority's own engineers, with Halloway's data as their founding document.

The original map hung in the ferry house now, framed behind glass, right beside the register window where every pilot and guide signed in and out. Sera had insisted on that spot. "Where the liars stand," she said. "Let them look at it while they sign."

At first light, the landing gathered again—smaller this time, quieter. The old woman from the unrecorded village had come downriver for it; she stood at the rail with her hands folded, and when Gideon passed she touched his wrist and said something in her own language that he didn't translate for me and I didn't ask about. Vess saluted the boat, actual salute this time, and grinned like a woman off duty. Even the young guard from the first weir had turned up, awkward in civilian clothes, holding a coil of line he'd brought as a gift.

Sera cast off the bow line. I took the stern. The *Elias* slid off the dock into the brown morning water, and the current took her the way it takes everything—without opinion, without memory, keeping its accounts elsewhere.

Sera at the tiller, me on the bow line, the landing falling away behind us. Upstream, the whole river waited: the Grinder, the portage, the split channel, the last gorge running full with the coming rains. A season's work. A lifetime's, maybe. Guides were already asking about runs above the second weir; there would be wrecks and rescues and arguments at the register window, and someday someone would hang a second frame next to Halloway's map, and I hoped it would be for somebody I'd trained.

Halfway to the first bend, Sera called back, "You going to sign the book when we come home?"

"Every time."

"Loud enough for the back row?"

"Louder."

She laughed, and put the nose of the boat into the current, and the Vane carried us up into the white noise of the falls, where the water began—the way it always begins, and ends, and begins again—as rain.
