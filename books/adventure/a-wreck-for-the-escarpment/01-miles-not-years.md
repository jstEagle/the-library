## 1. Miles, Not Years

The anemometer on the bench had a wobble in it, and Marguerite Zhou did not like wobbles.

She held the cup assembly at eye level, turned it with one finger, and watched the drag. A hairline catch, somewhere in the lower bearing—salt creep, most likely, or the cheap grease the manufacturer used because cheap grease photographed well in the spec sheet. She'd recalibrated forty-one of these units over six years of storm work and every one of them had the same flaw past the two-year mark. She'd written the manufacturer twice. They'd sent her a branded keychain.

"Bearing's shot," she said, to no one, because the garage was empty and she liked it that way before dawn.

The garage sat at the landward end of Corren's Wharf, a corrugated shed that had been a net-mender's shop, then a scooter repair outfit, and was now hers by a lease she paid in installments timed to the tide of her bank account. Salt air had taken the paint down to rust in long vertical stripes. Her tools lived on a wall of pegboard she'd mapped like a coastline—every wrench a promontory, every drawer an inlet. She could find a torque driver blind. She had, twice, when the power failed during a blow and the only light was the green flicker of a failing generator.

She pressed the bearing free with a punch, cleaned the race with solvent that smelled like lemons and lies, and repacked it with the good marine grease she kept for herself and never for clients. The cup assembly spun true after that, silent through a full rotation, and she held it there a moment longer than the job required, listening to the absence of sound the way other people listened to music.

Distance was the only honest unit. That was a thing she'd decided a long time ago, back when she still measured other things, and she'd never found a reason to un-decide it. A mile was a mile at the start of a job and at the end of it. A year was a rumor. A debt was a story somebody told about your future until you stopped believing your version and started believing theirs.

The phone buzzed against the bench. Screen up: **VOSS**. Under it, the word she'd assigned the contact—*optimist*—because Voss had called himself that the first time they met, grinning like a man showing you a puppy. *I'm an optimist, Ms. Zhou. I invest in people.*

She let it ring out. Fourteen seconds, which was long enough for him to leave a message if he wanted and short enough that he'd know she'd seen it. Then she put the phone face-down under a rag and went back to the anemometer.

The message came through anyway, because Voss's messages came through walls, through rags, through intent.

*Morning, Marguerite. Beautiful day on the coast. Thought we'd touch base about the quarterly. No pressure. Call when the light's good.*

The light was never good. That was the joke she didn't make out loud.

* * *

By seven the wharf was awake and the garage had a customer, a prawn-boat skipper named Aldous with hands like rope and a wind sensor on his mast that had stopped reporting in a squall two weeks back.

"Salt," Marguerite said, before she'd even opened the housing, because it was always salt, and because saying it with confidence saved twenty minutes of diagnostics.

"You haven't looked."

"I don't have to. It's salt. It's always salt. The sea is a jealous thing; it wants everything reporting back to it." She popped the housing and there it was, the green-white crust across the connector, and Aldous made the face he always made, the one that said she was a witch, and she let him think it because witch was cheaper than engineer.

While she worked he leaned on the bench and talked, the way skippers talked, about the season and the prices and the boy who'd quit his crew to go work the river runs upcountry. "River pays better now," Aldous said. "All that concession money moving up the Kessing. You hear about that? Big survey outfit, escarpment. They're buying everything with a hull."

"I heard."

"You should bid on some of that work. You're the best hand with instruments on this coast and you're sitting in a shed fixing prawn boats."

"I like prawn boats."

"You like eating." He said it kindly, which was worse. "How's the, ah." He gestured vaguely, the way people did when they meant her debt and were too polite to say it. Everyone on the wharf knew. That was the design. A debt you could hide was just money; a debt everyone could see was a leash.

"The what's fine, Aldous. Sensor'll be ready by noon."

He left cash on the bench, more than the quote, and she didn't mention it because mentioning it would have made them both honest, and honesty before nine a.m. was a luxury.

The phone buzzed again. Not Voss this time. A number with an upcountry prefix, the kind of prefix that meant satellite relay and money. She almost didn't answer. Then she did, because rent was eleven days out and the grease and solvent were down to fumes and pride didn't calibrate anemometers.

"Zhou Instrument Services."

"Ms. Zhou. My name is Renata Okafor, I'm a logistics coordinator with the Kessing Escarpment Survey." The voice was crisp, rehearsed, the voice of someone reading a script she'd read forty times that week. "We have a delivery requirement. Concession permits and a crate of communications equipment, to be conveyed upriver to our forward expedition. Discretion is a term of the contract."

"Discretion meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning the contents are sealed, the manifest is sealed, and nobody at the locks needs to know what you're carrying. Standard for permit conveyance. Companies are protective of their filings."

"Companies are usually protective of filings because the filings are boring."

The pause on the line was a half-second too long. "The rate is three thousand, plus conveyance costs, plus a completion bonus."

Marguerite looked at the bench. At the anemometer she'd fixed for Aldous, worth forty. At the solvent tin with maybe a centimeter of lemon-smelling lie left in it. She did the arithmetic she always did, the only arithmetic that had never lied to her: three thousand was four hundred miles of not thinking. Four hundred miles was a quarter's breathing room. A quarter's breathing room was enough time to find the next four hundred.

"Where to?"

"Middle Kessing, past the third locks. There's a station at the escarpment reach—our expedition's forward base. You'd hand the seals to the station lead, get a signature, come home."

"Boat's mine. Crew's mine. Route's mine."

"Within reason."

"Route's mine," she said again, "or the rate doubles and you can explain to whoever signs your budget why you're paying double."

Another pause, shorter this one. She was getting better at these pauses. Six years ago a pause would have sent her babbling to fill it. Now she let silence do her negotiating for her, because silence was free and she was not.

"Agreed," Okafor said. "Can you start tomorrow?"

* * *

She spent the afternoon not calling Voss, which was a full-time occupation with no pay.

There was a system to it. Voss called in the morning—caught, she thought, before she'd armored up—and again in the late afternoon, when the light went gold on the water and a person's resolve got soft. The morning call she could ignore on principle. The afternoon call was the dangerous one, because at four-thirty in a coastal garage with the tide going out, the future looked like exactly what Voss said it was: a thing you could buy back, installment by installment, if you just kept making the payments and didn't ask what the payments were buying.

She'd signed with the optimists twenty months ago. That was the part she didn't tell people, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed hook. Not that she owed money—half the coast owed money, the banks had made sure of that after the last two storm seasons gutted the fleet. It was *how* she'd signed. In a bright office that smelled of coffee and ambition, with Voss sliding the papers across a glass table and narrating them like a tour guide—"standard terms, standard terms, this one's just boilerplate, this one's actually in your favor"—while his associates nodded around him, three of them, all smiling, all called by their first names, all of them radiating the specific warmth of people who had never once needed a loan. She'd signed nine pages. She'd read four. The interest rate lived on page six in a font sized for people with better eyes and worse prospects.

Since then: eleven payments. Each one bought her a receipt and a fresh calculation of how much was left, and the number left never shrank the way it should have. It shrank the way a coastline shrinks on a chart—as a technicality, a rounding, a thing that was technically happening while the actual distance between her and free stayed roughly the same.

At four-twenty she gave in and called him first. Control of the clock, if not the debt.

"Marguerite!" Warm as a heater. "I was just thinking about you. How's the wharf? How's the boat?"

"The boat's fine, Voss. I'm calling about the quarterly. I can get you eight hundred by the fifteenth."

"Eight hundred." He said it the way a sommelier says a wine he's about to forgive. "Marguerite, you know what I admire about you? You always lead with a number. Most people lead with a story. You lead with a number. That's why I invested in you."

"Voss."

"Eight hundred works. It works beautifully. And you know what—because I'm an optimist, and because I believe in momentum—I have something for you. A little opportunity that crossed my desk this morning. A conveyance job, upriver. Permits and equipment for the Kessing survey. Good money, clean work, and it gets you out of that garage for a few weeks, get some river air in you." A chuckle. "You'd be doing me a personal favor, too. The survey's a client of ours. We like to see our clients' paper move on time."

The garage went very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed at another gull about something that would not matter in an hour.

"You're sending me the Kessing job," she said.

"I'm *recommending* you for the Kessing job. Big difference. Recommendation is a gift. Delivery is just work." The warmth never wavered, never tipped, never showed the machinery underneath. That was the thing about Voss: he was so far inside his own friendliness that even he probably couldn't find the seam. "Permits expire at the end of the month. The expedition's expecting their equipment. You'd be doing science a favor, Marguerite. Think of it that way. You, a patron of the sciences."

"I'll think about the rate."

"Think about the fresh air." And he was gone, cheerful as a closing door.

She stood in the garage a long time with the phone in her hand. The tide was out. The mudflats shone like hammered tin all the way to the channel markers, and the wind off the water had that flat, waiting quality it got before weather, before anything, the wind of a world holding its breath between one mile and the next.

Three thousand, Okafor had said. Plus costs. Plus a completion bonus she hadn't asked the size of, because asking would have made it real, and she'd wanted one hour of it being a good thing before it became a thing she owed.

The job Voss had "recommended" this morning. The job Okafor had called about at seven.

She turned it over the way she'd turned the cup assembly, looking for the wobble, the catch in the bearing, the place where it would drag. It was too neat. It was the neatest thing that had happened to her in twenty months, and neat was what trouble looked like when it had money behind it.

But the bearing spun. The number was three thousand. And the route was hers, she'd made them say it twice, and a route was a thing you could measure, and miles were the only honest unit there was.

* * *

That evening she walked the Tern.

The boat lived on a mooring off the wharf's end, a forty-foot former survey launch with a wheelhouse too small for her hull and an engine too big for her wheelhouse, which was Marguerite's favorite ratio in any machine. Her father had run her as a coastal courier before the banks took his route and then, two winters later, took him, quietly, in his sleep, in a rented room upcountry. Marguerite had inherited the boat, the tools, and a talent for leaving, in roughly that order.

She dropped into the cockpit and ran her hands along the gunwale, checking the patches by touch. Six years of storm work had taught her hands a vocabulary her eyes didn't need. Here, the seam she'd re-glassed after the Ondine blow. Here, the patch over the old through-hull, solid as a tooth. The Tern was a wreck that refused the title, and Marguerite had never decided whether she loved the boat or merely recognized herself.

"You're going upriver," she told her. "Both of us. Don't make it weird."

The boat said nothing, being a boat, which was one more honest party in her life than most people could claim.

She sat there while the wharf lights came on one by one, yellow coins dropped on black water, and let herself do the thing she never did in daylight, which was count in the wrong unit. Twenty months since the glass table. Eleven payments. Nine pages, four read. She was thirty-four years old and she could not have said what she'd done with any year of the last decade, only where she'd been in each of them, and where was just miles wearing a calendar's clothes.

The phone buzzed. She flinched before she saw the name: not Voss. Okafor.

"Ms. Zhou. Confirming start tomorrow, oh-six-hundred, at the company wharf past the grain terminal. You'll receive the sealed permits and the equipment crate there. One more crew consideration—we can offer a licensed river pilot for the locks and the escarpment reach, at our expense. The middle Kessing is not a river for the uninitiated this time of year."

"I have a navigator."

"A navigator isn't a pilot, Ms. Zhou. The reach above the third locks has water nobody charts twice." A pause, and then, in a tone that was almost human, almost warning: "Take the pilot. Trust me on the reach."

The line went dead before Marguerite could ask what a logistics coordinator knew about the reach that made her voice do that.

She looked down the black water, up the invisible river, three hundred uncharted miles to an escarpment she'd never seen, carrying sealed paper for men who measured the world in permits. The wind had come around. It was coming out of the interior now, off the high country, and it smelled green and mineral and far away, and it was blowing at eleven knots, steady, from the direction she'd be going.

Marguerite Zhou did not believe in omens. She believed in wind speed, in distance, in the drag of a bad bearing.

But she sat in the cockpit a while longer anyway, listening to the river wind, and for the first time in twenty months she could not make the arithmetic come out clean.
