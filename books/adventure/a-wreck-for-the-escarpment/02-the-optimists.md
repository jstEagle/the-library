## 2. The Optimists

The office of Brightwater Marine Salvage & Survey was a folding table, two chairs, and a window that looked out on a scrapyard crane. I kept the name because it sounded like more than one person worked there, and because Marguerite Zhou, Sole Proprietor had the ring of someone who'd chosen her life rather than fallen into it.

Voss arrived at nine sharp, which told me something before he said a word. Men who hold your debt come late when they want you to feel it. Coming early meant he wanted something, and men who want something from you are the most dangerous kind.

He brought three associates. That was the first odd thing. You don't bring three people to repossess an outboard motor or shake down a storm chaser for arrears. You bring three people when you're selling something.

"Marguerite Zhou." He said my name like he was tasting it, checking it against a list. He was a compact man, silver at the temples, dressed the way rich people dress when they want to be mistaken for comfortable—soft jacket, no tie, shoes that cost more than my boat's engine. "Edmund Voss. We spoke on the phone."

"We spoke eleven times."

"And every conversation was a pleasure." He smiled. It was a good smile. Practiced down to the millimeter, warm without ever reaching anything that could be called eyes. "May we sit? This is Anselm, this is Priya, and this is Doctor Rehm. We're with Meridian Growth Partners."

I pulled up a crate for each of them. The office smelled of diesel and old rain gear. Priya set a leather folio on my table as if it were a gift.

"I don't take investment," I said. "I've been through that. It ends with other people owning my boat."

"We're not here to invest in your boat," Voss said. "We're here to invest in *you*."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Meridian Growth Partners before that morning: nothing. Here is what I learned in the next hour: they were very good at making nothing sound like everything.

Voss talked the way weather builds—slowly, pleasantly, pressure rising so gradually you don't notice your ears until they pop. Meridian, he explained, was a consortium of private investors who believed in emerging river economies. They believed in infrastructure. They believed, above all, in people.

"People like me," I said.

"People exactly like you." He opened the folio. Inside were photographs of my own career laid out like evidence: the Tern running ahead of a squall line off Corvane Point; me, younger, holding a trophy at the Halvern Regatta; a news clipping about the cyclone season I'd spent ferrying medics up the drowned coast. "You have instincts. Instincts can't be taught and can't be faked. Our investors look for instinct."

"What do your investors want with instinct?"

"The same thing everyone wants. Delivery." He slid a photograph across the table. A river, brown and wide, vanishing into green haze. Above the haze, faint as a pencil line, an escarpment. "The Kessing Expedition. Up the Sarelle, past the locks, into the monsoon country. They're surveying the Kessing Escarpment concession on behalf of a development partner. They need resupply—concession permits, radio equipment, sundries. The regular contractors won't run it this time of year."

"It's nearly monsoon."

"It is," Voss agreed, delighted, as if I'd passed a test. "Which is why the fee is what it is."

He named the number. I made myself not react, which was hard, because the number was roughly double what I owed across every ledger I appeared in, including one or two I'd stopped opening.

"That fee clears my debt?" I asked. "All of it?"

"With room to spare." He spread his hands. "Marguerite, let me be candid, since I know you value it. You're carrying obligations. We've reviewed them. Some of our partners acquired paper recently—various notes, various creditors—and frankly, the situation struck us as wasteful. A woman of your capability, hobbled by arithmetic. We'd rather see you working."

"You bought my debt."

"We *consolidated* your debt. It sounds better and it's more accurate." He smiled again. "And if you complete this delivery, the consolidation resolves itself. Cleanly. Everyone walks away whole."

Anselm, who hadn't spoken yet, leaned forward. He had the build of a man who lifted weights for function, not appearance, and a face designed to be forgotten. "It's a good deal," he said. His voice was mild. "Most people don't get offers like this."

Priya tapped the folio. "The terms are standard. Service agreement, indemnity waiver, confidentiality rider. Nothing you haven't signed before."

That was true, which was how lies work best. I had signed things before. I had signed things during the bad year, when Dez's medical bills and a failed charter season stacked up like water against a weak seam, and I had learned that the words *standard* and *nothing you haven't seen* are the two most expensive phrases in any language.

Doctor Rehm spoke for the first time. Older, precise, with the detached air of a man cataloguing specimens. "The radio equipment is sensitive. Calibrated instruments. It will be crated and sealed under bond—you deliver it unopened, intact, to the expedition lead or their designated deputy. The permits likewise. You are a courier, Ms. Zhou, not a party to the contract. That insulation protects you."

"Protects me from what?"

"From complications," Rehm said, and went back to studying my bookshelf as though it were a rock core.

* * *

I'll say this for myself: I read contracts. After the bad year I read everything, twice, with a pencil. But there's reading and there's understanding, and the Meridian papers were built to defeat the second while permitting the first. Forty pages. Cross-references. Definitions section longer than some of my chart annotations. Every clause that mattered was defined in another clause, which was defined in an appendix, which referenced a schedule that wasn't attached.

"Schedule C is forthcoming," Priya said smoothly, when I asked. "Standard practice. It covers the delivery coordinates, which the expedition finalizes en route."

"So I sign a contract referencing a document I've never seen."

"You sign a contract referencing a document *your client* controls. Again—that's the protection. You're the boat, Ms. Zhou. Not the voyage."

Voss watched me with the patience of a man fishing with a line he knows is baited right. Outside, the crane swung a rusted hull section across the window, and for a second the light in the office went orange and then gray again.

"The fee," I said. "Paid on completion?"

"Half on departure. Half on confirmed delivery." He produced a pen—not cheap, heavy, balanced—and laid it on the table between us like a small ceremonial object. "And on completion, the consolidated notes are discharged. In full. You'd owe nothing to anyone. Imagine that. Starting a season at zero."

Zero. I tried to remember what zero felt like. It had been six years since I'd owed nobody, and even then I'd probably owed somebody and forgotten.

Dez would tell me later that I should have walked out. Dez says the room went cold the moment the third associate sat down, and Dez reads rooms the way I read sky. But sitting there, with the number still ringing in my ears and the image of a clean ledger floating in front of me like a shoreline, all I felt was tired. Tired of dodging calls. Tired of measuring my life in installments. Tired of being a woman whose entire future could fit inside a payment schedule.

"How long is the run?" I asked.

"Nine days upriver, weather permitting. The expedition expects resupply within the fortnight." Voss turned the folio toward me, open to the signature page. "The window matters, of course. Once the monsoon sets in properly, the upper Sarelle closes for the season."

Of course. Everything about the offer had a clock in it—the fee, the discharge, the window. Urgency is how you keep a person from doing arithmetic.

I read the signature page. It was the only honest page in the stack: my name, the fee, the discharge clause, a liability waiver broad enough to drive a barge through. *Contractor assumes all risk incident to river transit, weather, civil disturbance, and acts of third parties.* Civil disturbance. On a survey delivery. I looked up. Nobody blinked.

"And if the expedition isn't there?" I asked. "If I run nine days upriver and find nobody to sign for the crates?"

Voss's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a small recalibration, quick as a helm correction. "Then you'll have completed transit in good faith, and the discharge provisions apply to transit milestones. You'd be covered." He said it easily. Too easily, maybe. Or maybe I only thought so afterward, which is the trouble with hindsight—it retrofits suspicion onto everything.

I picked up the pen.

"Permits and radio crate," I said. "Sealed under bond. Delivered unopened. Anything else in the load?"

"Sundries. Provisions for the team. Nothing interesting." He glanced at Priya, who passed over a second folder—thinner, stamped with a seal I didn't recognize at the time: a stylized escarpment profile over crossed lines, embossed in dark blue wax. Concession documents, expedition authorization, a letter of introduction naming the expedition lead. Dr. A. Kessing. The handwriting underneath the typescript was small and impatient.

"These stay sealed too?"

"Those you may read," Voss said. "They're yours to deliver. Consider them your credentials. Anyone official on the river asks, you show those and you're carrying government-sanctioned freight." He paused, and added, lightly, "Not that anyone official will ask."

* * *

They loaded the crates that afternoon. Two of them: the radio equipment in a bonded case with a tamper seal and a serial number Rehm recorded in a little book, and the permit case, steel-edged, locked, heavier than paper has any right to be. Anselm carried both down the scrapyard lane to where the Tern lay at the municipal dock, and he carried them like they weighed nothing, which told me he'd carried heavier things to worse places.

I stood on the Tern's deck while they stowed the crates aft, lashed under canvas, and I felt the boat change under my feet. Boats do that. She knew she was carrying something now.

"One more thing." Voss stood on the dock, hands in his soft jacket pockets, entirely at ease. Behind him the harbor light was coming on, yellow in the early dusk. "Radio discipline. The expedition checks in nightly on channel nine. If you're within range, acknowledge. If you're not, don't worry about it—there are dead stretches above the locks. But once you're through the gorge, check in daily. Our partners like to know their investments are progressing."

"I thought I wasn't the investment."

"You're the vehicle the investment travels in." He smiled his practiced smile, and for just a moment the warmth dropped out of it, the way wind drops out of a day before a front arrives, and what was underneath was simple and patient and cold. "Deliver the crates, Marguerite. Keep the schedule. Do that, and you're the freest woman on this river. Fail—" he spread his hands, rueful, almost apologetic, "—well. Let's not plan for failure. We're optimists."

"Right," I said. "Optimists."

He shook my hand. His grip was dry and exact. Then the four of them walked back up the lane—Voss, the weight-lifter, the folio woman, the doctor—and I noticed what I should have noticed at nine that morning: they walked in step. Four strangers, moving like a crew.

* * *

That night I sat in the wheelhouse with the letter of introduction and read it three times. *To the officials of the Upper Sarelle District: the bearer, M. Zhou, is engaged under concession authority to provision the Kessing Escarpment Survey.* The seal at the bottom matched the wax on the permit case. Dark blue. The escarpment profile over crossed lines.

Crossed lines. I turned the letter over, held it to the lamp. Underneath the embossing, pressed deep into the paper, there was a second mark, older, mostly flattened out—someone had reused a die, or the die had been reground. Whatever was under the escarpment had been there first.

I put the letter down and got out my charts instead, because charts never lie; they just wait. Nine days to the escarpment reach. The upper Sarelle narrowed after the locks into a gorge no wider than a church, then opened into floodplain country where the maps went vague and the soundings were forty years old. Dead stretches on channel nine, Voss had said, like it was nothing. Like dead air was a feature of the landscape instead of a warning.

The fee was real. The discharge clause was real—I'd have Dez's cousin the notary look at the papers tomorrow, but the money was real, and the zero at the end of it was real, and God help me, I wanted it.

I wanted it enough to sign forty pages I couldn't fully read, referencing a schedule I'd never seen, sealed with a stamp whose history had been ground away.

Outside, the harbor lights shivered on the black water, and somewhere inland, nine days and a hundred rapids away, an expedition that needed these crates was waiting—or wasn't. I lashed the last of the canvas down over the bonded cases, patted the Tern's rail the way you'd steady a nervous animal, and started making my crew list.

Two names came to mind immediately. One of them was going to hate this.
