## 3. Crew of the Tern

The Tern sat in her cradle at Brightwater Marine like a patient who'd stopped believing in doctors, and I stood under her hull with a grease pencil, marking every soft spot in her planking the way you'd mark a bruise on a child.

Forty-one marks. I counted twice. The second count was for luck, which was Dez's department, not mine, but lately I'd been borrowing from everyone's departments just to get through a morning.

"Forty-one," Dez said behind me. He'd appeared without sound, the way he always did, a squat man in coveralls gone gray with engine oil and older stains he refused to name. He was chewing something that might have been betel nut or might have been a bolt. "Forty-one's not a good number."

"It's a number of planks that need patching."

"It's forty-one." He said it the way other men said *the tide's turning* or *she's going down*. Dez believed numbers had weather in them. Thirteen was wet. Nine was dry and mean. Forty-one, as far as I knew, had no established character yet, but he was clearly prepared to give it one.

"Then we'll make it forty-two," I said, and drew an extra circle around a spot that only needed caulking, and watched his shoulders come down half an inch. That was the whole science of managing Dez: you didn't argue with his arithmetic, you edited it.

He spat, considered the hull, and nodded once, the way a priest nods at a font. "I'll start aft. Aft first. Boats forgive from the stern."

Boats forgave from wherever you put resin, but I let him have it. We'd been together six years, Dez and me, since the night his own boat burned at anchor off Sallow Point and he walked into my garage smelling of smoke and asked if I needed a mechanic. I'd said no. He'd started working anyway. By the time I admitted I needed him, it would have been cruel to say so.

That was how a crew happened, if you were me. Not hired. Accumulated. Like barnacles, except the kind you'd miss.

* * *

Okonjo arrived at noon on a bicycle with no seat, carrying a cardboard box of charts against his chest like a bride.

He was nineteen, maybe twenty — he wasn't sure himself, which he confessed once, embarrassed, as though everyone else got issued a birthday with their papers. He'd shown up two years ago asking to learn navigation, and when I told him I didn't teach, he'd swept my garage floor for a week without being asked, then organized my chart drawer by current year instead of alphabetically, which was so obviously correct that I pretended I'd been meaning to do it for a decade.

"You got the contract," he said. It wasn't a question. Word traveled upriver faster than water.

"I got a contract."

"The Kessing expedition. The escarpment." He set the box down on my workbench and opened it with the reverence of a man unwrapping relics. Charts of the middle reach, hand-corrected, some of them old enough to show villages that weren't there anymore. "Nobody runs cargo past the locks this time of year. Nobody."

"Nobody with sense."

"So we're not nobody with sense." He grinned. He had a way of turning my refusals into itineraries. I'd learned there was no point correcting him; he'd just log the correction and keep grinning.

"We're delivering permits and a radio crate," I said. "Two hundred miles upriver, before the monsoon closes the reach. Then we come home, and I pay what I owe, and everything continues exactly as it has been."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him do the thing he did — take the shape of my sentence and hold it up to the light, checking for cracks. He was too young to be good at hiding what he found there.

"That's the plan," he said finally, agreeably, in the tone of a man agreeing with a forecast he didn't believe.

We spent the afternoon loading. The radio crate went amidships, lashed to the keelson where a wave couldn't reach it — Voss's people had been very specific about the crate, more specific than they'd been about anything else, including my signature. The permit case rode in my cabin, in the drawer where I kept the flares. I told myself the symmetry meant nothing.

Dez worked aft, talking to the boat. He talked to engines, to rudder posts, to bilge pumps. Not prayers, exactly. Negotiations. *"You and me, girl, we've both been run hard, but nobody's scrapping us yet."* When Okonjo asked him once who he was talking to, Dez said, "Whoever's listening," and Okonjo, who believed in currents and dead reckoning and nothing else invisible, had decided this was charming rather than alarming, which told you most of what you needed to know about their friendship.

By dusk the Tern sat low in her cradle, heavy with fuel drums, rice, resin, spare plank, the crate, the case, and three people's worth of optimism, which weighed nothing and carried everything.

* * *

We ate on the foredeck because the cabin still smelled of resin fumes. Rice and tinned fish and the last of the limes, cut four ways even though there were three of us, because Dez insisted the fourth lime was for the river.

"For the river," Okonjo repeated, deadpan, and ate his quarter.

"You can laugh," Dez said. "But when she takes us through the gorge with our skins on, you'll pour the next one yourself."

"When," Okonjo said, and pointed his fork at him. "You said *when*. Write it down. Witnessed."

The evening came down warm and violet over the harbor. Somewhere across the water a party was starting on one of the charter boats, music thinning out over the flats. I listened to my crew argue about whether a compass could be haunted (Dez: yes, if its owner died angry; Okonjo: no, but a bearing could be) and felt the particular ache that comes from having something you can't afford to lose.

Because here was the arithmetic I didn't say out loud. The Tern was mortgaged. The garage was mortgaged. And I was mortgaged, to Voss and his smiling associates, down to a number I made myself stop counting years ago because distances were easier — and every mile of this delivery was bought with their money, floated on their patience, aimed at their escarpment. If the trip went wrong, it wouldn't be my skin alone. It would be Dez's smoke-smelling future and Okonjo's unissued birthday and this boat with her forty-two forgiving planks.

"Skipper." Okonjo, quiet now. "What's at the escarpment? Really. Permits and a radio don't need a storm-season run. Somebody wants this delivered fast."

"Somebody's paying me to deliver it fast."

"Yeah." He turned his lime quarter over on his knee. "That's what I figured."

Dez reached over and patted the deck twice, gently, the way you'd calm a horse. "She'll tell us," he said. "Boat knows before we do."

The Tern creaked in her cradle, settling under her new load, and I chose to hear it as agreement.

We cast off at first light.
