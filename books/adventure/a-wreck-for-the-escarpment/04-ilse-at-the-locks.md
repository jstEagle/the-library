## 4. Ilse at the Locks

The first locks on the Vell sat in a fold of country that couldn't decide between river and swamp—cane brakes, drowned fences, a church steeple leaning like a drunk against the treeline. The lock itself was a concrete throat cut into the bank, twin gates, a control shed with a tin roof gone orange with rust. There was a queue. There is always a queue at the Vell locks in storm season, because half the river's commerce wants to get upstream before the water gets ideas, and the other half wants to get downstream before it gets worse ones.

We idled at the end of the line behind a fuel barge and a raft of timber so long it had its own weather. Okonjo worked the throttle in small corrections, keeping us off the barge's wash, and Dez stood at the bow with a cup of tea he never drank, watching the water the way other men watch television.

"Two hours, maybe three," Okonjo said, checking the queue against some private arithmetic. "Lock keeper's slow this time of year. They run one chamber at a time when the level's like this."

"Then we eat," I said. "Real food while we can. Once we're above the locks it's tins and regret."

I was halfway through frying eggs on the gimbaled stove when the stranger came aboard.

I want to be clear about the mechanics of it, because I've replayed that morning a hundred times and the mechanics matter. The Tern was tied up at the queue buoy. There was a gap of open water between us and the barge astern, maybe two meters, maybe less. And into that gap, without hail, without asking, came a gray inflatable skiff with a woman standing in it like she'd been born standing in things that move.

She had a boat hook. She used it once, catching our rail, and then she was over the side and onto my deck with a duffel on her shoulder and her hand already extended, and the whole operation had taken perhaps four seconds.

"Paloma Brightwater," she said. "Marguerite Zhou. Tern, out of Saltmarsh, currently contracted for an upstream run." She said it like a customs declaration. Like she'd read it off something. "You need a pilot for the escarpment reach. I'm offering to be your pilot for the escarpment reach."

I turned off the burner before the eggs burned, which is the only reason I didn't answer her immediately, and the delay was a mistake because it gave her time to look around. And she did look—not the way strangers look at boats, which is either covetous or dismissive, but the way a doctor looks at a patient. Her eyes went to the patched seam on the port quarter, then the radio mast, then the crate lashed amidships under the tarp, and stopped there for exactly one beat too long.

"Get off my boat," I said.

"Ilse Vantol." She shook the extended hand at me, unhurried, as if I'd taken it. "Licensed river pilot, class two, escarpment reach certified. You can verify the certification at the lock office, except you can't, because the office burned down in March, but the license is real and so am I, and you're about to run ninety kilometers of floodwater through a gorge that killed two boats last season, with a crew of two and a hull that's been patched at least twice since the paint went on."

"That's a lot of information for a stranger."

"I'm a pilot. Information is the trade." She set her duffel down on my deck without asking, which I noted, and which she noted me noting. "You're running the reach in rising water, ahead of the monsoon, on a delivery contract. I know because there are exactly three contracts moving upriver this month and yours is the only one small enough to be stupid about it."

Dez had come aft, silent, tea still in hand. Okonjo stayed at the helm but he'd turned on his seat, and I could feel him listening the way you feel weather change.

"We've run the reach before," I said.

"In low water, in daylight, with a cargo that floats." She glanced at the crate again. That second look. "What's in it?"

"None of your business."

"Fair." She said it easily, no offense taken, and that ease was its own kind of warning. People who take offense are telling you where their edges are. People who take nothing personally have usually sanded their edges off on something hard. "Here's my offer. I take the reach for you. Gorge transit, side channels, the flooded forest above the middle station—all of it, all of it currently underwater and unmapped. You pay standard pilot's rate plus hazard, and I sleep aboard. I have my own food. I don't drink. I don't ask questions I can answer myself."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the skiff back to the lock office, and you run the reach with a chart that's four years out of date and a sonar that—" her eyes flicked to the transducer mount, "—isn't going to read through silt load like this. And I'll be honest, Ms. Zhou, I'll watch you go with real regret, because I need this run more than you need to be polite about it."

There it was. The first true thing she'd said, offered up like bait, or like a gift, or like both. I looked at her properly then. Forty, maybe, hard to say—weathered the way river people weather, all one age from thirty to sixty. Short gray-streaked hair cut like she did it herself with a knife. Hands that had rope calluses in the right places. And eyes that did something I've only seen in a few people in twenty years on the water: they kept moving. Not nervous-moving. Working. Reading my deck, my crew, my face, filing it all somewhere.

"Where are you coming from?" I asked. "Skiff's not a pilot's boat. Pilots work out of the stations. You came downriver, against the current, alone, to find me. How did you know to find me?"

"Queue talk." A shrug. "The barge crew mentioned a storm chaser running upstream with a sealed crate. Storm chasers are famous. You're famous, Ms. Zhou. The woman who chased the Corvane squall into the harbor mouth and lived. There are people at every fuel dock on the lower Vell who'd know your hull on sight."

That was true, and I hated how true it was, and I noticed she'd given me a longer answer than the question needed, which is what people do when they're covering the ground around a hole.

"Dez," I said. "Opinion."

He'd been circling at the edge of the conversation the way he does, and now he came forward and looked at Ilse the way he looks at engines—down and close and without hurry. She let him look. That scored points with me, honestly. Most people flinch under Dez's attention or fill the silence with talk. She stood in it like it was a room she owned.

"Hands are right," Dez said finally. "Boat hook was clean. She stepped aboard port side, gave the rail her weight, didn't scratch the paint." He looked at me. "She's been on boats her whole life. But—" and here he did that thing where he talks to the boat instead of to me, "—she's carrying like someone who expects to leave fast."

"I'm carrying like someone who travels light," Ilse said. "There's a difference, and the difference is six years of practice."

Six years. She gave it to us flat, no weight on it, and then moved on so smoothly that a less suspicious woman would have missed it entirely. I didn't miss it. I put it in the pile. The pile was getting tall.

"Okonjo?"

He came back to the stern slowly. "The reach is bad this year," he said. "I've been saying it since Saltmarsh. The gorge in rising water, without current intel on the side channels—" he shook his head. "I can do it. Probably. But I'd rather not probably."

"There it is," Ilse said. "Democracy. I love a crew that votes." She picked her duffel back up. "Rate's negotiable. I'll take half now, half at the escarpment. If I get you there, you'll say it was the best money you ever spent. If I don't, you'll be in no position to complain."

"And if you get us there and then turn out to be something other than what you say?"

"Then you'll have had ninety kilometers to work out what I am," she said, "and I'll have had ninety kilometers to work out whether to worry about it. That's the deal, Ms. Zhou. Nobody boards a boat with a stranger without buying some of that."

It was the honesty that did it, or the shape of honesty. I've been lied to by professionals—Voss's whole operation is built out of people who lie like they breathe—and I know the texture of a constructed lie, all load-bearing and smooth. This wasn't that. This was a woman handing me true facts arranged to hide a shape, and some animal part of my brain, the part that had kept me alive through eleven seasons of weather other people fled, looked at that arrangement and said: she's dangerous, and she's useful, and those are the same thing right now.

"Half now," I said. "You eat at my table, you stand a watch, you touch nothing under the tarp. And Ilse—" I waited until her eyes came back to mine. "The day I catch you in a lie that matters, you're over the side, moving or not. Agreed?"

"Agreed," she said, and something crossed her face too fast to name—relief, maybe, or recognition, or the look of a woman who'd just been given a rule she'd been hoping someone would enforce.

She paid the half-rate in cash, old notes, counted out of a oilskin roll. Then she stowed her duffel in the forepeak without being shown where, which told me she'd already mapped the boat in the four seconds she'd spent looking at it, and she took the first watch without being asked, standing at the bow beside Dez, saying nothing, watching the queue creep toward the lock gates.

* * *

The lock took us up two meters in a chamber that smelled of diesel and wet concrete, and then the river changed.

Above the locks the Vell stops being a river and becomes a rumor of one—braided channels, sandbars that move overnight, cane islands that weren't there last week and won't be there next week. The water came down the color of milk coffee, thick with silt, carrying whole trees sideways like it was delivering them somewhere on a schedule. Okonjo ran the middle channel with the chart on his knees and his eyes doing the real work, and Ilse stood at his shoulder and said, for the first hour, almost nothing.

Then, at the mouth of the second braid, where the chart showed a sandbar and the water showed a channel, she said: "Left of the dead fig. The bar moved in the last flood. There's four meters through if you cut it close, six if you go wide and eat the current."

Okonjo looked at the dead fig. Looked at the water. Looked at me.

I nodded once.

She was right. Four meters, close in, the fig's drowned roots combing the current just off our port side, and beyond it the channel opened into slack water and the barge line we'd left at the locks was already a smudge behind us. Okonjo exhaled. Dez patted the deck twice. And Ilse stood at the rail with her hands in her pockets like a woman watching a clock run.

"You've run this reach recently," I said. It wasn't a question.

"Recently enough."

"The chart says the bar was at the fig. You said it moved in the last flood. The last flood was three weeks ago." I let that sit. "So you were on this river three weeks ago, or you talk to people who were."

"Both," she said, and gave me nothing else, and steered us with two fingers of one hand through a channel she clearly knew like her own hallway.

We made good distance through the afternoon—forty kilometers, maybe more, the Tern running light and eager under a sky that had gone the color of pewter without committing to rain. Ilse took the channel decisions and Okonjo took the helm and I took the stove and by evening we'd fallen into the rhythm that boats make out of strangers, the small negotiations of space and noise and watch rotation, and I caught myself almost relaxing, which is exactly when I don't.

Because here's what kept snagging. At the middle station bend, where the river splits around a long gravel island, Ilse said, unprompted: "Keep right. The left channel's silted since the concession survey teams started dumping spoil."

The concession.

Not "since the dredging." Not "since the works." The concession. She said it the way locals say a thing they've said a thousand times, with the flat ownership of long grievance, and then she went on talking about channel depth as if she hadn't said it at all.

I let it go until we'd anchored for the night in a cut behind the island, engine off, the first mosquitoes rising off the water in columns. Dez was below. Okonjo was on the bow running the anchor line through his hands, and Ilse was cleaning her boots with a stick, methodical, unhurried, a woman with nowhere to be and all the time in the world to not be there.

"You knew the cargo," I said.

She didn't look up. "Did I."

"The crate. You looked at it twice on my deck. And you knew about the concession before I ever mentioned it. I haven't mentioned it. Check my log, check what my crew will tell you—nobody's said the word concession on this boat since Saltmarsh." I stepped closer. "So either you've been reading my papers, or you already knew what was in the crate, or you know more about the Kessing Escarpment concession than a class-two pilot has any business knowing. Which is it?"

The stick stopped moving. For a long moment she crouched there over her boots, and the river talked against the hull, and somewhere in the cane brake a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"All three," she said finally. She stood up and faced me, and I'll give her this—she didn't reach for a smooth answer. "I knew the contract existed because there's nothing moving on this river I don't know about. I guessed the cargo because concession permits travel in sealed crates and radio gear travels in the same crates, and a storm chaser with a patched hull doesn't run floodwater for anything less. And I know about the concession because—" she paused, and I watched her choose, in real time, how much truth she could afford, "—because I've been following it for a long time. Longer than it's been a concession. Back when it was a survey application with a name on it I knew."

"What name?"

"That's not for sale today." She said it without heat. Almost gently. "Ask me again at the escarpment, Ms. Zhou. If I'm still aboard, I'll answer."

"And if you're not?"

"Then it won't matter." She picked up her boots and moved past me toward the forepeak, and stopped, close enough that I could see the river light reflected in her eyes. "You want to know the honest shape of it? You're carrying something upstream that other people want to arrive. I'm going the same direction for reasons of my own. Neither of us has to like the other. We just have to be useful, and I am very, very useful." A beat. "And you needed a pilot. Your navigator knew it, your mechanic knew it, and your boat knew it, because boats always know. You didn't take me aboard because you trusted me. You took me aboard because the arithmetic was ugly and I was the only number that made it work."

She was right, and I hated it, and I let her go below without answering, because the worst thing about a true sentence is that arguing with it only makes you look like the liar.

I stood at the rail a long time after. The pewter sky had gone fully to slate, and away south, over the country we'd come from, heat lightning was stitching the clouds from the inside—silent, patient, the monsoon clearing its throat two hundred kilometers off. Okonjo came and stood beside me. He didn't say anything for a while. He's learned that from me, or maybe he came by it honest.

"You're going to keep her," he said at last. It wasn't a question either.

"I'm going to use her."

"Same thing on a boat this size." He turned the lime quarter over in his pocket—I could hear it click. "She knew the word concession, Skipper. Before any of us said it."

"I know."

"And she knew the channel through the bar that the chart doesn't have."

"I know that too."

"So either she's the best pilot on this river," Okonjo said, "or she's been watching this boat a lot longer than this morning."

Down in the forepeak, faint through the deck, I could hear Ilse moving—settling into her berth, the small sounds of a person making a home for the night in a place that wasn't hers yet. Deliberate. Unhurried. Like someone planning to stay.

The heat lightning stitched the south again, closer this time, and for the first time since Saltmarsh I couldn't tell which of them I was more afraid of—the storm coming up behind us, or the woman riding ahead of it.
