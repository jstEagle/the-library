## 5. Dead Air on Channel Nine

Okonjo kept the radio schedule the way other people kept prayer.

Every night at nine, he climbed to the Tern's little wheelhouse, wound the set's crank if the battery was low, and called Channel Nine for the Kessing expedition. *Tern to Kessing base, Tern to Kessing base, radio check, over.* Then he'd sit with his hand flat on the receiver and wait through the static for a voice that never came.

The first night he'd assumed it was weather. The second night, distance. By the fourth night, forty kilometers upriver from the locks, with the banks narrowing and the air gone thick as wet wool, he had stopped making excuses out loud. He just wrote the time in the log—*2100, no contact*—and closed the book gently, like shutting a door on something sleeping.

Marguerite noticed. Marguerite noticed everything; that was the whole architecture of her, Okonjo thought. She'd built herself out of noticing—the wind shift before it arrived, the seam in a customer's story, the exact weight of silence in a room. She was up on the foredeck now, coiling line she didn't need coiled, and her eyes flicked to the wheelhouse every time he keyed the mic.

"Anything?" Dez asked from below, though he could hear the answer in the static bleeding down through the deck boards.

"Same as last night," Okonjo said.

"Which is nothing."

"Which is nothing."

Dez appeared at the top of the companionway wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white. He had a superstition about radios—he believed they carried more than voices, that bad news traveled along them faster than good because bad news traveled lighter—and so he stood at the bottom of the steps rather than come all the way up, as if the wheelhouse were a threshold he didn't want to cross while the set was live.

"Expedition's got a big antenna," he said. "Company money. They'd be heard from orbit."

"They would," Okonjo agreed.

"So either their set's broke, or—"

"Or." Okonjo let it hang. He'd learned that from Marguerite too: don't finish the sentence you're afraid of. Leave it hanging there where you can look at it or not.

Ilse came forward along the gunwale without sound, which was a thing she did that still unsettled him after four days aboard. A woman of her size should announce herself. Instead she just arrived, leaning against the wheelhouse doorframe, arms crossed, watching the river slide past dark and muscular under the first stars.

"Channel Nine," she said. Not a question.

"No contact since the locks," Okonjo said. "We got a confirmation ping when we cleared the last relay tower downstream. After that—" He shrugged. "Dead air."

Ilse looked at the radio the way Dez looked at black cats. "These reaches eat signals," she said. "Escarpment country bends them around. You can stand two hills apart and shout yourselves hoarse."

"You've run this stretch," Okonjo said. It wasn't quite an accusation. It also wasn't not one.

"I've run this stretch." She smiled, quick and crooked, and changed the subject the way she always did, smoothly, like water finding the low side of a rock. "Ask your captain what she wants to do about it."

* * *

What Marguerite wanted to do about it, she decided over cold rice and colder coffee, was keep going.

They sat in the cabin, the four of them crowded around the table that folded out of the bulkhead, the lamp swinging on its gimbal with the river's slow roll. Okonjo had laid out the charts even though everyone knew the route by now; laying out charts was how you made a conversation serious.

"We're four days behind a delivery schedule that was already generous," Marguerite said. "The permits have dates on them. Dates don't care about radio silence."

"The expedition might," Dez said.

"The expedition has thirty people and a satellite uplink and company money. We have a boat held together with resin and optimism." She tapped the chart. "If we turn back, we lose the season. If we lose the season, we lose the contract. If we lose the contract—" She stopped. Everyone at the table knew how that sentence ended. It ended with Voss, and Voss ended with numbers, and the numbers ended with the Tern sold out from under them and the three of them standing on a dock somewhere watching it go.

Okonjo watched her say it. He'd been crewing for her eighteen months, long enough to know that Marguerite Zhou did not talk about the debt unless she was using it as a wall—a thing to end arguments with, a fact so heavy nobody could climb over it. And he'd started to notice, lately, that she reached for it faster than she used to. That behind her eyes, when she mentioned Voss, there was something that wasn't calculation. Something closer to flinch.

"There's a middle option," Ilse said. "There's a fuel stop at Sarrow Bend, day and a half up. Port authority keeps a repeater there. We could raise Kessing base through it, or at least get word of them from the lockkeepers. Boats talk, even when radios don't."

"That's not turning back?" Marguerite asked.

"It's slowing down."

"Slowing down costs the same as stopping, this far in." Marguerite rubbed her face. In the lamplight Okonjo could see how tired she actually was, how much of her briskness was paint over rust. "One more day. We push to Sarrow Bend tomorrow, we ask around, and if anyone's heard from the expedition—if one lockkeeper, one fisherman, one drunk on a dock has seen thirty survey people alive in the last two weeks—then we keep going and stop worrying. Agreed?"

Nobody agreed out loud, but nobody disagreed, which on the Tern amounted to the same thing.

* * *

Sarrow Bend announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and diesel and drying fish, the particular sourness of a river town in the wet season. It was a straggle of stilt houses and tin roofs draped along the inside of the bend, a fuel depot, a repeater mast leaning like a tired man against a shed, and a single street of mud that passed for commerce.

Okonjo went ashore with Marguerite while Dez guarded the boat—or guarded the boat and avoided stepping on the dock cracks, which amounted to the same job—and Ilse stayed aboard too, saying she'd listen on the set. Okonjo caught the way Marguerite glanced back at her before they climbed the ladder. Measuring. Filing. He was starting to understand that Marguerite hadn't decided yet whether Ilse was a gift or a trap, and that until she decided, every kindness she showed the pilot would come with a receipt attached.

The town gave them its answers in pieces, the way towns do.

The fuel man remembered a convoy. "Survey boats, big ones, company-flagged. Went through—" he squinted at a calendar nailed to the wall, its pages stuck together with grease—"eleven days back? Twelve? Loaded heavy. Heading up to the escarpment reach."

"Radio trouble?" Marguerite asked.

"Wouldn't know. Company boats don't talk to us." He said it without bitterness, the way you'd say *the moon doesn't talk to us*. Fact of the sky.

The woman who ran the cookshop remembered something else. Two men, downstream-bound, fast launch, company markings, eight days ago. One of them bought cigarettes and wouldn't meet her eye. The other had a bandage on his hand and told her the expedition had finished early and gone home overland.

"Gone home overland," Marguerite repeated, walking back toward the dock. The mud sucked at her boots. "Thirty people, a hundred tons of equipment, and they hiked home overland through monsoon country."

"People lie," Okonjo said.

"Badly. That's the part I mind. If you're going to lie to a cookshop woman, at least make it expensive enough to believe." She stopped at the top of the dock ladder and looked upriver, where the valley narrowed into haze and the escarpment was a gray suggestion beyond the tree line, like a wall at the edge of the world. "Okonjo. Try the repeater."

The port authority repeater at Sarrow Bend was a dented cabinet bolted inside the fuel office, and the fuel man let Okonjo key it for a fee he pretended wasn't a bribe. Okonjo raised the standard hails—Kessing base, then Kessing forward camp, then the relay frequencies listed on their delivery manifest—and got back the same patient, empty hiss that the Tern's own set gave him every night. But on the last frequency, the emergency guard channel, something happened.

A voice. Thin, shredded by distance, there and gone.

"—say again, any station on Nine, we are—" Static ate it. Then, fainter, almost subliminal, a tone sequence Okonjo half-recognized from his navigation coursework: three short, three long, three short. Over and over, fading, like a light going down under water.

SOS. Or what was left of one.

He keyed the mic until his knuckles went white. "Station calling, this is vessel Tern, we read your signal, say again your position, over." Nothing. He tried again. Nothing. The hiss rolled on, indifferent, enormous.

When he turned around, Marguerite was standing very close, and her face had done something he'd never seen it do. All the briskness had drained out of it. What was left underneath was younger and much more frightened.

"You heard it," he said. Stupidly. Because obviously she'd heard it.

"I heard it." She looked past him at the repeater, at the mast leaning against the shed, at the river running north toward the escarpment with the patience of something that had drowned better boats than theirs. "Eleven days," she said quietly. "Voss said the permits expire in eleven days. And the convoy went through here eleven days ago."

Okonjo felt the arithmetic settle over him like cold water. He didn't know yet what it added up to. He only knew it wasn't weather, and it wasn't broken antennas, and it wasn't any excuse he'd written in the log.

* * *

Back at the boat, Ilse was waiting with the news already assembled, which was its own small alarm bell in Okonjo's head. How long had she been listening? What else did those ears of hers collect while the rest of them slept?

"Guard channel's been carrying fragments for two days," she said, before Marguerite could speak. "I didn't want to say anything until I was sure it wasn't ghost signal off the escarpment. Bounce can do strange things up there." Her eyes moved between them. "You heard the SOS."

"We heard part of one," Marguerite said. "Ilse. Who runs a survey convoy silent for eleven days in monsoon season?"

Ilse was quiet a moment too long. Okonjo counted it—one, two—before she answered. "Boats go missing on this river all the time," she said. "Weather. Bandits. River takes its toll." And then, softer, almost to herself, looking north: "But not usually this kind of quiet. This kind of quiet is chosen."

Marguerite stared at her. Dez had come up out of the engine hole, rag forgotten, superstition plainly itching all over him. The lamp swung. Somewhere down the street a dog barked itself out against the dark.

Then Marguerite did the thing Okonjo would think about for a long time afterward. She walked to the chart table, took the sealed document tube holding the concession permits, and set it down in the middle of the chart like a piece in a game whose rules nobody had explained to her.

"Tomorrow we run for the escarpment reach," she said. "Full days. No stops unless the boat forces one. We deliver, we find out what happened, and we get answers—real ones, not cookshop ones." She looked around the cabin, at each of them in turn, and Okonjo saw her put the wall back up, brick by brick, the debt and the dates and the contract stacking themselves into something load-bearing. "Because I signed for this delivery, and because whatever's wrong upriver, it doesn't get better with us sitting here."

It was a good speech. It was even, mostly, true. But Okonjo had spent eighteen months learning to read the difference between Marguerite's reasons and Marguerite's reasons, and tonight, for the first time, he couldn't find the seam.

That night he logged it anyway, in his careful navigator's hand: *2200, underway preparations complete. No contact, Channel Nine.*

And beneath it, after a moment, because the log was the one place he allowed himself to write what he actually knew:

*First true unease.*

He closed the book gently. Upriver, past the last lights of Sarrow Bend, the escarpment waited in the rain-dark like a held breath—and somewhere ahead of them, faint as a dying signal, three short and three long and three short went on repeating into the static, asking a question none of them were ready to answer.
