## 6. The Company's Reach

Ilse had been aboard the *Tern* for two days before she got a good look at the permit seals.

Not for lack of trying. Marguerite Zhou kept the delivery crate locked in the stern cabin under a floor panel that creaked when you stepped wrong, and she kept the key on a cord around her neck, and she slept like a woman who had learned somewhere expensive that sleep was something other people could steal from you. Ilse respected that. She'd have done the same. She had, in fact, done the same, back when she'd still had a name worth guarding.

But respect didn't stop her from counting.

She counted everything now. It was what six years of being nobody taught you: the world only let you keep what you could hold in your head. Names of locks and their seasonal water levels. The number of hours between radio checks. The exact shade of wax-red Halvern Consolidated used on its document seals, because wax was cheaper than embossing and Halvern was cheap everywhere it could afford to be.

She got her look on the second evening, at the mooring below the third lock, while the crew ate rice and tinned fish on deck and the rain came down in that patient monsoon way that said *I'm not hurrying for anyone.*

Marguerite came off the boat to argue with the lock keeper about transit fees. Dez went with her, ostensibly as backup, actually because Dez couldn't sit still through a meal. Okonjo stayed aboard but retreated to the bow with a chart, headphones on, mouthing coordinates to himself like a rosary.

Ilse waited ninety seconds. Then she went aft, knelt by the floor panel, and lifted it.

The crate was there, strapped down, stenciled with the Kessing expedition's call sign and a delivery manifest clipped to the top. Beside it, wrapped in oilcloth, the permit folio — thick as a Bible, corner-stamped, tied with government twine. Officially it was river authority paperwork, concession permits, transit clearances. Unofficially, everyone on this river knew what a concession folio meant: a company had bought a piece of the escarpment country, and the paper was the knife.

Ilse thumbed open the cover just far enough to see the first page's seal.

Wax-red. Circular. A stylized H crossed by a surveyor's transit line, and beneath it, in letters small enough to be deniable: *Halvern Consolidated Resource Development.*

Her hands did not shake. That surprised her. She'd imagined this moment for six years — imagined finding Halvern's mark on something she could touch, something she could burn — and in her imagination her hands always shook.

Instead she felt very cold and very clear, the way she'd felt the day she'd stood in a registrar's office in Kesselport and learned that Ilse Vantol did not exist. Not dead. Erased. No birth record, no pilot's license, no bank account, no brother. A clerk who wouldn't meet her eyes telling her, gently, that perhaps she was confused about her own name.

The permit folio sat in her hands, warm from the engine room, wearing Halvern's seal like a wedding ring.

She memorized the manifest numbers. Concession grant HK-114. Survey certification reference VS-7734, filed by an outfit called Meridian Terrain Associates — a name that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't spent six years reading Halvern's subcontractor chains, and everything to someone who had. Meridian was a shell. Meridian had certified surveys on three other concessions, all of them upstream of disasters that hadn't happened yet.

She heard boots on the deck boards and slid the panel shut with two seconds to spare, ending up in a crouch by the fuel line with a wrench in her hand, looking convincingly busy.

Okonjo's head appeared at the cabin door. "Ilse? Marguerite says we lock through at dawn. She wants your read on the reach above."

"Then she'll have it," Ilse said, and smiled, and hated how easily the smile came. Six years of practice. That was the thing nobody told you about losing yourself: you got very, very good at being whoever the room needed.

* * *

She gave them her read on the reach that night, spread across the chart table under the deck lamp while rain drummed the canopy. It was true, most of it. The channel above the fourth lock narrowed against the north bank; the shoals shifted every season; there was a fuel dump at Kilometer 190 run by a woman named Petra who charged fair prices and asked no questions. All true. All useful. All delivered in the voice of a pilot-for-hire who wanted paying.

What she didn't say: that the reason she knew the reach so well was that she'd surveyed it. That at twenty-four years old, under a name that still legally existed, she had run hydrographic lines up this same river for Halvern Consolidated, logging depths and gradients for engineers who never came, feeding data into models she wasn't cleared to see. That her brother Tomas had worked the escarpment audit team, and had come down from the plateau one October with a folder full of discrepancies and a face like a man carrying something fragile through a crowd, and had said, *Ilse, if anything happens to me, remember the elevation figures don't close.*

That he'd been right to worry. That eight months later his file had closed too — drowned, they said, during a routine gauge inspection, though the gauge he'd supposedly been inspecting had been decommissioned a year prior, and though no body had ever needed finding because there wasn't one.

And that when Ilse had gone asking questions — first politely, then loudly, then to a journalist — she'd woken one morning to discover she had never been born. Her license voided retroactively. Her accounts frozen pending an identity verification that would never conclude. Her landlord apologetic. Her employer's HR department unable to locate her employment history. A whole life dissolved like sugar in warm water, and no crime to point at, because erasure isn't a crime if the person it happened to doesn't exist.

So she'd become someone else. She'd taken work where names were optional — smuggling runs, salvage, pilot jobs paid in cash — and she'd worked her way back up this river year after year, following Halvern's fingerprints, waiting for the company to make a mistake big enough to matter.

And now here it was. Sitting in a crate on a debt-ridden storm chaser's boat, sealed in red wax, headed straight into the company's hands.

"You're quiet," Marguerite said.

They were alone at the chart table; the others had turned in. Marguerite had a mug of something bitter going cold beside her elbow and the particular stillness of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Tired," Ilse said. "The reach above the gorge is unforgiving. I was thinking about lines."

"Lines."

"Where we'll be when the monsoon peaks. If we're caught mid-channel in the gorge transit—" She let it hang. Fear was useful currency; she spent it deliberately, to buy credibility for later truths.

Marguerite nodded slowly. "Voss gave me eleven days of margin. After that the permits lapse and the contract's void." She said it flatly, without self-pity, which Ilse noted and filed away. People who stated their cages without rattling them were either resigned or planning. With Zhou, she suspected both.

"The permits," Ilse said, watching her. "You've read them?"

"I read enough." A pause. "Why?"

"Because paper like that has weight downstream. Companies that buy concessions don't like witnesses near them." Keep it general. Test the ground. "I've run deliveries for outfits like this before. When the client goes quiet, the smart move is usually to go quieter."

Something moved behind Marguerite's eyes — not agreement, not refusal. Calculation. "The expedition's lead scientist is Amara Kessing," she said. "You know the name?"

Ilse kept her face pleasant and empty. "Should I?"

"She's published on escarpment geology. My contact said the team's been radioing daily checks on channel nine until four nights ago." Marguerite turned her mug a quarter-turn. "Dead air doesn't scare me. Monsoons knock out radios. But Voss knew they were silent before I did. He told me to hurry anyway."

"And that doesn't strike you as strange?"

"It strikes me as exactly the kind of strange I can't afford to investigate." Marguerite looked up, and for a moment the restless competence dropped and Ilse saw the thing underneath it — a woman standing on a ledge, measuring the drop. "I owe money to cheerful men, Ilse. Cheerful men with associates. Whatever's happening upriver, my crew eats because I deliver this crate. That's the whole of my politics."

"I understand," Ilse said, and meant it, and despised how much she meant it, because understanding was the trap. Everyone on this river understood. That was how Halvern owned it — not with guns, mostly, but with arithmetic. With debts and wages and the slow, reasonable logic of people protecting what little they had.

She went to her bunk after that and lay awake listening to the rain and the river, doing her own arithmetic.

Here was the problem, laid out clean: she could take the evidence of the seals and slip away at the next lock. Find a radio, raise the port authority, start the long machinery of inquiry — a machinery Halvern had outlasted twice before. Or she could stay aboard, guide these people upriver toward whatever silence had swallowed the Kessing expedition, and try to steer a debt-hobbled captain and her found family toward a truth none of them had asked to carry.

Option one was sane. Option one preserved her. Six years of survival said: *take the seals, run, fight from the shadows, trust no one, especially not a woman whose leash is held by the men you're hunting.*

Option one also meant walking away from the first real thread she'd found since Tomas died. Because that was what the permits were — a thread. Concession HK-114, survey reference VS-7734, filed by Meridian Terrain Associates. If those surveys were forged — and her whole body knew they were, the way a pilot knows a shoal chart is lying before her keel proves it — then the paper in that crate wasn't a delivery. It was a confession, signed and sealed, traveling voluntarily to the scene of the crime.

And the people carrying it had no idea.

Guilt sat in her chest like ballast. She'd boarded this boat knowing more than she'd said. She'd watched Marguerite sign herself deeper into Voss's web without a word of warning. Every mile upriver, she was spending other people's safety to buy her revenge a closer seat.

*Tomas would say I've become them,* she thought. *Using people. Weighing them.*

*Tomas would also say the elevation figures don't close.* And they didn't. They never had. Somewhere above the fourth lock, behind the gorge, at the base of the escarpment, there was a team that had stopped answering its radio, and a scientist who had gone looking at bedrock, and a company that had gone looking right after.

She made her decision the way she made all of them now: alone, in the dark, with no one to consult because there was no one whose existence she could risk confirming.

She would stay. She would pilot them up the reach — genuinely, competently, keeping them alive, because dead passengers avenged nothing. And she would watch. If the expedition was simply scattered by weather, she'd get these people home and count it a cost. But if she found proof — real proof, the kind that survived lawyers — then somewhere on this river, Marguerite Zhou was going to have to choose between a debt and a truth.

And Ilse would be there, holding the truth out, hating herself for the timing, and doing it anyway.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the river running high and brown with the first real push of the monsoon. They locked through at first light, the *Tern* rising in the chamber like something being weighed, and then they were through, and the reach opened ahead of them — wide, braided, deceptively calm, the escarpment a blue smudge on the horizon that would take four days to stop being a smudge.

Dez was at the engine hatch muttering to it, which Ilse had learned was normal. Okonjo stood at the bow with the chart, calling depths. Marguerite ran the wheel with that economy of motion that Ilse was beginning to recognize as her native language.

Ilse took the pilot's position beside her and gave her the channel: south of the mid-river bar, favor the north bank past the drowned jetty, mind the set of the current around the bend at Kilometer 160. Marguerite took each instruction with a nod, and Ilse felt the strange double life of it — guiding them honestly along the surface while underneath, silent as the erased, she steered by another chart entirely.

At midmorning, Marguerite keyed the radio and raised channel nine. "Tern to Kessing expedition, Tern to Kessing, delivery inbound, requesting status check. Over."

Static. The long, wet hiss of a river full of rain.

She tried twice more, logged the attempts in a grease-pencil column on the chart, and said nothing. But Ilse saw her look at the date, and saw her do the arithmetic — days remaining, distance remaining, silence accumulating interest — and Ilse thought: *she feels it already. She just doesn't know its name yet.*

The name was Halvern. It was written in red wax in a crate ten feet from where they stood. And Ilse Vantol — dead six years, alive this morning, piloting a boat upriver toward her brother's grave — kept her hands steady on the rail and said nothing at all.
