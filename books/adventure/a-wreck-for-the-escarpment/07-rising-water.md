## 7. Rising Water

Dez woke to the sound of the river changing its mind.

He'd slept in the engine bay again, curled on the deck plates with a rolled tarp under his head, because that was where he did his best listening. Engines talked in their sleep. So did hulls. And at four in the morning, somewhere upstream in the dark, the Kessing River had stopped sounding like a river and started sounding like a freight train with no brakes.

He sat up too fast and cracked his skull on the exhaust manifold, which he took as an omen, because Dez took everything as an omen. Bad ones came in threes: cracked skull, dead bilge pump, and now this.

The rain hit ten minutes later.

Not rain like the coast had — coastal rain was polite, it knocked and then came in. This was a wall of water falling out of a sky that had simply given up pretending to be sky. It hit the Tern's cabin roof like gravel thrown by an angry giant, and Dez lay back down on the plates for exactly nine seconds, which was how long he allowed himself for self-pity on any given day, and then he got up and went to work.

* * *

By dawn the whole crew was awake, because you couldn't sleep through that kind of noise if you tried. Marguerite stood at the wheel in her oilskin with the hood down, letting the rain hammer her bare hair flat, watching the water the way she watched everything — like she was measuring it. Okonjo huddled under the chart awning with his maps in a plastic sleeve, drawing lines that kept getting shorter because the river kept getting wider. And Ilse stood at the bow rail like she'd been carved there, reading the current with an expression Dez couldn't parse and didn't trust yet.

Dez opened the engine bay hatch and his stomach dropped through the deck plates into the brown water below.

Six inches of standing water where there should have been none. The bilge pump — the good one, the one he'd rebuilt himself in the coastal garage while Marguerite argued on the phone with Voss — sat silent and drowned. He smacked it once, out of ritual more than hope, and it shuddered, gurgled, and died with the dignity of a thing that had already made its peace.

"Marguerite," he called, and didn't bother hiding what was in his voice, because she'd hear it anyway. "We've got a problem."

She came aft fast. She always moved fast; it was one of the things Okonjo idolized about her and one of the things Dez found quietly exhausting, all that velocity, all those miles. She looked into the bay, looked at the dead pump, and said nothing for three full seconds, which from Marguerite was a speech.

"How bad?"

"Seam's weeping. Port side, about amidships, low." He pointed without touching, because you didn't touch a weeping seam until you'd decided what you were going to do about it. "It's been seeping since the locks. I knew about it. It was a trickle. Now the river's up two meters overnight and pushing on the hull like it wants in."

"Can you fix it?"

Dez wiped rain off his face with a sleeve that was already soaked. "Define fix."

"Dez."

"I can slow it," he said. "I can't stop it. Not in this water, not from inside. To do it proper I'd need to beach her, careen the hull, dry the seam, and resin it. That's six hours of work and a shoreline that doesn't exist right now." He jerked his chin at the banks, or where the banks had been yesterday. There were no banks anymore. There were trees standing in water to their shoulders, and beyond them a gray blur of rain that ate the horizon.

Marguerite looked at the water rising in the bilge, then at the sky, then at him. "Slow it, then."

"That I can do."

* * *

The morning became a war fought entirely against water, which was fitting, because as far as Dez could tell the monsoon had declared war on everything else too.

He attacked the seam from inside with everything the Tern carried. Epoxy putty first, kneaded warm between his palms and slapped into the gap in fist-sized wads, each one pressed hard enough to leave his fingerprints in it like a signature on a confession. Then a sheet of rubber belting cut to size, screwed over the seam with self-tappers that bit into wet wood and held anyway, because Dez asked them nicely and Dez believed wood listened, the way he believed engines listened, the way he believed the river absolutely did not listen and never would.

Okonjo bailed. That was the boy's job now, handed over without ceremony: a five-gallon bucket on a rope, dipped and swung and dumped, over and over, a rhythm like rowing backward. The kid didn't complain. Okonjo complained about cold coffee and bad charts and Ilse's habit of giving directions that consisted of single words, but he didn't complain about work, and Dez had decided early on that this made him worth teaching.

"You know what my grandmother said about rivers?" Okonjo shouted over the rain, mid-swing.

"She had opinions?"

"She said a river is a road that hates you."

Dez grunted, pressing another wad of putty home. "Your grandmother ever run cargo?"

"No."

"Then she only knew half of it. A road that hates you still goes where you're going. This—" he nodded at the bilge, where the water level had paused its climb, holding, holding, "—this is a road that's trying to eat the truck."

The rhythm held through the morning. Bail, dump, bail. Putty, screw, press. The Tern pushed upstream against a current that had tripled its appetite overnight, engines laboring at an angle because Marguerite had learned within the first hour that you didn't fight the flood straight on — you quartered it, crab-walking upriver, trading distance for survival, which Dez noted was the most honest thing he'd ever seen her do.

Ilse came aft around noon, ducking under the awning, rain streaming off her jaw. She looked at the bilge, at Dez's improvised patch, and gave a single nod that managed to contain both approval and pity.

"It'll hold?"

"It'll hold until it doesn't."

"When will it not?"

Dez shrugged. "When the river decides. I don't set the schedule. I just work here."

Something flickered across Ilse's face — not quite a smile, more like recognition. "My brother used to say that about machines."

"Smart man."

"He was." She said it flat, the way people say things they've said so many times the words have worn smooth as river stones, and then she was gone, forward again, reading water.

Dez filed that away. He filed everything away. Superstitious, Marguerite called him, half-affectionate, and he didn't argue because arguing was bad luck, but superstition was just memory wearing a costume. You remembered what happened before the bad thing, and next time you saw the before, you paid attention. Ilse saying *my brother* in that voice was a before. He didn't know what came after it yet. He'd keep watching.

* * *

They anchored at midafternoon behind a stand of flooded fig trees, because Marguerite refused to run the boat blind after dark in floodwater, and even she couldn't argue with visibility measured in boat-lengths. The anchor bit into something soft and held, barely, and the Tern swung on its rode like a dog at the end of a chain, pointing upstream, wanting to go, unable to.

That was when Marguerite broke out the stores and did the arithmetic.

Dez watched from the engine bay hatch, hands black with epoxy, while she laid out the rations on the chart table under the lamp: the rice in its sealed drums, the tins, the dried fish, the fuel cans, the drinking water in its jerry jugs. She counted everything twice. She wrote numbers in the margin of the chart, small and precise, and her mouth did the thin-line thing it did when the numbers were talking back to her.

"We planned for nine days to the escarpment at normal water," she said finally, to all of them, because she was fair that way — bad news delivered to everyone at once, no favorites. "At this current, running days, we're looking at fourteen. Maybe sixteen if we have to anchor out more nights like tonight."

Nobody said anything. The rain filled the silence for them.

"So we recalculate." Her pen moved. "Half rations on the rice. Fish every other day. Water's the real problem — we can boil river water, but fuel for boiling is fuel for running, and I won't gamble the engines. So: water strict, food strict, and nobody volunteers to eat extra because they did extra work today. Everybody did extra work today."

"I'll take less," Okonjo started, and Marguerite cut him off with a look that could have caulked a seam.

"You'll take your share. That's an order. The day this crew starts rationing itself by guilt is the day we starve politely instead of eating meanly." She capped the pen. "Questions?"

"Yeah," Dez said. "One."

She looked at him.

"The patch holds now. But the river's still climbing — look at the fig trunks, we've come up another hand-span since we anchored. Every meter of rise is more pressure on that seam and more hours on the pump, and I've got one working pump and a hand bailer. If this rain keeps up three more days, I can't promise the Tern floats to the escarpment. I can promise she floats *toward* it. After that it's weather and luck."

"Luck," Ilse said quietly, from the shadows by the galley, "is just weather you haven't met yet."

Dez pointed at her. "See, that's the kind of thing my grandmother used to say, and she also believed crows carried messages, so I'm going to need you to be right about it."

"I usually am," Ilse said, and there was that flicker again, that worn-smooth something, and Dez filed it deeper.

Marguerite rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. For a second — just a second, and Dez was probably the only one who caught it because he spent his life catching things before they failed — she looked tired. Not sleepy-tired. Carrying-tired. Like the weight wasn't the boat or the debt or the rain but something underneath all three, some number she was always calculating and never sharing.

"Then we go toward it," she said. "And we keep going toward it until the going stops. Understood?"

Understood, they said. All except the river, which said nothing, and kept rising.

* * *

Night watch fell to Dez, because Dez liked night watch, because night was when the boat told the truth.

He sat in the engine bay with a hurricane lamp turned low and one hand resting on the patched seam like a doctor taking a pulse. The backup pump — the little one, the emergency one, the one rated for a boat half the Tern's size — chugged away in the corner, swallowing the weep and spitting it overboard, and Dez listened to its heartbeat and matched his breathing to it, the way you did, the way you did with anything you depended on.

The rain had settled from assault into siege. It came down steady and endless, drumming the cabin top, hissing off the river, and beneath it all the deep animal sound of the flood moving — millions of tons of water sliding past in the dark, carrying whole trees, carrying who knew what else, carrying the old river away and bringing a new one in its place.

His grandmother would have said the river spirit was angry. Dez figured the river didn't need a spirit to be angry. Rising water was just physics with a grudge.

He checked the seam. Weeping, but slower. The putty was curing. The screws held. He let himself feel one full minute of satisfaction, timed against the pump's rhythm, and then he spent it, because satisfaction hoarded was just pride waiting to be taxed.

Footsteps on the deck. Okonjo, wrapped in a blanket, lowering himself onto the crate beside the hatch.

"You're supposed to be sleeping."

"Can't." The boy pulled the blanket tighter. "Every time I close my eyes I hear the water coming in."

"It is coming in. That's not your imagination, that's the job."

"You know what I mean." Okonjo stared out at the black river. "Dez. Can I ask you something? You've known her longest."

"Known who?"

"Marguerite. Back in the garage, before all this. Was she always—" he groped for it, "—like this? Counting everything? Miles, liters, days left on the permits? She counts like the counting is keeping her alive."

Dez considered lying, decided the boy deserved better, and considered telling the whole truth, decided the boy couldn't carry it. "People measure what they're afraid of losing," he said. "Some folks count money. Some count years. She counts distance, because distance is the one thing you can always put behind you." He tapped the seam under his palm. "You want to worry about something, don't worry about her counting. Worry about the day she stops."

"What happens the day she stops?"

"Either she's found something worth staying for," Dez said, "or something's caught her. And I haven't figured out which one scares me more."

Okonjo chewed on that. The pump chugged. Somewhere out in the dark, a tree groaned and went under, and the current carried the sound downstream like a rumor.

Then the radio crackled.

Both of them froze. Channel Nine — the expedition frequency, the one Marguerite had them monitor every night on schedule, the one that had answered nothing but static since the locks. Static now too, thick with storm hash. But underneath it, buried, half-swallowed—

A voice. Two syllables. Maybe three. Gone before Dez could make sense of it, swallowed by a burst of interference that rose like a wave and erased everything.

Okonjo lunged for the set, twisted the gain, called into the handset — "Kessing station, Kessing station, Tern calling, say again, say again—" — and the static rolled on, indifferent, enormous.

"Did you hear it?" the boy whispered. "Dez. Tell me you heard it."

Dez had heard it. A human voice, down in the noise, coming from upstream, from the direction of a camp that had been silent for days. He thought about omens. He thought about threes. He thought about the seam under his hand, weeping slower, and the river outside, rising faster, and the arithmetic Marguerite had written in the margin of the chart — fourteen days, maybe sixteen — and how none of those numbers accounted for whatever had just spoken out of the dark.

"I heard it," he said.

Above them, the cabin light snapped on, and Marguerite's boots hit the deck, already moving fast.
