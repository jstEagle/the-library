## 8. The Abandoned Camp

The river bent north at dawn and the escarpment showed itself for the first time—a blue-gray wall on the horizon, so far off it could have been weather. Okonjo spotted it from the bow and called it out like a sighting of land, and for a minute nobody said anything, because the escarpment was the far edge of the map and everything past it was the kind of work you didn't come back from.

"Two days at this current," Ilse said. She stood at the tiller with her weight on one hip, reading the water the way Dez read engine noise. "Less if the rain holds. More if the rain holds harder."

"That's not a forecast," Dez said from the engine hatch.

"That's the monsoon."

We made the forward camp by late morning of the second day, though none of us knew that's what it was yet. Ilse had marked a cut in the bank on her chart—a tributary mouth, wide as a street, where the Kessing expedition was supposed to have staged its equipment runs. The coordinates came from the delivery manifest Voss's people had given me, and the manifest said, in the cheerful bureaucratic language of people who invest in other people: *Forward staging site, Kessing Escarpment Survey, occupied.*

The tributary mouth was occupied, all right.

I saw the mast first. A radio mast, guyed with wire, leaning at the angle of something that had been pushed rather than fallen. Then the orange domes of expedition tents through the trees, and then the smell—wet canvas, woodsmoke gone cold days ago, and under it something else, something chemical, that I didn't have a name for until we were close enough to see the burn scar.

"Take us in slow," I said.

Ilse took us in slow. The Tern slid off the brown main channel into water the color of tea, and the trees closed over us, and the sound of the river dropped away like a door shutting.

"Nobody's here," Okonjo said. He had the binoculars up. "I don't see anybody."

"People leave camps," Dez said. "That's a thing camps do."

"People don't leave masts bent over," Ilse said.

She was right, and we all knew she was right, and we came alongside the bank anyway, because the debt didn't allow us to do anything else and because—though I wouldn't have said it out loud then—some part of me had been waiting since Channel Nine went quiet for exactly this, the way you wait for a diagnosis you already know.

* * *

The camp sat on a low terrace above the flood line, six tents in two rows, a cook shelter, a gear rack, a latrine trench screened with palm fronds. It had been a working camp, an organized one, the kind of camp you build when you expect to be somewhere for a season. Whoever ran it had numbered the tents. The numbers were still stenciled on the canvas: 1 through 6, in a neat surveyor's hand.

Every tent had been slashed.

Not opened. Not unzipped and searched, the way you'd search a camp you wanted to strip for intelligence. Slashed—cut open from one end to the other, the blades going through the canvas and whatever was inside it, bedding and clothing and field books, everything opened to the rain. The rain had been at it for days. Sleeping bags had gone to gray pulp. A pair of boots stood outside Tent 4 where someone had left them neatly, toes toward the fire pit, and they were full of water and green with mold.

"Slashed from outside," Ilse said. She crouched at the cut on Tent 2 and ran her thumb along it. "One stroke. Someone strong, someone in a hurry, or someone who didn't care how it looked." She stood. "This wasn't theft. Theft is quiet. This was a message."

"For who?" Okonjo asked. "There was nobody here to read it."

"For whoever came next," Ilse said, and looked at me, and I felt the look land somewhere under my ribs.

I walked the rows. I made myself do it slowly, the way I'd calibrate an anemometer—methodical, one axis at a time—because the alternative was to walk it fast, and fast was how you missed things, and missing things on a river gets you killed. Six tents. Six slashed. The cook shelter had been overturned and the stove dismantled, not broken—dismantled, its parts laid out on the ground in a neat line, which was somehow worse than if they'd been scattered. Someone had wanted the camp to be unusable and legible. *We were here. We did this. We had time.*

The gear rack was empty except for a single climbing harness, hung on its hook, mildewed. The instrument cases were gone—every one. No theodolite, no levels, no sample crates, no coring tubes. Whatever the expedition had been measuring, someone had carried the measuring away.

"Radio's gone too," Dez called from Tent 1. He had his head and shoulders inside the slashed canvas and his voice came out flat. "Base station. There's a mounting plate, a battery box, cable ends cut and coiled. They took the set and cut the cable so nobody could jury-rig anything."

"So they didn't just silence them," Okonjo said. "They took the expedition's voice with them."

Nobody answered that, because it didn't need answering.

* * *

It was Ilse who found the fire pit.

I'd walked past it twice. It looked like what it was supposed to look like—a ring of stones, a bed of ash, the wet black remains of a fire that had been put out thoroughly. But Ilse crouched at its edge the way she crouched at everything, low and patient, and after a while she said, "Marguerite," in a voice I hadn't heard from her before. Not the pilot's voice. Not the sharp, knowing voice she used on the tiller. Something older.

I came over. The ash was deep, deeper than a cooking fire leaves, and under the top layer, where the rain hadn't reached, it wasn't all ash. There were scraps of paper in it. Burned, but not burned through—the fire had been hot and then smothered, doused, killed before it finished its work. Someone had burned documents in a hurry and then someone else—or the rain, or regret—had put it out too soon.

"Careful," I said, and we lifted them out with a tent stake and a piece of sheet metal, fragment by fragment, and laid them on a dry board from the cook shelter.

Most of it was gone. Corners of pages, a strip of graph paper with a pencil grid on it, a fragment of a cover sheet with a printed logo that I recognized and wished I didn't: the interlocked H of Halvern Consolidated, the same mark that was embossed on the permit seals in the crate lashed under my deck tarp.

One fragment was bigger than the rest. Half a page, burned along one edge, water-stained along the other, but the middle had survived, and the middle was a survey ledger page.

I know survey ledgers. I've calibrated instruments for people who keep them; I've watched storm researchers log pressure readings in handwriting like seismographs. A survey ledger has a grammar—station numbers, backsight and foresight, instrument heights, reduced levels. This fragment had the grammar. It had the columns. And the numbers in the columns were wrong.

Not smudged, not ambiguous. Wrong the way a lie is wrong when you finally look straight at it.

The page was an elevation run—benchmarks up the escarpment's lower shoulder, station by station, the kind of careful incremental arithmetic that says *this is the shape of the land, this is how high it is, this is what the water will do.* And the reduced levels climbed the way the escarpment climbs, and then, at the third surviving station, they climbed wrong. A benchmark that the fragment showed at one elevation was cross-referenced, in the margin, in a different hand, in pencil, with a second figure—forty-one meters lower. The same point. Two elevations. One of them was in the ledger's neat ink, and one of them was in pencil, pressed hard, with a line under it and a single word that the fire had spared:

*Impossible.*

Below that, in the same hard pencil, half a sentence survived before the burn took it: *—cannot match Halvern's filed survey. The escarpment base is not where the concession says it is. If the base is—*

Then the page ended in char.

I stood there holding it, and the rain came down through the canopy in fat, intermittent drops, spotting the board, and I made myself read it again. And again. Because there is a moment, when you're chasing weather, when the barometer does something it isn't supposed to do—when it falls through a floor that's supposed to hold—and you have a choice. You can tell yourself the instrument's faulty. Or you can believe the instrument, and believe what's coming, and start doing the arithmetic of survival.

The pencil was the instrument. The pencil was telling me the storm had already made landfall, months ago, and I'd been sailing inside it.

"Marguerite." Okonjo's voice, quiet, behind me. "You've gone white."

"Read this."

He read it. His lips moved. He was young enough that I could watch the understanding arrive in his face in stages—the numbers first, then the two hands, then the word *impossible*, then the half-sentence, then all of it at once, the whole shape of it.

"The filed survey," he said. "The one the concession permits are based on."

"Yeah."

"The permits we're carrying."

"Yeah."

He looked at me the way you look at someone when you're both standing in the same rising water and only one of you is holding the map. Behind him, Dez had come out of the tents and was standing very still, and Ilse was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, because it wasn't surprise. That was the thing I'd notice later, in the dark, when I couldn't sleep. It wasn't surprise.

"Forty-one meters," Dez said. "That's not a rounding error. That's not a bad day with a level. That's a different river."

"A different valley," Ilse said. "If the escarpment base is forty-one meters lower than the filed survey says, then every calculation behind that concession—every dam height, every flood projection, every subsidy claim—is built on a valley that doesn't exist." She said it evenly, factually, the way she called channel depths. Too evenly. Like a woman reading from a page she'd read before.

"How do you know how dam calculations work?" I asked her.

"I'm a river pilot," she said. "I know what water does. It's the one honest thing on this river." And she turned away and started walking the camp again, and I let her, because the alternative was to press, and I wasn't ready to press, and some animal part of me understood that pressing her in that slashed camp, with that page in my hand, would cost me something I couldn't price yet.

* * *

We searched the rest of it because searching is what you do instead of panicking. We found a boot print in the clay by the latrine trench—a military-pattern sole, big, deep, more than one set, overlapping. We found a shell casing in the fire pit's ash, brass, rifle-sized, and Dez held it and turned it and said nothing, and his silence was louder than anything he'd said all week. We found a child's flip-flop under Tent 3, which meant the camp hadn't been only scientists, which meant the slashing and the burning and the taking-away had happened around people who had children with them, and I put that thought down the way you put down something hot, and it kept burning my hands anyway.

And we found, pinned inside the collapsed cook shelter, a scrap of tent fabric with writing on it in grease pencil, the letters big and hurried, angled like they'd been written by someone kneeling:

*GONE UPSTREAM — A.K. — FOUR OF US — IF YOU ARE CREW OF A DELIVERY BOAT, DO NOT DELIVER*

I read it four times. *Do not deliver.*

The rain picked up. It came harder through the canopy, drumming on the dead tents, filling the boot prints, and somewhere upstream thunder spoke in a voice that didn't echo so much as roll, long and continuous, the sound of a sky that had decided something.

"Who's A.K.?" Okonjo asked, though I think he knew.

"Amara Kessing," I said. "Lead scientist. The name on the manifest. The name on the radio schedule." The name Okonjo had called into the static every night for a week, night after night, *Kessing expedition, Kessing expedition, Tern calling, over,* and gotten back nothing but the river's own hiss.

"She wrote this for us," he said. "She figured a delivery boat would come."

"She figured right."

"So do we—" He stopped. He looked at the crate under the tarp, the crate with the permits and the radio equipment, the crate I'd signed for in a bright office full of cheerful men who invest in people. "Do we deliver?"

And there it was. The whole question, laid out in grease pencil on a scrap of canvas, in a dead camp, in the rain.

I looked at the page in my hand. *The escarpment base is not where the concession says it is.* I looked at the shell casing. The child's flip-flop. The boots outside Tent 4, toes toward a fire nobody was coming back to. I thought about my crew—Dez with his superstitions and his hands that could fix anything, Okonjo with his pilot's papers still folded in a plastic sleeve, both of them owed, both of them aboard because I'd told them this run would clear us, would buy us out, would make everything square. I thought about Voss's voice on the satellite line, warm as bathwater: *investments expect returns, Marguerite.*

And I thought about the pencil note under the burned figures, pressed hard enough to score the paper. Somebody had looked at the land with their own instruments and written *impossible* and then written the truth about it, and then somebody else had burned the truth and slashed the tents and taken the voices, and the only reason any of it was in my hand was that the fire had been smothered too soon.

"Nobody delivers anything yet," I said. "We secure the camp, we mark it, we take the page and the note and the casing. Then we push upstream."

"Upstream toward what?" Dez asked.

"Toward Kessing," I said. "Four of them went up. Fen—her deputy—might be with them. And whatever they went up there to prove, they went to prove it at the escarpment itself." I folded the ledger fragment along its surviving crease and put it inside my jacket, against my chest, where the rain couldn't get it. "We're not a delivery crew anymore. I don't know what we are. But we're not turning around."

Ilse was watching me from the edge of the camp, half in shadow under the trees, and when our eyes met she nodded once, small, like a person confirming a sum. And that nod frightened me more than the slashed tents had—because it wasn't the nod of a woman learning where we were going.

It was the nod of a woman who had known all along.

I filed that away, the way I file a pressure reading that doesn't fit, and turned to the crew, and gave the orders, and the Tern took us back out into the brown current with the dead camp sliding away behind us through the rain.

Upstream, the thunder kept rolling, and the escarpment stood on the horizon getting closer, and in my jacket, over my heart, the burned page said *impossible* in a hand that had pressed hard enough to mean it.
