## 9. Someone Is Following

Okonjo had learned to read fog the way other navigators read charts. His mother had grown up on the lower delta, where fog came off the water at dusk like a curtain being drawn, and she'd taught him that fog was never empty. It was a room with the lights off. Something was always in it with you.

He was on watch at the Tern's bow, third shift, which meant the hours between two and dawn when the river went quiet enough to hear its own thoughts. The monsoon hadn't arrived yet — Dez kept saying *any day now, any day* with the grim satisfaction of a man who liked being right — but the air had gone heavy and wet, and the fog had thickened overnight until the banks on either side of the river were suggestions rather than facts. Marguerite had ordered them to anchor at midnight rather than run blind, so the Tern sat at the edge of a wide bend, riding a slow current, her running lights hazed to soft orange coins.

That was why he heard the launch before he saw it.

An engine, running low. Not idling — *loping*. The steady, throttled-back note of a boat moving fast but trying not to sound like it. Anyone else might have taken it for a fisherman checking nets in the dark, but Okonjo had spent two years on delta patrol boats before Marguerite hired him, and he knew the sound of an engine being polite. Fishermen didn't lope. They puttered. They knocked. This was a boat holding a speed just under the threshold where sound carries — the speed of someone who wanted to arrive without being announced.

He stood very still at the bow rail and listened it into position. Upstream, maybe six hundred meters, hugging the eastern bank where the current ran slack and the fog pooled thickest. Coming downriver. Toward them.

He didn't call out. He didn't wake Marguerite. Some instinct, old and unglamorous, told him that the most useful thing he could do in the next four minutes was nothing at all — to be a piece of the Tern, a stanchion, a rail, something that had always been there and therefore wasn't worth noticing. He eased down behind the anchor winch and watched the fog.

The launch came out of it like a rumor becoming true.

It was long and low, a patrol hull, the kind the mining companies ran on the upper reaches — the kind with a reinforced bow for pushing through debris and an open cockpit for a man with a rifle. No lights. No registration markings he could make out. A single figure at the helm, and behind him, in the shadow of the canopy, the suggestion of more. The hull was dark gray, the gray of a thing designed not to be described later.

It passed the Tern at perhaps a hundred meters. Close enough that Okonjo could hear the water folding off its bow, a soft repeated *shh, shh, shh* like someone shushing a child. Close enough that the figure at the helm turned his head — Okonjo felt the look pass over the boat, over the winch, over the place where he crouched — and held there for a three-count before facing forward again.

Then the launch was past, sliding back into the fog downstream, and the engine note climbed as it opened the throttle, and in thirty seconds it was gone, and the river was just a river again, and Okonjo's heart was going like a drum in a parade.

He counted to two hundred before he moved. Then he went aft and woke Marguerite.

* * *

She came up on deck in forty seconds, dressed, no questions wasted, which was one of the hundred reasons Okonjo would have followed her into a burning building. Dez took longer, arriving in a rain jacket over his underwear with a wrench in his fist, as though a wrench had ever settled anything.

"Say it again," Marguerite said. "Slowly."

"Patrol hull. No lights, no marks. Came down the east bank at maybe eight knots, throttled low. One at the helm, two or three under the canopy. He looked at us. I'd say he counted us."

"He didn't stop."

"He didn't stop."

Marguerite stood at the stern rail, looking back upriver into the fog as though she could rewind it. The Tern's wake had long since flattened. The anchor light swung on its little pendulum, orange, patient, absurd.

"Could be a police boat," Dez offered. "Could be anybody's."

"At two in the morning, unlit, unmarked, running quiet?" Okonjo said. "Police boats like being seen. That's most of what a police boat is."

Dez looked at him. "You're very awake for a man I've never seen before four a.m."

"Occupational hazard."

Marguerite turned from the rail. "Wake Ilse."

* * *

Ilse came up on deck already awake, which Okonjo noted and filed away. She'd been aboard eleven days now, and he had built a quiet catalogue of her habits, the way he built a chart of any new water: where she slept, when she slept, what she did in the hours she claimed to be sleeping. She slept light. She slept dressed. And on at least three nights when Okonjo had come off watch, he'd heard her moving softly near the stern, near the radio locker, near the crate of permits and equipment that was the entire reason for this voyage — though she'd never once asked what was in it, which was its own kind of strange. Everyone asked. Curiosity was the cheapest currency on a boat.

Now she stood at the rail with her arms crossed, looking downriver into the fog, and Okonjo gave his report a second time and watched her face while he did it.

She didn't look surprised. That was the thing he couldn't stop seeing. She looked like a woman confirming an appointment.

"You said patrol hull," she said. "Flat bottom, high bow, open cockpit?"

"That's right."

"Gray. No rub rail."

"Right."

She nodded slowly, and then she said — smoothly, easily, the way a person says something they've had time to prepare — "Concession security, probably. Halvern runs patrol launches on this reach. They're supposed to log with the river authority, but half the time they don't bother. They're not looking at us. They're running a perimeter on the escarpment reach. It's standard. I've seen it a dozen times."

"Perimeter against what?" Marguerite asked.

"Smugglers. Poachers. Survey teams that wander off their permits." A small shrug. "The concession is paranoid. It's a lot of money sitting on a lot of rock."

It all sounded reasonable. It all sounded, Okonjo thought, exactly like what a person would say if they'd already decided what to say. He couldn't have pointed to the seam in it. There was no seam. That was what bothered him — the answer came out finished, hemmed, ready to wear. Real answers were usually rougher.

"Did they see us?" Dez asked.

"Oh, they saw us." Ilse's voice was flat, matter-of-fact. "But we're a delivery boat with a contract. There's nothing to see. They'll log us as traffic and forget us by breakfast."

"Then why run dark?" Okonjo said. "If they're legitimate, why not light up and hail us? Ask our business?"

Ilse looked at him then, and for a moment — half a second, maybe less — something moved behind her eyes, some recalculation, some quick reweighing of him. Then it was gone, and she smiled, a small dry smile, and said, "Because it's two in the morning and hailing a stranger in the fog is how you get shot by a jumpy fisherman with an unlicensed gun. Running dark is courtesy out here. You know that. You ran delta patrol."

It was true. It was all true. Every piece of it was true, and it fit together, and it explained everything, and Okonjo stood there in the wet dark feeling like a man who'd been handed a receipt for something he hadn't bought.

Marguerite made the decision the way she always did — fast, clean, no theater. "We weigh anchor at first light. We run the reach in daylight where we can be seen, because being seen is our protection, and we keep the radio checks on schedule." She looked at Ilse. "Anything else I should know about how Halvern operates this stretch?"

"Nothing that'll help you," Ilse said, and turned to go below, and then stopped, and added, without turning back, "Okonjo. Good ears. Most people would've slept through it."

He watched her go down the companionway. Her footsteps on the ladder were even and unhurried, and he thought: *she knew that boat the way I know the Tern. She knew it by its sound.*

He didn't say it. Not yet. He wasn't sure yet whether the thing he'd seen in her face was a lie, or just a woman carrying a weight she hadn't decided to share. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and he was young enough to still believe the difference would keep.

* * *

First light came up gray and sourceless, the fog burning off in rags, and the river reassembled itself around them — the red clay banks, the drowned trees standing in the shallows like a congregation, the long green wall of the forest pressing in from both sides. The Kessing Escarpment was still two days upstream, a rumor of blue-gray ridgeline at the top of every chart. They ran the reach wide open, exactly as Marguerite had said, the Tern's old engine hammering, Dez up on the bow like a figurehead with a boathook, ready to fend off debris.

Okonjo navigated from the chart table under the small canopy, calling depths and bends, and all morning he also watched the water behind them.

At 0940 he saw it again.

Or he saw the shape of it — a wake, long and straight, holding their course a mile back, too disciplined to be driftwood, too steady to be a fisherman working a line. It sat in their wake like a hook in a fish's mouth, and when he altered the Tern's course ten degrees to test it, the wake altered with them, patient, unhurried, a thing that knew exactly where they were going and saw no reason to hurry.

He watched it for twenty minutes. Then the fog bank off the western point swallowed it, and it didn't come out.

He called Marguerite on the deck line. He told her. She came back to the chart table and stood over the plot with him, and they looked at it together — the Tern's neat pencil line running upstream, and the second line he'd drawn behind it, dotted, the track of something that had followed them for twenty minutes and then stopped existing.

"Twice in twelve hours," Marguerite said.

"Twice that I've seen."

"You think there's a third time you didn't?"

"I think," Okonjo said carefully, "that a boat like that doesn't come out twice by accident, and doesn't lose us by accident either. I think it wanted us to see it the second time. I think the first pass was a look, and the second was a message."

"Which is?"

"*We know where you are. Keep going. We're watching.*" He heard how it sounded as he said it, and made himself finish. "Or it wanted us to think that. I don't know which is worse."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Her thumb moved along the edge of the chart table, back and forth, a habit he'd catalogued too — the only fidget she allowed herself, the tell of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Get Ilse," she said.

But Ilse was already at the canopy's edge, and had been, Okonjo realized, for some unknown span of time. She was holding a mug of tea she wasn't drinking, and she was looking at the dotted line on the chart with an expression he could only call *recognition*, and she said, before either of them could speak:

"Still back there?"

"You saw it?" Marguerite said.

"I've been watching it since the point." She set the mug down. "It's the same hull. It'll shadow us to the middle station and then drop off — that's their pattern, they hand off to the next boat at the reach boundary. It's a monitoring run. Boring, bureaucratic, and not worth burning fuel to outrun, because you can't outrun a relay."

"You're very calm," Okonjo said.

"I'm very used to it." And she picked her tea back up and went forward to relieve Dez on the bow, moving with the boat's rhythm like she'd been born on it, and Okonjo looked at Marguerite and Marguerite looked at him, and neither of them said the thing.

The thing being: *used to it* was not what a river pilot said. *Used to it* was what a target said.

* * *

They anchored that night in a cut behind a mid-river island, engines cold, one lantern lit, and Okonjo took first watch because he knew he wouldn't sleep anyway. The fog came back with the dark, and the island became a smell of wet leaves and nothing, and the river talked to itself in the reeds.

At 2200, channel nine's scheduled check-in window came and went. The Kessing expedition's frequency stayed dead — had stayed dead for six nights now, and every night Marguerite called it anyway, the same call sign, the same protocol, her voice level and professional and, if you knew her, tight as a mooring line. Okonjo had the watch; the radio was his. He made the call at 2200 exactly. Static answered. He logged it, as he'd logged the five before it: *No response. Conditions normal.*

Except the conditions weren't normal, were they. There was a gray launch on their wake with a handoff schedule. There was a pilot aboard who knew the sound of its engine and the pattern of its patrols and the name of the company that owned it, and who had an answer ready for every question before the question was asked. There was a crate in the hold that Ilse never asked about, and a contract Marguerite wouldn't discuss, and a debt Okonjo had only ever seen in the shape of Voss's phone calls — cheerful, patient, the voice of a man who knew he'd already won.

He sat in the cockpit with the log open and, after a while, wrote a line he didn't transmit to anyone:

*Day 11. We are being followed by something we are pretending not to see, piloted by someone we are pretending to know. The expedition upstream has been silent for six days. Everyone on this boat is calm. I have decided to be suspicious of the calm.*

He read it back. It sounded like a boy's diary. He kept it anyway.

Toward midnight, footsteps on the deck — Ilse, wrapped in a blanket, coming to the cockpit rail. She stood beside him a while without speaking, watching the fog, and he let her, and the river filled the silence the way it always did, generously, with sound that meant nothing.

"You're angry with me," she said at last.

"I don't know you well enough to be angry with you."

"That's not a no."

Okonjo kept his eyes on the water. "You knew that launch. Not the way you said. You knew it the way I know the Tern — by its engine, by its habits, by its *schedule*. You had the answer finished before Dez stopped talking. I've navigated with liars before. Liars hesitate. You didn't hesitate."

Ilse was quiet for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and even, and it was the first time he'd heard anything in it that wasn't smooth.

"You're right," she said. "I knew the boat. I know it better than I've told anyone on this boat, and I'm not going to tell you tonight, on watch, in the fog, because what I know doesn't change what's behind us, and it might change how you look at what's ahead of us, and I need you looking ahead." She turned then, and looked at him straight, and her eyes in the lantern light were steady and tired and old in a way her face wasn't. "Ask me again when it matters. I promise you it will matter, and I promise I'll answer. That's the only honest thing I can give you tonight."

"And if I ask Marguerite to put you off at the middle station?"

"Then she'd be right to, and I'd go." No flinch. No performance. "But I'd tell you this first: that launch is not following us because of what we're carrying. It's following us because of what we're *near*. And the closer we get to the escarpment, the more you're going to wish you'd never heard of the Kessing expedition — and the more you'll be glad you did. Both things are true. Hold onto both."

She left him with that, footsteps fading down the deck, and Okonjo sat with the log and the lantern and the dead radio, and out in the fog, far downstream, so faint he might have invented it, he heard an engine lope once and go quiet — as if something out there had settled in for the night, patient as a debt, certain as a schedule, waiting to see which way they'd run at dawn.
