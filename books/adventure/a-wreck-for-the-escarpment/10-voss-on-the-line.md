## 10. Voss on the Line

The rain came in the night like a debt collector—no warning, no courtesy call, just there. Marguerite woke to it hammering the Tern's cabin roof and lay still for a moment, cataloguing sounds the way she always did before opening her eyes: Dez snoring in the forward berth, Okonjo's breathing steady and even in the aft, the river working against the hull with a low insistent mutter, and underneath all of it, faint and wrong, a ticking from the engine bay that hadn't been there yesterday.

She got up quietly, pulled on her jacket, and went out into the wet.

The anchorage was a bend of brown water below a clay bluff, the kind of overnight stop Ilse called "good enough" and Dez called "the river's front porch." Mist sat on the water in long gray ropes. The Tern rode at anchor with her running lights doubled because Okonjo had insisted after the fog two nights back, after the launch they'd seen and lost and never quite stopped talking about.

Marguerite stood under the canopy over the cockpit and let the rain drum on her shoulders for a while. Then she went below, opened the electronics locker, and checked the satellite terminal's signal strength for the fourth time since midnight.

Full bars. The storm hadn't taken the uplink yet.

Which meant Voss could reach her.

She'd been telling herself for three days that the silence from Channel Nine was weather. Monsoon season ate radio signals; everyone knew that. The Kessing expedition was camped somewhere along the escarpment reach where the cliffs played hell with line-of-sight, and their transmitter was old, and their schedule had always been loose. There were eleven innocent explanations and she'd recited most of them to herself while recalibrating the anemometers, while re-taping the crate seams, while doing every small competent task she could find to keep her hands busy and her mind off the arithmetic she couldn't stop running.

Eleven days left on the permits. Eleven days to deliver them or watch whatever Voss held over her turn from a leash into a noose.

The terminal chirped at 0640.

Not a text. A voice request. Priority flag. She looked at the caller ID even though she didn't need to—the routing prefix alone told her it was coming through a corporate relay, the kind of clean expensive connection that cost more per minute than Dez made in a day.

She let it ring four times. Long enough to seem busy. Then she answered.

"Brightwater."

"Marguerite!" Voss's voice came through warm and round as ever, undistorted by distance or weather, as if he were sitting across the cockpit table instead of wherever optimists sat when they weren't investing in people. "There she is. Do you know, I've been thinking about you? I tell people about you. I say, that Marguerite Zhou, she's the most reliable contractor I've worked with in fifteen years on this river. Reliable. It's not a word I use lightly."

"It's six-forty in the morning, Voss."

"Is it?" A laugh, genuine-sounding, which was the trick of him. Everything about Voss sounded genuine. His cheer had the texture of the real thing, and she'd learned over the years that this was precisely what made him dangerous. Liars who sounded like liars you could discount. Voss sounded like your favorite uncle calling with good news. "Time zones, my dear. I'm downstream where the sun is polite. How's the river treating you?"

"Wet."

"And the cargo?"

"Sealed. Dry. Untouched." She kept her voice flat and professional, gave him nothing to work with. "You'll have it when I deliver it. That was the contract."

"That was the contract," he agreed pleasantly. She heard ice move in a glass on his end. Six-forty in the morning, and the man was drinking something with ice in it. "And how is our delivery going? Making good time? I know the gorge has been lively this year."

"We're ahead of schedule."

"Mm." He let the syllable sit there, soft as silt settling. "That's what I'd expect from you. You know, I had a look at your route plan last night—I keep copies of everything, occupational habit—and I noticed you're past the second locks already. Very good. Very good indeed. Which puts you, what, four days from the escarpment reach if the water stays kind?"

"Three, if it doesn't."

"Efficient. See, this is why I invest in people like you." Ice again. "Now. The reason I'm calling. Two small things, both pleasant, I promise. First: the permits. You've got the concession permits sealed aboard, yes? Delivery documents for the Kessing survey team, Halvern Consolidated letterhead, all quite official. Those permits carry an expiry date. Eleven days from today. After that, the concession office voids them automatically and we have to start the whole process over—fees, filings, the ministry's charming bureaucracy. Nobody wants that. So: eleven days. I know you don't need reminding, but I'm a careful man, and careful men remind."

"I read the expiry date when you sealed the crate, Voss."

"Of course you did. Of course." Warmth again, undimmed by being caught stating the obvious. "Second thing. And this is just housekeeping, really. The expedition's daily check-in. Channel Nine, twenty-one hundred hours, standard protocol—you know how these government-adjacent surveys are, everything logged, everything scheduled. They've missed a few."

Marguerite didn't answer. She made herself not answer. On the Tern's deck above her head she heard Okonjo's footsteps cross toward the bow, heard him pause, heard the rain take his footsteps away.

"A few," she said finally.

"Four," Voss said cheerfully. "Maybe five now, depending on when their evening falls. Now, I want to be clear, because I know how imaginations work on the river—this is not unusual. Weather knocks out comms. Equipment fails. Dr. Kessing's team runs old gear on a shoestring budget, bless them, idealists, the whole lot of them. So nobody at Halvern is alarmed. We are patient people. But it does mean that your delivery is now the only link in the chain, doesn't it? The permits, the radio equipment—that lovely new crate we loaded at the locks, the one with the long-range set inside. Once your delivery arrives, their silence ends. Problem solved. Everyone happy." A pause, measured out like rope paying off a winch. "So you see why the eleven days matters so much to me. To us. The optimists."

"The optimists," Marguerite repeated.

"My associates. You met some of them. Cheerful crowd, aren't they? They ask after you constantly. Danner wanted to know if you'd fixed the leak in your bilge pump yet—he does follow your maintenance logs, he finds them soothing." He laughed again, and this time she heard what lived underneath the laugh, the way you hear current under calm water. "They're investors, Marguerite. All of us. We put money into things—boats, people, surveys—and we expect returns. That's all optimism is, really. The expectation of return. And right now, our return is sitting in a sealed crate on your boat, four days from where it needs to be, with eleven days on the clock. So. No pressure. Just clarity."

She stood in the cabin with the rain loud on the roof and did the thing she was best at, the thing she'd done her whole life: she measured the distance between what he'd said and what he meant.

He knew the expedition was silent. Not from her—she'd reported the missed check-ins to no one, told only her own crew, kept it off any channel. Which meant Halvern had its own listening posts, its own logs, its own quiet ways of knowing exactly what moved on this river. Which meant the trailing launch in the fog wasn't paranoia. Which meant—

"You sound like you expected them to go quiet," she said.

A beat of silence. The first one he'd allowed. In it she heard the glass set down on a hard surface, a small precise click.

"I sound," Voss said, and his warmth came back up like floodwater filling a channel, "like a man who reads weather reports. Storms knock out radios, Marguerite. It's monsoon season. I'd think a storm chaser of all people would give the sky the benefit of the doubt." Then, lighter, breezier, the uncle again: "Anyway! I haven't called to worry you. I called to encourage you. You're doing wonderfully. Keep the pace. Mind the gorge—it's running high, they say. And Marguerite?"

"What."

"If anything should delay you—if the boat gives trouble, if the crew gets sick, if the river simply decides it doesn't want you upstream—don't be a hero about it. Call me. We have resources. Launches, pilots, spare parts, whatever you need. We take care of our investments." The word landed softly and stayed. "We would hate for you to struggle alone out there. Struggling alone is how reliable people become unreliable."

"I don't struggle alone," she said. "I have a crew."

"I know you do. Dez and the boy. Good people, I'm sure. People like that depend on the person holding the contract, don't they? Whatever happens upstream, they come home on your decisions." Another pause, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "Make good ones. Eleven days. Talk soon."

The line went dead.

* * *

She stayed in the cabin a long time after the terminal dimmed. The rain kept on. Above her, Okonjo's footsteps came aft, paused outside the cabin door, and knocked once.

"You're up," he said through the wood.

"Come in."

He ducked down the companionway, hair wet, holding two mugs of the terrible coffee Dez brewed by the pound instead of the scoop. He handed her one and folded himself onto the chart bench, watching her face with the open attention he never bothered hiding. Nineteen years old and transparent as glass, that boy. It terrified her sometimes, how much he trusted what he saw in her.

"That was the relay prefix," he said. "Corporate. Downstream?"

"Voss."

Okonjo's jaw tightened. He'd been at the garage the day she signed the papers. He'd seen the cheerful men in their uncreased shirts, the ones who laughed too easily and asked after her bilge pump. "What did he want?"

"To remind me the permits expire in eleven days."

"That's not news."

"No." She turned the mug in her hands. The coffee steamed and smelled burnt and she drank it anyway. "The news is what else he knows. Okonjo. The check-ins. Channel Nine. We never broadcast that the expedition's gone silent. We discussed it on this boat, at night, on the low-power set. Nobody else."

Okonjo went very still. She watched him get there, watched the arithmetic land behind his eyes the way it had landed in hers—first denial, then the slower colder sum. "He said they'd missed check-ins."

"Five. He counted them for me. Cheerfully."

"But that means—" He stopped. Started again. "They're listening. Somebody's been listening the whole time. Or—" and here his voice dropped, because the other possibility was worse, "or they already knew. Before us. Before the fog. Maybe that launch wasn't following us. Maybe it was checking on us."

"Maybe."

"Marguerite." He set his mug down on the chart table with exaggerated care, the way he did when his hands wanted to shake and he didn't want them to. "Why does a finance man know how many nights a survey team has missed its radio call? Why does he count?"

Because the counting started before the silence did. Because the silence might not be weather. Because a man who invests in people also liquidates them, and the paperwork for that starts with knowing exactly when a thing stops reporting in. She didn't say any of it. She looked at the boy's face and thought: not yet. Not until I know more. Not until I can tell him something true that isn't just fear wearing a coat.

"He counts because that's his business," she said. "Counting. Returns. Deadlines." She stood, drained the coffee, and made herself smile, the professional version, the one she used at docks and customs offices. "Go wake Dez. Tell him I want that ticking noise in the engine bay looked at before we weigh anchor. And tell him to check the bilge twice. If Voss knows about the pump, I want the pump perfect."

Okonjo hesitated in the companionway, half-turned, and she saw the question he was carrying—*are we okay, are we safe, are you okay*—and chose, for now, not to ask it. "Eleven days," he said.

"Ten and a half. Go."

His footsteps went forward. A moment later she heard him thumping on the engine room hatch, heard Dez's muffled complaint, the two of them falling into their usual bickering rhythm, and the sound of it steadied her more than the coffee had.

* * *

Ilse was on the bluff.

Marguerite found her there when she went up to scout the day's run—a clay path through the wet grass, the mist burning off below, the river unspooling north toward the high country. Ilse stood with her hands in her pockets, watching the water the way she always did, like a woman reading a letter from someone she didn't trust.

"You were on the sat phone," Ilse said without turning. "Forty minutes ago. I saw the antenna light."

"Voss."

Something moved through Ilse's shoulders, quick and contained, gone before Marguerite could name it. "Your creditor."

"My leash." Marguerite came up beside her. Below, the Tern swung gently at her anchor, small and patched and stubborn on the brown water. "He knows the expedition's gone silent, Ilse. Five missed check-ins. He counted them. We never told anyone."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Her eyes stayed on the river, but Marguerite had spent enough days beside her now to read the difference between her silences. This one was working.

"Companies like that keep listening stations," Ilse said at last. "At the locks, at the fuel depots. Everything that moves on this river gets logged somewhere."

"I know. I've run this river ten years. But logging traffic isn't the same as counting a dead man's heartbeats." Marguerite turned to face her. "Voss wasn't worried. That's what I keep coming back to. A man whose investment is stranded in monsoon country with no radio contact should be worried. He was *pleased*. Like the silence was on schedule."

Now Ilse turned. Up close, in the gray morning light, her face had the stillness of deep water, and Marguerite thought—not for the first time—that she had never met anyone who hid so much behind so little effort. "What do you want to do?"

"I want to know what you're not telling me."

The words came out flatter than she'd intended, and she let them lie there between them rather than soften them. Ilse didn't flinch. She looked at Marguerite for a long three seconds, and then, deliberately, she looked back at the river.

"I'm telling you what I know when I know it matters," Ilse said. "Right now what matters is that you have ten days, a leaking boat, a crew that trusts you, and a delivery that somebody upstream may not want delivered. That's enough to steer by."

"It's not."

"It's what there is." Ilse's voice stayed level, but something in it had hardened, a seam closing. "Ask me again when it matters more. I promise you'll know."

Marguerite studied her—the forged papers she pretended not to have noticed, the piloting skill too good for any licensed reach, the way she'd known the escarpment channels without consulting a single chart. Every instinct she owned said the woman was a locked room. Every practical bone in her body said she needed her anyway.

Below them, Dez's head emerged from the engine hatch, and his voice carried up across the water, cracked with indignation: "Who ran this pump dry? Who? This bearing's cooked, this is criminal, this is a crime against machinery—"

Okonjo's laughter followed, bright and unguarded, and for a moment the morning was just a morning, and the Tern was just a boat, and they were just four people on a river with a job to finish.

Then the mist lifted off the far bank, and Marguerite saw it: a smudge of wake on the water downstream, a kilometer back, moving when nothing else was moving. It held their speed. It held their line. And then, as she watched, it slid behind the bluff's shoulder and was gone.

Ilse had seen it too. Marguerite knew by the way she'd stopped pretending to look at the river.

"Eleven days," Marguerite said quietly. "He gave me eleven days, and something's already following us."

She turned and started down the clay path toward the boat, toward her crew, toward the crate she was beginning to understand she might be carrying for entirely the wrong side. Behind her, Ilse stood on the bluff a moment longer, motionless, watching the empty water where the wake had been—as if she recognized it.
