## 11. What Ilse Knows

The ledger page sat on the chart table between them like a thing that had crawled out of a fire and refused to die.

Ilse had watched Marguerite pick it up that morning, back at the abandoned camp, watched her thumb the charred edge and read the impossible numbers twice, and Ilse had known then that the question was coming. She'd had six years to prepare an answer and she still hadn't managed to make it simple. So she stood at the Tern's stern rail with the river sliding brown and heavy beneath them and waited for Marguerite to say the word *forged* out loud.

Marguerite didn't say it. She was better than that.

"You knew the camp was empty before we got there," Marguerite said. "You knew what we'd find."

"I knew what I'd find," Ilse said. "I didn't know what you'd find."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have left."

The monsoon had settled in for the evening, a steady grey drumming on the canopy, the kind of rain that didn't rage so much as *persist*, wearing the world down grain by grain. Dez was below decks with a tarp and a bucket, arguing with the seam that wouldn't hold. Okonjo was in the bow under a poncho, running their position against a chart that had stopped matching the river two days ago. Which left the stern, and the two of them, and the page.

Marguerite turned it over. The burned half was a mirror image of itself, ink ghosting through from the other side, elevations stacked in a surveyor's neat hand: *bench 4, 212 m; bench 5, 208 m; bench 6, 211 m.* Benches on the Kessing Escarpment that could not be two hundred meters above the river because the escarpment's lip, where anyone with eyes could see it, rose barely ninety. Ilse had stood on that lip once. She had stood there with her brother's field notes in her pocket and understood, in one cold drop of certainty, that the numbers in the concession filing were not mistakes.

"You've seen the escarpment," Marguerite said. It wasn't a question either.

"Once. Years ago."

"And you knew the figures were wrong."

"I knew the figures were *impossible*," Ilse said. "There's a difference. Wrong is a mistake. Impossible is a decision."

Marguerite set the page down and flattened it with both palms, the way she did with charts when a squall was coming, pinning the world in place. Ilse had been watching her for two weeks now, long enough to know the habits. The woman measured everything—distance to the next bend, liters in the tank, hours of fuel, the exact angle of a cloud. She did it the way other people prayed. Ilse recognized it because she'd once done it herself, before she'd learned that some things couldn't be measured into safety.

"Say it plainly," Marguerite said. "No more of this honest-adjacent business. What is Halvern Consolidated, and what did they do to that camp?"

Ilse looked downstream, where the river bent north into the drowned forest, and made her calculation. Not whether to tell the truth—she'd decided that at the locks, the moment she'd seen the permit seals—but how much of it. Truth was like ballast. Too little and you capsized. Too much and you sat so low in the water you couldn't maneuver at all.

"Halvern Consolidated is a resources holding company," she said. "On paper. In practice it's a machine for turning government concessions into cash. They find a resource, they file a survey, they secure subsidies and development guarantees against the survey, and then—" she spread her hands, "—the resource turns out to be smaller than advertised, or wetter, or deeper, or in the wrong country entirely. But by then the money's been drawn down and the subsidiary dissolves and the men who signed the filings are sipping something cold in a country with an extradition treaty in their favor."

"And the Kessing concession?"

"The Kessing concession is the largest one they've ever filed in this basin. Hydrological surveys claiming the escarpment plateau is a shallow basin over impermeable bedrock. Ideal for a dam-and-reservoir scheme. The surveys were filed eighteen months ago. The subsidies were approved eleven months ago. The expedition—" Ilse paused, and made herself finish it, "—the expedition was commissioned by the basin authority as an independent verification. Standard procedure. Halvern expected a rubber stamp. Instead they got Dr. Amara Kessing."

Marguerite's jaw tightened. "You know her."

"Of her." That much was safe. That much was true. "Everyone in this basin's survey community knows her. She's the one who caught the Meridian Delta filings inflated by forty percent. She doesn't rubber stamp. Halvern would have known that. Which means they didn't hire her to verify. They hired her to *delay*. A six-month independent review buys a lot of subsidy disbursement."

"And she found the forgery."

"She found it before her boots were dry." Ilse let herself lean on the rail, let the rain run off her chin. "You saw the benches in that ledger. Two hundred and ten meters. The plateau isn't a basin, Marguerite. It's a shelf of fractured limestone over karst channels that drain straight into the river system. You put a reservoir on it and the water doesn't sit. It *leaves*. Through every crack in the escarpment. The dam would hold nothing, the reservoir would drain in a season, and the collapse would take the water table for a hundred kilometers downstream with it. Every village on the middle reach. Every well. Every field."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. The rain filled the silence with its patient static.

"That's not a bad survey," she said finally. "That's a catastrophe with a filing number."

"Now you understand why the camp was slashed and the instruments are gone."

* * *

Marguerite pushed off the rail and paced the length of the stern, three steps each way, the Tern's old deck boards complaining under her boots. Ilse let her pace. She knew what it was to need your body moving while your mind did arithmetic it didn't want to finish.

"Say I believe you," Marguerite said. "The surveys are forged. Kessing found proof. Halvern found out she found it. Then what—she ran?"

"She didn't run." Ilse heard the edge come into her own voice and didn't bother to sand it down. "Kessing doesn't run. She went *up*. That's the point of her, that's the whole woman—she doesn't accept a finding until she's stood on the ground it's about. The burned page is a copy of Halvern's filed survey. She'd have taken her own instruments to the escarpment to take her own readings. If she's alive, that's where she is. At the top, with her data, doing it properly."

"If she's alive."

"If she's alive."

Marguerite stopped pacing. She looked at the chart table, at the sealed permit tube lying beside it—Halvern's blue wax, Halvern's ribbon, the delivery that was supposed to be the whole of Ilse's employment—and Ilse watched her look at it and watched the woman's whole economy of a face do its accounting. Marguerite Brightwater did not wear her thoughts, but she wore her *arithmetic*. You could see the columns.

"Those permits," Marguerite said slowly. "You knew the seals. At the locks. You looked at them like you'd seen a ghost wearing a familiar coat."

Ilse had known this question was coming too, and she had prepared her half-answer for it the way she'd prepared all the others: true, verifiable, and hollow at the center.

"Halvern's seals are distinctive," she said. "Blue wax, the twin chevrons, a registration code stamped into the ribbon. They use the same seals on every subsidiary filing. I've seen them before."

"Where?"

"On documents that crossed my desk. Years ago." She kept her voice level, kept her hands still on the rail. "I used to work in survey administration. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before I stopped."

Marguerite studied her with those flat grey eyes that took measurements and filed them somewhere Ilse couldn't reach. It was, Ilse reflected, the look of a woman who had been lied to by professionals and had developed a taste for the exact texture of a half-truth. She wasn't buying the whole package. She was deciding how much of it she needed.

"You said at the locks you knew the escarpment reach," Marguerite said. "Every sandbar, every dead channel. You said it like a pilot. You're talking now like a clerk. Which are you?"

"Both. Neither." Ilse turned her palms up, let the rain hit them. "I'm a river pilot who used to file other people's numbers, and I got out, and now I'm on your boat because your boat is going where I need to go. That's the truth of me, Captain. It's not all of the truth of me. But it's all I'm giving you tonight."

"Why not all of it?"

"Because all of it is mine," Ilse said, "and I've had very little of mine for a very long time. Ask me again when we're through the gorge. If we're through the gorge."

* * *

Marguerite didn't answer right away. She went below, and Ilse heard her speaking low with Dez about the seam, and then she came back up with two mugs of the terrible chicory they drank instead of coffee and handed one over without ceremony. It was, Ilse understood, a treaty offer. Not friendship. Terms.

"Here's where I am," Marguerite said. "I have a contract. Permits and radio equipment, delivered to the Kessing expedition, signed for by Dr. Kessing or her designated deputy. That contract pays off a debt I am not going to describe to you, and that debt has people attached to it who smile too much. If the expedition is scattered or dead, the contract is void, and I'm hauling a boat full of nothing back down a river that's trying to drown us, and the people who smile too much don't accept void contracts gracefully."

"I know," Ilse said. "I've met the people who smile."

Marguerite's eyes came up sharply. "You said you'd never—"

"I said I'd seen Halvern's seals on documents. I did." Ilse drank the chicory. It was terrible. She was grateful for it. "I didn't say I'd met the men. I'm telling you now: I know the type. The cheerful ones. The investors in people. Halvern doesn't hire thugs, Captain. It hires optimists. It's worse."

"So if I deliver these permits to nobody—"

"You deliver them to the deputy, if there's a deputy. Fen Okafor, if she's alive. She was Kessing's second. If there's no one, you document the attempt and the contract's terms protect you." Ilse paused. "And if there's a Kessing at the top of that escarpment with a case full of verified data, then those permits are worth exactly the paper they're printed on, and the radio equipment is worth a great deal more, and you'll want to think about which of those things Halvern actually sent you upriver to deliver."

The silence that followed had weight. Ilse let it sit. She had learned, in six years of living under a name that wasn't hers, that the most dangerous thing you could hand another person was a thought they hadn't had yet. You couldn't take it back. You couldn't control what they built with it. You could only watch.

Marguerite looked at the permit tube. She looked at it for a long time.

"You're saying the delivery might be the weapon," she said.

"I'm saying Halvern doesn't spend money on river deliveries out of generosity. That radio crate is a Halvern radio crate, on a Halvern frequency plan, going to a Halvern-controlled site. Think about what a company that forges surveys would want a radio *for*."

"Then what do we—"

"Deliver it," Ilse said. "Deliver all of it. Exactly as contracted. And then be standing next to the person who signs for it, and watch what they do."

* * *

Okonjo came aft at dusk with the chart board under his arm and rain streaming off his poncho, young and earnest and so obviously listening that Ilse almost pitied him. Almost.

"Captain," he said. "The middle station's forty kilometers upstream if the chart's right. If the chart's wrong, it's anywhere."

"The chart's wrong," Ilse said. "The station's there. It's on the outside of the big bend, under the dead fig tree—you'll see the mast before you see the building. Forty-one kilometers. Call it five hours at this water."

Okonjo blinked at her, then made his note, and Ilse watched Marguerite watch her do it. The small test. The small proof. Every accurate kilometer was a coin in the trust account, and Ilse was spending them deliberately, one at a time, because she had learned that trust built in bulk collapsed in bulk.

"Five hours," Marguerite said. "And then?"

"And then we find out who's left," Ilse said. "And then, Captain, you decide what kind of courier you are."

Marguerite's chin came up a degree. "I know what kind I am."

"No," Ilse said, and kept her voice gentle, because it needed to be, "you know what kind you've *had* to be. Those are different animals. I'll see you at the middle station."

Marguerite held her gaze for a long moment, and Ilse met it and did not flinch and did not fill the silence with anything she'd regret. Then Marguerite picked up the burned ledger page, folded it along its charred edge with a care that bordered on tenderness, and slid it inside her jacket, against her chest, where the rain couldn't get it.

"Half-truths," Marguerite said, turning to go below. "That's what you're paying me in. I'm keeping count."

"I'd expect nothing less," Ilse said to her back. "You'd be bad at this job if you didn't."

She stood at the rail long after Marguerite had gone, drinking the terrible chicory, watching the drowned forest slide past in the rain. Somewhere behind them, she was nearly certain, a launch was running without lights. Somewhere upstream, a woman with a case full of the truth was feverish on a goat path or dead in a ravine. And somewhere in Ilse's chest, in the locked room where her brother's name lived, something that had been still for six years had begun, very quietly, to move.

Five hours to the middle station. She'd measured it honestly.

It was the only thing she'd measured honestly in years, and she suspected Marguerite knew that too.
