## 12. The Gorge Transit

Dez heard the gorge before he saw it.

It wasn't a sound so much as a pressure—a low, continuous growl coming up through the hull of the Tern, through the deck plates, through the soles of his boots, into his knees. He'd spent eleven years around engines and he knew what machinery in distress sounded like, and the river ahead sounded like every bad bearing he'd ever replaced, multiplied by a thousand, and it was getting louder.

"That's not a rapid," he said. "That's a grudge."

Nobody laughed. Okonjo stood at the bow rail with the binoculars, and Marguerite was at the wheel beside Ilse, and both of them had the particular stillness Dez had learned to hate—the stillness of people doing arithmetic they don't want to finish.

"River's up another half meter since this morning," Okonjo called back. "The chart shows the gorge running maybe two hundred meters wide. It's not. It's maybe ninety, and there's a rock garden in the middle."

"Show me." Ilse took the binoculars without asking, and Dez watched her face while she looked. She didn't go pale. She didn't go tight. She did something worse: she nodded, slow, like she was greeting an old acquaintance she owed money to.

"I've run it three times," Ilse said. "Twice at this level. Never higher."

"And never in a boat with a patched seam and a cargo hold full of radio crates," Dez said.

"No," Ilse agreed. "Never in this boat."

Dez went aft and opened the engine hatch, mostly to have something to do with his hands. The port engine was running hot—not dangerously, not yet, but the raw-water intake was sucking silt in this brown flood, and the impeller was chewing grit with every revolution. He cleaned the strainer, checked the belt, said a small private word to the engine the way he always did, the way his grandmother had taught him: you talk to what carries you, or it stops carrying you.

The Tern was carrying them. That was the thing people forgot about boats. She wasn't a vehicle, she was a promise, and every promise has a limit.

Marguerite came back to him while he was wiping down the housing. She crouched at the hatch edge, out of the spray, and her voice was low enough that only he could hear it.

"If I told you to turn around," she said, "would the engines take it?"

Dez thought about it honestly, because she'd asked him honestly. "Turning in that current isn't an engine question. It's a suicide question. Once we're in the throat, there's no turning. There's only through."

"That's what Ilse said."

"Ilse also said she's run this twice at flood stage, which is either a boast or a confession, and I haven't decided which."

Marguerite almost smiled. It didn't reach her eyes. "Bail everything that floats. Tie down everything that doesn't. And Dez—" she put her hand flat on the engine block, like she was taking its pulse, "—keep her alive."

"Her'll do her best," Dez said. "Her always does."

* * *

The gorge swallowed them at 0940 by Okonjo's watch, though Dez didn't need a watch to mark it. The light changed. The walls came up on both sides—black wet stone streaked with mineral stain, close enough that the overhangs dripped on the deck—and the river, which had been a broad brown animal all week, became something with a spine. Standing waves. Holes you could park a bus in. Water moving so fast and so much of it that the surface didn't look like water anymore. It looked like muscle.

Ilse took the wheel and Marguerite gave it to her without a word, which told Dez everything about how scared Marguerite actually was. She didn't give the wheel to anyone. Not to him, not to Okonjo, not in six years, not in weather that would make a sea captain weep. She gave it to the stranger.

"Dez," Ilse said, eyes on the water, "port engine full, starboard at half. I'm going to ask for asymmetric power. When I ask, I mean now, not soon."

"Understood."

"Okonjo—bow. Call the water for me. I can't see the holes until I'm on top of them. You can."

Okonjo scrambled forward. Dez saw the kid's hands shaking as he gripped the rail, and saw him grip harder until they stopped, which was the whole of Okonjo in a single gesture, and it was why Dez had decided, somewhere over the last week without admitting it, that the kid was going to be all right.

The first rapid took them before anyone called it.

The Tern climbed a standing wave the size of a chapel, hung there with her propellers singing, and came down the back side so hard Dez's teeth clicked. Water came over the bow in a single green-brown sheet and found every gap in the deck. He heard the crates shift in the hold and swore and threw himself down the ladder to check the lashings.

The hold was ankle-deep already. Not a leak—a flood. The bilge pump was running, he could hear it, but the pump was rated for rain and spray, not for a river coming aboard by the ton.

"Crates are holding!" he shouted up the companionway. "Boat is drinking!"

"Then pour her a little less!" Marguerite shouted back, and even now, even here, the woman joked. Dez had decided long ago that the jokes were how she kept from screaming, and he'd decided to let her have them.

The second rapid was worse because Ilse chose the line herself, no call from Okonjo, threading between two holes with maybe a meter of margin on either side, and Dez, hauling himself back up to the engine bay, felt the hull kiss rock once—a long grinding shudder down the starboard side, like the river running its hand along the boat to take inventory.

"Starboard hull!" he yelled.

"I felt it!" Ilse yelled back. "It let go. It's not done with us yet."

"That's not comforting!"

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate."

Dez got to the starboard side and put his ear to the inner planking. He heard it before he saw it—a tick, tick, tick, water finding a seam. Then he saw it: a hairline crack in the third plank up, weeping in a fine bright thread that thickened as he watched. Not a hole. Not yet. A crack under stress, in a hull under stress, in a river that was going to keep asking.

He grabbed the epoxy kit. Marine epoxy, two-part, the kind you mix in your palm and press into the wound and pray over. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the deal he'd made with the world somewhere in his childhood, that his hands would never shake no matter what the rest of him was doing.

"Marguerite!" he called. "I've got a crack, starboard, third plank. I can slow it. I can't stop it. If we take one more hard hit on this side—"

"I heard you!"

The third rapid was the one that nearly took Okonjo.

* * *

It came at them around a bend, unannounced, because the bend itself was the announcement—a right-angle turn against a wall of stone where the current piled up and doubled back on itself, and Ilse saw it a half-second too late to do anything but commit.

"Full power," she said, very quietly, and Dez gave her everything both engines had.

The Tern hit the seam between the outflow and the backwash and the whole world went sideways. Dez lost his feet, caught the engine housing, and looked up in time to see Okonjo—bow watch, rail grip, doing everything right—take a wave across the back and go over the rail like he'd been plucked.

No scream. That was the thing Dez would remember afterward, the thing that came back to him at night: the kid didn't scream. The river took him the way it takes everything, without any change in its voice.

"MAN OVERBOARD!" Dez was already moving, throwing a coil of line off the stern, and Marguerite was at the rail with a life ring, and the river had Okonjo now, rolling him, showing him to them and taking him back, one dark hand and one flash of orange from the vest, one hand and one flash—

"Ilse!" Marguerite's voice cracked across the gorge. "Can you—"

"I can put him on the eddy line," Ilse said. Her voice was flat and fast and completely without fear, and Dez understood in that instant that this was what she was for. This was why she'd barged aboard at the locks, why she knew the reach, why she'd nodded at the gorge like an old debt. Not the delivery. Not the pay. This. "Dez—starboard engine full, port idle, on my mark. I'm going to swing the stern across the current and backwater. The eddy behind that point rock holds. He'll drift into it if the river gives him back."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we've lost him and I've lied about my piloting, and you'll have plenty of time to discuss it after." She didn't look at Dez. She didn't look at the water where Okonjo had been. She looked at the point rock, the tongue of foam, the seam—and she read it, all of it, in the time it took Dez's heart to beat twice. "Mark."

Dez slammed the throttles. The Tern slewed, stern-first across the current, and for one horrible moment they were broadside to the flood and Dez was certain the river was going to roll them like a log, and then the stern bit into the eddy and the boat swung around like a door closing, and there—there, in the slack water behind the point, an orange flash, an arm, a head.

"He's in the eddy!" Marguerite was already throwing the ring, a beautiful long arc, and Okonjo got a hand through it on the second try, or maybe the first, Dez couldn't see for the spray.

They hauled him in over the stern. He came aboard coughing the river back up, half a liter of brown water, and then he was on his hands and knees on the deck, shaking, alive, and Dez grabbed him by the vest and held on, because his hands were steady but the rest of him was doing something else entirely.

"You," Dez said, "are grounded. No more bow. You ride in the hold with the crates."

Okonjo looked up. Water ran off his chin. "The crates," he said, and coughed, "are terrible conversationalists."

Dez laughed. It came out of him sideways, ugly and relieved, and he didn't care.

* * *

They ran the last of the gorge in ninety minutes that felt like a season.

Ilse didn't relax once. Dez watched her from the engine hatch, epoxy drying on his hands, and he watched the way she read the water—not the way a pilot reads a chart, but the way a person reads a face of someone they've loved and fought with. Every wave, she was already past it. Every hole, she'd decided about it before it decided about them. Twice more the Tern touched stone, and both times it was a whisper instead of a scream, and both times Ilse had already corrected before Dez could call it.

The crack in the third plank wept the whole way. Dez kept his thumb against it like a doctor taking a pulse that was getting away from him. The epoxy held. The epoxy mostly held. The epoxy held the way a promise holds when both people keep working at it.

And then, all at once, the walls fell back, and the river spread out, and the growl faded behind them into ordinary water noise, and the sky was just the sky again, enormous and gray and raining, and nobody said anything for a full minute.

Marguerite cut the throttles back to a cruise. The Tern settled. The bilge pump, for the first time in an hour, started winning.

Okonjo sat wrapped in a blanket, gray-faced, holding a mug of tea he hadn't drunk. Marguerite stood at the rail looking back at the gorge mouth, and Dez stood next to her, and for a while the three of them watched the black walls drip.

"Ilse." Marguerite said it without turning. "Where did you learn to read water like that?"

A pause from the wheel. Dez looked over. Ilse's hands were still on the spokes, and they were, for the first time since the locks, not entirely steady.

"My brother taught me," she said. "On this river. He used to say the gorge doesn't forgive and doesn't punish—it just tells you the truth about your boat, your crew, and yourself, all at once, and charges by the minute."

"Your brother," Marguerite said. "Where is he now?"

The rain ticked on the deck. The bilge pump chugged. Dez watched Ilse's face do something small and closed, like a door in a house he'd only seen one room of.

"Downstream," Ilse said. "In a manner of speaking."

She turned the wheel and brought the Tern around the next bend, and Dez went below to check the plank, and that was when he saw it—when he lifted the crate with the permit seals to get at the bilge fitting beneath it, and the crate shifted, and the seal stamp inside rocked against its case, and he saw the mark pressed into the wax on the manifest copy taped to the lid.

He knew that mark. Not from the permits. From six years ago, from a news sheet he'd read in a laundromat in Port Meridian and never forgotten, because the story had been about an auditor who went up the Kessing reach to check a company's numbers and never came back, and the mark on the auditor's employer's letterhead had been a stylized H with a river running through it.

The same mark was on their permits. The same mark was on the crate he was holding.

Dez put the crate down very carefully, the way you put down something that might be listening, and stood in the dim hold with the bilge water slopping around his boots, and did not say anything to anyone. Not yet.

Above him, through the deck, he could hear Ilse's voice, low, giving Marguerite the line for the next reach, and Marguerite's voice answering, and for the first time since the locks, Dez found himself wondering which of them was keeping a bigger secret—and which secret was going to crack first, the plank, or the crew.
