## 13. The Middle Station

The station appeared out of the rain like a rumor.

Okonjo called it first, from the bow, one hand shading his eyes even though there was no sun to shade against. "Smoke," he said. "Or steam. Something."

I took the binoculars off the hook by the wheel and found it: a long low roofline on the left bank where the river bent north, corrugated iron gone the color of rust and old blood, a mast leaning at an angle that said *nobody's maintained me in years*. The Middle Station. It was marked on Ilse's chart with a pencil circle so old the graphite had faded to gray. On the company chart Dez had found sealed in the radio crate, it was marked in red ink with a word I didn't like: *DECOMMISSIONED*.

"Decommissioned stations don't have smoke," Dez said. He'd come up from the engine well with resin on his knuckles and a look on his face like a man who'd been arguing with a boat all morning and losing. "Decommissioned stations don't have anything."

"Could be squatters," Okonjo said.

"Could be the people who slashed those tents," Dez said.

Nobody answered that, because nobody wanted to. We'd been running on the burned ledger page for two days now—Kessing's impossible elevations folded into my breast pocket, riding over my heart like a second debt—and every mile upstream had made the silence on Channel Nine heavier. Dead air has a texture. After enough nights of calling into it, you start to feel it pressing back.

Ilse stood at the wheel beside me, hands light on the spokes, reading water the way other people read faces. She didn't look at the station. She looked at the current approaching it, the eddy line peeling off the point, the depth of the green under the surface.

"Backwater on the left will take us right up to their dock," she said. "If there's still a dock."

"And if someone's watching the front?"

"Then we're already seen." She shrugged. "The river doesn't do surprise, Marguerite. It only does sooner or later."

I looked at her. Six days aboard and she was still a locked room to me—a pilot who knew this reach too well, who'd lied smoothly about the launch in the fog, who watched Halvern's permit seals the way you watch a dog you've been bitten by before. But she'd run us through the gorge when the Tern cracked a plank and Okonjo went half over the side, and she hadn't asked for a single thing except to keep going upstream. People show you who they are in rapids. Everything else is negotiation.

"Take us in," I said.

* * *

The dock was still there, mostly. Half of it had collapsed into the water and the other half sagged under a skin of green mold, but the pilings were ironbark and ironbark doesn't quit just because the paperwork did. Ilse brought the Tern alongside with a touch so gentle the hull barely kissed the timbers. Dez threw a line around a piling and made fast, then stood there breathing through his mouth.

"Smell that?" he said.

"Rain," Okonjo said.

"Under the rain." Dez's nostrils flared. "Woodsmoke. Old woodsmoke. And—" He stopped, and his superstitious face went more superstitious than usual. "Cooking fat. Somebody cooked here. Recently."

I climbed down onto the dock with the crowbar from the tool locker because I'm not proud, and because a crowbar is honest about what it's for. The station resolved as we came closer: a main hall with most of its windows boarded, a smaller building that might once have been a lab, a fuel shed with its doors hanging open on emptiness, and a radio mast guyed with wire that sang a low note in the wind. There was a generator housing, open, the machine stripped to its block. Whoever left had left methodically. Whoever stayed had stayed quietly.

On the wall beside the main door, someone had painted over the old sign. Underneath, showing through the new gray paint in ghost letters, I could read: **HALVERN CONSOLIDATED — MIDDLE RIVER STATION — KESSING SURVEY SUPPORT**. Over it, in the same gray, nothing. Just paint. An erasure done carefully, by hand, with time to spare.

"Ilse," I said. "You knew this place."

She was looking at the painted-over sign with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "Everyone on this river knew it. It was the halfway house for the whole survey. Boats stopped here, mail stopped here, paychecks stopped here." A pause. "It employed forty people, once. Maybe more."

"And now?"

"Now it's decommissioned." She said the word like she was tasting something spoiled.

We went in through the main hall because the door stood open six inches and because standing outside in the rain deciding felt worse than going in. Inside: dust, and the smell Dez had caught, faint but real, of cooking. Rows of bunk frames stripped bare. A long table with chairs pushed back from it—not stacked, not stored, just pushed back, the way you leave a table when you stand up in a hurry. On the table, a mug with tea gone to black sludge at the bottom. A paperback swollen with damp. And a map of the escarpment reach, weighted at four corners with stones, with a route penciled up the tributary and a single word written across the summit plateau in a small, furious hand:

**RE-VERIFY.**

"Fen's handwriting," Ilse said quietly.

"You know Fen?"

"I know of her. Dr. Kessing's deputy. Geomorphologist." Ilse's finger hovered over the penciled route without touching it. "They say she can read sediment like a ledger. Which, given what we found in that camp, is exactly the wrong skill to have around Halvern."

Dez had drifted toward the back of the hall while we talked, and now he made a sound—not a word, just a sound, low in his throat. He was crouched by the cold stove, and he was pointing without touching, at the floor beside it.

Blood. Old blood, darkened to the color of the rusted roof, in a smear maybe a meter long, and drag marks leading away from it toward the back door. Someone had tried to mop it up. Someone had run out of mop, or time, or both.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the iron roof like fingers getting impatient.

"Channel Nine," Okonjo said suddenly. "Every night for eleven nights. Nobody answered. Not 'we're busy,' not 'stand by.' Nothing." His young voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they're carrying something heavy for the first time. "This is why. This is what happened to them."

"We don't know that," I said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left my mouth.

"Marguerite." He looked at me, and I remembered him at sixteen, begging to crew for me, reciting my own storm numbers back at me like scripture. "There's blood on the floor and a table full of people who left in a hurry and a word on a map that says somebody went up that mountain alone. We know that."

He was right. That was the worst part. He was twenty-two years old and he was right, and I had signed papers in a bright office with cheerful men, and somewhere between that office and this bloodstain the delivery had changed shape without my agreeing to it.

"Spread out," I said. "Careful. Dez, you're with me. Okonjo, stay with the boat—if anyone comes down that river, you blow the horn and cast off, no heroics. Ilse—"

"Outside perimeter," she said. "I'll walk it." She was already moving, and I noticed she took the side away from the back door, away from the blood, and told myself it was tactics and not avoidance.

* * *

The back rooms were a laboratory once. Benches, sinks, shelving with labeled jars gone cloudy. Here the hurry was different—the main hall had been abandoned in a rush, but this room had been searched. Drawers pulled fully out and set on the floor. Cabinets opened and emptied. A filing cabinet lying on its back with its drawers jimmied, the lock punched clean through. Whatever they'd wanted, they'd taken all of it, and they'd been thorough about making sure nothing paper remained.

Except.

Behind the reagent shelf, wedged flat where a bottle had fallen forward to hide it, there was a notebook. Small, waterproof cover, the kind survey crews carry. I got it out with two fingers like it might bite.

Most of the pages were routine—daily readings, flow rates, turbidity logs, in a cramped left-slanting hand. Then, three-quarters through, the entries changed. Shorter. Faster. The pen pressed harder.

*Inspection team arrived unannounced. Six men, company livery, "credential review." Voss's name on the authorization. They want Kessing's raw files. AK refused. Argument in the hall.*

*I copied the discrepancy pages. Elevations cannot be reconciled. Either our instruments are wrong or their surveys were never done. AK says instruments are calibrated weekly. She would know.*

*They took the boat. They say the team is being "relocated downstream for safety." Fen and I are not relocating. AK is going up to the escarpment to re-verify against the benchmark. If she's right, the concession is built on paper and the paper is a lie.*

*Last entry. They came back at night. No livery this time. M—*

That was where it ended. Mid-word. Mid-letter. I sat down on an overturned crate and held the notebook and listened to the rain and understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives, that I was holding a dead man's or woman's last sentence, and that the sentence ended with the men coming back.

"M," Dez said behind me. He'd come in silent, which for a man his size was a professional accomplishment. "Marguerite. Back door. You need to see this."

I followed him, notebook in my pocket now, next to the ledger page. Two documents, riding together over my heart. I was starting to collect them whether I meant to or not.

The back door opened onto a lean-to woodshed, and beyond that a strip of cleared ground, and beyond that the green wall of the monsoon forest. In the woodshed, firewood stacked with care. Against the far wall, a pallet of blankets. A tin plate with old grease. A candle stub. And pinned to the wall above the pallet, a strip of mirror glass angled to catch the view downriver through a gap in the boards.

Somebody lived here. Somebody hiding, watching the river approach, sleeping with a sightline to the water.

"Somebody's been using this," Dez whispered. "Days. Maybe a week."

"The smoke," I said. "The cooking fat."

As if the words summoned it, there was a crash inside the station—a bench going over, a body moving fast—and then Okonjo's voice, cracking upward with alarm: "MARGUERITE!"

We ran.

* * *

She came out of the lab's side room like an animal out of a culvert—all elbows and desperation, a machete in one hand, and she had Okonjo backed against the doorframe with the blade up before any of us could get a word out. She was small, wiry, hollowed down to wire and gristle, with a week of hunger on her and something worse than hunger in her eyes. Her left leg was bound in strips of bedsheet gone brown at the thigh, and she was favoring it hard, weight thrown onto the good leg, which meant she couldn't run, which meant she'd decided she wasn't going to.

"Don't," she said. "Don't come near me. I'll cut. I swear I'll cut."

"Fen," Ilse said from the doorway behind us, calm as glass. "Fen Adeyemi. It's Ilse. You remember me. I ran barges past this station two seasons ago—you corrected my chart of the middle shoals. You said my draft marks were optimistic."

The machete wavered. Fen's eyes went from face to face—mine, Dez's, Okonjo's trembling one, Ilse's steady one—and I watched her do the arithmetic of survival, counting strangers, weighing blades, hunting for the trap.

"There's no inspection team," I said slowly. "No company men. We're a delivery boat. Tern, out of the coast. We were carrying permits and radio equipment for the Kessing expedition." I took my hand off the crowbar and let it hang empty. "Eleven nights of dead air on Channel Nine. We came to find out why."

"Why," Fen repeated. The word came out cracked. She laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in years. "Why. Eleven nights." The machete dropped until the point rested on the floorboards, and she leaned on it like a cane, and I saw how much of her strength had been theater, held up by fury and nothing else. "Because they came at night. Because they weren't inspectors, they were cleaners. Because Amara wouldn't give up her data so they decided to take the whole station apart and scatter us into the bush and let the river do the bookkeeping." Her voice was climbing. "Two of my people went into the water and didn't come up. Two. And the rest walked downstream out of this because walking downstream was the only direction left, and I couldn't walk, so I crawled into a woodshed and ate candle wax and watched the river every day waiting to see if they'd send someone back to finish the job."

Silence. Rain. Okonjo had gone gray.

"They shot you," Dez said. It wasn't a question. He was looking at her leg.

"They shot at me. Got meat instead of bone." She said it flat, a field report. Then her legs did what her voice had been holding off, and she started to go down, and I moved without deciding to and caught her under the arm, and she was lighter than any grown person should be, and she was shaking.

"Easy," I said. "We've got you."

"No you haven't." But she let me take the weight anyway. "You don't understand what you've sailed into. Those permits you're carrying—that cargo of yours—it's not equipment, it's permission. It's the legal spine of the whole concession. As long as those papers exist and get delivered, everything they did to us is worth doing." Her eyes found mine, fever-bright, and in them I saw the burned tents, the impossible elevations, the mid-word ending of a notebook entry. "And the men who did this? They'll be coming back up this river. Voss doesn't leave loose ends. He leaves *stations*. Decommissioned ones."

Ilse, in the doorway, had gone very still.

"How long," Fen said, "before your cargo reaches the escarpment?"

"Four days," I said. "Five, if the water keeps rising."

"Then you have four days," Fen said, "to decide what you're actually delivering."

Outside, downriver, faint under the rain, Okonjo's head came up like a deer's. None of us heard it yet. But he turned toward the window, toward the gray sheeting water, and his face told me before his voice did:

"Engine," he said. "Far off. More than one."

The stakes had just arrived on the river, and they were coming upstream.
