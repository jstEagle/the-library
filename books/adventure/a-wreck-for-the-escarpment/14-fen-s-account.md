## 14. Fen's Account

They cleaned the wound first. Marguerite insisted on it before she'd hear a word, and Ilse worked with the efficiency of someone who had stitched skin before—river skin, dock skin, maybe worse. Fen sat on an upturned crate in the middle station's flooded pump room with her left arm stripped to the shoulder, watching a stranger named Dez hold a pressure lamp steady while rain drummed the corrugated roof like a fist that never tired.

The bullet had gone through the meat below her shoulder, clean in, messier out. Ilse packed it, sewed it, and taped a strip of sailcloth over the dressing.

"You'll live," Ilse said.

"I was going to anyway," Fen said. "I had a schedule."

Something like a laugh went around the pump room—Dez, the young navigator Okonjo, even Marguerite, though hers was thin, the laugh of a woman doing arithmetic behind her eyes. Fen knew that laugh. She'd worn it for eleven days herself, out here in the green dark, counting the hours between the pain and the fever and the sound of engines on the water.

Marguerite crouched in front of her. Up close she had the look of someone who had been awake too long and intended to stay that way. "You're Fen Oduya. Deputy on the Kessing survey. The Kessing Escarpment concession survey."

"Was."

"Your expedition went dark on channel nine nineteen days ago. We've been checking in every night since the locks."

"I know." Fen looked at the radio crate stenciled against the wall—*HALVERN CONSOLIDATED / KESSING EXPEDITION / CONCESSION PERMITS & COMMS*, the seals still unbroken. "Those are yours to deliver, are they? The permits."

"That's the contract."

Fen nodded slowly, and the nod hurt, and she let it hurt, because it kept her honest. "Then before you deliver anything, you're going to listen to me. All of you. Because I've spent eleven days deciding whether the next person who found me was going to hear this story or become part of it, and you're the first ones who came upriver instead of down."

The lamp hissed. Rain on the roof. Somewhere out in the drowned channel, water moved through the dead trees with a sound like slow applause.

"Start at the beginning," Marguerite said.

* * *

"The beginning is that Dr. Amara Kessing does not put her name on things she hasn't checked twice." Fen shifted her arm and kept talking through the flare of it, because the pain was a kind of proof and she wanted all the proof she could get. "The escarpment concession was named for her family—her grandfather surveyed that country in the fifties, made the first elevation maps. When Halvern Consolidated filed the concession, they licensed her family's survey data and asked her to lead verification. She said yes because she thought it was a chance to do it right. Baseline hydrology, sediment load, the whole escarpment face. Three years of work before a single road went in."

"And?" Okonjo asked. He was the young one, the navigator, and he asked questions like a man taking bearings.

"And by month eight she had a problem. The elevation figures in the licensed data didn't match what we were measuring on the ground. Not by a little. By a hundred and forty meters in places. Whole sections of the escarpment's eastern face were drawn as stable sedimentary shelf when the actual rock is fractured basalt over waterlogged clay. You can't terrace that. You can't road it. You can't dam the tributaries feeding it without the whole lower face slumping into the river inside a decade."

Dez frowned. "So the surveys were bad. Sloppy."

"They weren't sloppy." Fen let that sit. "Sloppy surveys get corrected. These were corrected, and then the corrections came back *unchanged*. We filed a variance report in month nine. It was accepted into the Halvern system and then vanished—no rejection, no query, nothing. We filed again. Same. Amara started keeping a second ledger. Paper only. Handwritten. She said—" Fen stopped, and her throat worked, and she made herself finish it. "She said, *when the numbers are this wrong on purpose, the wrongness is the product.*"

Marguerite had gone very still. "The wrongness is the product," she repeated.

"The concession only works on paper. That's the point. Halvern files against the falsified surveys, draws development subsidies and carbon-offset financing against land that can never actually be developed, and cashes out before the escarpment ever has to hold the weight of what they promised it. The river country pays. The investors get paid. Nobody ever has to build a thing."

Ilse was standing by the door with her arms crossed, and Fen watched her, because Ilse had not asked a single question since they'd pulled her out of the pump housing, and people who didn't ask questions usually already knew the answers.

"Go on," Ilse said quietly. "Tell them about the inspection."

* * *

"The inspection came nineteen days ago." Fen's voice flattened out, the way it had flattened every time she told this part, which was twice now, once to the dark and once to these strangers. "Six men off a gray launch, company lanyards, very polite. They said they were a compliance audit from the regional office. They asked for all our field data—raw instruments, calibration logs, Amara's notebooks. Amara asked to see their warrant. The polite one smiled and said the concession agreement *was* the warrant, and while two of them talked to her, the rest went through the camp."

She looked down at her bandaged arm as if the wound could testify.

"They took everything. The instruments, the drives, the paper files. One of them found Amara's second ledger—the handwritten one—and when she told him it was worthless without the calibration logs, he burned it in the cook fire to make a point about how worthless it was. Half a page survived. Half a page, and I've got it memorized, and it's the eastern face figures, the impossible ones, in her own hand with her own corrections."

"So they had the data," Marguerite said. "Why the violence?"

"Because of where Amara was when they came." Fen met her eyes. "She wasn't in camp. She'd gone upriver two days earlier with two of our team—Sefa and Brandt—to the escarpment itself. She'd stopped trusting anything we could measure from the water. She wanted to re-verify the readings from the rock, with her own hands, and bring back a record nobody had touched. The inspection wasn't an audit. It was a retrieval, and when they couldn't find her, it became a search."

One of the men had grabbed Fen by the band of her pack and asked, twice, very reasonably, where the doctor had gone. When she said she didn't know, the third time he asked, he used the machete flat against her shoulder first and the gun second. She'd told them the truth the second time: upstream. Toward the escarpment. And they'd put a man in a skiff and gone after her, and left two behind to "close out the site," which was a phrase she now understood to mean *make sure nothing that could contradict the file was left breathing*.

"I got into the water during the shooting," Fen said. "I've been in the bush and the flooded timber since. Eleven days. I watched the middle station from the reeds for two of them before I worked up the nerve to come inside, and then you arrived, and I thought—" she exhaled, "—I thought you were them. I nearly put the fish-gaff through the first hand that came down that ladder."

"That was my hand," Dez said.

"Then I owe you an apology and a drink."

"Both accepted."

Marguerite hadn't moved. "The men who came after her. The gray launch. Have you seen it since?"

"Once. Two nights ago, running downriver, fast, no lights. Going the wrong way to be leaving. Going the right way to be reporting."

* * *

The pump room had gotten darker while Fen talked, or maybe the day was just failing outside, the monsoon pressing the light flat. Ilse lit a second lamp without being asked. Marguerite stood and walked to the radio crate and put her hand flat on the stenciled wood, and Fen watched her do it, and understood what she was touching: the contract. The thing she was paid to deliver. The thing that would pay her crew.

"Your permits," Fen said, "authorize the next phase of the concession. Road survey teams, staging depots, a blasting schedule for the eastern tributaries. I helped draft the logistics annex, back when I believed the numbers. If those permits reach the escarpment, they don't rescue anyone. They make everything we just described *legal*. They turn the fiction into a schedule."

"I hear you," Marguerite said.

"But?" Fen said, because there was a but; she could see it standing in the woman like a mast.

"But my crew eats on this contract. And I've got a debt that doesn't care about hydrology." Marguerite said it flat, no self-pity in it, which Fen respected. "So I'm not promising you anything yet. What I'm asking is: where exactly is Kessing going? If we go upstream, what are we going toward?"

"Amara's grandfather's old survey route. There's a goat path on the western spur that climbs to the summit ridge, and on the ridge there's a relay tower—government-built, forty years old, still on the emergency band. She said if she could verify the readings at the face and get to that tower with a transmitter, no one could quietly bury it. Not a burned ledger, not a vanished variance report. A broadcast from the summit, on the record, in her name." Fen almost smiled. "She's a stubborn woman. She's been carrying that tower in her head for three years like a talisman."

"And you believe she made it?"

"I believe that if Amara Kessing is alive on this river, she is climbing," Fen said. "And I believe that the men in the gray launch know it too."

Silence, except the rain. Then Dez, the mechanic, the superstitious one, spoke from beside the lamp. "The river's rising. You all heard it last night—that seam in the bank let go above the gorge. If we're going anywhere, we're going today or we're not going."

"We're not delivering permits into that," Okonjo said. It wasn't a question either.

"I didn't say we were," Marguerite said, and Fen caught the precision of it, the way the words were shaped to promise nothing. "I said I'm not promising anything *yet*."

Ilse pushed off from the door. "The gorge transit is four hours in water like this. After the gorge, the tributary forks, and the western fork runs to the base of the escarpment. I can take you there." She paused, and for the first time since the locks, Fen saw something unguarded cross her face. "I've been up that fork before."

"When?" Fen asked.

"Long time ago," Ilse said. "Ask me again when we're through the gorge."

Marguerite looked around the pump room—at Dez with the lamp, at Okonjo already mentally plotting the fork, at Ilse, at the crate with its unbroken seals, at Fen with her stitched arm and her half-page of burned truth.

"Get the Tern ready," she said. "We run the gorge tonight."

Fen leaned back against the cold pump housing and let the pain have her for a moment, and underneath the pain, something in her chest unclenched for the first time in nineteen days. Not because she was safe—she wasn't, none of them were, and the woman giving the orders hadn't promised a thing. But because for nineteen days the story had ended with men in a gray launch and a ledger burning in a cook fire, and now, for the first time, it had a next line.

Upstream. Toward the escarpment. Toward the tower.

She just hoped Amara was still climbing.
