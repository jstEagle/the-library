## 15. Loyalty's Arithmetic

Fen slept in the chart room with a blanket pulled to his chin and a pistol under his hand that nobody had given him permission to keep. Marguerite stood at the Tern's stern rail in the grey hour before dawn and did the only thing she was truly good at besides reading weather: she counted.

Not money. She'd stopped counting money years ago; it was a language she'd lost fluency in, the way you lose a childhood tongue. She counted distances. That was her arithmetic. Miles upriver to the escarpment: sixty-one, by Ilse's chart, though the river had rewritten half of it overnight. Miles back down to the locks, where Voss waited with his cheerful voice and his eleven-day deadline: ninety-four. Fuel remaining: enough for one direction, not both. Food after last night's recalculation: nine days if they ate like monks, six if they ate like people.

And then the other column, the one she'd been avoiding since the burned ledger page came out of the ash can at the abandoned camp.

She took out the permits.

They were sealed in oilcloth inside the radio crate, wrapped around a steel rod so they couldn't be creased, because Voss's lawyers liked their documents pristine. Concession permits, Halvern Consolidated, Kessing Escarpment Survey Area One through Four. Signatures of officials whose names meant nothing to her and everything to someone. If she delivered them—if she put them into the hands of whatever remained of the expedition, or into the hands of whoever was waiting for them upstream—then the machinery kept turning. The concession lived. The falsified surveys became furniture, background paperwork, the kind of thing nobody reads until twenty years later when the valley is drowned and the fishery is dead and some graduate student writes a thesis about it.

If she delivered them, Voss forgave the debt. All of it. Forty-two thousand, three hundred and eleven dollars as of Tuesday, accruing daily at a rate he described, always, like a man describing weather rather than a knife. Dez would get paid what Marguerite owed him from two seasons of deferred wages. Okonjo could sit his pilot's exams instead of working them off. The Tern could be re-planked properly instead of patched with prayer and resin.

That was the first column. It added up clean. It always had. That was the whole genius of Voss's design—he never offered her anything crooked. Just an honest debt, honestly collected, against an honest delivery. A contract. Paper. Work for hire, like any other.

The second column didn't add up at all. That was its problem, and also, she was beginning to understand, its entire point.

* * *

Ilse found her at the rail. Marguerite heard her come—she moved differently than Dez or Okonjo, no wasted sound, a woman who had spent years being careful about where her feet landed—and said nothing until Ilse spoke first.

"You're doing the math," Ilse said.

"I'm always doing the math."

"Not that math." Ilse leaned on the rail beside her, forearms on the wet wood, looking downstream the way you look at something you've already decided to leave. "The other one."

Marguerite turned the permit roll over in her hands. Oilcloth, cold with river damp. "You want to tell me what I should do with these?"

"No."

"That's a first."

"I told you what I know," Ilse said. "Kessing found proof the surveys are forged. Halvern silenced her team. Whatever you do with those papers is your weight to carry. I'm not going to make it lighter or heavier for you. I've watched too many people decide things because somebody else leaned on them." A pause, and then, quieter: "I'll tell you one thing, though. Free of charge."

"Go ahead."

"Voss doesn't need the permits delivered. He needs them *seen* delivered. There's a difference. The expedition is scattered. Fen's the only one left this side of the escarpment and he's got a hole in his shoulder. Nobody upstream is waiting to sign for a crate." Ilse's eyes stayed on the water. "So ask yourself what the delivery actually accomplishes. Ask yourself who benefits from the paper trail existing. Because it isn't the river, and it isn't the expedition, and it isn't you."

Then she went below, because that was Ilse all over—she gave you exactly as much truth as would fit in one breath and made you carry the rest yourself.

Marguerite stayed at the rail and let the river answer for a while. It was running high and brown, carrying whole trees sideways, and somewhere under that brown water was the road she'd been driving for twenty years. Rivers didn't care about debts. That was the thing about rivers. They were the only honest ledger left.

* * *

Dez was awake when she came below, sitting cross-legged on the engine deck with his tools laid out in their exact order, which was how he prayed. He looked up at her with the expression he wore whenever he'd been thinking about something too long without saying it.

"We're still going up," he said. Not quite a question.

"Fen needs a doctor past the middle reach. And the expedition—" She stopped. She'd been about to say *the expedition might have survivors*, but Fen had already told them there weren't any, not likely, not above the station. "We're still going up."

Dez nodded slowly and tapped a wrench against his palm, once, twice. "I fixed the bilge pump again. Third time. You know what my grandmother used to say? A boat tells you when it wants to die. This boat's been telling us since the gorge." He wasn't arguing. Dez never argued; he just set small true statements in front of you like offerings and let you trip over them. "I'll keep her floating as long as she wants to float."

"How long is that?"

"Upstream?" He almost smiled. "Longer than we deserve. Shorter than we need. Somewhere in between is where we live."

Okonjo was at the nav table, correcting the chart by hand, drawing in the new channels the flood had opened and closed. He was nineteen and he still believed the river rewarded effort, and Marguerite loved him for it and dreaded the season that would take it out of him. He glanced up when she passed.

"Ilse says the side channel past Kilometre Forty-One is passable if the gauge drops below four meters," he said. "It doesn't drop below four meters during monsoon. So it's not passable. But it saves eleven kilometres."

"Then we run the main channel."

"Yes, ma'am." He bent back to the chart, then paused. "Skipper. Can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"Why are we still going up?" He said it plainly, the way only Okonjo could—not accusing, not afraid, just genuinely asking, because he trusted her enough to believe there was an answer. "The delivery's dead. Whoever was supposed to receive it is gone. We've got a wounded man aboard, a holed hull, and a launch following us that shot at nobody yet but keeps showing up. Every reason says turn around. You haven't turned around. So there's a reason I don't have."

Marguerite looked at him for a long moment. Here it was, she thought. The moment where you either lie to the people who trust you or you tell them a truth you haven't finished deciding yourself.

"The reason is Fen," she said. "He knows where Dr. Kessing went. And I don't leave people on this river. That's the whole rule. That's the only rule I've got."

It was even true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and she watched him accept it—watched him file it away with the rest of the things he believed about her—and felt the specific loneliness of a captain, which is the loneliness of knowing the course while everyone else knows the heading.

* * *

Midmorning, the satellite phone rang.

She'd been expecting it since the locks. Voss called at intervals calibrated like medication doses—often enough to remind her the leash was there, rarely enough to seem casual. She climbed to the wheelhouse top for signal and answered leaning into the wind.

"Marguerite! Good morning. Or good afternoon—I never remember which way the river runs relative to the sun. How's our girl? The Tern, I mean."

"She floats."

"Atta girl. Listen, quick housekeeping item. Permits expire in ten days now. After that it's a re-filing process, new fees, new signatures, and frankly the investors get twitchy about re-files. They start asking questions about the carrier. You follow me?"

"I follow you."

"And how's the weather up there? Reports say the monsoon came in early. That's unusual. That's the kind of thing that happens when surveys are wrong, you know. Weather patterns, drainage basins—the escarpment changes all of it. Funny how much depends on getting the numbers right." A light laugh, warm as bathwater. "Anyway. Status?"

"Last confirmed position of the expedition was the middle station. We're making for it."

A pause. Half a second too long. "Middle station's a ruin, Marguerite. Hasn't been staffed since the port authority pulled funding. Nothing there but bats and rust."

"Then it should be a quick stop."

Another pause, and when Voss spoke again the cheer was still there but it had gone thin, like paint over rust. "You know, I want to tell you something, captain to client. Investments expect returns. That's not cruelty, that's just physics. Everything I've done for you—every extension, every accommodation—it's because I believe in you. My associates believe in you. We're optimists. But optimism has a shelf life, and yours is coming due, and I would hate—genuinely hate—for you to arrive back at the locks having complicated something that could have been simple." The wind gusted. Downriver, far behind them, the fog sat thick over the channel where a fast launch had appeared twice and vanished twice. "Simple is good, Marguerite. Simple is what friends do for each other."

"Is that all, Voss?"

"That's all. Give my regards to the river." Click.

She stood holding the dead phone against her thigh and understood, with total clarity, that he knew. He knew the expedition was silent. He knew what the middle station held—or had held. He knew she'd find things up here that couldn't be unseen, and he was calling anyway, cheerful as a morning show, because the call itself was a message: *we see you, we see where you are, and the leash still reaches.*

The optimists. She thought about that word. Voss and his associates, investing in people, believing in her, expecting returns. She'd spent three years thinking of them as creditors—a category of person, like weather, impersonal and survivable. Standing in the wind with the phone dead in her hand, she finally saw them correctly: not creditors. Custodians. Men who held other men's futures in filing cabinets, who had built an entire friendly architecture out of debt, and who had sent an armed inspection party upriver when a scientist read the numbers too closely.

Her debt wasn't a number. It was a leash, and leashes were for dogs, and dogs were for keeping.

* * *

She went to the chart room and sat across from Fen, who woke instantly the way soldiers and fugitives do, hand finding the pistol before his eyes found her.

"Easy," she said. "Question."

He lowered the blanket but not the hand. "Ask."

"When your team scattered—when the inspection happened—did anyone come looking for the data afterward? Halvern's people. Did they search?"

"They searched everything." His voice was rough, days of disuse and pain. "They took the drives, the paper files, Kessing's field notebooks. They burned what they couldn't carry. But Amara—" He corrected himself, professional distance reasserting. "Dr. Kessing kept duplicates. She always kept duplicates. She used to say trust was a redundancy problem. If they didn't find everything, it's because she made sure they couldn't find everything in one place."

"And the original readings. The real ones. Are they verifiable? Independently? If someone else ran the instruments, would they get the same numbers?"

Fen studied her. He had a long face made longer by exhaustion, and right now it carried the particular patience of a man watching someone approach a door he'd already walked through. "Yes," he said. "That's why they're afraid of her. The forgery isn't subtle once you know to look. Any licensed surveyor with a week and a theodolite could confirm it. That's why the data has to stay lost, Marguerite. Not disputed. Lost. Dispute takes years. Loss takes nothing."

"Where did she go?"

"Upstream. To the escarpment itself. She wanted to re-verify on site, with her own instruments, somewhere Halvern's people couldn't reach the record." He shifted, winced, kept his eyes on hers. "Why are you asking me this?"

"Because I'm the one holding the permits," Marguerite said, "and I'm trying to figure out what they're actually worth."

"And what did you conclude?"

She stood. Outside the porthole, the river ran brown and enormous, going where it went. "That they're worth exactly what Voss paid for them," she said. "Nothing more."

* * *

At noon she called a crew meeting on the aft deck, which was a grand name for four square meters of scarred planking with a tarp for shade. Five people: herself, Dez, Okonjo, Ilse, Fen propped against the rail with his arm bound tight.

"Fuel check," she said. "Dez."

"Sixty hours at cruising, forty at push. Upstream against this current, call it fifty-five hours to the escarpment base if the gauge behaves."

"It won't behave."

"No, ma'am. Call it sixty."

"Food?"

"Nine days. Six if Okonjo keeps eating like that."

"I eat normal," Okonjo said.

"You eat like a growing boy, which is worse."

"Rations tighten tomorrow," Marguerite said. "Nobody starves, everybody's hungry. Fen gets double portions until that wound stops weeping—that's not negotiable, don't argue, Fen, I can see you inhaling to argue." A couple of tired smiles. Good. She needed them loose before she said the next part. "Here's the situation. The delivery contract is functionally void. There's no receiving party upstream. What's upstream is a missing scientist, a company that wants her lost, and a launch that keeps finding us. Turning around means ninety-four miles of open channel with them behind us and Voss waiting at the locks with a smile and a bill. Going on means sixty miles of worse channel with them behind us and no idea what's at the top except maybe answers."

Silence. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere in the flooded forest a bird screamed like a hinge.

"So I'm asking," Marguerite said. "Not ordering. Asking. Who wants to turn around?"

Nobody spoke. Dez looked at his tools. Okonjo looked at her. Ilse looked at the water with an expression Marguerite couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Who wants to go on?"

Dez raised his hand first, slow, like a man volunteering for something he'd already volunteered for in his head days ago. Then Okonjo, fast, eager, nineteen. Then Ilse, last, without ceremony, the way you sign your name.

Fen didn't raise his hand. He pushed himself upright against the rail instead, grey-faced, and said, "Take me up. If she's alive, I'm the one she'll believe."

"All right," Marguerite said. "We go on."

She let the meeting break. She let Dez go below to his engines and Okonjo to his charts and Fen back to his fever and Ilse to wherever Ilse went when she wasn't standing beside you telling you half of something. She stayed on the aft deck alone with the oilcloth roll in her pocket, and she did the final sum, the private one, the one she hadn't spoken aloud.

Delivering the permits saved her crew and cleared her debt. Destroying them saved the river and ruined them all—no wages, no boat, no clean way home, and Voss's optimists between them and every exit. Those were the two columns, and both of them ended badly, and the difference between them was only a question of who ended badly: her people, or everyone downstream of a dam built on lies.

There was a third column. She'd seen it forming for two days now and hadn't written it down anywhere, not on paper, not aloud, not even in her own head in full sentences, because plans spoken become plans known and plans known become plans betrayed. The third column was simple. Deliver the permits—technically. Put them in the hands of the person they were addressed to. Dr. Amara Kessing, lead scientist, Kessing Escarpment Survey. Let the woman they'd tried to erase hold the paper that erased the valley, and let her decide what got broadcast and what got burned.

It was a lawyer's move and she hated it and it might save all of them. If Kessing was alive. If they reached her. If the launch didn't close the gap first. If the Tern floated sixty more miles on a seam Dez had fixed three times.

Marguerite stood at the rail and measured the distance to the escarpment one more time—fifty-eight now, the river eating the miles whether she willed it or not—and made her decision the way she made all of them: alone, in silence, with the numbers in front of her.

She would go on. She would say yes to everything Voss asked on the phone. She would keep the permits dry and visible and ready to hand over, and she would tell no one—not Dez, not Okonjo, not even Ilse, especially not Ilse—what she intended to do with them when they arrived. Trust was a redundancy problem. Kessing had understood that. Marguerite was learning it.

Behind them, far down the brown channel, the fog lifted for a moment, and in the clearing she saw it: the fast launch, holding position, patient as interest accruing. Watching them choose.

Marguerite turned her back on it and rang for full ahead, upstream, toward the escarpment, carrying her cargo of paper and her secret like ballast.
