## 16. The Pursuit Closes

Okonjo saw the launch first because he was the one who never stopped looking.

It was a habit Marguerite had drilled into him on his first week aboard the *Tern*, back when the river was still a thing he trusted. *Eyes up, eyes back, eyes up again,* she'd said, tapping her temple. *The river doesn't kill you from in front. It kills you from behind.* He'd taken it as a joke then. Now, three days past the abandoned camp and one day past Fen's account of what Halvern's men had done to the expedition, it felt like scripture.

They were running the lower reach below the middle station with the rain coming down in gray sheets, Ilse at the wheel, Marguerite forward checking the depth sounder against a chart she no longer fully believed. Dez was below decks keeping the bilge pump company—it had developed a stutter that made him talk to it like a sick animal—and Okonjo stood his watch at the stern rail under the canvas awning, scanning the water behind them.

That was where the fog thinned.

A quarter mile back, maybe less, a shape that wasn't weather. A hard white line where there should have been soft gray. He watched it for ten seconds, telling himself it was a rock, a snag, a trick of the current throwing spray. Then the line moved against the current, and kept moving, and grew.

"Ilse," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he expected. "Stern quarter. Something coming."

She didn't turn around. That was the first strange thing—she just tilted her head a degree, listening to the water the way she did, reading the engine note through the deck plates. "How fast?"

"Faster than us. Closing."

"Marguerite." Still not turning. "Your call."

Marguerite came aft along the gunwale with the chart still rolled in her fist, and Okonjo watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done—the closing speed, the distance, the minutes they had left—and watched her arrive somewhere worse than arithmetic. She raised her binoculars.

"Company hull," she said quietly. "White, low freeboard, twin outboards. There are men aboard. Four, five. They're not fishing."

Okonjo took the binoculars when she passed them back. Through the rain-haze he made out what she meant: men in matching dark jackets, one standing braced at the bow beside something mounted on a swivel that might have been a spotlight or might not. The launch ran flat and mean through the chop, the way boats run when the people driving them don't care about the boat.

"They've been following us since the camp," Okonjo said. "Off and on. In the fog."

"I know," Ilse said.

"You *know*?"

"I suspected. Fog makes liars of everything on this river." She finally turned her head, and her face was calm in a way that frightened him more than the launch did. "I hoped I was wrong."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Something passed between them—not quite an argument, more like two people agreeing to postpone one. Then Marguerite said, "Options."

"We can't outrun them. Not with our load, not with the pump stuttering, not in this water." Ilse ticked it off flatly, a pilot's honest ledger. "We can't outrun them, we can't fight them, and if we stop, we find out what happened to Dr. Kessing's team from the inside."

"So we don't stop."

"So we don't stop."

Ahead, the reach bent left around a bluff of black stone, and beyond the bend Okonjo could see nothing but rain and the green wall of the floodplain rising off both banks. The river here was high—monsoon-high, weeks early—and the forest on either side stood drowned to its waists, the water threaded between trunks in silver sheets. It looked, he thought, less like a river than like a road that had forgotten its edges.

The launch closed to two hundred meters. A voice came across the water through a loudhailer, cheerful and distorted by rain, and Okonjo's stomach dropped because he knew the shape of cheerfulness like that. He'd heard it in Voss's office, in the garage, in every room where someone smiled while they took something from you.

"*Motor vessel Tern, motor vessel Tern. This is a concession compliance inspection. Please reduce speed and prepare to be boarded. This is routine. You have nothing to be concerned about.*"

"Routine," Dez said, appearing at the companionway with grease to his elbows and a wrench in his hand like a talisman. His face had gone the color of old rope. "Routine like the camp was routine? Routine like Fen's shoulder?"

"Heave to," Marguerite murmured, half to herself, testing the words. "Heave to and let them come aboard with the permits sealed in the hold and a burned survey ledger hidden under my bunk and a wounded witness in the engine room." She shook her head once, sharp. "No."

"Then we run," Ilse said, "and running is an answer too. They'll read it however they want."

"They'll read it exactly how it is," Marguerite said. "We're not a delivery anymore. We haven't been since the camp. Maybe since the locks." She looked at Okonjo, and he realized with a jolt that she was asking him—not ordering, asking, as if his opinion weighed something now. "You're the one who spotted them. You're the one who's been watching our wake for three days. What does your gut say?"

His gut said a lot of things. It said *turn back* and it said *you can't* and it said *Dez can't swim well and the water is brown and fast and full of drowned trees.* But underneath all of it, steady as a bearing, it said what it had been saying since Fen told them about the inspection that turned violent: *these people cut a survey team apart in the bush and called it compliance.*

"My gut says a boarding party doesn't bring five men and a mounted light to check permits," Okonjo said. "My gut says we were never going to deliver these papers anywhere good."

Marguerite held his eyes for a second longer than was comfortable, then nodded, and that nod went through him like current through wire. *She trusts me.* He filed the feeling away to examine later, if there was a later.

"Ilse," she said. "Get us out of this."

Ilse's smile was thin and joyless and entirely professional. "Hold on to something you love."

* * *

What happened next, Okonjo would remember afterward in fragments, the way you remember a storm—by its pieces, never its whole.

Ilse brought the wheel over hard to starboard without slowing, and the *Tern* heeled until the rail kissed the floodwater and Dez swore fluently below decks. The launch behind them, expecting a chase along the channel, overshot the turn by a boat-length before its driver understood. That boat-length was the whole game; Okonjo understood that even as his hands were clamped white on the rail. Ilse hadn't been trying to outrun anyone. She'd been waiting for the bend, for the bluff, for the exact place where the drowned forest pressed closest to the channel, and she'd spent their head start buying one mistake from the other helmsman.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin at a gap in the trees that Okonjo genuinely could not see. "Starboard of the dead fig. Two meters of water if the gauge is honest. Pray it's honest."

"It's not going to be honest," Dez said.

"Then pray louder."

She took the gap at speed. The world went green. Rain hammered the canopy overhead and came through it in ropes, and the *Tern* ran down a flooded lane between tree trunks with branches raking the awning and the depth sounder chirping like a dying bird—two point one, one point eight, one point six—and Okonjo found himself calling the numbers aloud without being asked, because someone had to, because calling numbers was a thing hands could do instead of shaking.

"One point four!"

"Hold," Ilse said, to the boat or the river or God.

The keel touched. Once—a long shuddering scrape along some submerged log, a sound like a file drawn across the hull's spine—and then lifted free, and the lane widened into a flooded clearing where the water lay flat as poured tin among the trunks, and Ilse killed the engines to a bare idle and brought them gliding to rest against the bole of an enormous kapok like a child hiding behind its mother's leg.

Silence, except the rain.

Okonjo discovered he was breathing in sips. Through the screen of trunks, faint and getting fainter, he heard the loudhailer again, angry now, the cheerfulness stripped off it: voices, engine noise circling, the whine of an outboard probing somewhere off to the north. Then, gradually, the sounds sorted themselves into distance, and distance into absence.

"They'll work the main channel first," Ilse whispered. "Then the tributaries. We've bought hours, not days."

Nobody answered. There didn't seem to be anything worth spending breath on.

* * *

They drifted deeper into the drowned forest as the light failed, Ilse poling them trunk to trunk with a broken oar, choosing water by eye and memory in a way that seemed to Okonjo less like navigation than like conversation—as if she and the flooded country were speaking a language the rest of them only heard. Twice she stopped them in open water to listen. Once she reversed course for no reason she explained, and twenty minutes later they heard an outboard pass through the space they'd vacated, moving fast, running lights sweeping the trunks like a searchlight.

After that, nobody suggested going back to the channel.

They tied up for the night in the lee of a termite mound island that broke the surface like the back of a sleeping animal, tall enough to keep the tent dry, which was the only good news the day offered. Dez went straight to the bilge and reported, grimly, that the scrape had opened the seam's neighbors and the pump's stutter had become a stammer. Rations were recalculated. Nobody ate much.

Okonjo took first watch, which nobody argued with, because he'd earned it—he'd seen the launch first, and Marguerite had said so in front of everyone, and he was young enough that the praise sat in his chest like a coal.

From the mound's crest he watched the darkness where the channel should be. The rain had softened to a fine persistent whisper. Somewhere far off, thunder worked at the horizon like a man trying to open a door.

Marguerite climbed up beside him near midnight, moving carefully on the wet earth, and handed him half of a biscuit he hadn't asked for. They ate without talking for a while. That was another thing she'd taught him: silence shared was different from silence alone.

"Do you think we made the right call?" he asked finally. "Back at the bend."

"Yes," she said, too fast, and then, honestly, "I don't know. Both things. Ask me tomorrow."

"And if they find us?"

"Then we find out who we are." She said it lightly, but it landed heavy, and he turned to look at her. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and the pale smudge of the scar over her eyebrow. "Every contract I've ever signed, Okonjo, I signed so I wouldn't have to find out. Debt is very convenient that way. It tells you who you are: someone who owes. Simple. No further inquiry required." She brushed biscuit crumbs from her knee. "Tomorrow there's no contract. Just whatever we decide to be."

He wanted to say something worthy of that. What he said was, "You could have turned back at the locks. Before me, before any of this. Why didn't you?"

Marguerite was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Below them, he could hear Dez arguing with the bilge pump in a low, tender voice, and Ilse not sleeping, the way she apparently never slept, sitting upright against the kapok root with her arms around her knees.

"Because the river only goes one way," she said at last, "and I've spent twenty years pretending that's a tragedy." She stood, stiffly, joints announcing themselves. "Wake me for second watch. And Okonjo—keep watching our wake. Whatever else happens, keep watching."

He watched. Toward dawn, in the last of the dark, he saw it: far back through the lattice of trunks, a single white running light, patient as a moored buoy, holding position at the mouth of the flooded forest.

Not searching anymore. Waiting.

He woke Marguerite with a hand on her shoulder and told her, and she looked at the light for a long time, and then she said the sentence that ended the last of the pretending, quietly, the way you close a door:

"They're not chasing a delivery boat anymore. They're besieging it." She turned away from the light, toward the drowned green country ahead of them, where the water deepened and the maps gave out. "So we'd better figure out what we're carrying that's worth a siege."
