## 17. Stranded in the Green

The side-channel swallowed the Tern whole and then stopped giving.

Dez felt it before she saw it—the way the current went from a shove to a grip, the water thickening with silt and root-rot until the propeller was chewing soup. Ilse stood at the wheel with her jaw set like a rivet, threading them between drowned trunks that rose out of the flood black as burned matches. Somewhere behind them, faint through the rain-hiss, an engine note that wasn't theirs.

"Dead end," Okonjo said from the bow, binoculars up. "Channel closes maybe four hundred meters on. It's all forest."

"It's not closed," Ilse said. "It's slow."

"Those are different words for the same thing when something's chasing us."

Dez didn't say anything. She was down in the bilge already, because she'd been feeling the wrongness in the hull since they'd come off the main channel at speed—Ilse had put the Tern's shoulder into a submerged fence line or a fallen tree or the ghost of one, and Dez had heard the sound a boat makes when it takes a wound: a deep wooden cough, felt through the soles of your boots more than heard.

She pulled the inspection hatch and shone her lamp into the dark under the engine bed.

Water. Not trickling. Climbing.

"Ilse," she called up. "We're holed. Starboard side, forward of the stringer. I need to get her aground somewhere I can work, or we're going to be swimming with the catfish."

The channel bent left around a stand of fig trees standing waist-deep in their own drowned orchard, and there—like the river had set a table for them—a termite mound broke the surface, big as a chapel, its crown hung with vines. Behind it, half-submerged ridges of old levee earth ran toward higher ground where the flooded forest thinned into actual land.

Ilse brought the Tern alongside the mound with a delicacy Dez would have admired any other day, easing the port gunwale against the termite-cement slope while Okonjo threw a line around a vine-thick trunk and hauled them snug. The boat settled with a list to starboard that told Dez everything she needed to know about how much water was aboard.

They were, as far as Dez could tell, the only living things for three kilometers in any direction, unless you counted termites, and she did count termites. Termites had built the island they were standing on. Termites understood engineering. Termites did not run from debt collectors.

* * *

By the time the rain eased to a steady grey drizzle, they'd gotten the worst of it handled—or the worst of what could be handled. The hole was ugly: a gash maybe forty centimeters long where a snagged piling or a deadfall limb had punched through between two planks, right along a seam that Dez's predecessor had caulked with whatever was cheapest in the year of our lord whenever-that-was. She'd stuffed it with a rag wad and a wedge of soft pine and slathered the whole insult with the last of the marine epoxy, which was meant for hairline cracks, not wounds, and which she knew—knew in her hands, in her teeth—was a tourniquet, not a stitch.

"You can fix it," Okonjo said. He said it the way you say a thing you need to be true, standing over her in the bilge with the lamp held high.

"I can keep it from getting worse," Dez said. "In still water. If nobody drives this boat hard again, ever." She sat back on her heels and looked at the patch, at the water seeping around its edges at a rate she could measure in minutes-per-bucket instead of seconds. "This boat needs a haul-out, a dry dock, and a carpenter who likes her. We have none of those things."

"So we're stuck here."

"We're stuck here until I figure out how to lie to physics convincingly."

Marguerite appeared at the companionway, rain sheeting off her oilskin, and Dez watched her do the thing she always did—take inventory. Eyes on the bilge, on the patch, on Dez's face, reading the report Dez hadn't given yet.

"How bad," Marguerite said.

"She'll float. For now. She won't run, not hard, and if the river comes up another meter she won't float either, because she'll be pinned against this mound and torn open like a letter." Dez climbed out, wiped her hands on a rag that was already beyond help. "I need dry wood, resin, sailcloth, and about six hours without anyone asking me questions."

"You have five of those six things."

"Then I'll improvise the sixth."

Marguerite almost smiled. Almost. There was something else moving behind her face tonight, some arithmetic running that she didn't share, and Dez had known Marguerite Brightwater long enough to know when the captain was counting something she didn't want the crew to see on the ledger. She filed it away with all the other small worries, in the drawer that was getting full.

* * *

They camped on the mound itself, because the Tern was taking water faster than the bilge pump wanted to argue with, and because a termite mound, it turned out, was the driest real estate in a drowned world. The crown of the thing was hard as fired brick, warm even under the rain, riddled with ventilation shafts that breathed faint heat like the whole island was a sleeping animal. Fen—wounded, feverish, furious about both—was wrapped in every dry blanket they owned and propped against the trunk of a fig with Ilse sitting close, doing her quiet sentry thing.

Dinner was rice and the last of the smoked fish, cooked under a tarp on the single burner because open flame on a termite mound struck Dez as tempting fate in a language fate spoke fluently.

It should have been a quiet meal. They were alive, hidden, the pursuit launch presumably circling the main channel looking for a boat that no longer existed as far as the river was concerned. Instead, by the time the plates were empty, everyone's exhaustion had curdled into something with edges.

"We lost the permits' schedule," Okonjo said. He said it to the fire, but he meant it at Marguerite. "Eleven days was already tight. Now we're wedged in a tree."

"We're not delivering permits anymore," Ilse said. Flat. Final. The voice of a woman stating the weather.

Okonjo turned on her. "You decided that?"

"I decided nothing. The company decided it when they started shooting."

"They haven't shot yet. They've followed us. There's a difference, and the difference is that we still have cargo, and cargo is the job, and the job is what keeps us—" He stopped himself, but too late; the shape of the word was already out. *Paid.*

"The job," Dez said quietly, "is keeping us breathing. I've been listening to you two argue for two days and I want it on record that I can hear the launch engine in my sleep now. That's not a job. That's a chase."

"Nobody asked the mechanic for a motion," Okonjo snapped, and immediately looked like he wanted to eat the words, because Dez was twenty years his senior and had rebuilt his first radio with him when he was sixteen and still afraid of thunder.

"No," Dez agreed. "Nobody ever asks the mechanic anything. You just all assume the boat will hold because I'm back there with my wrenches making wishes come true." She held up her hands, blistered, split at the cuticles, stained black with epoxy. "Well. Here's my professional wish: I wish somebody would decide what we're actually doing, because a crew without a plan is just passengers, and I have never once been a passenger."

Silence. Rain on the tarp. Fen stirred in her blankets and said, hoarsely, "Kessing is upstream," as if that settled everything, and maybe it did, and maybe it settled nothing, because Marguerite said nothing at all.

That was the part that scared Dez. Not the arguing. Arguing was how families talked. What scared her was Marguerite, sitting outside the firelight with her arms crossed, letting her crew fracture around her without steering. Marguerite Brightwater had never once in eight years let a disagreement go unmediated. She argued like other people breathed.

Tonight she was quiet as a held breath, and Dez had spent enough years around engines to know that the most dangerous sound a machine could make was none at all.

* * *

Bail watch was four hours on, four off, and Dez took the first shift alone because she wanted to be alone, or thought she did, until the solitude turned out to be just her own thoughts with the volume up. She sat in the bilge with a bucket and a headlamp and bailed, and listened to the Tern talk to her.

Boats talked. Dez had believed this since she was nine years old and her father had put her hand flat on a hull and said, *feel that? She's telling you what hurts.* The Tern was telling her plenty. The seam aft of the galley was working loose. The rudder post was weeping. The epoxy patch was holding the way a promise holds—technically, temporarily, with visible strain.

"You and me both," Dez told her.

She thought about the money. She tried not to, but the mind goes where it goes at three in the morning with a bucket in your hands. Her sister's kids in the coastal town, the school fees, the roof that needed replacing before last season's storms and hadn't been replaced. Every cent of Dez's margin went into the Tern, and the Tern was currently a wounded animal wedged against a bug cathedral in a drowned forest, and the job that paid for all of it was, depending on which member of the crew you asked, either delivering permits to a silent expedition or delivering truth to a war.

A splash out in the black. Dez froze, bucket halfway raised.

Then a fish, probably. Everything out here was probably a fish. Probably.

She finished the shift, woke Okonjo for his, and climbed up onto the mound crown to try to sleep, and found Marguerite awake, sitting with her back against the fig, watching the channel where it vanished into the drowned trees.

"Can't sleep either?" Dez lowered herself down beside her.

"I slept on the crossing from Port Meridian once, in a gale, with the deck awash." Marguerite's voice was low. "I don't sleep well when I've made a decision I haven't told anyone about."

Dez kept very still. "And have you."

Marguerite was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for her. Then: "Get some rest, Dez. Tomorrow we'll need your hands."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got tonight."

Dez lay back against the warm brick of the mound and stared up through the fig canopy at rain coming down through leaves in threads. She thought about waking Okonjo and comparing notes. She thought about cornering Ilse, who knew more than she said—that woman lied the way some people hummed, absent and constant—and asking her straight out what the captain was carrying.

She thought about her sister's kids, and the roof, and the fact that every plan on this river now ended either in ruin or in something worse than ruin, which was choosing.

Sleep came eventually, thin and shallow, and she dreamed of the Tern talking to her in her father's voice, saying *feel that? She's telling you what hurts,* and in the dream Dez put her palm flat against the hull and the hull said: *she knows something you don't.*

She woke before dawn to Ilse shaking her shoulder, and to a sound she'd hoped never to hear again so soon: an engine, out in the green, moving slow. Searching.

"They're poling the side-channels," Ilse whispered. "Small craft. Two boats, maybe three. Get Okonjo. Wake Fen gently. And Dez—" Ilse's eyes in the pre-dawn grey were steady and cold as ballast. "Whatever happens next, nobody speaks above a whisper, and nobody touches that radio."
