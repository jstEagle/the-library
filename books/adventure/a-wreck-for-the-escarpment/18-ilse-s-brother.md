## 18. Ilse's Brother

The termite mound island was maybe forty meters across at low water and shrinking by the hour, a gray dome of compacted earth with three stunted trees on top like hairs on a mole. The Tern sat wedged in the drowned forest thirty meters out, listing to port, her hull breathing river through the gash in her planking with every wake that came through. Dez had pronounced her stable, which in Dez's vocabulary meant *not actively sinking*, and had then spent two hours rigging a tarp between the trees with the solemnity of a man building a cathedral.

Now it was dark, the rain had eased to a fine drifting mist, and Okonjo had gotten a fire going in a ring of salvaged tin. Not a good fire — nothing on this river was good anymore — but a fire, hissing and spitting, throwing orange light against the green wall of the flooded forest. Fen sat closest to it, wrapped in every dry thing they owned, his bandaged shoulder seeping through the cloth in a way nobody mentioned. Marguerite sat with her back against a trunk, arms crossed, watching the water.

Ilse watched her watch the water.

She had been watching Marguerite Zhou for eleven days now, ever since the locks, and she had learned to read her the way you learn to read a reach of river: where the current set, where the snags hid, what the surface told you about what lay underneath. Marguerite's surface said *delivery contract, professional obligation, none of your business*. Underneath — Ilse had known since the burned ledger page, since the confrontation at the rail — underneath there was a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want anyone else to see.

Tonight the arithmetic was going to come out into the open. Ilse could feel it the way you feel weather coming behind a ridge.

She fed a branch to the fire and decided not to wait for the question.

"You want to know who I am," she said.

Marguerite didn't move. "I know who you are."

"You know the name I gave you at the locks." Ilse turned the branch over. The bark caught and flared. "That name is printed on papers I bought in Port Sarrow from a man who sells them out of a suitcase. It belongs to a woman who died of fever in the coastal settlements four years ago. I've been wearing a dead woman's identity down this river because my own got erased."

Okonjo looked up from the fire. Dez stopped fiddling with the tarp line.

"Erased," Marguerite said. Flat. Not a question, but an invitation to keep going.

"Halvern Consolidated keeps very clean books," Ilse said. "That's the first thing you have to understand about them. They don't bribe sloppily or threaten loudly. They file things. They have clerks. When someone becomes inconvenient, they don't kill him if they can help it — they make him stop existing. Licenses revoked. Bank accounts frozen for irregularities. Leases terminated. References withdrawn. A man wakes up one morning and discovers that as far as every institution in the country is concerned, he was never born."

"And they did that to you."

"They did it to my brother." She heard her own voice go careful, the way it did when she talked about Tomas, each word placed like a foot on wet rock. "Tomas Vidal. He was a survey auditor. Third-party certification work — the boring, essential kind. When a company files survey data to get a concession approved, an independent auditor walks the ground and confirms the numbers are real. That was Tomas. He was good at it. Pedantic, stubborn, laughed at his own jokes before he finished them. Our mother said he'd have made a wonderful priest if he hadn't loved maps more than God."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, something moved through the drowned trees — a snake, probably, or just the current working at a root — and everyone's head turned and turned back.

"Six years ago he took a certification contract on the Kessing Escarpment surveys. Halvern's original submission. The one the concession permits were built on." Ilse kept her eyes on the fire. "He went upriver with two assistants. He sent back one message saying the figures didn't reconcile — that the elevations were wrong by a margin no honest mistake could produce. Then he stopped sending messages. His assistants came back without him. They'd been paid off, both of them, and both of them told the same story: Tomas went out alone to re-verify a transect and never returned. Drowned, presumably. The river takes people."

"And you didn't believe it."

"I believed the river takes people. I grew up on this river; I've fed it my share." She finally looked up, found Marguerite's eyes across the fire. "But I also knew my brother. He didn't take chances on the water. He double-knotted everything. And I knew Halvern had just been handed a reason to need him gone. So I asked questions. Formally, through the licensing board. Informally, through anyone who'd talk to me. Within eight months I couldn't get a contract. Within a year my pilot's license was suspended pending review of irregularities that had never existed. My bank closed my account. My landlord suddenly needed the flat. Every door I'd ever walked through closed at once, quietly, politely, with paperwork." She smiled, and knew it wasn't much of a smile. "I learned something useful, though. When they erase you, they teach you exactly how they do it. I've spent six years learning to live in the gaps they leave. Forged papers, borrowed names, piloting jobs under other people's licenses. And following Halvern's concessions. Every one of them. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Okonjo. Young enough to ask directly.

"For proof. Because suspicion gets you erased, and proof—" she spread her hands, "—proof gets you killed too, but slower, and in public, where it counts."

* * *

Dez broke the silence first, because Dez always did when silence got heavy. "The ledger page," he said. "The one half-burned at the camp. Those elevation figures."

"Were impossible," Ilse said. "You saw them. The escarpment bench sits eighteen meters lower in Halvern's surveys than it does in reality. Eighteen meters. That's not instrument error. That's the difference between a concession that's viable and one that would require damming the tributary system to make the numbers work — drowning villages, flooding the middle reach, all of it, to manufacture a site that doesn't exist. They faked the ground. And Dr. Kessing found the fake."

"Fen said Kessing went upstream to re-verify," Marguerite said.

"She did. Which means somewhere above us, on that escarpment, there is a verified dataset sitting in the hands of a woman Halvern has already tried once to disappear." Ilse let that sit. "The permits in your hold, Marguerite. The ones you're being paid to deliver. They're the amended concession filings — the renewal package. If they reach the expedition's relay point and get countersigned, the falsified surveys become legally laundered. Permanent. No inquiry will touch them after that, because the state will have ratified them."

"So delivering them—"

"—finishes what they started with my brother. Yes."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face. "Voss holds my debt," she said at last, and Ilse heard the cost of saying it aloud, the pride going out of her voice like air from a punctured tire. "Twenty years of storm chasing on credit. Boat repairs, instruments, fuel, the bad seasons when nothing flew. Voss bought the paper two years ago. He calls himself an investor. He says things like *we believe in people*." Her mouth twisted. "Eleven days until the permits expire. He's reminded me twice."

"He knows exactly what he's carrying you toward," Ilse said. "That's why the debt exists. You're not his client, Marguerite. You're his delivery mechanism."

"I know." Marguerite said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "I've known since the satellite call. I keep knowing it and I keep steering upstream anyway, and I haven't let myself ask myself why."

"Because your crew eats off this boat," Dez said. No accusation in it. Just fact.

"Because my crew eats off this boat," Marguerite agreed.

The fire settled. Somewhere out in the flood, the Tern groaned against whatever held her wedged, a long wooden complaint, and Dez's head came up like a dog's before he made himself relax.

Ilse reached into her shirt and pulled out the thing she had carried against her skin since the locks: a photograph, soft-cornered, water-stained along one edge. Two young people on a dock, squinting into sun. The man held a surveyor's transit case like a trophy. The woman was laughing at something outside the frame.

She passed it around the fire. Nobody spoke while it moved hand to hand. Okonjo held it longest, studying the man's face, then hers, finding the resemblance that six years and a forged name hadn't managed to erase.

"Tomas," she said, taking it back. "Last picture before the escarpment contract. He was so pleased with himself. He thought he was going to catch a rounding error and cost some company a fine." Her throat worked. "He had no idea what he'd stepped on. Neither did I. I still don't, entirely. I know Halvern wanted those surveys certified, and I know what they did to me for asking about him, and I know what they did to Kessing's team last week. I don't know how far up it goes. That's what frightens me. Not the men with guns. The size of the thing behind them."

* * *

It was Fen who shifted the night, the way wounded men sometimes do when they decide their remaining strength is better spent on truth than on rest.

"The inspection team," Fen said, from inside his nest of tarps. His voice was rough. "You called it that. It wasn't an inspection. There were nine of them. Two wore port authority jackets over company boots. They took our data drives first — before anything, before they even secured us, they went for the drives. Like that was the cargo." He paused. "They shot Havel in the leg when he argued. They put me against the wall and discussed, in front of me, whether I was worth the bullet. They decided the paperwork wasn't worth it. Left me in the latrine trench." He said it without self-pity, a man reporting instrument readings. "Kessing had already gone upstream with the originals. Paper copies. She never trusted drives after the first audit cycle. She used to say paper burns but it doesn't lie about being burned."

"That sounds like her," Ilse said quietly.

Fen's eyes came up. "You knew her?"

"I audited her once. Six years ago. Before." Ilse folded the photograph away. "She tore my preliminary report apart in front of the licensing board and she was right to. I'd missed a datum error in the eastern transects. She stood there and dismantled me line by line, polite as anything, and then bought me dinner afterward and showed me how I'd missed it." Something loosened in her chest, saying it. "If anyone alive has the true numbers for that escarpment, it's Amara Kessing. And if she went up there to verify them herself, she'll have done it properly. Triple-checked. Witnessed. The kind of dataset you can't shout down."

"So we find her," Okonjo said. Simple. Twenty-two years old and ready to climb an escarpment in monsoon season because the story required it. Ilse felt an old, dangerous affection for him already.

"We find her," Marguerite said.

And there it was. Ilse watched her say it — watched the woman who had signed papers she didn't read, who had steered eleven days upstream telling herself it was a delivery job, put the fiction down in front of witnesses. Not a speech. Three words and a jaw set like a cleat.

"You understand what you're choosing," Ilse said. "Not the abstraction. The specifics. If we go after Kessing's data instead of delivering the permits, Voss doesn't forgive your debt. He collects it. These people erased my life with paperwork and shot their way through a scientific team, and you'd be moving from asset to obstacle. There's no version of this where you deliver nothing and lose nothing."

"I did the arithmetic on the boat," Marguerite said. "Alone, after the camp. Delivering the permits saves my crew and clears my debt and drowns the middle reach and buries your brother a second time." She looked into the fire. "I've been measuring my whole life in miles. Distance covered, distance owed, distance left to run. Tonight I'm trying to measure something else. Ask me tomorrow if I've managed it."

Nobody answered that. There wasn't an answer. Dez poked the fire and Okonjo added wood and the rain started again, softly, a whisper on the tarp, and Ilse sat with the strangest feeling she'd had in six years: that she was sitting inside a crew instead of beside one. That the forged name on her papers mattered less than it had yesterday. That these four — the debtor, the kid, the superstitious mechanic, the man pulled out of a latrine trench — were, for reasons none of them had chosen, hers.

* * *

Later, when the others slept in shifts and Ilse took the middle watch, Marguerite stayed up. Of course she did.

"How long have you known about the permit seals?" Marguerite asked, low, so as not to wake Fen.

"Since the locks. The wax impression. Halvern's mark — a stylized escarpment profile inside a hexagon. I've seen it on every document that ever hurt my family." Ilse kept her voice level. "I should have told you on day one. I told myself I needed to be sure of you first. That was half true. The other half is that I've spent six years trusting no one, and it turns out that's a habit you can't break just because you need to."

"Six years is a long time to hunt alone."

"It's not hunting, mostly. It's waiting. Hunting implies you get to move." She looked out at the black water, the drowned trees standing in it like a congregation. "Tomorrow we dig the Tern out, or we don't, and either way we go up the tributary on foot if we have to. Kessing is up there with the only weapon that matters. I intend to be standing next to her when she fires it."

Marguerite nodded slowly. Then: "The launch that's been trailing us. The one you lied about knowing."

"Yes."

"When it comes again — and it will — whose side are its orders on? Yours or mine?"

"Ours," Ilse said. "That's what should frighten you. We're on the same side now, Marguerite. Which means we're on the side with no boats, no permits worth delivering, no money, and one wounded scientist somewhere on a cliff. Everything Halvern has is pointed at us."

Marguerite almost smiled. In the firelight it looked, for a second, like the face of someone younger, before twenty years of debt had drawn all over it. "Good," she said. "I've never done well on the winning side."

She rolled into her tarp. Ilse took the watch, and the river breathed around the island, and high above the drowned forest — invisible in the cloud, but there, she could feel it the way Tomas must have felt it, that last morning — the escarpment waited with its true numbers in its stone.
