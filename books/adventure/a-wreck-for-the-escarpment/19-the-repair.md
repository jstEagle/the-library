## 19. The Repair

Dez heard the river before he saw it—no, that was wrong. Dez heard the *boat* before he saw anything, because the Tern had started talking to him in the dark, a low groan from the portside seam that he'd been pretending for six hours was just the water shifting against the hull.

It wasn't the water.

He lay in the bilge with a headlamp clamped between his teeth, one hand flat against the planking, and felt the seam breathe. Open, closed. Open, closed. Like a wound that wouldn't clot. Every time the Tern rolled on the flood current, the gap widened a finger's width and sucked in a gallon of the brown monsoon river, and every time she rolled back the planks ground together with a sound like teeth.

"You're not helping," he told her.

The Tern groaned.

Above him, through the deck boards, he could hear the camp: Marguerite's voice, low and even, doing the arithmetic she did when she thought nobody was listening. Okonjo pacing. Ilse saying nothing, which was its own kind of sound—Ilse's silences had weight, like ballast.

Dez sat up, cracked his head on the engine mount, swore in three languages his grandmother would have washed his mouth out for, and climbed out into the rain.

* * *

The termite mound island stood maybe forty meters across, a cathedral of packed orange earth rising two meters above the flood, crowned with a single drowned fig tree that the water had taken but not killed. The crew had strung a tarp between the trunk and a stanchion they'd driven into the mound, and under it sat everything they owned in the world that wasn't bolted to a sinking boat: three crates of rations recalculated down to eleven days, the radio crate—still sealed, still sacred, still the whole reason any of them were here—a tool roll, a coil of line, and Marguerite's anemometer, which she'd lashed to the fig tree like a flag.

The anemometer spun. It had spun without stopping for two days. Dez had stopped looking at the numbers.

"Talk to me," Marguerite said.

Dez wrung out his shirt, which was a gesture, not an achievement. "She's holed. Port side, third strake down, about a meter and a half of seam let go where she hit the snag. Plus the crack from the gorge, plus the plank we cracked in the gorge, plus whatever the gorge did that I haven't found yet." He held up a hand before Marguerite could speak. "I can fix it. I want to be clear about that, because I know how it sounded. I can fix it. But not with what's in the tool roll, and not with her sitting in the water."

Marguerite looked at the Tern. She lay heeled against the mound's edge, held by two lines, half her freeboard gone, the river running through her like she was already a feature of the landscape. Dez watched Marguerite look at her the way he'd seen Marguerite look at debt figures—like something that could be survived if you refused to flinch at the total.

"She weighs what?" Marguerite said.

"Empty, maybe four hundred kilos. She's not empty."

"So we make her empty."

* * *

They spent the last of the light unloading her. Everything came off: the engine, the fuel, the water, the crates, the anchor, the spare prop, the cushions, the charts, the flares, the tinned fish Dez had been hoarding against a bad day. Okonjo ferried it up the mound in the dark, chest-deep in places, cursing with a fluency that Dez noted with something close to paternal pride. Ilse worked the lines, reading the current by feel in a way Dez had stopped questioning around the time she'd run the gorge. And Marguerite—Marguerite carried the radio crate herself, refusing help, refusing even to look like she might want it, and set it down on the highest ground on the island with the tenderness other people reserved for children.

By full dark the Tern floated high and light and still leaking, and the rain came down in the kind of sheets that made headlamps useless, and Dez stood on the mound in his underwear because everything else was soaked through and there was no point pretending otherwise, and looked at the job.

A meter and a half of open seam. Underwater. In the dark. In a river moving faster than a man could swim.

"Okay," he said, to nobody, to the boat, to God if God was taking calls tonight. "Okay."

* * *

Okonjo had never seen anyone work the way Dez worked, and he'd grown up around boatyards.

It wasn't speed. It was the opposite of speed—a total, unhurried economy, like the man had made peace with the night and the river and the impossibility of the task, and was simply going to do the next right thing until the next right thing was done or he was dead. Okonjo sat under the tarp with the tool roll open beside him, handing Dez whatever was asked for before it was asked for, and watched.

The plan, such as it was, had come out of Ilse.

"Sailcloth," she'd said, when Dez had finished cataloguing what they didn't have—no fiberglass, no epoxy worth the name, no spare planking. "Every boat on this river used to carry sailcloth. Not for sails. For this." And then she'd gone into the flooded undergrowth at the edge of the island with a machete and come back an hour later with an armful of resin vine bark and a lump of something dark and waxy that she called, in a language Okonjo didn't know, a word that sounded like *tree-blood*, and which she said the river pilots used before there were chandleries, before there was anything, and which hardened in water, which was the whole trick, the whole ancient trick.

They'd boiled the resin in a tin over the camp stove while Dez cut the sailcloth—salvaged from the Tern's own storm cover, which felt like a kind of surgery, the body donating tissue to itself—into overlapping patches the size of door panels.

Now Dez stood waist-deep in the black water beside the hull, holding a patch against the open seam with one hand while Okonjo, above him on the heeled deck, held the second line that kept Dez from being pulled under the boat and fed down the hot resin brush.

"Talk to me," Dez said. It was what they said to each other now. Marguerite said it. It had spread like a superstition, and Dez, of all people, understood superstition: you said the words because the words were a rope, and you held the rope.

"She's breathing," Okonjo said. "Seam's open. I can see your patch through it."

"Charming. Brush."

Okonjo dipped the brush in the tin of molten resin—black, smoking faintly even in the rain, smelling like a forest fire and a chapel at once—and passed it down. Dez worked it into the cloth in long strokes, and Okonjo heard him talking under his breath, and leaned out to listen, and realized Dez was talking to the boat. Not praying, exactly. Negotiating.

"You carried us through the gorge," Dez was saying. "You carried us through the fog with that launch on our tail. You carried a dead man's engine for six hundred kilometers on a fuel line I fixed with a bicycle inner tube. So you're going to hold this patch, and I'm going to hold you, and neither of us is going to embarrass the other. Agreed?"

The Tern groaned.

"I'll take that as agreed."

* * *

The lightning came around midnight, and it changed everything.

It started as heat flickers behind the western clouds, silent, the storm clearing its throat. Then a strike hit the far bank close enough that the thunder arrived with the light, one single concussion, and the whole drowned forest lit up green-white, every tree a negative of itself, the river a sheet of hammered tin, and Dez, in the water beside the hull, looked up at Okonjo with his face split by a grin.

"That's the one," Dez said. "That's the one I was waiting for."

"For what?"

"For light. We work by strikes. Every flash, I press and hold. Between flashes, you pour. We've got maybe four hours of this cell. Go."

So that was the night: lightning and darkness, lightning and darkness, the world strobing between photograph and blackout. In the flashes Dez moved like a man in a series of still photographs—hand flat on the patch, arm cocked, face turned up—each frame perfect, each frame gone. Between them Okonjo poured resin by feel, counting strokes the way Dez had taught him to count: *seven strokes across, five down, overlap the edges, don't let it cool in the tin.* The rain hammered. The river pulled. Once a log came down the current, black and huge, and Ilse's voice came out of the dark from the mound—*left, left, LEFT*—and Dez went left and the log went by close enough to touch and the work resumed as if nothing had happened, because nothing had, because everything had, and there was no time to know the difference.

Somewhere in the third hour Okonjo's arms stopped being arms. They were two aching ropes attached to a tin of hot resin, and he kept pouring because Dez kept holding, and Dez kept holding because Okonjo kept pouring, and Okonjo understood, in a way he'd never understood anything before, that this was what a crew was. Not a payroll. Not a name on a hull. It was two people in the dark keeping a boat alive because the boat was the only thing keeping them alive, and neither one willing to be the first to stop.

"Dez."

"Stroke."

"I'm just saying your name."

"Then say it quieter and keep pouring."

Okonjo laughed, and the laugh came out cracked and half a sob, and he kept pouring.

* * *

Marguerite stood at the edge of the tarp and watched her crew choose each other, and did not go down to help, because there was nothing she could do down there except be one more body in the dark, and because someone had to keep the stove fed and the resin hot, and because—she admitted it to herself, finally, in the lightning-light—because watching them was the only prayer she knew.

Dez, who crossed himself when the engine started and wouldn't sail on a Friday, holding a boat together with his bare hands and a dead language's worth of tree sap. Okonjo, who'd signed on because he idolized her, pouring resin with the steadiness of a man twice his age, and she could see the moment—the exact flash-lit moment—when the idolatry burned off and something better replaced it. Not worship. Partnership. He wasn't working for her anymore. He was working *with* Dez, and for the boat, and for the three other people on this island, and Marguerite felt that land in her chest like a change in the wind.

They had followed her into this. She had signed the papers, taken the contract, told them it was a simple delivery, and they had followed her up a river that was trying to kill them, and here they were at three in the morning, in a monsoon, on a termite mound, saving a boat they could have abandoned—could still abandon, could walk away from the whole rotten contract and let the river have the Tern and the permits and the debt and all of it.

They hadn't. They wouldn't. And she knew, watching Dez's hand press the last patch home in a blaze of lightning, that whatever she decided about the permits—whatever she'd already decided, in the locked room behind her eyes where she kept the real plans—they would follow her there too. That was the weight of it. Not the debt. Not Voss's cheerful voice. This: that they trusted her, and trust was the one cargo she couldn't jettison.

The stove hissed. She fed it the last of the resin bark and thought: *eleven days of rations, ten days on the permits, one night of work left in this boat and in us.*

* * *

Dawn came gray and without ceremony, the rain down to a steady drizzle, the lightning cell moved off east. Dez hauled himself up the mound on his arms because his legs were done, lay flat on his back in the mud, and started laughing—a low, cracked, unstoppable laugh that went on long enough that Okonjo sat down beside him and waited it out.

"What," Okonjo said.

"She held." Dez lifted one shaking hand and pointed at the Tern. She floated upright now, on her lines, and the bilge pump—Okonjo had reconnected the battery an hour ago—was cycling slower. Slower meant drier. Drier meant holding. "The old girl held. Sailcloth and tree-blood and spite. You write that down in your navigator's log, boy, because they don't teach it in any school: sailcloth, tree-blood, spite."

Okonjo wrote it down.

Ilse came down to the water's edge and looked at the hull for a long time, and then at Dez, and said something in her own language, the one with the word for tree-blood in it, and whatever it meant, Dez's ears went red, and he said, "That's—well. That's very kind," in a voice that suggested it had been very kind indeed.

Marguerite came last. She crouched at the edge, looked at the patched seam, at the pump cycle, at Dez flat in the mud, and said, "How long?"

"Engine's dry, hull's dry, fuel's on the mound. Give me today to let the resin cure and get her running, and we float on the morning tide of current." Dez squinted up at her. "Where are we floating to, Captain? Because I've been underwater all night, but I can count, and the permits expire in nine days, and the escarpment is five days upstream if the river lets us, and that launch is still out there, and I'd like to know what I'm bailing toward."

The camp went quiet. Even the anemometer seemed to slow.

Marguerite stood at the edge of the island with the gray light behind her, and Dez watched her do it—the arithmetic, the flinch, the refusal to flinch—and then she looked at each of them, one by one, Dez and Okonjo and Ilse, her crew, her people, and said:

"Upstream. All the way. We're not delivering permits."

She let that sit exactly one second.

"We're delivering the truth, and we're going to need every hour of this repair to get ahead of what's coming. So rest. Eat. Because tomorrow we run for the escarpment, and I don't intend to be slow."

Dez lay back in the mud and laughed again, and this time Okonjo laughed with him, and above them the rain thinned, and the river ran on, brown and endless, toward the thing they were now, all of them, committed to reaching.
