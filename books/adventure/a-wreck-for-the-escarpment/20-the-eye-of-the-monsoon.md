## 20. The Eye of the Monsoon

The rain stopped all at once, the way a door closes.

One breath the world was white noise and brown water, and the next Dez was standing in the cockpit with his mouth open, catching nothing on his tongue. Okonjo looked up from the chart like he'd been caught stealing. Even the river seemed embarrassed by its own sudden quiet.

"Engine's still good," Dez said, as if someone had accused it.

"I know," I said.

I didn't know anything. That was the problem. Twenty years of chasing weather and I'd never stood inside one like this — inside the eye of an early monsoon that had come up the valley two weeks ahead of every forecast, that had chased us out of the drowned forest, that was now holding its breath around us in a ring of cloud so tall and so black it looked like a country border. The light through it was strange, greenish, underwater light. The air was warm and utterly still. Somewhere beyond that wall, the storm was doing whatever storms do to the places they don't like.

Ilse came forward from the stern, wiping her hands on a rag she kept for no reason. She looked at the sky the way you look at a stranger who's just done you an unexpected kindness.

"How long?" I asked her.

She shrugged. "An hour. Maybe two. Maybe less." She turned her face up into the silence. "My father used to say the eye is the storm checking its work."

"Cheerful man."

"He's dead." She said it without weight, the way you report a depth sounding, and went back to the wheel.

* * *

We ran.

There was no discussion about whether to run. That decision had been made somewhere behind us, in the flooded side-channel, in Fen's hiding place at the middle station, in the burned pages of a survey ledger I now carried against my ribs like a second passport. The only question left was speed, and speed we had — what was left of it. The Tern limped along at three-quarters throttle because Dez wouldn't give her more than that, not with the patched plank sweating resin under the floorboards, not with the hull flexing in ways hulls aren't supposed to flex.

"She'll hold," Dez told me when I asked him straight out. He said it looking at the engine, not at me. "She held through the gorge. She'll hold."

The gorge had nearly killed us. I let him have the sentence.

The river in the eye was something I'll remember until I forget everything else. Flat. Glass-flat, or near enough, with the drowned forest standing mirrored on both sides of the channel — whole trees submerged to their crowns, their leaves hanging motionless in the green water like hair. Birds had come down into the calm from wherever birds go in a storm, and they were everywhere, feeding frantically on insects driven low by the pressure, swallows cutting the surface mist in long silver arcs. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also the most frightening thing I'd ever seen, because beauty at that scale, in that light, meant we were inside the machinery of something that did not know we existed.

Okonjo navigated by memory and nerve. The charts were useless — half the tributary markers were underwater, and the other half were lying. He stood at the bow with a hand shading his eyes even though there was no sun, reading the water itself: the color changes where the current ran deep, the smooth boil over a submerged fence line, the V of a snag just breaking the surface. He'd gotten good at it fast. Faster than I liked, sometimes, because it reminded me how young he was and how much of this river he was learning from a woman with a forged name.

"Channel holds left past the church," Ilse called from the wheelhouse. "Then open water to the escarpment road."

"The church is underwater," Okonjo said.

"The bell tower isn't. Aim for the tower, then bear left before you think you should."

He aimed for the tower. The tower rose out of the flood like a grave marker, brown water swirling around its louvers, and Okonjo bore left exactly when Ilse said, and the Tern slid through a gap between two drowned houses no wider than the boat herself. I watched a curtain rod drift past the gunwale. Someone's laundry, still clipped to it, gone gray with days of rain.

Nobody spoke about the villages anymore. We'd stopped somewhere after the third one. Halvern money had bought them out — bought them *out*, that was the phrase in the permit documents, as if the people were inventory being cleared — and the people had gone, and then the early monsoon had come, and now the money's work was finished by the water. Whole streets under six feet of current. A school roof with a flagpole through it. A pickup truck parked on the roof of a house, which meant someone had driven it there ahead of the rise, which meant someone had thought that would be enough.

Fen sat amidships with his wounded arm strapped tight, watching the drowned country go by with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. He hadn't slept more than an hour since we pulled him out of the middle station. Every time the engine changed pitch his whole body flinched, and I understood why without asking: the last boat sounds he'd heard had been Halvern's men coming for his team.

"Dr. Kessing went upstream," he'd told us, fever-bright, gripping my wrist with his good hand. "To the escarpment itself. To re-verify with her own instruments, because she knew they'd call her readings forged otherwise. She wanted it undeniable." His grip had tightened. "She made it undeniable. I saw the data. It's real. It's all real."

And now the storm had opened a door for us, and we were running through it.

* * *

At the first wide reach, I went below.

I told myself it was to check the bilge. The bilge was fine; Dez had it dry as a sermon. I told myself it was to check our stores, and I did that too, moving the ration tins around so they'd look counted. Then I sat down on the crate.

The radio crate. The original delivery. HALVERN CONSOLIDATED — EXPEDITION CONCESSION PERMITS & FIELD COMMS, stenciled on the side in cheerful block letters, sealed with tape printed with the company's little sunburst logo. Eleven days ago — twelve? — I'd have cut that seal with a box knife and been glad of the work. A contract was a contract. You delivered the thing, you got paid, you paid the interest, you stayed alive another month. That arithmetic had carried me twenty years across four coastlines and I'd never once questioned it, the way you never question the tide.

But arithmetic was different now. I'd learned that from a burned page.

I took the ledger page out of the oilskin wallet where it lived, inside my jacket, against my skin. Half-burned, soft as cloth at the scorched edge, the elevation figures marching down it in Dr. Kessing's cramped hand — figures that could not be true, Fen had said, unless the escarpment's geology was nothing like the surveys Halvern filed, unless the concession was a fiction built to harvest subsidies and damn a river. Impossible numbers. Numbers that had gotten tents slashed and instruments seized and a research team scattered into the bush and a deputy shot and left to die in a derelict station.

Numbers Voss knew about. Voss, whose voice on the satellite phone had been so warm, so reasonable, so full of returns.

I laid the ledger page flat on top of the crate. Then I took out the permits.

They were beautiful objects, I'll give them that. Heavy stock, embossed seals, the sunburst stamped in gold foil. Concession rights, transit authority, communications licenses — papers that would make the Tern legitimate at any checkpoint on this river, papers that would clear my debt the moment Voss confirmed delivery, papers that were, I understood now, the leash itself. Deliver these and I deliver the fiction. Deliver these and every drowned village downstream becomes legal. Deliver these and Amara Kessing's impossible numbers stay impossible forever.

I sat there in the rolling quiet of the eye and held a debt in one hand and the truth in the other, and I noticed that both of them weighed almost nothing.

That was the joke of it. Twenty years of carrying that debt, and it weighed nothing at all. It was paper. It had always been paper. What weighed something was Dez's hands, scarred and steady, keeping a dying boat alive out of loyalty he'd never once put into words. What weighed something was Okonjo learning this river from a liar because he believed the liar's lies were worth believing. What weighed something was a woman who'd lost her name and her brother to the same sunburst stamp, running this river on forged papers to finish a job the dead man started.

I wasn't going to destroy the permits. Not yet. Destroying them was a statement, and statements get you searched, and if Halvern's launch found us with torn-up concession papers in the bilge, every one of us would hang from the nearest convenient charge. No. The permits would travel. They'd sit right here in their beautiful crate, sealed and official, riding upriver toward the escarpment like a hostage.

But they weren't going to ride alone.

I took the canvas tool roll from under the bunk — the one I'd carried since my first season chasing, waxed and salt-stained, older than Okonjo. I unrolled it empty, laid the permits inside, and laid the ledger page on top of them, scorched edge to gold seal. Then I rolled it up and tied it with the drawstring, and the truth and the lie went into the dark together, wrapped in the same oilskin, bound for the same address.

It felt like loading a gun. It felt like writing a letter I'd never be able to unsend. Mostly it felt like the moment on a chase when you've committed to the intercept course and all that's left is to drive.

"Marguerite."

Ilse's voice through the deck above me, pitched low. I stuffed the roll back under the bunk, under the spare impeller kit where nobody looks, and climbed up into the green light.

* * *

She was pointing north.

The channel opened ahead of us into a flooded plain, and beyond the plain, for the first time since the storm closed in, we could see it: the Kessing Escarpment, rising out of the haze like the wall of the world. From this distance it didn't look like rock. It looked like weather — a cliff face a thousand feet high wearing its own private cloud, waterfalls threading down it in silver stitches, dozens of them, fed fat by the monsoon. The tributary we needed ran straight at its base, a brown ribbon through drowned green, and at the far end of the visible reach, tiny with distance, the relay tower stood on the summit ridge like a needle someone had forgotten.

"That's the road," Ilse said.

"That's the road," I said.

Okonjo had his binoculars up, sweeping the reach ahead, and I watched him stop sweeping. Watched his shoulders change.

"Stern wake," he said quietly. "Two points off our quarter. Big."

I didn't look right away. Looking fast is an amateur tell. I finished my coffee — cold, terrible, necessary — and then I turned, casual as Sunday, and raised my own glasses.

There. In the mirror-flat water of the eye, maybe four kilometers back, a shape. White superstructure, low profile, running without lights in broad daylight because it didn't need to hide from us and didn't care who else saw. It had been there the whole time, I realized. Inside the eye with us. Riding the same held breath.

Halvern's launch. Or one of them. Voss's, maybe — Voss himself, with his warm voice and his eleven days and his investments expecting returns, closing the distance while the storm checked its work.

"They're making eight knots," Okonjo said. "Maybe nine. We're making seven, best case, if Dez gives her everything."

"Dez won't give her everything," Dez said from the hatch, having appeared the way he always does when the boat is being discussed. "Not with that plank. She comes apart at full power in a seaway, and this—" he gestured at the impossibly calm river, the wall of black cloud ringing the horizon— "this is a seaway. This is the calmest seaway there is."

"So we can't outrun them," I said.

"No," Ilse said. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to reach the escarpment road first, and the road narrows." She was already turning the wheel, easing the Tern onto a heading that shaved the drowned treeline close enough to touch. "A big launch can't follow us up the tributary once the channel drops under two meters. And it will drop. The escarpment road isn't a road for them." Something moved through her face, quick and cold, and I recognized it — I'd seen it in the mirror often enough. "It's a road for us."

"You're sure about the depths?"

"I piloted supply barges up that tributary for two seasons." Two seasons, under a name that wasn't hers, for a company that erased her brother. She met my eyes and didn't look away. "I'm sure."

Behind us, the white shape grew by degrees, patient as interest accruing. Ahead of us, the escarpment wore its cloud, and the waterfalls stitched their silver seams, and somewhere up there — feverish, guarded, alive — a scientist sat on proof that could break a company, waiting to find out who reached her first.

The eye held. The river lay down flat as glass and carried us north, and every mile of it felt borrowed.

"Hold on to something," Ilse said, and gave the Tern all she had.
