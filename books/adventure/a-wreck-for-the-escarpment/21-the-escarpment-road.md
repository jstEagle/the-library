## 21. The Escarpment Road

The tributary began where the river forgot it was a river.

Okonjo saw it first from the bow, where he'd spent the last hour reading water the way Ilse had taught him—looking for the seams, the places where current folded over current and told you what lay underneath. The main channel bent east toward the lowlands, broad and brown and confident. But on the north bank, half-hidden behind a curtain of flooded bamboo, a narrower water opened into the hills. It didn't look like a road. It looked like a wound.

"That's it," Ilse said behind him. "The Kessing Reach. Old survey name. Nobody's run it commercial in twenty years."

"It's barely wide enough for the Tern."

"It's barely wide enough for a canoe. We're going anyway."

Marguerite stood at the helm with her arms crossed, watching the entrance the way a gambler watches dice leave the hand. "Dez. How's she sitting?"

Dez's voice came up from the engine well, muffled and unhappy. "Sitting like a boat that's been through a gorge, a side-channel, and a forest. Which is to say: sitting."

"Patch holding?"

"Patch is holding. Patch and I have an understanding. I don't ask it questions, it doesn't drown us."

"Then take us in."

The Tern swung north, and the main river let them go.

Okonjo felt it immediately—the change in the water's temperament. The main channel had been a crowd, a broad brown multitude of currents all pushing the same direction. The tributary was a whisper. The banks closed in, green and dripping, and the current slowed until the boat seemed to move through something closer to soup than river. Every sound got louder: the engine's laboring note, the tick of rain on the canopy above, the creak of the patched plank under Dez's feet as he shifted his weight.

They'd been running the flooded side-channel for two days since the escape from Halvern's launch. Two days of drowned forest, of backing out of dead ends, of bailing and bickering and sleeping on the termite mound island with one ear always cocked for engines. The repair had held. Barely. The sailcloth patch over the cracked plank had cured into something like scar tissue—ugly, functional, permanent only in the way that scars are permanent. The Tern was alive, but she was limping.

Now they were asking her to climb.

"Depth," Ilse said.

Okonjo swung the lead line out and hauled it back. "Four meters. Bottom's soft."

"Four meters." Ilse said it like a prayer of thanks. "In the dry season this is knee-deep. The whole reach. That's why they abandoned it."

"Abandoned what?" Dez asked, coming up from the engine well with grease to his elbows.

"You'll see."

He saw it twenty minutes later, when the bamboo curtain parted and the first village rose out of the water.

Okonjo had grown up on the delta, in a stilt town where the river came and went with the seasons and everyone built their lives around the water's moods. He knew what drowned country looked like. He knew flood marks on posts, knew the brown tide line on a wall, knew how a town looked when the river was winning.

This wasn't that. This was a town that had *chosen* the water, and then been left to it.

The houses stood on concrete stilts—concrete, not wood, the kind of stilts you pour when someone's paying you to build for permanence. Two stories of them, three, laid out in a grid along streets that were now canals. The roofs were corrugated steel, new-looking under the grime. A school building sat at the center of the grid with a flagpole in front of it, and the flagpole's halyard clinked against the metal in the wind, a small bright sound that carried impossibly far over the water.

Nobody was there.

"Slow down," Marguerite said quietly.

Ilse already had. The Tern drifted down what had been the main street, past houses with doors standing open, past a market shelter where the stalls were still bolted down, past a playground where a swing frame stood in two meters of green water, the swings themselves long gone. On the school's wall, someone had painted a mural—children holding hands around a globe, the letters HALVERN BUILDS TOMORROW arched over their heads in cheerful yellow. The paint was peeling. The globe's oceans had faded to the same color as the floodwater, so the children appeared to be holding hands around nothing.

"Resettlement scheme," Ilse said. Her voice was flat, the flatness of someone reading from a document she'd memorized. "Halvern bought the concession rights to this whole valley. The surveys said the dam would raise the water forty meters. So they paid people to move up. Concrete stilts, steel roofs, a school, a clinic. 'Build tomorrow.' That was the actual slogan."

"And then?" Dez asked, though Okonjo could hear in his voice that he'd already guessed.

"And then the water came up eleven meters instead of forty, and stopped. The dam design was based on surveys that said the valley walls were solid granite all the way down. They weren't. The water found the porous rock and just—left. Seeped away into the escarpment like it was never there." Ilse turned the wheel a few degrees, easing them past a submerged pickup truck, its cab roof just breaking the surface, a garden of algae growing across the hood. "Eleven meters was enough to drown the old villages. Not enough to justify the new ones. So Halvern stopped paying, the services stopped coming, and everyone who could leave, left. The ones who couldn't—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. The village finished it for her: the open doors, the market with nothing to sell, the swing frame standing in the water like a gallows.

Okonjo thought about the survey ledger page they'd pulled from the burned camp, the elevation figures that made no sense, the numbers Dr. Amara Kessing had died—or nearly died—chasing. He'd understood it abstractly until now. Fraud was a word for documents. Standing in the drowned street of a town built on the fraud, he understood it as a physical thing, a thing with weight and water and a clinking flagpole.

"They knew," he said. He didn't mean to say it out loud. "Halvern knew the surveys were wrong when they built all this."

"Of course they knew," Ilse said. "The falsification *is* the business model. You don't falsify a survey because you believe it. You falsify it because the subsidy payments are tied to construction milestones, and construction milestones are tied to the survey. You build the dam, you collect the money, the dam doesn't work, you write it off as geological bad luck, and you move to the next valley." She looked back at him, and for a moment the pilot's mask slipped and Okonjo saw what was underneath—six years of this, six years of drowned villages and burned ledgers and a brother's name nobody would say. "The escarpment is the next valley. Unless Kessing's data says otherwise, loudly enough."

Marguerite hadn't spoken since they entered the reach. She stood at the helm with her jaw set, and Okonjo watched her look at the school, at the mural, at the open doors, and he watched her do the thing she always did—the arithmetic, the cold beautiful arithmetic she thought nobody could see her doing. Deliver the permits, save the crew, clear the debt. Or. Or. He'd seen her face when she'd packed the permits and the ledger page together in the same waterproof bag, back in the storm's eye, and he'd understood then that some kind of decision had been made, even if she hadn't said it, even if she might not know it herself yet.

He didn't ask. You didn't ask the captain her arithmetic mid-voyage. You just made sure the boat was ready for whatever she decided.

* * *

The second village was worse, because it was emptier.

The first one at least had the dignity of abandonment—things left behind, doors open, a town that had been lived in and then not. The second village had never been finished. The stilts rose out of the water in rows, concrete pillars with rebar sticking out of their tops like stubble, whole streets of foundations with no houses on them. A sign at the entrance read PHASE II — KESSING RIVERSIDE ESTATES, and beneath it, smaller: A HALVERN CONSOLIDATED COMMUNITY.

"Estates," Dez said, with a laugh that had no humor in it anywhere.

"They sold these," Ilse said. "Downstream. Pre-construction. Fishermen, farmers, people who wanted to be near the new dam economy. Paid deposits. Some of them paid everything they had." She eased the Tern through a gap between two foundation rows, the patched plank groaning as the hull kissed a submerged formwork board. "There's a family in Port Meridian, the Adeyemis, who've been litigating for four years to get their deposit back. Halvern's lawyers have buried them in motions. The husband drives a taxi now. The wife cleans offices at the Halvern tower." She said it without emphasis, without heat, the way you'd recite a cargo manifest, and somehow that was the worst part. She'd had years to make a list. This was one entry of many.

Okonjo took soundings and called them, and Ilse steered, and the unfinished village slid past on both sides. Rain came and went in the way of monsoon country—hard for ten minutes, a mist for twenty, hard again. Everything dripped. Everything was the color of wet stone and wet leaves and wet concrete. The world had been reduced to a single palette, and the palette was grief.

At the far edge of the village, on the last row of foundations, someone had painted over the Halvern sign. Okonjo saw it as they passed—white paint, slapped on in a hurry, and under the white, in red, in letters big enough to read from the water:

WE WAITED FOR THE WATER. THE WATER WAITED FOR US.

"Who wrote that?" Dez asked.

"Doesn't matter," Ilse said. "It's true either way."

* * *

They made camp that night on the roof of the clinic.

It was the only dry ground for kilometers—the tributary had widened into a drowned floodplain, and the clinic's flat concrete roof stood a meter and a half above the waterline, a gray island with a rusted rain gauge bolted to one corner. Dez got a stove going on a sheet of tin. Marguerite recalculated rations for the fourth time since the gorge, her pencil scratching in the lamplight, and Okonjo climbed to the highest corner of the roof with the binoculars and stood watch, because that was his job now and he'd stopped needing to be told.

From up there, the drowned country spread out to every horizon. Water to the south, where they'd come from. Water to the north, deepening toward the escarpment, which he could just see in the last gray light—a long dark line across the world, like a wall built by something with no interest in human affairs. Between here and there: rooftops. Dozens of them. Some with houses, some without. A whole archipelago of the almost-built, rising out of the flood like the bones of a promise that had died before it was born.

He heard Ilse come up beside him. She didn't say anything for a while. Neither did he. That was the thing about Ilse—he'd learned that silence with her wasn't empty. It was just two people agreeing not to fill the air with anything false.

"My grandmother's town looked like this," Okonjo said finally. "Not drowned. But the same shape of it. A company came through when she was young—different name, same company really, they all wear different names—and they promised a road. Built the first eight kilometers. Paved it, painted the lines, put up a sign. Then the concession changed hands and the road stopped. My grandmother walked that eight kilometers her whole life, to the place where the pavement ends, and turned around. She said the worst thing a road can do is start."

Ilse was quiet a moment. "She sounds like she understood Halvern."

"She understood people who sell tomorrow." He lowered the binoculars. "Ilse. The data. Kessing's data. If we get it out—if it works—does any of this come back? These villages. The people."

"Some of it. The water'll drop when the dam's decommissioned, or never finished, whichever. The land will come back. The land always comes back." She looked north, at the dark line of the escarpment. "Whether the people do is another question. You can't give someone back four years of a taxi they drove to pay for a house that never existed. But you can stop it happening to the next valley. That's what the data buys. Not a rescue. A fence."

Okonjo nodded slowly. He was twenty-three years old and three weeks ago he'd thought the biggest thing he'd ever do was earn his pilot's papers. Now he was standing on a drowned clinic in a valley that had been sold and unsold, carrying a scientist's proof of a lie, and the fence they were building was made of radio waves. It didn't feel small. It felt like the eight kilometers of pavement, but going somewhere.

Below them, Marguerite closed her ledger. "First watch, Okonjo. Then Dez. Ilse, you're on at three—we run at first light. I want the escarpment base by tomorrow night."

"You want a lot," Dez said, but he was already rolling into his bedroll.

* * *

First light came the color of pewter, and they ran.

The tributary narrowed as it climbed. The floodplain gave way to a channel, the channel to a gorge of green, and the current, which had been a whisper, began to find its voice again. Okonjo stood in the bow with the lead line and called the depths, and Ilse stood behind him with her hands on the wheel, and between them the Tern climbed the drowned road toward the wall of the world.

They passed a third village at midmorning. This one was different. This one was occupied.

Not by people—Okonjo saw that within seconds—but by the evidence of a departure so recent it was still warm. Laundry hung on lines between two stilts, soaked and sagging, abandoned mid-chore. A cooking fire on a platform platform still smoked, or seemed to—maybe it was just the rain hitting warm ash. A dog stood on a roof and watched them pass without barking, which was somehow worse than barking. And on the clinic wall, over a faded HALVERN HEALTH FOR ALL, someone had painted a date. Three weeks old. The week the expedition went silent.

"They didn't leave," Dez said quietly, from the engine well. "They were made to leave."

"Halvern cleared the valley before the inspection teams came through," Ilse said. "Can't have witnesses to the second round of surveys. The ones that had to match the first ones." Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her voice was not, quite. "My brother audited one of these clearances. Upstream. That's in the report too, if we get it out. It's all in the report."

Okonjo called a depth—"Three meters, rock shelf, port side"—and Ilse took them past, and the village with the smoking fire slid away behind them, and nobody spoke for a long time.

The rain thickened toward afternoon. The escarpment grew until it wasn't a line anymore but a presence, a gray-green cliff country filling the northern sky, waterfalls threading down its face like the strings of some enormous instrument. Okonjo had grown up around water his whole life and had never seen water fall from that height. It didn't fall, exactly. It *descended*, with the patience of something that had been descending since before there were people to watch.

And then, in the last hour of light, with the escarpment base close enough to see the individual trees on its slopes, Okonjo heard the engine.

He knew engines. He'd grown up around them, catalogued them the way other kids catalogued birds—the two-stroke stutter of a fishing punt, the diesel knock of a cargo barge, the high clean whine of a fast patrol launch. This was the third one. High. Clean. More than one engine, running in formation, and coming from behind.

He didn't shout. He'd learned that too, in the side-channel, in the forest, in the long paranoid days since the launch had first appeared in the fog. He turned, caught Marguerite's eye at the helm, and held up three fingers. Then pointed back the way they'd come.

Marguerite went still. Then she was moving—killing the running lights, dropping her voice to a register Okonjo had only heard twice before, both times in the gorge. "Ilse. Options."

Ilse's eyes went to the escarpment, to the cliff base sliding past on the right, to the black mouths of side ravines where the tributary's water spilled off the plateau in braided silver threads. Her jaw worked.

"Two ravines ahead," she said. "First one's a dead end, I think. Second one—there's a cave channel. Behind the falls. If it's still open this season."

"If it isn't?" Dez asked.

"Then we're a leaking boat in a box canyon with company men behind us and a cliff in front of us." She spun the wheel. "So let's hope it is."

The Tern surged forward, running dark, running for the wall of the world, and behind them the formation engines came on—no longer hiding, no longer pretending to be anything but what they were—and the first white searchlight beam swung out over the water and began to sweep the gorge, coming for them.
