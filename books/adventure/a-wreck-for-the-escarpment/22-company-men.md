## 22. Company Men

The bend at Kilometre 340 was the kind of bend Ilse hated—wide, blind, and framed on both sides by drowned forest that gave a boat nowhere to go but forward or backward. She'd run it a dozen times in another life, under another name, hauling survey gear for men who smiled too easily. She knew what waited around bends like this.

She'd been hoping she was wrong for two days.

"Current's pushing us wide," Okonjo said from the chart table, pencil hovering over the river trace. "If we hug the inside we'll catch the deadfall."

"Then we take it wide and pray," Dez said from the engine hatch, grease to his elbows. "Praying's free."

Ilse didn't answer. She was watching the water ahead of them, where the morning fog sat low and grey over the current, and where—just at the edge of visibility—a shape moved that wasn't fog. A long shape. Low. Sitting still against the current the way only an anchored boat can sit still.

"Marguerite," she said quietly.

Marguerite came up from the cabin with the ledger page folded in her jacket pocket, as she'd carried it for three days now, like a coal she couldn't put down. "What?"

"Company launch. Anchored across the channel." Ilse kept her hands easy on the wheel. "Two hundred metres past the bend. Maybe less."

Okonjo's pencil stopped. "You can see that far?"

"I know the anchorage. There's a gravel bar there. It's the only place a boat that size can hold position in this current without burning fuel." She said it flatly, and let them hear what was underneath it: *I've done this before.*

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Three days ago Ilse had told them about Halvern, about forged surveys, about Kessing going upstream alone—and had kept back the rest, the part with her brother in it, the part that lived behind her ribs like a swallowed hook. Marguerite hadn't pushed. That was worse than pushing.

"How fast can you turn us?" Marguerite asked.

"Not fast enough. Not here." Ilse nodded at the banks—the flooded forest came down to the waterline in a solid wall of trunks and vine, no side channels for six kilometres either way. She'd checked. She'd been checking all night. "They picked their ground. We're in it."

Dez closed the engine hatch slowly, the way a man closes a coffin lid. "So we talk to them."

"We talk to them," Marguerite said.

* * *

The launch was white and new and wrong for the river—too clean, too straight-backed, its hull unscarred by a single sandbar. It sat across the channel with the calm of something that had never been told no. On its foredeck stood four men in matching rain jackets, and behind them, under a canopy, a fifth man in a dry shirt, hands clasped, smiling before anyone could see his face clearly enough to know he was smiling.

Voss.

Ilse felt the recognition go through her like cold water through a boot. She'd never met him. She'd met his type, whole offices of his type, the cheerful men who'd signed off on the escarpment surveys while her brother's audit report went into a shredder and her brother went into a river. But she knew the name from six years of digging, and seeing him in the flesh—dry, comfortable, *waiting*—made her understand how much money had come upriver after them. You didn't send Voss for a routine stop. You sent Voss when you wanted something finished politely.

"Tern, ahoy!" one of the jacketed men called, cheerful, hand raised. "Easy on approach! Mr. Voss would like a word!"

Marguerite brought the Tern alongside with the smoothness of twenty years of bad docks, and Ilse watched her do it and thought: *she knows too.* Of course she knew. Nobody runs a delivery this deep into monsoon country without doing the arithmetic. The permits were in the hold. The debt was in Marguerite's chest. And Voss was sitting under a canopy offering both of them a way out.

"Captain Zhou." Voss stepped to the rail as the boats kissed fenders. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, silver-haired, ruddy, with the open face of a man who coached children's football. "What a pleasure. I feel like I know you already—from your file, of course. May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He stepped aboard the Tern like a man stepping into his own kitchen, and two of the jacketed men followed, and Ilse counted the ones who stayed on the launch without moving her head. Two on the launch. One at the wheel of it. Three aboard. Plus whatever was out of sight below the launch's deck, because there was always more below decks with these people.

"Ilse." Voss turned to her and offered his hand.

She didn't take it. "You have me confused with someone else."

"Do I?" His smile widened fractionally, and for half a second something moved behind it—not menace exactly, but inventory. A man pricing a thing. "My mistake. These rivers are full of people starting over. It's one of the things I love about them."

"You don't love anything," Ilse said. "You hold liens on it."

Voss laughed, delighted, and looked at Marguerite as if to say *isn't she wonderful.* "Captain, your pilot is a treasure. Wherever did you find her?"

"The locks," Marguerite said. "She found me, mostly. What do you want, Voss? You didn't burn diesel all the way up from the delta to shake my hand."

"No, I didn't." He sighed, and the sigh was a performance so good Ilse almost believed it. He walked aft, unhurried, trailing one finger along the Tern's rail like a buyer at auction. "I want to tell you a story. Indulge me. There was once a delivery contract—concession permits, some radio equipment—for a scientific expedition on the Kessing reach. Routine work. Honest work. The captain who took the contract is capable, experienced, and—" he glanced back at her, warm as a hearth— "unfortunately in debt. Substantially. To people who invested in her because they believe in her."

"The optimists," Marguerite said.

"The optimists," Voss agreed happily. "And here's the tragedy, Captain. The expedition has gone silent. Communications lost. Possibly disaster, possibly foul play—we simply don't know. Which means the permits may be worthless, the contract void, and the investment—" he spread his hands, sorrowful— "at risk. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the captain completes her delivery. Permits transferred, equipment handed off, paperwork signed. Then the investment is protected. Then everyone is made whole." He paused, and let the silence do its work, and then said, lightly, as if it were an afterthought: "And in fact, if the delivery is completed promptly—within, say, four days—I'm authorized to forgive the balance entirely. Principal, interest, penalties. A clean slate. Imagine it, Captain. No more running."

Ilse watched Marguerite's face and saw the offer land exactly where it was designed to land. Twenty years of measuring life in miles. Twenty years of waking up already owing. And here was a small silver-haired man saying *you can stop*—and meaning none of it, meaning the opposite, meaning *you will be mine forever,* but saying it in the only language Marguerite had ever been allowed to hear.

"And the radio crate?" Marguerite asked. Her voice was level. Too level.

"Delivered as contracted. To whom is your choice, of course." Voss smiled. "Though I'd note the expedition is silent, Captain. Delivering to nobody helps nobody. Whereas delivering to our advance team at the middle station—they could attempt rescue coordination. Very civic-minded."

*The middle station.* Where Fen was hiding, wounded, three days behind them. Ilse kept her breathing slow.

"There's a second matter," Voss went on, and turned, and looked at Ilse again, and this time he didn't pretend it was casual. "A formality. Our records indicate a person of interest may be traveling on this vessel. A woman using documents that don't belong to her. Formerly employed—loosely speaking—by a firm we acquired some years ago. She took things when she left. Data. If she's aboard, and she steps onto my launch voluntarily, the company considers the matter closed. No charges. No inquiries. Captain Zhou's clean slate remains untouched." He spread his hands. "Everyone walks away. Isn't that better than everyone drowning in each other's problems?"

The rain had started again, fine and needling. Nobody moved to get out of it.

"And if she doesn't exist?" Ilse said.

"Then nothing changes." Voss's eyes stayed on hers, friendly, bottomless. "But we both know she exists. I've read her file. Well—what's left of it. Files are funny things. So much depends on who gets to them first." He tilted his head. "Your brother was thorough, you know. For an auditor. Thoroughness is admirable. It's also what got him noticed."

The words went into Ilse like a blade finding an old scar by memory alone. Six years she'd hunted this man's signature through other men's mouths, and here he was, saying her brother's name sideways, in the rain, on a leaking boat, smiling. She felt Dez shift behind her. Felt Okonjo go very still by the chart table. Felt Marguerite watching her, weighing her, deciding things.

"Get off my boat," Marguerite said.

It came out quiet, which somehow made it heavier. Voss blinked at her, genuinely surprised, the first honest expression he'd shown.

"Captain—"

"You heard me. You boarded without invitation, you've insulted my crew, and you've offered me a bribe dressed up as mercy. Get off my boat." Marguerite stepped between him and Ilse, and Ilse saw that her hands were shaking—not with fear, Ilse realized, but with the effort of not doing something louder. "Four days. Fine. I'll consider your offer. Now leave, before I remember I'm the one holding the permits you want."

Voss studied her for a moment. Then he laughed again, softer, and shook his head with something that looked almost like respect.

"There she is," he said. "The woman we invested in. Captain, I'll be at the confluence below the gorge for the next four days. My offer stands. All of it." He put a dry card into Marguerite's hand—white, heavy stock, absurd upriver—and patted her once on the shoulder. "Think of your crew. They're lovely people. It would be a shame if loyalty cost them more than it earned."

He stepped back onto the launch. The fenders parted. The jackets waved, cheerful, and the white boat swung its bow downstream with obscene ease, and in ninety seconds the fog took it and the river was just the river again.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

* * *

Dez broke the silence first, spitting over the rail. "Four days. He gives us four days like it's a gift."

"It is a gift," Okonjo said. "It means they can't stop us yet. If they could, we'd be at the bottom of the channel right now." He looked younger than usual, and scared, and trying to reason his way out of it. "Right? They need the permits signed. That's why Voss came himself. That's leverage."

"That's bait," Ilse said.

She went to the wheel and brought the Tern around the bend, past the gravel bar where the launch had anchored—there were cigarette ends in a tin on the bar, still dry, and a mooring stake driven fresh into the gravel—and then upstream, into the narrowing channel, because stopping felt like dying.

Marguerite came and stood beside her at the wheel. For a while neither of them spoke. The rain thickened, then eased, then thickened, the monsoon breathing.

"He knew your brother's name," Marguerite said finally. "Or enough of it."

"Yes."

"And you've known who he was since the locks. Since before the locks." It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was accurate.

"Yes," Ilse said again. She kept her eyes on the channel. "I told you about Halvern. About the surveys. I didn't tell you the rest because the rest is mine, and because every time I've given it to someone, it's been used to price me." She paused. "That's not an excuse. It's an explanation. You can throw me off at the gorge if you want."

Marguerite was quiet long enough that the engine noise filled the space between them.

"Voss offered me everything," she said at last. "Twenty years of debt, gone, in exchange for handing permits to the men who gutted an expedition. And do you know what I thought? For about four seconds?" She laughed, short and joyless. "I thought about what it would be like to sleep without calculating."

"I know," Ilse said. "I priced my brother's life against a name once. Four seconds is generous."

Ahead of them the channel bent north, toward the gorge, and the walls of the valley began to rise on either side—green stone going up and up into cloud, the escarpment showing itself for the first time, vast and indifferent. Somewhere above it, in that weather, Amara Kessing was carrying verified data down a mountain or dead on one. Somewhere behind them, a white launch was idling at a confluence, counting days.

"Four days," Marguerite said. "Gorge transit takes one, if the water lets us. Middle station's another half day after that." She turned and looked at Ilse, and her eyes had the flat brightness Ilse had learned to read as decision forming. "Fen's at the middle station. Fen saw what happened. If we're going to spend those four days on anything worth spending them on—"

The radio crackled. Channel nine, the expedition frequency, dead for eleven days.

"—Tern, Tern, this is relay point seven, do you copy—"

Ilse's hands went still on the wheel. The voice was thin, storm-chewed, half-swallowed by static—but it was a voice, a live one, coming from somewhere high and close, from the direction of the escarpment itself.

Okonjo lunged for the set. "Say again, relay point seven—say again—"

The static rolled, and beneath it, faint as a pulse, the voice began again.
