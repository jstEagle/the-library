## 23. The Refusal

Voss's launch sat in the current like a knife laid flat on a table.

It had come around the bend at half past noon, when the rain thinned to a gray drizzle and the river ran the color of wet slate. No running lights, no hail on the radio—just the low diesel growl and then the shape of it, white hull, company blue stripe, riding high because it carried men instead of cargo. It swung across the Tern's bow with the casual authority of something that had never once been told no, and Voss stepped out onto its deck under an umbrella held by one of his cheerful associates.

"Marguerite Zhou," he called across the water, and his voice carried the way voices carry in rain-quiet air, every syllable arriving intact. "You look terrible. Monsoon doesn't agree with you."

Marguerite stood at the Tern's rail with her hands loose at her sides and her heart going like a bilge pump. Behind her, she heard Dez come up the companionway without being told to. She heard Okonjo kill the engine—not because they'd been ordered to but because Okonjo understood that right now the Tern's only armor was stillness. Ilse stayed below. That was the arrangement, made in a whisper ten minutes ago when the launch first appeared on the fog line: if it's him, I'm not on deck. He can't want what he can't see.

"I've been busy," Marguerite said.

"Delivering." Voss smiled. He was wearing a dry shirt, a pressed collar, a watch worth more than the Tern. "That's my girl. That's exactly what I paid for." He gestured with two fingers, and the launch drifted closer, fenders already hanging over its side as if this were a friendly raft-up, as if they were all colleagues. "Here's where we are. The permits expire in nine days. The expedition is unreachable, which is regrettable but not my department. What *is* my department is you, and the crate in your hold, and the small matter of four hundred and sixty thousand in principal plus interest plus handling fees, which I am authorized—" he let the word sit there, gleaming, "—to make disappear. Today. Right now. You hand over the permits and the radio equipment, you keep your boat, your crew keeps their jobs, and Ilse—" he paused, delicately, the way a man pauses before naming a disease, "—comes aboard with us. That's it. That's the whole transaction. Everyone walks away richer or, in her case, everyone walks away."

The rain ticked on the deck plates. Downriver, behind them, the falls they'd been threading toward all morning made a sound like continuous distant applause.

"You know who she is," Marguerite said.

"I know who she was," Voss said. "Past tense matters in my business. People are assets until they become liabilities, and she crossed that line six years ago in an audit office in Halvern's own building." He spread his hands, the umbrella tilting, rain beading off his shoulders. "Look, I'll be honest with you, because I've always liked you, Marguerite—you remind me of me before I got smart. You're not a criminal. You're not even a rebel. You're a woman with a number attached to her name, and numbers are just weather. They pass. I'm offering you the umbrella."

Marguerite thought about the garage on the coast, three years ago, the smell of solder and the phone ringing for the ninth time that day. She thought about signing papers she hadn't read while cheerful people watched her do it. She thought about the burned ledger page in the waterproof bag against her ribs, the impossible elevations, Fen's face when he'd described the inspection that wasn't an inspection. She thought about Amara Kessing somewhere up that cliff, feverish, holding the truth in her hands like a live wire.

And she thought—clearly, coldly, with the part of her brain that read storms—that Voss had just told her everything she needed to know. He'd said *everyone walks away*. He'd said it about a delivery job. Nobody offered debt forgiveness and a clean exit for a box of permits unless the box was the whole game.

"The permits are worthless," she said. "Aren't they. That's why you need them back. Not to deliver—to bury."

Something moved behind Voss's smile, quick as a fish under a boat. Gone before it surfaced.

"Marguerite."

"Because if they were real," she went on, and her voice came out steadier than she'd expected, "you'd want them delivered. Delivery is the whole point of a concession. You'd be begging me to hurry. Instead you're offering me a fortune to turn around." She shook her head slowly. "I spent twenty years reading weather, Voss. I know what a pressure drop looks like. This is one."

He sighed. It was a good sigh, practiced, almost fond. "I was hoping we wouldn't do this the expensive way."

"There's an expensive way?"

"There always is." He glanced past her shoulder, at the Tern—at Dez at the rail, at Okonjo frozen at the helm, at the wheelhouse with its dark interior where a pilot with a forged name might or might not be listening. "Last chance. The offer has a shelf life, and so, honestly, does the patience of the men behind me."

Marguerite looked at the men behind him. Four of them, maybe five, standing along the launch's deck with the loose readiness of people who did this often enough to be bored by it. One of them had a long gun slung across his back under a rain cape, and he was watching her the way she'd once watched a waterspout: professionally, without malice, calculating distance and drift.

She thought about saying yes. God help her, some tired animal part of her—the part that had been running since the coast, since before the coast, since the first storm she'd fled instead of measured—whispered that four hundred and sixty thousand was freedom, was sleep, was never hearing this man's cheerful voice again. Take the deal. Hand over the paper. Let the escarpment keep its secrets; secrets have kept fine for years.

But the animal part of her had been wrong about a lot of things, and it had been loudest right before the worst decisions.

"No," Marguerite said.

Voss tilted his head. "No?"

"No." And then, because she wanted him to hear it plainly, wanted it on the record of the rain and the river and whatever happened next: "I'm not giving you the permits. I'm not giving you Ilse. And I'm done measuring what I owe you. Find another asset."

For a moment nobody moved. The two boats knocked gently together, fender to hull, intimate as a handshake. Voss looked at her with genuine, unfeigned sadness—the sadness of a man closing a file—and said, quietly, to the man with the rain cape: "Board her. Recover the cargo. The crew can swim."

What happened next happened fast, and Marguerite's memory of it would always come back to her in pieces, the way violent weather does.

She remembered throwing herself sideways into the wheelhouse and screaming *go go go* and feeling the Tern lurch under her as Okonjo—bless him, nineteen years old and terrified and absolutely reliable—slammed the throttle forward before the order finished leaving her mouth. The gun on the launch cracked twice, flat sounds swallowed by water, and the portside rail sparked and shrieked where a round peeled a groove through the steel.

She remembered Ilse bursting up from below as if she'd been waiting with her hand on the throttle the whole time—which, Marguerite would later learn, she had—shoving Okonjo aside at the helm and taking the wheel with both hands, saying one word, "*Falls*," in a voice that closed the discussion.

"You're insane," Dez shouted from the deck, hauling himself through the door as a third shot punched through the wheelhouse window and starred the glass into a white flower. Something hot drew a line across Dez's upper arm and Dez looked down at it with offended surprise, more inconvenienced than hurt, blood welling slow through the sleeve. "They're shooting at us! At the *boat*!"

"They were always going to shoot at the boat," Ilse said, eyes on the water. "Hold on to something bolted down."

The launch fell in behind them, engines opened up, and Marguerite understood with a lurch that the geometry was simple and bad: the Tern was loaded, patched, and slow, and the launch was none of those things. In open water they'd be run down inside of ten minutes. Which meant Ilse wasn't running away from the falls.

She was running toward them.

"Ilse." Marguerite grabbed the back of the pilot's chair. "Ilse, that's a class-five approach in flood conditions, nobody runs the Kessing falls in flood—"

"Nobody who doesn't know there's a channel behind them." Ilse's voice was flat and total, the voice of someone reciting a thing learned at the cost of something irreplaceable. "My brother found it. It's on the old survey maps, the real ones, before Halvern redrew them. There's a slot in the east wall behind the curtain—a cave channel, a back door cut by the river when the escarpment was younger. It goes nowhere useful. It goes *away*, which today is the same thing." She threw the wheel hard over. "Pray it hasn't silted in."

The falls grew ahead of them with terrible speed. From this distance it didn't look like water; it looked like the edge of the world, a brown-white cataract the width of a town, dropping thirty meters into a pit of spray and thunder. The sound arrived in Marguerite's chest before it reached her ears, a physical pressure, the river announcing itself. Behind them the launch's engine note climbed, hungry. Ahead, the water went over the edge in a hundred braided tongues, and between two of the largest—where the east wall leaned out and the falling water curved away from the rock—there was a darkness. A seam. A shadow that might have been depth or might have been nothing at all.

"That's not a channel," Dez said. "That's a rumor."

"It's a door," Ilse said. "Okonjo—bowline, now, both hands. Dez—get below and put your body between the radio crate and anything that comes through that hull. Marguerite." She glanced at her, and for the first time since the locks, since the forged papers and the too-perfect knowledge of the reach, Ilse looked afraid. Not of the falls. Of failing. "If I'm wrong, I'm sorry. About all of it."

"You're not wrong," Marguerite said, and found she believed it, and hated how much she believed it, and held on.

The Tern hit the lip of the turbulence and the world became noise.

Later, Marguerite would try to describe the transit and fail, because there was no describing it—only the memory of it, stored in the body like a scar. The bow dropped. The river picked the boat up the way a gull picks up a fish, casually, entirely, and threw it. Water came over the foredeck in a solid green wall and took the spare fuel drum with it, gone, fifty liters of diesel donated to the flood. The starboard gunwale groaned a note Marguerite had never wanted to hear a boat make. Okonjo was screaming something that was either a prayer or a heading correction. Spray filled the air so completely that the sky and the river traded places, and through all of it Ilse stood at the wheel making tiny corrections, minute, surgical, reading the chaos the way Marguerite read cloud bands—as a text, as instructions—steering the Tern along a thread of smoother water that existed for perhaps forty meters between the falling curtain and the black rock, a corridor the width of a church aisle with a waterfall on one side and a stone wall on the other and death in both directions wearing different clothes.

A shot cracked somewhere above the roar—pointless, absurd, a man firing into a waterfall—and then even that was gone, erased, because the curtain closed behind them and the sound of the world changed.

Silence. Not true silence—nothing near those falls was ever silent—but a sudden, shocking hush relative to what had been, the roar reduced to a deep constant drumming beyond the rock, felt in the teeth more than heard. The Tern drifted in a long chamber of black water and wet stone, lit by a single shaft of gray daylight falling through a crack in the ceiling far overhead, catching the mist, holding it suspended like the inside of a pearl.

They had come through. The door had been real. Ilse's brother had found it, and Ilse had kept it, six years, waiting for a day worth spending it on.

Marguerite became aware of sounds she didn't like: the engine coughing, water sloshing in the bilge with a new urgency, and Dez, sitting on the deck plating with his back against the bulkhead, pressing his forearm and laughing in short, shocked bursts.

"Grazed," he said, before anyone could ask. "Grazed, it's a scratch, don't look at me like that." But his face was gray, and the blood was not stopping fast, and Marguerite tore the sleeve off her own shirt and knelt and wrapped it hard while he hissed.

Then she saw the radio.

The wheelhouse set—their link to channel nine, to the middle station, to Fen, to any ear in the world—had taken the round that came through the window. The casing was split, the dial glass shattered, a smell of hot solder and burnt insulation rising from the guts of it. Okonjo touched it with one finger, gently, the way you touch a hurt animal, and looked up at Marguerite with an expression she recognized because she'd worn it herself on the day the debt papers were signed: the look of a person watching a door close.

"We can fix it," Okonjo said. "Probably. Maybe. Dez and me, we can—"

"Later," Marguerite said. Her voice sounded strange to her, very calm, very far away. She looked around the cave—at the black water, the pearl-light, the crew bleeding and shaking and alive—and understood that something had finished happening, permanently, irreversibly, somewhere between saying *no* and the moment the curtain of water closed behind them. There was no version of events now in which she turned around, handed over the permits, and went back to being a woman with a number attached to her name. Voss had fired on them. Voss had said *the crew can swim* in front of witnesses, in front of her, in front of the river. Whatever happened next, the optimists could not allow any of them to reach a shore with a working mouth.

They were past the point where choices were offered. Now there were only directions.

She reached inside her jacket and took out the waterproof bag. The permits. Nine days left on them, four hundred and sixty thousand dollars of leash, Voss's whole game folded into a few stamped pages. She weighed them in her hand for a long moment, feeling the paper through the plastic, feeling twenty years of running.

Then she put the bag away, unopened, and looked up at the crack of light in the ceiling, and at Ilse, who was watching her with an expression that asked a question neither of them needed spoken aloud.

"How far does this channel go?" Marguerite asked.

"Up," Ilse said. "Behind the escarpment. All the way to the old survey trail—if the maps are honest, and mine are the only ones left that might be." She turned back to the wheel. "There's a light zone ahead. We move in it, quiet, and we find out where the river used to go before men started drawing it wrong."

Marguerite went aft to check the bilge, stepping over Dez's bloodied shirt sleeve, past the wounded radio, into the dark of the hold where the crate sat—the original delivery, the reason for all of it, sealed and patient. Somewhere above them, muffled by tons of falling water, she imagined she could hear the launch circling, looking for a boat that had walked through a locked door.

Let them circle, she thought. Let them count nine days.

She had stopped counting days. For the first time in twenty years, she was counting something else entirely.

* * *

They moved up the cave channel through the afternoon, engine at a whisper, Ilse poling through the shallows where the roof dipped low and the water went glass-still and knee-deep. The chamber narrowed, branched, chose itself. Twice they passed side mouths breathing cold air, passages that went who knew where into the heart of the escarpment, and once—Dez swore it, and Okonjo swore they all heard it—a sound from deep in the rock that was either settling stone or knocking.

Near dusk, the channel began to rise toward a second crack of light, wider than the first, and Ilse killed the engine altogether and let the Tern drift.

Beyond the opening, Marguerite could see green. Upland green, rain-beaten, terraced with old stone walls that no map still in print admitted to. And on the bank beside the water, half-swallowed by vines, a rusted survey marker with letters she could just make out through the mist:

**K.E.S. — STATION 4 — ELEV. 1,140 M**

Kessing Escarpment Survey. Station four. Someone had been here before them, years ago, with honest instruments and a reason to hide the road behind a waterfall.

Ilse looked at the marker for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"This is my brother's stamp," she said. "He stood right here."

Above them, through the crack in the stone, the first thunder of the returning storm rolled down the escarpment like a knock at a door—and somewhere up in that green, Marguerite knew, was a feverish woman with the truth in her hands, and men climbing.
