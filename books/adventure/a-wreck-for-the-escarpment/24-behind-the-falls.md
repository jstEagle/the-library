## 24. Behind the Falls

The cave swallowed the engine noise behind us the way a mouth swallows a word.

Fen pressed her back against the wet stone and listened. Outside, the falls made their endless argument with the river—white noise, total noise, the kind of sound that hid a boat and its four fugitives as thoroughly as darkness hid a face. Inside, the only light came from the cave mouth behind them, a gray-green slit of storm, and from the chemical stick Dez cracked against his palm, spilling blue light over everything like a bad memory of daylight.

"Nobody moves until I say," Marguerite whispered.

Fen had said almost nothing in three days. Her ribs were strapped with a strip of sailcloth Dez had sacrificed, and every breath cost her something she pretended not to be counting. She'd gotten good at pretending. Six weeks of pretending, since the inspection boat came up the reach with its cheerful Halvern flag and its cheerful men and its cheerful questions that ended with a rifle butt against her temple and Dr. Kessing's data cases going over the side of the launch into brown water.

But she knew this cave.

That was the thing none of them understood yet, because she hadn't been able to make herself say it. She knew this cave the way you knew your own hallway in the dark. She'd slept in it. She'd argued in it. She'd eaten cold rice in it while Amara Kessing hunched over a theodolite by lamplight, muttering elevations to herself like a woman praying in a language only she was fluent in.

"Dr. Kessing used this place," Fen said.

The blue light swung toward her. Marguerite's face came out of the dark—weathered, watchful, the face of a woman who had been recalculating something continuously since the gorge and hadn't liked any of the running totals.

"Say that again," Marguerite said.

"Amara. Dr. Kessing. She found this cave in the first survey season, four years ago. It's a back channel—there's a slot in the rock upstream of the falls that only runs in high water, and she spotted it on the sonar and couldn't leave it alone." Fen's voice came out rusty. She hadn't spoken this many sentences in a row since Fen had watched the company men burn the forward camp. "She called it the pantry. She cached equipment here. Instruments, spare batteries, paper records—she never trusted the digital copies after the first audit came back, ah, adjusted."

"Adjusted," Ilse repeated, from the bow. There was something in her voice Fen couldn't read yet. She'd learn. The river pilot was a locked room, and Fen had only been aboard her for two days, but she'd already catalogued the way Ilse listened—with her whole body, like a person waiting for a sound she'd heard once before and never wanted to hear again.

"Adjusted," Fen said. "The concession surveys. The ones that said the escarpment shelf was stable limestone at forty meters."

"And it isn't," Marguerite said. Not a question.

"It's shale over karst. It's a honeycomb. It's a lie you could put your fist through." Fen pushed off the wall, and her ribs screamed, and she kept going because she was tired of screaming being a reason to stop. "Amara proved it. She proved it twice, with two independent methods, and she sealed the data because she knew what would happen if she just sent it downstream. And then the company came for it anyway."

Dez's chemical stick trembled slightly. The superstitious mechanic had been crossing himself at intervals since they'd entered the cave, and now he crossed himself again and said, quietly, "Then we're standing in a dead woman's pantry."

"She's not dead."

Everyone looked at Fen. Even Okonjo, the young navigator who'd spent the whole run up the tributary with his eyes on the water and his hands white on the wheel, turned around.

"You don't know that," Marguerite said gently, and Fen hated gentleness, hated it the way you hate a hand on your shoulder at a funeral.

"I know she went upstream," Fen said. "After the inspection, after they scattered us—Osei and Priya and me into the bush, and the others—" She stopped. There were names on the other side of that stop that she hadn't said out loud yet and wasn't going to say in a cave full of strangers. "Amara took the sealed cores and went up. Toward the escarpment. She said—" Fen closed her eyes. She could hear it, the exact cadence, the professor's voice that never rose even when everything was falling. *Data doesn't survive by being carried, Fen. It survives by being redundant. We make it redundant.* "She said the summit relay tower was the only transmitter on this reach that Halvern didn't control. The old geological survey built it in the sixties. It's still on the registry. It's still licensed. And it's above the flood line."

The blue light held steady on Fen's face. She let it.

"So if she's alive," Marguerite said slowly, working it the way Fen had watched her work a river bend, reading the current before committing the boat, "she went up the goat path to the tower. And if she's not alive, the data went up without her. Either way—"

"Either way it's in that direction," Fen said. "And either way, we're the first people to walk this cave in six weeks, which means we're the first people Halvern hasn't found."

* * *

They went in deeper because there was nowhere else to go and because Fen told them to, and for once nobody argued with the wounded woman giving directions.

The cave ran back at a gentle climb, the floor rising out of the flood zone in terraces of slick limestone. Dez went first with the light stick, then Fen, because her memory was the map. Marguerite and Ilse brought up the rear with the boat's remaining flashlight and the salvaged radio crate between them—the original delivery, the concession permits and the company's own radio equipment, which Fen had watched them load with an expression she'd been careful not to explain. Ilse had carried it out of the flooded side-channel personally, hip-deep in water, and had said only, "Evidence travels both directions."

The air changed. Fen felt it before she could name it—dryer, cooler, moving. A draft.

"Ventilation," she said. "There's a chimney in the rock. Amara used it to—" And then the light found it, and Fen stopped talking, because there it was.

The camp.

It looked like a photograph of a camp, like something paused mid-sentence rather than abandoned. A dome tent, guyed out with the neatness Fen remembered, its fly half-collapsed under a season of drip from the chimney above. Two equipment cases, stacked. A folding table with a lamp on it, the lamp's glass fogged with condensation. A camp stove. A line strung between two stalagmites with a single sock drying on it, stiff as a shed skin.

And on the table, weighted down with a rock, a notebook.

"Nobody touches anything," Marguerite said, but her voice had changed. It had gone quiet in the specific way Fen had learned meant the opposite of calm. "Dez. Light. Okonjo, watch the cave mouth. Ilse—"

"Watching the water," Ilse said. She'd already turned back toward the entrance, and Fen saw her silhouette settle against the gray light, arms folded, a sentry who had done this before in some other life she didn't talk about.

Fen reached the table first. Her hands were shaking and she let them shake because there was no one left to perform steadiness for.

The notebook was Amara's field book—the olive-green, waterproof-paper kind, the kind that cost more than Fen's monthly stipend and was worth every unit of currency. The cover was swollen with damp but the pages inside were dry, and the handwriting was the handwriting Fen had copied out of a hundred lab protocols, small and upright and merciless.

Fen read. The others waited, and she was only distantly aware of them waiting, because the notebook was a voice, and the voice was speaking, and Fen had spent six weeks believing this voice was at the bottom of the river with the data cases.

*Day 31. Re-verified shelf elevation at three new stations. All consistent with original finding: karstic voiding to 60m+, shale interval incompetent under load. Halvern's submitted survey shows competent limestone at 40m. There is no honest way to get their numbers. There is only a dishonest one.*

*Day 33. Sealed duplicate set. Cores, plates, calculations. One set stays in the pantry. One set goes up.*

*Day 34. If you are reading this, then either the river gave you my cave or the company did. If it was the company, put the book down and leave. If it was the river—*

The next line had been underlined twice.

*—then you are wet, desperate, and probably in debt, which means the company owns you, which means I should not be writing this. But I have no one else. The tower relay is licensed to the regional geological survey, frequency 121.5 emergency band, and it will carry a data burst to every downstream station including the port authority. Halvern can buy men. They cannot buy the sky. Take the sealed case from under the tent floor. Take it to the tower. Transmit everything. Do not negotiate with them first. They will only offer you your own leash with a longer lead.*

Fen looked up. The blue light made everyone's face into something underwater.

"She wrote this for us," she said. "For whoever came. She wrote it six weeks ago and she's been waiting."

"Or she wrote it and died on the mountain," Dez said, not cruelly. Just a man stating the weather.

"Or she's alive," Fen said, "at the top of a goat path, with a transmitter, and a case under the tent floor, and a six-week head start on every company man on this river." She turned the book around so Marguerite could read the underlined line. "Read it. Read what she says about negotiating."

Marguerite read. Fen watched her read it, and watched something move behind her eyes that Fen had seen once before, on the night Marguerite had taken the satellite call from the man named Voss and come away from it with her jaw set like poured concrete.

"She's describing me," Marguerite said.

"She's describing all of us," Ilse said from the entrance, without turning around. "That's why it's good writing."

* * *

The case was under the tent floor exactly as the note promised, because of course it was; Amara Kessing had never once in Fen's four years of working for her left a promise unkept, and Fen had privately believed this made her a bad survivor. She'd been wrong, apparently. She was going to have to revise a great many things.

The floor panel came up with a suck of mud, and beneath it, wrapped in two layers of plastic and a groundsheet, was an equipment case the size of a small coffin.

They opened it on the folding table, and the blue light fell into it, and Fen heard Dez make a sound like a man finding his own name written somewhere it shouldn't be.

Inside, packed in foam cut to fit with obsessive precision: core samples in sealed tubes, each labeled in that merciless hand with station numbers and depths. Photographic plates in sleeves. A field hard-drive in a waterproof pouch, and beside it—because Amara trusted paper over everything—a bound printout of the entire analysis, tabbed with colored flags. And on top of it all, a sealed envelope with *READ FIRST* on it in block capitals, and under that, smaller: *and then read it again, because you won't believe it the first time.*

"Open it," Marguerite said.

Fen opened it. She read it once. Then she read it again, because she hadn't believed it the first time, exactly as instructed.

*To whoever holds this:* the letter began. *The Kessing Escarpment concession, as surveyed and certified by Halvern Consolidated, is geologically fraudulent. The shelf formation cited as the dam anchor is karstic shale riddled with solution voids. A reservoir at the certified height would not hold water; it would dissolve its own foundation within five years and fail catastrophically, with an estimated downstream population of forty thousand in the flood path. Halvern's own internal audit reached this conclusion fourteen months before certification. They certified anyway, because the concession's value lies not in a working dam but in the subsidy schedule: disbursement is front-loaded against survey milestones, all of which were falsified, and the company's exit position is already secured. They are not building a dam. They are building an invoice.*

Fen set the letter down. Her hands had stopped shaking, which was somehow worse.

"They're not building a dam," she said out loud, to the cave, to the sock drying on the line, to the ghost who'd left all this here and climbed into the rain.

"They're building an invoice," Ilse said.

Fen turned. The pilot had come back from the entrance without anyone noticing—she moved like that—and she was standing over the case, and she was looking at the core samples with an expression Fen had no framework for at all. It wasn't scientific interest. It was recognition. It was the look of a woman meeting something she'd been introduced to years ago by a different name.

"You knew," Fen said. "Not the details. But you knew there was something wrong with the concession."

"I knew a man who audited the escarpment surveys," Ilse said. "Six years ago. He found numbers that didn't reconcile." She paused, and the falls filled the pause with their white argument. "He didn't come back from it."

Nobody spoke. Dez's light stick was dying, the blue going greenish, the camp sinking into gloom by degrees, and in that gloom Fen watched the four of them become, for the first time since the side-channel, a single organism. Marguerite with her hand flat on the case like a woman taking a pulse. Okonjo come forward from the cave mouth, young and solemn, reading the letter over Marguerite's shoulder with his lips moving. Dez pulling a fresh light stick from his jacket and cracking it, because a man like Dez understood that some things needed to be seen clearly even if seeing them cost.

And Ilse, whose dead had been in these numbers all along, standing very still, the way people stand at graves.

"The tower," Marguerite said at last. Her voice was flat and decided, the voice of a woman closing a ledger. "How far."

"The goat path starts at the back of this cave system," Fen said. "There's a chimney climb, twenty meters, then a ledge walk, then the path proper. Four hours in dry weather." She looked toward the cave mouth, where the rain came down in gray ropes and the falls roared like something being born. "This is not dry weather."

"Six to eight, then," Marguerite said. "And the company?"

"The company's launch can't follow us in here," Fen said. "But they know this cave exists—it's on the original survey, their survey. They just haven't had a reason to come look. Yet." She tapped the letter. "The moment Voss realizes we didn't drown in the side-channel, he'll start checking every hole in this escarpment. We have hours, not days."

Marguerite nodded slowly. Then she did something Fen didn't expect. She picked up the sealed envelope, the letter, the notebook—and she set them back down, all of them, deliberately, and looked at Ilse.

"You've been carrying that radio crate since the channel," Marguerite said. "The company's own equipment. I told myself we were keeping it to deliver. Then I told myself we were keeping it as leverage. Then I stopped telling myself anything." She exhaled. "The tower relay runs on the old geological survey band. Will the company's radio talk to it?"

Ilse was quiet for a moment. When she answered, she was looking at the crate, not at Marguerite.

"It'll talk to anything," she said. "That's what makes it dangerous."

"Then we take it," Marguerite said. "All of it. The case, the letter, the cores, the crate. We climb it up the goat path in a monsoon, and at the top of this escarpment, if Dr. Amara Kessing is alive, we hand her every piece of the weapon she built and we ask her how she wants to fire it." She looked around the blue-lit circle of faces, and Fen saw her save the hardest look for last, and aim it at herself. "And if she's not alive, we fire it ourselves."

The light stick hissed. Somewhere back down the cave, the river kept up its argument with the falls, and the rain kept up its argument with everything.

Fen picked up the notebook, and held it against her chest, and said the thing she'd carried six weeks up a river full of men with guns:

"Then we'd better start climbing."

Behind them, at the mouth of the cave, the storm shifted its weight—and through the roar of the falls, thin and far away and unmistakable, came the sound of an engine.
