## 25. The Climb

The Tern looked wrong out of the water.

Okonjo had never seen her beached before—never seen her at all except afloat, riding the current like she'd been poured into it rather than built. Now she lay on her side against the rocks behind the falls, propped on salvaged logs and two broken oars, her hull split along the seam Dez had fought since the monsoon began. Rain sheeted off her deck plates in silver ropes. She looked like something that had been caught mid-sentence.

"She'll float again," Dez said, for the third time that morning. He said it the way other people prayed.

"Sure," Okonjo said, because there was nothing else to say that wasn't true.

Fen came down from Kessing's field camp with the packs already divided: the data drives wrapped in oilcloth and taped inside a cookpot, the ledger pages sealed in a ration tin, and the transmitter—the one they'd pulled from the wrecked radio crate, its casing cracked but its heart apparently intact—strapped to Marguerite's back like a child too big to carry. It weighed maybe thirty kilos. Marguerite had put it on without comment, which was its own kind of statement.

Okonjo carried the fuel cells. Six of them, lashed to a frame he'd built out of a broken tent pole and fishing line, because someone had to think about weight distribution and apparently that someone was always going to be him. He didn't mind. Weight distribution was navigation's quiet cousin, and navigation was the thing he was best at, the thing that had gotten them through the gorge and the side-channel and every mile of drowned green between here and the locks. On the water, he knew where he was.

He did not know where he was going to be for the next thousand vertical meters.

* * *

The goat path started as a rumor.

Ilse found it—or claimed to have found it—a quarter mile upstream from the falls, where the escarpment's base folded into a ravine choked with ferns. There was no trailhead. There was a gap between two boulders the size of doorways, and beyond it a slope so steep it read less like ground than like a wall that had gotten tired halfway up.

"You're sure this goes to the top," Marguerite said. Not a question. A dare.

"Kessing's note says summit relay tower." Ilse tapped the scrap of paper Fen had given them, the one with coordinates and a hand-drawn arrow. "There are exactly three ways up this face, and two of them are waterfalls right now."

"And the third is a goat path."

"The third is a goat path."

Okonjo watched Marguerite weigh it. He'd gotten good at reading her over these weeks—good at reading all of them, if he was honest, though he'd have died before admitting how much time he spent doing it. Marguerite's face did this thing when she was calculating, a small tightening around the eyes, like she was measuring distance to something only she could see. He'd assumed for most of the trip that she was measuring miles. Lately he suspected she was measuring odds.

"We go light, we go fast, we go before the rain comes back full," she said. "Dez, can you climb?"

"I climbed a grain silo once in Port Anselm."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I've got."

Marguerite looked at him a moment longer, then nodded, which Okonjo understood to mean: *we're all carrying each other today, some of us just don't know it yet.*

They went up.

* * *

The first hour wasn't climbing. It was wading—through ferns to the chest, through mud to the knee, through air so wet it felt like breathing through a cloth. The rain had come back the way Ilse promised it would, not the monsoon's full fury but its advance scouts, drizzle thickening to proper rain and then thinning again, as if the storm were deciding whether to bother with them.

Okonjo counted his steps. It was a navigator's habit and a nervous man's tic and he made no apology for either. Four hundred steps to the first ledge. Six hundred more to the stand of pines that marked where the ravine narrowed. The numbers kept him honest about progress, and progress kept him honest about fear, and fear—if you let it get a word in—would tell you things like *you are carrying six fuel cells up a cliff in a storm for a woman you met eleven days ago,* and that was not a useful thought.

Except it was, a little. Because it was true, and the truth of it kept rearranging itself into something he hadn't expected.

Eleven days ago he'd signed onto the Tern because Marguerite Brightwater was the best storm chaser on the coast and he wanted to learn from her. That was the whole story he told himself. Somewhere between the dead air on channel nine and the slashed tents and Fen's wounds and the fire on the termite mound island where Ilse had finally said her brother's name out loud, the story had changed shape. He wasn't learning from Marguerite anymore. He was crew. Actual crew. The kind that doesn't get paid off at the end of the run.

He was still turning that over when the path turned vertical.

* * *

"Hands, not feet," Ilse called back, her voice half-swallowed by the ravine walls. "Feet lie to you on wet rock. Hands don't."

She climbed like she piloted—economically, almost lazily, finding holds without seeming to look for them. Okonjo had stopped being surprised by anything Ilse did somewhere around the gorge transit, when she'd threaded the Tern through rapids that the charts marked with a single word—*impassable*—and done it while shouting instructions to Dez in three languages. But watching her move up the escarpment face, he understood something new about her. She wasn't comfortable up here. She was *familiar*. This was country she'd been in before, or country like it, and her body remembered even if she'd never say what it remembered.

Above her, Marguerite climbed the way she did everything: grimly, competently, with the transmitter swaying on her back like a bell that refused to ring. Fen went next, favoring the wounded leg but refusing help with a ferocity Okonjo respected. Then Dez, who climbed exactly the way he'd climbed the grain silo in Port Anselm, apparently—that is, badly, loudly, and successfully.

Okonjo took the rear. Someone had to make sure nobody fell off the world, and the fuel-cell frame made him slowest anyway, so rear guard was arithmetic, not heroism. He preferred it that way. Heroism looked exhausting.

The path narrowed to a shelf barely wide enough for his boots. Below him the ravine dropped away into white noise—falls on falls, the tributary they'd abandoned eating itself into foam. Above him, gray sky and gray rock and the thin black line of the others ascending like notes on a staff.

He did not look down again. He counted steps instead. Two hundred to the pine stand. One hundred and forty. Ninety.

At sixty, Dez slipped.

It wasn't dramatic. No scream, no long fall. Dez's boot skated off a wet root, he grabbed a stunted tree, and the tree held, and for three seconds the whole column froze while Dez hung there swearing with tremendous fluency and commitment. Then Ilse was beside him—not climbing to him, somehow just *there*, the way she was always just there—and had his pack strap in her fist, and Dez got his feet under him, and the line moved again.

"Silos don't have roots," Dez said shakily, when they reached the pines.

"No," Ilse agreed. "They don't."

Okonjo realized his hands were shaking around the fuel-cell frame and made himself breathe until they stopped. Four hundred meters up. Maybe five hundred left. The rain was thickening.

* * *

They ate cold rations under the pines, huddled against trunks, and nobody talked much. Talking cost breath, and breath cost energy, and energy was now a currency they were all counting.

It was Fen who broke the silence, quietly, mostly to the fire she couldn't light.

"I climbed this path in March," she said. "With Amara. She stopped at this exact spot and ate an orange and said—" Fen's mouth did something complicated. "She said, 'You know what's funny? From up here you can't see any borders. You can't see any concessions. It's just watershed.' She thought the escarpment would save itself. Geology as alibi." Fen shook her head. "Halvern doesn't care what the rock says. They care what the survey says. And they wrote the survey."

"So we write a better one," Marguerite said.

"With a cracked transmitter and six fuel cells and a goat path."

"With whatever we've got. That's the whole job, Fen. It's always been whatever we've got."

Okonjo chewed his ration brick and thought about Marguerite's debt, which he knew about the way everyone on the boat knew about it—unspoken, ambient, a weather system of its own. Voss. The optimists. Eleven days on the permits, last he'd heard, which meant fewer now. He thought about how easy it would have been, back at the middle station, for Marguerite to turn downstream. Deliver the permits, clear the debt, keep the Tern afloat and the crew fed. Nobody would ever have known what she chose not to do.

But she'd packed the permits and the burned ledger page together, back in the eye of the storm, and Okonjo had seen her do it, and he'd understood then that she'd already chosen. Whatever she told herself. Whatever she hadn't said out loud.

He finished his ration. "Two hundred meters to the treeline," he said. "Then open rock. We want to cross the open rock before the rain decides."

Marguerite stood. "You heard the navigator."

* * *

The treeline gave out at the cloud layer, which was a sentence Okonjo had never expected to think, let alone live.

Above the pines the escarpment opened into bare stone terraces, and the clouds came down to meet them, and suddenly the world was twenty meters of visibility and the sound of wind doing unspeakable things to the cliff face above. The temperature dropped. The rain returned in earnest—horizontal now, needling, each drop arriving with intent.

Ilse led. Marguerite followed with the transmitter. Then Fen, then Dez, then Okonjo and his fuel cells, the same order, the same discipline, everyone's hands doing their work while their minds went somewhere warmer.

This was the part Okonjo would remember afterward in fragments: a cairn taller than he was, ancient, furred with moss, marking a turn the path made around a buttress. A mountain goat—actual goat, absurd, unbothered—watching them pass from a ledge with the contempt of a native for tourists. The wind picking up Dez's voice mid-curse and carrying it away unfinished. Marguerite stopping once, only once, to re-tape the transmitter's strap, her fingers clumsy with cold, and Okonjo kneeling to help without being asked, and her looking at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

"How far?" Dez shouted, at some point that could have been an hour into the traverse or three.

Okonjo checked the coordinates against the altimeter on his battered wrist unit. "Four hundred meters of elevation left. Call it two hours if the path holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we invent a path. We're good at that."

Dez laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and Okonjo felt it land somewhere in his chest and stay there. This was the thing nobody told you about crews, he thought. About families, maybe. That they were built out of moments like this, stupid jokes at four hundred meters per hour of vertical, in horizontal rain, with company men somewhere below and a feverish scientist somewhere above and absolutely no guarantee that any of you would be alive by Thursday. Built out of laughing anyway.

* * *

The storm cell hit them at the last terrace.

No warning—one minute the rain was merely hostile, the next the wind arrived like a hand pressing them flat against the rock, and the temperature fell through the floor, and somewhere above them thunder cracked with a sound like the escarpment itself splitting. Okonjo flattened against a boulder and held the fuel-cell frame down with his whole body and thought, with strange clarity: *this is what the instruments feel.* All those years calibrating anemometers in coastal garages, measuring storms from the safe side of glass. This was the other side. This was what the numbers meant.

"Thirty seconds!" Ilse screamed, pointing. "Overhang! Move!"

They moved. Okonjo lost count of everything—steps, seconds, the difference between up and sideways—and then there was rock overhead instead of sky, and seven people crammed into a hollow beneath a leaning slab of sandstone, gasping, streaming, alive.

Outside, the storm worked itself into a fury. Hail came, small and hard, bouncing off the terrace like gravel thrown by an angry god. Lightning strobed the cloud layer below them—they were above the clouds now, actually above them, which Okonjo filed away to think about later, much later, preferably never—and the thunder came so close together it stopped being discrete events and became a single continuous roar.

Dez was shaking. Not from cold. Okonjo saw it, saw Fen see it, saw Fen put a hand on Dez's shoulder without a word and leave it there.

"Used to watch these from the boat," Dez said, to nobody. "From outside. Always figured I understood them."

"Nobody understands them," Marguerite said. "That's the whole point of chasing them."

"How long?" Fen asked Ilse.

Ilse had produced the note again, studying Kessing's hand-drawn arrow by the light of a shielded lamp. "If the tower's where she marked it, the ridge tops out a kilometer ahead. The storm cell will pass—it's moving fast, this front. Twenty minutes. Maybe thirty."

"And Halvern?"

"Below us. Behind us. Slower than us in this." Ilse's jaw tightened. "But they don't stop, Fen. You know that better than anyone."

Fen did know that. Everyone knew that. The silence of the expedition, the slashed tents, the burned ledger, the launch in the fog—all of it said the same thing: Halvern did not stop.

Okonjo leaned his head back against stone and closed his eyes and listened to the storm spend itself against the escarpment, and did the only math that mattered: six fuel cells, one cracked transmitter, one kilometer of ridge, and however many minutes of lull the sky was willing to sell them.

It would be enough. It had to be enough. Everything they had was always exactly enough or nothing at all; there was no in-between country anymore.

* * *

Twenty-three minutes later, the wind dropped as if switched off.

They came out of the overhang into a world gone strangely still and strangely bright—the storm's eye, or something like it, a corridor of pale gold light laid across the cloud tops with the summit ridge rising out of it ahead of them, black and clean-edged. And on the ridge, silhouetted against the gold, a lattice of steel: the relay tower, guyed and gaunt, exactly where a dead woman's careful hand had drawn it.

Kessing wasn't dead. Fen had said so a dozen times, and Okonjo believed her, mostly. But seeing the tower standing there in the eye's calm, he felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was clenched.

"There's our road," Marguerite said.

Okonjo shifted the fuel-cell frame higher on his shoulders and started walking. Behind them, far below, the clouds were already boiling at the edges—the eye closing, the storm reloading—and somewhere inside all that gray, men were climbing.

The tower waited. The lull wouldn't last. Nobody said any of it out loud, because none of it needed saying.

They climbed.
