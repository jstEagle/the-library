## 26. Kessing Alive

The rain came back an hour after we left the cave, and it came back angry.

Okonjo led the way up the goat path with Fen's compass in one fist and the other hand flat against the rock face, reading the escarpment like braille. Behind him came Fen, moving on a leg that had been stitched with fishing line and sealed with Dez's last tube of epoxy, refusing every offer of a rest. Then Ilse, then me, then Dez hauling the salvaged transmitter in a sailcloth sling across his back like a body. The graze along his arm had stiffened overnight; he'd stopped complaining about it somewhere around dawn, which worried me more than the complaining ever had.

The path was not a path so much as an argument the mountain was losing. Switchbacks cut into wet limestone, handholds worn smooth by decades of goats and the occasional fool. Below us the tributary we'd come up had become a brown rope thrashing through drowned forest, and beyond it the whole floodplain lay under water to the horizon, the villages gone, the concession roads gone, everything Halvern had surveyed and sold reduced to a single featureless sheet of moving water.

"Nice place for a dam," Dez said, and spat over the edge.

Nobody laughed, but everybody wanted to.

We climbed through the morning. The rain alternated between a fine needling mist and full vertical assault, and between the two there were moments when the cloud tore open and I could see how high we'd come—the river a vein, the fog banks drifting below us like slow animals. My legs burned. My hands were raw where the limestone had taken its toll. Somewhere around the ninth switchback I caught myself doing the old arithmetic—two hundred meters gained, four hundred to go, ninety minutes at this pace—and made myself stop. For once the distance didn't matter. There was nowhere else to be but up.

Fen called a halt at a shelf of rock overhung by a fig tree whose roots gripped the cliff like fingers. She drank half a cup of water, poured a capful over her bad leg, and looked out at the flooded valley without expression.

"She'll have a perimeter," Fen said. "Amara doesn't trust open ground. After what happened at the middle station—" her jaw worked "—after the inspection, she set watches. Two people stayed with her when she went upstream. Okafor and Lindqvist. If they see five strangers coming up their mountain in the rain, they will shoot first and identify later."

"So we announce ourselves," Okonjo said.

"With what? We've got no radio that transmits worth a damn, and shouting up a cliff in monsoon country gets you ignored or shot." Fen rubbed her eyes. "They'll have a vantage near the summit relay. There's a saddle before it—a col, maybe forty meters below the tower. They'll make contact there or not at all."

"And if they don't make contact," Ilse said quietly, "they'll make contact."

* * *

The col appeared out of the mist all at once, a saddle of scree and wind-bent grass between two shoulders of rock, and we walked into it single file like beads on a string, and the first thing I registered was the sound—not a voice, but the dry mechanical snap of a bolt being thrown.

"Stop. All of you. Stop right there."

Two figures stood up out of the grass twenty meters ahead, and my brain did the terrible fast math it does: two rifles, both leveled, both steady. The nearer one was a broad woman with a shaved head and rain streaming off her jaw, and she held her weapon the way people hold weapons when they have already decided to use them. The second figure was younger, thinner, half-hidden behind a boulder, his rifle tracking Dez because Dez had the biggest silhouette and the sling that might have been a weapon case.

"Okafor," Fen said. "Lindqvist. It's Fen."

The rifles did not lower. The woman—Okafor—took one step forward, boots grinding scree, and her voice came out flat and hard as the rock under us.

"Fen died at the middle station. We heard it on the radio traffic. Company men took the station and nobody walked out."

"I walked out," Fen said. "Through a window. It cost me." She pulled up the hem of her oilskin and turned her thigh toward them, and even from twenty meters I could see the ruin the stitching was trying to hold together. "Ask me something only Fen knows. Ask me anything."

Okafor's rifle dipped a centimeter. A centimeter. "What did Amara call the seismograph?"

"The *old liar*," Fen said. "Because it kept telling her the escarpment was stable and she refused to believe it. She talked to it. She apologized to it when it turned out to be right about the deep strata." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Tomas, don't. Tomas, look at me. It's me."

The younger one—Lindqvist—had come out from behind his boulder, and his rifle was shaking now, and his eyes were wet, and he said, "Prove it. Prove it, because if you're not her, if this is how they finally get to her, I will—" He couldn't finish it.

"It was me who broke the anemometer mast at forward camp," Fen said. "Third night. I backed the generator truck into it. Amara made me write it up in the ledger and I wrote *structural failure due to wind* and she read it and looked at me for a long time and said, 'Fen, the wind is going to get blamed for a lot on this river. Don't start early.'" She let the oilskin fall. "That's your proof. Nobody living knows that except the three of us, and one of us is standing here bleeding."

For three seconds nobody moved. Rain ticked on rifle barrels. Wind combed the grass flat.

Then Okafor lowered her weapon all the way, and the sound she made wasn't a word—it was older than words, a sound like something unclenching—and Lindqvist sat down on the scree like his strings had been cut, rifle across his knees, laughing and crying at the same time in the ugly way that people do when terror leaves faster than the body can process it.

"You're alone?" Okafor managed. "You got out alone?"

"Not alone." Fen stepped aside, and suddenly five strangers were the whole conversation again, and two exhausted soldiers were looking at us the way you look at weather you can't predict. "This is Marguerite Zhou. She was hired to deliver the permits—Halvern's permits. She didn't deliver them. This is Ilse, our pilot. Dez, mechanic. Okonjo, navigator. The Tern is wrecked in the side-channel. We climbed here because of this." Fen patted the sling on her own shoulder, where Kessing's field notebooks rode wrapped in oilcloth next to ours. "Because of what Amara found."

"Found," Okafor repeated. Something moved behind her eyes—hope, and the fear of hope. "She's sick. You should know that before you see her. Fever four days now. The wound went bad before we got the antibiotics into her, and we got them in late." She turned and started up the last slope without waiting, because there was nothing left to negotiate. "Come on. Come and see her before she's—"

She didn't finish that either.

* * *

The camp was tucked into a hollow beneath the relay tower's concrete footing, three tents and a tarp and a cook fire burning low under a metal shield, and it smelled of woodsmoke and carbolic and sickness. Instruments everywhere—Kessing's instruments, the ones the company hadn't seized because Kessing had never let them out of arm's reach. A theodolite lashed to a rock. Sample cases stacked and numbered. And in the largest tent, on a cot that had clearly been carried up this mountain on someone's back, Dr. Amara Kessing lay with her eyes closed and her breath coming fast and shallow, and she was so much smaller than her reputation that for a moment I thought Fen had brought us all this way to a grave.

Then her eyes opened.

They were gray and fever-bright and absolutely, ferociously present, and they fixed on Fen first, and something in Kessing's face reorganized itself around relief so violent it looked like pain.

"You're supposed to be dead," Kessing rasped.

"You're supposed to be careful," Fen said, kneeling. "Look who I found."

Kessing's gaze traveled. Over Fen's shoulder, past Okafor in the tent mouth, to me. To Ilse behind me. To Dez filling most of the entrance with the transmitter sling still on his back. I watched her do what I would later understand she did with everything—weigh it, measure it, test it against the data.

"Zhou," she said. "The delivery contractor. Voss's paperwork had your name on it. Concession permits and a radio crate for the Kessing survey, freight prepaid by Halvern Consolidated." A ghost of a smile moved across her cracked lips. "I signed for that contract six weeks ago. I never expected the courier to climb the mountain."

"The courier's late," I said. "There were complications."

"There usually are, on this river." She tried to push herself upright and failed, and Fen eased her back against the rolled jacket that served as her pillow. "Complications. Yes. Mine started when my elevation readings wouldn't reconcile. Do you know what it means, Ms. Zhou, when a surveyor's numbers won't reconcile? It means one of two things. Either she's incompetent, or somebody fed her fiction. I checked the benchmarks myself. Twice. On my hands and knees in the mud with a level and a staff, because I did not want to believe the third thing."

"Which is?" Ilse asked, from the shadows at the tent's edge. Her voice was very controlled. Six years of control.

Kessing found her in the dimness and studied her for a long moment. "Which is that the concession was never real. That the surveys Halvern filed to win it were drawn from whole cloth—that the escarpment geology can't take the damming their extraction plan requires, that the whole scheme is a subsidy harvest dressed up as infrastructure, and that when it fails it will fail catastrophically, with the floodplain downstream of it." She coughed, wet and long, and Fen held a cup to her lips. "I put it in a report. The report went upstream. Then men came downstream instead, and I understood how the river carries news here. One direction only."

"We found your forward camp," I said. "The tents. The burned ledger page."

"Lindqvist's work. He burns everything twice; it's a habit from a previous life." Kessing's eyes flicked to the young man, and something passed between them that had no words in it. "The page you found—the impossible elevations—that was deliberate. I left it where it would be found by anyone following us. A trail marker. Proof-of-concept for whoever came asking questions." Her gaze sharpened on me. "Did you come asking questions, Ms. Zhou? Or did you come delivering permits?"

And there it was. The question I'd been climbing away from for eleven days, waiting at the top of the mountain like a toll collector.

Ilse shifted beside me. Dez had gone very still. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"I came delivering permits," I said. "Eleven days ago that was the whole truth. Deliver the crate, clear the debt, keep my crew alive. That was the entire shape of my future and I'd measured it down to the kilometer." I crouched so I was level with her, so she wouldn't have to lift her head. "Then I found your camp. Then I found Fen. Then I watched drowned villages go by under the water that your report says will happen ten times worse if Halvern builds. So no, Doctor. I'm not delivering the permits. They're in my pack, and they're staying there until I decide they're evidence instead of cargo."

Kessing looked at me for a long time. The fever was in her cheeks and in the tremor of her hands, but underneath it there was an engine running, some core of her that the infection hadn't touched and wouldn't.

"Evidence," she said. "Yes. That's the correct disposition." She turned her head toward the tent opening, toward the concrete footing looming above us through the rain. "Do you see the tower?"

"The relay," Okonjo said from outside. "It's dead. We checked the frequency on the way up. Nothing but carrier hum."

"It's not dead. It's switched off. There's a difference." Kessing's voice gathered strength, or borrowed it from somewhere. "Halvern built that tower to run their own telemetry—pump monitoring, gate sensors, the machinery of the concession. But it sits on the highest point in two hundred kilometers, and its transmitter is a general-purpose unit, and its antenna array covers every band from marine VHF to shortwave broadcast. I spent two nights in it before we came down the mountain. I know exactly what it can do." She closed her eyes, and when she opened them they were fixed on the rain. "My verified data is in those cases. Every benchmark, every cross-section, every comparison against the filed surveys, with the discrepancies documented page by page. What it needs is altitude and an audience. Altitude, I have. Audience—" her breath caught "—audience has been the problem. Every channel I tried from the valley was monitored. Every message died in transit. That's why the expedition went silent, Ms. Zhou. Not lost. Silenced. There is a difference, and I need the world to learn it."

Fen's hand was on Kessing's shoulder. Okafor had come into the tent and stood with her arms folded, and I realized she was crying without any change in her expression at all, tears just running off her jaw into the mud, because her scientist was alive and her scientist was talking about broadcasting, and broadcasting was the thing that had gotten everyone hunted.

"The storm," Ilse said.

Everyone turned. She'd come forward into the light, and up close I could see what it was costing her to stand in this tent, in front of this woman, with her brother's name unspoken between them like a stone in her throat.

"The monsoon has an eye," Ilse said. "It's rare this far south, but it happens—we ran one two years ago, a thirty-minute lull, pressure wall on either side. When it comes, the atmospheric noise drops out. Every band goes clean. A transmission made in the eye carries farther than the same transmission made in calm weather, because there's nothing fighting it." Her voice was level. Only her hands, at her sides, told the truth. "If you're going to say something the whole river needs to hear, you say it in the eye."

Kessing regarded her. "You've done this."

"I've run rivers in worse than this," Ilse said. "And I've waited six years for a clean channel."

Something moved across Kessing's fevered face—recognition, or its beginning. "Six years," she repeated softly. "Then you knew. Before you ever met me. Before the permits, before any of it—you knew what Halvern was."

"I knew what Halvern does," Ilse said.

The rain thickened against the tarp. Under it, for a moment, nobody spoke, and I felt the shape of the next hours assemble themselves out of the parts scattered around that tent: a dying transmitter in a sailcloth sling, a tower with a switched-off heart, a woman too sick to climb but too stubborn to die, a pilot with a clean channel in her head and a dead man's audit in her chest, and me, with a pack full of paper that had been the whole point of my life eleven days ago and was now just one more exhibit.

"How far to the tower base?" I asked.

"Forty meters of ladder and catwalk," Okafor said. "The stair inside flooded in the first blow. Ladder's exposed—anyone on the scree below can see you against the sky."

"Then we go in the eye," I said. "When they can't climb and we can't be seen. Fen, can she travel?"

"She can travel if we carry her," Fen said. "And she'll hate every meter of it."

"I will," Kessing agreed promptly, and coughed, and grinned through it, and for a second I saw the woman who'd talked back to a seismograph in the mud.

Dez set the transmitter sling down inside the tent flap and flexed his bad arm, wincing. "Radio's rough. Took a round through the casing. I can get power to it and probably signal out of it, but I'll want an hour with the boards and a dry place to work."

"You have a dry place," Lindqvist said. He'd come to the tent mouth, and his voice had steadied into something harder and better. "And an hour. Maybe less—the barometer's been falling since noon. When the eye opens, it opens fast, and it closes faster." He looked at each of us in turn, and I saw him deciding something, the same decision Fen had made on a scree slope an hour ago, the same one I'd made somewhere on the ninth switchback without noticing. "Whatever you're planning to say up there—say all of it. Names, dates, the filed survey numbers. Everything. Because if they take this mountain back, there won't be a second chance, and none of us will be alive to give testimony."

Outside, the wind changed.

Not stronger—different. A new note in it, a steadiness underneath the gusts, the sound of a very large system reorganizing itself around a hole. Okonjo, who had the best ear for weather of anyone I'd ever crewed with, lifted his head like a dog scenting rain, and then looked at me across the tent with an expression I'd never seen on him before.

"That's it," he said. "That's the pressure turning. Captain—the eye's coming. Tonight."

Kessing reached out and closed her hand around my wrist. Her grip was fever-hot and surprisingly strong.

"Ms. Zhou," she said. "Tell me exactly what's in that pack besides my death warrant. I want to know everything we're broadcasting before we climb."

I unbuckled the pack, and began.
