## 27. What the Surveys Prove

Fever is a strange country. I had been living in it for three days, maybe four—the rain made it impossible to tell—and its borders kept shifting. Sometimes I was back at the university, defending my thesis to a panel that included my dead father. Sometimes I was on the escarpment rim watching the river below turn the color of old brass. And sometimes I was in a canvas shelter that smelled of kerosene and wet basalt, with two loyal men standing guard and a woman's voice cutting through the fever like a blade through tarpaulin.

*Dr. Kessing. It's Marguerite Zhou. Fen sent us.*

Fen. Alive. That name alone pulled me partway out of the fog, because I had spent eleven days believing Fen was dead, or worse—recruited, turned, one of Halvern's smiling instruments now. When they brought her into my shelter, soaked to the bone, with a bandage crusted brown along her forearm, I made a sound I didn't know I could make. Not quite a word. Something older than words.

She knelt and took my wrist like she was taking a pulse, which she was, but also like she was holding onto something.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been better," I said, and my voice came out as gravel. "The data. Tell me you have the data."

"Every page," she said. "And we brought company."

* * *

They built a small fire under the overhang where my camp sat wedged into a fold of the escarpment's flank—a fold deep enough to hide smoke, high enough that the floodwater below couldn't reach it. My two remaining team members, Sefu and Adeyemi, watched the newcomers with rifles held low but not put away. Six years of institutional trust had died for me somewhere between the middle station and the escarpment rim; I wasn't going to pretend it came back easily.

Marguerite Zhou I knew by reputation only—a storm chaser who ran cargo upriver when the weather allowed and ran from her own history the rest of the time. She had the look of someone perpetually calculating an exit. But she'd climbed a goat path in monsoon rain carrying a transmitter and a stranger's data, so I decided to extend provisional credit.

The pilot, Ilse, I did not know. Or rather—I looked at her twice, then a third time, and something ticked at the back of my mind. A face half-remembered from a hearing room, from a corridor outside a deposition. I filed it away. The fever made filing unreliable.

"Talk to me," I said to Fen, once the fire was going and Zhou had pressed a tin cup of something hot into my hands. "Start anywhere. Start with what they told you."

"They told us nothing," Fen said. "That's the point. They just took."

So I talked instead. Because I had spent nine months on this escarpment, and I had carried the whole terrible shape of it inside me since the night I verified the last core sample, and there is a limit to how long a person can hold a thing like that alone.

* * *

"Here's what everyone downstream believes," I said. "Halvern Consolidated files a concession claim on the Kessing Escarpment reach—yes, named for my grandfather, the irony is not lost on me—based on geological surveys showing stable sedimentary strata suitable for terraced extraction. Low permeability. Minimal seepage risk. The surveys were submitted, stamped, certified. Subsidies flowed. Permits were issued." I gestured at Zhou. "Permits like the ones in your crate."

Zhou's face went very still. I noted that. Whatever she'd been told about this delivery, she hadn't yet connected it to its purpose.

"The problem," I said, "is that the escarpment is not sedimentary. It's karst over fractured basalt, sitting on an aquifer that feeds every tributary in the lower basin. You don't terrace into karst. Karst doesn't hold walls. Karst dissolves. Any large-scale extraction requires dewatering the aquifer, and dewatering the aquifer means the tributaries drop, and the tributaries dropping means—" I spread my hands. "Everything downstream dies. The fisheries. The floodplain agriculture. The port itself, eventually, because the channel silts without seasonal flush."

Adeyemi shifted near the fire. He'd heard all this before; he'd helped me prove it.

"And Halvern knew?" Zhou asked. Her voice was flat, careful. The voice of someone asking a question whose answer will cost her something.

"Halvern wrote the geology themselves," I said. "Or rather, they commissioned it, received honest preliminary results, and then commissioned again until they got the answer they wanted. That's not unusual. What's unusual is the scale of the lie. Their certified survey shows the escarpment's base stratum at forty meters above where my cores place it. Forty meters. That's not an error. That's architecture. Someone drew the geology they needed and buried the geology that exists."

I let that sit. Rain hammered the overhang beyond the firelight, and somewhere below us the flooded tributary roared through its drowned course.

"Why?" the young navigator asked—Okonjo, Fen said his name was. "If the extraction would fail anyway, why spend the money?"

"Because the money isn't in the extraction," I said. "That's the part that took me longest to understand. Follow the subsidies. Halvern claimed the concession against three separate development funds—infrastructure guarantees, disaster mitigation credits, a regional stabilization package. The funds pay out on certified survey milestones. Survey complete: payout. Extraction phase approved: payout. Remediation bond released: payout. None of those payouts require a single tonne of ore to come out of the ground. They require paper. Certified, stamped, defensible-until-audited paper."

"So they cash out," Ilse said quietly, "and walk away before the first wall collapses."

"Before the first *tributary* collapses," I corrected. "And here's the beautiful, monstrous part. When the dewatering fails—and it will fail, within eighteen months of any serious drilling—the damage won't read as corporate negligence. It'll read as natural disaster. Karst collapse, aquifer breach, unprecedented flooding. Halvern will have already booked their subsidies, sold their position to some shell consortium, and dissolved. The fixers will have scattered. And the river will be blamed for destroying itself."

* * *

Silence, except for the rain. I watched them work through it, each in their own way. Okonjo looking young and sick. Fen staring into the fire with the expression of someone re-seeing every moment of the past month. Zhou doing arithmetic—I could see it happening behind her eyes, some private ledger being balanced, and I suspected the numbers involved her debt.

It was Ilse who spoke next, and when she did, her voice had changed. All the smooth pilot's calm had burned off it.

"The audit," she said. "Six years ago. There was an internal audit of the original survey certification. It never published."

I turned to look at her properly. "How do you know about the audit?"

"Because my brother wrote it," she said. "He found the same forty-meter discrepancy. He filed his findings. Three weeks later he was dead, ruled accidental, and the audit was sealed. His name was struck from the record. So was mine, eventually, because I wouldn't stop asking questions." She met my eyes across the fire. "I've been looking for your proof for six years, Dr. Kessing. I just didn't know it had a face yet."

Sefu's rifle came up an inch. I put a hand on his arm. Some instincts you learn to trust faster than others, and everything in me said this woman's grief was load-bearing, not decorative.

"Then you understand what we're holding," I said to her.

"I understand what it cost," she said.

"What we're holding," I repeated, "is pages. Cores, logs, spectrometer readings, photographs of the base stratum with location markers. Right now it's evidence. In a drawer, it stays evidence forever—evidence that can be lost, burned, drowned, like my forward camp was burned. Evidence has no power until it's *broadcast*. Until it's public, timestamped, replicated, confirmed by people who can't be disappeared. Until it's on every channel at once, so loudly that killing the messenger becomes the story itself."

"That's why you went upstream," Fen said. "The relay tower."

"The summit relay," I said. "Built during the last infrastructure grant—Halvern's own tower, feeding their own communications net, with range clear down to the port authority stations. If the data goes out from there, it goes out on Halvern's signal, from Halvern's altitude, and every downstream receiver picks it up simultaneously. No interception point. No single throat to cut." I smiled, and felt how thin it was. "They built the instrument of their own exposure. It's almost elegant."

"It's also a climb in monsoon rain guarded by men with guns," Zhou said.

"Yes," I agreed. "There is that."

* * *

The fever broke sometime in the small hours—or receded far enough that I could sit up without the world tilting. Fen dozed against a pack. The crew of the Tern had spread themselves around the fire, exhausted, and I lay listening to the rain and thinking about the shape of what I'd told them.

Because I had left things out. Not facts—those I'd given whole—but weight. I hadn't told them what the numbers meant for the eleven thousand people in the tributary villages, the ones already gone quiet, bought out and moved on, their houses standing empty behind Halvern fences. I hadn't told them that my grandfather's name was on the escarpment because he'd mapped it honestly a century ago, and that I sometimes thought the mountain had been waiting for someone in the family to come back and tell the truth about it. I hadn't said that I understood, exactly, what Zhou was carrying in that crate—permits that represented her freedom, her crew's wages, her whole arithmetic of survival—and that every hour she stayed with us, that arithmetic worsened.

Some truths you hand people all at once. Others you let them arrive at, so they can decide for themselves what the knowing costs.

Near dawn, the rain thinned to a whisper—that strange, pressurized hush I knew from thirty years of fieldwork, the sound of a storm gathering itself for a second act. I got up on legs that shook and walked to the lip of the overhang.

Below, the flooded world stretched gray-green to the horizon. And far off, at the limit of sight, a dark shape was moving against the current—too deliberate for debris, too fast for a wreck.

A launch. Climbing toward us.

I went back inside and woke them all.

"We need to move," I said. "Now. While the lull holds. Because whatever window the storm gives us, it's giving it to them too."
