## 28. Voss's Last Offer

The radio had taken a bullet two days ago and still worked the way a man with a broken rib works—badly, with complaint, but working. Dez had it spread across the cave floor like a patient on an operating table, its casing cracked, one board scorched black where the round had passed through the housing and missed his hip by the width of a prayer.

"It'll transmit," he'd said that morning, not looking up from the soldering iron. "Receive's the problem. Half the time it hears ghosts."

"Maybe it does," Okonjo said, because he was twenty-three and believed radios could hear things.

Marguerite stood at the mouth of the cave behind the falls, watching the water curtain bend the afternoon light into green glass. Behind her, Fen was copying Kessing's verified data onto waterproof paper in triplicate, because Fen trusted nothing to exist in only one place anymore. Ilse sat apart, sharpening a machete she would probably never use, doing it anyway, the way some people pray.

Kessing slept by the fire, feverish, her breathing shallow but stubborn. Two of her people kept watch at the back passage. The Tern lay dead in the flooded tributary below, and somewhere downriver, Halvern Consolidated's launch was anchored in the rain, waiting for an answer.

The radio crackled at 1600 hours exactly. Of course it did. Voss was a man who respected schedules the way other men respected God.

*Brightwater.* His voice came through shredded by static and the damaged receiver, but the cheerfulness survived intact. It always did. It was the most durable thing about him. *Do you copy?*

Dez looked up at her. She nodded once, took the handset, and stepped toward the cave mouth so the others wouldn't have to hear what she already knew was coming.

"I copy, Voss."

*A miracle of engineering, that radio. I told them you were resourceful. Nobody believed me.* A pause filled with rain-hiss. *How is Dr. Kessing? I hear she's been unwell.*

"You heard wrong."

*I hear everything, Marguerite. That's my whole job. I'm the listening end of optimism.* He laughed, a small warm sound, genuinely warm, which was the horror of him. *You sound tired. You sound like a woman standing in a cave.*

She didn't answer. Silence was a tool; she'd learned that from the sea. Let him spend words filling it.

He spent them gladly. *Here's where we are. The permits you're carrying are void as of this morning—paperwork error, very embarrassing, someone will be fired. So your delivery contract is, tragically, worthless. Your debt, however, remains very much alive. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and change, plus accrued interest, plus the penalty clause for non-delivery, which I'm afraid we wrote rather aggressively. You signed it. Chapter nine, subsection four. Everyone signs it.*

"I read chapter nine," she said.

*You skimmed it. There's a difference, and the difference has a number attached.* Another laugh. Then the warmth dropped away—not gone, just set aside, like a man removing his glasses before he says something serious. *But here's the thing. I'm authorized to make all of it disappear.*

The rain filled the pause. Behind her, she heard Fen's pen stop moving.

*Every figure. Zeroed. Not forgiven—forgiven implies you owed something, and I want to be clear that this is a transaction, not charity. Debt cleared. Your crew paid out at contract rates plus hazard bonus—I know what hazard bonus means to a mechanic like Dez, and I know what it means to a young navigator who hasn't bought his own boat yet. And Ilse—* he said the name carefully, tasting it, and Marguerite's grip tightened on the handset— *Ilse's record gets wiped. Every warrant, every flag, every forged-identity charge in three jurisdictions. She walks clean. She can be whoever she wants to be. Isn't that what she's always wanted?*

"She's listening," Marguerite said, "if you want to say this to her face."

*I'd rather say it to yours. You're the captain. Captains decide.* A breath of static. *In exchange: the data. Kessing's field records, the verified surveys, whatever she hid up there in the rocks. All of it, surrendered, and everyone walks away rich and free and dry. That's the offer, Marguerite. That's the whole offer. There isn't a second version with worse terms, because there isn't a second version. There's this, and then there's what happens if you don't take it, and I genuinely don't enjoy describing that part.*

"How long do I have?"

*Until the eye closes. Six hours, maybe eight. After that the storm owns the river again and my launch has to run for shelter, and then we're no longer negotiating—we're just two stories that ended differently.* His voice softened, and this was the worst part, because the softness was real too. Voss never lied about anything that mattered less than everything. *You've been running for twenty years, Brightwater. I've read your file. I know what you're carrying. This is the only door you will ever be offered that actually opens onto solid ground. Take it. Go home. Buy a boat that doesn't leak. Measure something longer than a delivery route.*

She looked out through the falling water at the drowned tributary, brown and boiling, and at the escarpment rising above it, black rock streaked silver, a wall a kilometer high that had stood through every monsoon since before anyone had a name for monsoons.

"Give me an hour," she said.

*An hour,* Voss agreed pleasantly. *I'll keep the kettle warm.*

The channel died into static. She set the handset down on the rock ledge and stood there a long moment, letting the spray hit her face, cold enough to feel honest.

* * *

They gathered without being called. That was the thing about her crew—they read weather in each other. Dez sat cross-legged by the ruined radio, soldering iron cooling in his hand. Okonjo perched on a supply crate, knees up, watching her with the open, terrible faith he'd carried since the day she'd hired him off a dock in Corvane. Ilse stayed by the fire, machete flat across her thighs, saying nothing. Fen stood nearest, arms folded over the waterproof pages, as if the data itself needed guarding from the conversation.

"He offered everything," Marguerite said. No point dressing it. "Debt's gone. Crew paid. Ilse's record wiped. In exchange for the data."

Silence. Rain on stone. The falls thundering their one note.

"Fourteen thousand," Dez said finally. "Plus penalties." He whistled low, a mechanic's whistle, the kind that priced things. "That's a boat. That's a shop. That's ten years of not eating rice out of a can."

"That's the point," Ilse said quietly.

Okonjo's voice came out smaller than she'd ever heard it. "If you take it, I'm not saying—" He stopped. Started again. "I'd understand. Captain. Whatever you do, I'd understand."

And that, more than the money, more than the threat, was the weight on the scale. Because she knew he meant it. He'd follow her either way—that was the trouble with loyalty, it didn't do arithmetic—and she could spend his future as easily as her own. She'd been spending other people's futures for years. Fuel borrowed against the next job. Risks taken with Dez's hands and Okonjo's neck because the debt made every choice urgent and urgency made every choice look like the only choice.

Voss understood that. It was his entire business model. He didn't buy people; he bought their exits and sold them back one at a time.

"The data proves the concession is a fraud," Fen said. "It proves the damming plan floods forty kilometers of valley. It proves they knew before they filed. If it goes downstream with us instead of to them, Halvern doesn't lose a survey—they lose the company."

"If it goes downstream with us," Dez said, "we come down off this rock with nothing. No boat, no pay, no names worth having. Some of us don't have names worth having *now*." He glanced at Ilse, and had the grace to look sorry immediately.

"My record's mine to spend," Ilse said. "Not yours. Not hers." She looked at Marguerite, and her eyes were steady and tired and older than her forged papers said she was. "I've been chasing this proof for six years. I buried my brother without it. I am not going to tell you what to do with your debt, Marguerite. But I'll tell you what I know about men like Voss." She turned the machete over slowly. "The wipe is real. He keeps his deals. That's how they stay in business. And the moment the deal is done, the valley drowns, and Kessing's people stay lost, and every audit after this one finds the same clean falsified numbers, because the falsifiers are still in business. He's not offering you a way out. He's offering you a seat. There's always room for one more optimist."

Marguerite said nothing. She walked to the back of the cave, where Kessing lay sleeping, and crouched beside her.

The scientist's face was gray with fever, but her hands, even asleep, were curled around a notebook like a woman holding a rail in high seas. On the tarp beside her lay the instruments, the sealed sample tubes, the pages Fen had copied in triplicate. Twenty years of someone's life pressed into paper and glass. Amara Kessing had come up this river to measure the truth of a mountain, and when the company came to take the measurement away, she'd climbed higher with it, into the rain, into the fever, into a cave behind a waterfall, and she had not let go.

Marguerite thought about the garage in Corvane. About the anemometer on the bench, its cups worn smooth, and the odometer on the Tern's dash reading distances in a language with no past tense. Miles to the next job. Miles until the payment cleared. Miles between her and wherever Voss's paperwork lived. Twenty years of measuring her life in distance because distance was the only unit that never accused her of anything. Distance didn't ask where you were going. Distance didn't ask why you never arrived.

Voss had said *go home*, and the word had landed in her chest like a hook.

She didn't have a home. She had a heading.

But crouched here in the wet dark, watching a stranger guard the truth with a fever and a notebook, Marguerite felt something shift in the old arithmetic—the first gear-change in twenty years, the odometer slipping, a different dial surfacing. She found herself measuring not miles but years. Years since she'd stood anywhere on purpose. Years since she'd finished anything instead of outrunning it. Years ahead, if she took Voss's deal—a paid crew, a clean boat, a debtless life—and saw them suddenly for what they were: a long flat road with nothing on it. And years ahead if she refused—shorter, maybe, harder, possibly ending badly on a wet stairwell or a brown flood—and saw those instead, and they had *people* in them. They had Dez arguing with engines and Okonjo earning his papers and Ilse under her real name, whatever it was, and a river station with a new roof, and mornings.

Voss was offering to buy her future. The trick of it, she saw now, was that he could only buy the part she was willing to sell. He thought she was selling distance. He'd read her file. He knew she ran.

He didn't know she'd stopped.

She stood up. Her knees cracked. Outside, the light through the falls had begun to fail, and the storm's breathing had changed—the long inhale of the eye gathering itself, the held breath before the walls closed.

"Dez," she said. "Can the radio reach the relay tower frequency from here, or does it need the tower?"

Dez blinked. "From here? Maybe, in the lull, low power, half the alphabet missing. From the tower?" He started to smile, then stopped himself, waiting.

"Then we climb tonight, in the eye window. We carry the data, the transmitter, and Kessing if she can be moved." She turned to the others. "Voss gets his answer at the top."

"And the answer is?" Fen asked, though her hands were already packing the waterproofs.

Marguerite picked up the handset one last time, keyed the damaged radio, and put enough power through the dying board to reach a launch anchored downriver. Her voice came out steady, and she noticed, distantly, that it sounded like a woman who had measured something and gotten her number.

"Tell your principal the delivery's proceeding as contracted. Original destination. Original cargo." She paused. "He should know I finally read the whole contract. There's no clause covering what happens next."

Static swallowed the channel whole. Below, faint through the roar of the falls, a launch engine woke up angry.

Above them, through a tear in the cloud, the first pale light of the eye opened over the escarpment like a door.

* * *

They moved in the strange gold hush of the storm's center. Okonjo went first up the goat path with the transmitter strapped to his back like a coffin for something small and important. Fen carried the data in triple copies, distributed across three bodies so that no single fall could kill it. Dez half-carried, half-dragged a stretcher frame with Kessing lashed in, her fever-bright eyes open now, watching the rock walls slide past. Ilse took the rear with the machete, counting the switchbacks aloud, and Marguerite climbed in the middle, empty-handed for the first time in twenty years, carrying only the decision.

Behind and below, small in the green distance, she could see the Halvern launch putting men ashore. Tiny figures in orange, starting up the trail.

She didn't measure how far behind they were.

For the first time, it wasn't the number that mattered.
