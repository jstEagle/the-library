## 29. The Broadcast

The lull came at 4:17 in the morning, and Ilse knew it for what it was because her brother had taught her to read the sky the way other people read faces. The rain didn't stop so much as hold its breath. The wind swung around through a quarter circle and went soft, and above the escarpment the clouds tore open along a seam of gray light, like a letter being opened.

"Eye of it," she said. "Or something close enough. We have maybe two hours. Maybe less."

Marguerite stood at the base of the goat path with Kessing's data case strapped across her back and looked up at the tower the way a person looks at a thing they've already decided to do. "Then we climb now."

They climbed.

The path was less a path than an argument the mountain had won against everyone who'd ever tried it—switchbacks cut into wet shale, handholds of root and rusted rebar left over from the tower's construction forty years ago. Okonjo went first with the salvaged transmitter in a waterproof duffel, moving with the strange sure-footedness he'd shown all week, as if his body had finally caught up to what his charts already knew. Fen came behind him, favoring a leg but refusing help. Dez carried the battery pack and said nothing about the graze on his arm except once, when the bandage soaked through, and then only a word Ilse pretended not to hear.

Ilse took the rear, because someone had to watch the way they'd come.

The company men were below them. She'd counted lanterns at the last switchback before the lull broke—six points of light strung out along the riverbank like a constellation that meant murder—and when she turned now, braced against the shale with the whole drowned valley spread beneath her, she could still see them. Smaller. Moving. Not retreating.

Climbing.

"They're on the path," she called down. No one answered, which was its own kind of answer. Everyone had known this was how it would go. You didn't steal a company's future out of its own filing cabinet and expect to be thanked.

Her hands were shaking, and she was honest enough with herself to admit it wasn't the cold.

Six years. Six years since Halvern Consolidated had taken Jonas's audit report off the record and Jonas himself off the map, since she had learned that a person could be erased not with violence but with paperwork—a name struck from a registry, a death certificate filed under a spelling of her surname that wasn't quite hers, an insurance payout signed by a cousin who swore afterward he'd never touched it. Six years of living under a name she'd bought from a drunk ferry clerk in a port town three rivers away, six years of answering to *Ilse* the way you answer to a coat that isn't yours. She had told them everything around the termite-mound fire—her brother, the audits, the long slow hunt—and Marguerite had listened without interrupting, which was more than Ilse deserved and exactly what she'd needed.

Now the proof was in a case on Marguerite's back, verified by Amara Kessing's own instruments at the escarpment itself, and the only thing between it and the world was four hundred meters of wet rock and a relay tower that hadn't been maintained since before Okonjo was born.

She climbed.

* * *

The tower rose out of the summit scrub like the skeleton of something that had once believed in itself. Lattice steel, guyed wires singing in the returning wind, a maintenance shack at the base with half its roof peeled back to the sky. The paint had gone the color of old bone. A placard by the door read HALVERN CONSOLIDATED — COMMUNICATIONS INFRASTRUCTURE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, and Ilse put her palm flat against it for a second, feeling the irony settle into her bones like rain.

Kessing's note had been specific: *Relay is passive-repeater, still hot, still licensed to the regional grid. Any transmitter with range can push through it. They never decommissioned it because decommissioning costs money.*

Halvern's greed had built the weapon. That felt right. That felt like the whole story in miniature.

Okonjo had the shack open in ninety seconds—the lock was older than he was—and inside there was a desk, a dead lamp, a wall of junction panels furred with salt corrosion, and a window looking east over the drop. He got the transmitter out of the duffel and set it down with the care of a man handling an egg made of glass.

"Power," he said.

Dez was already unrolling cable. "Battery's good for maybe twenty minutes of transmit at full draw. Less if we're pushing every channel." He glanced at Marguerite. "So we'd better know what we're saying."

Marguerite set the data case on the desk and opened it. Inside: Kessing's field notebooks, sealed in plastic. The original survey ledgers with their impossible elevations. Fen's photographs. And the ledger page from the burned camp, half-charred, the forgery visible in the ink where someone had altered the figures and gotten the arithmetic wrong—wrong in a way any competent surveyor would catch in an afternoon, wrong in a way that would be obvious to a port authority hearing, to a journalist, to a licensing board, to anyone with eyes and a reason to look.

"We say everything," Marguerite said. "Names first."

Ilse stepped forward. "Let me do the talking."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind was beginning to move again—not the full monsoon yet, but the eye closing, the sky remembering what it was for. Somewhere far below, faint through the steel, boots on shale.

"You're sure?" Marguerite said. "Once it goes out, it doesn't come back. And your name goes with it. The real one. Everything."

"That's the point." Ilse heard her own voice come out steady, which surprised her. "They erased my brother with paperwork. So we answer in paperwork. On the record. Every channel. Let them spend the rest of their lives trying to strike it."

Marguerite nodded once and stepped back.

Okonjo wired the transmitter to the battery while Dez held the light. The radio's casing was cracked from the shooting behind the falls, and one of the channel selectors stuck until Dez worked it loose with a screwdriver and a curse. Fen sat on the desk edge with Kessing's notebooks open, feeding Ilse pages, and Ilse wrote as fast as her hand would go—compressing six years and one dead man and one falsified concession into language that would survive being heard by strangers in the dark.

Not a plea. A report. Jonas had taught her that too: *If you sound like you're begging, they'll treat you like a beggar. Sound like a surveyor reading numbers, and they'll have to check the numbers.*

* * *

At 5:03, with the first fat drops of the returning rain starting to tick against the peeled roof, Ilse keyed the transmitter.

"This is a priority broadcast on all regional channels. I am transmitting verified survey data concerning the Kessing Escarpment concession, license number HC-1147, held by Halvern Consolidated. What follows is not opinion. It is measurement."

She read.

She read the elevation figures from the original Halvern surveys, and then the corrected figures from Kessing's instruments, and the difference between them—the difference that meant the escarpment's geology could not hold a dam, that the concession was unworkable without catastrophic flooding, that Halvern had known and filed the lie anyway and planned to cash out on development subsidies before the river exposed them. She read the alteration in the ledger, the changed digit, the arithmetic that didn't close. She named the surveyor whose signature appeared on the forged certification. She named the licensing officer. She named the subsidiary that had processed the subsidy applications.

She named Voss.

Not his title—he had many, all of them cheerful—but his name, and the names of the men who traveled with him, and the phrase *enforcement contractors retained to suppress survey personnel*, and then, flatly, the facts: the expedition silenced, the camp slashed, Dr. Amara Kessing driven into hiding, her deputy wounded by company employees, and an armed launch presently pursuing the people transmitting this message.

Fen fed her pages. Dez watched the battery gauge fall. Okonjo kept his ear pressed to the receiver, listening to the frequencies wake up one after another—a fishing boat answering, a downstream station repeating fragments, a voice asking *say again, say again, who is this*—and each time he looked up at Ilse with an expression she couldn't afford to examine too closely, because it was awe, and awe makes people careless.

Outside, the rain arrived in earnest. The wind found the guy wires and made them sing a rising note. And below—she checked twice, leaning out the broken window into the wet dark—the lanterns were higher. Two hundred meters down, maybe less. Moving faster than tired people should move, because they were being paid to.

"Ilse." Marguerite's voice, low. "Battery."

"I know."

"There's a response coming through on nine. Someone official. Port authority relay wants verification."

"Give me the mic."

The voice on channel nine belonged to a woman with a clipped accent and the tone of someone who had heard hoaxes before and punished them. "Transmitting party, identify yourself and your source data. Repeat: identify."

Ilse closed her eyes for half a second. This was the door. Names were currency; hers was counterfeit, had been for six years, and spending it here meant it could never be spent anywhere else again. Behind her, she felt the crew go quiet. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

"My name," she said, "is Vera Lindqvist. Daughter of Anneli Lindqvist. Sister of Jonas Lindqvist, auditor, who reported falsified surveys on this same concession six years ago and was subsequently erased from every registry in the territory. I have lived under a false identity since, because telling the truth about Halvern Consolidated was fatal to him and would have been fatal to me. My brother's original audit findings are included in the data package now being transmitted, cross-referenced against Dr. Amara Kessing's field verification of this month. Check both. They agree. That agreement is the verification you're asking for."

Silence on the channel. Rain on the roof. Boots on stone, closer.

Then the woman's voice again, different now. Slower. "Lindqvist. Stand by. We have the Kessing expedition listed missing since the ninth. Stand by."

"Standing by," Ilse said, "is what everyone did for six years. Transmit the data. That's how you stand by."

She killed the channel and kept reading. There were eleven pages left and the battery gauge was in the red and the lanterns below had stopped being lanterns and become voices—shouts, thin with distance and wind, ordering them to cease transmission, identifying themselves as licensed security personnel, promising consequences with the bland confidence of men who had always been paid on time.

Dez moved to the stairwell door with a pry bar in his good hand. Okonjo picked up a length of rebar. Marguerite stood beside the transmitter with her arms crossed, watching Ilse the way she watched weather—as if measuring something only she understood.

"Keep going," Marguerite said. "We'll keep them busy."

"No," Ilse said, and surprised herself. "Nobody holds a stairwell for me. Not this time. Jonas died alone because everyone who could have helped decided it was safer to wait." She keyed the mic again. "I finish this in four minutes. Everyone else gets down the goat path and takes the data copies with them. That's not a request."

"It's a terrible plan," Dez said.

"It's the only plan where the truth survives even if I don't."

Marguerite met her eyes across the shack, and whatever passed between them needed no radio. Then Marguerite pulled the duplicate memory cards from the case, distributed them—Fen, Okonjo, her own pocket—and said, "Two minutes, Ilse. Then we all go together or not at all. I don't run crews that die in pieces."

The rain became a wall. The first boot hit the ladder platform below the shack, and metal rang like a bell.

Ilse read the last page. She named the subsidy amounts. She named the dates. She said, "This data has been transmitted simultaneously on fourteen channels to downstream stations, the port authority, and two press relays. It cannot be recalled. Halvern Consolidated's concession is built on a forgery, and now everyone knows it," and she signed off with her real name, spoken aloud into the storm for the first time in six years—

—and the stairwell door burst inward.
