## 30. The Tower Fight

Dez had always believed that machines told you the truth before people did. The Tern's engine had warned him for three years before she died—a tick in the idle, a whisper of metal where there should have been oil. He'd heard it and he'd loved her anyway, and when she broke apart on the rocks below the escarpment he'd apologized out loud like a fool, because some partings deserve words even if nobody hears them.

Now the relay tower was talking to him too.

It was an old lattice structure, forty meters of rusted steel bolted into the escarpment's shoulder, built by some government survey crew decades back and abandoned to the birds. Its guy wires sang in the wind—three different notes, off-key, like a choir that hadn't practiced since the last survey team went home. And beneath the singing, under his palms where they pressed against the tower's cold leg, he could feel it: a vibration coming up through the rock. Rhythmic. Heavy.

Engines. More than one.

"Okonjo," he said quietly.

The boy was crouched at the base of the tower with the salvaged transmitter open across his knees, feeding cable while Ilse worked the panel above, her hands moving over switches that hadn't been thrown in twenty years. Marguerite stood at the cliff edge with Kessing's field glasses, watching the brown flood of the river far below. Fen was higher up the goat path with Dr. Kessing slung between two loyalists who still looked at the rest of them like they might be company men wearing borrowed faces.

"Dez?" Okonjo didn't look up from the transmitter. His fingers were steady. They'd been steady all night, through the lightning work on the Tern's hull, through the climb, through everything. Dez was proud of him and terrified for him in equal measure, which he was learning was just what love felt like when it lived inside a body.

"Feel the rock."

Okonjo put his palm flat against the tower's concrete footing. A second passed. Two.

"How many?"

"Two engines minimum. Maybe three. Coming up the tributary side—the old haul road, I think. The one that used to serve the quarry."

Marguerite lowered the field glasses. She didn't ask how Dez knew. He'd spent six days listening to this country—the river, the rain, the groan of drowned trees shifting their roots—and somewhere in those six days the country had started answering him back. Or maybe he'd finally learned its language. Either way, when he said *engines*, she believed him.

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes. Maybe less if the road holds better than I think it does."

Ilse's voice came down from the panel, flat and fast. "I need eleven minutes to warm the transmitter and find the frequency spread. Twelve to be safe. Fifteen if the modulation board fights me, and it will fight me, because it's older than everyone on this mountain except possibly me."

"Then we hold them for fifteen minutes," Marguerite said.

Nobody said the obvious thing, which was: *with what?* They had a machete, a boat hook, two flare pistols with four flares total, Okonjo's signal mirror, and whatever the storm decided to lend them. Halvern's men would come up that haul road with rifles and numbers and the particular patience of people who were paid by the hour to make problems disappear.

Dez looked at the stairwell.

It wasn't much. The tower had an internal ladder-and-landing arrangement, steel treads bolted zigzag up the inside of the lattice, narrow enough that two men couldn't pass abreast. At the base, a cage of angle iron framed the entrance—the kind of thing meant to keep goats out, not gunfire. But a stairwell is a stairwell. It funnels. It makes five men into one man, one man at a time, as long as somebody's willing to stand at the bottom and be the reason the funnel stays plugged.

He'd held worse positions for worse reasons. He'd once spent nine hours in a flooded engine room keeping a bilge pump alive with a belt and a prayer, because the alternative was the sea. This was the same arithmetic. Something behind him needed protecting. So he stood.

"I'll take the stairs," Dez said.

* * *

They argued about it for ninety seconds, which was eighty seconds more than they had.

Marguerite wanted to take it herself. Of course she did. Dez had watched her do this math her whole way up the river—herself first, always herself first, her body offered up like currency against every debt. But she was the one Ilse needed calm beside her at the panel, and she was the one Kessing trusted, and she was the one whose voice on a radio channel could buy them thirty seconds of hesitation from men who'd rather not shoot a woman they'd been ordered to capture intact.

Okonjo wanted to take it with him. That argument lasted less time, because Dez simply said, "You're faster than me. If I go down, someone has to tell them what happened, and it can't be a ghost." Which was a lie, because Okonjo wasn't faster than him, not really—but the boy believed it, and belief would keep him moving when the bad moment came.

So it was the two of them. Dez with the machete and the boat hook and a flare pistol loaded with a red cartridge he'd saved from the gorge, praying to no god in particular that he wouldn't need it. Okonjo with the second flare pistol and a coil of the Tern's salvaged hawser, which he'd rigged across the lower landing in a tripwire that would have embarrassed any professional but might embarrass an amateur enough to matter.

Above them, Ilse threw the master switch, and the transmitter began to hum—a low, rising note that Dez felt in his teeth, in the soles of his boots, in the fillings of his jaw. The whole tower became a struck bell, slowly winding up to its note.

"They'll hear that from the haul road," Okonjo said.

"That's the idea." Dez rolled his shoulders. "Let them come angry. Angry men run upstairs."

"And tired men hold them," Okonjo said, and grinned at him, and Dez thought: *this kid. This ridiculous, brave, impossible kid.* Six weeks ago Okonjo had been a navigator who idolized Marguerite from a careful distance, all charts and deference. Now he was rigging tripwires on a mountain in a monsoon, grinning at a mechanic twice his age like they were brothers-in-arms in some story they'd both read.

"We're not heroes," Dez told him. "I want that on the record. We're a mechanic and a navigator standing in a doorway."

"Best doorway in the world," Okonjo said.

Below them, faint through the rain, the engines stopped.

* * *

They came at fourteen minutes past the hour, which meant Ilse had maybe ten minutes left on her warm-up, and they came fast—no caution, no scouting, exactly the way angry men run upstairs. Dez heard them before he saw them: boots on wet gravel, then boots on the first steel treads, and a voice barking orders in the clipped cadence of hired professionals. *Two up. Rest at the landing. Watch your corners.*

The first man around the corner of the cage was young, rain-slicked, rifle up—and he hit Dez's hawser at knee height, which was exactly the height tired men forget about. The line caught his ankle mid-stride and the world performed its oldest trick: gravity, indifferent and total. He went down hard on the angle iron, rifle clattering, and Dez was already moving, boat hook sweeping the weapon off the landing into the dark below, machete flat against the man's throat—not cutting, just present, just a conversation in the only language that ends arguments quickly.

"Turn around," Dez said. "Walk down. Tell whoever's next that the stairs are occupied."

The man's eyes went wide, then calculating, then—he was young—young enough to choose living. He scrambled backward down the steps and Dez heard him shouting below: *There's two of them! There's only two!*

Only two. Dez almost laughed. For fifteen minutes, two would be enough.

The second man came smarter—low, slow, clearing the tripwire with a boot before stepping over it. He was older, gray at the temples, and he didn't rush the machete. He shot first.

The round sparked off the angle iron a hand's width from Dez's ear and the world went white and ringing. He dropped flat behind the cage frame, felt splinters of concrete sting his cheek, heard Okonjo's flare pistol cough above him—and then red light filled the stairwell, a burning star hissing and spitting on the treads between them, and the gray-templed man flinched back from it with a shout, because no training in the world fully prepares you for fire in an enclosed space.

Dez moved in the light. Later he would remember almost nothing of it—just shapes and noise and the terrible intimacy of close quarters, the boat hook's crack against a forearm, the machete turning a rifle aside, his own breath roaring in his ears. What he remembered clearly was Okonjo beside him the whole time, never retreating, feeding him angles—"Left! He's on your left!"—the boy's voice steady as a compass bearing, holding Dez's blind side the way Dez had once held the Tern's engine room alone.

They bought the first three minutes that way. Then the fourth cost them.

A third man came up behind the gray-templed one, and he didn't fight fair or smart—he fired wild, three rounds into the lattice, and one of them found Dez.

Not center mass. Not clean. It tore through the outside of his right thigh, high, above the muscle's thickest part, and the pain arrived a half-second late like thunder after lightning, and then all at once, total, a white scream down his leg. He went down onto his good knee. Blood sheeted warm and shocking down his shin, pooling in his boot, and the stairwell tilted, and somewhere very far away Okonjo was screaming his name.

"Don't—" Dez grabbed the boy's collar as Okonjo lunged forward into the open. "Don't chase. Hold. *Hold.*"

Because that was the job. The job was never to win. The job was to be a door.

He braced his back against the cage frame, dragged his ruined leg under him, and raised the flare pistol toward the darkness below, where boots were hesitating on the gravel, deciding whether two bleeding men on a staircase were worth the price of climbing past them.

Red starlight burned above the transmitter's rising hum. Eight minutes gone. Seven to go.

"Hold," Dez said again—to Okonjo, to himself, to the tower, to the storm.

* * *

They came twice more, and both times the stairwell did what stairwells do.

The fourth assault nearly broke them—a big man with a crowbar who simply refused to acknowledge the machete, absorbing a slash across the shoulder like it was weather, getting a hand on Dez's collar before Okonjo brought the boat hook down across his knuckles and Dez drove his forehead into the man's face. They went down together in a tangle on the landing, and Dez's thigh screamed so loudly the world went gray at the edges, and when the big man finally retreated Dez discovered he couldn't stand anymore. Couldn't quite feel the leg at all now, below the wound.

So he sat. Back against the angle iron, machete across his lap, blood ticking steadily onto the steel treads like a slow leak in a hull he could no longer reach.

"Dez." Okonjo was kneeling beside him, pressing something against the wound—sailcloth, from the patch kit, of course it was sailcloth, Dez almost wept at the poetry of it. "Stay with me. Talk to me. Tell me about the engine room. Tell me about the pump."

"The belt," Dez managed. "Nine hours. Slipped twice. You tie a bilge belt like—like a figure eight, not a granny knot, anybody who ties a granny knot deserves to drown—"

"I'm listening."

"—and the prayer doesn't work unless you mean it, that's the thing nobody tells you, you can't fake a prayer to a pump, pumps know—"

Okonjo laughed, wet-eyed, reloading the flare pistol with their last cartridge. "Pumps know. Got it."

Below them, the voices changed. Dez heard it through the pain-haze—the cadence shifting from *assault* to *report*, and then a new voice joined the chorus, and even muffled by rain and distance and ringing ears, Dez knew it. Cheerful. Reasonable. The voice of a man who invests in people.

Voss.

He was down there on the haul road, shouting up through a megaphone now, offering things—amnesty, money, the debt erased, all of it, everything, just come down, just stop, just let the silence finish what it started. And Dez sat in his own blood on a steel staircase and listened to the kindest-sounding monster he'd ever met make the best offer anyone had ever made him, and he thought about the Tern going to pieces on the rocks, and about Ilse's brother dead six years in a file nobody read, and about Okonjo's hands, steady as a compass bearing.

"Hey," Dez called down, weakly, into the rain. "Voss. Hey."

A pause. "Yes, my friend? You've made your point. Come down. Nobody else needs to be hurt."

"You ever listen to a machine?" Dez shouted. "Before you scrap it? Before you decide it's worth nothing?" He was laughing now, or crying, or both; the leg had stopped mattering. "Machines tell you the truth first. Every time. Your surveys lied, Voss. Your whole company's a granny knot. And everybody who tied it deserves to drown."

Silence below. Then, softly, without the megaphone, almost gentle: "Take the stairs."

* * *

What happened next, Dez watched from the landing, because he could no longer do anything but watch.

They came up five abreast at the bottom, stacking for the funnel—and the river answered first.

It came as sound before sight: a deep, rolling concussion from the valley, like the sky clearing its throat, and then the flood surge hit the base of the escarpment. Dez felt it through the tower's legs, a shudder in the bedrock, and heard the screams start below—short, shocked, human. The haul road, that old quarry track cut along the waterline, vanished. He saw headlights sweep sideways through the rain, saw torch beams cartwheel, heard the megaphone clatter away down rock. The storm surge took the boats first—Halvern's fast launches, moored careless at the road's edge, picked up like toys and slammed into the quarry wall with sounds like doors closing forever. Then it took the road itself, a whole section of it peeling away into the brown chaos, men scrambling upslope, dropping rifles to claw at mud and root.

One of the five stacked at the bottom of the stairs turned and ran. Then another. Discipline is a machine too, Dez thought dimly, and machines tell the truth first, and the truth tonight was that the river did not care who held the concession.

The gray-templed man held longest. He stood at the bottom of the stairwell in the torchlight, looking up at Dez—at the blood, the machete, the burning red star still hissing on the treads—and Dez looked back down at him, and neither of them moved, and above them the transmitter's hum climbed toward its note.

Then the man's radio crackled with orders Dez couldn't hear, and his face went strange—listening to something upstream, something downstream, something already loose in the world that couldn't be called back—and he lowered his rifle.

"Fall back," he said, to whoever was left to hear. "Fall back to the ridge. All of you. *Now.*"

Boots receded into the rain. The stairwell emptied. The red flare guttered and died, and in the sudden dark Dez sagged against the cage frame, and Okonjo caught him, and above them—through the lattice, through the rain, through the whole wounded listening night—the relay tower began, at last, to speak.

Ilse's voice rode the carrier wave, raw and clear and unstoppable, naming names.

Dez closed his eyes and held the door.
