## 31. The River Answers

The tower shook every time the wind hit it, and Okonjo had learned to read the shaking like a second language.

There was the shudder of gusts against the lattice steel—short, percussive, a drummer's roll. There was the longer groan of the guy-wires taking strain, a sound like a whale thinking out loud. And there was the deep, arrhythmic sway that meant the whole structure was leaning into the storm and deciding, moment by moment, whether to keep standing. He'd been on the platform for six hours now, wedged in the transmitter shack with Ilse while rain came sideways through every gap in the corrugated walls, and he had catalogued all three sounds and decided he hated the third one most.

"Signal's still clean," Ilse said. She had her earpiece pressed hard against her skull with one hand and the transmit key held down with the other, and she was shouting over the noise without seeming to notice she was shouting. "Port authority relay just acknowledged. They're repeating us downstream."

"How far downstream?"

"All of it." She grinned at him through the dark, lit blue by the console's glow. "Every station from here to the delta is hearing a dead woman's survey data read out by a woman who doesn't exist. You can't unring that bell, Okonjo."

He wanted to feel triumphant. He kept checking for the feeling the way you check your pocket for something you're not sure you brought. It wasn't there yet. What was there was the stairwell below them, where Dez had been holding the line with Marguerite for the last hour against men climbing in the rain, and what was there was the sound—when the wind dropped between gusts—of small arms fire, thin and distant, like someone cracking walnuts two rooms away.

The transmission itself had been running for forty minutes. Kessing's data didn't need forty minutes—a summary would have taken five—but Ilse was reading all of it. Every elevation figure. Every core sample. Every page of the original Halvern field surveys set beside Dr. Amara Kessing's verified readings from behind the falls, the discrepancies flagged and named, the names spelled out slowly, twice, so that no half-attentive radio operator anywhere on this river could miss them. Voss's name. The concession directors. The survey office that had signed off on numbers that could not have been measured by any instrument ever built.

"She's reading the appendices," Okonjo said. "Nobody reads the appendices."

"Somebody will," Ilse said. "Somebody always does. That's how this works—you don't convince the people at the top, you convince the bored clerk three desks down who suddenly realizes his signature is on something that's about to be worth nothing. Or worse than nothing."

The console chirped. An incoming signal, weak, riding the edge of the storm's interference. Ilse pulled one earcup aside, listened, and her grin changed character entirely—it went from fierce to something almost disbelieving.

"What?"

"That's the port authority duty officer." She listened another few seconds. "They're asking us to confirm our source documentation. Standard verification protocol." She laughed, a short bark. "They're treating it as real. Okonjo—they're *treating it as real*. That means it's already past the point where anyone upstream can make it go away."

Okonjo leaned toward the mic. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Somewhere over the last month—the locks, the fog, the gorge, the drowned forest, the climb—he seemed to have used up whatever portion of himself was capable of panic, and what remained was a strange, level calm that felt borrowed from Marguerite, or maybe from Dez, or maybe from the river itself, which did not hurry and did not stop.

"This is Tern station," he said. "Confirming source: verified field data of Dr. Amara Kessing, lead scientist, Kessing Escarpment Concession Survey, transmitted under her authority. Repeat, under her authority. She's alive. She's up here."

Ilse looked at him sharply. That part hadn't been scripted. Naming Kessing alive was a risk—if Halvern still had any reach left, they'd just been told their problem wasn't solved after all. But Okonjo had watched Fen carry water up the goat path for a woman too feverish to walk, and he'd watched Marguerite sit through the night holding Kessing's hand and talking to her about anemometers, of all things, and he understood something now that he hadn't understood at the locks when all this started: silence was the weapon. Silence was the whole weapon. Everything else—the permits, the debt, the launch, the guns—was just machinery for maintaining it. And the only countermove was to say the name out loud, on every channel, until the silence broke apart like ice going out in spring.

The duty officer's voice crackled back, and this time it wasn't asking questions. It was giving information, fast, tumbling: the port authority was convening an emergency session; the concession review board had been woken; a journalist named Adaeze Okafor—who had been chasing the escarpment story for two years and had been sued into silence once already—was standing by on the maritime band and requesting direct contact.

"Put her through," Ilse said.

* * *

Adaeze Okafor talked like someone who had been waiting years for this call and had rehearsed it in the shower.

She asked sharp, precise questions—dates, document numbers, chain of custody—and Ilse answered them with a fluency that made Okonjo realize, again, how long she'd been carrying this. Six years. Six years of knowing exactly which figures were forged and being unable to get anyone to listen, because the people who needed to listen were paid not to, and the people who might have listened for free couldn't hear her over the sound of their own concessions.

Then Okafor said the thing that changed the shape of the night.

"I should tell you," she said, "that I have a source inside Halvern's survey office. Has had for months. Wouldn't give me anything usable—too scared, or too well-paid, I never found out which. But twenty minutes ago he called me. Unprompted. He said—" and here Okonjo heard her pause, heard her choose to deliver it exactly as it had been delivered to her, "—he said, 'It's true. All of it. Check the 4-series cores. We never drilled past forty meters.' Then he hung up."

The rain filled the pause.

"He's confirming," Ilse said quietly. "Halvern's own surveyor is confirming."

"On record if I can get him to a microphone. Off record as of tonight. Which, combined with your transmission, gives me—" Okonjo could hear her smiling, a dangerous sound, "—a story that writes its own ending. Get your data to me in duplicate. Keep transmitting. And keep your heads down, because the next hour is when frightened companies do stupid things."

As if summoned, the wind slammed the tower sideways and the third sound arrived—the deep sway, the leaning, the deliberation—and somewhere below them the walnut-cracking stopped.

* * *

The silence from the stairwell lasted four minutes. Okonjo counted them against the console clock, and each minute was longer than the one before.

Then boots on the stairs, descending fast, and Marguerite's voice coming up through the hatch, hoarse but unmistakably hers, carrying that flat competence that had fooled him completely back in chapter one of his own life, back when he'd thought she was just a delivery captain with good instruments:

"Stand down! They're pulling back!"

He took the ladder two rungs at a time. At the bottom, in the lee of the tower's base where the generator shed cut the wind, he found a scene he would remember for the rest of his life, though he wouldn't understand all of it until much later.

Dez was sitting on the bottom step with his leg stretched out and a strip of sailcloth knotted around his forearm above a graze that bled more than it hurt, judging by his expression, which was mostly indignation. Marguerite stood at the corner of the shed with a signal lamp, watching the tree line. And down the slope, through the rain, Okonjo saw them: headlamps bobbing away downhill, six or seven of them, moving fast and low toward the river, toward the launch, toward downstream. Not advancing. Retreating. Men who had climbed a mountain in a monsoon to seize a tower now climbing down off it in a hurry, leaving their sandbags and their spare magazines and, near the base of the ladder, a radio handset that had been thrown down hard enough to crack its casing.

"They got the call," Marguerite said, without turning around. "Everyone got the call. Their own surveyors are on the air confirming the forgery, Okonjo. Their lawyers are telling them that everything they've done in the last month just changed from 'aggressive asset protection' to 'evidence.'" She lowered the lamp. "You don't shoot your way out of that. You run, and you lawyer up, and you hope you're not the name everyone remembers."

"Voss?" Dez asked.

"Gone first. His launch cast off before his men reached the beach." Something moved across Marguerite's face—not satisfaction, Okonjo thought. Something older and wearier. "He'll be at the locks by morning, trying to be the helpful witness. They always do that. The cheerful ones are always the fastest runners."

Dez tried to stand, winced, and settled for raising his good arm in a gesture that managed to be both a salute and a complaint. "I held the stairwell," he announced, to no one and everyone. "Against seven men. With a bolt-action and bad knees."

"With three men," Marguerite said, "and excellent knees, and Okonjo's rifle covering the switchbacks, and a storm doing most of the work. But yes. You held it."

Okonjo felt the missing feeling arrive then, finally, late and slightly embarrassing, the way joy does when it has to climb a mountain in the rain to find you. It wasn't triumph exactly. It was closer to the sensation he'd gotten at fourteen, the first time he'd steered a boat through current instead of fighting it—that sudden understanding that the water had been willing to carry him all along, if he'd only stop bracing against it. The world was turning. Not because they were strong enough to turn it, but because they'd finally stopped holding it still.

Above them, faint through the hatch, Ilse's voice went on reading elevation figures into the storm, patient as tide.

* * *

The answers kept coming all night.

That was the thing none of them had predicted—the sheer volume of it. For six hours Ilse had shouted into what felt like a void, and now the void turned out to have been full of listeners the whole time, the way a night river is full of fish you can't see until you hang a light.

The maritime band became a chorus. Station operators downstream, some official, some not, began relaying fragments: the port authority session had voted unanimously to freeze all Halvern vessel movements pending inquiry; the concession review board had issued a suspension order effective immediately; the delta customs house reported that Halvern's regional director had attempted to board a coastal freighter at midnight and had been detained at the gangway by two very polite officers and a growing crowd.

At 0300, Adaeze Okafor came back on the main channel, live, and her voice had changed—no longer the rehearsed interviewer but something rawer, running on adrenaline and vindication. Her inside source had agreed to go on record. He was already in her studio. His name was Pelin Aru, senior geotechnical surveyor, eleven years with Halvern Consolidated, and when he spoke, his voice shook and then steadied, and what he said was simple enough that even a young navigator with no legal training understood every word:

"The 4-series cores were never drilled. I signed the logs. I signed them because my supervisor told me the drilling program had been completed by a subcontractor, and because I checked, and there was no subcontractor. There was no drilling rig. There was a payment, and a template, and a signature block with my name pre-filled. I have spent three years telling myself someone else would catch it." A breath. "Someone caught it. From a mountain, in a monsoon, on a stolen transmitter. I'm sorry it took me this long to be that brave."

Okonjo sat on the wet grating outside the shack and listened to a grown man cry on the radio, and beside him Dez, leg propped on a crate, said softly, "Six years Ilse waited for that. Six years." And wiped his eyes and blamed the rain, and nobody argued.

By dawn the pattern had inverted completely. All night the world had been answering them; now the world began calling *for* them. Requests to repeat sections of the data. A request from the inquiry clerk for the original documents' location—which Fen answered from the shack, describing the sealed case in Kessing's field camp behind the falls with the precision of a man who had carried it up a mountain himself. A request from the port authority patrol coordinator for their position and condition, and whether they required medical evacuation, and Marguerite's answer—*one wounded, stable, we'll bring him down ourselves when the river allows*—sounded so much like the woman who had recalibrated anemometers in a garage a lifetime ago that Okonjo had to turn away and look at the escarpment to keep his composure.

And once, briefly, a signal that was not a request. A Halvern corporate frequency, encrypted, brief, and—because grief makes companies sloppy—partially readable on the open scanner: instructions to all field personnel to cease operations, secure records, and cooperate fully with authorities. Signed by someone whose title Okonjo didn't recognize, because the man whose title he did recognize was, at that moment, in custody at the locks or very close to it.

"It's over," Dez said. "Just like that? After everything—it's just *over*?"

Marguerite was quiet for a while. The rain had thinned to a steady grey drizzle, and the east was doing something pale and hopeful behind the cloud deck.

"It's not over," she said. "It's *turned*. Over takes months. Trials, inquiries, receivership, all of it. But the turning happens in one night, and you know it's happened because the powerful people start talking in the passive voice." She almost smiled. "'Mistakes were made.' 'Irregularities have come to light.' Watch for it. By noon it'll be everywhere."

She was right, of course. By noon it was everywhere.

But before noon, before the descent, before any of the rest of it, Okonjo climbed back up to the shack one more time. Ilse was still there, though the transmission was done; she was just sitting with the headset around her neck, looking out at the escarpment through the broken window, at the country her brother had died measuring.

"They confirmed it," Okonjo said. "Your brother's numbers. Pelin Aru's confession matches the audit findings exactly. Someone's going to enter them into the record now. Officially."

Ilse nodded slowly. She didn't say anything for a long moment, and Okonjo had been on the river long enough now to know that some silences weren't silences at all, just conversations happening at a depth he hadn't learned to sound yet.

"He would have liked the method," she said finally. "The broadcast. He was always saying the data was useless unless it was loud." She straightened the headset on the console, squared it, the way Dez squared tools. "All right, navigator. Go wake your captain. The river's dropping, and we've got a wounded man, a dead boat, and a doctor to carry home."

Okonjo went down the ladder with the storm easing behind him and the whole river talking, and at the bottom he found Marguerite already awake, already packed, already looking downstream—toward the middle station, toward the wreck of the Tern, toward whatever came after the turning of the world.

"One problem," she said, when he told her the patrol launch was coming. "They're coming to rescue survivors of the Kessing expedition." Her eyes went to the crippled hulk of the Tern where it lay wedged in the drowned forest below, and then to the salvaged radio crate—the original delivery, the one contract that had started everything—lashed under the tarp. "Nobody down there knows yet that we were ever a delivery crew at all. And there's one item in that crate that says otherwise."

Okonjo followed her gaze to the crate, and remembered, with a cold drop in his stomach, exactly which papers were sealed inside it alongside the radio equipment.

The permits. The Halvern concession permits. Still stamped, still signed, still addressed for delivery upstream.

Still, technically, undelivered.
