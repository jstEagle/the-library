## 32. Descent in Flood

The rain came back like a debt collector.

Marguerite felt it before she heard it—a pressure change in her ears, the same tell she'd chased across three coastlines for twenty years—and by the time she said "brace" to no one in particular, the storm had already swallowed the summit. The relay tower vanished behind a gray curtain, and with it went the last hour of their lives: the transmission, the names read aloud into every open channel, the company men beginning their climb.

"Down," Ilse said. Not a suggestion.

They went down.

Dez couldn't walk. That was the arithmetic they started with, and it was bad arithmetic. The shrapnel graze from the tower fight had gone septic-fast in the wet heat, or maybe it had always been worse than Dez let on—Dez was constitutionally incapable of admitting a machine or a body was failing until it failed spectacularly. Now she hung between Okonjo and Fen on a stretcher improvised from the tower's canvas equipment wrap and two climbing poles Kessing's people had cached at the goat path's head, and every jolt of the descent pulled a sound out of her that Marguerite pretended not to catalog.

Kessing came too. Nobody had argued about it. The fever had broken sometime during the night in the cave camp—whether from Fen's antibiotics or sheer stubbornness, Marguerite couldn't say—but the doctor moved like a woman relearning her own legs, one hand on the rock, and she refused the stretcher with a look that ended discussion. "I've been carried enough," was all she said. "I'll walk down my own escarpment."

*Her* escarpment. Marguerite noticed the possessive and filed it away. Six weeks hiding above the cloud line, verifying readings that would kill a billion-dollar concession, and Amara Kessing had stopped being a visitor here somewhere along the way. Maybe that was what staying looked like when it wasn't surrender.

Two of Kessing's team led with machetes, opening the flooded goat path. Ilse took point behind them, reading the rock the way she read water—which is to say faster than it could lie to her. Marguerite brought up the rear with the data case strapped to her chest like a child, because nobody had needed to discuss that either. The verified surveys, Kessing's field notebooks, the sealed originals of everything Ilse had broadcast from the tower: it all rode against Marguerite's sternum, and if she went over the edge, the case went with her. She'd made that decision without noticing she'd made it, which was how her best decisions usually arrived.

The first kilometer took four hours.

Not because of distance. Because of water. The monsoon had spent six days loading itself over the plateau, and now it was spending, all at once, directly onto the escarpment's face. Waterfalls appeared where the maps showed dry gullies. The goat path became a stream, then a chute, then a series of pools linked by handholds. Okonjo went ahead of the stretcher at each bad section, testing holds with his whole weight before he'd let them bring Dez through, and Marguerite watched him do it and thought: *I trained him for weather, not this.* Then she thought: *He's better at this than I am.* Both thoughts were true, and neither one helped.

At the second switchback, the path simply ended.

A slide had taken it—half the hillside, trees and rock and forty years of soil, sheared off and sent down into the white roar below. Kessing's lead scout stood at the lip and looked back at them with the particular blankness of a man doing math he doesn't like.

Ilse got there first. She lay flat at the edge, rain hammering her back, and studied the scar in the hillside for a long minute. Then she pointed—not down the slide, but across it, where the path resumed on the far side, a pale thread eight meters away.

"We traverse," she said. "One at a time. The debris is fresh—it hasn't settled, but it also hasn't been undercut yet. We move before it decides."

"And if it decides while we're on it?" Fen asked.

"Then we decide faster."

They crossed Dez first, because you cross your most fragile cargo first, while everyone still has hands and judgment. Okonjo and Fen carried the poles; Ilse went ahead calling footing; Marguerite followed with a rope around her own waist paying out slack, which meant if anyone slid, she slid last, into whatever caught them. The debris field shifted underfoot like something breathing. Halfway across, a boulder the size of a truck cab groaned and settled ten centimeters, and Dez—feverish, half-conscious—said clearly, "That's a bearing going, that noise," and then laughed at her own joke until the laughing turned into coughing.

They made the far side. All of them. Marguerite stood on solid rock with her heart going like an anemometer in a squall and understood, distantly, that she had just done the most dangerous thing of her life for a woman she'd hired to drive a boat, and had not once considered doing otherwise. When had that happened? Somewhere between the locks and the tower, apparently, her crew had become the thing she measured everything else against.

She didn't have time to examine it. The rain didn't permit examination. It only permitted the next step, and the next.

* * *

By late afternoon the escarpment's lower terraces opened into drowned forest, and the river told them what it had done while they were up on the rock.

They heard it first—a sound like sustained thunder that never resolved into lightning—and then the trees thinned and they saw it: the tributary they'd hauled the Tern up two days ago was gone. Not high. Not flooded. *Gone*, replaced by a brown ocean moving at walking-pace speed but carrying houses in it. Whole trees ran past them crown-first, spinning slowly, like things deciding. A roof went by, intact, riding the current with terrible calm. Somewhere inside it, Marguerite knew, was a village that had accepted Halvern money and signed papers it hadn't understood, and now the concession's falsified flood models had come due on schedule—the real ones, the ones Kessing had found buried under the forged ones, had predicted exactly this.

The company had known the dam scheme would drown these villages. They'd priced it in as relocation costs, then pocketed the relocation money too. And the river, indifferent to paperwork, was collecting anyway.

"The Tern," Okonjo said quietly. He wasn't asking. He was standing at the flood's edge with his pilot's eyes measuring current and debris load, and Marguerite watched him do the same math she was doing and arrive at the same answer and hate it.

"She was tied above the high-water mark we calculated," Marguerite said. "We calculated wrong."

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying at volume. Dez, drifting in and out on Fen's shoulder, murmured something that might have been an apology addressed to the boat, and Marguerite turned away so she wouldn't have to have a face about it.

They camped that night on a granite shelf above the flood line, the whole party pressed together under one tarp, taking turns holding the edges because the wind kept trying to take the tarp as tax. Kessing sat with her back against the rock and talked, low, mostly to keep Dez oriented—asking her about engines, about the Tern's refit, letting the mechanic narrate repairs to a boat that was probably currently a distributed feature of the delta. It was the gentlest thing Marguerite had ever seen a scientist do, and she realized Kessing had probably done it before, for other frightened people in other bad camps. You don't run a survey team into monsoon country without learning how to hold people together.

Marguerite took first watch. From the granite shelf she could see the flood plain stretching to the horizon, moonless, lit only by the storm's internal flicker, and she did the thing she always did: she measured. Distance to the middle station, if it still existed. Distance downstream to the locks. Distance Voss's men would have to cover, assuming any of them had survived the tower and the climb down. Her mind ran the numbers automatically, odometer turning, and then—she noticed herself doing it—she stopped.

Instead she counted heads. Nine. Two scouts, Kessing, Fen, Ilse, Okonjo, Dez, herself, and the data case making ten if you counted cargo. Everyone accounted for. Everyone breathing. For the first time in twenty years, the number that mattered wasn't a distance.

It was a strange feeling. It fit badly, like new boots. She decided she could get used to it anyway.

* * *

The last day of the descent, the forest ended and the flood took over completely, and that was when the descent stopped being a hike and became a negotiation with the water.

The middle station's ridge was visible across four kilometers of brown ocean—the station itself just a dark smudge on high ground, its radio mast either standing or not, impossible to tell at this distance. Between them and it: drowned fields, submerged fences, the tops of trees dragging currents into eddies. Wading was suicide; swimming was worse. But Kessing's scouts had grown up on this river, and they found what they were looking for after an hour of searching the shoreline: a long dugout canoe, company property, abandoned by some Halvern crew that had evidently decided the escarpment business was unhealthy and fled downstream ahead of the news.

"Six seats, leaks at both ends," the older scout reported. "Like everything else in this country."

They loaded Dez in the center, wrapped in the tarp, with Fen holding her head. Kessing took a paddle. Ilse took the stern, because Ilse always took the stern, and Marguerite had stopped being surprised by it. Okonjo and the scouts filled the remaining seats. Marguerite sat at the bow with the data case between her knees and a paddle she intended to use mostly for steering clear of floating debris.

Ilse gave them the rules in her flat pilot's voice: stay out of the current seams, watch for strainers—trees pinned underwater that would hold a hull and drown everyone aboard—and if the canoe swamped, get upstream of it and hold on, because a canoe is a floatation device even full of water, and so are you if you don't panic.

"Fen," Dez said weakly, from under the tarp. "If I die, tell the inquiry the leak at the stern was pre-existing."

"Noted for the record," Fen said.

They pushed off.

The crossing took two hours and cost Marguerite several years of her life in installments. The ocean-brown water was deceptively slow on top and murderous underneath; twice the canoe got gripped by an eddy line and spun, and both times Ilse killed the spin with three strokes and a word Marguerite chose not to translate. A floating fence post nearly took out Okonjo's paddle arm. At the worst moment, crossing a current seam above a submerged orchard, the whole canoe lifted and surfed sideways on a boil of displaced water, and for three full seconds they were a crew of nine riding a wave in the middle of a drowned world, and Dez—who should have been unconscious—sat up inside the tarp, looked at the water, and said, "Huh," in the tone of a mechanic diagnosing something interesting.

Then the seam released them, and the ridge was ahead, and the middle station resolved out of the rain haze: mast standing, walls standing, dock standing, and tied at the dock—

A launch. Gray hull, company lines, riding out the flood on a long painter like it belonged there.

Every paddle stopped.

"That's not ours," Fen said unnecessarily.

"It's not Halvern's either," Ilse said, after a long look. "Wrong lines. That's port authority." She paused, and Marguerite heard something in her voice that Marguerite had never heard there before: hope, handled carefully, like it might spill. "That's a rescue patrol. Somebody picked up the broadcast."

The older scout laughed once, sharp, and wiped rain out of his eyes. The younger one began, very quietly, to cry, and paddled harder so no one would notice, and everyone noticed, and no one said anything.

They came alongside the dock in the grey afternoon light, and a woman in a port authority slicker was already there with a line in her hand and questions in her face, and behind her, on the station porch, someone had rigged a tarp and a stove and there was steam rising off something hot.

Marguerite climbed out of the canoe with the data case hugged to her chest and her legs shaking under her, and handed the case up to the patrol officer before she did anything else, before she even fully stepped onto the dock.

"This is Kessing's survey data," she said. "Verified originals. It proves the Halvern concession is built on forged surveys, and it explains why the tributary villages are underwater right now. It needs to go downstream tonight, with a chain-of-custody receipt, signed by you, witnessed by me."

The officer looked at the case. Looked at Marguerite—at the mud, the rope burns, the salt crust of twenty years finally cracking—and took the case with both hands.

"You're the ones from the broadcast," she said.

Behind Marguerite, Dez was being lifted onto a stretcher by people whose job it was, and Okonjo was telling the story already, fast and tumbling, and Ilse stood at the end of the dock looking downstream toward the locks, toward Voss, toward the machinery of consequence grinding its way upriver.

"No," Marguerite said. "We're the delivery crew. The package finally arrived."

The officer frowned. "Arrived where? This is a river station, not—"

But Marguerite was looking past her, at the mast, at the rebuilt antenna Kessing's people had strung, at the small warm light in the station window burning steady against the wall of rain. Somewhere downstream, a patrol boat was closing on the locks. Somewhere in the flood, the Tern was wherever lost boats go. And here, on this ridge above the brown ocean, was a building with a stove and a mast and room in it—for how long, she didn't know yet, and for the first time in her life, she found she wanted to find out.

"Get me a radio," she said. "There's a man at the locks who thinks he still owns me. I want to hear his voice when he learns he doesn't."
