## 33. The Tern's End

Dez knew before they rounded the last bend. He felt it in his chest, the way you feel a tooth go bad—some low, wrong note that had been humming in him since they left the Tern wedged in the drowned forest three days ago. Boats talked to the men who fixed them. The Tern had been talking all night, and what she was saying was goodbye.

"Slow down," he said to Okonjo, who was poling the salvaged skiff through the floodwater with Fen and one of Kessing's people. "Slow down. Let me listen."

Okonjo gave him a look—the look Dez had been getting a lot of lately, the one that said *you're running a fever and we both know it*—but he shipped the pole and let the current carry them.

The rain had eased to a fine gray drizzle. The tributary that had been a river when they came up it was now something else entirely, a brown ocean with treetops growing out of it, and somewhere under all that water were the villages, the roads, the fences, the whole ordinary world the monsoon had decided to eat this year. Dez put his hand flat on the water and let it drag there, reading the current like braille.

There. A snag of current, a stutter where water broke wrong over something hard.

"There," he said again, and pointed.

They found her on the rocks below the old ferry landing, or what had been the old ferry landing before the flood took everything above the third step. The Tern lay on her port side, half out of the water, and she was not the Tern anymore. She was planks. She was a shape. She was the idea of a boat that some patient person had once loved into being, and now the river had loved her back out of existence.

Dez didn't say anything. He couldn't have said anything if he'd tried. Okonjo brought the skiff alongside the rocks as gently as he could, which wasn't very gently, because the water was moving like a freight train with no brakes, and Dez stepped off onto wet stone and stood there with the drizzle coming down on his bare head—he'd lost his hat somewhere back at the tower, along with two pints of blood and most of his good arm—and looked at her.

The hull was stove in amidships where she'd come off the wedge. The seam he'd patched in chapter after chapter of this trip—the one he'd sworn to Marguerite would hold, the one he'd held himself awake over with resin and sailcloth and prayer—had opened like a mouth. Her cabin roof was gone. Her rail was gone. Her name, painted on the stern by Dez's own hand four years ago in a harbor bar with more confidence than skill, was gone too, though he could see the ghost of it, a paler rectangle where the paint had been protected from the sun.

*TERN.*

He sat down on the rock. His legs made the decision without consulting him.

"Dez." Okonjo crouched next to him. "We can't stay long. Water's still rising."

"I know."

"We need anything worth taking?"

"I know," Dez said. "Just—give me a minute."

Okonjo didn't give him a minute. Okonjo gave him ten, standing there in the drizzle with his hand on Dez's shoulder, saying nothing, which was the most generous thing anyone had done for Dez since the stairwell, since the company men came up the steps and Dez had put himself between them and the transmitter and caught a rifle butt across the ribs and a bullet-graze along the scalp for his trouble. His head was singing. It had been singing for two days. Marguerite kept telling him he was concussed and he kept telling her he was fine, and the truth was somewhere in between, closer to her version every hour.

But he could still hear the boat.

People thought that was superstition, the way he talked about engines and hulls, the little rituals he did before a run, the way he wouldn't let anyone whistle aboard. They thought it was a mechanic's eccentricity, a bit of theater. It wasn't. It was just that Dez had spent twenty years listening to machines the way other people listened to music, and he knew that everything built by human hands has a voice, and that voice is made of stress and wear and hope, and if you're quiet enough you can hear when it changes.

The Tern's voice had changed three days ago, wedged in the flooded timber, holed and listing, and Dez had known then what she was telling him: *I got you this far. I can't get you farther.*

Now he sat on the rock and told her he was sorry.

It came out loud, which embarrassed him, but not enough to stop. "I should've doubled the frames," he said. "Back when I bought you off Okafor, I should've stripped you to the keel and started over. I knew the stringers were soft. I knew it. I told myself she'd do for coastal work, short hops, fair weather, and then—" His throat closed. He worked past it. "Then Marguerite signed us onto this run and I told myself she'd hold because I needed her to hold, and that's not how boats work. That's not how anything works. You don't get to want a thing and have the wanting count as maintenance."

Fen had come ashore and was standing a few feet away, awkward, holding a salvage bag. Kessing's other man was already picking through the wreckage with the practical eye of someone who had lost camps before and knew the drill. But Dez kept talking, because the fever and the grief had gotten together somewhere in the last day and formed a committee, and the committee had voted to let him speak.

"You carried us," he said. "You know that? Through the locks, up the reach, through the gorge with the water like that—I still don't know how you took that third drop without breaking your back, and I'll never know now, because you're—" He gestured at the planks. At the shape. "And then the side channel, and the forest, and you let us camp on your deck while we bailed you, and you never complained, not once, not the way engines complain, and I checked you every morning and every night and I thought, *she's tired but she's game,* and I'm sorry. I'm sorry I asked you for the gorge. I'm sorry I asked you for the channel. You deserved a dry season. You deserved to be hauled out and scraped and repainted and have someone sit on your deck drinking beer talking about nothing. That's what I wanted for you. That's what I wanted for all of us, honestly, and none of us got it, and it's nobody's fault except maybe a mining company's, and they're going to pay for it, so."

He stopped. The drizzle ticked on the rocks. Somewhere upstream, the flood muttered against the drowned trees.

Okonjo sat down beside him on the wet stone and didn't say anything, which was right. There wasn't anything to say. Every boatman knew this moment; most of them had stood on a rock like this one looking at a shape like this one, and the words were always the same words, and they never helped, and you said them anyway.

After a while, Fen cleared her throat. Gentle, but a clearing nonetheless.

"Dez," she said. "The crate."

Right. The crate.

The radio crate. The original delivery, sealed in Voss's office with the permits, the thing Marguerite had been hired to haul upriver at the start of all this, back when Dez had thought the worst danger was a leaky seam and a client who smiled too much. Halvern Consolidated's own equipment, packed by their own hands, and now it was the single most incriminating object on the river—serial numbers, manifests, internal shipping codes tying the company to the concession, to the falsified surveys, to the burned camp, to the men who'd scattered Kessing's team into the bush. Ilse had spent an hour at the middle station explaining why they couldn't leave it behind. Evidence, she'd called it. *The paper trail has a paper trail.*

Dez got up. His legs cooperated this time, mostly. He picked his way along the rocks to where the Tern's aft section had come to rest against a fallen tree, and there, jammed under a collapsed bunk frame, lashed down with the double hitch he'd tied himself because he never trusted anyone else's knots, was the crate.

Water had gotten into the boat everywhere else. The crate was dry.

He laughed at that—a short, cracked sound that hurt his ribs. Of course it was dry. He'd sealed it himself, back at the coast, with wax and tape and the particular paranoia of a man who'd once lost a client's spare propeller overboard and never forgiven himself. Whatever else failed on this trip, whatever the river took, the crate was going to arrive intact. That had been the job. That had been the only job, once upon a time, eleven days and a lifetime ago.

"Help me lift," he said.

Okonjo took one end. Fen took the other. Between the three of them they walked it off the wreck and onto the rocks, and Dez set it down in the driest spot he could find and knelt beside it and cut the lashings—not to open it, just to check. The seals were unbroken. Voss's cheerful little stamps stared up at him from the lid, the optimistic logos of the optimists, and Dez thought about spitting on them and decided the crate deserved better than to be spat on, even by proxy.

"That's it?" Fen asked. "That's the delivery?"

"That's the delivery," Dez said. "Concession permits and radio equipment for the Kessing expedition, per contract, one lot, delivered to—" He paused. "Well. Delivered somewhere. Close enough."

"The permits are gone," Okonjo said. "Marguerite had them at the tower. Did they burn?"

Dez looked at him. Then at Fen. Then back at the crate.

"You haven't heard?"

"Heard what?"

Dez sat back on his heels. His head sang. He let it sing.

"Marguerite broadcast them," he said. "During the transmission. Ilse read the permit terms on air—all of them, the concession boundaries, the survey references, the expiry clauses—and then she read the survey numbers the permits were issued against, and then Kessing's verified numbers, side by side, so every station downstream could hear the difference. You can't un-broadcast a thing like that. The permits aren't gone. They're radioactive. Anybody who honors them now is on record honoring them." He tapped the crate lid. "This is the rest of it. The physical trail. Serials match the manifest, manifest matches the concession file, and the concession file matches the surveys that were forged. It's a chain, Okonjo. We're carrying the chain."

Okonjo absorbed that with the expression he got when he was recalculating a position fix—everything behind his eyes quietly replotting. "So the delivery we almost died for," he said slowly, "is the thing that kills them."

"The delivery we almost died for was always the thing that killed them," Dez said. "We just didn't know which direction it pointed."

*

They worked the wreck for another hour, and it was the hardest hour of Dez's life, harder than the stairwell, harder than the gorge, because salvage is just grief with a checklist.

Kessing's man pulled a coil of line, half-usable. Fen found the tool roll wedged in the bow compartment, soaked but sound, and handed it to Dez without a word, because she'd seen him check for it twice. Okonjo recovered the water jug and the emergency flares, which turned out to have flooded anyway, which figured. Dez went through the debris plank by plank, not because there was anything to find but because he couldn't not. It felt like searching a room where someone had died.

Under a shelf of torn decking he found the galley box. Inside, dry as bone: a tin of tea, a jar of honey with about two fingers left in it, and the mug. The blue mug. Marguerite's mug, chipped on the handle, the one she drank from every morning of the run while pretending she wasn't superstitious about it either.

He wrapped it in the tea towel and put it in his jacket, against his chest, where the bruised ribs complained about it, and he decided the complaint could wait its turn.

The engine was gone. He'd known it would be, but knowing didn't help. The flood had taken the whole aft mount and the block with it, and what was left of the lower unit was wrapped around a tree forty meters downstream, visible at intervals when the swell dropped, winking at him like it was sorry. Twenty years of keeping engines alive and he hadn't been able to keep this one alive, and it wasn't his fault, he knew it wasn't his fault, the water had risen six meters in a night and no engine ever built was rated for that—but fault wasn't the currency of grief. Grief ran on a different ledger. Grief just wanted to know why you hadn't tried harder, and it didn't accept answers.

"Dez." Marguerite's voice, from the skiff. She'd come down from the high ground where she and Ilse had been scouting the descent route, and she stood in the bow with the rain running off her chin, looking at the wreck with an expression Dez had never seen on her face. He'd seen her scared, angry, broke, determined, and once—in the gorge, for about four seconds—genuinely uncertain. This was none of those. This was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"We need to move," she said. "Water's coming up another meter by dark, Ilse thinks. After that, these rocks are gone and we are swimming."

"I know."

"Take your minute. Take five minutes. Then we move."

"It's not a minute kind of thing," Dez said. "It's a years kind of thing. But I'll take the five."

She nodded and didn't push, and he loved her for it, and he filed the love away with everything else he'd been filing away since the tower, because there wasn't time to feel things properly yet. Later, he promised himself. In the dry season, if there was a dry season, he'd feel all of it at once, in order, like unpacking crates.

He stood at the wreck a while longer. The drizzle had thickened again, fat drops ticking on the planks, on the rocks, on the back of his neck. Downstream, the brown ocean waited, patient as debt.

"Listen," he said to the Tern, quiet, just for her. "I can't fix you. First time in my life I've said that sentence and meant it. But here's what I can do. I can carry the crate in. I can carry the mug. I can tell the inquiry exactly what you hauled and how far and through what, and I'll make it boring and detailed and undeniable, because that's how you win cases—you bury them in maintenance logs. And when this is over, when the company's in pieces and the escarpment's protected and everybody's safe, I'm going to come back to this rock, and I'm going to build you a marker. Something a boat would like. Not a cross. Boats don't want crosses. Maybe a cleat. A brass cleat, bolted down good, so any pilot who comes up this reach in a flood knows there's a wreck here that earned her keep." He wiped his face, or tried to; the rain made it pointless. "You were a good boat. Better than I built you. That's on me. Rest of it's on the river, and the river doesn't apologize to anybody, so somebody's got to do it for her. Sorry. Sorry. Sorry."

The Tern shifted on the rocks as the swell came through, settling a little deeper, a little more final, like a sleeper turning over.

Dez picked up the crate.

*

They loaded the skiff to the gunwales and pushed off, and Dez took the last look, because someone had to and because he'd earned it. The Tern lay in the rising water with her broken back exposed, gray planks and pale seams, smaller than he remembered her being, though he knew that wasn't true either. Boats never shrink. Only the distance does.

"Hey," Okonjo said softly, poling them out. "The mug. Whose was it? Before Marguerite?"

Dez blinked. "What?"

"The blue mug. She drinks from it every morning. I always figured it was hers from way back."

"It's mine," Dez said. "Bought it at a dockside stall in Corvo Bay, year I got my papers. Left it aboard the Tern the day I sold her my labor and Marguerite my navigation, and she just—started using it. Never asked. Never gave it back." He touched his jacket, where the mug rode against his ribs. "Twenty years I let her steal my mug. You want to know how partnerships work? That's how partnerships work."

Okonjo laughed, and even Fen smiled, and the skiff slid out into the brown flood with the crate lashed amidships and the wreck falling away behind them, and Dez watched until the drizzle took her, and then he watched a while longer than that.

Ahead, upstream, the escarpment stood black through the rain, and somewhere on its flank was a relay tower, a wounded crew, and a woman named Ilse who had a plan for getting them all down off it before the water finished eating the world. Marguerite stood in the bow, scanning the route, measuring distances the way she always did—miles, hours, meters of climb—and Dez saw her hand drift once to her pocket, where the permits used to be, and come away empty, and close anyway, like a fist around a decision.

"Hold on," she called back to them, over the rain. "Ilse says there's a station track below the second bench. If we make it before dark, we sleep under a roof tonight."

"A roof," Dez said. "Any roof."

"And tomorrow?"

Tomorrow. Dez looked at the crate between his boots, at the seals that had survived everything the river could throw at them, and thought about the inquiry waiting downstream, and the trial, and the long slow work of making a company answer for a river.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we deliver."
