## 34. The Rescue

Fen had learned, in the weeks since the inspection that broke the expedition apart, to sleep in shifts and wake at nothing. So it was Fen who heard the engine first—a low diesel note under the rain, wrong for the river. The monsoon didn't have engines. The monsoon didn't come upriver against its own current with a searchlight.

She was on her feet before she'd decided to be, one hand on the rock wall of the overhang where they'd sheltered, the other finding the cold barrel of the flare gun Kessing's people had left her. Below, the flooded tributary ran brown and wide as a lake, and out of it, through the gray curtain of dawn rain, came a light.

"Boat," she said, and her voice cracked on the word because she hadn't used it in hours. "There's a boat coming."

Marguerite was awake instantly—Fen had noticed that about her, the way she surfaced like a diver, all at once, no groggy middle ground. She was beside Fen at the lip of the overhang before Fen finished speaking, squinting into the rain.

"Company?"

"Wrong sound," Fen said. "Halvern runs fast planing hulls. This is heavy. Displacement." She'd spent four years listening to river traffic from the middle station; she knew the voices of engines the way other people knew the voices of friends. "Patrol launch. Port authority pattern."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure enough to spend a flare on it."

Behind them, in the shallow cave where they'd stacked the wounded, Dez stirred. Okonjo was already sitting up, radio crate pulled against his chest like a child he'd been told not to put down. Ilse stood at the far edge of the overhang, motionless, watching the light grow.

"If you're wrong," Ilse said quietly, "we've just told them where we are."

"I'm not wrong."

"You said that about the side-channel too."

"The side-channel kept us alive," Fen said. "It just wasn't comfortable."

Ilse didn't answer that. Fen understood why. The side-channel had cost them the Tern, and no amount of being right made a drowned boat anything but a drowned boat.

Marguerite looked at the light for three more seconds. Then: "Fire it."

Fen braced the flare gun against the wet rock, aimed high and wide of the launch—the last thing she wanted was to put a burning phosphorus star directly over the heads of people who might decide they were targets—and fired. The report was swallowed by rain. The flare went up, stuttered, and bloomed red, and for a moment the whole drowned world turned the color of an open wound: the water, the escarpment's black shoulder above them, the faces turned up around her—Okonjo's young and strained, Dez's gray with fever, Marguerite's set like something carved.

The launch answered with its own light. A white strobe, three flashes, pause, three flashes. Fen let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the day Halvern's men had come up the station path with their polite smiles and their requisition orders.

"That's port authority," she said. "That's the real thing."

* * *

Getting down to the water took an hour that felt like a day. The track below the second bench had washed out in the night's peak, so they went the slow way, hand over hand down the root-laced slope, Dez half-carried between Okonjo and Marguerite, the radio crate slung in a harness of sailcloth across Ilse's back. Fen went first and last both, testing each hold, calling warnings up the line. Twice Dez slipped and both times the rope caught him, and both times he apologized—to the rope, to the slope, to Okonjo, to nobody in particular. Fever did that. Fen had seen it in Kessing, in the first days after the escarpment climb, when Amara had apologized to a compass for dropping it.

The patrol launch stood off from the bank in deeper water, its bow angled into the current, floodlights washing the drowned trees along the shore. A crewman in orange foul-weather gear waded out to meet them, arms out, and Fen watched Marguerite do the arithmetic of trust in real time—the flick of her eyes to the launch's hull number, the registry markings, the two officers visible on deck, none of them carrying anything heavier than a boat hook.

"Port Authority Launch *Meridian Four*," the crewman shouted over the rain. "Responding to broadcast traffic. You the tower party?"

"We're the everything party," Marguerite said.

He looked at them—at Dez leaning on Okonjo, at the crate, at Ilse's face under the hood, at Fen herself, still wearing the same shirt she'd been hiding in the bush in for eleven days—and something in his expression shifted from procedure to recognition. He'd heard the broadcast. They all had. Every station downstream had heard it by now; Fen had listened to the relay repeat twice more during the night, Ilse's voice flat and relentless over the storm, naming names, reading elevation figures that could not be true unless every survey Halvern Consolidated had ever filed was a forgery.

"Get them aboard," the crewman called back. "All of them. Careful with the man—he's septic."

They were. Fen sat with Dez on the deck plates while a medic—there was a medic, an actual medic, with actual antibiotics—started a line in his arm. Dez watched the bag fill and drain with the expression of a man who had spent his whole life fixing things himself and couldn't quite believe someone else was doing it.

"Flood's peaked," the coxswain told Marguerite at the wheel. "We're riding the drop. Twelve hours to the locks if the channel holds."

"The locks," Fen repeated.

The coxswain glanced at her. "That's where we're headed. That's where everyone's headed." He said it the way people say things that are larger than they sound. "Port authority sealed the locks at 0400. Nobody goes downstream without inspection. There's—" he hesitated, then seemed to decide there was no point softening it for people who'd climbed an escarpment through a monsoon, "—there's a launch held at the upper gates. Company vessel, *Charitable Intent*. Registered to Halvern's subsidiary. Its master is refusing boarding."

Fen felt the deck go very steady under her, the way it does when something you've been dreading finally arrives and turns out to have a shape you can look at.

"Who's its master?" Marguerite asked. Her voice was even. Too even. Fen had learned to read that evenness over the past days—it was the sound of Marguerite holding something down.

"Voss," the coxswain said. "Emil Voss. He's asking to speak to whoever made the broadcast."

* * *

They rode the flood down through the drowned country. Fen stayed on deck most of the way because she couldn't help it—because this was her river, the reach she'd patrolled and measured and loved from the middle station, and she needed to see what the water had done to it.

It had done everything. Villages she knew by name were roofless or gone entirely, their houses floated off their posts and jammed in the trees like birds' nests. A church bell hung from a submerged frame, ringing faintly whenever the swell moved it. At the mouth of the third tributary, a herd of cattle stood belly-deep on a levee the size of a tennis court, waiting with terrible patience for the water to make up its mind.

And everywhere, the signs of the concession. Survey stakes with Halvern's orange caps, snapped and floating. A barge mounted with drilling equipment, abandoned at a list, its crane folded against a tree. The concrete footings of an access road that had been poured in the dry season and never finished, now just a row of broken teeth running into the flood.

"This is what it would have been everywhere," Fen said. She didn't realize she'd spoken aloud until Okonjo, beside her, answered.

"The dam would have been worse."

"The dam would have been this," Fen said, "permanently. Forty meters of it. Every village you see drowned, forever, and the surveys said the gorge walls could hold it." She looked up at the escarpment's receding wall, gray in the rain. "Amara proved the rock is fractured all the way through. It was never going to hold. They knew. They filed the numbers anyway because the subsidy schedule paid out on construction milestones, and by the time it failed they planned to be cashed out and gone, and the failure would be blamed on the weather." She heard her own voice shake and stopped.

Okonjo was quiet a moment. "Ilse's brother found that out first. Didn't he? Six years ago."

"Audited the surveys and reported it," Fen said. "And Halvern erased him. Not killed—erased. His findings withdrawn, his name struck from the register, his family notified he'd left the country. Ilse told me some of it on the climb. I think telling it cost her more than the climb did."

Down the deck, Ilse stood at the bow rail with the rain running off her hood, watching the water, and Fen did not say any of this within her hearing. Some stories belong to their owners.

* * *

The locks appeared out of the rain in the late afternoon—first the mast lights, then the concrete shoulders of the upper gates rising sheer out of the flood, then the gantry cranes, and the cluster of vessels rafted in the approach basin like animals sheltering from a fire. Word had traveled faster than the water. Half the river trade on this reach was backed up behind the seal: fuel barges, produce boats, a passenger ferry with children hanging off its rail to stare at the patrol launch as it slid in.

And at the upper gates, alone in the holding pen, sat *Charitable Intent*.

Fen had seen the launch before—from a distance, at the concession dock, the day the inspection arrived. It had looked sleek and friendly then, white hull, company livery, a name chosen by someone with a sense of humor so dry it was practically geological. Now it sat with its lights on and its crew visible at the rail, and two port authority cutters lay athwart its bow and stern like brackets, and it looked exactly like what it was: a trap that had closed on its own spring.

A gangway officer waved *Meridian Four* to the quarantine berth. Before they'd even made fast, a harbor official was aboard—an older woman in a soaked uniform jacket, clipboard in a plastic sleeve, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has been awake for thirty hours and intends to stay awake for thirty more.

"Which of you transmitted from the summit relay?" she asked.

"I did," Ilse said.

The official looked at her for a long moment. "You'll need to make a statement. In writing. And there will be an inquiry panel sitting at the port within the week—you'll be asked to repeat it under oath." She glanced at the others, at the crate, at Dez on the stretcher. "All of you. But first—" she consulted the clipboard, and her voice changed, became careful, "—there's a matter at the gates. The master of the company vessel is requesting contact with the delivery contractor. Marguerite Zhou."

Every head on deck turned. Fen watched Marguerite stand very still, the way she'd stood at the overhang that morning, surfacing all at once into something she'd already known was coming.

"He's been asking since 0400," the official said. "First through the harbormaster, then through his lawyers, then through the harbormaster again. As of noon he's been advised that any communication will be recorded and may be used in proceedings. He says he understands." A pause. "He says he only wants to talk to you. Five minutes. He says you'll want to hear what he has to say before the inquiry does."

"Do I have to?" Okonjo asked—not to the official, to Marguerite. His voice was young and fierce. "You don't owe him five minutes. You don't owe him five seconds."

"No," Marguerite said. To the official, though her eyes stayed on Okonjo a moment longer. "But I'm going to take them anyway."

"Why?" Dez said from the stretcher, thickly, fever-bright. "Margo. Why."

"Because everything he says in those five minutes is evidence," Marguerite said. "Because he thinks he can still talk, which means he doesn't understand yet what's happened to him, and I want to watch his face when it starts arriving." She pulled her jacket straight. "And because I signed papers once without reading them. I'm not going to walk away from the last page."

* * *

Fen insisted on coming. So did Ilse. The official allowed both—one witness each, she said, standard practice—and led them down the quay toward the gates, past the rafted boats and the staring crews, past a knot of journalists who had arrived on the afternoon ferry and were being held back by two constables who looked grateful for the assignment.

The holding pen at the upper gate was a concrete box of churning green water, and *Charitable Intent* sat in the middle of it with its engines off for the first time in Fen's memory. Voss stood at the rail.

Up close, for the first time, Fen saw that he was smaller than his voice. On the satellite calls Marguerite used to take, hunched and silent, Voss had sounded vast—cheerful, patient, inevitable, a voice like a warm room you couldn't leave. In person he was a neat, silvering man in a dry company jacket, and the only remarkable thing about him was how completely unmarked he was. Everyone else on this river was soaked, exhausted, grieving. Emil Voss looked like he'd stepped out of a boardroom an hour ago.

"Marguerite," he said warmly. "Thank God. I've been worried sick—all of you, out in this. And Dr.—Fen, isn't it? The deputy. And you must be the pilot." His eyes touched Ilse and moved on, quickly, the way a hand moves away from a hot stove. Fen noted that. Everyone noted that.

"Five minutes," the official said, and raised a recorder.

"Marguerite, listen." Voss leaned on the rail, confiding, reasonable. "Whatever's happened here, whatever's been said, it's recoverable. That's what I do—I recover things. Situations. Debts." A small smile. "Yours, for instance. Whatever that broadcast contained, whatever got onto whatever tower—you were under duress, obviously. Coerced by persons with their own agendas. I can make that the record. Debt cleared, your crew compensated, no inquiry testimony required from you at all. You deliver nothing, you say nothing, and Monday you're free. Clean slate. You've earned a clean slate, Marguerite. You're one of the few people on this river who actually has."

Fen watched Marguerite listen. She watched her do it the way she did everything—completely, giving the moment her whole attention, as if it were a stretch of water she was reading for the line through it.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" Marguerite said at last. "The garage. When you first called me. You said you invest in people. And I thought—that's a strange thing for a debt collector to say. But it wasn't strange, was it? It was accurate. That's exactly what you do. You find people who are drowning, and you buy them, and you call it optimism." She shook her head slowly. "I measured my whole life in miles because miles end. Miles run out. You can always get to the far side of a distance. You built a business out of people who couldn't see that debts aren't like that. Debts don't end. They just change whose ledger they're on."

"Marguerite—"

"Mr. Voss." The official's voice, mild and final. "The recording is complete. Thank you for your cooperation."

Something crossed Voss's face then—the first true thing Fen had seen on it. Not fear. Calculation failing. The look of a man reaching for a lever and finding the machine unplugged.

"You should know," he said, quieter now, and the warmth was gone entirely, leaving something old and cold underneath, "that my associates are not all standing at this rail. Some of them are already downstream. Some of them are very good at disappearing, and very good at—"

"At recovering things," Marguerite said. "Yeah. We've met."

They walked away. Behind them, at the rail, Voss began talking to his lawyer in a low rapid voice, and Fen did not look back, because looking back was a luxury of people who weren't running, and for the first time in eleven days, nobody on this quay was running.

* * *

By full dark, the news had worked through the basin the way dye works through water. The port authority announced formal fraud referrals. Two of Halvern's subsidiary directors resigned by statement, which everyone understood meant flight. The 'optimists' themselves—the cheerful men and women who had invested in people all up and down this coast—began, with remarkable speed, to become former associates, unnamed acquaintances, persons unknown. Fen watched it happen from the quarantine berth, watching names peel away from the enterprise like paint off a hull that had finally been hauled out of the water.

Dez slept under proper blankets for the first time in a week, the antibiotic line dripping, his fever breaking near midnight. Okonjo sat with the radio crate between his boots and wouldn't let anyone else touch it, not even the evidence officers who came for it at dusk—they photographed it, tagged it, and, at Marguerite's flat insistence, let it stay aboard overnight. "It was a delivery," she told them. "Let us finish delivering it."

Near midnight, Fen found Ilse at the stern rail, watching the lock gates, where the floodwater thundered white through the sluices.

"They'll open the gates tomorrow," Ilse said. "Drop the level, start letting traffic through. First boats down will be the inquiry witnesses." She was quiet a moment. "Six years I've been coming up this river under a name that isn't mine. Tomorrow I go down it under my own."

"Does it feel different?"

Ilse considered. "Ask me at the bottom."

Fen laughed—her first laugh in eleven days, short and surprised out of her—and beside them, unnoticed until now, Marguerite stood at the rail with a chart case under her arm, looking down at the churning gates, at the water falling away toward the port and the trial and the long slow work of consequence.

"Tomorrow," Fen said, "we deliver."

Marguerite nodded once, eyes still on the gates.

"And then," she said, "for the first time in twenty years, nobody owns my morning."
