## 35. Debts and Ledgers

The letter came on paper, which was how Marguerite knew it was official. Nobody sent paper anymore unless they wanted you to keep it.

She read it twice at the table in the middle station's back room, where the rain drummed the corrugated roof and Dez's radio crate sat open in the corner like a reliquary. The letterhead was a court registry downstream, four hundred kilometers and a world away. *In re: Halvern Consolidated (in receivership). Notice to creditors and debtors.* Her name was in a list of names. Her number was in a column of numbers. And beside it, in the flat language of law: *obligations held by or through Halvern Consolidated or its subsidiaries are void ab initio pending inquiry findings.*

Void. As if it had never existed. Twenty years of interest, compounding like floodwater, erased with a phrase.

She set the letter down and found her hands were shaking, which annoyed her more than anything else about the morning.

"You're smiling," Okonjo said from the doorway. He had a coil of antenna wire over one shoulder and rain in his hair. "You never smile at paperwork."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your mouth is doing something."

"It's doing nothing." She folded the letter along its creases, then unfolded it, then folded it again. "It says my debt is dead."

Okonjo leaned against the doorframe and let that sit for a moment. He was good at letting things sit; she'd taught him that on the river, when silence was the difference between hearing a channel marker and hitting one. "And?"

"And what?"

"And what does that feel like?" He wasn't being clever. He was twenty-three and sincere as a plumb line, and he genuinely wanted to know.

Marguerite considered lying, decided it wasn't worth the effort, and said, "Like taking off a pack after a long carry. You keep reaching for the strap that isn't there."

* * *

The trouble with a dead debt, she discovered over the following week, was that it had been load-bearing.

She'd built her whole life around it the way the Tern had been built around its cracked keel—everything shaped by the thing underneath. Every contract accepted because the payment cleared a month of interest. Every route chosen for fuel economy rather than sense. Every relationship kept shallow enough to abandon. Voss hadn't just owned her money; he'd owned her shape. The optimists had invested in people, they'd said, and they had, the way you invest in a mule—with an eye to how much work you could get out of it before it dropped.

Now the mule was free and had no idea what it was for.

She took inventory the way she always did when the ground moved: distances, supplies, obligations. The Tern was gone—shattered on the rocks below the escarpment, and she still couldn't think about that without Dez's face coming with it. The salvage money from the insurance claim would be a rounding error. Her reputation, thanks to the broadcast, was either ruined or made, depending on who you asked; two charter companies had already sent inquiries, both wanting the story more than the storm chaser. There was work out there. There was always work. Boats needed delivering, instruments needed calibrating, rivers needed running.

So why did every road out of the middle station feel like a corridor?

She caught herself measuring the distance to the coast. Old habit. Two hundred and sixty kilometers to the delta locks, three days' run in fair water. From there, anywhere. That was the whole point of anywhere—it was always available, always one departure away, and therefore never anything you had to actually be in.

Ilse found her doing the arithmetic on the veranda rail with a grease pencil, numbers half washed away by spray off the rising river.

"Coast again?" Ilse said.

"Force of habit."

"Your habits have good posture." Ilse sat on the rail, balancing the way she balanced everything, casually defying gravity. Below them, the port authority's patrol launch idled past on its morning circuit, and one of the crew lifted a hand. Word of the broadcast had traveled faster than any boat. Half the river knew their names now. It was, Marguerite thought, like being famous for surviving a fire—you kept getting introduced as the person from the fire.

"Kessing wants to see you," Ilse said. "This afternoon. She's got paperwork."

"Everyone's got paperwork."

"Not like this." Ilse slid off the rail. "Wear something that isn't salt-stiffened. She notices."

* * *

Dr. Amara Kessing received her in the station's old survey office, which was slowly becoming Kessing's office whether anyone had voted on it or not. The fever had burned down to embers but not out; she worked wrapped in a blanket with a hot plate under a kettle, and her hands still trembled when she thought nobody was watching. But her eyes were the same eyes that had stared down a company from the top of an escarpment, and they went through Marguerite like a sounding line.

"Sit," Kessing said. "Tea?"

"Whatever's fastest."

"Nothing here is fast. That's the point of the place." She poured two cups anyway. On the desk between them lay a folder, a pen, and—Marguerite noted with a lurch—the original delivery manifest, the one for the permits and the radio crate she'd carried upriver into all of it. Someone had annotated it in red.

"You never delivered," Kessing said. Not an accusation. An observation, the way she'd observe a barometer falling.

"The permits burned with everything else. Or drowned. I've stopped keeping track." Marguerite turned her cup. "Does it matter? Halvern's gone. The concession's dead. Those papers are confetti now."

"They're evidence. Everything is evidence for the next ten years—that's what an inquiry is, a machine for turning paper into consequences." Kessing pushed the folder aside. "But that's not why I asked you here. Fen tells me you're leaving."

Marguerite didn't confirm it, which was itself a confirmation. "Fen talks a lot for someone who spent a week hiding in a wall."

"Fen earned the right to talk." Kessing sipped her tea. "So do I. I'm rebuilding the middle station. Properly—staff, instruments, a hydrology program the port authority has already agreed to fund, mostly because they're terrified of being on the wrong side of another broadcast. I need someone to run it."

The words landed in the room and just stayed there. Marguerite looked at the desk, the window, the rain. Anywhere but the folder.

"I'm a chaser," she said finally. "I chase. That's the job. Storms come, I go toward them, I take measurements, I leave before the water takes me. It works because I'm not attached to anything the water can reach."

"That's a description of your method, not your life."

"It's been both for twenty years."

"And how's that working?" Kessing's voice wasn't cruel. It was clinical, which was worse. "You had a debt. Now you don't. You had a boat. Now you don't. You had a crew—" she paused delicately, "—except they're all still here. Did you notice that? Dez is rebuilding the generator shed. Okonjo is studying for his pilot's papers. Ilse is filing testimony. Your crew, Marguerite, has stopped moving, and you're the only one still pacing."

Marguerite set down the cup harder than she meant to. "I don't pace."

"You measure," Kessing said. "Fen told me. Distances, not years. Everything in kilometers to the next departure." She opened the folder and turned it around. Inside was a contract—one page, plain terms, no seals, no optimistic investors. *Station keeper. Middle River Station. Salary, housing, instrument budget. Term: renewable annually.*

"A year," Marguerite said. "That's a long time."

"It's twelve months. It renews or it doesn't. Nobody's chaining you to the dock." Kessing leaned back. "Here's what I know about you, Zhou, from six weeks of watching you run my rescue without asking anyone's permission. You are the best field meteorologist I've ever seen operate without institutional backing, and I've seen most of them. You read weather the way Ilse reads water. And you are so frightened of staying anywhere that you'd rather die solvent than live settled. I can't fix the second thing. But I can offer you a reason to try."

Marguerite stared at the contract. The rain filled the silence.

"Why me?" she said. "You could hire anyone. Half the coast owes you their good opinion now."

"Because the station needs someone who'll stay when the storm comes instead of running ahead of it. Chasers run. Keepers hold." Kessing smiled, thin and fever-bright. "I need a keeper who understands chasing. That's a short list, and you're on it alone."

* * *

She didn't answer that day. She didn't answer the next day either. She told herself she was thinking, and she was, but mostly she was walking the station's perimeter at odd hours, counting exits.

There were four. The main track down to the dock. The goat path up toward the escarpment, still slick from the floods. The service road south, washed out at the culvert but passable on foot. And the river itself, always the river, wide and brown and going somewhere else.

Dez found her at the culvert on the third evening, elbow-deep in the guts of the backup generator, and didn't say anything for a long time. Dez was good at that—mechanic's patience, the willingness to let a machine talk first. When she finally spoke, it was to the generator.

"Heard Kessing offered you the post."

"News travels."

"Okonjo told Ilse, Ilse told Fen, Fen told everyone. We're a small population." Dez wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands. "You going to take it?"

"I don't know."

"That's a new one for you." Dez sat back on her heels. "Usually you know. Usually you've known since before anyone asked. Debt's due, route's clear, storm's building—Marguerite knows, Marguerite moves." She squinted at the sky, where the clouds were doing their slow monsoon bookkeeping. "You know what I did when the Tern broke up? I apologized to her. Out loud. Okonjo heard me and has never once let it go."

"I know. He told me. Twice."

"She carried us," Dez said simply. "Boats do that. People too, if you let them." She stood, joints cracking, and slung the rag over her shoulder. "I'm not telling you to stay. I'm telling you we're here, and the generator shed needs a second pair of hands that isn't mine, and if you leave I'll understand, and if you stay I'll understand that too. That's the whole speech. It's shorter than Okonjo's version."

"What's his version?"

"Forty minutes and possibly tears." Dez walked backward toward the shed. "Take the contract, skipper. Worst case, you leave in a year and it's just another departure. Best case—" she shrugged, and the shrug said everything the speech hadn't.

* * *

That night she dug out the old logbooks.

She kept every logbook, always had—a crate of them, one per season, going back twenty years. She'd packed them from garages to boats to rented rooms the way other people packed photographs. She'd never understood why until now, turning pages by lamplight while the rain came down. They weren't records of storms. They were records of departures. Page after page of distances: *ran 140 km to the cut, 90 km to the fuel barge, 210 km ahead of the cell.* Twenty years of motion, and nowhere in any of it a single entry that assumed tomorrow would happen in the same place as today.

Except.

Near the bottom of the third-season book, in her own younger handwriting, there was a page where the columns fell apart. A week moored at some estuary town waiting on parts, and instead of distances she'd written descriptions: the name of the woman who ran the chandlery, the taste of the mangoes, a joke Dez had told that still worked. Seven days of staying, recorded in a hand that grew looser and happier with each entry. Then the parts arrived, and the columns resumed, and she'd left, and she'd never written about a place like that again.

She'd forgotten it. Or she'd buried it. Same thing.

She closed the book and sat with the lamp hissing and the rain on the roof, and she understood that the debt had never really been Voss's invention. Voss had just found the shape of her fear and bought it. The running predated him. The running was hers. Which meant the stopping had to be hers too, and no court registry could void it for her.

* * *

On the fifth morning she went to Kessing's office and put the contract on the desk between them, unsigned.

"One condition," Marguerite said.

Kessing raised an eyebrow. "Keepers don't usually negotiate up. They negotiate for more rain gauges."

"I want the station to run a chase program. Storm intercepts, field telemetry—the kind of data only someone crazy enough to drive at a cell can get. Funded. With a boat." She heard her own voice steady out on the word *boat*, and kept going. "You get your hydrology and your early-warning network. I get to keep the part of the work that's mine. And whoever runs this station doesn't have to choose between holding and chasing. They can do both."

Kessing was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed—a real laugh, rusty from the fever—and pulled the contract back across the desk. "Amendment two," she said, uncapping the pen. "You're going to be insufferable about this."

"Already am."

Kessing wrote fast, her hand steadier than it had been all month. Marguerite watched the words appear—*field operations annex, vessel allocation, seasonal intercept schedule*—and felt something shift in her chest that she refused to name until it was done.

Then Kessing held out the pen.

Marguerite took it. For a moment she saw the whole ledger of her life spread out behind the page: every garage, every departure, every kilometer run from a thing instead of toward it. Voss's cheerful voice. The optimists investing in people. The Tern going to pieces on the rocks with all of them aboard and none of them lost, which was the only miracle she'd ever witnessed and the only one she needed.

She signed.

"There," Kessing said. "Now you live here."

"I have a one-year term."

"You have a home with an exit clause. Everyone does. That's called being alive." Kessing filed the contract and, in the same motion, handed her a key on a loop of wire. "Station keeper's quarters. Second floor. The roof leaks on the north side; Dez knows."

Marguerite turned the key over in her palm. It was warm from Kessing's pocket and heavier than any key had a right to be.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine silver drift, and the river ran high and glittering past the dock, going somewhere else, as it always did. She stood on the veranda a long time, watching it. Then she went inside, climbed the stairs, and unlocked her own door.

She was hanging her jacket on the hook—her hook, in her room—when Okonjo's voice came up the stairwell, breathless, too loud, the voice he used when something had happened.

"Skipper! Skipper, you need to come down—there's a boat coming up the channel, and Ilse says—" he caught himself on the railing, grinning and scared at once, "—Ilse says she recognizes it. Says it belongs to the inquiry. They're here for her. About her brother."
