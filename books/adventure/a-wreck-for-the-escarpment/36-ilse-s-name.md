## 36. Ilse's Name

The registry office in Port Kessing smelled of wet paper and diesel, the way every government building on the river did—like a place that had flooded once and never fully dried out. Ilse stood at the counter with a form in front of her and her hand shaking so badly she had to set it down flat to stop it.

The clerk, a young woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair, waited with the patience of someone paid by the hour.

"Take your time," she said.

Ilse looked at the blank line where the name went. *Name of applicant.* Six years she'd been Ilse Varga, a name bought from a forger in a lock town two hundred miles downstream, a name that fit like a borrowed coat—close enough to keep the rain off, wrong enough that she always felt the seams. Before that there had been another name, a real one, attached to a brother and a houseboat and an audit report nobody wanted published. The company had taken the name along with everything else. That was how erasure worked: not with violence, mostly, but with paperwork. A flagged identity file. A travel restriction. A bank account frozen pending review, the review pending indefinitely. You didn't die; you just stopped being legible, and a person who isn't legible can't testify, can't own, can't be owed anything—including an apology.

She picked up the pen.

*Name of applicant:* she wrote *Maren Oduya-Halvorsen*, and the letters came out steadier than she expected, because it turned out the name hadn't gone anywhere. It had just been waiting under the other one, the way a river waits under ice.

"That's not the name you've been using," the clerk said, not unkindly, checking the temporary inquiry credentials Fen had arranged for them all.

"No," Ilse said. "It's the one I'm taking back."

* * *

It had taken three weeks for the inquiry to open its doors to people like her. Three weeks since the broadcast, since Halvern Consolidated's shares had gone into freefall and its board had resigned in a staggered, dignified procession that the port papers called *a reckoning* and Okonjo called *rats leaving a burning barge*. Three weeks since Voss was marched off the patrol launch at the locks in handcuffs, still smiling, still trying to make eye contact with Marguerite as though they were old friends who'd simply had a disagreement about terms.

The inquiry had set up in the old customs house, and every morning a queue formed outside it: surveyors who'd been pressured to sign falsified reports, lock operators who'd been paid to look away, families from the drowned villages upstream who wanted their displacement entered into the record. And people like Ilse—the erased ones, the ones whose names had been flagged or forged or filed out of existence because they'd asked questions about elevation figures.

Fen had come down from the middle station to help coordinate testimony. He moved stiffly now, his wound healing into a permanent reminder, and he'd appointed himself Ilse's unofficial advocate, which meant he'd spent two days arguing with an inquiry officer about whether a posthumous submission required the original audit documents or certified copies.

"The originals are in Dr. Kessing's field camp," Fen had said, "at the base of the escarpment, behind a waterfall, guarded by two very tired scientists."

"Certified copies will suffice," the officer had finally conceded, "if the certification is done by a licensed surveyor."

Kessing herself had certified them, sitting in the customs house annex with a fever finally broken and a fountain pen she'd carried through the entire disaster, signing each page with the care of a woman inscribing scripture. When she finished, she'd looked up at Ilse across the table.

"Your brother found this first," she said. "Six years ago. I want that in the record exactly as it is—not as corroboration, not as background. As origin."

Ilse hadn't trusted herself to answer. She'd nodded, which Kessing seemed to understand.

Now, at the registry counter, after the name, came the second form. *Declaration of Posthumous Submission.* It was new paperwork, drafted hastily when the inquiry realized how many dead people had left findings in drawers and footlockers and burned camps. There was a line for the deceased's name, a line for the relationship of the submitter, and a long blank space for a summary of the material.

*Deceased:* Tomas Oduya-Halvorsen.

*Relationship:* Sister.

Ilse stared at the word *sister*. She hadn't written it anywhere official in six years. On the Tern, in the gorge, in the drowned forest around the termite mound fire, she had been the stranger with the forged papers and the too-good knowledge of the escarpment reach. She had earned her way into the crew through piloting and truth-telling, both delivered late and in pieces. But *sister*—that was older than any of it. That was the name under the name.

"Summary of material," the clerk prompted gently.

Ilse began to write. Her brother had been a junior auditor contracted to verify Halvern's escarpment surveys ahead of the concession application. He had found that the bedrock elevations were fabricated—that the surveys assumed a geology that didn't exist, one that would allow a modest diversion weir instead of the catastrophic damming the actual rock would require. He had filed a dissent. Eleven days later his identity was flagged, his contract voided for cause, his access revoked. Four months after that he was dead—a drowning, officially, in a reach of river he had piloted since he was twelve. Officially. The inquiry officer had already told her, in the careful voice reserved for such things, that the death would be reopened.

She wrote all of it in the summary space, small and even, until she ran out of room and asked for another sheet.

* * *

Marguerite was waiting outside on the customs house steps, sitting in the sun with her forearms on her knees, watching the river traffic the way she always did—reading it, measuring it, though Ilse had noticed over the past weeks that the measuring had changed quality. Less odometer, more weather. She wasn't counting distance anymore. She was learning to count duration.

"You're staring," Marguerite said without turning her head.

"I'm deciding whether to thank you or pretend I don't need to."

"Both options are fine. One of them's faster."

Ilse sat down beside her. Below, the harbor was busy in the way it got before the dry season fully settled—barges loading, patrol boats repainted, a crane swinging crates onto the rebuilt station dock upstream. Somewhere in that traffic was the wreck buoy they'd placed over the Tern, orange and patient, marking the spot where the boat had ended and everything else had begun.

"They need a witness for the declaration," Ilse said. "Someone who can attest to my identity. To who I am." She laughed, short and dry. "Which is strange, because six weeks ago I was lying to you about who I am in a lock office."

"You weren't lying," Marguerite said. "You were telling the truth in installments. I've read contracts like that."

"I need you to swear, in front of an officer, that you know me. That the woman filing as Maren Oduya-Halvorsen is the same woman who ran the Tern through the gorge." Ilse turned the paper over in her hands. "I keep thinking about what that means. For six years nobody could attest to me. My name was forged, my history was sealed, and if I'd drowned in the gorge, I'd have been buried as Jane Doe, river salvage, no next of kin. Tomas would have stayed a footnote. The whole thing—" she gestured vaguely at the customs house, the inquiry, the scandal rolling through the port like weather, "—none of it happens unless somebody stands up and says *I know her.*"

"So ask me," Marguerite said.

"It's a lot to ask. You barely know me. You know the version of me that showed up at your locks with a forged license and a grudge."

"I know the version of you that held the Tern together in floodwater when Dez was bleeding and Okonjo was half-drowned and I was standing there doing arithmetic instead of steering." Marguerite finally looked at her. "That's not nothing. That's actually most of what I know about anybody."

Inside, the hearing room was smaller than Ilse expected—a table, an officer, a recording device, a window with a view of the harbor. The inquiry officer was a heavyset man named Brannic who had the unhurried manner of someone who had spent thirty years processing cargo manifests and was not going to be impressed by drama.

"State your name for the record."

"Maren Oduya-Halvorsen. Filing previously as Ilse Varga."

"And you're submitting audit findings on behalf of Tomas Oduya-Halvorsen, deceased."

"Yes."

Brannic glanced at the second form. "You've listed a witness to identity. Ms.—" he checked his sheet, "—Zhou. She'll need to affirm that she knows you and can speak to your character in relation to this submission." He looked up. "Ms. Zhou, do you understand that a false attestation before this inquiry carries penalties?"

"I understand," Marguerite said. She stood, and Ilse watched her—watched the storm chaser who had spent twenty years outrunning everything stand perfectly still in a government room and put her hand on the back of Ilse's chair.

"I met Ilse Varga at the river locks," Marguerite said, "when she hired on as pilot for a delivery run to the Kessing expedition. She lied to me about her name. Not about her skill, not about the river, and eventually not about anything else. She ran our boat through the gorge in flood conditions and got us all out alive. She told us the truth about Halvern when telling it cost her the chance to disappear quietly, which—I learned later—was the option she'd planned for. She climbed the escarpment in monsoon rain carrying her brother's findings in a waterproof bag against her chest, and she transmitted them from the relay tower while men were shooting at the stairwell below her." Marguerite paused. "I know her. Whatever name she files under, that's who I'm attesting to."

Brannic made a note. "For the record, Ms. Zhou, the witness has referred to the declarant as 'Ilse' throughout her statement."

"That's what I call her," Marguerite said. "Names take. You can't help it."

Something loosened in Ilse's chest that she hadn't known was tight. She had braced for this moment to feel like a legal procedure—forms, affirmations, a stamp. Instead it felt like being introduced properly at last, the way you introduce someone at a funeral or a wedding: full name, full weight, spoken aloud by someone who means it.

Brannic stamped the declaration. "The submission is registered. Your brother's findings will be entered as primary evidence in the escarpment survey matter, with attribution. The reopened death investigation will contact you as next of kin." He slid the papers back across the table. "Congratulations, Ms. Oduya-Halvorsen. You exist again."

Outside, the light had gone gold. Marguerite walked beside her down the customs house steps and didn't say anything for half a block, which Ilse had learned was her way of giving a thing room.

"Maren," Marguerite said at last, trying it out. Then, honestly: "It's going to take me a while."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because it's true. Names take." She stopped at the corner where the road bent toward the harbor. "But here's what I keep thinking. You didn't get the name back today. You got the *witnesses* back. The name was always yours. What the company took was everyone who could say so."

Ilse looked at the harbor, at the water that had officially killed her brother and unofficially given her a crew. "Tomas used to say a river doesn't care what you call it. It just keeps being itself underneath whatever name you put on the charts."

"He sounds like he'd have liked the Tern."

"He'd have loved the Tern. He'd have hated how we treated her." Ilse smiled, and it hurt, and it was good. "He'd have loved the crew more."

They walked on toward the docks, where Fen was waiting with news—he could see him from here, standing too straight, holding a telegram in a way that meant it couldn't wait—and behind them, in the registry drawer, the declaration sat filed under a name six years dormant and one day old.

Fen lifted the telegram as they approached. "From the inquiry," he said. "Voss's counsel is moving to suppress the audit evidence. They're arguing chain of custody." His jaw tightened. "They're arguing the documents were handled by a woman with a forged identity."

Ilse took the telegram and read it twice, and felt the old fear rise—and then felt something else rise faster, steady as floodwater, because the name on her new registration card was real, and the witness who'd sworn to it was standing right beside her.

"Then we'd better go tell them," she said, "exactly who handled them."
