## 37. Dry Season

The river dropped a meter in October and kept going.

Okonjo noticed it first at the mooring post — a rusted ring that had spent the whole monsoon underwater now sat above the waterline, furred with green, and every morning after that it climbed a little higher out of the current like something surfacing for air. By November you could see the old flood line on the station pilings, a brown tidemark two meters up, and below it the wood was pale and clean where the water had scoured it. The river was giving back what it had taken. It did this every year, Ilse said. It just never gave back everything.

Okonjo had learned to read the water the way other people read faces. He'd come aboard the *Tern* barely able to hold a compass, a kid who thought navigation meant following the dotted line on a chart, and Marguerite had put him on the wheel in the gorge because there was no one else and because she believed — or pretended to believe — that he wouldn't kill them all. He hadn't killed them all. And somewhere between the drowned villages and the escarpment climb, between bailing brown water by lamplight and holding the stairwell while the tower transmission ran, the dotted lines on charts had turned into actual rivers in his head: braided, shifting, alive, each one different from its own chart.

Now he stood on the rebuilt deck of the middle river station with his examiner's clipboard under his arm and watched the morning fog burn off the channel, and he could name every sandbar out there without looking at the chart pinned inside the wheelhouse door. The one off the eastern point had migrated forty meters downstream since the flood peak. The channel past the termite-mound islands had deepened — you could tell by the color, tea-dark over the old bed, where the shallows went milky. The snag that had nearly taken the *Tern*'s rudder in September had rotted apart into three pieces, and two of them were beached on the point like bones.

"You're doing it again," Dez said behind him.

"Doing what."

"Reading the river instead of drinking your coffee. Coffee goes cold. River isn't going anywhere." Dez lowered himself onto the rail beside him with the careful, deliberate economy of a man whose body still wasn't entirely his own. The wound at his shoulder had healed into a puckered star of scar tissue; the deeper damage was the leg, the one that had been crushed against the tower stairwell when Halvern's men came up through the rain, and some mornings it worked and some mornings it argued. This morning it seemed to have accepted terms. "Examiner coming today?"

"Eleven hundred. Launch from the port authority."

Dez grunted. "You'll pass."

"You don't know that."

"I know you've been running this reach since August without killing anyone, which is more than I can say for half the licensed pilots on this river." Dez sipped his coffee. "I also know you've checked the fuel lines four times since dawn, which is a mechanic's vice, not a navigator's, but I'm choosing to take it as a compliment to my profession."

Okonjo laughed. It still surprised him sometimes, how easy laughing had gotten. On the *Tern*, in the bad weeks, laughter had been rationed like diesel — small measured amounts, deployed only when the alternative was panic. Now it came loose and unbidden, and nobody flinched when it did.

Below them, the station spread along the bank in its new skin: the main house rebuilt on the old footings, roofed in fresh corrugated iron that threw back the sun like a mirror; the instrument yard fenced and graveled; the dock extended twenty meters into the channel with a crane arm for hauling survey gear. Kessing had drawn the plans herself on graph paper taped to the wall of what was now the mess, and she'd annotated them in three colors of ink with notes like *this wall must survive a boat hitting it* and *generator shed DOWNWIND, I am not living through that again.* Fen had managed the build, riding the contractors with the same flat ferocity she'd once used on field teams, and the result was a station that looked less like a government outpost than like a ship that had decided to stop moving — everything braced, everything doubled, nothing trusted to luck.

It had cost most of the inquiry's first grant money and all of Fen's patience, but it was done. Mostly. The radio mast still leaned two degrees east, and Dez refused to straighten it until the ground settled, and everyone else had learned not to mention it.

*

The examiner arrived at quarter past eleven, which for a port authority launch was practically early.

His name was Oduya, a broad, gray-templed man with reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and the manner of someone who had examined four hundred pilots and been impressed by none of them. He came up the dock ramp, shook Okonjo's hand with a grip like a mooring cleat, looked around the station with professional disinterest, and said, "Where's your vessel?"

"The *Tern* is—" Okonjo stopped. Everyone at the station had developed the same stutter at the *Tern*'s name, a half-second hitch before the tense sorted itself out. "We've got the station launch. Sixteen-foot, outboard, sound. She's no *Tern*."

"No one's vessel is the vessel they loved," Oduya said, in the tone of a man reciting something from the regulations, and Okonjo realized with a start that it probably *was* in the regulations, or close enough. "Chart table?"

They did the classroom portion in the mess, Oduya working through rules of the road, buoyage, distress procedure, while Okonjo answered and tried not to notice Marguerite pretending to do paperwork at the far end of the room, her pen not moving. Then they took the launch out onto the channel, and Oduya became a different animal: quiet, watchful, feeding him situations like cards from a deck. *Fog closing from the west, cargo barge ahead, your call.* *Man overboard off the starboard bow, current setting south.* *Your engine dies here — right here — what do you do?*

What Okonjo found, running each scenario, was that he didn't have to think. His hands knew. Somewhere in the months of runs up and down the reach — ferrying survey crews, hauling timber, carrying mail to the upstream villages that were slowly, tentatively reoccupying themselves now that Halvern's money had dried up and blown away — the river had written itself into him. When Oduya said *engine dies,* Okonjo's hand was already on the anchor line, already reading the set of the current, already calling the drift in his head: *we'll swing stern-first onto the gravel bar, soft grounding, no danger, drop here.*

He said all of it aloud. Oduya wrote things down without expression.

On the run back, the old man finally spoke at length. "You trained under Brightwater," he said. Not a question. Everyone on this stretch of river knew whose station this was; the inquiry had made her name unavoidable, and the trial downstream had made it famous.

"Yes, sir. And Ilse Vantol. And a mechanic named Dez who taught me engines by making me fix the ones I broke."

"Ilse Vantol." Oduya's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The one from the broadcast. The name she filed under." He was quiet for a moment, watching the channel. "You understand what she did, taking that name back. Half the pilots on this river are running on papers that aren't theirs. Names bought, borrowed, inherited from dead cousins. She made it possible to imagine handing them back." He glanced sideways at Okonjo. "Which makes yours worth having. A clean license, earned clean, on a river full of ghosts. Don't lose it."

"I won't, sir."

"Hm." They tied up at the dock. Oduya signed the clipboard, tore off the carbon copy, and handed it over — a flimsy pink slip with a stamp and a number, the least impressive object Okonjo had ever been given and the one he would keep longest. "Provisional, six months, then full papers if you keep your nose clean. Congratulations, Pilot." He said the word deliberately, testing its weight, and Okonjo felt it land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

Marguerite was waiting at the top of the ramp, arms crossed, expression arranged into studied indifference. "Well?"

"Well *what*?" Okonjo held out the pink slip.

She read it for a long time. Longer than the document required. Then she handed it back and said, gruffly, "About time," and walked away toward the instrument yard, and Okonjo saw — because he'd learned to read faces too, hers especially — that her eyes were bright, and that she'd gone to be alone about it, because that was how Marguerite did feeling: privately, briefly, and then never mentioned again.

Dez appeared at his elbow. "She cried, didn't she."

"She absolutely did not cry."

"Noted for the record. No crying occurred." Dez grinned and clapped him on the good shoulder. "Pilot. Look at you. I remember when you couldn't tie a cleat hitch without narrating it."

"I still narrate it."

"Yes, but now it's *for style.*"

*

That evening the whole station ended up on the dock, which happened more and more as the dry season settled in — the evenings grew long and gold and too beautiful to spend indoors, and the midges drove everyone out of the mess anyway, so they gathered at the rail with their plates and their cups and watched the light go down the channel.

Kessing sat in the canvas chair that was officially hers and unofficially fought over, her fever long broken but her stamina still a rumor, going through data printouts even now, because Amara Kessing resting was a theoretical concept like frictionless surfaces. Fen stood at the rail beside Ilse, and the two of them had become, over the autumn, a unit — the survivor and the avenger, or maybe just two people who understood exactly what the other had lost, talking in low voices about the escarpment reserve boundaries with the ease of people planning a garden.

Ilse looked different in the dry season. Okonjo couldn't have said how, precisely, except that the coiled watchfulness had loosened by a degree or two. She still scanned the channel when any launch passed. She still slept light. But she'd planted things — actual plants, in actual beds, tomatoes and a row of something green and stubborn along the south wall of the generator shed — and Okonjo figured a woman planting tomatoes was a woman who had decided, provisionally, that next year existed.

"So," Dez said, around a mouthful of rice, "pilot's papers. That means wages. Real ones. Port authority scale."

"It means provisional wages," Okonjo said. "Port authority scale minus humility."

"And what does a man do with real wages, on this river, in this economy? I'll tell you what. He buys a boat."

The silence that followed was the specific, careful silence of five people who had all been thinking about the same thing and none of them saying it.

It was Marguerite who broke it, eventually, turning her cup in her hands. "There's a hull," she said. "Down at the locks. Thirty-footer, steel, ex-survey launch. The authority impounded her off a smuggler in September and they've been trying to give her away ever since, because nobody wants the paperwork." She said it lightly, the way she said everything that mattered. "Needs work. Engine's seized. Deck's soft in places."

"How soft?" Dez asked, and Okonjo heard it — the mechanic's pulse quickening under the joke, the hunger to rebuild something.

"Soft enough to keep you busy through the dry season. Which I understand you're going to need, since you refuse to sit still and Fen has threatened to make you inventory the storeroom again."

"Fen can have my inventory when she pries it from my cold hands."

"There's money," Kessing said quietly, not looking up from her printouts. "From the inquiry fund. Station operations budget covers a support vessel. I'd have to justify it." She did look up then, and her eyes moved around the circle — Marguerite, Dez, Okonjo, Ilse, Fen — with the clinical precision she applied to everything, and something almost warm at the bottom of it. "I don't think justification will be difficult. A station on this reach needs a launch. And I'd rather it were crewed by people who've already proven they'll bring it home."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Downriver, a fish jumped, a silver slap against the darkening water.

"We'd need a name," Fen said.

And there it was — the thing underneath the thing, the reason the silence had lasted as long as it had. Okonjo looked at Marguerite, because it was her call, had always been her call; she'd owned the *Tern* through debt and storm and fire, and whatever came next would carry whatever she decided the old name should mean.

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment, looking down the channel to where the last light lay on the water like spilled brass.

"We'll see," she said. "Boat first. Name when she earns it."

But she smiled when she said it, small and private, and Okonjo understood that the name was already chosen, had probably been chosen in August, standing on the rocks where the wreck came ashore. Some names you didn't retire. You just waited for something worthy of carrying them.

*

Later, after the dishes and the generator check, Okonjo climbed to the wheelhouse of the station — the little glassed-in lookout on the main house roof that Fen had insisted on and everyone else had mocked until the first night they used it to spot a distressed canoe upstream. He liked it up there. From the wheelhouse you could see the whole reach: the channel winding north toward the locks, the dark line of the tributary where the drowned villages stood empty and remembering, and far off, faint in the last light, the escarpment itself — a blue-gray wall on the horizon, the place where everything had changed, wearing its new protection like a garment it hadn't asked for.

He spread the pink slip on the chart table and read it again. *Provisional Pilot's Certificate. Holder: T. Okonjo.* His full name, typed, official, belonging to him the way Ilse's name belonged to her now — claimed, documented, real.

Six years ago he'd been a harbor kid running messages along the docks, measuring his life in trips, in fares, in days until the next boat left. He'd met Marguerite Zhou and thought she was the most complete person he'd ever seen, a woman who needed nothing fixed because she never stayed anywhere long enough for anything to break. He'd wanted to be that. He'd spent two years learning distance from her — miles, nautical miles, the clean arithmetic of leaving.

It had taken a wreck to teach him the other lesson: that she'd never been complete at all, just fast. That staying was a skill, harder than any of it — harder than the gorge, harder than the climb — and that the people on this dock had learned it together, the way you learn anything worth knowing, badly and at cost and too late to be anything but grateful.

Below him, the station lights came on one by one. Dez's workshop glowed yellow, the man himself visible through the window, hunched over the station launch's carburetor because apparently celebrating meant fixing something. In the mess, Kessing and Fen argued amiably about budget lines. In the tomato bed, a lantern bobbed — Ilse, checking her rows in the dark, guarding them from something, because vigilance was a habit and habits were the last thing to heal.

And at the edge of the instrument yard, silhouetted against the dusk, Marguerite stood alone at the base of the anemometer mast, looking up at the spinning cups, and did not come inside for a long time.

Okonjo watched her and felt the future open out ahead of him like a channel at flood stage — wide, unmapped, going somewhere. Provisional papers. A hull at the locks waiting for hands. A dry season's work, and a wet season after that, and seasons beyond it, stacked up like charts in a drawer.

For the first time in his life, he wasn't counting the days until departure.

He was wondering what the river would do tomorrow — and planning, calmly, happily, to be there to see it.

Out on the horizon, where the escarpment stood blue against the last of the light, the air had begun to move. Okonjo felt it on his face before the instruments caught it: a breath from the northeast, warm and restless, the season's first hint of weather gathering somewhere over the plateau.

The anemometer cups spun faster. Below, Marguerite tilted her head, listening to it, and stood very still — like a woman hearing a voice she'd spent her whole life following, and deciding, at last, what to say back.
