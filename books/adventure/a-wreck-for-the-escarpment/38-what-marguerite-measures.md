## 38. What Marguerite Measures

The anemometer came in a crate the size of a coffin, and Dez insisted on opening it like one.

"Respect the dead," he said, prying at the lid with his good hand. His arm was still in a sling from the tower—three months now, the bone knitting slow in the humidity—and he'd appointed himself foreman of every job on the station that required standing around and supervising.

"It's not dead," I said. "It's new."

"That's what makes it worse." He got the lid up and looked inside at the packing straw like it might apologize to him. "New instruments are how this whole thing started."

He wasn't wrong, but I didn't give him the satisfaction. The old anemometer—the one that had ridden with me through eleven years of storm seasons, that had been bolted to the Tern's mast, then to the truck cab, then to a rented room in three different port towns—had gone into the river behind the falls along with everything else we couldn't carry. Somewhere down there it was still spinning, or it wasn't, and either way it belonged to the water now.

This one was Kessing's requisition: a proper station-grade unit, three-cup rotor, heated bearings for the wet season, a data logger that would hold two years of readings before anyone had to think about it. The kind of instrument you buy when you intend to be somewhere long enough to fill its memory.

I'd signed the requisition myself. That was the part that kept catching, like a splinter under the skin. My signature, on paper that assumed a future.

* * *

The mast went up on the point above the station, where the escarpment's shadow ended and the river valley opened out to the south. Okonjo surveyed it with the transit he'd bought with his first pilot's wages, shooting angles and calling numbers while Ilse held the tape measure taut and pretended not to enjoy being ordered around.

"Two degrees off true," Okonjo announced, squinting through the scope.

"Off true which way?"

"East."

"Then it's facing the weather," I said. "That's not error, that's intent."

Ilse snorted. She'd been doing more of that lately—snorting, laughing, saying things in her own voice instead of the flat careful voice she'd arrived with. Her name was back, the real one, the one that had been erased: Anneliese Voss-Reiter. No relation to *that* Voss, she'd had to explain to the inquiry clerk four times, though the coincidence had made the lawyers' eyes light up anyway. The paperwork restoring her identity had come through in the dry season's first week, an official stamp on a piece of her that Halvern had spent six years pretending didn't exist.

She'd framed the certificate. Actually framed it, and hung it in the station's common room between the river chart and the photograph of the Tern's crew taken the day before we left the locks—five faces, none of us knowing yet.

"Level," Okonjo called. "Bolt it."

Dez supervised. I bolted. The rotor spun up in the afternoon breeze with a sound I knew better than my own pulse, and the data logger blinked awake on its little green screen, and for a moment I just stood there with my hand on the mast feeling the vibration of moving air come up through the metal into my palm.

Wind speed, wind direction, gust interval. Numbers that used to mean *how fast can we run, how far can we get, how much distance is left between me and the thing behind me.*

I'd measured my whole life in miles. Miles of road out of towns I owed money in. Miles of river between me and a contract. Miles of open water between me and any season that asked me to stay through it. Even my debts had been denominated in distance—if I ran charters at this rate, so many miles per month, I could outrun the interest by winter. It never worked. Interest doesn't get tired. Distance does.

Kessing found me up there at dusk, climbing the path with her stick, breathing hard but refusing help the way she refused everything. The fever had burned down to embers months ago, but it had left her thinner, grayer, with a tremor in her left hand she hid by keeping it in her pocket.

"It's reading," she said, nodding at the logger.

"Fourteen knots, gusting twenty-two. South-southeast."

"Good siting." She stood beside me, watching the cups turn against the last orange light. "You know what I did for thirty years before the escarpment? I measured things. Cores, strata, flow rates. You spend enough years measuring, you start to believe the measurements are the world. That if you can put a number on something, you understand it." She paused. "Halvern taught me better. They had beautiful numbers. Every figure in those falsified surveys was precise to three decimal places, and every one of them was a lie. Precision isn't truth, Marguerite. It's just precision."

"What's the difference?"

"Truth takes longer." She looked out at the river, brown and slow and carrying half the escarpment's red soil toward the delta. "You have to watch a thing through a whole cycle. A year, minimum. Better several. You have to see what it does when it's bored, when it's frightened, when nothing's happening. Anyone can read a moment. Staying is how you read a place."

I didn't say anything. She didn't need me to. She'd said her piece and she climbed back down the path, slower going than coming, and left me alone with the anemometer turning in the dark.

* * *

The calendar was Okonjo's idea, though he'd deny it.

He'd earned his pilot's papers in the second month of the dry season—passed the practical on the middle reach with an examiner who reportedly said, and I quote Dez, "the boy runs the gorge like he grew up in it," which, given the gorge, was nearly a eulogy. To celebrate, he'd nailed a proper wall calendar to the station kitchen, the kind with a different landscape photo every month, all of them of places none of us had ever been.

"You're supposed to mark things on it," he told me, tapping it. "Birthdays. Deliveries. When the fuel barge comes."

"I know how calendars work."

"Do you?" He said it without malice, which was somehow worse. "Because I've watched you. Someone asks when you'll be somewhere, you say 'three days' run' or 'a week's steam.' Never dates. Distances. Like if you name a date, it can be missed."

The kid had been reading too many of Fen's psychology monographs. But he wasn't wrong, and he knew he wasn't wrong, and I knew he knew, and there was nothing to do about any of it except pick up the pencil he was holding out to me.

I marked the fuel barge. Then the quarterly supply run. Then, because it felt reckless in a way that had nothing to do with danger, I marked the first of the wet season—the date the models said the monsoon front would make landfall, give or take four days. October 14th. A circle around a number on a grid, meaning: *I will be here.*

My hand shook slightly doing it. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear. The body doesn't always distinguish between falling and landing.

* * *

Voss's trial began on a Tuesday, downstream at the provincial capital, in a courthouse with air conditioning and a docket so crowded they'd split the proceedings into parts. Part one: the concession fraud. Part two: the deaths—the survey team members who hadn't made it out of the bush, the station workers at the drowned villages, and, listed among the counts in a way that made Ilse go very quiet whenever it came up, her brother, whose file had been reopened after six years of official shrugging.

Everyone assumed I'd go. I was the delivery contractor; I was the one who'd broadcast the data; I was, in the prosecution's phrase, "a material witness to the chain of custody." The subpoena arrived on the mail launch in a stiff envelope, and I sat with it on the dock for a long time, watching the water, before I took it up to the station.

Fen met me in the common room. Fen had stayed on as Kessing's deputy at the rebuilt station, running the science program with the same clipped efficiency she'd used hiding in a derelict pump house with a bullet graze and a radio she didn't dare use. She read the subpoena over my shoulder.

"You don't have to attend in person," she said. "Deposition's an option. Recorded statement, cross-examined remotely. Kessing already arranged it with the prosecutor's office—she pulled the same string for herself. Her health, officially." A dry pause. "Unofficially, neither of you wants to spend six weeks in that city listening to lawyers say the word 'concession' eight hundred times."

"Is that what you'd tell me to do? Take the deposition?"

Fen set the envelope on the table and considered me with the look she used on suspect data—not hostile, just unwilling to let anything pass unexamined.

"I'd tell you that trials are for establishing facts, and the facts here were established in a stairwell on a relay tower by a mechanic with a bad arm and a navigator who held the door. Whatever happens in that courtroom is bookkeeping." She straightened the envelope's corner, a small compulsive gesture. "But I'd also tell you that avoidance isn't the same as indifference, and if you're declining because you want to decline, fine, and if you're declining because going would mean sitting still in one place long enough to feel something, then maybe ask yourself which one this is."

I hated her a little, in the way you hate people who are right in a language you haven't learned yet.

"I'll do the deposition," I said.

"Good." She turned to go, then stopped. "For what it's worth—I've read your statement eleven times. The part where you describe choosing to broadcast. You wrote 'I stopped calculating.' Do you know how rare that sentence is in testimony? Everyone else in these files calculated until the end. Voss calculated. The optimists calculated. You're the only witness who describes the exact moment the arithmetic failed." She almost smiled. "The court will find that inconvenient. Juries love it."

* * *

I recorded the deposition in the station office on a Thursday morning, into a recorder the prosecutor's office mailed upstream, with Fen acting as custodian and Dez standing guard outside the door for no reason either of us acknowledged. Two hours and forty minutes. I described the permits, the seals, the ledger page from the burned camp, the call where Voss reminded me the permits expired in eleven days. I described the eye of the monsoon and the relay tower and the sound the transmission made going out on every channel at once—a sound I have never successfully explained to anyone who wasn't there, like the air itself clearing its throat.

At the end, the remote examiner asked his final question: whether, knowing everything I knew now, I would do it again.

I thought about the honest answer versus the true answer. They weren't the same. The honest answer involved the debt, the crew, the years of running, the fact that I hadn't chosen the truth so much as finally run out of directions to flee in. The true answer was simpler and less flattering and more mine:

"Yes," I said. "Sooner."

* * *

The trial opened without us. We got the coverage in fragments—Okonjo rigging the station's antenna to pull a news frequency out of the static, the whole crew gathering in the common room evenings to listen like families once gathered around wirelesses in wars. The prosecutor laid out the forged surveys. Halvern's own retired chief surveyor, granted immunity, confirmed them from the stand in a voice like a man setting down something heavy he'd carried too far. The optimists—the cheerful investors, the ones who'd shaken my hand in that bright office and talked about believing in people—scattered into their own legal representation, each insisting they'd been deceived by the others.

Voss's lawyer argued, variously, that the surveys were preliminary estimates rather than representations, that my deposition was the product of coercion by persons with ideological motives, and that the deaths at the escarpment, while regrettable, could not be causally attributed to his client, who had merely enforced contractual obligations.

"He's good," Ilse admitted, on the third evening, listening to the argument with her arms crossed and her face unreadable. "That's the worst part. He believes it. Or he's made himself believe it, which comes to the same thing. That's how it works. Nobody thinks they're the villain. They think they're the adult in the room."

"And you?" Dez asked gently. "When you were forging papers and hunting proof for six years. Did you think you were the adult in the room?"

"No." Ilse's mouth twitched. "I thought I was the fire."

Nobody laughed, exactly, but the room changed shape around the words, the way rooms do when something true gets said in them.

* * *

October came up on the calendar faster than I expected, which is the entire point of calendars—they ambush you with time.

I'd filled in more squares by then. The fuel barge, twice. Fen's birthday, which we celebrated with a cake Dez baked that tasted like sweetened drywall and was eaten completely anyway. The anniversary of the tower, which nobody marked out loud but everyone glanced at across the table, the way you glance at a scar. The date the inquiry's final report was due. Small administrative futures, stacked up like sandbags.

And underneath them, in the margin of the October page, in pencil so light you'd miss it unless you were looking, I wrote a line I didn't show anyone:

*Miles tell you how far you've run. Years tell you whether you've arrived.*

It was the kind of sentence that would have embarrassed me a year ago. It still embarrassed me, a little. But embarrassment, I was learning, was just the feeling of becoming someone your old self wouldn't recognize, and I'd decided I could live with that particular ache. It was better than the other one—the odometer ache, the one that counted every mile twice, once going and once in regret.

The anemometer logged its ten-thousandth hour on the point above the station. The logger's screen ticked over without ceremony. Wind from the south-southeast, fourteen knots, gusting twenty-two. The same numbers as the day we raised it, or near enough. A coincidence, probably. Or the valley's way of introducing itself properly, one full cycle later, the way Kessing said you had to let a place speak.

I was up there checking the bearings when I saw it.

South, past the point, past the wide brown water and the drowned villages' rooftops turned into islands, past everything the flood had rearranged and the dry season had begun to reveal—a cloud line building on the horizon. Too early. Weeks too early, by every model Fen had posted in the office, by every date I'd penciled into the calendar with my own cautious hand.

It sat there on the edge of the world, tall and blue-black and patient, and the wind on the point shifted a degree, and the anemometer's cups spun a little faster, and I understood that the river was about to test whether I meant any of this—whether the calendar was real, whether the staying was real, whether the woman who'd installed the instrument intended to be there to read what it recorded.

I stood on the point a long time, watching the storm decide.

Then I climbed down to tell the others the season was starting early, and that we had work to do.
