## 39. A Wreck for the Escarpment

The inquiry's findings came out on a Tuesday, in a document two hundred and forty pages long, and Ilse read every word of it standing at the middle station's window while the dry season wind pushed dust against the glass like a hand trying to get in.

She had expected to feel something when she reached the section with her brother's name in it. *Auditor Joren Vasik, deceased, findings posthumously verified.* Six years she had carried that name like a stone sewn into her coat lining, and now it sat on a public record in plain type, attached to the truth he had died trying to file. She waited for the relief, or the grief, or whatever was supposed to come.

What came instead was a strange, flat clarity. The stone was still there. It had just been set down where other people could see it too.

*Halvern Consolidated knowingly falsified geotechnical surveys of the Kessing Escarpment between 2019 and 2023,* the summary read. *The proposed concession is hydrologically unworkable without catastrophic impoundment of the upper river. The company's internal audit division identified these falsifications no later than March 2022. Auditor Joren Vasik raised formal objections. Mr. Vasik died under circumstances the inquiry has referred to the criminal division.*

*Under circumstances.* Even now, even here, the language flinched away from what it meant. But it named him. It put his objections into the record with his name attached, and somewhere a clerk would file it, and someday someone researching the river would find it and know that a man had looked at the numbers and refused to lie about them.

Ilse closed the document. Outside, Okonjo was repainting the station's mooring posts a cheerful institutional yellow, and Dez—walking again, favoring the leg that had taken a bullet on the tower stairs but walking—was arguing with him about whether the second coat was necessary. Marguerite stood at the anemometer mast with a wrench in her hand, doing something precise and unnecessary to an instrument that did not need it, which was how Marguerite prayed.

Ilse went down and told them.

"Protected," Dez said, when she'd finished. "Like a park."

"Like a watershed," Ilse said. "No concession. No dam. No company men with clipboards telling villages their land is now somebody's asset." She heard her own voice go careful on that last part, because three of the drowned villages they'd passed on the tributary had taken Halvern money before the flood took everything else, and protection didn't un-take it. The inquiry had a reparations annex. Annexes were slow. She knew better than to promise anyone speed.

Okonjo set down his brush. "So it's over."

"It's published," Ilse said. "Which is different." She had learned the difference the hard way, in six years of knowing things nobody would print. "But it doesn't unpublish. That's more than I had last year."

Marguerite climbed down from the mast and wiped her hands on a rag, looking at nothing in particular, which was how she looked when she was deciding something. "There's one thing left," she said.

* * *

They went downriver two days later, the four of them plus Fen, who had invited himself with the particular stubbornness of a man who had been shot at once already and considered himself owed a say in any further violence-adjacent activity. The port authority lent them a patrol launch for the trip—a gray, utilitarian thing with a radio that worked and a hull that didn't leak, both of which felt like luxuries Ilse kept waiting to be billed for.

The wreck site was four hours downstream of the gorge mouth, on the stretch of river where the current spread out and forgot its anger. They found it by Dez's reckoning and Okonjo's charts and Marguerite's memory, which between the three of them triangulated to a patch of brown-green water indistinguishable from every other patch, except that Ilse felt it before anyone marked it on paper—the way you feel a grave in a field that isn't fenced.

The monsoon had done what monsoons do. Of the *Tern*, there was nothing visible. No planks, no mast stub, no debris line. The river had taken the boat apart and distributed it across forty kilometers of bank and bottom, and by now the pieces were furniture: driftwood, maybe, or mulch, or part of some sandbar that would erode next season and move the story along.

"She's all through here," Dez said quietly. He stood at the launch's rail with his cap off, which Ilse had never seen him do on open water. Superstition said bareheaded on the river invited lightning; superstition also said you showed respect to a drowned vessel, and apparently the second rule outranked the first. "Everywhere and nowhere. That's not a bad way to end up, for a boat."

"She wasn't a good boat," Okonjo said. Then, immediately, as if the sentence had escaped custody: "I mean—she leaked from day one. The bilge pump ran more than the engine. I used to log her fuel and water in the same column because they moved in the same direction."

"That's not an insult," Dez said. "That's a eulogy."

Fen laughed, short and surprised, and then covered it with a cough, because Fen was still learning how to be a person who hadn't spent nine weeks hiding in a derelict station eating canned peaches. Ilse watched him do it and thought: this too is what the boat bought. Not just the data delivered, the tower held, the names broadcast. This. A man laughing on the water instead of flinching at engine noise.

Marguerite had brought the buoy.

It was not, strictly speaking, a buoy. It was a navigation float salvaged from the station's stores, painted white, with a stenciled number that belonged to some other piece of equipment entirely. Over the number, Marguerite had painted—in Dez's workshop paint, in letters that wobbled slightly because Marguerite's hands were steady on instruments and not on brushes—one word:

**TERN**

Below it, smaller: *served 11 years, sank doing the right thing.*

Dez had argued for a longer inscription. Okonjo had argued for coordinates. Marguerite had overruled them both with the economy of a woman who had spent twenty years learning exactly how much weight a sentence could carry before it sank.

They weighted the float with a chain and a scrap of ballast iron from the patrol launch's own stores—borrowed, logged, returned, because Ilse had learned the hard way what happened to people who borrowed things from institutions without paperwork—and lowered it over the side at the spot where Dez said the hull had gone down, or close enough. The water took it with barely a ripple. It settled upright, riding the current's long swell, white against the brown.

For a while nobody spoke. The engine idled. Somewhere upstream a fish hawk screamed and got no answer.

Ilse found she was thinking about her brother, which was not unusual, but thinking about him *here*, which was. Joren had never seen this river. He had audited it from a desk in a city with air conditioning, working from figures other men had invented, and the closest he ever came to the escarpment was a helicopter survey he'd requested and been denied. He had died for a place he never touched. And now his findings were bolted into the law of the watershed, and his sister was standing on a patrol launch watching a painted float bob in the current, and the distance between those two facts was the whole shape of what she'd spent six years trying to cross.

"You okay?" Marguerite asked, low, beside her. Marguerite had a talent for asking questions she already knew the answer to, which Ilse had decided was a form of kindness rather than an annoyance.

"I'm here," Ilse said. "That's most of it."

Marguerite nodded like that was a complete sentence, which, between the two of them, it was.

* * *

They anchored for the noon meal, because Dez insisted the dead deserved a toast and the living deserved rice, and the two requirements could be met simultaneously. Someone had brought beer—Fen, it turned out, who had rowed six kilometers to the trading post to buy it and told no one, which was the most Fen thing Ilse had yet witnessed. They drank warm lager from the bottle and ate rice with tinned fish and chili sauce, and the talk went where talk goes at such times: sideways, gently, around the thing itself.

"I applied for my pilot's papers," Okonjo said, apropos of nothing, staring at his rice. "Exams are in the spring. Dez says I'll fail the oral section because I talk too fast when I'm nervous."

"Because you talk too fast always," Dez said. "Nervous just makes it worse."

"I don't talk fast."

"You're talking fast right now."

"I'm *explaining* fast. There's a difference."

Ilse let it wash over her. A year ago she had been a woman with a forged identity moving through the world like smoke, trusting no one, planning exits from rooms she'd just entered. Now she was sitting on a government launch eating government rations with a mechanic, a navigator-in-training, a scientist's deputy, and a debt-ridden storm chaser who had once pointed a gun at her over a burned ledger page and now passed her the chili sauce without being asked. If someone had described this scene to her a year ago she'd have assumed it was a trap with unusually good catering.

"Your turn," Marguerite said.

"For what?"

"Whatever you were going to say and then decided not to. You do the thing where your jaw moves first."

Ilse looked at the water. The buoy was visible from the anchorage, a small white point holding its position against the current, which was the entire job of a buoy and harder than it sounded.

"I filed the paperwork," she said. "To reclaim my name. It went through last week." She turned the bottle in her hands. "I'm Ilse Vasik again. Legally. On paper. Everywhere it counts." She had rehearsed saying it plainly and found that plainly was harder than any speech. "It turns out I was never very good at being nobody. I just didn't have a choice for a while."

Nobody made a ceremony of it. Dez raised his bottle an inch. Okonjo said, "Vasik," like he was testing the fit, and then, "It suits you better," and Fen said nothing but wrote something in the little notebook he carried everywhere now, and Ilse chose not to ask what.

Marguerite just looked at her for a moment with that instrument-reading attention of hers, and then said, "Good," and that was enough. That was, Ilse understood, the highest grade Marguerite Brightwater awarded anything: *good*, delivered flatly, meaning *this is correct and I have measured it.*

* * *

They stayed through the afternoon, because leaving felt like a kind of lie. The light went long and gold across the water, the way it only did in the dry season, when the river ran clear enough to show you its bones—sandbars, snags, the dark shapes of submerged trees that would be death in flood and scenery now. Ilse sat on the rail with her boots hooked in the lifeline and watched the buoy hold its station and thought about the difference between a wreck and a loss.

A wreck was a fact. Boats wrecked; rivers kept the receipts. But a loss required someone to carry it, and carrying was optional, and she had spent six years carrying hers like luggage on a journey with no destination. Setting it down didn't mean the brother came back or the company got forgiven or the language of the inquiry stopped flinching. It just meant her hands were free. There was a station upriver that needed running. There was a watershed that needed watching. There was a dry season, and after it a wet one, and after that another, and for the first time in her adult life Ilse Vasik was not planning an exit from any of it.

"We should head back before dark," Fen said eventually. "The patrol boys want their launch logged in by eighteen hundred, and I've seen what happens to people who annoy the port authority."

"They shoot at you?" Dez asked.

"They *fine* you," Fen said gravely. "It's worse. There's paperwork."

They weighed anchor. As the launch swung its nose upriver, Marguerite lingered at the stern rail, and Ilse drifted over beside her, and for a while they watched the buoy shrink behind them, white on brown, holding.

"You know what I keep thinking," Marguerite said. "That boat was the worst thing I owned and the best thing I ever did. Everything that mattered happened on her deck. Every decision. Every person I—" She stopped, recalibrated, the way she did. "I spent twenty years measuring in miles. Miles to the next job, miles ahead of Voss, miles between me and anywhere I'd have to stay. And that boat was just miles with a hull around it." She nodded at the shrinking white point. "But it turns out the miles were going somewhere. Took me thirty years to notice."

"And now?"

"Now I keep a calendar." Marguerite said it like a confession, and smiled like it wasn't. "Kessing thinks I'm reformed. Dez thinks I've had a stroke. Okonjo thinks it's a phase." She shrugged. "Maybe it's a phase. Maybe next season I'll get restless and point myself at the horizon. But I don't think so. I think—" She looked upriver, toward the escarpment, invisible from here but present anyway, the way big things are. "I think I finally picked a direction worth staying in."

The launch thumped gently through the current. Behind them the buoy held its ground against the whole moving weight of the river, and lost, and held, and lost, and held—which Ilse supposed was the entire job, and enough.

Ahead, far up the valley, the sky over the escarpment had gone the particular bruised violet that meant the season's first build-up was stacking itself against the high country. Heat lightning flickered inside it, silent at this distance, patient.

Dez noticed it too. He squinted at the horizon, then at Ilse, and grinned the grin of a man whose leg ached and whose boat was dead and who had never been happier.

"Early this year," he said. "Storm season's coming early."

Ilse looked at the violet wall building over the escarpment, and at the station waiting upriver with its new anemometer and its yellow mooring posts and its people, and felt the old animal quickening in her chest—not dread, not anymore, but its honest descendant: attention.

"Good," she said. "Let it come. We'll be watching."
