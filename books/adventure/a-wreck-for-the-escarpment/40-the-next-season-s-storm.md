## 40. The Next Season's Storm

The anemometer came down off the escarpment road on the back of a mule named Bishop, who had opinions about switchbacks and expressed them by stopping at every third one until someone scratched his ears. Marguerite walked beside him the whole way up from the landing, because she had learned in a year of running the middle station that instruments were like people—they arrived better if you talked to them.

"You're going to love it here," she told the crate. "Terrible view. Constant wind. You'll never be bored."

Bishop snorted.

The station sat on the shelf of rock where the river bent around the escarpment's shoulder, three buildings and a mast and a rain gauge that Fen had rebuilt from salvage with the patience of a man who had once hidden in a wall cavity for nine days and now considered all other problems decorative. The main building still bore the scorch mark from the old generator fire—Kessing refused to let anyone paint over it. "Evidence," she said. "Of what we came back from." So they worked around it, and the black streak became as much a fixture of the place as the mast or the flagpole Okonjo had insisted on, though no flag flew from it yet. He was waiting for the right one. He said he'd know it when he saw it.

Marguerite bolted the anemometer to the mast head herself, forty feet up, harness creaking, the whole escarpment face sliding past below her whenever she leaned out. A year ago she would have climbed that fast, half-afraid, already thinking about the descent before her hands finished the ascent. Now she took her time. She checked each bolt twice. She let the wind push her around and studied how it moved—the way it came off the plateau in long even sheets, then broke apart against the cliff edge into gusts that tumbled like cats.

From up there she could see the whole reach of the river they had come up in blood and floodwater: the drowned side-channel where the Tern had died, marked now by a white buoy that Ilse rowed out to repaint every month whether it needed it or not; the bend where Voss's launch had opened fire; the falls behind which Kessing's field camp had waited out the war. The port authority had put a navigation light on the wreck site last dry season. At night it blinked green, steady as a pulse.

She tightened the last bolt, patted the mast, and said, "There. Now we'll know things."

That was the difference, she thought, climbing down. For twenty years she had measured everything in miles. Distance to the next job, distance to the horizon, distance between herself and whatever was gaining on her. An odometer soul. Now she measured in knowing. Wind speed. Rainfall totals. River stage at dawn, noon, dusk. The station existed to know things about this one stretch of water and sky, and knowing required staying, and staying—she had discovered, slowly, the way you discover a bruise only after the adrenaline fades—did not actually kill you.

* * *

The barometer started falling on a Tuesday.

Fen noticed first, because Fen noticed everything first; he read the instrument shelter the way other men read letters from home. "Thirty point one and dropping fast," he said at breakfast, not looking up from his porridge. "Pressure gradient says something big is organizing offshore."

"How big?" Dez asked. Dez ate standing up, always, a habit from engine rooms, and he still favored his left arm in cold weather—the tower stairwell had cost him some tendon, and the surgeon downstream had done good work but not perfect work. He ran the station's power plant now, a solar array and a diesel backup he had coaxed into reliability through what he called "aggressive friendship."

"Big," Fen said.

Okonjo was already at the window, looking east, where the sky over the plateau had gone a strange bruised yellow-green that Marguerite knew from twenty years of chasing and had never once gotten used to. "First of the season," Okonfo said—he still stumbled on his own excitement sometimes, mixing words, and nobody corrected him anymore because correcting him made him sulk for hours. "First of the season, and it's coming straight up the valley. It'll follow the river. They always follow the river."

"Then we should prepare for a guest," Ilse said, and went to check the mooring lines on the station skiff.

They spent two days getting ready, and Marguerite found she loved the preparation more than she had ever loved the chase. There was a rightness to it. Lashing the skiff under its tarp. Sandbagging the door of the instrument shelter. Running the radio checks downstream—middle station to lower station to port authority, the chain of voices that their broadcast had helped build, the network that now covered the whole river so that no expedition, no village, no lone boat would ever again go silent without someone noticing. When she keyed the mic and heard the port authority answer on the first call, crisp and awake, she felt something in her chest that she refused, on principle, to name.

Kessing spent the preparation days doing what Kessing always did: working. She was older now, or at least she moved like it, the fever and the months in the bush having taken a toll that surfaced in stiff mornings and a cane she used with defiant elegance. But her mind ran like the river itself. She had the escarpment survey re-done properly—honest instruments, honest figures—and the protected status had brought funding, and funding had brought a small permanent science team, which meant Kessing now supervised four earnest graduates who adored her and were slightly terrified of her, which Marguerite considered the correct ratio.

On the second evening, Kessing came out to the mast where Marguerite was checking the guy wires and stood squinting at the eastern sky.

"It'll make landfall tomorrow night," Kessing said. "Maybe midnight. Maybe later. Storms are punctual about arriving and dishonest about everything else."

"I know."

"You've chased hundreds of these."

"Hundreds." Marguerite tested a turnbuckle. "Chasing's different. Chasing, you're always leaving somewhere. You're always ahead of it or behind it, and either way you're moving. Standing still while one comes at you—" She shrugged. "New experience."

Kessing watched her for a moment. "You could go downstream. The lower station would take you in for the blow. Nobody would think less of you."

"I know that too." Marguerite looked at the yellow-green sky, at the river running high and brown and patient below them. "But I'm not going anywhere."

Kessing smiled—one of her rare ones, quick as a fish surfacing—and said, "No. I didn't think you were," and went inside to argue with her graduates about data loggers.

* * *

The storm made landfall at 11:40 at night, and it did not arrive so much as announce itself.

They gathered in the main building's common room, which had been built with one entire wall of reinforced glass facing the river—a extravagance Fen had argued against and lost, and which everyone else now silently blessed him for losing. Outside, the world was going to pieces in the most magnificent way. The wind hit the escarpment face and stood up, literally stood up, columns of spray torn off the river and flung a hundred feet into the dark. Rain came not in drops but in freight. Lightning walked along the plateau rim like something pacing a cage.

Inside: lamplight, tea, the hum of instruments.

And that was the thing Marguerite kept noticing—the hum. The station was alive with quiet sound. The anemometer she had bolted up herself ticking away at the top of the mast, its signal scrolling across Fen's console. The barograph scratching its needle across the drum. The radio receiver murmuring with traffic from every station on the river, all of them awake, all of them reporting, the whole watershed talking to itself through the storm. Dez had rigged the console speakers so you could hear the data feed as soft tones, wind speed rising and falling like breathing.

"Forty meters per second," Fen reported, calm as a man reading a menu. "Gusting fifty-two. Pressure's down to twenty-eight-six and still falling."

"That's a strong one," Dez said.

"That's a monster," Okonjo said happily. He had his notebook out. He was sketching the lightning patterns over the escarpment, because Okonjo documented everything now—it was his way of making sure nothing that happened to them ever happened unwitnessed again.

Ilse sat by the glass with her tea untouched, watching the river. She had cut her hair short over the winter, and without the long curtain of it her face looked different—younger, more open, a face that belonged to somebody whose name was real. Her brother's audit findings had been entered into the inquiry record in the spring, posthumously credited, his name read aloud in a government hearing room six years too late. Ilse had attended. She had worn a gray dress and sat in the front row and not cried until afterward, outside, where only Marguerite saw.

"What are you thinking?" Marguerite asked her now, settling into the chair beside her.

"I'm thinking my brother would have loved this room." Ilse nodded at the humming console, the glass wall, the storm throwing itself against the escarpment beyond. "He liked weather. He liked knowing things. He would have sat right here and been insufferable about pressure gradients." She glanced over. "You're not afraid."

It wasn't quite a question, but Marguerite answered it anyway. "I keep waiting to be. Twenty years, I was always afraid—that was the fuel, basically. Fear with a boat attached." She turned her cup in her hands. "But look at us. Look at this place. Everything that matters is inside these walls or tied down outside, and everyone I—" She paused. Started over. "Everyone is accounted for. What's there to run from?"

"The building could fail."

"The building won't fail. Dez overbuilt it."

"I heard that," Dez called from the kitchen doorway, where he was pretending not to listen while making more tea. "And yes. I did."

Outside, the storm reached down and took hold of the river.

They all saw it happen—the water seemed to rise up out of its bed all at once, not flooding but leaping, a brown wall of it smashing against the escarpment's base and exploding into mist that the wind then threw across the whole shelf of rock. The glass shuddered. Somewhere outside, something metal tore loose and went away downwind, singing. The lights flickered, held, flickered again, and steadied—Dez's diesel growling up somewhere beneath them like a loyal animal.

"River stage just jumped two meters," Fen said. "Three. Four." His voice stayed level but his hand moved faster across the console. "This is the surge coming up the valley. It'll crest in maybe ten minutes."

Nobody spoke. Okonjo stopped sketching. Even Kessing set down her pen.

The crest came at 12:17, and it was the largest thing any of them had ever seen move. The river stood taller than the landing stage, taller than the flagpole, a rolling hill of water and debris that slammed into the rock below the station and sent shockwaves up through the floor. The glass bowed inward and sprang back. The mast sang—a deep, straining note, the guy wires shrieking—and Marguerite was on her feet without deciding to be, hand on the door, ready to go out into all of that to save the anemometer, and Ilse caught her wrist.

"It'll hold," Ilse said. "You built it to hold. Let it do its job."

Marguerite stood there, dripping rain that had come through the door seal, watching the needle on the wind display climb—fifty-four, fifty-seven, sixty meters per second—and forced herself to sit back down. The mast groaned. Held. Groaned again. Held.

At 12:31 the pressure bottomed out, and the wind began, slowly, grudgingly, to die.

* * *

They went outside at first light, because of course they did.

The world was soaked and rearranged. Half the landing stage was gone. The skiff's tarp had shredded into ribbons, though the skiff itself was fine, wedged safe behind the sandbags. A tree none of them had ever particularly noticed lay across the goat path, roots torn entirely out of the earth, its root ball standing higher than Okonjo's head. The buoy marking the Tern's wreck site was gone—swept away, sunk, or possibly deposited in a farmer's field forty miles downstream—but the green navigation light still blinked, absurdly, faithfully, out on the water.

And the anemometer was still up there. Turning. Reporting.

Fen checked the console and laughed out loud, which he almost never did. "Complete record. Not one gap. Sixty-one meters per second peak, logged clean." He looked up at Marguerite. "Your bolts held."

"My bolts held," Marguerite agreed, and felt a satisfaction so plain and complete that it embarrassed her.

They spent the morning walking the station grounds together, assessing, cataloguing, arguing cheerfully about repair priorities. This was the work now. This was the life. Dez kicked the dead generator's housing and pronounced it "wounded but salvageable, like me." Okonjo photographed the fallen tree from eleven angles. Kessing's graduates emerged from the bunkhouse blinking, took one look at the devastation, and lit up like children presented with a museum. Ilse stood at the edge of the shelf looking down at the river—high, brown, powerful, already beginning its slow retreat—and Marguerite came to stand beside her.

"It'll take the banks apart for a few miles," Ilse said. "We'll want to survey the channel before the port authority boats start running again. There'll be new snags, maybe a shifted bar below the bend."

"We'll survey it tomorrow."

"Tomorrow." Ilse rolled the word around. "You know, when I barged onto your boat at the locks, I figured you for a runner. Good instincts, bad anchor. I gave you six weeks before you sold the Tern and vanished south."

"And now?"

"Now I figure you'll be here when I'm old." Ilse's mouth curved. "God help us both."

Below them the river ran on, carrying the storm's leavings toward the sea—branches, silt, the occasional unidentifiable piece of somebody's roof. Somewhere far downstream, at the locks, at the port, in the courtrooms where Voss's trial had ended in convictions that still gave Marguerite a small cold satisfaction when she let herself think about it, the machinery of consequence continued its slow grinding. But up here there was only the water and the sky and the people.

The sky was already clearing in the west. That was the monsoon's way—one storm down, the season stretching ahead, more coming, always more coming. Okonjo pointed at a smudge of cloud building on the horizon and said, "Next one's a week out, maybe ten days," and Fen said, "I'll have the pressure trace analyzed by tonight," and Dez said, "Somebody help me drag this tree off the path before the next one turns it into a battering ram," and everybody moved, together, toward the work.

Marguerite lingered a moment at the rail.

In the main building, on the wall above the console, hung two objects. One was the half-burned page of the falsified survey ledger, framed, the impossible elevation figures visible under the scorch marks—the lie that had started everything, preserved as evidence, as warning. Beside it hung a calendar. A paper calendar, the kind sold in port-town shops, with a photograph of a sailboat on it and all twelve months in cheerful block print. On it, in her own handwriting, were the station's days: inspections due, supply runs, birthdays—Okonjo's papers ceremony in March, Dez's anniversary of his surgery in June, the day each year they rowed out to repaint the Tern's buoy.

A year ago she had kept an odometer. Today she kept a calendar. It was such a small thing. It was the largest thing she had ever done.

The wind came up the valley, warm and wet and smelling of green distance, and stirred the pages of the calendar, and settled. Out on the mast, the anemometer turned and turned, measuring the breath of a place she was not leaving.

Marguerite Brightwater stayed to watch the season come in.
