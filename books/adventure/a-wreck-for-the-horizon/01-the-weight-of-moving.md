## 1. The Weight of Moving

The thief left footprints in the salt like a man who wanted to be followed.

Noor Joubert crouched over them at dawn, the flats around him blinding white and flat as a held breath. The prints were small, narrow-heeled, set too close together — a walker, not a runner, which told him the thief wasn't afraid yet. Afraid men ran. This one strolled, out here where the salt crust rang like broken crockery underfoot and there was nothing to steal from the horizon but distance.

He touched the print's edge. The crust had not yet slumped. Hours old, maybe fewer.

"Strolling," Noor said aloud, to nobody. He'd gotten into the habit of narrating his own work during his third year alone in the eastern districts, and eleven years later the habit had outlived every posting, every partner, every reason to talk.

He stood and checked his water — two skins, three days if he was disciplined, one and a half if he was honest — and set off along the trail at the thief's own insolent pace.

The compass sat in his mind the way a splinter sits in a thumb. Brass-cased, lid dented at the corner where his father had dropped it off a camel in the Karsh hills and laughed about it for a decade afterward. The needle that wobbled left before settling right, a stutter Noor had once thought was a flaw and later understood was the whole point: his father used to say a true thing trembles before it settles, and a false one doesn't tremble at all. Noor had been eight. He'd thought it was about the compass.

He'd had it for nineteen days. Nineteen days since the reassignment order came through, the eleventh in eleven years — *Ranger Joubert, transfer effective end of season, report to Meridian Post* — and nineteen days since he'd sat in the rented room in Harrow Bend with the compass on the table and the packing crates open and thought, not for the first time: *this time I will leave nothing behind, because I never brought anything.*

Then he'd gone down to the provisioner's for an hour, and come back, and the window was open, and the table was bare.

* * *

The thing nobody tells you about a rootless life is that it gets easier every year, right up until it doesn't.

Noor had learned early to pack light. His father had died when he was sixteen — a surveyor's fall in the Karsh hills, quick, the report said, as if that were a kindness — and after that Noor had moved the way water moves, downhill and without argument. Ranger service suited him. The Corps posted you for a season, then a year, then moved you on before the town learned your face well enough to miss it. He'd guarded granaries in Doss, aquifers in the Vell, a caravan road through the Ashen Pass where the only crime in two years was a man stealing his own brother's mule out of spite.

He'd been good at it. That was the trick of it — you could be very good at a thing that kept you moving, and the being-good would feel almost like belonging. Towns thanked him. Councils signed his papers with real warmth. And every season the order came, and he'd fold the thanks into a satchel and go, and the strange grief of it — because it *was* grief, he'd finally admitted, somewhere around the sixth transfer — was that no one had ever taken anything from him. He simply never arrived anywhere long enough to be kept.

His father had been the opposite. Amal Joubert had spent thirty years mapping the same rivers, walking the same canyons, arguing with the same hydrology clerks, until the land knew his name better than his own family did. *You're a compass, Noor,* he'd said once, the only time he'd ever sounded disappointed. *You point well. You never stay where you're pointed.*

Noor had left home two weeks after that. He'd had nineteen days, this spring, of holding the one object his father had left him — the will had been a single line, *to my son, the compass*, no explanation, no sentiment, just the instrument and the dented lid — and then a thief with narrow heels had taken even that.

So no, he wasn't chasing a brass case and a wobbling needle. He knew exactly what he was chasing, and he knew why he kept his pace steady instead of pushing to close the gap. Because the chase had a shape, and the shape was simple: follow the prints, read the ground, make the next decision. While he was chasing, he didn't have to sit in Meridian Post with his crates unpacked and feel the full weight of the thing he'd been outrunning for eleven years.

His father, dead. The compass, gone. And Noor, as always, in between — moving.

* * *

The salt gave way to hardpan by midday, and the hardpan to cracked clay scored with dry washes, and the thief's trail threaded through them with the confidence of someone who'd read this country before. That bothered Noor more than the theft had. Strangers wandered the flats. Strangers didn't cut across the clay at the one point where the washes narrowed enough to jump, saving a half-mile of detour, unless they'd studied the maps.

He caught sight of the man at dusk — a figure on a low rise, small with distance, pausing to look back. Not hurrying. Checking, the way you check a lure.

Noor didn't wave. He sat, drank a measured mouthful, ate a strip of dried fig, and watched the figure watch him. Then the figure turned and walked on, unhurried, and Noor understood the message perfectly: *I know you'll come. I'm not running. Catch me if you can.*

It should have angered him. Instead it settled over him like a familiar coat. He'd been following things his whole life — trails, orders, the fading shape of a father's habits. At least this one left prints.

He made camp in the lee of the rise, and for the first time in nineteen days the silence didn't feel like an empty room. It felt like a road. He lay on his bedroll with the stars wheeling overhead, indifferent and precise, and thought about his father's hands — ink-stained at the fingers from a lifetime of drafting, the right thumbnail permanently thickened from prying dented brass lids open — and let himself feel, for exactly as long as he could stand, the size of what had been taken.

Then he slept, because grief was a country too, and he'd never learned to stay anywhere.

* * *

On the third day the land began to smell of iodine and old fish, and Noor knew the flats were ending. The clay darkened, then softened, then gave way in slow spongy miles to the tidal margin — the great grey littoral where the sea came and went like a thing breathing in its sleep. The Corps maps marked it as dead ground. Nothing to guard, nothing to garrison, nothing worth a ranger's time.

The thief's prints headed straight into it.

Noor followed them down onto the exposed seabed as the light went long and coppery. The tide was far out — farther out than the maps suggested it should go — and the retreating water had uncovered a country no survey had ever recorded: channels in the mud, ribs of ancient wrecks, and ahead, perhaps two miles on, a black line of stone rising out of the wet like the spine of something drowned.

The prints led to it.

He walked the last mile fast, his boots sucking at the mud, his ranger's calm thinning into something younger and hungrier. The stone formation resolved into a headland of dark rock, and at its base, where the sea had scoured for centuries, a cave mouth opened in the cliff — wide as a gatehouse, its rim carved, when he got close enough to see, with lines.

Not erosion. Lines. Deliberate, fine, dense as scripture: the whole face of the rock was etched with the branching form of a river system he had never seen on any map, and Noor had spent his life around maps. The channels ran down the stone like veins, converging, and at the heart of the carving, where all the lines met, was a circular recess the size of a closed fist.

The thief's prints ended at the cave mouth. Beyond them, the mud was smoothed by water, and the sea was already beginning its long inhale back across the flats behind him.

Noor stood at the threshold with the light dying and the tide turning, and felt the old restlessness in him go suddenly, completely still — the way it did only when the ground changed under a trail and he knew, with the certainty of nineteen years of reading sign, that whatever came next would not let him keep moving.

The carved recess at the heart of the river-map was round, and dented brass was round, and his father had mapped rivers for thirty years and vanished from every record Noor had ever searched.

He stepped inside.
