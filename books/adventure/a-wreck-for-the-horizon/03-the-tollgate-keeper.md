## 3. The Tollgate Keeper

The lamp Ilsa lit was made from a gull's skull.

Noor watched her carry it along the wall of the inner chamber — a hollowed cranium wired to a stub of tallow, the flame sitting where the brain had been — and decided that the strangeness of it was the least strange thing in the room. The chamber itself was round as the inside of a bell, its walls covered floor to ceiling in inked hide: river maps, dozens of them, layered and corrected and corrected again until the Qadash looked less like a river than like a scar that had been reopened so many times it had forgotten how to close.

"Sit," Ilsa said, "before you fall down."

Noor didn't sit. He was standing over Sef, who was on his knees on the wet stone with Noor's boot pinning the strap of his pack, and the compass was in Noor's hand, and the two strangers who had ambushed him at the door — a broad man with a boat hook and a younger one with a crossbow he hadn't lowered — were watching with the flat patience of people paid to watch.

"Your thief," Ilsa said, settling onto a stool carved from a single block of driftwood. "Or your brother?"

The word went through Noor like a fishhook.

"I don't know him."

"Mm." She turned the skull-lamp so its light fell across Sef's face. The boy — no, not a boy, twenty-five maybe, with river mud to the elbows and eyes the exact gray-green of water over silt — didn't flinch under it. "He said the same thing about you. Word for word. You two rehearse together?"

"He stole from me," Noor said.

"He *took back*," Ilsa said, "what was already his. Whether he knows it or not."

Sef's head came up. For the first time since the ambush, something moved behind his face — not guilt, not fear. Recognition, arriving late, like thunder after lightning.

"Who are you?" Sef asked her.

"Ilsa. Keeper of this tollgate, such as it is. Third of my line to keep it, though the first two kept it for different masters." She folded her hands in her lap, and Noor saw that her knuckles were mapped too — scarred, cracked, a lifetime of salt water and cold work. "And you are standing in the antechamber of the River Qadash, ranger, which makes you either very lucky or very unlucky, and in my experience the difference is mostly a matter of what you do next."

"The Qadash is a story," Noor said. "A dry-season tale. Mothers tell it to children so they'll drink their water ration."

"So is the tide," Ilsa said, "if you've never seen the sea."

* * *

She told it the way old women tell things — unhurried, certain, leaving gaps you were meant to fill yourself or suffer for.

The Qadash ran once, she said. Ran full and ran bright, from a source high in the western massif down through eleven villages to the sea, and the eleven villages had been rich in the way that only water-poor country can be rich: carefully, gratefully, every drop accounted for. Then the cartographers came. Empire men, map-men, measuring everything so it could be owned properly. And one of them — the best of them, the one whose maps were so true they were said to be dangerous — found the source.

"And here is the part they don't put in the schoolbooks," Ilsa said. "The source isn't just water. It's a gate. Some old engineering, older than the empire, older than the villages — a mechanism in the rock that can open the aquifer wide or choke it to a thread. Whoever holds the key holds the river. And the legend grew up around it because legends grow around anything people need badly enough." She looked at Noor. "*Drink first at the source, and belong.* That's the shape of it. Reach the water before anyone else, drink, and the river knows you. You'll have a place. Forever. Every rootless soul in this country has dreamed that dream, ranger. Don't pretend you haven't."

Noor hadn't. That was the obscenity of it. He'd stood on a hundred temporary postings in a hundred borrowed rooms and thought, *somewhere there is a place that would keep me*, and here was an old woman saying the place existed, saying it could be drunk, and hating how much of him leaned toward it.

"Legends are leverage," he said. "You said the first two keepers kept it for different masters. Who's yours?"

Ilsa smiled, and it was not a kind smile. It was the smile of someone doing arithmetic.

"That depends," she said, "on who walks out of this cave holding the key."

* * *

It happened while Ilsa talked — Noor would replay it later and still not catch the moment it began. One breath Sef was kneeling under his boot; the next there was a twist, a handful of thrown grit, and the broad man's boat hook sweeping low to trip Noor rather than the thief, because the broad man had been paid, apparently, to let the boy run.

Sef went through the far archway into the dark like water finding a crack.

"After him!" Noor was already moving. The crossbowman shifted — not to stop Sef. To stop *him*. A bolt thudded into the doorframe a hand's width from Noor's shoulder, quivering, and the message was legible enough.

"No further," Ilsa said mildly. "Not without paying the toll."

"He has my compass!"

"He has *Amal Joubert's* compass," Ilsa said, "and if you'd let me finish, you'd understand why that sentence should frighten you more than it does."

The name landed in the chamber and sat there. Even the broad man stopped shifting his weight.

"You know that name," Noor said slowly.

"Everyone who keeps this gate knows that name." Ilsa rose, joints cracking like green wood, and carried the skull-lamp to the largest of the hide maps — the oldest, black with age at the edges, stitched to the stone itself. Her finger traced the blue-inked line of the Qadash from the sea to the mountains, and stopped at a small symbol near the source: a circle bisected by a needle-line. North and south. A compass rose, drawn small as a fingerprint.

"Twenty-six years ago a cartographer came through that archway with a brass instrument and a commission from the Survey Office. He mapped what no one had mapped. He found the gate. He was offered everything — money, title, the drink itself. First at the water. Belonging, forever, by right of discovery." Her finger pressed the little rose until the lamplight made its shadow jump. "And he took the key out of the lock, closed the gate behind him, walked back down to the coast, and vanished from every record the empire keeps. They struck his name from the rolls. They burned his field notes. Officially, Amal Joubert never existed."

The chamber got very quiet. Somewhere above them, faint through forty feet of limestone, the tide was turning, and somewhere deeper, that gathering sound continued — patient, enormous, the sound of a thing that had been waiting longer than anyone in the room.

"No," Noor said. It came out before he could decide whether he wanted it to. "My father drove a supply wagon. Salt and lamp oil, coast to the interior towns. He died with rope burns on his hands and nothing in his pockets but—"

"But a brass compass he never used for navigation," Ilsa finished. "Because navigation wasn't what it was for. Show me the back of it, ranger. Oh — wait." Her smile returned, thinner. "The thief has it."

* * *

They negotiated while the water rose.

That was the truth of the next hour, though Noor hated the word *negotiated* — it sounded too clean for what it was: an old woman calmly pricing his father's ghost while the flood-sound in the deep tunnels climbed from a murmur to a conversation to a argument. The seasonal flood, she explained, came early some years. This was going to be one of those years. When it came, the lower galleries drowned, the tollgate sealed, and nothing went upriver or down until the spring.

"You want the compass," Noor said. "That's the toll."

"I want what the compass opens," Ilsa said. "There's a buyer coming within the week — empire men, surveyors' sons, whatever they're calling themselves now. They've bought the gate's output for eleven years running. Every drop the Qadash gives, piped west to the mining camps, while eleven villages downstream watch their wells go brackish." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "I keep the door. What comes through it was never mine to judge. But the buyer pays for exclusivity, and exclusivity requires the key, and the key has been missing for twenty years." She looked at him. "Until a dead man's son and a living man's son both came walking through my archway inside of one tide."

"His son?" Noor laughed, and it came out wrong. "He's nobody. He's a cutpurse with river mud on his—"

"He has Amal Joubert's chin," Ilsa said, "and Amal Joubert's way of leaving a room mid-conversation. I knew your father for three days, ranger, and I have spent twenty-six years failing to forget his face. The boy wears it younger. Draw your own conclusions."

Noor drew none. He couldn't. His whole life, his father had been a fixed point — poor, honest, restless in the way of men who drive the same roads for thirty years, gone too soon and mourned cleanly. A wagon driver did not have secrets that required burning. A wagon driver did not leave sons.

*Sons.*

The plural arrived in his chest and refused to leave.

"What happens," Noor said, "if I go after him. Upstream. Now, before the flood."

"Then you're not a customer anymore," Ilsa said. "You're a complication. And complications get reported to the buyer's men, who are camped at the river mouth as we speak." She picked up the gull-skull lamp and held it between them, the flame steady in the dead bird's crown. "But I'll tell you something for free, ranger, keeper's privilege. Twenty-six years ago your father stood exactly where you're standing, and I told him the buyer's terms, and he listened, and then he locked the gate anyway and gave up everything — name, wages, family, all of it — rather than let the water be sold." Her voice dropped. "He told me someone would come for the key someday. He said it might take a generation. He said—" she paused, and for the first time the old woman looked uncertain, "—he said *they'd* come. Plural. He wouldn't say why."

The flood-sound swelled. Dust sifted from the ceiling. In the archway where Sef had vanished, the darkness seemed to lean outward, listening.

Noor thought of the compass against his sternum for twenty years — the weight of it, the warmth of brass in cold weather, the way his father had gripped his shoulder at the funeral-eve and said only, *keep it moving, Noor, never let it sit still.* He'd thought it was grief talking. A dying man's superstition.

It had been instructions.

"Which way did he go," Noor said, "and how fast does the water come?"

Ilsa blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark, her voice was almost gentle.

"Upstream and faster than you," she said. "Run."
