## 4. Two Thieves

Sef had been running from the ranger for three days when he finally stopped to ask himself why the man was so angry.

It wasn't the compass itself. A brass compass was worth maybe two weeks of water rations in the salt towns, less if the needle wobbled. Sef had stolen better and been chased with less fury. No — this was different. The ranger ran like a man chasing a ghost that owed him something.

He learned the name on the second night, crouched behind a dune of wind-carved gypsum while the ranger made cold camp below, close enough that Sef could hear him talking to himself. Not praying. Rangers didn't pray; they inventoried. *Water, four liters. Ration bars, six. Compass —* and there his voice caught, a small snag in the recitation, like a boot catching on a root.

Joubert. The name went through Sef like a fishhook.

He'd grown up on that name the way other children grew up on lullabies. His mother had said it in anger, mostly, and once — only once, when she was dying and past caring who knew what — in something that might have been tenderness. *Amal Joubert.* The cartographer. The man who mapped the River Qadash and then walked out of their lives as cleanly as ink lifted off wet paper. The man who had a wife in the river towns, or so the rumors said, and a son there too, a legitimate son with a legitimate name, while Sef got nothing but a first name and a mother's bitterness measured out in nightly doses.

*He chose the river over us,* she used to say, sharpening her skinning knife by lamplight. *Remember that, boy. Men like that will always choose the work. You are not the work.*

But Sef had looked at the maps. That was the thing nobody understood. He'd found copies in a trading post outside Qalat Maru, pages soft as cloth from handling, and he'd traced the lines of the Qadash with his finger until he knew its bends better than his own scars. And the maps were beautiful. Not accurate-beautiful — though they were that too, every contour lovingly rendered — but *cared-for* beautiful. Nobody drew a river like that unless they loved it.

So which was true? The father who abandoned them, or the hand that drew those lines?

Sef had spent twenty-six years unable to answer. Then, three weeks ago, in a border market where he'd gone to sell a forged transit seal, he'd seen an old brass compass hanging from a ranger's belt, and stamped into the case, worn nearly smooth: **A.J.**

He hadn't planned it. Planning would have meant admitting what he wanted. He'd simply followed the ranger through the market, waited for the crush at the well, and lifted it off the belt with fingers steadier than they had any right to be. Then he'd run, because that part he did know how to do.

* * *

Now he sat in the dark above the tide cave, turning the compass over in his hands, and admitted it to himself at last: he hadn't stolen a compass. He'd stolen a father.

The thing was heavier than it should be. He'd felt that from the first moment — a weight beyond the metal, as if something were packed inside the case besides gears and a needle. He'd tried prying it open twice and stopped both times, some superstitious fear staying his knife. What if he broke it? What if the one thing Amal Joubert ever made that could be held in two hands came apart under Sef's fingers and turned out to be empty?

Below him, the sea sighed against the rocks. The tide was going out. He'd watched it retreat all evening, peeling back from the cliffs meter by meter, and now — there. A black seam in the rock face, widening as the water withdrew. A cave mouth, breathing.

His mother had told him stories about the Qadash's source. Every child in the river country had. *Drink first from the source and you'll belong somewhere forever.* It was a nursery tale, a comfort for orphans and wanderers, the kind of story you told so children wouldn't cry about having no home. Sef had stopped believing it around age nine, when he realized his mother told it with tears in her eyes.

But Amal Joubert had believed something. A man didn't map a river the way he'd mapped the Qadash — secret bends, unnamed tributaries, annotations in a cipher Sef had spent years failing to crack — without believing in something at the end of it.

And now here was a cave that appeared only at low tide, and a compass that weighed too much, and a ranger down there who carried the same surname Sef had spent his whole life refusing to claim.

*Two sons,* he thought. *One inheritance.* His mouth twisted. Typical of Amal Joubert to die before anyone could ask him which of them counted.

* * *

He climbed down while the cave mouth was still yawning wide, because waiting meant thinking and thinking meant feeling, and Sef had gotten very good at neither. Inside, the air smelled of salt and old stone and — faintly, impossibly — green. Growing things. In a sea cave on the edge of the salt country.

His eyes adjusted. The walls were covered in lines.

Not scratches. Lines. Cartographer's lines, fine and confident, sweeping across the stone in ink-black mineral stains: river channels, canyon systems, elevation hatching, and everywhere the cipher — those looping characters Sef knew from the copied maps, the private shorthand of Amal Joubert, covering these walls like scripture.

Sef stood in the dark with his heart hammering and understood that he was standing inside his father's head. This was where the man had worked. Or hidden. Or prayed, if men like Amal Joubert prayed.

"Who's there?"

The voice came from deeper in — a woman's, old and dry as the salt flats. Sef froze, hand on his knife, and that was when the light flared behind him.

He spun. A figure stood silhouetted at the cave mouth, lantern raised, and even before the glow reached the face Sef knew the build — broad-shouldered, deliberate, a body that moved like it was used to being responsible for things. The ranger. Of course. The trail had led here because the compass had led here, because the compass was a key and keys pull.

The ranger saw him and everything in his posture changed. Grief went out of him and rage came in, fast as a squall.

"You," the ranger said.

"Me," Sef agreed, and did the arithmetic. One exit, currently occupied. One old woman somewhere in the dark, location unknown. One compass in his pocket that he suddenly, desperately did not want to surrender in front of witnesses. "You want it back, I'm guessing."

"It's mine."

"It's *his*," Sef said, and heard his own voice crack on the word, and hated himself for it.

The ranger took a step forward, lantern high. "You don't get to say that. You don't know what that means. You're just a thief who got lucky in a market crowd—"

"I know exactly what it means." Sef pulled the compass out and held it up between them, brass catching the lantern light. "A.J. Amal Joubert. Cartographer of the Qadash. Vanished off the face of the earth eleven years ago, erased from the guild rolls, unmentioned in every record from Qalat Maru to the coast. I've spent half my life digging up whatever scraps of him the world left lying around. So don't tell me what I don't know, *ranger*."

Something flickered across the other man's face. Confusion, then a kind of horrified dawning.

"How do you know all that?"

"Because," Sef said, and the words came out before he could stop them, twenty-six years of swallowed truth forcing the gate, "I'm his son too."

The silence that followed was long enough to hear the tide turn. Somewhere behind them, water began whispering against stone — coming back in, slow and patient, the sea reclaiming its tollgate.

"That's a lie," the ranger said finally, but his voice had no conviction in it. He was doing the same math Sef had done a thousand times: the dates, the geography, the years Amal Joubert had supposedly been mapping alone. Room in the timeline for another woman. Another town. Another boy.

"Why would I lie about that?" Sef asked quietly. "What does it get me?"

The ranger had no answer. Behind them both, the old woman's voice drifted out of the dark again, closer now, unhurried: "Boys. If you're going to fight, fight quick. The tide doesn't wait for family drama."

Neither of them moved toward each other. Neither moved away. They stood in the lantern light like two halves of a broken instrument, measuring each other, each seeing the same unbearable thing: his own jawline, his own stubbornness, worn by a stranger.

Sef looked at the compass in his hand — *their* compass, apparently, *their* dead — and felt the bitter symmetry of it settle over him like sand after a storm. Two sons. One absent man. Both raised on rumors. Both chasing the same brass shell hoping to find a father inside it. And neither, he already knew — knew it in his bones, the way you know weather coming — willing to share.

The water was rising. The old woman was saying something about locks and tolls and the source of the Qadash, words that should have sounded like legend and instead sounded like business. And deep in the cave system, past the cipher-covered walls, Sef could feel the river pulling at him like a hook set in his chest — upstream, always upstream, toward the place where belonging was supposed to begin.

The ranger started toward him. Sef pocketed the compass and ran — deeper, into the dark, into his father's labyrinth — because if the inheritance couldn't be shared, then it had to be won.

Behind him, boots splashed. Ahead, the old woman laughed.

The tide was coming in, and the race for the source of the Qadash had begun.
