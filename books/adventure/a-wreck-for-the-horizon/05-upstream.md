## 5. Upstream

The water came for them at dawn, and it did not knock.

Noor heard it first as a sound he had no name for — a low, grinding roar rolling up through the cave's throat like a stone being swallowed. Then Ilsa was screaming from her alcove, *the tide turns, the gate closes, run you fools*, and the passage behind them filled with a brown wall of sea, foam-capped and furious, swallowing the tollgate chamber whole.

He didn't see Sef go ahead of him so much as feel it — a hand clamping his wrist in the dark, hauling him left where instinct said right, both of them stumbling up a sloping fissure while the flood chewed at their heels. The compass was a hard knot against Noor's chest, strapped there since the cave, and Sef's grip on his arm was the second time in two days the thief had touched him without trying to take something.

They came out onto a ledge high above a canyon, gasping, salt-stung, alive. Below them the Qadash ran silver-green through rock the color of old rust, and behind them the cave mouth exhaled one last gout of spray and went quiet, sealed until the next low tide.

Sef sat down hard on the stone. "I hate the ocean," he announced, to no one.

"You stole from a ranger on the salt flats," Noor said. "The ocean is the least of your problems."

"My problems are arranged in order," Sef said, "and currently the top one is that I'm stuck with you."

* * *

They walked upstream because downstream was the sea and the flooded cave and Ilsa's knowing smile, and because the compass — Noor checked it every hour, half expecting it to have grown cold or strange — pulled faintly northward along the canyon floor, needle steady, patient, like a dog that knew the way home.

The Qadash was not what Noor expected. He'd grown up on ranger maps, and rivers on ranger maps were blue veins, generous, assumed. This one ran narrow between its banks, barely knee-deep over gravel bars, moving with a kind of exhausted persistence. In places the canyon walls were scored with high-water marks — dark mineral stains three, four meters above the current line, old flood debris wedged in cracks far overhead.

"Your river used to be bigger," Noor said on the second morning.

"It's not my river."

"Your father's river, then."

Sef's jaw worked. He walked a few paces before answering. "He talked about it like it was alive. Letters my mother kept. *The Qadash sings in the narrows*, that kind of thing. Poet garbage." He kicked a stone into the water. "Man writes love letters to a river instead of coming home. You tell me which of us he loved more."

"I never met him," Noor said. "He died when I was nine. All I got was the compass and a name I couldn't spell."

"And a hundred reassignments," Sef said.

Noor stopped walking. "How do you know about that?"

"Rangers keep records. Records can be read." Sef shrugged, unapologetic. "You've been posted to eleven towns in fourteen years. Never longer than two seasons anywhere. I looked you up before I took the compass. Wanted to know who I was robbing." A crooked smile. "Turns out I was robbing a ghost. Man with no town, no wife, no post he ever fought to keep. Just a badge and a dead man's brass."

"You read my file and decided to steal from me anyway."

"I read your file," Sef said, "and recognized the handwriting."

That shut Noor up for most of the morning.

* * *

By the third day they'd developed rules without discussing them. Sef cooked; he was better at it, and admitted it with insufferable grace. Noor took first watch; he slept less, and didn't explain why. They didn't touch each other's gear. They didn't say the father's name after dark.

The canyon narrowed and deepened. The walls rose until noon light only touched the water in a thin gold ribbon, and the air cooled, and the river's voice changed — a lower note, almost musical, in the stretches where the bed smoothed to polished stone. Noor caught himself listening for it. *The Qadash sings in the narrows.* Damn the man, he'd been right.

It was in one of those narrows that Noor noticed the diversion channel.

It was subtle — that was what bothered him. Not a crude cut but a careful one: a masonry lip set into the eastern bank, low enough to look natural, silted enough to look old. Beyond it a canal ran off into a side canyon, dry now, its bed swept clean. Someone had built this to take water, and someone had maintained it, and recently — the mortar in the repairs was pale, months old at most.

He crouched at the lip for a long time. Sef doubled back when he realized Noor had stopped.

"What?"

"When's the last time this river flooded?" Noor asked. "Really flooded. Those marks on the walls."

Sef glanced up at the high-water stains, then down at the channel, and Noor watched the same arithmetic happen behind his eyes. "Seasonal flood comes in—" He stopped. "The flood that's chasing us upstream. It should already be filling this canyon. The keeper said—"

"The keeper said the flood drives everyone to move." Noor stood, wiped grit from his palms. "So where is it? We're three days up a canyon with flood scars older than either of us, and the river's running thin as a goat's piss. Something upstream is drinking it."

Sef was quiet. Then, reluctantly, as if the words cost him: "My father's survey notes. The ones my mother kept. He mapped intake structures all along the upper Qadash. I thought they were ruins. Old irrigation, abandoned." He met Noor's eyes. "They weren't abandoned. Were they."

"No," Noor said. "They weren't."

They stood together over the masonry lip, two men who had nothing in common except a dead cartographer and a stolen compass, looking at evidence neither wanted to name. The river murmured past their boots, thin and tired, still singing its narrow song.

"Maybe he didn't know," Sef said finally. It wasn't a question. It was a man asking permission to keep believing something.

"Maybe," Noor said, and gave it to him, because he wanted to keep believing it too.

* * *

On the fifth day Sef saved his life, and pretended he hadn't.

A gravel bar gave way under Noor's boot in a deep stretch — the river was shallow everywhere except where it wasn't — and the current, even thin, took him sideways into a strainer of flood-felled tamarisk before he could shout. He got an arm up, got a breath, felt the branches comb at his pack, and then Sef was there, flat on the trunk, hauling him out by the collar with a stream of curses in two languages.

They lay on the bank afterward, dripping, breathing.

"You're a terrible swimmer," Sef said, "for a man raised near water."

"I'm a fine swimmer. That was a strainer. Strainers kill good swimmers."

"Mm." Sef wrung out his sleeve. "Eleven towns. Did any of them teach you anything useful?"

"Fishing. Fencing. How to spot a liar in a market crowd." Noor rolled onto his back. The canyon walls leaned together overhead, a seam of blue sky. "You?"

"Sleight of hand. Lock work. How to be forgotten in under a minute." A pause. "Useful skills, if nobody wants you anywhere."

Noor turned his head. Sef was staring at the sky too, and for once there was no smirk on him, just a young man's face with the edges worn off. "Is that what you think this is?" Noor asked. "Nobody wanting us?"

"I think our father chose a river," Sef said. "Over two sons. Do the math."

"He didn't choose it over me. I never even—" Noor stopped. Started again. "He died before I understood what he was. Before I understood what any of this was. You at least got letters."

"I got rumors," Sef said. "And a mother who went quiet every time his name came up. You got the compass." He sat up. "The actual object. The thing he carried. So don't tell me about getting nothing, ranger. You got the part of him that pointed somewhere."

Noor lay there a long moment, hand resting on the strap across his chest, feeling the brass through the canvas. Pointed somewhere. His whole life he'd thought the compass pointed nowhere — that it was a keepsake, a dead man's toy, a weight. But it had never been broken. It had been waiting. And some treacherous part of him understood that Sef was right, and hated him a little for it, and understood that too.

"We should keep moving," he said. "Daylight's going."

"Yes, *ranger*," Sef said, and the title had almost lost its edge.

Almost.

* * *

They made the mouth of a side canyon by dusk and stopped because they had to — the main gorge ahead plunged into a slot so narrow the river itself seemed to have to apologize to pass through. Noor built a small fire against a rock shelf while Sef scouted, and when Sef came back he was carrying something he hadn't had an hour ago: a strip of oiled canvas, rolled tight, tied with wire.

"Picked up on the trail," he said, tossing it to Noor. "Half a kilometer back. Freshly dropped."

Noor unrolled it. Inside were tools — fine ones, surveying instruments, a brass theodolite smaller than his fist — and a folded paper stamped with a seal he didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a channel, rendered in expensive ink.

"That's not a ranger mark," he said.

"No. And those aren't cheap tools. Somebody's paying for precision work up here." Sef crouched opposite him, firelight moving on his face. "Intake crews. Maintenance teams. Whatever's bleeding this river has people on the ground, and we just walked into their commute."

Noor looked at the seal again. A circle bisected by a channel. Water, taken and owned. He thought of the pale mortar, the dry canal, the absent flood. He thought of his father mapping these canyons twenty years ago, and of what a man might be paid to map, or paid to keep quiet about.

"How far to the source?" he asked.

"Two days. Three if the slot canyon floods." Sef nodded at the strip of sky above the fire. "Which it will. The water has to come from somewhere, Noor. Even a stolen river remembers how to drown people."

Noor rolled the canvas closed and set it by the fire, and did not sleep well, and in the gray hour before dawn heard — or thought he heard — voices in the wind off the upper canyon. Men's voices. Many.

When he woke Sef, the thief was already awake, listening too.
