## 6. The Diverted Village

The river told the lie before anyone did.

Noor had been watching it all morning, the way a ranger watches anything: sideways, without seeming to. The Qadash ran beside the trail, and it was wrong. Not dirty, not dead — wrong the way a man is wrong when he's lost weight too fast. The banks stood high and pale where water used to lick them. Old mooring rings sat a full arm's length above the current. Somewhere behind them, back in the canyon country, the river had been thin. Here it was thinner.

"You keep looking at it," Sef said. "Either say something or stop."

"Ranger habit. I count things."

"Count what?"

"Water." Noor nodded at a line of dark silt staining the bank, three feet above the flow. "That's a flood mark. Last season's. This river used to run double what it runs now, right here, and upstream it was worse. Rivers don't get smaller going downstream. They get bigger. Tributaries feed them. Something's taking the water out before it gets here."

Sef walked a few paces in silence. Then, without looking up: "My father's maps show eleven side channels between the tollgate and the lowlands. Irrigation cuts, mostly. Old ones."

"You've read his maps."

"I've memorized his maps." Sef said it flat, like a man stating a debt. "There's a difference."

Noor let it go. He was getting better at letting things go with Sef, the way you get better at walking on a sprained ankle — not because it heals, but because you have no choice.

The town appeared in the early afternoon, and it appeared the way towns do when they're dying: gradually, then all at once. First a broken aqueduct striding across the flats on stone legs, dry as a bone. Then orchards — dead, but dead recently, the wood still pale where someone had cut it, the stumps not yet weathered gray. Then the walls. Then the smell.

Not rot. Dust. The particular dust of a place where the fountains had been shut off and nobody had bothered to sweep since.

"Qasr al-Miya," Sef said, reading a faded lintel inscription as they passed under the gate. "The Palace of Water." He laughed once, without humor. "Someone had a sense of humor."

* * *

The town wasn't empty. That was the first thing that surprised Noor. He'd expected abandonment, the clean tragedy of it, everyone gone. Instead there were perhaps two hundred people left in a town built for four thousand, and they were still farming — dry-farming, coaxing melons out of sand with hand-dug wells that went down farther every year.

They gathered the way dying towns gather strangers: slowly, from doorways, then all at once in the square. An old man with a surveyor's squint came forward, introduced himself as Dakan, and asked the question Noor had been dreading.

"You two from the water authority?"

"No," Noor said. "Ranger service. We're passing through."

"Rangers." Dakan tasted the word. "We filed a complaint with the ranger service. Four years ago. Then three years ago. Then every season since." He said it without heat, which was worse. Heat would have meant hope. "You want water? There's a well behind the mosque. Bucket's on a rope. Take what you carry."

In the shade of a collapsed granary, over flat bread and well water that tasted of iron, Dakan told them how it happened.

"It didn't dry up. That's what I need you to understand. Everyone downstream says the Qadash dried up, and it's a lie, and they know it's a lie, and they say it anyway because it's easier." He drew in the dust with a stick: the river's course, a long wavering line. Then, twenty miles upstream, a branch. "There's a cut. A canal, engineered, lined with fired brick, with sluice gates — iron ones, good ones, the kind that cost money. It takes maybe half the river. Maybe more in the dry season. And it doesn't go to any field I've ever seen. It goes into the hills, to a compound, and past that, nobody knows."

Noor looked at the line in the dust and felt something cold settle behind his ribs. "Who built it?"

"Men who came with papers." Dakan spat. "Authority papers. Concession papers. Stamped and sealed and signed by people whose names we never learned. They paid the district governor — you can guess how — and when the elders objected, they paid some more, and when the young men broke a sluice gate one night, they came back with hired guards and rebuilt it in a week with a wall around it." He looked up. "You said you're a ranger. Where were the rangers?"

Noor didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't an indictment, and Dakan knew it, and let him sit in it.

"The water goes somewhere," Sef said quietly. "Canals don't dead-end."

"No," Dakan agreed. "They don't. And here's the thing your ranger friend should hear, since he counts things. Two years ago, a woman came through. Old, sharp, ran a tollgate on the coast road — cave country. She was buying. Not water. *Access*. She wanted anyone who knew the upper river, guides, maps, anything. She said — and I had this from a cousin who talked to her — she said the source was going to be sold. Sold, like a goat. One buyer, one key, and everyone downstream could drink sand." He fixed Noor with his surveyor's squint. "You know her?"

Noor's mouth had gone dry, and it wasn't the well water. "I've met her."

"Then you know more than we do." Dakan brushed the map out of the dust with his palm, erasing his own town. "That's usually how it works."

* * *

They found the diversion itself at dusk.

Sef led, and Noor let him, because Sef had been muttering distances and bearings for the last mile like a man praying, and because — Noor admitted it to himself now, in the privacy of his own skull — Sef knew this river. Not the way a ranger knows a river, from patrols and flood reports. The way a child knows a lullaby. He took turns he couldn't have known were turns, cut through a stand of tamarisk because "the map says the bank erodes here," and came out exactly where he said he would.

The canal opened off the Qadash behind a shoulder of rock, invisible from the main channel. It was beautiful, Noor hated to admit. Fired brick in a herringbone pattern, iron sluice gates green with patina, a gauge tower with a graduated scale painted on the stone. Half the river slid through the cut and vanished into the hills, and the other half limped on toward Qasr al-Miya and every town like it, a river on a starvation diet.

Sef stood at the head of the cut for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man handling something fragile.

"This isn't new construction dressed up old. Look at the brick. Look at the mortar joints. This is *old*. Decades old." He crouched, touched the herringbone. "And look at the survey marks." He pointed to a small chiseled glyph on the gatehouse lintel, a circle quartered by a river-line. "That's a cartographer's bench mark. That's — " He stopped.

"That's what," Noor said.

"That's my father's mark." Sef's jaw worked. "I'd know it anywhere. He put it on everything. Every map, every cache, every — " He stood up fast, like the stone had burned him. "Your compass. The one I took. Turn it over. The baseplate."

"I know what's on the baseplate."

"Then you know." Sef turned on him, and his eyes were bright and furious and frightened, all three at once. "He built this. Or he surveyed it. Or he *designed* it. The man whose maps I've had in my head since I was six years old — the man I came all this way to find — he's the one who taught this river to bleed."

Noor said nothing, because his own throat was full of the same thing: a cold, rising, unspeakable *maybe*. His father. Amal Joubert, who had carried a brass compass through a rootless life and passed it to a son he saw one season in three. Amal, who Noor had built into a monument out of almost nothing — a compass, a handful of letters, a voice on the wind. And now a bench mark on a sluice gate above a dying town.

"Or," Noor said finally, and the word came out rough, "he mapped it. Surveyors map things they didn't build. That's the job."

"Is it?" Sef said. "Is that what you're going to hold on to?"

"I don't know what I'm going to hold on to." Noor heard his own voice crack and hated it. "That's the problem."

* * *

They came back to the town after dark, and the town was waiting for them, and it wasn't a friendly waiting.

Torches in the square. Dakan in the middle of it, and behind him maybe forty men and women with farm tools, and in front of them, being held by two of the biggest, a stranger in road leathers with a compound crest stitched on the shoulder — the hills crest, the canal crest. A rider. His satchel had been opened and its contents were being passed around the torchlight: papers, a sealed letter, a small brass instrument that made Dakan's face go strange.

"Your ranger friend will want to see this," Dakan called, and there was no warmth in it at all.

The letter, once Noor had pried it from the crowd's grip, was short and formal and made his stomach turn over. It was an invoice. A schedule of payments. And at the bottom, in a clerk's careful hand, a line item that Noor read three times because the first two readings refused to be real:

*Final installment due upon transfer of source access, per agreement with the tollgate keeper. Buyer's representative to present key.*

The tollgate keeper. Ilsa. The old woman with the cave full of river maps and the voice like dry leaves, who had spoken of legend and belonging and the source that grants a home to whoever arrives first. Not a legend. A *vendor*. The source wasn't a mystery at the end of the river. It was inventory. It was a lot with a gate on it, and the gate had a lock, and the lock had one key, and the key was riding in Noor's pocket at that very moment, warm as a living thing.

Beside him, Sef read it over his shoulder. Noor felt him go still.

"They were never going to let anyone drink," Sef said. "The legend, the race, the belonging — it's a story to keep people moving toward the gate. To keep the key *coming*." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "We're not competitors. We're couriers."

The crowd was watching them now, all of them, and Dakan stepped forward with the surveyor's squint that missed nothing.

"You've got the key," he said. It wasn't a question. "I don't know how I know, but I know. You two came down the river with a ranger's walk and a thief's eyes and you've been looking at that canal like it owes you money." He raised his voice so the square could hear. "So here's my town's complaint, ranger, four years late and four seasons ignored. Either you're with them — " he nodded at the rider, at the hills, at the whole machinery of paper and iron — "or you're with us. But you don't get to walk through Qasr al-Miya neutral. Neutral is how we got here."

The torches cracked. Somewhere a child cried and was hushed. Noor's hand found the compass through his shirt, and it was just brass, just an inheritance, just the last warm piece of a father who might have built the thing that killed this town — and downstream, twenty miles and more, the seasonal flood was already gathering in the high country, and upstream, past the canal and the compound and the hills, the source waited behind its lock for a buyer's man with a buyer's key.

"We need to move tonight," Sef said quietly, at his shoulder. "Before they send more than one rider."

Noor looked at the crowd, at Dakan, at the dry fountain at the square's heart with its basin cracked like a lip.

"We move tonight," he said. "And when we come back down this river, it runs full."
