## 7. The Buyer's Men

Sef smelled them before he saw them: leather oil and woodsmoke, the particular stink of men who'd been paid to be patient.

He stopped on the ledge above the narrows, one hand out to halt Noor behind him. Below, the Qadash ran thin as a rumor through its own canyon, and on the far bank four figures crouched among the tamarisks, still as stones. Not rangers. Rangers fidgeted. These men had the settled quality of hired patience — crossbows resting across their knees, a fifth man already climbing the scree slope that would put him above their trail.

"Buyer's men," Sef breathed.

"You can tell from here?"

"I can tell from the way they're not looking at the river." He eased back off the ledge. "Nobody camps in this country for the view."

Noor's hand went to his belt, found the compass case, pressed it flat against his ribs the way he'd been doing since the diverted village — unconsciously, constantly, like a man checking a wound. Sef hated that he knew the gesture so well. Hated more that he'd started reading Noor's body the way you read weather, without deciding to.

"They want the compass," Noor said.

"They want what it opens." Sef crouched, studying the scree. The climber above was good — moving only when wind moved the brush — but good wasn't the same as local. He was taking the long ramp because he didn't know about the chimney behind them, the crack in the canyon wall that their father had marked with a faded red *A* thirty years ago. Amal Joubert had left his initial all over this river like a man signing work he couldn't claim. "There's a way up and around. We lose them at the split rocks, we're two days ahead instead of half a day behind."

"And then?"

"And then we get to the gate before they do, and before the flood does." Sef glanced upstream, where the sky over the high country had gone the color of a bruise. Three days, maybe four, until the seasonal flood came down the canyon like a fist. The old surveyors' notes he'd memorized were blunt about it: *the Qadash rises angry; nothing standing in the narrows survives.* "We move now, quiet, and we don't stop to argue."

They climbed. It should have been simple. It was not simple, because Noor argued anyway, in whispers, while hauling himself up rock with the compass strapped to his chest like a second heart.

"You said Ilsa sold access," Noor muttered. "You said the buyer bought the source. So why send men? Why not just use whatever key she gave them?"

"Because her key is ceremonial. A copy. My father made one true key and hid it in plain sight for thirty years as a dead ranger's keepsake." Sef pulled himself onto a shelf, breath burning. "The buyer has money and a diversion canal and no way through the last gate. We are carrying the only thing in the world he needs. Congratulations. We're not travelers anymore. We're cargo."

* * *

They lost the first pursuer at the split rocks, where the canyon forked and their father's map showed a route no hired man could follow blind. Sef felt almost good for an hour after that. Almost light. Then the second ambush came at dusk, at the place where the canyon widened into a gravel wash, and it came wrong — not from ahead but from behind, which meant the buyer's men had split, which meant someone competent was running this hunt.

Three of them, rising out of the shadows of a fallen slab. Crossbows. The leader was a broad woman with a scarred jaw who spoke like she was reading terms aloud.

"The compass. Toss it here and walk away. You have my word nobody follows."

Noor's hand moved toward the case — and Sef saw it, saw the exact moment the ranger decided to negotiate rather than fight, because Noor was a lawman down to the marrow and lawmen counted bodies before bullets.

"Don't," Sef said.

"It's one compass against three bows—"

"It's my father's compass." The words came out of him hot and ugly, before he could dress them up. "And you don't hand over your father's bones because someone gives you their *word*."

The woman sighed, the sigh of a professional whose easy option just expired. She lifted two fingers.

Sef didn't think. That was the thing he would turn over later, night after night, unable to make it make sense. He didn't think; his body simply decided. When the first bolt came he was already moving — not away from Noor, which was the sensible direction, but *across* him, shoulder into the ranger's chest, driving them both sideways behind the slab. The bolt meant for Noor's throat took Sef high in the back of the shoulder instead, a punch of heat and then a cold spreading down his arm like the river itself had gotten inside him.

They ran. Noor half-carried him, which was humiliating, up the wash and into the chimney crack, while bolts sparked off stone behind them and the woman's voice called out something about terms still being open. Sef laughed at that, a wet, wheezing sound, and Noor looked at him like he'd gone mad.

"She's offering *terms*," Sef managed. "Very civilized murder."

"Shut up and breathe."

They climbed until the sounds below went quiet, then higher, until the canyon floor was a silver thread far beneath them and the bruised sky pressed close. In a shallow overhang crusted with old soot — some forgotten prospector's camp — Noor finally set him down and cut the shirt away from the wound.

It was bad. Not dying-bad, but bad enough: a deep furrow through the meat of the shoulder, blood welling slow and dark. Noor worked in silence, washing it with water from his flask, packing it with the bitter green paste he carried in his kit, binding it tight with strips of shirt. His hands were steady. Ranger hands. Sef watched them and felt something in his chest he refused to name.

"Why?" Noor asked quietly.

"Why what?"

"You took a bolt for me. A stranger. A rival. You've wanted that compass since the salt flats." Noor tied off the bandage, sat back on his heels. "One honest answer. That's all I'm asking."

Sef stared up at the overhang, at the soot-black stone, and searched himself for the honest answer and found only a hole shaped like one. He thought of every reason he could offer — tactical, self-interested, cruel — and none of them were true. The truth was that when the bolt came, there hadn't been a decision at all. There had just been Noor, falling, and a world Sef's body had refused to enter.

"I don't know," he said finally, and it cost him more than the wound did. "I hate you. You got the name. You got the grave to visit. You got a whole grief with a shape to it, and I got rumors and a mother who went quiet every time his name came up. If anyone deserves to bleed on this mountain it's—" His voice cracked. He let it. "I don't know why. Ask me again when I'm not leaking."

Noor was silent a long moment. Then he passed over the flask, and Sef drank, and neither of them said anything else, because some silences are the closest two angry men can come to an embrace.

* * *

They traveled through the night, because the countdown had changed shape. Sef lay it out plainly while Noor dressed the wound a second time at dawn: the flood was coming down the narrows, the buyer's men were flanking them along the rim, and the source gate sat two days' hard march upstream through country their father had mapped and no one else alive knew.

"We can't outrun both," Noor said.

"We don't outrun both. We outrun the flood and let the flood deal with them." Sef flexed his bandaged arm, testing it. Fire, but functional. "My father's notes say there's a cache above the third bend. Stone door, hidden spring. If the map's right, we sleep safe tonight."

"And if the map's wrong?"

Sef looked at him. At this stubborn, grieving, infuriating man who carried their father's brass heart against his ribs and still didn't understand what he was carrying.

"Then we learn what kind of cartographer he really was," Sef said. "Come on, brother. The river's waiting, and so is everyone trying to kill us."

Above the third bend, the canyon walls closed in and the air grew cool and wet, and somewhere far upstream, too far to see, the snowfields were already letting go.
