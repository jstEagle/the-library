## 8. Amal's Last Page

The storm caught them a mile short of the cache.

Noor saw it first — a brown wall crawling down the canyon's throat, eating the light. Not rain. Rain would have been a mercy. This was dust and gravel and the sky coming apart, and behind it, higher up the watershed, the seasonal melt was already moving. He could feel it in the ground, a low shudder through his boot soles, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Cache," Sef shouted, and pointed.

It was set into the canyon wall the way a swallow's nest is set into a cliff — invisible until you knew the shape of it. A slab of sandstone, cut and hinged, and behind it a room no bigger than a ranger's supply locker, lined with oilcloth and sealed with wax that still smelled faintly of pine. Whoever built it had built it to last.

Noor knew whose work it was before he saw the handwriting. He'd grown up with that handwriting, or the ghost of it — the careful, upright capitals of a man who believed a map was a promise and every letter a signature.

His father had built this room. Decades ago. And then walked away from it.

"Get the lamp lit," Noor said. "I'll seal the door."

They got the slab shut with a minute to spare. The storm hit like a fist, a grinding roar against the stone, and then the world outside ceased to exist. Inside: one lamp, two men, and the smell of old paper.

Sef was still gray from the wound in his side — Noor had rewrapped it that morning with strips of his own shirt, and the bleeding had slowed, but the man moved like his body was a borrowed thing, handled too roughly. He lowered himself onto the oilcloth floor and watched Noor with that flat, guarded stare he'd been carrying since the tollgate.

"You knew about this place," Sef said.

"I knew my father kept caches. Ranger habit. I didn't know they were *here*." Noor ran his hand along the wall. Shelves. Rolled charts in waxed tubes. And on the top shelf, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed cord, a bundle the size of a prayer book.

He didn't want to touch it. He touched it anyway.

* * *

The journal was bound in canvas, water-stained at the corners, the spine cracked from being opened and reopened and opened again. Amal Joubert's initials were stamped on the cover in brass — the same brass, Noor realized, with a lurch, as the compass. The same alloy. The same hands.

The early pages were what Noor expected. Survey notes. Elevations. The Qadash rendered in his father's precise, patient script: *tributary, seasonal; flow strong; water sweet.* Sketches of the canyon they'd climbed through. Sketches of the sea cave, drawn from inside, the tollgate arch rendered with something close to reverence. In the margins, small annotations in a different hand — looser, faster, a young person's hand — and Noor felt the floor tilt under him.

"Whose writing is that?" he asked.

Sef had gone very still. He leaned over, looked, and something moved through his face like weather.

"Mine," he said. "That's mine. I was — eight, maybe nine. He used to bring me up here. In the summers. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought I'd dreamed it. My mother said he took me on survey trips. I remembered a canyon and a river and a man who talked to his maps. I thought I invented him."

Noor looked at the marginalia. A child's careful copy of his father's capitals, not quite straight, trying so hard. *TRIBUTARY, SEASONAL.* A drawing of the compass in the corner of one page, with an arrow pointing to it and a word in a boy's hand: *mine?*

The question mark gutted him.

"Keep reading," Sef said quietly. "The back. It gets worse."

* * *

It got worse the way rot gets worse: gradually, then all at once.

The river's flow numbers, steady for years, began to drop. Amal noticed it the way he noticed everything — in decimals. *Flow at marker 41 down 6%. Down 9%. Down 14%. No drought signature. Upstream cause.* Then the tone changed. The careful capitals started to lean, as if the hand writing them was being pushed.

*I found the cut. A diversion channel, engineered, feeding a closed cistern system above the third narrows. Not rangers' work. Not any work I can attribute. Someone is taking the Qadash apart from the head and selling the pieces.*

*I reported it. My report was returned unfiled. The clerk who received it has been reassigned. I have been reassigned. Interesting.*

*They have struck my name from the river registry. Twenty-two years of survey, and the Qadash is now credited to "J. Amal, deceased." A dead man with my first name. They erased me with a comma.*

Noor's hands were shaking. He set the journal on his knees and pressed them flat and made himself breathe, because the alternative was to tear the pages.

Twenty-two years. His father had served twenty-two years on this river and been deleted from it — and Noor had spent his whole life believing the opposite story: that Amal Joubert had abandoned the service, abandoned his posts, abandoned his *sons*, because he loved the river more than people. That was the story the service told. That was the story Noor had carried through every reassignment, every empty season, every town that never learned his name. He had hated his father for a choice his father never made.

"They didn't erase him because he vanished," Noor said. His voice came out wrong. "They erased him because he wouldn't stop."

"Keep going," Sef said. His voice was worse.

* * *

The last twenty pages were written fast, in the dark, by a man who knew he was running out of both.

*The diversion is not a theft. It is a sale. I have traced the cistern contracts to a buyer — a water concern with offices in the capital, names I will not write here, they read mail. They intend to own the source outright. The tollgate at the sea cave is the original gate — the river's own door — and the compass is its key. There is a second mechanism above the third narrows. The buyer's engineers built it to throttle the river. It can also, I believe, be broken.*

*I have tried the lawful road. The lawful road is paved with the buyer's money. What remains is the river itself.*

*I have two sons. This is the part I have failed at, so I will write it plainly, because plainly is all I have left.*

*I made the compass for both of them. The brass is one piece, cut in two and rejoined — Noor has the needle housing; the key-tooth is a separate insert, and Sef, if you are reading this, it is in the lining of the case, under the felt. Neither half opens the gate alone. I designed it that way on purpose. A river is not a thing one man owns. Neither is a father.*

*I am sorry. I chose the river because I thought I could save it alone, and I could not, and the cost of my failure is that my sons will have to finish it together. That is not an inheritance. It is a duty. I would give you both a farm and a quiet life if I could. I can only give you the truth and the current, and my love, which was always real even when I was not there to prove it.*

*The flood comes at the turn of the season. When it comes, the gate will open wide for one day. If the diversion still stands, the buyer takes the source and the downstream villages take the dust. If it falls, the river runs free.*

*Go together. That was always the whole of the instruction.*

*Go together.*

The last page was dated nine days before the flood. By the old almanac reckoning Noor carried in his head — the reckoning every ranger carries — the flood was coming *tomorrow*. Maybe tonight. The shudder in the ground hadn't stopped since the storm began; it had gotten worse, a deep patient pulse, the river gathering itself in the dark above them.

Noor sat with the journal open on his knees and did not move for a long time.

He had chased a thief across the salt flats for a keepsake. A piece of a dead man he could hold. And the dead man had been trying, the whole time, to put the piece in *both* their hands — had built a lock that could not be opened by a son who came alone, because he had known, with a cartographer's terrible foresight, that his sons would come alone, each certain the other was the obstacle.

"Under the felt," Noor said.

Sef already had the compass out. His hands were steadier than Noor's. He peeled back the lining of the case, and there it was: a small brass insert, a tooth of machined metal, sitting in a recess Amal Joubert had sewn into his own gift and never told anyone about. Sef lifted it out and turned it in the lamplight, and Noor saw his face do something it hadn't done in all the miles since the cave — crack, all the way down, into something young and unspeakably sad.

"He was going to give it to me," Sef said. "He was going to come back."

"He was going to give it to both of us." Noor held out his hand. After a moment, Sef set the insert in it, and Noor fitted it into the needle housing, and the two halves of the brass met with a small clean click — the sound of a thing becoming whole. The needle swung once, twice, and settled, pointing not north but up-canyon, toward the third narrows, toward the source, with the certainty of a promise finally kept.

Noor thought about the diversion channel. The dying town. The cistern contracts. His father's name scraped off a registry and replaced with a comma. Grief and fury arrived together and fused into a single hard thing in his chest, and he found he was grateful for it, because grief alone would have put him on the floor and fury alone would have made him reckless, and he could afford neither.

"It was never a prize," he said. "It's a job. It was always a job."

Sef laughed — a short, wet, disbelieving sound. "Our father's whole legacy is homework."

"Then we'd better not be late."

Noor stood and slung his pack. Outside, the storm was dying, and through the seam of the stone door he could hear the other sound underneath it, growing: the flood, coming down the canyon like a debt being collected. Above the third narrows, somewhere in the dark, the buyer's men would be moving too — and they knew, by now, exactly what the compass could do.

Sef snuffed the lamp. In the sudden black, the needle's faint glow seemed to hang in the air between them, pointing upstream.

"One day of open gate," Sef said. "One day to break what he couldn't."

"Then we don't sleep," Noor said, and shouldered the door open onto the roaring dark.
