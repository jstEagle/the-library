## 9. The Source

Noor felt the flood before he heard it — a tremor in the soles of his boots, a shiver running up through the canyon walls like the whole gorge was clearing its throat.

They climbed through the last hours of dark, Sef ahead with Amal's journal pages folded against his chest, Noor behind with the compass open in his hand. The needle no longer pointed north. It hadn't since the third narrows. It pointed upstream and slightly left, into solid rock, and every hundred yards the pull grew stronger — not magnetic anymore but something older, as if the brass had remembered what it was made for.

"You feel that?" Sef called back over the noise.

"The ground shaking? Yes."

"Not that." Sef stopped on a ledge above the churning water and pressed his palm flat to the wall. "The river's under us here. Under the rock. It's been running beneath the canyon for miles — that's why it looked so thin. He found the buried channel."

*He.* Their father. Noor was still learning how the word sat in his mouth when Sef said it — like a stone too large to swallow and too heavy to spit out.

The sky went from black to iron to a bruised gray, and then they came around the shoulder of the final ridge and saw it.

The canyon ended in a wall of red stone perhaps two hundred feet high, split down the middle by a seam of white marble, and set into the seam — half natural formation, half deliberate craft — stood the gate. Two doors of weathered bronze, tall as watchtowers, covered in relief carvings worn nearly smooth: rivers, hands, a figure with arms outstretched giving water away. At their center, where the doors met, was a socket. Round. Brass-sized.

And below it, cut into the rock in letters deep enough to read from across the gorge:

**QADASH — FOR ALL WHO COME THIRSTY**

Below that, someone had chiseled a second line, later, cruder, with less care: **BY LEASE OF THE KARST CONSORTIUM. ACCESS BY PERMIT ONLY.**

"They defaced it," Sef said quietly. "They put their name over his."

Noor didn't answer. He was looking past the gate, up the face of the cliff, to where a massive iron flume jutted from the rock fifty feet above the doors — the head of the diversion, the throat through which the river had been bled for years toward some buyer's fields while the villages downstream drank dust. Pipes ran from the flume along the canyon rim, vanishing east. Even now, with the flood roaring closer, a thin silver thread of stolen water trickled from its lip.

Behind them, far down the canyon, a whistle sounded. Then another. Then voices.

"They're close," Sef said.

"Then we're on time." Noor started down the talus slope toward the gate, compass held out before him like a lamp.

* * *

Sef had imagined this moment his whole life, and in every version of it he had been alone.

That was the detail that kept snagging him, even now, even with the buyer's men closing fast and his brother — *brother*, the word still fit badly, like a boot on the wrong foot — scrambling down the scree ahead of him. In every version, Sef reached the gate alone, drank first, and became someone who belonged somewhere. That had been the whole architecture of the theft. Take the compass. Take the inheritance. Take the name.

Now he crouched at the base of the bronze doors with Noor Joubert beside him, and the socket waited above them both, and Sef understood with sudden terrible clarity that there was only enough room in the legend for one drinker. Whoever reached the source first. Whoever the water chose. The old woman Ilsa had said it like a blessing; standing here, with the carving of the outstretched figure giving water *away*, Sef finally heard it as a warning.

"Noor." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "When this opens — you should be first."

Noor looked at him. In the gray light his face was hollowed, sleepless, streaked with canyon dust, and for a moment he looked so much like the man in the journal's one surviving sketch that Sef's chest hurt.

"I know what you're doing," Noor said.

"I'm being practical. You're the ranger. You've got the standing. If anyone's going to—"

"No." Noor turned back to the doors. "You haven't been listening. Neither have I, not really — not until last night, reading his pages by lamplight. He didn't map this river to own it, Sef. He mapped it because he loved it, and loving something isn't the same as keeping it. The legend's wrong. Or we're reading it wrong. Either way—" he tapped the compass against his palm, once, twice, "—I'm not climbing all this way to trade one kind of alone for another."

Down the canyon, the first of the mercenaries came around the bend — six figures, then more behind them, moving fast, crossbows slung across their backs.

"Company," Sef said.

"I see them."

"How long do you need?"

Noor measured the height of the socket, the handholds in the marble seam, the worn steps carved into the doors' lower edge. "Two minutes. Maybe three."

Sef looked at the narrows behind them — the slot where the canyon pinched to twenty feet, where the flood would have to funnel through, where six men with crossbows could hold off an army or die trying. He thought about every version of this moment he'd ever imagined, and how none of them had included this: turning his back on the gate to face downstream.

"Go," he said, and drew his knife, and walked toward the narrows.

* * *

The bronze was cold through Noor's gloves, colder than the morning justified, as if the metal had been waiting in winter all these years.

He climbed. The carved steps were shallow, made for smaller feet or more patient ones, and the flood's tremor was stronger here — the whole gate humming like a struck bell, the buried river pressing up against the doors from within. Halfway up he dared a look back: Sef was a small dark shape in the narrows, arms spread, filling the gap, shouting something Noor couldn't hear over the water. Beyond him, the mercenaries had stopped. They'd seen the flood too. Any man with sense would retreat to high ground.

These men didn't have sense. They had orders.

Noor climbed.

At the top, the socket was exactly the size of the compass — of course it was; his father had built the key and the lock in the same year, the same grief, maybe — and for one absurd moment Noor hesitated, the way a man hesitates before burning a letter he knows he'll want to read again. The brass was warm in his hand. His father's thumb had worn a shine into the lid. Every town he'd never belonged to, every reassignment, every empty season — he had carried this through all of it, and carrying it had been the closest thing to home he had.

Then he heard Sef shout again, and this time he caught the words: *"They're shooting!"*

Noor set the compass into the socket.

It fit the way a word fits a sentence. The needle spun free of north, spun free of everything, and began to turn — faster, faster, a blur of light in its glass face — and the hum in the doors rose to a note Noor felt in his teeth. Somewhere inside the mechanism, counterweights the size of oxen began to move. The bronze grew warm under his palms.

The doors opened inward, and the sound that came out was not water.

It was *light*. A column of spray and roar and morning sun caught mid-fall, the source itself rising from the deep channel in a geyser of white, and the smell of it — wet stone and green things, the smell of a country Noor had never lived in but recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face from a photograph — hit him like a wave. The flood behind him surged forward to meet it, and the two waters met in the gorge below with a concussion that knocked him flat against the door.

For three seconds — he counted them, he would count them for the rest of his life — Noor Joubert stood at the forbidden source of the River Qadash, first to arrive, heir to the legend, and the water rose around his boots and offered itself.

All he had to do was kneel. All he had to do was cup his hands. Belonging, on tap, guaranteed by whatever old law had carved those words into the cliff.

Instead he looked up. Fifty feet above, the iron flume jutted from the rock, its stolen thread of silver still trickling east to the buyer's fields, and below it he could see the diversion mechanism now — a great geared wheel, half exposed by the shaking, chained to the cliff face, throttling the river's throat.

*One day of open gate,* Sef had said. *One day to break what he couldn't.*

Noor took the crowbar from his pack — salvaged from the cartographer's cache, his father's own tool, which felt right in a way he couldn't explain — and began to climb toward the flume.

* * *

Sef bled from a graze along his ribs and held the narrows anyway.

The mercenaries had found their courage, or their stupidity, and were trying to come through the gap four abreast, and the flood was coming down on all of them equally, indifferent, magnificent. Sef had wedged himself between two boulders with his knife out and his father's journal pages stuffed inside his shirt, and he shouted the only thing he could think of that might stop men who wouldn't stop for water:

"The gate's open! The permit's worthless! Your buyer's buying a dry ditch!"

It worked better than reason would have. Two of them faltered. One looked back the way he'd come. The flood did the rest — it came around the bend like a brown wall, and the narrows filled to Sef's waist in seconds, and the men broke, scrambling for the ledges, dropping a crossbow into the churn, gone.

Which left Sef alone in the roaring gap, holding nothing, watching the gate.

He saw Noor reach the flume. Saw him swing the crowbar once, twice, against the chain anchoring the diversion wheel — and saw the chain hold, and Noor brace, and swing again, and again, small and stubborn against all that iron, and Sef understood suddenly what his father must have looked like, all those years ago, standing in this same gorge fighting the same fight and losing it.

"Noor!" The name tore out of him. "The wheel's pinned — go for the *pin*!"

Noor heard. Of course he heard; they'd been finishing each other's complaints for days. He shifted, hooked the crowbar under the great iron locking-pin at the wheel's hub, and threw his whole weight onto it.

The pin sheared. The wheel spun free with a shriek of metal, the chains whipped loose like cut tendons, and the flume — the throat, the theft, the whole rotten architecture of it — collapsed inward with a boom Sef felt through the water at his waist.

And the river, unthrottled for the first time in years, went through the broken flume like a fist.

Noor was still on the cliff face when it hit. Sef saw it happen — saw the surge catch his brother, tear the crowbar from his hands, and rip something else from him too: the compass, torn from its socket by the same convulsion, spinning up into the light, flashing brass-bright once against the sun.

Then the flood took it, and the flood took the flume, and the flood took the old defaced words and the new ones alike, and Sef stood hip-deep in the narrows with blood on his side and watched the River Qadash run full and free and furious down the canyon toward the dying villages — carrying his father's compass somewhere into the deep water, unrecoverable, unownable, given away at last.

Noor made it down to him ten minutes later, soaked, empty-handed, laughing in a way that sounded almost like sobbing.

"It's gone," Noor said.

Sef looked at the water rushing past them, brown and gold in the morning light, going where it pleased.

"Yeah," he said. "It is."

And downstream, in the villages that had forgotten how to hope, the first clean water of the new season was already arriving.
