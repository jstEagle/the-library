## 2. Six Tides of Nothing

The salt flats ended the way a lie ends — not all at once, but in a slow unraveling of detail until you realized the ground beneath you had stopped being anything at all.

Odile Coldharbour rode out of the white on the third morning, and the world began to forget itself around him.

For two days the flats had been honest country, if brutal: cracked pan stretching to a horizon so flat it seemed drawn with a rule, heat shimmering off the crust like breath on glass. He'd made good time there, following the sign Vess Corrane left behind her without meaning to — or meaning to, he could never decide which was worse. A scuffed heel-drag here where she'd climbed a rise of buckled salt. The bright thread of a torn sash-cord snagged on a fence post that hadn't seen a fence in forty years. Once, impossibly, a boot print pressed into a patch of clay too hard for rain to reach, small and sharp-edged, the tread pattern of a woman who walked light and bought her boots in the port districts. He had knelt over that print longer than he should have, the way a man kneels over a letter he's already memorized.

Now the pan gave way to something else. The ground turned from white to gray to a bruised lavender-gray, and the cracks underfoot changed character — no longer the polygonal shatter of dried mud but long, sinuous channels, branching, braided, running in every direction like the veins of a leaf the size of a province.

He reined up and looked at them, and his stomach did something quiet and wrong.

Those weren't cracks. Those were channels. Water-channels. Old ones, ancient ones, worn smooth as river stone, and they ran downhill in every direction at once, converging somewhere behind him and ahead of him and everywhere, the way water runs when there is an enormous amount of it and only one place for it to go.

*This is a seabed,* he thought, and then thought it again more slowly, because the first time through it hadn't stuck. *I am riding across the floor of an ocean.*

The maps said otherwise. Odile carried three maps in his saddlebag — the ranger survey, the port authority's coastal chart, and his mother's old road atlas, soft-cornered and ink-blotted, the one document of hers he'd never let Quill see. All three agreed on this stretch of country. All three called it the Verren Dry, marked it in the flat uninterested hatching reserved for places where nothing happened, and showed its southern edge terminating against the Corvel Escarpment some sixty miles on. No basin. No depression. No sea, dead or otherwise. Just nothing, politely labeled.

But the land under him sloped. It had been sloping since yesterday afternoon, so gently he'd told himself it was imagination, and now the slope was undeniable, and the channels were getting deeper, and the air —

The air was wrong. That was the thing he couldn't shake. There was a smell to it, faint but persistent, that did not belong to any desert he knew: iodine and cold stone and something greenly rotten, like kelp left in the sun. It came and went on gusts that had no business carrying such a smell, because there was nothing within a hundred miles to make it.

He checked his compass because checking his compass was what he did when the world misbehaved. It was a good instrument, brass-housed, compensated, the kind rangers trusted with their lives, and it had been lying to him since dawn.

Not lying, exactly. Spinning. When he held it level, the needle swung in a slow, dreamy circle, north to east to south to west, patient as a man counting sheep. When he set it down on a flat stone it would settle for a moment — always somewhere between northwest and northeast, never twice the same — and then drift off again like a drunk leaving a bar. He'd tapped it. He'd remagnetized the needle against his knife blade. He'd taken it apart at last night's camp and found nothing wrong with it at all, which was somehow worse than finding something.

A compass didn't fail in open country. A compass failed near iron, near ore bodies, near the buried bones of the earth — or near nothing at all, when the failure was in the man holding it.

He put it away and navigated by the sun instead, and by the channels, which all ran one way if you looked at enough of them together: south-southeast, toward whatever low place the land was hiding from the mapmakers.

Vess Corrane was heading into it deliberately. That was the thought that kept his spine straight through the morning. She wasn't lost; lost people wandered, doubled back, left trails that argued with themselves. Her trail ran straight as a plumb line, straighter than any trail had a right to run across broken ground, as though she knew exactly what she was going to find and exactly how far away it was. As though she were being *pulled.*

And Odile Coldharbour, ranger of the Verren district, holder of a commission signed in a port town that owed its existence to the salvage fleet, was following a thief into unmapped seabed because a debt told him to.

He thought about the debt the way you probe a sore tooth: briefly, involuntarily, and with dread.

Twelve years ago, in the flood year, a boy had gone through river ice outside Coldharbour's Crossing — not a river that should have been there either, that whole season had been full of water where water had no business being — and a stranger had gone in after him. The stranger had come back up with the boy and had nearly not come back up himself. Pneumonia after. Three weeks in the Coldharbour house, coughing blood into a rag while Odile's mother sat with him through the nights and Odile's father — no. Not his father. While *Corrane's* father sat with him, because it had been Corrane's boy under the ice, and the debt was Corrane's, and Corrane had spent the rest of his life paying it in the only currency he had left: service. Silence. Whatever the Coldharbour family asked.

His mother had died before she could spend much of it. His mother died and left a hole shaped like herself, and Harrow Quill had stepped into the hole with a compass and a smile and twelve years of patient instruction, and the debt had passed to Odile like a coat handed down.

Then Vess Corrane stole from a fleet warehouse, and the debt spoke with Odile's voice: *Bring her back. Bring her home. Her father bled for yours.* And Odile had ridden out into the white with a warrant in his pocket and resentment in his teeth, hating the yoke and wearing it anyway, because a man who wouldn't honor his debts was just a thief with better manners than Vess Corrane.

Except she wasn't running anymore. He'd understood that yesterday. Running thieves didn't leave clean trails into nowhere; running thieves went where the pursuit couldn't follow, and this — this drowned country that no map admitted to — was exactly that. Unless she wanted him to follow. Unless the trail was an invitation.

He touched his knee where the warrant sat in its oilskin sleeve and told himself, again, that it didn't matter what she wanted. Duty didn't require wanting.

The land disagreed. The land seemed to be saying that nothing here required anything except surrender.

* * *

By midafternoon the channels had become gullies and the gullies had become ravines, and he led the horse down into their company because the rim was breaking up and the going above was treacherous with hollows. Down below, the ground was different again — darker, finer, almost soft in places, and studded with things.

Shells.

He dismounted at the first of them without quite deciding to. It lay half-buried in the gray sediment, big as his spread hand, a fan-shaped thing ridged like a thumbprint, and when he worked it free the sediment fell away from its other side and it was whole. Unbroken. Whatever had killed it had done so gently, or quickly, or both.

There were thousands. Tens of thousands. They paved the ravine floors in drifts, white and gray and rust-red, mixed with the dark shapes of things he had no names for — coiled like horns, flat like coins, spired like church towers. An entire nation of creatures, exquisitely preserved, arranged in beds and banks exactly as they must have lived, and every one of them dead for longer than human memory.

He stood among them and felt the specific vertigo of a man whose profession is knowing where he is.

Rangers learned the land the way priests learned scripture. Odile could read a hillslope like a ledger: where the water went, where the fire would run, where the old roads slept under the grass. It was the one inheritance from his mother that had nothing to do with paper or seals — she'd taught him that the land never lied, that maps lied and men lied but dirt told the truth to anyone patient enough to dig. He'd built his whole life on that article of faith.

And now the land was lying to him. Or the maps were. One of the two, and he no longer knew which, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like swallowed river water.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not resolve: for this basin to hold a living sea — for these creatures to have lived and bred and died in their millions — the ocean must have reached a hundred leagues inland, past the Corvel Escarpment, past three mountain barriers that every chart in the port authority's office swore had never known surf. Either the sea had fallen out of the world since those charts were drawn, which was madness, or the charts had been drawn over a truth someone preferred forgotten, which was worse.

Someone had decided this place should not exist. Someone with the authority to keep it off the surveys, year after year, survey after survey. And Odile knew exactly which institution employed the surveyors of the Verren coast, and exactly whose signature authorized their charts, and the knowledge arrived in his stomach like a dropped anchor.

*The fleet.* The salvage fleet charted these waters. The fleet's hydrographers drew the maps he carried. The fleet's concession covered everything the maps showed and everything they conveniently didn't.

He looked south, down the throat of the ravine, toward the low place the land was hiding, and for the first time since the crossing he wondered whether he was chasing Vess Corrane at all — or whether she was simply the first domino, tipped years ago by hands he'd eaten dinner with.

* * *

He found her camp an hour before dusk, in the lee of a ridge of compacted clay that rose from the ravine floor like the hull of a buried ship. Which, he realized as he approached, was possibly exactly what it was — the ridge had a sheer face on its northern side, streaked with sediment layers, and a curve to it that no natural erosion produced. He filed the observation away and did not examine it closely, because examining it closely meant admitting how deep the water here had once been.

The camp was abandoned mid-meal.

That was the detail that raised the hair on his arms. A tin plate sat on a flat rock, holding the remains of a fish — an actual fish, silver-skinned, fresh, gills still pink. There was no water within a day's ride that he knew of. There was certainly no water that held fish. Beside the plate, a cup of something dark had been drunk half down. A small cookfire had been built with practiced economy and doused, not kicked apart — doused carefully, with sand, the way a careful person does it, the way a person does it who expects to come back.

Her bedroll was still there. Rolled, but present. A spare shirt hung on a stick like a scarecrow's ghost, drying. Her tracks led away from the camp toward the south and the deepening dark of the ravine system, walking pace, unhurried, and did not come back.

Odile crouched by the plate and did not touch anything for a long moment.

People abandoned camps in haste or in fear, and neither haste nor fear left a meal half-eaten and a bedroll rolled. People abandoned camps when they'd finished with them, and finished people took the fish and the cup and the shirt off the stick. This camp said: *I will return before dark.* This camp said: *I live here now.* This camp said, in the tidy grammar of a woman who cooked a fresh-caught fish in the middle of a dead ocean: *you have no idea where you are, ranger, and I do.*

The fish bothered him most. He turned the problem over while the light went copper and then violet. Fresh fish meant fresh water nearby, and fresh water meant life, and life meant the land was not as dead as its shells. Unless the fish hadn't come from freshwater at all. Unless there was something else feeding this basin from underneath, some artery the old sea had never entirely closed, and the tide-smell on the wind, the iodine and the cold stone, was not memory but rumor.

He made his own camp small and cold — no fire, not down here, not with the channels funneling every sound and scent toward the south — and ate hard bread and dried meat while the stars came out in numbers that startled him. With no settlements for a hundred miles, the sky burned. He found his bearings off them the way a certain man had taught him, years ago, kneeling in the dust behind the fleet barracks with a warm hand on his shoulder: *there, lad, that's your true north, and no magnet on earth can lie about it. Stars don't spin. Remember that when the needle fails you.*

*Remember that when the needle fails you.* As if Quill had known. As if he'd been preparing Odile for this country for thirty years without ever mentioning it existed.

Odile lay on his bedroll and watched the sky turn and did not sleep so much as sink, and in the gray hour before dawn a sound woke him fully, and he sat up with his hand on his knife and his heart going like a drum.

It was a long, low exhalation from the south. Rhythmic. Patient. Almost like breathing.

Almost exactly like breathing.

He stood in the violet dark and listened to the dead ocean inhale, and exhale, and inhale, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the land had not been lying to him at all.

It had been waiting.
