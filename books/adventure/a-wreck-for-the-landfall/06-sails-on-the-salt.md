## 6. Sails on the Salt

The sea came back the way a debt comes due: without warning, without mercy, and with interest.

Odile stood at the cave mouth and watched it fill the basin, brown-green water climbing the salt crust in slow, greedy licks. It had taken three hours from the first groan of the earth to this — a shallow sea where no sea should be, stretching to the horizon in every direction, holding the light like something cupped in two hands. The Meridian Lark lay behind him in the flooded dark, her timbers breathing barnacles, her nameplate green with two hundred years of patience.

He had not slept. He had tried, once, stretched against the warm stone with Vess Corrane sitting watch across the fire she'd built from driftwood that had no business existing this far inland — wood that smelled of tar and long voyages — but sleep would not take him. Every time he closed his eyes he saw the nameplate again. *Meridian Lark.* And beneath the awe, coiling tighter with each hour, a thought he could not shake:

His mother's compass pointed here. His mother's papers were here. His mother had known about this ship, this impossible ship, this sea that returned like a memory.

And if she had known, who else had?

The answer came at dawn. Or what passed for dawn, the sun hauling itself up through haze off the water, turning the basin into a sheet of hammered brass. Odile was standing at the rim of the cave's outer shelf, knee-deep in the new shallows, when Vess's voice cut across the water from above.

"Ranger. You'll want to see this."

She was crouched on the ledge where the cave opened onto the basin, shading her eyes with one hand. Odile waded toward her, boots squelching, and followed her gaze northward across the flood.

Sails.

Three of them, then four as the light strengthened — tall ships, square-rigged, running down the length of the basin before a wind that had also, apparently, come back with the sea. They moved with the unhurried confidence of vessels that knew exactly where they were going, hulls dark against the bright water, and even at this distance Odile could read the truth of them in their lines. These were not explorers. Not lost traders blundering into a drowned country. These were working ships, heavy in the water, craned and rigged for lifting.

Salvage ships.

"They found it," Vess said. Her voice was flat, but Odile had spent two weeks reading her across the salt flats, and he heard what lived under the flatness. "Or they always knew."

"Nobody always knows," Odile said. "This basin has been dry since before my grandmother was born."

"Has it?" She turned to look at him, and there was something almost gentle in the look, which frightened him more than contempt would have. "Your mother hid a ship here, ranger. Ships don't hide themselves. Somebody drew the charts. Somebody kept the tide-tables. Somebody waited."

Odile said nothing. The sails grew.

* * *

They watched from the high shelf inside the cave mouth, doused fire, quiet as stone. The lead ship came on until Odile could count the figures along her rail, could see the crane arm folded against her mast like a sleeping heron, could read — when she swung close enough to the cave's bearing — the name painted on her bow in letters the color of dried blood:

*Concession's Pride.*

The salvage fleet. The Coldharbour Concession Fleet, chartered by the port authority, licensed to work every wrecked vessel between the Teeth and the southern shallows. The fleet whose permits hung framed in every harbor office from here to the coast. The fleet his mother had —

His thoughts snagged on it, the way a boot snags on buried wire. The strongbox below bore his family name. The concession the fleet held over these waters — if there were waters — was exactly the kind of paper his mother had spent her life auditing, challenging, dragging into the light. She had died for less than that. Everyone in the district said so, though no one said it loudly.

And now the fleet was here, sailing on a sea that wasn't supposed to exist, aimed at a wreck that wasn't supposed to be found, and Odile did not believe in coincidence. He had stopped believing in it somewhere around the dried well, four hundred miles back, when he'd understood that Vess's trail had been left for him to find.

Below, the ships began to anchor. Chains roared out — he felt the sound in his chest through the rock. Boats dropped from davits. Figures moved with the crisp economy of people following orders they'd rehearsed.

"They'll dive at slack water," Vess murmured beside him. "First thing. Standard practice — you take your bearings while everything's still."

"You know a lot about salvage fleets."

"I know a lot about stealing things," she said. "It's adjacent."

* * *

They came ashore an hour later, and Odile stopped hiding, because hiding was pointless and because part of him — the part he didn't examine — wanted to be seen.

He walked down to the shingle where the fleet's boats were grounding and stood with his ranger's badge visible on his chest, the desert-worn tin of it catching the light, and waited. The first boat grounded. A bosun jumped out, big man, rope-scarred hands, face like a closed ledger. Behind him came divers in canvas suits, surveyors with slate boards, and a knot of armed men whose weapons were carried casually in the way of men paid to carry them often.

And then, from the stern of the second boat, stepping down onto the shingle of a sea that had been dust for a century, came Harrow Quill.

Time did something strange. The basin went silent — or Odile went deaf to it — and there was only the man walking toward him across twenty years.

Quill had aged the way good rope ages: grayed, hardened, more useful and more terrible. He was broad still, though the breadth had gone soft at the edges; his beard was iron-colored now instead of black; and he wore the fleet's colors with the same unselfconscious rightness he'd once worn a salvager's oilskins. But his walk was unchanged — that rolling, deliberate gait, a man planting each foot as though claiming the ground. Odile had learned to walk by copying that walk. Had learned to read stars by the man's blunt finger on his wrist, tilting his chin up. *There. That's the North Star, boy. Everything else moves. You find the thing that doesn't move, and you can't be lost.*

"Odile." Quill said his name the way he'd always said it — like setting down something heavy with care. "I wondered how far you'd chase that thief."

"You know her?"

"I know her reputation. Vess Corrane. Fastest fingers on the flats." Quill's eyes went past Odile's shoulder, found Vess on the rocks above, and held there a moment — an assessment, cold and complete — before returning. "I'm surprised she's still alive. I'm more surprised you are. This country eats people."

"The country was seabed," Odile said. "Yesterday it ate nobody."

Quill smiled. It was the old smile, the one from the kitchen of the waystation where Odile had grown up, the smile that meant *you've said something clever, boy, and I'm proud of you, and don't let it go to your head.* Twenty years dead, his mother, and Quill's smile could still put Odile at eleven years old, barefoot on a cool floor, safe.

That was the weapon. Odile saw it now, seeing it too late, seeing it clearly for the first time: all those years of safety had been building toward this. Every lesson, every meal, every night Quill had sat with him teaching knots and stars and patience — all of it had fitted Odile, the way a key is filed to fit a lock. And here was the lock, and here was the key, turning.

"There's a wreck in that cave," Quill said. "Two centuries old, by her timbers. The fleet holds concession rights to all salvage in this district. You know that better than most — your mother fought our charters her whole career." Something flickered through his face at the mention, quick as a fish under ice, gone before Odile could name it. "So whatever you think you've found here, son, it belongs to the permit. That's the law of it. Clean and simple."

"Nothing about this is simple," Odile said. "This basin was desert yesterday. Your concession covers coastal waters. Unless you'd like to show me the survey that says otherwise."

Something changed in Quill's face. Not much. A settling, like ballast shifting.

"Come aboard tonight," he said. "The *Pride*. Supper in my cabin, like old times. Bring the thief if she'll come — I'd rather talk than hunt." He put one huge hand on Odile's shoulder, and the weight of it was the weight of every year Odile had been glad to have him. "You look tired, boy. You look like a man carrying something too heavy. Let me carry some of it."

And God help him, some traitor part of Odile wanted exactly that — wanted to set it all down, the strongbox and the compass and the questions, wanted to climb into the warmth of that old certainty and sleep for a week.

"I'll come," he heard himself say. "Supper."

Quill squeezed his shoulder once and turned away, calling orders to the bosun — Tam Osk, the big man's name was, shouted up from the boat — and the world resumed its sounds: water, chain, gulls that had no reason to exist here crying overhead like accusations.

Vess materialized at Odile's elbow as the fleet's men spread along the shore. "Well," she said. "That was cozy."

"He raised me."

"I know. I did my research." She was watching Quill across the shingle with an expression Odile couldn't parse. "Which is why I'm going to say the thing you don't want to hear. He didn't find this basin by accident either. Nobody finds anything here by accident. Ask yourself what he knew, and when, and who told him."

"My mother's been dead twenty years."

"Yes," Vess said softly. "And the fleet's been sailing toward this sea for months. Do the arithmetic, ranger."

* * *

He did the arithmetic all afternoon, sitting alone on the high shelf with the flooded basin glittering below and the ships riding at anchor like patient animals. He did it while the fleet's divers went down at slack water and came up with reports. He did it while surveyors' flags bloomed along the shoreline, red and white, marking a territory into law.

His mother had audited the fleet's charters. His mother had died — officially of fever, officially mourned — within a season of filing her last challenge. Quill had appeared at the funeral with flowers and stayed afterward, unasked, and had never really left. Fourteen years of raising him. Fourteen years of stories about her: her stubbornness, her brilliance, her coldness, her abandonment of the boy for her work. *She loved her causes more than she loved anyone, Odile. Don't grow up to be a woman who loves a cause more than people.*

He had hated her a little, for years, on Quill's telling. He had loved Quill for giving him someone to hate that wasn't himself.

Now there was a strongbox below with his family name on it, and a compass that pointed to it, and a thief who claimed both had been sent to him — sent by whom? By his mother, dead two decades? Through what chain of hands, what debts, what patience? And the fleet was here, anchored on a sea that shouldn't exist, and its enforcer had greeted the discovery of an impossible wreck with the weary calm of a man closing out a job he'd started long ago.

The tide turned in the late afternoon. He felt it before he saw it — a change in the breathing of the water, the current at the cave mouth reversing — and watched the basin begin to drain, exposing the Lark's upper works inch by inch, black timber rising through the clearing water like a spine surfacing.

Six tides, Vess had said. She'd counted them off on her fingers the night before, crouched by the fire: six tides until the equinox water expired and the basin locked itself dry again, six tides until whatever legal machinery the fleet was already assembling made the wreck theirs forever. He hadn't understood then why she was counting. He understood now. Everything here was a countdown — the sea, the permits, the patience of the man who had taught him the stars.

Evening came. Lanterns bloomed along the *Concession's Pride*'s deck, and a boat put off from her side, pulling for the shore where Odile stood.

Supper in the great cabin, like old times.

He looked down at the wreck, half-raised from the water, her nameplate just breaking the surface — *Meridian Lark*, gleaming wet in the last light, as if the ship herself were watching to see what he would do. Then he looked at the approaching boat, and at the lantern-lit cabin waiting beyond it, and understood that he was walking into the one place on this water where he was most loved and least safe.

He climbed down to the shingle anyway.

Behind him, unseen in the cave's dark, the strongbox sat sealed in the captain's cabin, bearing his name, keeping his mother's secret — and somewhere above it, on the flagship, Harrow Quill was pouring wine into glasses and arranging his face into tenderness, the way a man arranges a net.
