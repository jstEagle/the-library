## 9. First Tide Lost

The tide came in on schedule, indifferent to what it carried.

Odile stood on the cave rim as the water rose through the basin, silvering the salt crust, climbing the ribs of the Meridian Lark until the wreck floated in her own drowned cathedral. It was a strange mercy, watching the sea reclaim her — as if the ship could be hidden again, kept safe by the same deep water that had betrayed her a hundred years ago. He knew better. The water wasn't hiding her. It was handing her over.

By mid-morning the fleet's ships had anchored in a loose crescent across the basin, hulls groaning at anchor, their shadows lying across the flooded cave mouth like bars. Divers went down in relays. He could see their bubbles rising in strings, silver beads strung upward through the green water, and every string that broke the surface seemed to take something with it.

He had slept two hours. He had spent those two hours drafting a document he'd written four times and torn up three.

*You have the ranger's authority,* he told himself. *You are the law on this land.*

The land, he was learning, was a technicality.

* * *

He went down to the waterline in his ranger's coat, the brass badge polished, the commission papers folded in an oilcloth wallet against his chest. Vess came with him, uninvited, unshakable. She had spent the night in the cave, sleeping in the wreck like a rat in a granary, and she walked the salt flats with the light, quick stride of someone who had already decided how she'd run if it came to running.

"They'll laugh at you," she said.

"Then they'll laugh."

"They'll laugh at you *legally.*"

He didn't answer. The water was lapping at his boots, cold through the leather, and the nearest fleet ship — a salvage brigantine called the *Iron Mercy*, all black tar and white gunwale — had lowered a work boat. Men were hauling a crate of tools down into it, and a bosun with a face like a closed fist stood in the stern calling numbers.

Odile waded out until the water was at his knees and raised the commission papers over his head.

"By authority of the Territorial Ranger Commission!" His voice carried flat across the water, swallowed by the basin's acoustics, and he heard it come back to him thin and strange, a voice in a jar. "This vessel is a site of historical and legal interest. All salvage operations will cease until a claim can be properly adjudicated!"

The work boat paused. The bosun turned, unhurried, and looked at him the way a man looks at weather.

"You the ranger?"

"I am."

The bosun considered this. Then he cupped his hands. "You want to come say that to the captain? Water's deep out here, ranger. Might get your papers wet."

There was laughter from the ship, faint, carried on the wind like gulls. Odile stood in the rising tide with his papers held high and felt the first cold thread of it — not the water, but the understanding. He had chased a thief across two hundred miles of salt. He had faced down a flooded cave and a hundred-year-old drowned ship and the fact of his own mother's hidden war against the fleet. He had not, until this moment, stood in water deeper than his boots and been laughed at.

He swam. It seemed important, somehow, to arrive wet.

* * *

The *Iron Mercy* took his papers, read them, and gave them back with coffee.

The captain was a woman named Brakka Voss, broad and gray, with hands like mooring line and the patient eyes of someone who had waited out storms, governments, and husbands. She read his commission twice. She read the salvage injunction he'd drafted — *cease and desist, pending adjudication of historical claim* — and she read it the way a jeweler reads a forgery.

"Ranger Coldharbour." She set the papers on her chart table and weighed them down with a brass divider. "Your commission covers the territory of the Saltreach District. Land, wells, roads, range stock, and the people on them. Am I right?"

"You are."

"Is this land?"

He looked through the stern gallery window. The basin lay below, flooded and glittering, the cave mouth a black seam under thirty feet of green water. "It was."

"Was." She nodded slowly, as if he'd made her point for her. "There's the trouble, ranger. The basin was seabed when the concession was drawn. The concession covers submerged land. The moment that water crossed your high-tide line last night—" she tapped the chart, where someone had already drawn a neat red boundary, "—you were standing in fleet water. Your jurisdiction ends at the waterline. It always did. You just never had a waterline before."

"The wreck is a grave," Odile said. "There are crew remains aboard. There are records. Under the Territorial Heritage Act—"

"Which applies to *territorial* land." She said it without cruelty. That was the worst of it. She said it the way you'd tell a man his wife was dead — gently, and with the matter already settled. "The fleet holds a valid concession. Signed, sealed, filed at the port authority. I've seen it. Have you?"

He hadn't. He'd seen Quill's copy, once, in the man's cabin aboard the flagship — a beautiful document, ribboned and waxed, granting the Salvage Fleet exclusive right to all wrecks in the basin "and such other waters as may be determined." He had been sixteen when Quill showed it to him, proud of it, the way you're proud of a father's sword.

"Adjudication can be requested," Odile said. "The port authority sits in emergency session when a heritage claim—"

"Ranger." Captain Voss leaned back. "I've stripped forty wrecks. Every one of them had someone standing on a shore with a paper. The paper is not the problem. The paper is never the problem." She pushed his commission back across the table. "The problem is that the sea doesn't care who you are, and the law follows the sea. Go home. Dry out. If the adjudication comes, we'll honor it. Until then—" she nodded toward the gallery window, where another string of bubbles rose, "—we work the tide."

He stood to leave, and she stopped him with something almost like kindness.

"Whoever taught you the Heritage Act taught it well. You cited the burial clause before the salvage clause. That's the order a lawyer uses, not a ranger." Her eyes were steady on his. "Someone wanted you ready for this."

He thought of Quill, teaching him law at the same chart table's twin, a thousand miles from here, his hand warm on Odile's shoulder. *Learn where your power ends, boy. That's where the real work begins.*

"Yes," Odile said. "Someone did."

* * *

They put him back ashore in the work boat, and he stood in the shallows watching the fleet strip his mother's ship.

They worked with terrible efficiency. A crane swung out over the cave mouth and lowered a grapple, and the divers below guided it with hand signals, and the first thing to come up was a bower anchor, crusted white, hauled dripping into the light and dropped on the deck with a boom that Odile felt in his teeth. Then a ship's bell, green with age. Then crates. Then more crates. A manifest clerk sat on the *Iron Mercy*'s deck with a ledger, checking items against a list that had been written a hundred years before the ship was found — because the fleet had always known what the Meridian Lark carried, or believed it did, and the list was the shape of their greed.

Vess stood beside him in the shallows, arms crossed.

"You're going to stand here all day," she said.

"Yes."

"Watching them take it."

"Watching them take what isn't theirs. Yes."

"They took your jurisdiction this morning. By afternoon they'll have taken the hold. By tomorrow the cabin." She turned to look at him, and her face was set in that sharp, unrepentant line he was beginning to read like a chart. "You're a patient man, ranger. I'm not. While you stand here learning helplessness, I'm going to go learn something useful."

"Vess—"

"Tam Osk," she said. "The bosun with the closed fist. He checked my name against a wanted list this morning and got the spelling wrong. A man who misspells a name he's been paid to remember is a man with a grievance. Grievances can be bought." She was already wading toward the rocks at the basin's edge, where the fleet had pitched its shore camp. "Don't follow me. You're a lawman. It would embarrass us both."

He watched her go. He should have stopped her. He was a ranger; she was a thief; the correct action was written into his commission in plain language. Instead he stood in the shallows with the water rising around his knees and let her walk away, and understood, in the place where understanding hurts, that the law had given him everything except a way to use it.

* * *

The tide turned at dusk.

He knew the moment it happened — the whole basin seemed to exhale, the water going slack and glassy, and the fleet's work slowed with it, the divers recalled, the crane stilled. Six tides, the permits had said. Six tides of salvage before the concession finalized and the wreck became fleet property past any appeal. He had arrived with six. He had spent the day learning that the law was a shoreline, and shorelines moved.

He climbed to the cave rim and sat with his back against the stone, above the waterline, above the fleet's lanterns, above their red boundary line on their neat chart. Below him the Meridian Lark lay in the slack water like a held breath. They had taken her anchor and her bell. By morning they would take her cargo. By the next tide, her bones.

*They'll take the strongbox,* he thought, and the thought went through him like a hook. *The cabin comes down tomorrow. They'll take the strongbox, and they'll open it, and whatever my mother died to hide will be in Harrow Quill's hands by the day after.*

Unless it wasn't a strongbox that opened with hands.

He thought of the compass in Vess's coat — his mother's compass, stolen and carried and finally given back, its needle swinging to point not north but *down*, always down, toward the wreck, toward the cabin, toward whatever his mother had locked and meant for him alone. A lock keyed to a compass. A key that looked like nothing. A key that a salvage crew would haul up in a crate of brass instruments and never once look twice at.

The fleet had a manifest. The fleet had a list. The fleet did not have the compass.

He sat in the dark and let the helplessness finish its work, the way a fever finishes, the way a tide finishes — not destroying him but turning him. He had spent the day learning the exact dimensions of his cage: the waterline, the concession, the six tides, the old man's signature on the old man's beautiful paper. Fine. A man who knows his cage knows its bars. And a man who knows its bars knows which ones are farthest from the keeper's chair.

Below him, the fleet's lanterns burned. The divers slept in their hammocks, dreaming of salvage shares. Tam Osk's work boat knocked against the flagship's hull with the small, patient rhythm of water against wood.

Five tides left. The next one came in at dawn.

He would not be standing on the shore when it did.

* * *

He found Vess after midnight, at the edge of the fleet's shore camp, crouched in the shadow of a salt-crusted boulder. She had the look of someone who had spent the evening listening, and the particular stillness of someone who had heard something she didn't like.

"You're supposed to be on the shore," she whispered, "being helpless."

"I'm done with it. What did you hear?"

She hesitated. He watched her weigh it — the old thief's arithmetic, trust against survival — and watched something in her decide, for the second time, to bet on him.

"Osk talked to the mate on the *Iron Mercy*," she said. "The cabin comes up tomorrow, second tide. Everything in it. Manifest says 'strongbox, iron, one' — and there's an order, ranger. A written order from Quill himself. The strongbox doesn't go to the manifest clerk." Her voice dropped. "It goes to the flagship. To Quill's own cabin. Nobody else touches it."

Odile looked out at the dark water, at the lanterns, at the flagship riding her anchor with her stern gallery lit gold — the one lit window, the one he knew, the one that had been lit above him every night of his childhood while a man who loved him read law by lamplight.

"He knows what's in it," Odile said.

"He knows what he *put* in it." Vess's eyes were bright in the dark. "Which means we have until the second tide tomorrow to get into that cabin before he does. Or—" she smiled, thin and sharp, "—we get there first, and we learn what your mother locked, and we learn it with the key in our own hands."

The tide turned in the dark below them. Five tides now. The water began, softly, to rise.

"Then we'd better not waste the night," Odile said, "learning anything else."

He started down toward the water, and Vess fell in beside him, and behind them the fleet slept on, dreaming in its lantern-light, one tide poorer and not yet knowing it.
