## 10. Bargain with the Bosun

Vess Corrane had stolen many things in her life, but never a conversation. She was finding it harder than lockwork.

The salvage fleet's flagship, *Iron Meridian*, rode at anchor over the drowned basin like a knife held above a throat, and its decks were loud with men who had no reason to speak to a salt-dusted woman in a borrowed oilskin. So she listened instead. She had been listening for two days — to the riggers complaining about the diving shifts, to the cook's boy cursing the galley stove, to the slow grind of the windlass hauling up timbers and brass and whatever else the *Meridian Lark* was willing to give back after forty years under a sea that had abandoned it. And threaded through all of it, one name repeated like a knot in rope: Tam Osk. Bosun. Twenty years in the fleet. Passed over for quartermaster twice.

A man passed over twice was not a man you bought. He was a man you *found*.

She found him on the third evening, at the rail of the lower deck where the lantern light didn't quite reach, drinking something from a tin cup and watching the water of the temporary sea go black. Up close he was broader than she'd guessed, with forearms like mooring posts and a face that had been weathered past anger into something flatter and more durable. A scar split his left eyebrow. His hands, wrapped around the cup, were the hands of a man who had spent decades making rope do what rope did not want to do.

"You're the ranger's shadow," he said without looking at her. "The thief."

"I'm the woman buying you a drink." She set a second cup on the rail between them — real spirits, smuggled up from the port town in her pack, the good kind that burned going down and kept burning after. "The fleet's is vinegar."

He looked at the cup. Looked at her. Did not take it, which she respected; only fools took things quickly.

"Quill knows you're aboard," Osk said.

"Quill knows I'm *somewhere*," Vess said. "There's a difference, and the difference is called plausible deniability, and it's the most valuable cargo either of us will ever carry." She let that sit. "You were passed over for quartermaster."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, then buried. "Fleet business is fleet business."

"Fleet business is Quill's business," she said. "Everyone else just bleeds for it. How many dives did your people make today?"

"Fourteen."

"And how many came up with anything worth fourteen?"

His jaw worked. "The hold's mostly stripped. What's left is ballast stone and rot. We're pulling planking now. Planking." He spat over the rail, into the water that shouldn't have existed and would be gone again within the week. "Forty years this ship sat in the dark, and we're rendering her down for deck timber. Like she's a fence post."

"So you love her."

"I don't love ships." But he said it too fast, and they both heard it. He took the cup after all, drank, and grimaced at the quality — which meant it was exactly as good as she'd claimed. "What do you want, thief?"

"Access to the hold. The lower compartments. The ones the divers haven't opened yet."

"The sealed ones." He turned to look at her fully for the first time, and his gaze was a surveyor's: measuring, pricing. "Those are locked by order of Quill himself. Not fleet seal. *His* seal. You know what that means?"

"It means there's something in them he doesn't want found before he's ready."

"It means there's something in them he doesn't want found *ever*," Osk said, "and every man who asks about them gets reassigned to the pump crews, and every man who gets reassigned stops asking, because the last one who didn't—" He stopped. Drank again. "There was an incident. Two seasons back, on the *Charity's Grace*. A man went into a sealed compartment off-schedule. Came up talking about papers. Three days later he was transferred inland and nobody's seen him since."

Vess felt the cold come up through the deck plates and into her boots. She'd known Harrow Quill by reputation long before Odile had spoken his name with that particular mixture of love and dread — the enforcer whose permits were always in order, whose accidents were always someone else's fault. But reputation was a portrait. This was the sitter, breathing.

"And you're telling me this why?" she asked. "Free of charge?"

"Because you're paying," Osk said flatly. "And because twenty years ago I signed articles under a captain who believed a ship belonged to her crew, not to whoever held the paper. If that belief makes me a fool, then I've been a fool a long time, and I'm too old to change trades." He set down the cup. "What are you offering?"

Vess had spent the last two days calculating what she could afford to lose. The answer was nearly everything, which made the arithmetic simple. She named a figure in hard coin — half now, half when she was out of the hold — and watched him not blink.

"That's a year's wages," he said.

"It's a year of not being transferred inland," she said. "Prices vary."

*

He came for her at moonset, when the flagship slept in the way large ships sleep — never wholly, always creaking, but with the watch thinned and the officers below. He brought her a diver's spare suit that smelled of tar and other men's sweat, and led her down through the ship's belly to the well deck where the diving operations launched, past racks of helmets and air lines coiled like sleeping snakes.

"No line," he said, handing her the suit. "No tender. You go down on a weighted stage and you come up when the bell rings twice. If you're not up by the third ring, I cut the stage loose and you become part of the wreck. Those are terms."

"Generous," Vess said. "Most men would at least pretend to hesitate."

"I hesitated for two days. That was the pretending." He checked the clasps on her harness himself, rough and thorough, a bosun's hands doing a bosun's work. Then, as he cinched the last buckle, he said quietly: "Compartment C-4. Aft of the main hold, behind the false bulkhead. The seal on the hatch is Quill's personal wax — black with a red thread through it. Don't break the seal if you can help it. Lift the hatch on its hinges. Wax seals can be reset. Broken ones can't."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since the fleet anchored here," Osk said. "Now listen, because I'll say this once and then I'll be a man who never said it." He gripped her shoulder, and his hand was heavy enough to feel like a decision. "The compartment was sealed *before* the fleet ever raised anchor. The harbor master at Corvane Port keeps records of charter cargoes — I checked, quiet, through a cousin. The *Meridian Lark*'s final manifest lists her as chartered fleet tonnage. Fleet. Forty years ago. Which means whatever's in that compartment went down with the knowledge and consent of the fleet that now claims to be salvaging her by right of discovery." His voice dropped further. "You don't discover what you already own, thief. Unless you need the world to think you never owned it at all."

The words went into Vess like a fishhook. She thought of the strongbox in the captain's cabin, Coldharbour stamped in fading gilt. She thought of Odile, sleeping — or not sleeping — somewhere on the flagship, playing the obedient prisoner, wearing compliance like a mask she could almost see through. She thought of a dead woman who had paid a debt across decades to bring her son to this exact patch of drowned ground.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and hated how much she meant it, because gratitude was a leash and she'd worn enough leashes.

"Don't thank me. Get down there and get up again." He rang the first bell himself.

*

The descent was a descent into a lung.

Vess had dived before — pearl beds off the southern coast, once, badly — but this water was different. It was young water, newly returned, still learning how to be a sea, and it carried the taste of salt flats and mineral dust along with the brine. Her lamp threw a cone of trembling gold ahead of her, and the *Meridian Lark* rose out of the darkness to meet it like a cathedral collapsing in slow motion.

Forty years underwater and still she was beautiful. That was the thing no one warned you about wrecks: they didn't die so much as change trades. The hull's great ribs arched overhead, furred with white filaments of new growth, and the flooded decks stretched away into gloom, and everywhere there was the patient industry of small creatures turning wood into reef. Fish scattered from her lamp in silver consternation. Somewhere below, the fleet's day-shift damage still gaped — planking torn away, hatches wrenched open, the wounds of men in a hurry.

She swam aft, following Osk's directions, counting bulkheads. The main hold yawned around her, stripped near bare, its ballast stones shifted into gray dunes. And there, at the aft end, behind a partition the salvors had cut around rather than through — she saw why immediately, and her blood went colder than the water —

— because the partition wasn't original. It was newer than the wreck. Newer by decades. Planted, grown-over, disguised, but a *false bulkhead*, exactly as Osk had said, built into a ship that had supposedly sunk by accident, sealing off a compartment that had supposedly never existed.

Behind it, a hatch. On the hatch, a seal of black wax threaded with red, intact, unbroken, glowing faintly in her lamplight like a wound that had learned patience.

Vess hung in the black water before it, and for a moment she wanted, with a purity she hadn't felt since childhood, simply to be elsewhere. Anywhere. A dry room, a locked door, a name that wasn't hers. Because she understood now — not everything, but enough — and understanding was a weight, and the water pressed it down upon her from all sides. Whatever was behind this hatch, Harrow Quill had killed to keep it sealed. Had perhaps sunk a ship to keep it sealed. And she was about to put her hands on it.

*Odile's mother put it here,* she told herself. *Or near here. Or knew of it. Either way, she chose you — or chose your father — or chose the debt. You're not trespassing. You're delivering.*

It helped. It helped the way a plank helps a drowning man.

She lifted the hatch on its hinges, careful as a prayer, the seal stretching but holding, and swung herself through into the compartment beyond.

Inside was dry.

Not damp — *dry*, sealed so perfectly against four decades of absent ocean that the air within still tasted of tar, of hemp, of the ghost of some spice long unloaded. Her lamp showed crates stacked in careful rows, their stenciling faded but legible: fleet marks, fleet numbers, a charter code she didn't recognize but recognized the *shape* of. And at the far end, a writing desk bolted to the deck, its drawers sealed with the same black wax, and atop the desk, weighted under a brass bar—

Papers. Dozens of them. She couldn't read them all, not here, not now, with the bell waiting and her air gauge ticking down like a small impatient heart. But she read the topmost page, and the topmost page was enough.

It was a countersigned charter. The *Meridian Lark*, fleet tonnage, sailing under orders — and at the bottom, in a hand she'd seen on forged concessions and permit copies and a hundred documents that had ruined smaller people than her:

*Harrow Quill.*

Dated forty years ago. When Quill had been, by every public record, a junior clerk who had never set foot on salt water.

Vess's breath rasped in the helmet. The compartment seemed to tilt around her, though the water outside was still. He hadn't forged the concession recently. He hadn't even forged it cleverly. He had *been there*. He had signed the ship onto her course, and the ship had gone down, and he had grown old and powerful on the strength of whatever those orders had been — and now the fleet's own paper trail was rotting quietly in a box he'd ordered blown apart rather than opened.

*Blown apart.*

The thought arrived whole, the way the worst thoughts do, and she seized Osk's sleeve of memory: *the last man who asked got transferred inland.* Transferred. Not silenced with a knife in an alley — transferred, bureaucratically, tidily. A man like that did not leave witnesses floating. A man like that did not leave *wrecks* floating either. Once the stripping was done — once every scrap of value was hauled up and the permits finalized and the world's attention moved on — what happened to a hull full of evidence at the bottom of a basin that would soon be dry land again?

She was moving before she finished the thought, stuffing the topmost papers into the waterproof wallet strapped inside her suit, taking only what she could carry and memorizing the rest — the crate codes, the desk, the wax, the arrangement — because there would need to be a second dive, a third, a proper recovery with witnesses, and none of that would happen if she drowned here being greedy.

She was halfway back through the hatch when her lamp swept the compartment's outer wall one last time — and stopped.

Fixed to the inside of the false bulkhead, packed in rows with the neatness of a man who took pride in his craft, were charges. Demolition charges, marine-grade, waxed against the water, wired together into a single circuit that ran up through the deck and away toward the fleet anchorage above.

They had not been placed yesterday. They had not been placed this season. The growth on their casings matched the growth on the bulkhead itself.

They had been placed *forty years ago* — spares, backups, the thoroughness of a man who left nothing to chance — and they had waited all this time for a signal that was surely already loaded into some detonator on the *Iron Meridian*, patient as their owner, ready to turn the *Meridian Lark* and everything she still held into drifting splinters and honest fog.

Vess swam for the surface as if the sea itself had turned against her, which, in every way that mattered, it had.

She broke water beside the well deck gasping, and Osk hauled her up by the harness with one arm, and the moment her helmet came off she grabbed his wrist and said, low and fast: "How much strip-work remains? Honest answer."

"Two days' diving. Maybe three." His eyes narrowed at her face. "What did you find?"

"A schedule," she said. "And a funeral. Osk — the moment the last crate comes up, Quill fires the charges. There are explosives in that hull older than half your crew. He's going to sink her a second time, and this time he's going to make sure there's nothing left to find." She pulled the waterproof wallet from inside the suit, felt the papers within it, hard and real and nowhere near enough. "We don't have six tides. We might not have three."

Above them, the flagship's bells marked the middle watch, soft and indifferent, and somewhere on her quarterdeck a light still burned in the enforcer's cabin — steady, unhurried, keeping its own count of the tides remaining.

Vess looked at it, and then at the black water, and understood with terrible clarity that the next move was not hers to make alone.

She needed the prisoner.
