## 13. Betrayal at Low Water

Tam Osk had spent thirty years learning the weight of other men's secrets, and he had learned this much: a secret was never heavy in your hands. It was heavy in the carrying, and heavier still when you set it down.

He stood at the rail of the *Charterhouse*, the fleet flagship, watching the basin go dark below him. The water had pulled back for the third tide since the divers started work, and the Meridian Lark lay exposed again like something gutted — her ribs bare to the moonlight, salvage lamps strung along her spine so the whole wreck glowed like a festival gone wrong. Men moved through her with pry bars and cargo nets. They were taking everything. They had been told there was everything worth taking.

Tam knew better now.

He turned his hands over in the lamplight. Bosun's hands, rope-scarred, salt-cured, honest as any hands on the water had ever been until four days ago when a thief with quick eyes and quicker fingers had slid a purse across a crate of chain and asked him for one small thing: a door left unlocked, an hour of dark, no questions.

He'd taken the purse. That wasn't the sin. A bosun on a fleet ship took purses the way gulls took scraps; it was practically liturgy. The sin came later, standing watch over the hold while two shadows slipped past him into the captain's cabin, and Tam had looked at his boots and thought about nothing at all, which is how a man sins best.

And then he'd heard them talking through the bulkhead. Heard the name Coldharbour spoken soft as a prayer. Heard papers rustle. And understood, with the slow cold certainty of a man feeling a deck tilt under him, that whatever was in that strongbox was worth more than every purse in the world, and that he was now standing between it and Harrow Quill, which was a place men did not stand twice.

* * *

Quill received him in the great cabin at midnight, because Quill received everyone at midnight; Tam suspected the man slept in daylight like a bat and did his loving in the dark.

The cabin smelled of cedar and lamp oil. Charts covered the table — the basin, the wreck, the concession boundaries drawn in fresh ink — and above them hung a brass orrery, its little planets frozen mid-orbit. Quill sat beneath it with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, and he looked up at Tam with those pale eyes that had made grown sailors apologize for breathing too loud.

"Bosun Osk," he said. "You wanted to see me."

"I have information," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than his hands felt. "About the Coldharbour boy. And the woman."

Something moved behind Quill's face — not surprise. Tam had expected surprise. What he saw instead was worse: a kind of patience, the look of a man who has been waiting for a specific knock on a specific door.

"Sit down, Tam."

Tam sat. The chair was real oak, salvaged off some drowned gentleman's yacht, and it was more comfortable than anything Tam had owned in his life, and he hated how much that mattered to him right now.

"Before you speak," Quill said, folding his hands, "let me tell you what I already know, so you can measure what you're selling me against what I'm buying." He didn't wait for agreement. "The girl came aboard my ship disguised as a chandler's runner eleven days ago. She stole a key impression from my second mate. She bribed you — I don't know the figure, but I know you, Tam, and I know your vices are modest, so let's say forty crowns. Last night she and Odile Coldharbour entered the captain's cabin of the Lark through the lazarette hatch, which someone left unbarred, and they were inside for fifty-one minutes by my chronometer. When they came out, the boy was carrying a document case against his chest like a man carrying a child out of a fire."

The cabin was very quiet. Somewhere below, the hull ticked and groaned as the tide turned.

"So," Quill said gently. "Tell me something I don't know."

Tam's mouth had gone dry. "There's a second copy."

Now the pale eyes sharpened, just barely, like a blade coming half out of a sheath.

"The woman kept something back," Tam said. "I heard them argue about it, low, before they went in. She said —" he closed his eyes, dragged the words up from memory — "she said *the compass keeps what the box can't*. He didn't understand her. Neither did I. But she wouldn't say more, and they went in anyway, and when they came out she was lighter by one compass and he was heavier by one case, and neither of them looked happy about either."

Quill was silent for a long moment. Then he rose, crossed to the stern windows, and stood looking down at the black water where the wreck waited.

"Do you know what I'll do to them when I catch them?" he said, not turning around.

"No, sir."

"Neither do I." He said it mildly, almost thoughtfully, the way another man might wonder aloud about the weather. "That's the truth of it, Tam. I've planned this operation down to the last rivet — permits, tides, divers, demolition charges sitting in the Lark's bilge right now, waiting for their paperwork to clear. Every contingency but the one where I have to decide what Odile Coldharbour is worth to me. I keep hoping I won't have to find out." He turned. "You're going to help me make sure I don't have to."

"How?"

"By telling me where they've gone to ground. The ranger has a cave he uses on the east rim — I've known about it since he was nine years old, which tells you something about how long I've been preparing, doesn't it? But caves have two mouths and boys have clever friends, and I'd rather not send armed men blundering into the dark after the only person on this earth whose life I give a damn about." His voice softened on the last words, and that softness frightened Tam more than any threat could have. "So. Where are they?"

Tam looked at his hands again. Forty crowns in his pocket. Thirty years of honest rope-work behind him. And ahead of him, if he answered wrong, either a dead boy or a dead bosun, and no way to be sure which answer bought which.

"They're not in the cave," he heard himself say. "They're under the Lark. In the ballast crawl. There's a gap behind the stone ballast where the frame's sprung — big enough for two, dry at low water. The girl found it her first week aboard. She's been sleeping there when she wasn't stealing from us." He swallowed. "She told me because she needed me to keep the divers out of that section tomorrow. Said the frame was unsound. Paid me for that too."

Quill nodded slowly. "And the documents. Where do they keep them?"

"On the boy. Always on the boy. She argued for splitting them up and he wouldn't hear of it. Said his mother trusted him with them and he wasn't going to scatter her trust across two hiding places like loose change."

For the first time, Harrow Quill smiled, and it was the saddest thing Tam Osk had ever seen on a living man's face.

"Of course he wouldn't," Quill said softly. "She raised him the same way I tried to. All spine and sentiment, God help us both." He came back to the table, opened a drawer, and set a purse on the oak — a real purse, heavy, far heavier than forty crowns — and beside it a folded paper bearing the port authority's seal. "Your pardon, Tam. Full immunity, signed and countersigned. Whatever you did, whatever you took, whatever you let happen — it never happened. You're a free man with a clean slate and a pension."

Tam stared at it. "And what does it cost me besides them?"

"It costs you nothing," Quill said. "That's the trick of it. They chose to break into my ship and steal from my concession. Everything that happens next is the sea's doing, or the law's, or theirs. You're just a man who remembered his duty." He pushed the paper across. "Take it. Take the money. Go back on watch and be surprised tomorrow morning like everyone else."

Tam took it. His hand didn't shake until afterward, walking back down the companionway, when he realized he was humming — some old shanty his father used to sing, mindless, cheerful — and couldn't stop.

* * *

They came at slack water before dawn, when the basin held its breath between tides and even the water seemed to be holding still.

Tam stood at the rail and watched it happen, because he had earned the right to watch, or lost the right to look away — he couldn't tell which anymore. Quill went himself. Of course he did. No boatswain's mates, no enforcers with lanterns and irons. Just Harrow Quill in his long coat, stepping down into a skiff with two men pulling quiet oars, crossing the black mirror of the basin toward the glowing ribs of the Meridian Lark.

Tam tracked the skiff by its wake, a thin silver seam closing behind it. The salvage lamps along the wreck threw long shadows, and the figures moving through them were small and slow, and then they stopped being visible at all, swallowed by the hull, and Tam found he was gripping the rail hard enough to hurt.

It took less than an hour.

When the skiff came back, the sky had gone the color of pewter. Odile Coldharbour sat in the stern with his wrists bound in front of him — cord, not iron, Tam noted, and hated himself for noting it, for reading mercy in the choice of restraint. His coat was buttoned wrong. His face was pale and set and terribly young, and he was looking at Quill with an expression Tam had seen once before, years ago, on a drowning sailor looking up from the water: not hatred, not fear, but the awful arithmetic of a man realizing the person reaching down toward him is the reason he fell.

Vess Corrane wasn't in the skiff.

"She ran," one of the oarsmen reported. "Went into the flats on foot at first light. We'll have her by noon."

"No," Quill said, climbing aboard. "Let her run. She's a thief; thieves survive. I want her tired and far away and unimportant." He glanced at the bound man. "See? Nothing happened to you. Nobody touched you. I want that understood, Odile. By you and by whoever asks later."

"You searched him," Odile said. His voice was hoarse, scraped raw by the night in the ballast crawl. "You took the case."

"I confiscated stolen property from a theft in progress. The port authority will confirm my authority to do so." Quill said it without heat, like reciting a chart legend. Then, quieter, only for the boy: "You should have come to me. When you found it. You should have walked onto this deck and put it in my hands and said *Uncle Harrow, explain this to me*, and I would have explained, and we would have worked out what to keep and what to burn together, the way we always have. Instead you went into the dark with a stranger and a grudge."

"You lied to me my whole life," Odile said. "About my mother. About how she died. About what she was."

"I protected you."

"You buried her."

The words dropped into the morning like a stone into deep water. Tam watched Quill take them — watched the man's whole body absorb the blow and refuse to bend, the way a mast takes a gust it was built for.

"Take him below," Quill said at last, turning away. "The good cabin. Lock it from the outside. Feed him properly." He paused at the companionway, his back to them all, and said, so quietly Tam nearly missed it: "Four tides, Odile. At the sixth, the permits finalize and none of this matters anymore. Be easy. It's almost over."

* * *

They put Tam back on watch, because that was part of the message too — everyone would know by noon what the bosun had done, and everyone would know what it bought, and everyone would learn the lesson Quill wanted taught: that loyalty to Harrow Quill paid, and betrayal of him paid better, and that both prices were set by the same hand.

Tam stood his watch. The tide came in over the basin, green-brown and patient, climbing the wreck's ribs the way it had climbed them for a hundred years, erasing the lamps and the ladders and the divers' marks, laying the Meridian Lark back under her blanket of water. Four tides, Quill had said. Tam counted them the way a condemned man counts stairs.

Below decks, in the good cabin, a boy was locked up with nothing but his grief and his mother's name. Out on the flats, a thief was running with a compass full of secrets toward a horizon nobody had mapped. And in the strongroom of the *Charterhouse*, in a document case against a wall, lay the proof that Harrow Quill had forged a concession, scuttled a ship, and murdered a crew — proof that would burn at the sixth tide along with everything else, because Quill did not leave evidence behind, and Tam suddenly understood, with a lurch like a deck dropping away, that the demolition charges in the Lark's bilge were only half the plan.

The other half was fire, and paper, and a clean slate.

Tam Osk looked down at his clean, pardoned, purchased hands, and for the first time in thirty years of honest rope-work and one dishonest night, he began to pray — not for forgiveness, which he hadn't earned, and not for courage, which he'd already spent, but for time. Four tides. Enough to hang a secret. Enough, maybe, if a man moved fast and prayed hard, to raise one.
