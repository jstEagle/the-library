## 14. Prisoner of the Permits

They gave him a cabin with a view.

That was the first thing Odile understood about his captivity: it was designed to be admired. The *Coriander's Wager* was the fleet's flagship, a black-hulled leviathan moored at the edge of the flooded basin, and the cabin they'd assigned him sat on the port side, one deck above the waterline, with a broad window looking out across the drowned desert toward the cave mouth where the *Meridian Lark* lay bleeding her secrets into other men's hands. From this window he could watch the divers go down. From this window he could count everything they took.

A prisoner with a view is a prisoner being shown something.

He stood at the glass on the first morning and watched the salvage lighters cluster around the wreck like gulls on a carcass. Cranes swung. Lines creaked. Figures in canvas diving suits went down into the green-brown water in pairs and came up hauling crates, timbers, brass fittings, anything with weight or worth. The tide moved through the basin twice a day, slow and patient as a clerk counting coin, and between tides the fleet worked the wreck like a wound being packed.

Four tides left. He'd heard the bosuns say it on the gangway when they brought him aboard — said it casually, the way men talk about weather. Four tides until the permits finalized. After slack water of the fourth, the *Meridian Lark* would belong to the salvage fleet entire, hull and hold and history, and every document Harrow Quill had taken from him would be ash by supper.

Odile pressed his palm flat against the cool glass and made himself look, really look, at what was being done to his mother's ship.

Then he turned away and began to learn the ship instead.

* * *

The trick, he told himself, was to be exactly what they expected. A ranger beaten. A man whose jurisdiction had ended at the waterline and who knew it. A guest, not a captive — because Quill had been careful never to use that word, not once, not even in private. *You'll stay aboard the Wager for your own safety,* Quill had said, hand warm on Odile's shoulder, eyes cold as ballast. *These waters are crowded with men who'd rather you weren't here.*

So Odile ate in the wardroom when invited. He thanked the stewards. He asked intelligent questions about the fleet's operations with the dull politeness of a man making conversation because he had nothing else. And all the while, beneath the compliance, he was sharpening himself on every detail the ship offered, the way a whetstone gives an edge to steel without either party meaning to.

By the third day he knew the *Wager*'s rhythm better than some of her crew.

He knew the tide schedule was posted outside the purser's office and updated at each change: high water at 0640 and 1905, low at 1240 and 0030, give or take the storm building somewhere south that made the barometer twitch. He knew the divers went down on the morning tide and again after noon, two shifts, and that the afternoon shift always ran short because the bosun Tam Osk — quiet, gray-eyed, with a burn scar roping his left forearm — argued with the mate about air tanks that were older than sin. He knew the paymaster came aboard by launch every second day, which meant the crew drank hard the night before payday and harder the night after. He knew the flagship's skiff was kept davited on the starboard side, aft, under a canvas cover, and that the boy who tended it was lazy about the lashing.

He knew these things the way his mother had taught him to know country: not by staring at any one thing, but by letting the whole pattern settle into him like sediment. She used to say a landscape told you everything if you refused to blink. He'd thought she meant deserts. He understood now she'd probably meant men like Harrow Quill.

And there was the other thing. The thing he turned over in the dark hours like a stone warmed by a fire.

Quill had taken the documents. All of them, so far as Odile knew — the charter countersigned in Quill's own young hand, the crew manifest, the survey notes proving the *Lark* had been scuttled deliberate, the whole paper corpse of the forgery, lifted from the captain's cabin of the wreck along with the strongbox's contents. But his mother had hidden those papers once, in a ship sunk a thousand miles from salt water, behind a lock keyed to a compass. A woman who hid a thing that well did not hide it only once. Odile had grown up in her silence; he knew its architecture. There was always a second door in a house she built.

He didn't know where the copy was. But he knew Quill didn't know either — and that knowledge was worth more than the documents themselves. He'd watched Quill's face across the wardroom table, watched the enforcer's famous charm run smooth over a certain hollowness. Quill had the papers, yes. But Quill was still searching. Odile had seen it in the way the man questioned the divers, in the orders passed down about searching the wreck's remaining sealed compartments *thoroughly*, in the flicker of irritation when the bosun reported nothing new. A man with everything in his fist doesn't keep digging.

Somewhere, his mother had left a second truth. And somewhere out in the flats, a thief who owed her own debts was still free.

* * *

On the fifth day, Quill came to dinner.

It was a performance, both of them knowing it, neither saying so. The wardroom was polished teak and silver plate, the fleet's money worn comfortably, and Quill presided over it like a priest over a generous god. He'd dressed in his shore coat, deep blue, the salvage fleet's compass rose stitched in silver at the collar. He looked, Odile thought, exactly like what he'd spent thirty years pretending to be: an honest man grown old in honest work.

"You're eating well," Quill observed, pouring wine he did not drink himself. "Good. I worried, the first days. You had that look your mother used to get."

"Don't," Odile said quietly.

Quill set the bottle down. For a moment the charm thinned and something older showed through — something that might have been genuine grief, or a very old habit of wearing it. "I found her, you know. At the end. Or near it. She sent for me." He turned his glass by the stem. "She wasn't repentant, if that's what you hope. She was *amused*. Right up to the last week, she was amused."

"What did she want?"

"For me to tell you she loved you. Which I did." Quill smiled, and it reached his eyes the way a knife reaches bone. "Eventually."

Odile ate. He kept his hands steady. Inside, he laid the moment down among the others — the schedule, the skiff, the barometer falling, the air tanks, the boy too lazy to lash the canvas — another piece of the pattern, another grain against the stone. Quill wanted him angry. Anger made a man stupid, and stupid men did desperate things in front of witnesses. So Odile gave him nothing but appetite and courtesy, and watched Quill's patience fray by a thread's width each night.

"The hearing convenes at the port authority the day after final slack water," Quill said, as if discussing cargo rates. "Formality. The concession stands, the wreck passes to the fleet, and you'll be free to go back to your flats and your thieves." He leaned forward. "Or — and I mean this, Odile, I have always meant this — you could stay. There's a berth for you. Third chair, survey division. Your mother's name would open doors her schemes closed."

"My mother's name," Odile said, "is on a strongbox you emptied."

"Her name is on half the coast, whether you like it or not." Quill sat back, and for a heartbeat the mask slipped entirely, and what looked out was not menace but something almost like pleading. "I taught you stars, boy. Every constellation you know, I put it in your sky. Don't make me watch you navigate by a dead woman's grudge."

"I won't," Odile said, and refilled his own glass. "I'll navigate by yours."

Something crossed Quill's face then — quick, involuntary, gone. Not anger. Recognition. The look of a man who has just heard his own voice coming out of someone else's mouth, and does not care for the echo.

He left early. Odile sat alone in the wardroom a long while, listening to the hull tick and groan around the tide, and permitted himself, briefly, to mourn. Then he went up on deck, nodded pleasantly to the watch, and counted the minutes until the moon set.

* * *

The storm arrived on the sixth day, riding up from the south the way the barometer had promised, and everything changed shape.

Wind came off the flats in long brown gusts, dragging salt dust across the flooded basin until the water looked like hammered tin. The fleet shortened its dive shifts — no bottom work in a chop like that — and the *Wager* swung at her anchors, restless, her cables singing. The crew grumbled. The officers shouted. And through all of it Odile walked the decks with his hands in his pockets and his eyes down, a bored guest watching weather, memorizing.

Three tides left. Maybe fewer, if the storm scrambled the schedule — and storms scrambled schedules. Storms also scattered attention. He noted which hatches were dogged and which were merely shut. He noted that the document Quill had taken — the papers themselves — were kept in the master's safe, and that the master's safe required two keys, one of which hung around Quill's neck and the other in the purser's locked drawer, and that the purser went ashore every evening to drink at the temporary shanty-town the fleet had raised on the basin's rim. He noted that the guards assigned to "his comfort" were two, that they changed at each tide, and that the evening pair drew straws for who got the lee side of the deckhouse out of the wind.

He was not ready tonight. He might not be ready tomorrow. But readiness was not a door that opened; it was a blade, and blades were honed by repetition, and so he honed.

On the second night of the storm, standing at his window with the rain writing long grey sentences down the glass, he saw the light.

It was small and wrong — a lantern moving along the rim of the basin, out past the fleet's shanties, where no one had business walking in weather like this. It bobbed, paused, bobbed again. Moving fast, low, with the particular economy of someone who had learned to cross open country without announcing herself. Odile's breath fogged the glass and he wiped it with his sleeve and watched until the light vanished behind the rocks above the cave mouth.

He should have felt fear for her. Vess Corrane, alone, hunted, carrying whatever she carried, walking into the teeth of the fleet's own country. Instead what he felt was the strangest thing: gratitude, sharp and unearned, like drinking cold water after days of dust. Because wherever she was going, whatever she'd come back for, it was the same thing he was waiting for. The second door. The copy Quill hadn't found.

His mother had built this house of secrets with more than one exit. And now, it seemed, both exits were converging on the same three tides.

Odile turned from the window. Outside, the wind rose another note, and the *Wager* strained at her cables like a hound smelling home. Three tides. He checked the posted schedule one last time, though he no longer needed to — high water at 0510, low at 1130, the storm pulling everything earlier, everything tighter — and then he went below, lay down in his comfortable bed in his comfortable cabin, and closed his eyes the way a blade closes into its sheath.

Patient. Honed. Waiting for the water to turn.
