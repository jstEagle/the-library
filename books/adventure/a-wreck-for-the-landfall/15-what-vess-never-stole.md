## 15. What Vess Never Stole

The desert forgave nothing and forgot less. Vess Corrane had learned that lesson at nine years old, when she'd tried to outrun a sandstorm on the flats below Karst Hollow and her father had dragged her by the collar into a culvert, shouting over the roar that you never beat the desert, you only outwaited it. She was outwaiting it now, crouched in the shadow of a fallen arch forty miles from the flooded basin, with the fleet's riders combing the hardpan behind her in slow, patient lines, like men raking ash for coins.

Her ribs still ached where Tam Osk's elbow had caught her during the escape — not his best work, she suspected. A man who meant to hurt you hit harder than that. A man who meant to let you go made it look like an accident. She filed the thought away with all the others she couldn't afford to examine yet.

The wind came off the salt in long shallow breaths, carrying the smell of brine and old iron from the basin behind her. Somewhere back there, under sixty feet of green water, Odile Coldharbour sat in a locked cabin on the flagship *Charity's Wage*, playing the obedient prisoner while he counted tides. Four left. Maybe three and a half by now. The permits would finalize at slack water of the sixth tide, and after that the Meridian Lark would belong to the salvage fleet as thoroughly as if Quill had built it himself.

And Vess Corrane was running the wrong direction.

She knew it. She'd known it since the moment her boots hit the skiff's deck and the storm-tide took them both — known it in the way you know a thing you've decided not to know, the way you know a debt is coming due. Every mile she put between herself and the fleet made her safer and made him deader, and the arithmetic of that did not care how she felt about it.

*You were sent to find one thing,* her father's voice said in her memory, calm as ever, patient as ever, the voice of a man who had spent his whole life being paid to keep secrets and had died keeping the largest one. *One thing, Vess. Not the ship. Not the man. One thing.*

She reached into her coat and drew out the compass.

It looked like nothing. That had always been its genius. A brass disc the size of a plum, dented along one rim, the glass clouded with age, the needle swinging lazily toward whatever it pleased — which, in this country, was usually the basin, and usually down. Her father had carried it for thirty years before he gave it to her with instructions so simple they'd sounded like a joke: *Steal it back. Take it to the cave. Put it in the boy's hands.*

Except she hadn't put it in his hands. She'd kept it. Through the whole mad chase across the salt, through the wreck and the strongbox and the night work and the flight, she'd kept it, telling herself it was leverage, telling herself it was hers by right of the job, telling herself anything except the truth, which was that she'd been afraid to open it.

Because there was a second thing inside it. There always had been.

Her father had told her that too, once, drunk on festival wine the winter before he died, when his tongue went loose and his hands went steady in the strange way they did. *The compass has a false back,* he'd said. *Coldharbour built it that way. The needle points to the ship, but the heart of it points to the truth. Two copies of everything, girl. Coldharbour women never trusted one copy of anything.* He'd laughed then, a short ugly laugh. *Smartest woman I ever worked for. Paid me half up front and half on delivery, and I'm still waiting on the second half.*

He'd been waiting thirty years. He'd died waiting. And now here was Vess, sitting in the lee of a broken arch with the fleet's riders fanning out across the salt behind her and four tides draining away like blood from a wound, finally ready to do the one thing she'd crossed two hundred miles of desert to avoid doing.

She turned the compass over in her fingers. The brass was warm from her body heat, which felt obscene somehow, like the thing was alive and complicit. Along the lower rim, hidden beneath a century of grime and her own reluctance, ran the seam her father had described: a hairline joint, almost invisible, where the back plate met the case.

*Twist against the needle,* he'd said. *Never with it.*

Vess set her thumbnail in the seam. Her hands were shaking. She noticed it with a kind of detached irritation, the way you notice weather. She was a thief. She'd cracked locks in the dark with armed men sleeping ten feet away. She'd held her breath in a flooding hold while the water rose past her chin. None of it had shaken her hands like this small brass disc, because none of it had mattered like this.

*Twist against the needle.*

She twisted.

The back plate resisted, then gave with a click so soft the wind swallowed it, and the compass came apart in her hands like a walnut: the working face with its mad needle, and beneath it a shallow recess lined in oilcloth, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Inside the recess lay a packet, thin as a psalter, wrapped tight and tied with cord gone brittle as straw.

Vess stopped breathing. It was a habit she'd developed young, this involuntary stillness at the moment of the prize — her body's way of saying *nothing else exists*. Usually it lasted a heartbeat. This time it lasted until her vision sparked at the edges, and she had to make herself inhale, ragged and loud in the quiet under the arch.

She broke the wax with her thumb. The cord crumbled. The oilcloth fell open.

Papers. Six of them, written in a hand she recognized without ever having seen it — Odile's mother's hand, because some things carry their author in the very shape of them, and these pages were sharp and slanted and unsentimental as a knife laid flat. The first was a charter: the Meridian Lark, fitted out under salvage fleet colors, countersigned by Harrow Quill, dated eleven years before the concession he claimed to hold. The second was a bill of lading for cargo the ship had never delivered. The third—

The third stopped her cold.

It was a letter. Unsent. Addressed in that same blade-thin hand to *my son, if he ever comes*, and Vess read it twice before she understood what she was holding, and then she had to fold it closed again and press the heel of her hand against her mouth and look out at the empty desert for a long time.

Because it wasn't evidence. The other papers were evidence — forged permits, falsified charters, proof enough to hang Quill's career from the port authority's highest yardarm. But the letter was a confession of a different kind. It was a woman writing to a child she expected never to see again, explaining why she had to disappear, why she had let everyone believe she'd abandoned him, why she had paid a smuggler named Corrane to hide a ship in a cave beneath a sea that no longer existed and trust the truth to tides and strangers.

*I could not fight them in the open,* the dead woman had written. *They would have buried me beside the ship and called it salvage. So I buried myself instead, and the truth with me, and I left the digging to whoever loved me enough to come looking. If you are reading this, Odile, then someone did. Forgive me the years. Forgive me the lie. It was the only armor I had that they couldn't take off me.*

Vess read it a third time with the wind moving through the arch above her, making a low moan across the stone like something grieving, and she understood at last the full architecture of the trap she'd been born into. Her father hadn't been hired to hide a ship. He'd been hired to hide a message, and the message was a son, and the son was a weapon, and every piece of it — the debt, the compass, the thief sent to steal it back — had been aimed at this moment with a precision that outlasted its maker by more than a decade.

Coldharbour women never trusted one copy of anything.

And now Vess Corrane held the second copy of everything, alone, hunted, forty miles from the man it belonged to, with the fleet's riders closing their lines behind her and three and a half tides left in the world.

* * *

She made camp at dusk in a dry wash six miles further on, because staying still was dying and moving was only dying more slowly, and between those two options she had always chosen motion. She ate half a strip of dried fish and saved the rest, drank two swallows of water, and laid the papers out on her bedroll in the last grey light, weighted at the corners with stones, and studied them the way she'd study a lock before touching it.

Five documents of evidence. One letter that was not evidence at all but was worth more than all the rest combined, though not to any port authority magistrate in the world. To a magistrate, the letter was sentiment. To Odile Coldharbour, it was his mother's voice, speaking across eleven years of deliberate silence, saying *I did not leave you, I was taken from you, and here is the name of the man who took me.*

Two different cargoes in one hull. Evidence for the hearing. Truth for the man.

And here was the arithmetic that had been grinding at her all day, the arithmetic she kept trying not to do and kept doing anyway:

If she went back — if she turned north tonight and rode the storm-tide roads down into the basin and did the mad impossible thing — she might reach the fleet's anchorage before the fifth tide. Might get aboard. Might get him out. The odds were bad. The odds were worse than bad; they were the kind of odds her father had taught her never to touch, the kind where you didn't calculate your chances, you calculated your corpse's travel time.

If she went east instead — east to Meridian Port, where the emergency session would convene at high water of the sixth tide — she could deliver the papers herself. Anonymous. Safe. The port authority had a standing bounty for proof of fraudulent concessions; she'd checked it years ago, professionally, the way you check exits. She could walk into the hearing, lay the documents on the bench, collect the bounty and the pardon both, and watch Harrow Quill fall without ever showing her face or spending her name.

Odile would hang in the middle of it — not literally, probably, the fleet didn't hang rangers, it just erased them — but alive, freed even, vindicated by paperwork he'd never touched. He'd come out of the basin with his mother's name cleared and no idea who had done it. He'd hate her, maybe, for vanishing. Or worse, he wouldn't. Worse, he'd understand. He was built out of duty the way ships were built out of timber; he'd recognize a debt paid the only way it could be paid, and he'd forgive her, and she would have to live the rest of her life having been forgiven by a man she'd abandoned in a locked cabin, and she found, testing the weight of that future the way she'd test thin ice, that she could not stand on it.

That was the trouble with loyalty. It never announced itself. It just waited until the moment of decision and then showed you what you actually were.

Vess sat in the dark with the papers spread around her and the compass open in her lap, its dead needle swinging slowly, patiently, toward the basin. Toward him. Even broken loose from its housing, even stripped of its secret, the thing pointed at Odile Coldharbour like it had made up its mind.

"You're a liar," she told it. "You've always been a liar."

The needle swung, and settled, and pointed down and north, unbothered.

She thought about her father then — really thought about him, not the tidy memorial version but the man himself, the smuggler who'd spent his life ferrying other people's secrets and had loved exactly one honest thing in all his crooked years, and that thing had been her. He'd taken Coldharbour's coin and kept Coldharbour's silence and passed the debt down to his daughter like an heirloom, and he'd never once told her whether he thought she should honor it. He'd only told her what it was. *One thing, Vess. Not the ship. Not the man.* But he'd been lying, or hiding, or both, because it had always been the man. The ship was just where the man lived.

The wind shifted. Out of the north, faint as a rumor, came a sound she knew too well now: the deep tidal groan of the basin filling, miles away, the dead sea remembering itself. High water coming. The fifth tide rising toward a shore where a flagship rode at anchor with a prisoner counting hours in a locked cabin, and an enforcer burning papers, and divers stripping a dead ship down to its bones.

Three and a half tides. Call it three.

Vess looked at the letter one more time — at the sharp slanted hand, at the words *forgive me the lie* — and folded it into her coat, over her heart, where she kept the things she'd decided about herself.

Then she began packing the evidence into oilcloth, sealing it against water, because whatever she chose, the papers had to survive. That was the discipline. Decide the part that can't be undone first, and let the rest follow.

She could send the papers ahead. That was the answer, the compromise, the thief's solution: buy passage with a salt caravan leaving at dawn, pay a runner she half-trusted to carry the documents east to the port authority, addressed to the hearing clerk, timed to arrive before slack water of the sixth tide. The evidence would land whether she lived or died. And then, with the irretrievable part secured, she could turn north with a clean conscience — or at least a conscience that had done its bookkeeping — and spend what remained of the tides on the mad impossible thing.

It was a good plan. It was the kind of plan her father would have admired: hedged, layered, nothing wagered that couldn't be survived losing.

She hated it. She hated it the way you hate a rope that fits.

Vess sat back on her heels and looked north, where the basin breathed its slow tidal breath into the dark, and she understood that the choice had already been made somewhere deeper than planning, the way the tide had already been decided somewhere deeper than the moon. She was going back. She'd been going back since the moment the skiff cleared the fleet's guns and she'd caught herself looking astern.

But the papers would go east regardless. Because Coldharbour women never trusted one copy of anything, and because love, whatever else it was, was not an excuse for bad tradecraft.

She banked her fire, checked her water, and slept two hours with the compass in her fist like a talisman, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old brass, and with it came the caravan.

Vess watched it from the rim of the wash: eight wagons and forty-odd camels crawling east along the salt road toward Meridian Port, flying the faded blue pennant of the Saltmerchants' Guild. Legitimate. Slow. Exactly what she needed. She slipped the sealed packet of evidence into her coat and climbed out of the wash, and was halfway to the road before she saw the rider.

He came out of the north at a canter, leading a second horse, and even at that distance she knew the silhouette — the heavy shoulders, the careful seat, the way he sat a horse like a man sitting in judgment. Tam Osk. Alone, unarmed as far as she could see, riding straight at her across open ground where any rifleman in the fleet's pay could cut him down. Which meant either that he was the bravest fool on the salt, or that he was exactly what he appeared to be: a bosun who had sold one betrayal to Quill and discovered it tasted like ash, now riding forty miles through hostile country to find the thief he'd failed to stop.

Vess stood her ground in the open, hands visible, and waited.

Tam reined in ten feet away and looked down at her for a long moment. His face was grey with dust and something else, something worn down to the quick.

"They moved the hearing," he said, without greeting. "Quill bought the port authority clerk. Session convenes at high water of the fifth tide, not the sixth. Tomorrow." He swung down from the saddle and stood facing her, and his voice dropped to gravel. "Your ranger's got one tide left, girl. Maybe less. And I don't think anyone's coming."

The wind moved across the salt between them, and far to the north, faint and patient, the sea that shouldn't exist breathed in.

Vess put her hand on the compass in her pocket, and made her choice.
