## 16. The Wreck Gives Up Its Dead

They brought me up on deck to watch, because Quill wanted me to watch.

That was the whole point of it. A prisoner who matters is a prisoner kept where he can see. Two of Quill's men flanked me at the rail of the *Charterhouse*, the fleet's flagship, and the morning came up gray and salt-hazed over the flooded basin, and below us the divers went down into the green dark of the Meridian Lark like flies settling on a carcass.

I had been four days aboard by then. Four days of playing the broken ranger, compliant, cowed, taking my meals in the wardroom under the eye of a bosun's mate who chewed with his mouth open and reported everything I said to no one in particular, loudly enough for me to hear. Four days of memorizing. The dive rotations changed at slack water and again two hours after. The document room was aft, third door past the galley, locked with a brass lever lock that a hairpin could shame. The copies Quill hadn't found — I was nearly sure now they were in the strongbox itself, because he'd had the box hauled up from the captain's cabin and set in his own quarters, and a man doesn't keep what he's already emptied.

But all of that was for later. That morning there was only the water, and the men going down into it, and the thing they were about to find.

"Beautiful morning for salvage," said the man on my left. His name was Dree; he'd told me twice, as if repetition were friendship. "You can see straight down today. Look at her."

I looked. The basin had flooded deep since the first tide took the cave mouth, and the Meridian Lark lay on her side in forty feet of clear cold water, masts gone, hull furred with weed, her stern nameplate catching the light like a coin at the bottom of a well. She looked less like a wreck every day and more like a wound that the sea had come back specifically to reopen. Fleet cranes stood on pontoons around her, and their shadows lay across her hull in long black bars, so that from above she seemed to be behind the ribs of something larger, something eating her slowly and without hurry.

The divers went down in pairs. I counted their bubbles.

* * *

Quill joined me at the rail an hour into it. He did this often enough now that the guards had stopped pretending to object. He came quietly, hands clasped behind his back, and stood close enough that I could smell the cedar oil he'd used on his beard for as long as I could remember, and for a moment we were only two men watching other men work, which was the most dangerous lie either of us had ever told.

"You're sleeping better," he observed.

"I'm tired."

"Tired men sleep." He nodded at the water. "Do you know what I admire about you, Odile? You never once asked me to let you go. Not once in four days. Most men would have begged by now. Begging is cheap and I'd have respected it less." He turned his head, and his eyes were the same pale gray they'd been when I was nine years old and he was teaching me to read the North Star off the smoke-hole of his cabin roof. "You're waiting. That's my boy."

*My boy.* He had said it ten thousand times across twenty years, and it had always sounded like shelter. It sounded different now. It sounded like a brand.

"I'm waiting for breakfast," I said.

He laughed — genuinely, warmly, the laugh that had raised me — and clapped my shoulder, and left, and I watched him go down the ladder toward his quarters, toward the strongbox, and I hated how much of my love for him had survived everything I'd learned, like barnacles on a hull: ugly, stubborn, impossible to scrape clean without tearing away some of the wood beneath.

Below, the divers reached the hull.

* * *

They'd been cutting for two days along the starboard side, stripping cargo, pulling fittings, working methodically aft toward the hold. Everyone knew the manifest by then; Tam Osk had grumbled it aloud within my hearing more than once, whether from carelessness or conscience I hadn't yet decided. Salt beef, dye-wood, tin ingots, wine. A merchant's load for a merchant's run. Nothing worth sealing.

But the sonar man — a nervous young fleet officer named Pell who talked to his instruments like lovers — had flagged an anomaly on the second day. A void. An air-tight space, or one that had been air-tight once, built into the hull between the hold and the sternpost. Not cargo. Construction. And Quill, when Pell reported it, had gone very still, and then very brisk, and ordered it opened last, after everything else, and I had watched him give that order and understood that he already knew what was inside.

He'd known for thirty years. That was the horror of it, though I didn't understand the full shape of it yet. He wasn't afraid of what the compartment held. He was afraid of being seen to care.

So that morning, with the tide slack and the light good, they cut through the inner skin.

I heard it before I saw anything — a sound carried up through the pontoon timbers and the water both, a groan of metal giving way, and then a long exhalation of trapped gas breaking the surface in a bloom of silver bubbles, and the smell reached us a heartbeat later. Every man on deck knew that smell. The bosun's mate stopped chewing. Dree made a sign against bad luck that he probably thought I didn't see.

The diver came up fast, too fast, breaking protocol, hauling himself onto the pontoon and tearing off his mask with shaking hands. Even at distance I could see his face had gone the color of the fog.

"There's men down there," he said. His voice carried flat across the water. "Bones. A whole watch of them. Sealed in alive, looks like — the hatch is dogged from the inside."

* * *

They gave me a boat, which surprised me until I understood it. Quill wanted a witness. A witness who loved him, if any scrap of that love could be salvaged from what was coming; failing that, a witness who would carry the weight of it and maybe, just maybe, keep quiet out of shared guilt. It was the oldest trick he had. He had taught it to me himself, one winter, over charts: *Never argue with a man when you can grieve with him instead.*

So I rode down to the wreck in a workboat with Tam Osk rowing and two divers who would not look at each other, and the water was so clear and so cold that the ship rose up to meet us like something drowning upward, and I put my hand into the sea and it was the temperature of grief.

We went in through the cut. The diver's lamp swung ahead of us, and the beam slid along stripped shelving, along a bulkhead furred white with something like mold or something like coral, and then through a jagged opening into the sealed space beyond.

The bones sat upright.

That was the detail that broke something in me and I don't think it ever knitted right afterward. They weren't scattered, weren't tumbled the way the drowned usually are. They sat in rows against the after bulkhead, eleven of them, backs to the planking, legs stretched out or drawn up, some leaning together like passengers dozing on a long crossing. One had a child-sized frame tucked against its ribs, arms folded around it. They had come down here, into the deepest belly of the ship, and dogged the hatch behind themselves, and waited. The air in the compartment had lasted — the diver said later, in the flat voice men use for arithmetic that keeps them sane — perhaps two days.

On the deck between them, wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas against rot, stacked with a deliberateness that no drowning man would bother with, lay the records.

* * *

They brought them up in the workboat because I insisted, and Tam Osk backed me, and the divers backed Tam, and Quill, arriving by launch with his face arranged into solemnity, found himself outmaneuvered by the simple decency of men who had just seen eleven skeletons holding each other. Chain of custody, I said. Ranger authority. My jurisdiction ends at the waterline, I know, but these men died inside your hull, Captain, and dead men are mine.

He couldn't refuse without looking like exactly what he was. So he let me carry them aboard the *Charterhouse* myself, and he walked beside me the whole way, and neither of us spoke, and the oilcloth bundles dripped seawater onto the deck planks in a rhythm like slow counting.

In the wardroom, under lamp, we opened them.

The first bundle was the ship's log, and the sea had taken its revenge on ink, but the early pages survived, written in a careful round hand: the Meridian Lark, out of Coldharbour Roads, chartered — and here my breath stopped, because I knew the charter form, had grown up seeing them pinned to the fleet office wall — chartered under fleet concession 114-C, countersigned at the bottom in a young man's sharp slanted script I had known my whole life as the signature on my school lessons, my ration chits, the deed to the house I grew up in.

Harrow Quill. Countersigned. Witnessed. Thirty-one years ago.

"She sailed under his authority," Tam Osk said quietly. He was standing too far back from the table, the way men stand from things that burn. "Under his signature. This isn't a smuggler's ship. This is a fleet ship."

The second bundle was correspondence, wax-sealed, mostly legible. Orders. I read them three times because the mind refuses certain sentences on the first two passes. The Lark had not been carrying cargo out. She had been carrying evidence *in* — ledgers, duplicate charters, testimony sworn by fleet clerks, a whole paper case against the concession system, gathered by someone inside the fleet itself and meant to be delivered to the port authority at Coldharbour. And the final order, dated six days before the log goes silent, was signed by the same young hand:

*Sink her in the basin. Nothing reaches the hearing. See to the crew.*

See to the crew. Eleven men and a stowaway child, sealed in the deepest compartment with the proof of their own murder, dogging the hatch from the inside because the alternative was worse.

The forgery wasn't the permits. The permits were a scab over this. Quill hadn't forged a claim to a derelict. He had murdered a ship full of witnesses, hidden them under a false seabed for thirty years, and then built an entire legal edifice — concessions, enforcers, a career, a *son* — on top of their graves.

And my mother. My mother, whose compass pointed here, whose papers were in the strongbox, who had spent her life — I understood it all at once, standing in the lamplight with dead men's ink on my fingers — trying to dig them back up.

* * *

Quill watched me read. He had positioned himself across the table, and he did not touch the documents, and his face was a masterpiece of sorrow, and I realized with a lurch that he had rehearsed this. Some version of it. For thirty years he had rehearsed the day the ship gave up its dead, and in every rehearsal he had played the grieving friend.

"The fleet was different then," he said softly. "There were men above me, Odile. Men who gave those orders. I was twenty-six years old and I countersigned what I was told to countersign, and I have regretted it every day of my life—"

"Stop." My voice came out strange. Level. "The last order is in your hand alone. No co-signature. No superior's seal. Just you."

The lamplight moved. Somewhere above us, boots crossed the deck, ordinary and oblivious.

"You taught me stars," I said. "You taught me that a navigator's whole duty is to know where he truly is. Not where the chart says. Where he *truly* is." I closed the log. "I know where I am, Harrow. I finally know where I've been standing my entire life."

Something moved behind his eyes — not fear. Grief, real grief, the kind that has waited decades for its appointment. "Then you know I can't let them take me," he said. "Not for this. Not for *her*."

For her. For my mother. And I understood, in the last hour of that terrible day, that the strongbox in his quarters held papers proving the forgery — but the *second* copy, the one Vess had been sent to find, the one in the compass's false back, might hold something else entirely. Something with a name on it. Something that said who had given the order, and why, and what my mother had been to the man who wrote it.

Quill put his hand briefly, gently, on the dead men's log, the way you'd touch a coffin lid.

"Bury them properly," he told the room. "All of them. Full honors." And then, to me, almost tenderly: "Two tides, Odile. After that, none of this matters anymore."

He left. I stayed at the table with the lamp and the bones' paper, and outside the porthole the tide had turned, and I could feel it turning, the whole gray sea beginning its slow pull back toward the wreck, and somewhere across the water — I was suddenly, absolutely certain of it — Vess Corrane was moving.
