## 17. Escape on the Fifth Tide

The storm came in off the dead sea like a debt collector.

Vess felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her back teeth, a taste of iron on the wind that came down through the cave mouth and stirred the flooded basin into something restless and gray. The fleet's ships had anchored for it, riding at uneasy angles, their lanterns swinging in long arcs that threw shadows across the water like hands reaching for something they couldn't hold.

She crouched in the rocks above the cave rim, soaked to the bone by rain that fell sideways, and watched the flagship's lights, and counted.

Four tides had been lost already. She'd spent two of them running — across the salt, up through the broken country north of the basin, doubling back along ridgelines no tracker could hold in memory because the storm kept rewriting them. She'd spent one more finding Tam Osk again, or rather letting him find her, because Tam was a man who wanted to be found; he just needed someone to make the wanting safe. And she'd spent the fourth doing the thing she was worst at: waiting.

Now the fifth tide was coming, and it was coming angry.

*He'll have moved him,* she thought. *Quill will have moved Odile somewhere worse.* But when she glassed the flagship through the rain-blurred spyglass she'd taken off a fleet lookout three days ago, she saw what she'd prayed to no god in particular to see: the skiff tied at the flagship's stern rail, and beside it, small in the wash of lamplight, a figure with his wrists bound to the rail itself, sitting patient as a fencepost in weather that would drown a lesser man.

Odile Coldharbour, waiting out the storm like he was waiting out a bad season. Like patience was a blade and he was honing it.

"Idiot," Vess said softly, and meant it as a compliment.

* * *

Getting aboard was the easy part. The storm did most of the work.

The fleet had posted watches, but the fifth tide was a monster — the sea coming back into the basin with a swell that lifted the anchored ships and dropped them hard, hulls groaning, rigging singing a note that lived in the bones. Men who'd spent four days diving a wreck in cold black water were below decks, dry and warm and asleep, and the men above decks were holding lines and losing arguments with the wind.

Vess came up the flagship's stern from the water side, riding the swell on a piece of wreckage she'd paddled out from the rocks, and let the sea throw her against the hull the way it threw everything else. She caught the stern rail one-handed. Her other hand held a knife between her teeth.

Nobody saw her. The storm was loud enough to swallow a scream, let alone the wet slap of her boots hitting deck.

Odile heard her anyway. He always did — some ranger trick of listening past the noise for the sound that didn't belong. His head turned, slow and casual, the way a man turns his head when he's checking the weather, and his eyes found hers in the dark, and she watched him go very still.

"Don't," she mouthed, when his mouth opened. "Don't say anything clever. We don't have time for clever."

His bound hands flexed against the rail. She knelt behind him, put the knife to the rope, and sawed. The rope was salt-stiff and wet-swollen and fought her the whole way, and every second of it she spent with her spine crawling, expecting a shout, a lantern, a crossbow bolt out of the dark. Quill would not miss twice. Quill did not miss at all, if the stories were true, and Vess had built her whole life on stories being true.

The rope parted.

Odile stood up like a man rising from a long illness — stiff, careful, then all at once sure of himself. He rolled his wrists, working blood back into them, and looked at her, and there was so much in his face that she held up a hand.

"Not now," she said. "Whatever it is — the thanks, the anger, the questions about my father, all of it — not now. Now we swim."

"I can't swim," he said.

"You're a desert ranger."

"We're a long way from any desert worth naming." He glanced over the rail at the black, heaving water, and she watched him do the arithmetic of it — the drop, the cold, the current running toward the cave mouth like a river deciding its course. Then he looked back at her, and something in his face settled. "Tell me you have a better plan than swimming."

"The skiff," she said. "It's right there. Quill keeps it fueled and provisioned because Quill is a man who always keeps an exit ready, and tonight, Coldharbour, we are going to steal his exit and make it ours."

For the first time in five days — maybe for the first time since she'd met him, which was saying something, because meeting Odile Coldharbour had involved a knife at her throat and very little humor — the man smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, gone almost before it arrived, but it was real.

"After you," he said.

* * *

They almost made it clean.

The skiff went off the stern rail into the swell and the storm took it gladly, spinning them, shipping water, the little engine coughing and catching and coughing again while Vess wrestled the tiller and Odile bailed with a bucket that had once, judging by the stencil on its side, held engine oil. Behind them the flagship swung at anchor, lights blazing, and nobody shouted, and nobody fired, and for ninety seconds Vess let herself believe the storm had simply decided to be kind.

Then the searchlight came on.

It cut through the rain like a white blade, swept left, swept right, and found them — pinned them, small and exposed on the gray water, a bug under a magnifying lens. Vess heard the shout come across the water, thin and torn by wind, and heard the answer: an alarm bell, deep and urgent, rolling out over the fleet.

"They'll launch boats," Odile shouted. He'd stopped bailing. He was looking back at the flagship with his ranger's eyes, reading it the way he read country — the movement of men, the weight of decision. "Two boats. Maybe three. They'll be faster than this thing in a straight line."

"So we don't run a straight line." She hauled the tiller over and the skiff heeled, wallowed, answered. "We run the tide."

"The tide runs into the cave—"

"The tide runs *everywhere*, Coldharbour. That's the whole point of tides." She pointed the bow at the black line of the shore where the cave mouth breathed, visible now even in the storm — a pale seam in the rock where the water inside caught the lightning and gave it back. "Your mother picked this hiding place. I'm starting to think she picked it for exactly this."

Lightning walked across the sky and showed her the basin entire: the fleet's ships heaving at their anchors, the launch already down and pulling hard, oars flashing, and beyond it the drowned forest of masts that was the Meridian Lark, just under the surface now, its spars close enough to the flood to snag anything unwary.

Anything unwary, or anyone willing to be dangerous.

"Hold on," Vess said, and drove the skiff straight at the wreck.

* * *

Later — much later, when there was time for later — Odile would ask her if she'd ever done anything like it before, and she would tell him the truth, which was that she'd never done anything like it and never wanted to again, and both of those things would be lies in different directions. Because in the moment it wasn't fear. In the moment it was the purest thing she'd ever felt: the skiff planing across the flood with the searchlight swinging wild behind them, the launch falling astern because their pursuers were sane men and she was not, and then the masts rising out of the water ahead of them like the fingers of a drowning giant, and her threading the boat between them at full throttle while the storm screamed and Odile Coldharbour, desert ranger, man of dry land and open horizons, gripped the gunwale and laughed.

Actually laughed. A short, disbelieving bark of it, torn away by the wind, but she heard it, and it did something to her chest she refused to examine until much later, in safer country, alone.

The mainmast passed so close to port that she could have touched it. Barnacles raked the hull with a sound like a file drawn across wood. Then they were through, into the open water of the inner basin, the wreck between them and the launch, and the cave mouth ahead, growing, opening, a throat of stone swallowing the lightning whole.

"They won't follow us in there," Odile shouted. "Not in this swell. Not without knowing the channel."

"They know the channel," Vess shouted back. "Their divers have been using it for days. But they won't risk a boat in it tonight, and by morning—" By morning the sixth tide would be turning, and the permits would expire at slack water, and everything would come down to what happened in a hearing room in a port town half a desert away. "By morning it won't matter. Hold on."

The cave took them.

One moment storm and searchlight and the roar of open water; the next, a blackness so complete it felt physical, walls close enough to echo their own breathing back at them, the engine note doubling and trebling off the stone. Vess killed the engine and let momentum carry them, and the skiff slid through the dark on the last of the flood, scraping softly against rock, rocking, settling, going still.

Silence, except for the storm outside, muffled now, and the drip of water from stone, and their own ragged breath.

"Well," Odile said, into the dark.

"Don't," Vess said. "Don't say *well*. Don't say anything. Give me a minute of not nearly dying before you summarize."

She heard him settle back against the thwart. Heard, after a moment, the wet rustle of him pulling something from inside his jacket — the oilskin packet, the one he'd carried since the night they cracked the strongbox, the one he slept with, ate with, guarded like a second heartbeat. The documents. The truth of the Meridian Lark, wrapped in waxed canvas against the water that had kept it thirty years.

"Still dry?" she asked.

"Still dry." A pause. "You came back."

It wasn't a question, but it deserved more than silence, and she found, to her irritation, that she wanted to give it more than silence. "I told you I would."

"You told me you'd get the copy from the compass and decide whether I was worth the risk. Your exact words were 'no promises.'"

"I'm a thief, Coldharbour. Precision matters in my trade." She fished the compass out of her own jacket — her mother's compass, his mother's compass, the false-backed thing that had started all of it — and held it up even though neither of them could see it. "I got the copy. It's here. And then I sat on a ridge for six hours watching that flagship and asking myself the same question."

"And?"

"And it turns out I'm bad at math," she said, "because the answer kept coming out wrong no matter how I ran it."

In the dark, she heard him breathe. Just breathe. And she understood that for a man raised on the lie that everyone leaves — a mother who abandoned him, a mentor who shaped him like clay around a wire of control — the simple fact of her return might weigh more than all the documents in the packet.

The storm chose that moment to remind them it hadn't finished. A boom rolled through the stone, deep as a drumbeat, and the water in the cave shivered against the hull.

"Tide's still rising," Odile said. "We should get clear of the waterline. If the fleet decides to fire a boat in after all—"

"They won't. But you're right about the rest." She found the mooring line by feel, made it fast to a spur of rock that had probably held ships' boats since before either of them was born, and climbed out onto stone that was slick and ancient and smelled of salt and old wood. "Come on, ranger. Let's get you and your papers to high ground. You've got a hearing to crash."

* * *

They were halfway up the ledge path, the skiff secured below, when the light found them.

Not the fleet's searchlight. Something smaller, closer — a shielded lantern, hooded to throw its beam in a single narrow bar, the kind of light a man uses when he doesn't want to be seen by his own side. It swung up from the rocks near the cave mouth and caught them both, froze them, and Vess's knife was in her hand before her mind caught up with her body.

"Easy." The voice was rough, tired, familiar. "It's me."

Tam Osk stepped into his own lamplight, and Vess felt the bottom drop out of the night.

He looked terrible. He looked like a man who hadn't slept in four days, which matched her own experience of the last four days, and he stood with the awkwardness of someone who has rehearsed a speech a hundred times and abandoned it a hundred times. His eyes went from her knife to Odile's face to the oilskin packet under Odile's arm, and stayed there.

"You sold us out once," Vess said. The knife didn't waver. "You sold us out for a pardon, and Quill took our documents and let us live because letting us live was the crueler mercy. So you'll forgive me if my hand isn't steady."

"I know what I did." Tam's voice cracked on it, and he hated that she could hear it crack, and she watched him hate it. "I've had two days to know it. Two days of watching them strip that ship like vultures on a carcass, and listening to Quill talk about firing charges once the holds are empty, and knowing—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Knowing what your mother paid my father to protect, Coldharbour. Knowing I helped hand it to the man who sank her."

The rain came through the cave mouth in gusts, silver in the lantern light. Nobody moved.

"Why are you here?" Odile asked. His voice was level, but Vess had learned to hear the load-bearing parts of it, the places where calm was carrying weight. "The pardon. The pay. Quill takes care of his people, Tam. You told me that yourself, the day you turned us in. He takes care of them like a man takes care of tools."

"Aye, he does." Tam looked down at the lantern in his hand, at the small circle of warmth it made against all that dark water. "And then tonight he gave the order for the demolition charges to be rigged early. Not after the hearing. Tonight, if the storm covers it. He means to blow the Lark before slack water, before anyone can rule on the permits, and call it storm damage." He raised his eyes. "There's no place in that future for a bosun who knows too much and a pardon that dies with the ship. So I'm asking — I'm asking, Coldharbour — is there room in yours?"

Vess looked at Odile. Odile looked at Tam. The storm boomed again, closer, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came a new sound: the rhythmic knock of mallets on wood, far off in the flooded galleries of the wreck, where men were setting charges in the dark.

"There's room," Odile said quietly. "But it's bought with testimony, Tam. At the hearing. In front of the port authority, with your name on it and Quill standing ten feet away. Can you do that?"

Tam Osk closed his eyes. When he opened them, something had burned away in them, some last comfortable lie, and what was left looked lighter and older and afraid.

"My father hid a ship for your mother," he said. "I reckon I can stand up in a room and tell the truth about one."

Behind him, out across the black flooding basin, the fleet's lanterns swung in the storm, and the sound of the mallets went on and on, patient as a countdown, driving toward the sixth and final tide.

"Then we'd better move," Vess said, and shouldered her pack, and led them up into the dark.
