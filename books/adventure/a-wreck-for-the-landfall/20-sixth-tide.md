## 20. Sixth Tide

The water came in like a verdict.

I stood at the rail of the port authority's hearing barge and watched the sixth tide climb the walls of the basin — that impossible sea returning to a seabed that had been dry for a hundred years — and I understood that the ocean had been the only honest witness in this whole affair. It kept its appointments. It rose when it was sworn to rise. It would fall again at slack water, and when it did, every concession, every permit, every forged signature Harrow Quill had ever pressed against fleet paper would either be void or would never have existed at all.

The hearing chamber smelled of lamp oil and wet rope and too many bodies who had ridden through the night to be present at the drowning of a legend. Commissioners in salt-stiffened coats. Fleet lawyers with ink still fresh on their counter-affidavits. Rangers from three districts who had come to see whether one of their own could actually bring down the Salvage Concession — or whether the fleet's reach simply extended past the edge of any law we carried.

And Tam Osk.

He sat on the witness bench with his hands flat on his knees like a man bracing against weather, and he would not look at me. He had not looked at me since they brought him in an hour before dawn, escorted by two port authority constables, his pardon papers already signed and folded into his coat pocket like a confession he couldn't stop touching. Betrayal at low water, then betrayal again at high. I had stopped counting which side of the ledger his soul currently occupied. The commissioners hadn't. They leaned toward him the way men lean toward a fire.

"State your name and station for the record," said the senior commissioner, a woman named Aldous-Vane whose voice had the texture of a hull scraped clean.

"Tam Osk. Bosun, late of the salvage vessel *Charity's Wage*, third ship of the Meridian Concession fleet."

"And you swear the testimony you are about to give is true, under penalty of the maritime code?"

Osk's jaw worked. I watched him find Quill in the chamber — Quill standing at the fleet's table, silver-haired, immaculate, wearing his ruin like a dress uniform — and I watched Osk look away from him deliberately, the way you turn your back on a man you once followed into a storm.

"I do."

* * *

What he gave them took the better part of two hours, and I will not pretend I heard all of it cleanly. Grief has a way of filling the ears with its own noise. But the shape of it came through like a keel under dark water.

Osk told them about the demolition charges. Not the ones Quill had ordered fired at the wreck after the escape on the fifth tide — those were public knowledge now, logged in the fleet's own damage reports as "structural clearing" — but the original order. The one dated weeks before the *Meridian Lark* was ever legally declared fleet property. *Strip her, then sink her,* the order read, in Quill's own hand, countersigned by two officers who had since been promoted into comfortable silence. *No hull worth surveying survives to contradict the concession.*

Osk told them about the sealed compartment the divers opened on the fourth tide. The crew remains. The charter papers lashed in oilcloth beneath the captain's berth — a fleet charter, properly executed, bearing Harrow Quill's signature as witness and surety, proving the *Lark* had sailed under fleet colors the whole time, which meant she had been scuttled by her own convoy, which meant the "wreck discovered in unclaimed waters" was a fiction built on top of a murder built on top of a forgery.

And then Osk said the thing that turned the chamber to stone:

"He sold us the story that the sea left this basin a century ago. He sold the crews that story so no one would ask why a deepwater ship lay in a cave behind a tide-gate. But I've served thirty years, commissioners, and I know ballast scarring when I see it. That ship didn't drift here. She was sailed here, at high water, through channels that open maybe twice a year — and the man who ordered her sunk knew exactly when those tides came, because he'd been studying them since before Odile Coldharbour's mother was dead."

My mother's name, spoken aloud in that room, went through me like a splinter finding its depth.

Quill's lawyer objected four times. Each objection was overruled with a patience that frightened me more than anger would have, because it meant the commissioners had already read the documents Vess and I had pulled from the strongbox — the ones Tam's first betrayal had cost us, the copies Vess recovered from the compass's false back while I rotted on the flagship — and they were no longer deciding whether Quill was guilty. They were deciding how much of the record needed saying aloud before slack water made it law.

* * *

Through all of it, Quill did not sit down.

He stood at the fleet's table with his hands clasped behind his back, and he listened to his own bosun dismantle him timber by timber, and his face held the expression of a man watching a fire consume a house he had built with his own hands — sorrow and pride fighting for the same ground. Once, only once, his eyes found mine across the chamber. There was no threat in them. That was the worst part. There was something older and more tired than threat: the look of a teacher watching a student's final examination, unable to intervene, uncertain until the last moment whether the student would pass or fail him.

When Aldous-Vane finally called for his response, he stepped forward and adjusted his cuffs.

"Commissioners." His voice filled the chamber the way it had filled my childhood — steady, warm at the edges, certain of its own music. "I will not insult this body by contesting documents I have not been permitted to examine. I will say only this: everything I did, I did for the fleet's continuity, and the fleet's continuity fed ten thousand people along this coast through two famine years. If the price of that was a ship, a secret, and—" a pause, measured to the heartbeat, "—and a boy's inheritance held in trust until he was ready for it, then I pay that price standing up, and I will answer for it wherever answers are taken."

It was beautiful. It was completely hollow. And I watched half the chamber lean toward it anyway, because charm like Quill's doesn't argue with the truth — it simply offers itself as a more comfortable place to live.

Aldous-Vane let the silence sit until it curdled.

"The chair notes the respondent's statement for the record." She turned a page. "The concession permits under challenge expire, by their own terms, at slack water following the sixth tide. This body has received testimony and documentary evidence sufficient to sustain findings of forgery, fraud, conspiracy to commit murder on the high seas, and destruction of evidence. Before ruling, the chair recognizes Ranger Odile Coldharbour, claimant of record."

* * *

I crossed the chamber floor and my boots sounded louder than my heart, which surprised me, because my heart was doing its best to break my ribs.

I had prepared a speech. Vess had helped me cut it down twice — *"They don't need poetry, Coldharbour, they need the dates"* — and what remained was short. I laid the compass on the table, its false back open, its needle swinging without rest because there was no north in that room worth finding. I laid the documents beside it. My mother's seal. The forged concession, signed by Quill eleven years before the wreck was "discovered." The charter. The demolition order.

"My mother hid the truth of this ship where only the tide could keep it," I said. "Harrow Quill taught me to read stars and charts and men. He taught me everything except how to lie, and I think that was the one lesson he regretted giving." I looked at him directly. "The permits expire at slack water. Let the sea take back what's hers. Let the truth stand on dry land."

I stepped back. There was nothing else. There never had been anything else; that was the whole point of me.

Aldous-Vane consulted her colleagues. Outside the barge's windows, the water had gone strange and glassy, climbing the basin walls in that final slow surge before the turn. Someone in the gallery whispered the time. A clerk marked it.

"The concessions of the Meridian Salvage Fleet are declared void," Aldous-Vane said. "Effective at slack water. All claims upon the wreck of the *Meridian Lark* lapse with them. The wreck passes to public heritage under ranger custody." Her gavel came down once, small and enormous. "So ruled."

The chamber erupted — constables shouting order, fleet officers on their feet, rangers roaring, Vess Corrane somewhere in the gallery laughing the wild bright laugh of a woman who had bet her life on a stranger's mother and won. I stood very still and let it wash past me, because I was watching Harrow Quill.

He had not moved. He stood at the fleet's table amid the collapse of everything he had built, and slowly, deliberately, he reached inside his coat and drew out a small black lacquered box no bigger than a prayer book, and set it on the table before him, and rested two fingers on its lid.

I knew what it was. Every soul in that chamber who had served under him knew. Demolition detonator. The charges were still in the *Lark*'s ribs — the divers had stripped her cargo, her papers, her dead, but no one had cleared the powder, because until this moment the powder had been legal.

"Quill." Aldous-Vane's voice cracked across the room like a whip. "Step away from that device."

He smiled at her. Then he looked at me, and his smile softened into something I had seen perhaps five times in my life, always at moments when he thought no one else was watching — the look he'd given me at fourteen when I first fixed a sextant true, the look he'd given my mother's grave when he believed I'd walked ahead out of earshot.

"You were the best thing I ever made, Odile," he said, and his thumb moved to the firing lever. "Forgive me for hating that."

He threw the switch.

* * *

The sound arrived a half-second after the flash — a deep, obscene concussion rolling up through the barge's hull, through the water, through the bones of everyone aboard. Out beyond the windows, the glassy surface of the sixth tide bulged and broke, and a column of spray and shattered timber leapt from the drowned cave mouth like the sea exhaling a wound.

The *Meridian Lark* died for the second time, and this time there was nothing left in her worth killing her for.

I was at the window before I knew I'd moved. Through the churn and settling smoke I could see fragments surfacing — a rib of oak, a stretch of planking furred green with a century of barnacle, the sternpost turning over slow and white-bellied like a dying whale. Somewhere under all that violence, the captain's cabin where my mother had knelt to hide a strongbox was becoming silt. The nameplate I had touched with my own hand on the first night, reading *MERIDIAN LARK* letter by letter by lamplight, was going down into the dark with the letters still on it.

"She's gone," someone said behind me. "He's burned it all—"

"No." Vess's voice, arriving at my shoulder, breathless from forcing her way through the panicked gallery. "Look again, Coldharbour. Look at what's floating."

I looked. And she was right.

The charges had torn the old girl open, yes — but a wreck stripped of her secrets is just wood and iron, and wood and iron float. Planks, beams, the great curved bones of her, a rain of timber spreading across the whole surface of the basin, riding the sixth tide in a widening halo. The fleet's skiffs were already putting out toward the debris, not to salvage — there was nothing left to steal — but because sailors cannot watch a ship die without gathering up her body. Even Quill's own crews were out there, hauling fragments aboard with the unconscious tenderness of men collecting relics.

He had wanted to erase her. Instead he had given her to everyone.

At the fleet's table, constables were closing on Quill with drawn irons, and he did not resist. He stood with his empty hands loose at his sides, watching the debris field spread, and whatever was burning behind his eyes — fury, grief, the ash of a plan forty years in the building — it burned down even as I watched, flame collapsing into ember, ember into gray. By the time they took his arms he was only a tall old man in a good coat, looking out at the water with an expression I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life: not defeat. Completion. As if, having failed to own the ending, he had at least insisted on writing it.

His gaze crossed mine one final time as they turned him toward the hold. He inclined his head — barely, a navigator's acknowledgment of a fix confirmed — and mouthed two words I chose, then and forever, to read as *well done*, though another part of me, the part he raised, suspected they were *my fault*, and a third part knew they might be both.

Then the constables took him below, and the tide, faithful to the end, began its long withdrawal.

* * *

We stood on the cave rim as the light went gold and the water fell away from the walls of the basin in sheets, uncovering the wreckage-strewn floor, the broken cave mouth, the scattered bones of the *Meridian Lark* gleaming wet in the last sun. Vess had the compass in her hand. Its needle had finally gone still — pointing, of all things, straight out at the wreck, as if my mother's instrument meant to keep watch over what was left.

"It's done," Vess said. Not a question. Testing the weight of it.

"It's done."

She was quiet a moment. "Your mother paid my father to guard a secret for a man she never met. My father passed the debt to me. I spent six years running from it, and it dragged me here anyway." She turned the compass over in her fingers. "Debts don't expire at slack water, Coldharbour. They just change hands."

Below us, on the drying flats, the port authority's boats were grounding, and constables were wading ashore toward the fleet's beached flagship — toward the records holds, the ledgers, the other names that would surface now that the first name had fallen. Because a forgery like Quill's was never a single document. It was a habit. And habits leave paper trails.

Aldous-Vane's clerk was already coming up the trail toward us with a warrant book under his arm and questions in his face, and behind him, far out on the gleaming mud, something glinted among the wreckage that neither of us had seen before — brass, catching the last light, half-buried near where the sternpost had come to rest.

Vess saw it the same moment I did.

"Well," she said, and grinned, and started down the slope. "Tide's out. Might as well see what else she's hiding."

The compass needle swung — and pointed down.
