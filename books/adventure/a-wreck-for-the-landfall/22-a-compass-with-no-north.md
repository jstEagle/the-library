## 22. A Compass with No North

**Vess**

The basin was drying the way a wound dries — slowly, and leaving a scar you could read.

Vess stood at the rim of it on the morning after the hearing, boots planted in salt crust that crackled like old parchment under her weight, and looked down at what remained of the Meridian Lark. The fleet's demolition charges had torn her open at the waterline, but the sea had already taken back most of what mattered. What was left was a skeleton: ribs of blackened timber rising from the white floor like the bones of something that had knelt down to die and changed its mind about getting up.

The tide had gone out for the last time an hour before dawn. The port authority's surveyors were down there now, tiny figures in yellow oilskins, picking through the wreckage with the reverent clumsiness of people who had arrived too late to save anything and too early to know what they'd found. One of them had tied a survey flag to the bowsprit, and the little pennant snapped in the wind off the flats, the only sail the Lark would ever carry again.

Vess turned the compass over in her hands.

It was a plain thing, really. Brass worn to the color of honey, glass clouded at one edge where moisture had gotten in years ago and never left. The needle didn't point north. It hadn't pointed north since the night Odile Coldharbour cracked the strongbox in the captain's cabin and the mechanism inside — some clever arrangement of counterweights and a lodestone core that his mother must have commissioned from a watchmaker who asked no questions — had done its work and gone quiet. Now the needle swung lazily, aimlessly, settling wherever gravity and mood suggested, like a dog that had finished its errand and forgotten why it had been sent.

She'd stolen this compass from Odile's saddlebag eleven months ago, on a night in Corriander Wells when he'd been three drinks into forgetting his mother and she'd been sober enough to feel ashamed of it. She'd told herself it was just a job. Someone had paid her — she'd never learned who, though she had her suspicions now, suspicions that began and ended with a dead woman's foresight — and the compass had seemed like easy coin. A trinket. An heirloom nobody would miss.

It had not been a trinket. It had been a key, and a map, and a message, and a mother's hand reaching out from beyond the grave to take her son by the collar and say: *come here. There's something you need to see.*

And Vess Corrane, thief, liar, occasional coward, had been the hand that delivered it. Badly. Late. With a great deal of running involved.

"You're going to wear a groove in that thing," said a voice behind her, "if you keep turning it like that."

She didn't startle. She'd heard him coming — she always heard him coming; the man walked like a herd of cattle with opinions — but she let him get close before she turned, because there was something in watching him approach that she wasn't ready to name yet. Odile came up the last stretch of the trail with his ranger's coat unbuttoned and his hat pushed back, and he looked, for the first time since she'd known him, like a man who had set down something heavy and wasn't sure yet whether his shoulders remembered how to stand without it.

"They gave you back your badge," she said.

"They gave me back my badge." He stopped beside her, close enough that their shadows touched on the salt. "Along with an apology, a commendation, and approximately four hundred pages of paperwork explaining why none of what happened was anyone's fault in particular."

"Was it?"

"Was it what?"

"Anyone's fault."

Odile was quiet for a while. Down in the basin, a surveyor's hammer rang against something — a hull plate, maybe, or a coffin nail. The sound carried up the cave walls and out across the flats, thin and bright as a bell.

"Quill's fault," he said finally. "Quill's fault mostly. And my mother's, a little, for trusting him with the truth instead of me." He shook his head. "No. That's not fair. She trusted him because she had no one else. That's not a fault. That's just what being alone costs."

Vess looked at him sideways. The wind was pulling at his hair, and the morning light was doing something generous with his face, softening the lines that the last six tides had carved there, and she thought: *you could tell him now. You could tell him what you were actually sent to find, and what you actually kept, and what the false back of the compass held besides the second copy of the papers.* But the moment passed, the way such moments do, and she only said:

"She paid my father to hide this ship. Did you know that? Before all of it. Before Quill, before the forgery. She found out what the fleet was going to do — scuttle her, forge the concessions, bury the whole rotten charter — and she couldn't stop it, so she made sure it couldn't stay buried. She hired a smuggler to beach the Lark in a dying sea and keep his mouth shut forever."

"I knew," Odile said. "Tam told me. After."

"After he sold us out, or after he testified?"

"After both. He's chatty when he's guilty." Odile's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He says my mother paid your father double what the fleet offered him to talk. Double, and a promise: that someday, if the debt was ever called, it would bring her son here."

"And it did," Vess said. "Eventually. Eleven months late, and by way of a thief who nearly got you drowned, shot, arrested, and blown up."

"Nearly," Odile agreed. "But not."

* * *

**Odile**

They walked down into the basin together, because Odile wanted to see her once more before the surveyors fenced her off, and because — though he wouldn't have said it aloud, not yet, maybe not ever — he wanted to see what Vess would do when she thought he wasn't watching.

What she did was stop at the gangway, or what remained of it, and put her hand flat against the hull the way you'd touch the flank of a horse you'd ridden hard and loved anyway. She stood there a long moment. Her lips moved. Whatever she said, she said it to the ship, or to the sea that had abandoned the ship, or possibly to a father Odile had never met who had spent the last twenty years of his life keeping a promise at the bottom of a dry ocean.

Odile looked away and gave her the privacy of it.

The interior of the wreck was a country of shadow and salt. The fleet's divers had stripped the cargo holds clean — everything of value, everything of evidence, hauled up and catalogued and entered into the port authority's ledgers under seal — but they couldn't strip the bones. Odile walked the main deck with his boots ringing on planks that hadn't felt sun in thirty years, and he thought about his mother walking these same boards, alive, furious, desperate, planning. He tried to imagine her voice. He couldn't. He had been six when she died, and what he remembered of her was a smell — cedar and lamp oil — and a pair of hands braiding his hair too tight, and nothing else. Everything else had been Quill's telling. Quill's version. *Your mother was restless, boy. Your mother loved the horizon more than she loved you.*

Lies. All of it lies, built like a seawall around a man's heart, and Odile had lived behind that wall for twenty-five years and called it love.

"She was brave," Vess said, appearing beside him out of nowhere, the way she did. "Your mother. I've read the letters. In the strongbox there were letters — you know that? Personal ones. The port authority has them sealed, but I—" She caught herself. Smiled the smile that had gotten her out of a hundred locked rooms and probably twice as many locked hearts. "I may have seen them. Briefly. Through no fault of my own."

"Of course you did."

"She wrote about you. In almost every one. *My son will come here when I'm gone,* she wrote to your father— to Tam's father, I mean, to the smuggler. *My son will come here, and when he does, tell him the sea remembers what men forget.*"

Odile stopped walking.

The cabin of the Meridian Lark was ahead of them, its door long since fallen, its windows broken outward by the demolition blast. Light fell through the gaps in slanted bars, laying stripes across the deck like the ribs of some enormous, patient animal. His mother had stood in that light. Had written those words in that room. Had hidden a compass in her son's saddlebag's future and a secret in a strongbox and a debt in a stranger's honor, and she had done it all alone, because the man she should have trusted had already begun selling her out.

"The sea remembers what men forget," Odile repeated.

"It's a good line," Vess said. "Smugglers' kids get all the good lines."

"My mother was a salvager's daughter. She married into the trade." He looked at Vess. "You could have kept the second copy. The one in the compass. You could have walked away at any point in the last week and been rich, or safe, or both. Why didn't you?"

Vess was quiet for a long time. Out beyond the wreck, through the broken stern windows, the salt flats stretched away to the horizon, blinding white, featureless, the floor of a sea that had given up on itself. Somewhere out there was the edge of the desert, and past the desert was the rest of Vess's life, which had always, until very recently, consisted entirely of getting away.

"Because you would have come looking," she said finally. "For the compass. For me. And I—" She stopped. Started again. "I've spent my whole life being the person who leaves first. It's easier. You never have to find out what it feels like when someone else leaves you. And then there was this idiot ranger chasing me across a salt flat for eleven months, too stubborn to quit, too honorable to lie even when lying would have saved him, and I kept thinking: *he'll leave too. Everyone leaves. Just wait.*" She shrugged, a small, sharp motion, like shedding a weight. "You didn't leave."

"No," Odile said.

"So I didn't either."

* * *

**Vess**

They parted at the edge of the desert, because that was the plan, and plans were the only language Vess had ever been fluent in.

The port authority had offered Odile a position — something official, something with a title, something that would have meant staying in the port city and attending meetings and being celebrated by the same institutions that had spent thirty years ignoring what the salvage fleet was. He'd declined. Vess hadn't asked him why. She already knew. Some men are built for harbors, and some men are built for the open country between harbors, and Odile Coldharbour, she was fairly certain, would die of politeness within a month of his first committee meeting.

So: the edge of the desert. Late afternoon, the light going long and amber, the sand giving way to salt giving way to the hardpan road that ran south toward whatever came next. Odile's horse stamped and blew. Vess's own mount — acquired legally, for once, purchased with the last of the money she'd sworn she'd never spend on anything honest — waited beside it with the patience of an animal that had learned not to expect much.

"This is where we split," Vess said.

"Looks like it."

"I go south. You go... wherever rangers go when they're not being rangers."

"North," Odile said. "There's a stretch of high country above the flats. Nobody patrols it. Nobody maps it. I've been thinking somebody ought to."

"Somebody ought to," Vess agreed gravely.

They stood there. The wind moved. Somewhere behind them, miles back, the basin was drying into legend — and it *was* a legend already; Vess had heard the stories mutating in the port city's taverns within two days of the hearing, growing new limbs and brighter feathers with each retelling. The ship in the desert. The ranger who fought the tide. The thief who stole the truth. By midsummer there would be songs, and by next winter the songs would be lies, and in ten years the lies would be the only version anybody remembered. That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked. The sea remembered what men forgot, and men, in Vess's experience, remembered whatever was most convenient.

She realized Odile was looking at her.

"What?" she said.

"You still haven't told me what else was in the compass."

Ah. There it was.

Vess looked out at the desert, at the horizon doing its eternal impression of a promise, and weighed, one final time, the cost of honesty against the comfort of a well-kept secret. The false back of the compass had held more than the second copy of the concession papers. It had held a letter — one letter, unsigned, in a woman's hand — addressed not to Odile, not to the smuggler, not to any authority living or dead, but to *the one who carries this*. To whoever ended up holding the compass when the story was done. To her, in other words. To Vess Corrane, thief, by way of a dead woman's arithmetic that had somehow, impossibly, accounted for her.

*If you are reading this,* the letter said, *then my son did not come alone. Good. He was never any good at being alone, though he'll insist otherwise. Keep the compass. It points at nothing now, and that is the point. A compass with no north belongs to whoever chooses the direction. Choose well.*

"He doesn't need to know," Vess said aloud.

"Vess."

"He doesn't need to know *everything*, Odile. Nobody knows everything. Not about their mothers, not about themselves. You got the truth that mattered. The forgery, the ship, the debt. You got your mother's handwriting and her plan and her stubbornness. Let a woman keep one letter."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, real laugh, the first she'd heard from him that wasn't aimed at anything.

"One letter," he said. "Fine. But I want it noted that I know you're keeping it, and that I'm choosing not to ask, and that this is the single most out-of-character act of restraint in the entire history of the Coldharbour family."

"Noted," Vess said. "I'll have it engraved."

* * *

**Odile**

He watched her ride south until she was a smudge, and the smudge was a speck, and the speck was gone.

Then he sat on his horse at the edge of the desert for a long time, doing nothing, which was a skill he had never previously possessed and was finding, at thirty-one, surprisingly difficult and surprisingly sweet.

The papers were gone — surrendered to the port authority, copied, sealed, entered into the permanent record. The story of them was his, though. That was the inheritance nobody could confiscate: the knowing. His mother's face in the words she'd written. The shape of the lie Quill had built around his childhood, and the exact dimensions of the door he'd walked through to get out of it. Harrow Quill himself, awaiting trial in the port city, had sent him one letter — a single page, no apology in it anywhere, only a navigation lesson, the last one, written in the same steady hand that had once taught a fatherless boy to read the stars: *True north is not a place, Odile. It is a decision you make every morning. I made mine badly. Make yours better.*

Odile had burned it. Then, standing over the ashes, he had wished he hadn't. Then he had decided that wishing was allowed, and that grief for a living man was still grief, and that both things could be true at once, the way a tide can be both a return and a leaving.

He turned his horse north.

The high country was waiting — unmapped, unpatrolled, full of whatever filled places nobody bothered to look at. He'd requisitioned survey equipment that morning. He'd filed a route plan with an office that had no idea what to do with it. He'd bought, from a bemused chandler near the docks, a brand-new compass, factory-true, needle swinging obediently toward a north everyone agreed on.

It was in his pocket now. He had no intention of using it.

He was halfway up the first rise when he heard the hoofbeats behind him, and he didn't turn around, because he was smiling too hard to manage it, and because some part of him — the part his mother had built, the part that had chased a thief across a salt flat for eleven months without ever quite wanting to catch her — had known, with a certainty that no instrument could improve upon, exactly which direction she would choose.

"Well?" he called, without turning. "South, is it?"

There was a pause. He could hear her horse deciding.

"As it happens," Vess Corrane said, drawing level with him, "I hear the north is unmapped."

"It is."

"Terrible state of affairs."

"Someone ought to do something about it."

They rode north together, side by side, into the wide amber evening, while behind them the basin dried and the legend grew, and the Meridian Lark slept on beneath her shroud of salt, dreaming, at last, of nothing — and the compass in Vess's pocket swung slow and idle in its brass housing, pointing at nothing at all, pointing, in other words, at everything they hadn't found yet.
