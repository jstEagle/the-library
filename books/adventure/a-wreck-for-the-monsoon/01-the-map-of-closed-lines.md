## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The rain came sideways off the fells, the way it always did in Harrow Junction, and Jude Underhill stood under the water tower's tin skirt and watched it turn the yard to soup. Six sidings, four of them dead, two pretending otherwise. A signal box that hadn't signaled anything since the company pulled its men out three winters ago. Mud to the axle.

He'd been keeping this place alive the way a man keeps a dying dog alive — out of habit, out of guilt, and because nobody had the heart to finish it.

"Jude!" Bramble's voice carried from the cook shack, thin over the drumming rain. "Grub's on, and Tamsin's about to fight Okonkwo again!"

"About what?"

"The boiler patch! She says he blessed it with ash and now it'll hold forever. He says she's a heathen who'll crack it by Friday."

Jude allowed himself half a smile. That was the whole arithmetic of his life these days: one crew, seven mouths, and just enough contract work — repairing sidings nobody used, reballasting track nobody rode — to keep them fed and the creditors at arm's length. The Northern Consolidated had closed this junction and every line feeding it, stamped CLOSED — GEOLOGICAL on the paperwork, and moved on. But track doesn't care what the paperwork says. Track rots or holds depending on whether somebody loves it.

Jude loved it. That was the problem with him, his ex-wife had said. He loved things that couldn't love him back.

Inside the cook shack it was steam and woodsmoke and the smell of Bramble's barley stew, which was mostly barley and only occasionally stew. Tamsin Cole sat with her boots up on an empty crate, seventeen years old and fireman-proud, her hands still black from the morning's firing despite Bramble's scrubbing rules. Across from her, Okonkwo turned his spoon with ceremonial slowness, as if eating were a rite he was permitting the food to undergo.

"She cracked the firebox door," Okonkwo said gravely, "and did not knock."

"I knocked," Tamsin said.

"You knocked *after*. The knocking must come before, so the old ones in the smoke know you're coming. Otherwise they think you're stealing their heat."

"They're dead, Okonkwo. They're not in the smokebox."

"That is exactly where they are." He said it without a flicker of doubt, and Tamsin threw a crust at him, and he caught it without looking, and Jude felt the familiar ache underneath the warmth — the ache that said *this is borrowed, all of it, and the bill is coming.*

The bill was literally coming. It was folded in his breast pocket, third letter that season, same heavy cream paper, same block capitals:

**UNDERHILL. THE ACCOUNT STANDS OPEN. WE WILL FIND YOU WHEREVER YOU WORK.**

No signature. No amount named. Just that open account, standing there like a hole in the track ahead of you at night. Seven years since Kestrel Pass. Seven years since Elias Wren went into that valley with a work train and forty tons of company freight and never came out, and the inquiry blamed Wren, and the blame had slid downhill like scree until it settled on the man who'd signed the maintenance reports under him. Jude Underhill. Disgraced, license suspended, unemployable anywhere the company's shadow reached — which was everywhere worth working.

They said Elias had been running too fast for the conditions. They said his survey notes were falsified. Jude had known Elias Wren for eleven years, had learned everything from him except how to stay out of trouble, and Elias had never once run fast for conditions. The man had treated speed like a debt too.

Jude reached into his coat and took out the compass. Brass case gone soft-edged with handling, the glass scratched, the needle doing its slow drunkard's circle whenever it got near the stove iron before settling north with a small stubborn dignity. On the back, worn nearly smooth: **E.W. — FOR THE MAN WHO READS THE GROUND.**

Elias had pressed it into his hand the last night they'd shared a campfire, up on the Kestrel survey, and said, "You'll need this more than me soon, lad," and laughed like he'd made a joke, and three weeks later the pass had eaten him.

*Reads the ground.* Jude set the compass on the table between the stew bowls. The needle swung, steadied, pointed north — toward the pass, though he tried not to think of it that way.

"You're doing the face again," Tamsin said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're talking to the compass instead of us."

"It's better conversation than yours."

Okonkwo looked at the needle, then at Jude, and said nothing, which from Okonkwo was a sermon. He believed instruments held the ghosts of their owners. He believed the compass still belonged to Elias, and was only lending itself to Jude, and would want to go home someday. Jude had told him once that was superstition. Okonkwo had replied that a man who talked to mud for a living should be careful about what he called superstition.

* * *

She came at dusk, when the rain had thinned to a gray drifting mist and the yard lamps made yellow coins in the wet air. Jude heard the gate first — nobody used the gate; people climbed the fence like honest folk — and then footsteps on the ballast, unhurried, placed precisely, the walk of someone who measured ground for a living.

Marguerite Voss had not changed except in the ways that mattered. Same lean frame in a good coat ruined by travel, same sharp chin, same eyes that went over everything like an assessor. Her hair was shorter. There was a new scar through one eyebrow. She stopped six feet away, which was the distance of a woman who knew exactly how much she was disliked.

"Underhill."

"Voss." He kept his voice flat. "I'd say welcome to Harrow Junction, but it's a mudhole with a flagpole."

"I've worked worse." She glanced at the dead sidings, the rusted points, the signal box with its blind windows. "You're maintaining lines the company abandoned."

"Somebody has to keep the dog fed."

"You could have gone coastal. Deepwater ports. They don't ask about old inquiries."

"And yet."

"And yet." She took off one glove, finger by finger, a negotiator's trick he remembered from a dozen survey camps: Voss undressing her hands meant Voss getting serious. "I have a contract, Underhill. Salvage and reopening, one line, one season. Good money. Money your crew hasn't seen since the company still pretended to care about this junction."

"We're fine."

"Bramble bought flour on credit Tuesday. From two different merchants."

Jude said nothing. Voss had always done her homework; it was what made her dangerous. That and the fact that she'd been right about the Glenmoor cut, and the Ashford trestle, and every argument they'd ever had, except the one that ended their partnership, where Jude remained convinced to this day that he'd been right and simply hadn't survived being right.

"Which line?" he asked, though he already felt the answer coming up through his boots like frost.

"Kestrel."

The word landed in the yard like a dropped rail. Somewhere down the dead siding, wind moved through the weeds growing between the ties, a long dry whisper.

"No," Jude said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms. The company closed that line. Closed means closed. Nobody touches Kestrel — not salvage crews, not surveyors, not fools. You know that better than anyone. Your backers can't have filed—"

"My backers filed as an independent recovery concern. The permits are clean. The window is short." She stepped closer, and the lamp caught the scar above her eye. "Calving season opens the upper valley in roughly five weeks, Underhill. For two weeks, maybe less, the ice pulls back far enough to reach the old right-of-way. Then it closes for another year. I need a crew in and out inside that window, and I need an engineer who can read ground that wants to kill him."

"Find another engineer."

"I found twelve. Eleven said no. One said yes and then read the route sheet and left town without paying his hotel bill." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "You're the twelfth. And the only one whose name is already written all over those mountains."

"Then your backers should hire a ghost."

"They tried. Ghosts charge too much." She let the joke sit, then killed it. "Jude. Five weeks. Two weeks of window. A line that's been shut eight years, a tunnel they blew shut, and a valley where—" 

"Don't."

"—where your mentor died. Where everyone says Elias Wren died running blind and fast and stupid."

Jude's hand had gone to his coat pocket without his permission. He could feel the compass through the wool, cold as a coin from a river. "Get off my yard, Marguerite."

"In a moment." She reached into her satchel, unhurried, and drew out a folded sheet of paper — not a contract, not a map. Something older, softer at the creases, handled many times. "Before I do. Tell me if this handwriting looks familiar."

She unfolded it. Even at six feet, even in bad light, Jude knew it. He'd spent eleven years reading that hand on survey notes and bridge calculations and margin jokes in the corners of gradient profiles. Elias wrote numbers the way other men wrote prayers.

"Where did you get that?" His voice came out lower than he intended.

"Elias sold me a set of Kestrel survey sheets, eight years ago, the week before he went up the final time. Paid me a visit I've never understood and never forgotten. He said—" Voss paused, and for the first time since she'd walked through the gate, she looked uncertain, and that frightened Jude more than anything else she'd said. "He said, *if I don't come back, give these to Underhill, because he reads the ground, and the ground remembers.*"

The rain ticked on the tin roof. Down the yard, Tamsin was laughing at something Okonkwo had said, the sound carrying bright and ordinary across the mud, and Jude stood very still with the whole past rising around him like floodwater.

"He gave you his survey sheets," Jude said slowly, "and told you to bring them to me. Eight years ago."

"Yes."

"And you waited eight years."

"I tried to get the contract approved eight years ago. The company buried my application so deep it came back fossilized." Her jaw tightened. "Someone didn't want that line reopened, Jude. Not then, not now. My current backers got the permit through channels I don't fully trust, for reasons they won't fully explain, and I'm going anyway, with or without you, because I have questions that have kept me awake for eight years. But I'd rather go with the twelfth engineer than alone."

Jude took the paper from her. Elias's figures marched down the page, immaculate, alive — and there, in the bottom margin, in that same prayerful hand, a note Jude had never seen, in a code the two of them had invented years ago for marking hazards too ugly to put in official reports:

**GROUND SOUNDS WRONG. NOT GEOLOGY. ASK WHO BENEFITS.**

The compass in his pocket seemed suddenly heavy as an anchor fluke. North, toward the pass. Toward the tunnel they'd blown shut. Toward whatever the company had needed buried badly enough to bury a mentor along with it.

Dread sat in Jude's chest like a stone. But underneath the dread, unwelcome as a spring thaw, something else was moving. Curiosity. The oldest disease of engineers.

"One condition," Jude heard himself say.

Voss raised an eyebrow. "Name it."

"I read the route first. All of it. Every sheet Elias left. Before my crew hears a word of terms." He folded the survey page and put it inside his coat, next to the letter, next to the compass — the whole weight of it settling against his ribs. "And Voss — if I find out why the company really blew that tunnel, I'm not signing anything until I know whose side of it you're on."

For a moment she just looked at him, and something passed across her face that was almost respect, or almost grief, or almost both wearing the same coat.

"Fair," she said. "Come to the hotel at eight. Bring your ghosts."

She walked back out the gate the way she'd come, precise steps in the mud, and behind her the yard lamps burned, and somewhere north, beyond the fells and the darkening rain, a glacier held its breath around whatever it had been paid to keep.
