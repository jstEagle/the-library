## 2. Hired Hands and Old Wounds

Tamsin Cole had three rules for reading a room: count the exits, count the money, and count who's lying. The yard office at Mirefoot Junction had one exit, no money, and everybody lying — which was why she liked it.

She stood at the window with a mug of Bramble's barley tea, watching the woman in the grey traveling coat walk the length of their dead siding like she owned it. Good boots. Surveyor's stride. A leather map case slung crosswise, the way soldiers carry rifles. Tamsin had grown up around company men and company women, and this one moved like neither. She moved like someone paying a debt of her own.

Inside, Jude Underhill sat with his back to the door, which was his way of pretending he hadn't heard the boots on gravel. He was cleaning a wrench that didn't need cleaning. He'd been cleaning it for ten minutes.

"That's her," Tamsin said. "The surveyor."

"I know."

"She's got a map case worth more than our whole engine."

"I know, Tam."

"She's coming here."

Jude set the wrench down with the care of a man defusing something. "Then she can knock like everyone else."

Marguerite Voss did not knock. She opened the door, took in the sagging stove, the peeling notice board papered with years-old closure orders, and Jude's back, and said, "Underhill. You've let the third rail of the east siding rot through. I could see it from the road."

"Nobody runs the east siding."

"Somebody will." She dropped a document tube on the table between them. "Kestrel Line. Thirty-one miles of mountain grade, closed eight years ago by executive order. I'm reopening it. I need a crew that can clear rock, rebuild two viaducts, and run a locomotive through country nobody's touched since the closures. I need you."

Jude finally turned. Tamsin watched his face do the thing it always did — go flat and careful, a man pulling shutters over windows. "No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"Don't need to. Kestrel Pass is a graveyard with a fence around it. Company closed it for geological instability, and geological instability doesn't care about your contract."

"The instability is seasonal," Voss said. "I have a window. Two weeks, when the calving season opens the high route before it seals it again. Clear the line inside that window or not at all."

Tamsin saw it then — the flicker behind Jude's eyes. Not greed. Something worse. Recognition. He knew that window. He'd been raised on that window, back when Elias Wren was alive and teaching him how ice kept time better than any timetable.

"And if we say no?" Jude asked.

Voss looked around the office slowly, cataloguing it: the cold stove, the empty coal bunkers, the letter from the debt collectors pinned under a rusted spike where Jude thought nobody could read it. Tamsin had read it. Everyone had read it. It was hard not to, the way it was pinned there like a threat nailed to a church door.

"Then you keep repairing sidings," Voss said, "for people who don't use them. Until the letters stop being polite." She left the tube on the table. "The offer stands until sundown."

Her boots faded up the gravel. Tamsin turned from the window. "Well?"

"There's no well about it. We're not going."

"We haven't voted."

"There's nothing to vote on—"

"Chapter four, section two," Tamsin said, grinning now, because she'd memorized it just for moments like this. "Crew decisions of consequence are made by majority, and the engineer gets one vote same as anyone, because he keeps saying he's just another hand when it suits him."

Jude stared at her. "You wrote that constitution when you were nineteen and hungover."

"And it's held up in every arbitration since. Okonkwo! Bramble! Meeting!"

* * *

Okonkwo heard the argument start before he reached the shed, and so he brought his tools with him, because in his experience arguments about contracts went better when at least one person present was holding a hammer.

He arranged himself on an oil drum near the stove and listened the way he listened to engines — not to the words but to the rhythm underneath. Jude's voice ran tight and quick, a bearing running dry. Tamsin's ran hot and bright. And Marguerite Voss, invited back in against Jude's wishes, spoke in long even strokes, like a file drawn across metal.

"The Kestrel Line is cursed," Okonkwo said, when there was a gap.

Everyone looked at him.

"Not in the way you're afraid of," he continued, addressing Jude directly, because Jude was the one whose fear needed managing; Tamsin's fear was decorative. "Every line has its dead. The Kestrel has more than most, and they have not been given their due. That is different from a curse. A curse you flee. A debt you settle."

"You sound like the debt collectors," Jude muttered.

"I sound like a man who has rebuilt forty engines and learned that what you refuse to look at is what fails first. Always the hidden crack. Always the weld nobody inspected." He tapped the drum beneath him. "You have been hiding in this valley for three years, Underhill. Hiding is a load-bearing behavior. Eventually the structure comes down."

Voss unrolled her maps across the bench, and Okonkwo leaned in despite himself. He loved a good drawing the way other men loved drink. The Kestrel Line climbed out of the junction country through the pass, along the shoulder of the glacier valley, over the great viaduct at Corrie Narrows, and up to the terminus at Kestrel Head, which had once fed ore to the coast and now fed nothing but snow.

"Two weeks," Voss said again. "The glacier calves in late spring. Before it does, the melt opens the lower route. After it does, the whole valley seals until autumn. We work inside the window or we don't work."

"What's the cargo?" Okonkwo asked. "Salvage pays by weight. What are we hauling out?"

"Rolling stock. Rails. Whatever's recoverable."

It was a smooth answer. Too smooth. Okonkwo had spent a lifetime listening to smooth answers come out of broken machines. But he also watched Jude's face while Voss gave it, and what he saw there was not suspicion. It was grief, old and well-practiced, the kind a man carries like a stone in his boot until he forgets which foot limps.

Elias Wren had died on that line. Everyone in the crew knew the shape of it, if not the details — Jude never gave details. Mentor, friend, the man who'd taught him everything, killed in the collapse that ended the Kestrel Line and ended Jude's career in the same breath. The company had blamed Wren. The company had blamed everyone except itself, as companies do.

"Your engineer says no," Okonkwo said to Voss. "But the engineer is one vote." He raised his hand. "I say yes. Not for your money. For the dead. Somebody owes them a proper accounting, and I think it might be us."

* * *

Bramble came last, as Bramble always did, carrying the supper pot because Bramble believed no decision of consequence should be made on an empty stomach and no meeting should be allowed to end without feeding the people who'd suffered through it.

He served stew into tin bowls and listened with his head cocked, the way he listened to everything, filing it. That was his role and he knew it: cook, historian, keeper of the crew's memory. Three years of Mirefoot Junction lived in his ledger — every job taken and refused, every coin earned and owed, every night Jude sat up with Elias Wren's brass compass in his hands, turning it over like a question he couldn't stop asking.

He'd written it all down. Someday, he told himself, it would matter. Historians were patient people. They had to be, to love the past, which never reciprocates.

"So," Bramble said, ladling out the last bowl. "Let me summarize for the record. Miss Voss offers us a contract on the one line in these mountains we've sworn never to touch, inside a window measured in days, for pay that would clear our debts twice over. Our engineer says no. Our mechanic says yes. Tamsin?"

"Yes," Tamsin said, mouth full. "Obviously yes. Have you seen our coal bunker? I've seen fuller graveyards."

"Bramble?"

Bramble sat down heavily on the bench and looked at Jude across the steam rising off the stew. "You know my opinion already."

"Say it anyway."

"Elias Wren deserves more than a compass on a shelf." He said it gently, but he said it plainly, because kindness without plainness was just flattery. "Three years you've kept that man's ghost in this office and fed it nothing but silence. I've watched you turn down six contracts that would take us within fifty miles of that pass. You're not avoiding danger, Jude. You're avoiding the place where the story stopped making sense. And stories that stop making sense don't heal. They fester." He tapped the ledger. "I vote yes. For the record. For the history."

Jude looked down at his untouched stew for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, bitter, defeated, the laugh of a man whose family has outvoted him and knows it.

"One condition," he said to Voss. "We do the work you contracted. Salvage, clearing, rails. Nothing else. If I find anything up there that isn't yours to take, it stops being a salvage job and starts being mine. Agreed?"

Something flickered across Voss's face — fast, quickly filed away, but Bramble caught it, and wrote it down later by lamplight: *The surveyor agreed too quickly. She wanted that clause in the contract. Ask yourself why.*

"Agreed," Voss said.

"Then I'm your engineer." Jude stood, and Bramble watched the change come over him — not joy, nothing like joy, but a kind of grim settling, a man picking up a weight he'd put down and finding his arms remembered how. "We leave in three days. Tamsin, the boiler on Number Seven needs a full washout before she'll climb a grade like Kestrel. Okonkwo, I want every brake shoe replaced and the tender doubled. Bramble—" 

"I know," Bramble said. "Food for twelve men for six weeks, packed for mountain transport."

"Fourteen men," Voss corrected quietly. "My people join you at the pass."

Jude paused. "How many of your people?"

"Two. A foreman and a powder man."

Bramble wrote that down too, and underlined it, because Voss had hesitated half a second before saying *two*, and in his long experience of reading ledgers and faces both, hesitation was where the truth kept its office hours.

That night, after the others slept, Bramble found Jude out by the water tower, standing in the dark with the brass compass open in his palm, its needle swinging slow and restless, hunting north like a dog that smells a storm coming.

"Which way does it point for you?" Bramble asked him.

Jude closed the lid. "Toward the pass," he said. "It's pointed toward the pass for three years. I just finally ran out of reasons not to follow it."

Above the eastern ridge, the glacier country lay white and silent under the stars, holding whatever it held, waiting for the melt.
