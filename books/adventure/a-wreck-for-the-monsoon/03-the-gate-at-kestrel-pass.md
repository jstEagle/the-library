## 3. The Gate at Kestrel Pass

The pass announced itself three hours before we saw it, the way bad news does. First the birds went quiet. Then the wind changed and came down off the ice carrying that smell I'd hoped never to breathe again — cold stone and old iron and something underneath like a struck match long after the match is dead.

Tamsin smelled it too. She stood up on the running board of the little Porter locomotive we'd hauled up in pieces and reassembled at the last water stop, her red hair whipping out from under her cap, and she shouted over the clatter: "Smells like somebody's been burning something."

"Rock dust," Okonkwo called back from the cab, where he rode with his hands resting on the throttle like a man calming a horse. "High passes always smell of it."

They were both wrong, and I didn't correct them, because the truth was worse. It wasn't burning. It was unburning — the ghost of powder smoke soaked so deep into the stone of Kestrel Pass that eight years hadn't washed it out. I knew that smell. Every man who ever set a charge under contract knew it. The company had used enough black powder at this pass to season the rock for a generation.

We came around the shoulder of the mountain at noon and there it was.

The tunnel mouth.

Or what was left of it.

* * *

I made Tamsin stop the loco two hundred yards out. I don't know why. Instinct, maybe — the same instinct that tells you not to walk up to a dog that's gone still. We sat in the cab and looked at it.

The Kestrel Line had run through solid granite here, a bore half a mile long, driven by Elias Wren's own survey crews back when the company still pretended to build things instead of bury them. Elias had talked about that tunnel the way other men talk about their children. *Clean rock,* he'd said. *The cleanest bore on the whole division. A hundred years, Jude. That tunnel would stand a hundred years.*

What stood now was not a tunnel. It was a wound.

The entire face of the portal had been brought down in a single engineered collapse — not a blast but an execution. I could read it from where we sat, the way you can read a sentence in a language you hate yourself for knowing. They'd drilled the charges in a fan pattern, deep-set, timed in sequence from the crown downward so the roof came first and the walls after, folding the mountain's own weight into the opening like a man folding a letter he doesn't want anyone else to read. Then they'd finished it by hand. There were scorch marks on the talus slope where they'd burned the debris with thermite lances to slag the rails into the rubble — I could see the glassy black runnels down the face of the spoil heap, frozen now, shining dully in the noon light like veins of tar.

"Geological instability," Marguerite Voss said behind me. She'd walked up from the second flatcar without my hearing her, which should have been impossible for a woman in surveyor's boots on ballast, and yet there she was, hands in her coat pockets, looking at the buried portal with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "That's what the closure order said. I pulled it myself from the division archive."

"You pulled it." I didn't turn around. "And you believed it?"

"I believed what it let me do." She came up beside me, close enough that I caught coal smoke and some sharp perfume fighting through it. "It got me a salvage permit. Nobody questions a salvage permit on a dead line. Nobody wants to think about it at all."

"That's the point of an order like that." I finally turned to look at her. "You don't write *we are hiding something here.* You write *the mountain is dangerous.* Mountains can't testify."

Something moved across her face — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Recognition, maybe. Or the discomfort of a woman realizing the man she'd hired had thought about this longer and harder than she'd budgeted for.

"You've seen demolition work before, Mr. Underhill."

"I've *done* demolition work before. That's how I know this isn't it." I pointed with my chin at the talus slope. "Look at the sequence marks. Crown first, then shoulders, then the invert. That's not panic blasting because a tunnel's failing. A failing tunnel gets plugged with whatever's cheap and fast — loose muck, timber cribs, a trainload of gravel if you have one handy. This was drilled. Timed. Finished with lances. Somebody spent weeks making sure nothing inside that bore would ever be reached by accident."

"And on purpose?"

I looked at her a long moment. "On purpose is your department, Miss Voss. You're the one with backers."

She held my look, and to her credit she didn't flinch, and she didn't lie, which I was beginning to understand was its own kind of tell with her. She simply said, "Get me through it, Mr. Underhill. What's on the other side of the rock is my problem."

* * *

We camped that night in the cut below the portal, because there was no other flat ground within four miles and because Voss wanted eyes on the face by first light. Okonkwo set the cook car against the cut wall with the firebox side inward, out of the wind, and Bramble produced a stew out of salt pork, dried apples, and what he claimed was a secret ingredient and what Tamsin swore on three separate occasions was just more salt pork. The crew ate well. Crews eat well when the work ahead frightens them; it's a superstition older than railroading.

I ate last, standing, the way I'd eaten for eight years. Old habit from the bad stretch, when eating sitting down felt like something a man with a debt had no right to do.

Elias's compass sat in my breast pocket. I'd taken to carrying it since the junction, since Voss said his name in that muddy yard and cracked something open in me that I'd spent eight years welding shut. It was a heavy brass thing, gimbal-mounted, the case engraved with his initials and, beneath them, in his own small precise hand: *K.P. 1889.* Kestrel Pass. He'd carried it driving this very line. Some nights I took it out and held it and tried to feel whether it pointed at north or at the past.

Tonight I didn't take it out. Tonight the pass itself was compass enough. Everything in me pointed at that sealed face of rock.

Okonkwo found me at the fire after the others turned in. He sat down across from me without asking, which was his way, and poured himself tea from the pot I'd let go cold, and drank it cold, which was also his way.

"You looked at that rock today like a doctor looks at a body," he said.

"Somebody killed it."

"Mm." He turned his cup slowly. "In my father's country, when a bad thing is done, they do not only hide it. They put a guard on it. Sometimes the guard is a man. Sometimes the guard is a story. Sometimes—" he nodded uphill, toward the faintly gleaming talus slope, "—it is forty tons of granite and a paper that says the mountain did it."

"You think there's a guard."

"I think a company that spends weeks drilling a fan pattern does not walk away afterward." He drank his cold tea and looked at me over the rim. "I think a guard does not need to be told to stay awake forever. I think a guard stays awake because staying awake is all he has left."

I wanted to ask him what he meant by that, and I was afraid he'd say *you,* and I was afraid he'd be right.

* * *

I woke before dawn to the sound of Tamsin swearing.

Not her ordinary swearing, which was creative and constant and mostly ornamental. This was short, flat, frightened swearing, the kind that means something real has broken, and I was out of my bedroll with my boots half-laced before my head finished waking.

The water stores were dead.

We'd cached six barrels at the camp — filled at the last good stream below the snowline, hauled up on the flats, enough for eleven days for eleven people plus boiler feed. All six barrels sat in their row against the cut wall exactly where we'd left them. And every one of them was empty. Not drained carelessly — drained *clean*, bungs pulled and set back on top of each barrel, burlap filters lifted out and folded and replaced, not a drop spilled on the ground. Sixty gallons of water, vanished into a night, leaving no puddle, no wet trail, no track in the frost-hardened mud except—

Except one.

A single line of boot prints came down from the direction of the pass, walked the length of the barrel row, and went back. Narrow heel. Even stride. No hesitation at any barrel, as if whoever it was had done this before, or had done it without needing to see. The prints were already softening at the edges as the morning frost gave up, dissolving even as we stood over them, so that in an hour there would be no proof at all that anyone had been there.

"They drank it?" Tamsin said. "Sixty gallons? Nobody drank sixty gallons."

"No," Okonkwo said quietly. He crouched at the edge of the print line, not touching it. "Nobody drank it. Somebody carried it away. Slowly. In something that did not slosh." He looked up at me. "There is a cistern somewhere. Or a pipe. Or—"

"Or the tunnel isn't as dead as it looks," Bramble said from behind us, and none of us laughed, because it wasn't a joke.

Voss came last, drawn by the noise, and stood over the barrels with her hands on her hips, and I watched her do the arithmetic I was doing — eleven people, no water, the nearest stream a full day's haul down the mountain, and the calving window Voss kept counting in her ledger like a woman counting heartbeats. Whatever she'd expected from this job, it hadn't been this. Or it had, and she'd hoped to reach the rock before it started.

"Well, Mr. Underhill," she said at last, and her voice was level, but I'd heard level voices on men standing on bridges that were about to fail. "You said somebody guarded this place. It appears the guard keeps regular hours."

"The guard knows we're here," I said. "He knew before we arrived. Those prints weren't made in a hurry. He walked down, opened six barrels, closed six barrels, and walked home. That's not sabotage." I looked up at the pass, at the black glassy runnels on the talus slope catching the first grey light. "Sabotage is anger. That was a message. *You are being watched. You may leave.*"

"And if we don't leave?"

I didn't answer her. Instead I dug into my breast pocket and took out Elias's compass, and held it flat on my palm between us, the needle swinging, settling, pointing — as it had pointed for eight years, as it would point tomorrow — not north.

At the pass.

"We're through that rock in three days," I said. "Water or no water. Guard or no guard. Because whatever the company buried here, Elias Wren died next to it, and I am done letting dead men do my pointing for me."

Behind us, up in the high silence of the pass, something shifted — a single stone ticking loose somewhere in the sealed face, rolling once, and stopping.

Like something clearing its throat.
