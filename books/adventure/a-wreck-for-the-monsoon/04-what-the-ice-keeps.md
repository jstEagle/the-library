## 4. What the Ice Keeps

The tunnel breathed cold at them for three days before it gave up its secret.

Tamsin felt it first as a draft on the back of her neck, a slow exhale from somewhere deep in the rock, like the mountain was deciding whether to let them in. She'd fired boilers since she was fourteen and she knew engines, knew coal, knew the particular temper of fire under pressure — but she didn't know ice. Nobody on the crew did, except maybe Okonkwo, who touched the tunnel mouth's shattered lip on the first morning and said nothing at all, which from Okonkwo was a sermon.

"Company blew it from inside," Jude had said that first day, running his thumb along the fracture lines in the rubble. "Charges set in sequence. Professional." He hadn't said the rest of what his face was saying. He didn't have to.

Now, three days in, they were through the worst of it. Voss's money had bought dynamite enough to open a throat through the collapse, and Tamsin's arms ached from swinging a pick beside Bramble, who swung his like he was chopping kindling for a stew he'd never finish. The air inside was old. That was the thing she kept coming back to — the *age* of it. Air that hadn't moved in years, tasting of stone dust and something sweeter underneath, something almost like engine oil gone to varnish.

"Steady on the left crown," Okonkwo called from below, voice flat as slate. "That shelf wants to come down and it wants to take your head with it."

Tamsin shifted her footing. Below her, lamplight wobbled across the debris field where the crew worked in a loose chain, passing rock hand to hand like a bucket brigade fighting a fire that had already won.

It was Bramble who found the first rail.

He stopped mid-swing, and the silence of him stopping carried further than any shout. Tamsin looked down and saw the lamp glow catch on something straight and true amid all the chaos — a length of track, polished clean by ice melt, running out of the dark ahead of them like a sentence someone had started years ago and never finished.

"That's not possible," Voss said. She'd come up the line quiet, the way she always did, and now she stood with her hands on her hips, surveying the impossible. "The pass collapsed. The whole approach is buried."

"The approach is buried," Jude said quietly. He'd come forward too, and Tamsin watched him crouch by the exposed rail, touch it the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This is past the collapse. This is *inside*."

They worked another hour in a silence that had changed texture entirely. Not the boredom-silence of hard labor anymore. Something closer to church.

Tamsin went in first when the gap finally opened wide enough for a body. She told herself it was because she was smallest. It wasn't. It was because she couldn't stand being behind anyone else one more minute.

The lamp found the train forty yards in.

She heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize — half laugh, half prayer — and then the others were crowding in behind her, and the light bloomed across the wreck, and nobody said anything for a long time.

A work train. A full work train, locomotive and six cars, lying exactly where it had died. The engine sat cocked against the tunnel wall at an angle no driver would ever have chosen, its boiler split along one seam like a ripe fruit, and the cars behind it were jackstrawed, some upright, some rolled, all of them furred with a rime of ice that caught their lamps and threw it back in a hundred small fires. Everything wore a skin of frost so thick it looked like the train had been dipped in candle wax. Mid-flight, she thought. Frozen mid-flight. Whatever had happened here had happened fast, and then the cold had closed over it like water.

"It's been sealed," Okonkwo said. His voice came out strange, hushed. "Sealed like a tomb. No scavengers. No salvage crews. Nothing has touched this in—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Everyone could do the arithmetic: the Kestrel Line had been closed for eleven years.

Voss moved along the wreck like a woman reading a letter. She climbed to the cab, wiped frost from the builder's plate, and read the number aloud. Then she found the manifest box, pried it open with her knife, and pulled out papers stiff as autumn leaves.

"Cargo manifest," she said. "Company seal." Her voice was level but Tamsin saw her thumb press white against the paper. "Tools. Rail stock. Bridge timber. Blasting supplies." She looked up. "Standard work train."

But Tamsin had already dropped into the second car, and the second car was wrong.

The crates were wrong.

She'd grown up around freight. She knew what a crate of tools weighed by the sound it made when you knocked on it — hollow, bright, a drum. These crates answered her knuckles with a dead thud, deep and dense, the sound of something packed solid to the corners. They were iron-banded, stenciled with numbers instead of words, and every one of them was chained down. Chained. To the car bed. With company-stamped shackles.

"Bramble," she called, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Come knock on this and tell me I'm mad."

Bramble came. He knocked. He listened the way he listened to everything, whole-bodied, and then he looked at her over the top of his spectacles and said, "Nobody chains down shovels, Tamsin."

"Nobody chains down shovels," she agreed.

Okonkwo appeared at the car door without seeming to cross the space between. He put his palm flat on the nearest crate and held it there a long moment, eyes half-closed, and when he took his hand away he wiped it slowly on his coat, once, like he was wiping off something that had gotten on him.

"Iron," he said. "Cast iron, stacked tight. Or lead." A pause. "Or worse than either."

"Payroll," Voss said from the doorway, too fast, too smooth. "Companies moved pay chests by rail all the time. It's nothing."

"Then why bury it under a mountain?" Tamsin asked.

The question just hung there. Voss didn't answer it. Jude, standing back near the engine with his lamp raised high, didn't answer it either, but Tamsin saw his face do something complicated — saw the dread in it sharpen into something else, something hungrier and more afraid.

* * *

They broke for hot food at the tunnel mouth, and the talk came fast and low, the way talk does when everyone's thinking the same dangerous thing. Forty tons of chained cargo. A train that died inside a tunnel the company swore had collapsed on its own. A demolition job done from the inside, precise as surgery.

"We should turn back," Bramble said, cradling his tin cup. "I want that on record. I'm saying it plain: we should turn back."

"You want to be paid," Tamsin said. "So do I. So does everyone."

"I want to be paid *and alive*, which I'm told is the better bargain."

Okonkwo stirred the fire with a length of wire, watching the coals. "The mountain knows what it buried," he said. "Question is whether it wants us knowing too."

Tamsin left them to it. She told herself she was going back in to check the bracing on the second car — real work, honest work — and maybe part of her was. But her boots carried her past the bracing and along the jackstrawed cars to the locomotive itself, to the cab where Voss had read the builder's plate, and she climbed up into it because the wreck had gotten its hooks in her and wouldn't let go.

The cab was a museum of one terrible moment. A lantern, unbroken, still hanging from its hook. A stoker's shovel leaned in the corner. On the floor, half-fused into the frost, lay a pocket watch, its hands stopped at twenty minutes to four.

And wedged down between the firebox casing and the wall, jammed there deliberate — she saw that right away, saw how it had been pushed deep and low, hidden, not lost — was a book.

She worked it free with numb fingers. Leather, water-swollen and refrozen a hundred times, cracked like old paint. She turned it toward her lamp.

*E. WREN — FIELD JOURNAL* was stamped into the cover, worn nearly smooth.

Tamsin stood very still in the dead man's cab with the dead man's book in her hands, and the wonder that had carried her all day curdled, slow and complete, into something colder. Elias Wren. She'd heard the name maybe twice, both times in Jude's silences rather than his speech. The mentor. The man the company blamed. The reason her engineer drank his tea staring at nothing.

He'd been on this train. Or close enough to hide his journal on it. And he'd hidden it — hidden it *deep*, hidden it like a man who expected someone to come looking someday, but only the right someone.

She thought about calling out. About holding it up in the firelight and letting Voss see it, letting everyone see it, because secrets were how crews got killed — she'd learned that much young.

But she looked at the journal again, and she thought about the crates chained down in the dark, and she thought about Marguerite Voss saying *it's nothing* too quickly, and she folded the book into her coat instead.

* * *

She found Jude alone at dusk, checking the bracework by lamplight, hammer in hand, tapping timbers the way a doctor taps a chest. He glanced up when her boots rang on the rail.

"Bracing's good," she said. "Better than good. You've got a gift for making rotten things hold together."

"That's the job description." He went back to his tapping.

Tamsin stood in the cold with her heart going harder than it should have. Then she reached into her coat and held out the journal, and watched his face when he read the name stamped on the cover.

Jude Underhill went absolutely still. The hammer slipped from his fingers and rang off the rail, and he didn't even flinch. He took the book in both hands like it might dissolve, turning it, touching the stamp, and when he looked up at her his eyes were wet and fierce and terrified all at once.

"Where."

"Wedge in the cab. Hidden on purpose. Jude — the crates are chained down, the demolition was inside-out, and the watch in the cab stopped at twenty to four. Somebody needs to tell me what happened on this line, and I don't think it's what Voss is paying us to believe."

He opened the journal with hands that weren't steady. The first page was dense with Elias's small, exact handwriting, and Tamsin watched her engineer read it, watched the color leave his face and something harder arrive in its place.

"Jude?"

"He wrote it down," Jude said softly. "All this time, I thought— He wrote it *down*." He looked at her, and behind him the tunnel mouth gaped black and breathing, keeping its forty tons of secret. "Tamsin. Get everyone away from the second car. Tonight. Before anyone else goes back in."

"Why? What's in the crates?"

Jude closed the journal and held it against his chest like a shield, or a wound.

"Not gold," he said. "Worse. Proof."
