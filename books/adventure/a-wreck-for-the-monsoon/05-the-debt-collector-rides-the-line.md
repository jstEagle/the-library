## 5. The Debt Collector Rides the Line

Marguerite Voss had learned to read men the way she read contour lines: by what they concealed. A ridge could hide a drop. A smile could hide a ledger. And Jude Underhill, standing at the edge of the firelight with his hands jammed in his coat pockets like a man holding himself together with friction, was hiding something the size of a mountain.

She waited until the camp settled. Tamsin had gone to check the water stores — again, since the sabotage two nights back — and Okonkwo was muttering over the locomotive's valve gear with a wrench and a prayer. Bramble had fed everyone until they were docile. That left the man himself, alone at the map table under a lantern, tracing a line on the Kestrel survey that Marguerite knew did not exist on any official document.

She crossed the camp without hurrying. Hurrying was for people with something to lose.

"Underhill."

He didn't look up. "Voss."

"You've been staring at that same stretch of grade for an hour. Either you've found a fault in my route or you've found something in it. Which?"

He turned the paper face-down, which answered her question better than words would have. "Go to sleep, Voss. You're paying me to drive spikes, not to talk."

"I'm paying you to do neither, if I'm honest. I'm paying you because you're the only engineer alive who's worked this line and isn't dead or bought." She pulled the second crate over and sat, uninvited, close enough to see his eyes in the lantern light. Grey, tired, and sharper than he wanted anyone to know. "So let's not pretend this is salvage."

The wind came down off the glacier with a sound like a door closing somewhere far away. Jude's jaw worked.

"Say what you came to say," he said.

"My backers aren't backers. They're company men. Directors, or close enough to it that their signatures still open doors this side of the mountains." She kept her voice level, conversational, the way you'd discuss weather. "They want something out of that valley. Quietly. Before the calving season seals the pass for another year."

"I figured as much the day you walked into my yard wearing boots that cost more than my crew's annual wages."

"Then you know why I can't tell them no."

"Do I?" He finally looked at her, and there it was — not anger, which she'd have known how to fight, but a kind of exhausted curiosity. "You're Marguerite Voss. You once re-surveyed the whole Delamere cut because the company's numbers were wrong, and you told them so in front of forty people. You don't scare easy and you don't sell cheap. So either they're paying you enough to drown your principles, or—"

"Or they're holding something over me." She said it flatly, before he could dress it up as anything gentler. "My brother. Alaric. He was a brakeman on the Kestrel Line. Six years ago."

Jude went very still.

"The company report says he died in a rockfall during routine maintenance," she continued. "I believed it for three years. Then I started asking questions, and the questions stopped being welcome, and then a man named Corliss showed up at my office with a folder of my brother's personnel file and a suggestion that a grieving sister with my debts might want to take a well-paid contract in the high country instead of making trouble." She smiled, and knew it didn't reach her eyes. "That's the arrangement, Underhill. I find their vault, they let me keep digging into how Alaric died. Or rather, they stop stopping me. It's not freedom. It's a leash with good leather."

For a long moment Jude said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere up-valley, the ice groaned — a low, tectonic complaint that Marguerite had stopped noticing days ago and now heard all at once, the way you hear your own heartbeat when someone mentions mortality.

"Elias Wren died on this line too," Jude said at last.

"I know."

"No, you don't." He reached inside his coat, and for one absurd second she thought he was going for a weapon. Instead he brought out a book — small, water-swollen at one corner, its canvas cover stained with something dark that had soaked in years ago. "Everyone knows Elias Wren died in the wreck of the Kestrel work train. Company version: he misjudged the load on the viaduct grade, took the curve too fast, killed himself and eleven others. They struck him from the rolls posthumously. Disgraced my mentor so thoroughly that his own family wouldn't claim the body." His thumb moved across the cover like a man touching a scar. "This is his field journal. Your crew found it frozen in the cab of that wreck we cleared last week. Tamsin gave it to me quietly, because she's smarter than both of us."

Marguerite looked at the journal and felt the ground shift under her assumptions — the specific vertigo of a surveyor whose benchmark turns out to be wrong. "And?"

"And read the page from the fourteenth of March." He opened it to a marked leaf and turned it toward her, angling the lantern.

The handwriting was precise, an engineer's hand, though the letters slanted hard, as if written in haste or cold:

*Company wire arrived tonight. Burial order confirmed. Not the tunnel — the PAYROLL. They mean to run her heavy and put her down in the ice above the collapse, seal the evidence with the bodies. Forty navvies in that shaft and the directors call it a geological event. W. says if I sign the manifest I'll be provided for. If I don't, I'll be blamed for whatever happens to the train. God forgive me, I think they mean to wreck her deliberately. I have copied the order and sent it south. If I do not come down the mountain, look to J.U.*

Marguerite read it twice. The second time, the words arranged themselves into a shape she recognized from six years of nightmares: *if I do not come down the mountain.* Her brother had been a brakeman. Brakemen rode the caboose. Eleven dead, the company said — but brakemen weren't listed among them, because Alaric hadn't been on the work train at all. He'd been part of the burial detail. He'd seen what went into the ice.

"They killed him," she said. Her voice sounded distant to herself. "Not a rockfall. He knew, and they—"

"He knew, and they put him on the next train down, and the next train down never arrived." Jude closed the journal gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "Elias was blamed for the wreck. I was his protégé, so I got the quieter treatment — ruined slowly, blacklisted line by line, hounded by debt collectors carrying paper I supposedly signed. Except I never signed it. The debts are manufactured, Voss. They exist to keep me running, because Elias mailed his copy of the burial order south, and the company has spent six years trying to find where it landed before I understood what I was holding."

The lantern flame bent in a gust. Up-valley, the ice spoke again, longer this time.

Marguerite sat with it. All of it. The contract she'd signed in a carpeted office while Corliss watched from the corner with the patience of a man who owned the clock. The folder on Alaric. The way the backers' instructions had grown more specific with every mile of track they reopened — *the vault first, the vault only, the vault before anything else* — until "salvage" had become a euphemism she'd been pretending not to translate.

"I thought I was using them," she said. "A funded expedition into the closed country, with me holding the survey. I was going to find the truth about Alaric on their money and hand them nothing but rocks."

"How's that going?"

"It was going fine until I realized they don't need me to succeed. They need me to fail *visibly*." She laughed, short and bitter. "If the expedition dies in the pass, it proves the line is cursed, closes the questions forever, and costs them nothing but equipment. Corliss isn't here to assist us, Underhill. He's here to make sure there are no witnesses either way. I've known that since the water stores were poisoned, because sabotage doesn't help the schedule and Corliss was the only man in camp who didn't complain about it."

Jude studied her across the lantern, and something in his expression changed — not softening exactly, but a recalibration, the way a bridge engineer re-rates a span after finding a crack. Two rivals, six years of professional contempt between them, discovering they'd been pulling against the same chain from opposite ends.

"So we're both hostages," he said.

"We're both hostages." She held out her hand, palm up, an old surveyor's gesture: *show me your instrument.* "Your move, engineer. We have maybe ten days before the calving opens and the window shuts. Corliss has eight men, rifles, and the only telegraph codebook. You have a crew that would die for each other, a journal that could hang half a boardroom, and a grudge the size of this valley. What do we do with all that?"

Jude looked down at the journal in his hands. When he spoke, his voice was low, meant for her alone.

"Elias wrote *look to J.U.* For six years I thought that meant hide. Stay invisible, stay alive, let the dead bury their own." He shook his head slowly. "But that's not what he meant. He meant *finish it*. Take the proof down the mountain. And I hid instead, because hiding felt like surviving."

"And now?"

"Now I'm starting to think survival and captivity are the same thing wearing different coats." He met her eyes. "We get the crew through the pass. We get the journal down. And when Corliss makes his move — and he will, probably at the worst possible moment the glacier can offer — we make sure the world sees exactly what the company buried under that ice. All of it. The gold, the order, the forty men, your brother, my mentor. Everything."

Marguerite extended her hand across the map table. After a moment, Jude took it. Her grip was cold and certain.

"Allies, then," she said. "Provisionally."

"Provisionally," he agreed. "Until you betray me, or I betray you, or the ice does it for both of us."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, Underhill."

Before he could answer, a whistle cut down the valley — not their locomotive, higher-pitched, coming from the direction of the pass. Marguerite was on her feet before the sound finished, because she knew engines the way she knew ridgelines, and that whistle belonged to a machine she'd only ever seen in company photographs.

Corliss's relief train. Arriving early. With cargo.

Jude was already reaching for his coat, the journal vanishing inside it. "Ten days just became fewer," he said.

Up-valley, the glacier groaned — long, deep, and unmistakably impatient — as if it had been listening the whole time.
