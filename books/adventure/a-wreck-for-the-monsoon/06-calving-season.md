## 6. Calving Season

Okonkwo woke to the sound of the mountain clearing its throat.

He had heard it before, in the copper country, in the flooded mines of his father's stories — a deep, grinding groan that came up through the soles of the boots before it ever reached the ears. He lay in his bedroll in the tunnel camp and listened to the glacier speak, and what it said was simple: *I am moving. Make your peace.*

By the time he had the stove lit, the others were stirring. Tamsin came out of the supply tent with her hair frozen flat on one side and her eyes already sharp. Bramble was stirring the porridge pot and muttering, as he always did, about the historical precedent for eating badly at altitude. Jude stood at the tunnel mouth with the field journal in his coat, staring up at the ice wall that crowned the pass like a judge's bench.

"Did you hear it?" Tamsin asked.

"Everyone heard it," Okonkwo said. "The mountain doesn't whisper."

Voss came down from the survey stakes with her notebook full of bad news. She spread the pages on a crate and weighted the corners with bolts.

"The calving's started early. Warm week. The seracs above the viaduct are shedding already." She tapped a penciled line. "Our window isn't weeks. It's days. Maybe six. Maybe four, if the wind turns warm."

Nobody spoke. The glacier groaned again, long and low, and a chunk of ice the size of a freight car sheared off the face above and fell two hundred feet into the valley with a crack that Okonkwo felt in his teeth.

"Four days," Jude said at last. "We've got a mile of debris-choked grade and a viaduct missing half its deck."

"Then we'd best stop standing around admiring it," Tamsin said, and went for her tools.

* * *

They worked the way a crew works when it has stopped being a job and become a siege.

Okonkwo took the locomotive — the old salvage engine Voss had hauled up in pieces, a stubborn, squat machine they'd spent three days reassembling at the pass — and he talked to it while he worked, because machines deserved honesty. "You and me both, old girl," he told her, tightening a gland that had wept steam since the second day. "Neither of us was built for this cold, and both of us are going to do it anyway."

The grade above the tunnel was buried under avalanche debris — granite, ice, and forty years of pine, all of it fused into a ramp the company had simply walked away from. Tamsin ran the donkey engine and the winch line; Bramble hauled stone in a barrow and recited, unasked, the casualty figures from the Andean lines of the 1870s; Jude and Voss worked the face itself, drilling and setting charges with a coordination that Okonkwo watched with interest, because they moved like people who had once been close and were pretending otherwise, and the pretense was wearing thin as the ice.

By the second day they had opened three hundred yards of grade. By the third, the viaduct came into view — and Okonkwo's stomach dropped, the way it always did at the sight of other men's shortcuts.

The span crossed a gorge where the meltwater ran white and furious. Its stone piers were sound — the company built piers to last, because piers were expensive to rebuild and reputations were not. But the timber deck was another matter. Half of it had rotted through. Half of the rest had been replaced, sometime in the line's last years, with lumber that Okonkwo would not have trusted to floor a chicken coop.

He spent the whole of the third evening on his belly on the deck with a mallet and an iron bar, sounding every timber the way a doctor sounds a chest. *Tap, listen. Tap, listen.* Solid wood answered with a knock. Rotten wood answered with a whisper.

He marked the good timbers with white chalk. The bad ones he marked with a small, deliberate cross.

He did not tell anyone what the crosses meant. Not yet.

* * *

Mr. Corliss arrived on the fourth morning, riding up the new-cleared grade in a wagon drawn by two mules, and Okonkwo knew him before the wagon stopped, because some men carry their intentions the way a knife carries its edge — invisibly, until it's against you.

He was a big man gone soft at the middle, with a lawyer's hands and a hangman's patience. He wore a company coat with the buttons polished, and he smiled at all of them in turn, and the smile never once reached anything above his cheekbones.

"Gentlemen. Miss Tamsin." He tipped his hat. "Mr. Corliss, on behalf of the backers. I'm here to assist with the recovery operation."

"Assist," Tamsin said.

"The vault is a valuable asset. The backers want it handled with appropriate care." He looked at the viaduct, at the tunnel, at the glacier, with the flat appraisal of a man pricing a coffin. "You've made remarkable progress. It would be a shame if anything... delayed the recovery."

"We're on schedule," Voss said, in a voice Okonkwo had never heard her use — flat, careful, a woman reading from a script she hated.

"Of course you are." Corliss smiled again. "I'll be observing. Do carry on."

He made camp apart from theirs, with two men Okonkwo took at a glance for muscle — big, quiet, hands never far from their coats. And that night, Okonkwo sat up by the stove and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding.

Corliss had come for the gold. But Corliss had also come because witnesses were a form of loose ends, and loose ends were the one thing a company man could not abide. The crew had seen the wreck in the ice. The crew had seen the manifest. The crew — or at least Jude — had seen what was in Elias Wren's journal. When the vault came out of that valley, everyone who knew how it got there would be a problem to be solved.

Okonkwo had survived a great deal by knowing which timbers would hold. He intended to survive this too.

* * *

He called no meeting. Meetings were how Corliss would notice. Instead he went from one to another, the way a man goes round a campfire topping up cups.

He found Tamsin first, greasing the donkey engine's drum.

"The viaduct," he said quietly. "You'll be running the winch line across it tomorrow. Listen when you cross. I've chalked the deck. White means sound. A cross means it'll go through you like wet paper. Don't step on the crosses."

"How bad?"

"Bad enough. The company re-decked it with scrap in its last year. They knew the line was dying, so they spent nothing. Half that span is a lie wearing paint."

Tamsin looked at him, and he watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes, the same arithmetic he'd done. "You think he'll need that bridge."

"I think a man like Corliss plans for the bridge to be needed. And I think we should plan for it too." He kept his voice low. "If it comes to it — and I'm not saying it will, and I'm not saying when — there are four timbers on the east quarter, third bay, that hold nothing but their own weight. If a heavy load ever found itself on that bay, the bay would find its honest opinion."

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Then: "You're telling me how to drop a bridge."

"I'm telling you which timbers will hold and which will not. What the bridge does with that information is between the bridge and God."

She almost smiled. "Okonkwo. You beautiful, devious old man."

"I am neither old nor beautiful," he said. "I am thorough."

He found Bramble second, at the cook fire, and told him the same in a different language — the language of loads and spans and the honest opinions of timber — and Bramble, who remembered everything, remembered it exactly, and asked one question: "And if Corliss puts his own weight on it?"

"Then the bridge will have no complaint from me."

Last, he went to Jude, who was sitting apart with the journal open on his knees and the compass in his hand, looking like a man reading his own verdict.

Okonkwo sat down beside him without a word and let the fire crackle.

"The deck's half rotten," Okonkwo said finally. "I've marked it. Tamsin and Bramble know. If we need the span to hold, it'll hold where I've chalked white. If we need it to fail—"

Jude looked up. "You think it's coming to that."

"I think Mr. Corliss did not ride two days through avalanche country to watch us work. I think a man who brings muscle to an engineering job is not there for the engineering. And I think," Okonkwo said, "that whatever is in that journal, the company has spent forty years and at least one man's good name making sure it stays in the ice. We are standing on top of the reason, Jude. The ice is not the only thing up here that can bury a man."

Jude closed the journal. For a long moment he said nothing, and the glacier groaned overhead, and somewhere in the dark a serac calved and the boom of it rolled down the valley like a struck bell.

"You sound them again in the morning," Jude said at last. "Every timber. And chalk it true. If we're going to trust our lives to that span, I want to know its honest opinion down to the last board."

"It's already true."

"Then true twice," Jude said. "For Elias."

Okonkwo nodded, and rose, and went to his bedroll. But he did not sleep at once. He lay listening to the glacier — the groan, the crack, the long white thunder of ice letting go — and he thought: *four days, maybe fewer, and a man with a hangman's patience is camped fifty yards away, and the only weapons we have are chalk and honesty.*

It was not, he reflected, the worst arsenal he'd ever carried into a fight.

He was nearly asleep when he heard it — footsteps, soft, circling the edge of camp. Not their footsteps. Corliss's men, walking the perimeter in the dark, counting tents, counting heads, counting everything except the chalk marks on a bridge that was already, quietly, learning how to lie.

Okonkwo closed his eyes and began, silently, to plan.
