## 7. The Burial Order

The lamp burned low and yellow in the foreman's car, and outside the wind worked at the canvas like a man trying to pick a lock.

Jude had waited three days to open the journal. He told himself it was because there was no time — that the line came first, that Tamsin's discovery in the frozen cab was salvage like anything else, to be catalogued when the work was done. But that was a lie, and he had been living on lies long enough to know their taste. The truth was simpler and worse: he was afraid of Elias Wren's handwriting. He hadn't seen it in nine years, not since the letter that arrived after the funeral, four lines long, unsigned by anyone but grief itself. He was afraid that opening the book would be like opening the tunnel at Kestrel Pass — that something inside had been sealed away for a reason, and that the reason would have his name on it too.

He opened it anyway. That was the whole of what courage amounted to, most nights. You opened it anyway.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary, and that ordinariness undid him first. Survey notes. Elevations. A sketch of a marmot done badly, with a caption — *ugly little landlord of the high country* — that made Jude laugh out loud alone in a railcar at midnight, and then stop laughing as if he'd been caught at something. This was Elias. Not the monument the company had built and then torn down, not the scapegoat in the inquiry transcripts, but a man who drew marmots badly and complained about the coffee and measured the world because measuring it was how he loved it.

Jude turned pages by lamplight while the glacier ticked and settled outside like a house cooling.

Then, forty pages in, the handwriting changed. It tightened. The letters got smaller, pressed harder into the paper, as if Elias had begun writing for someone who might read this over his shoulder — or for no one, which is its own kind of fear.

*Third survey of the lower Kestrel bore,* the entry began. *Company says instability. I say the numbers don't lie even when men do.*

Jude read it twice. Then he did what he always did with hard things: he made tea he didn't drink, and he kept reading.

Elias had found it in the spring of the last year of his life. The lower bore — the one below the pass, the one that wasn't on any map Voss had shown them — had failed. Not slowly, not with warning. The ceiling came down on a night shift, forty navvies under it, most of them men who'd crossed an ocean for the wage and couldn't have read the company's apology if the company had bothered to write one. And the company had not stopped the line. Had not reported it. Had instead run a second crew through to shore up what remained, and put the dead on the books as *laborers discharged*, and kept driving steel toward the pass with the bodies still in the rock behind them.

Because the tunnel was scheduled to carry something. Because there were investors, and timetables, and a payroll vault moving up the line that autumn that could not be delayed for forty men whose names didn't fit in a ledger column.

*They knew the ground was rotten,* Elias wrote. *I told Hargreave in March. I put it in writing. He thanked me and filed it. In June the ceiling came down and in July they were running trains through the same cut with timber shoring thinner than my patience. I asked him how he slept. He said, "On a good mattress, Wren, which you should look into."*

Jude set the journal down. His hands weren't steady. Outside, the wind had picked up something metallic and was dragging it along the rails, a small shrieking note, over and over, like the mountain tuning an instrument.

* * *

The middle entries were worse, because they were careful. Elias had been an engineer to the bone; even in horror he documented. He listed dates. He copied figures from the shoring reports into his own hand so they couldn't be altered. He noted which officials had signed the discharge papers for men who were dead, and where those signatures now lived — in a strongbox, in a company office, in a vault.

And then, near the end, the entry that changed the shape of everything:

*The company means to close the evidence, not the tunnel. Corliss's people have been asking about load weights on the pass grade. There is a work train being fitted out with ballast far beyond any clearing job's need. I believe they intend to wreck it deliberately — run it into the lower bore, collapse what remains, seal the whole valley under ice and rubble, and call it geology. Forty men buried once by accident and again on purpose, and a paper trail with them.*

*I can stop it or I can prove it. I do not think I can do both.*

Jude read that sentence until the lamp guttered and he had to trim the wick. *I can stop it or I can prove it.* He knew which one Elias had chosen, because he'd lived inside the consequences of the choice his whole adult life without ever seeing its outline. Elias had gone to the pass with witnesses. He had tried to stop the train. And the train had gone through anyway — with Elias's name signed to the movement order, a signature the inquiry called authentic and that Elias swore to his grave was forged, and everyone had believed the company, because the company had mattresses and lawyers and Elias had only a compass and a reputation.

They blamed him. They ruined him. And when he died — and Jude had never once, in nine years, let himself ask the question directly, but here in the lamplight it asked itself — when he died, it was convenient.

*If you are reading this,* the final entry said, *then I am past explaining myself, and you are past needing me to. Take the journal to the inquiry board in Meridian. Take it to a newspaper if the board is bought — test the board first; Hargreave dines with two of them. Do not take it to the company. Above all do not let them learn who has it, because they will come for it the way water comes for a foundation: patiently, everywhere, until you are hollow.*

*I am leaving it to my boy Underhill, who argues too much and drinks too little and will understand every figure in this book better than I do.*

*Tell him the debt was never his. Tell him the debt was theirs.*

Jude closed the journal.

He sat in the yellow circle of the lamp with his hands flat on the cover, and for a long time nothing in him moved at all, the way the air doesn't move before lightning.

* * *

The debt collectors. Nine years of them. He had built his whole understanding of his ruin on the company's word for it: that Jude Underhill had cosigned notes against Elias's estate, that he owed, that the debt was real and legal and his. He had hidden in junction towns repairing sidings nobody used because a man in debt keeps his head down. He had taught his crew to move quietly, to take contracts off the books, to live small — and he had thought he was protecting them. He had thought he was paying for something.

There was no debt. There had never been a debt. There was a hunting license, issued by men who had murdered forty navvies and their own work train and one stubborn surveyor's good name, and the thing they were hunting was not money. It was this book. It had always been this book. Every collector, every letter, every quiet man in a good coat asking after him in every yard from the coast to the divide — they weren't collecting. They were searching. And Jude had spent nine years doing their work for them, keeping himself small and findable and ashamed, mistaking captivity for penance.

Invisibility wasn't safety. It had never been safety. It was just a smaller cell.

He got up and stood at the window of the foreman's car. Below, the camp fires burned low. Okonkwo's silhouette moved between the cars on his rounds, patient as tide. Tamsin's bunk glowed faintly where she'd fallen asleep reading Bramble's paperback westerns again. Somewhere out there Marguerite Voss sat awake in her own car, holding her brother's death like a coin she hadn't yet decided which pocket to spend it from.

And somewhere beyond the pass, Mr. Corliss was coming up the line.

That was the piece that snapped everything else into place. Corliss. The name was in the journal — *Corliss's people have been asking about load weights* — written nine years ago by a dead man, and now the man himself was riding toward them with company authority and a face like a closed ledger. He hadn't come for the gold, or not only for the gold. He'd come because the ice was opening, because the wreck was exposed, because a fireman with quick hands had pulled a manifest stamped with the company seal out of a frozen cab. The burial order was failing. The mountain was giving up what it had been paid to keep. And Corliss was the second shovel.

Jude understood, standing there, that they had perhaps days. That the choice Elias had written about — *stop it or prove it* — had been handed down to him intact, like a tool passed from a dead man's hand, and that he had spent nine years believing he had no inheritance at all.

He went back to the table. He took a fresh sheet of the survey paper, and beneath the lamp, in his own cramped hand, he began to copy. Dates. Figures. Signatures. The load weights of a train fitted to drown. He worked the way Elias had worked — carefully, exactly, for someone reading over his shoulder — and when the first gray light came through the canvas seams he had twelve pages and a plan forming underneath them like rail under ballast.

They would finish the line. They would let Corliss come. And when the moment came, Jude Underhill was going to stop hiding in the high passes and stand in the open with his mentor's book held up where every lamp in the territory could see it.

Outside, the glacier groaned — long, deep, almost conversational, like something old turning over in its sleep and beginning, at last, to wake.

And down the valley, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Jude had been dreading since the pass: a locomotive whistle, climbing toward them through the dark.
