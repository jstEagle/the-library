## 8. Corliss Takes the Bridge

I have cooked for men in floods, in strikes, in the year the fever took half the navvy camps on the coast, and I will tell you what I know: you can read a crew by how they eat. A frightened man eats fast and doesn't taste. A man planning something eats slow, watching the door.

That morning at the viaduct camp, every one of them ate slow.

Corliss had come up the line two days before with six men and a crate of new rifles, smiling like a bank manager at a funeral. "To assist," he'd said. He said it again now, standing on the near abutment of the Kestrel Viaduct with the morning fog burning off the glacier behind him, and this time he had the vault crates stacked on the completed half of the span like a man arranging furniture.

"Mr. Corliss," Voss was saying, her surveyor's voice pitched flat and professional, "the load calculations for the second span aren't finished. If you move those crates onto—"

"I've read your calculations." Corliss didn't look at her. He was looking at Jude, who stood at the foot of the ramp with his arms folded and his face doing that thing it does when he's decided to be made of stone. "Underhill. Your engineer signed off on the first three spans."

"The first three spans are sound," Jude said.

"And the fourth?"

"The fourth is timber we pulled out of avalanche debris and sistered with green wood because somebody's schedule wouldn't wait for seasoned stock." Jude's voice didn't rise. It never rises. That's the frightening thing about him — the quieter he gets, the closer you are to the edge of something. "It'll hold a handcar. It might hold a locomotive at walking pace, light. It will not hold forty tons of company gold, and any man who tells you different is selling you a bridge that isn't there."

Corliss smiled again. Then two of his men stepped down off the abutment and stood where they could cover the whole crew yard with their rifles, casual as men leaning on fences, and the smile stopped mattering.

"Well," Corliss said. "Then we'll want to be careful."

* * *

Here is what I did while the rifles came out: I kept ladling porridge. That is not cowardice. That is a cook's doctrine — you feed people through the bad hours, because a fed crew is a crew that can think, and thinking is the only weapon we were going to be allowed to keep.

Tamsin came through the mess line with her tin held out, eyes forward, jaw set like a rivet. She didn't look at me. She said, quiet as a valve bleeding pressure: "Okonkwo's charts. The fourth span. Bay two and bay five."

I said, "More porridge?"

"Bay two and bay five," she said, and moved on.

So that was the shape of it. Okonkwo had spent three days walking that viaduct with his little hammer, tapping every timber like a doctor sounding a chest, filling a notebook with marks no one else could read — which timbers were heartwood and which were rot wearing paint, which joints would sing before they failed and which would go without a whisper. He'd told us all once, around my fire, in his careful way: *A bridge does not fall down. It falls through. You must know where the floor of it is thin.*

Bay two and bay five. The floor was thin there.

And Corliss wanted to roll the vault car across it.

* * *

The trouble with being the cook is that nobody watches you. I have used this my whole working life. Men will say things over a stew pot they would never say in an office, and they will let a fat old historian of the rails wander past with a basket and never once wonder what's in it besides bread.

By noon I had been everywhere I needed to be. I'd stood beside the vault car — a black iron box on a flatcar, stamped with the company seal, heavy enough that its wheels sat deep in the ballast — and counted the tie-downs. I'd walked the ramp to the fourth span and noted, without stopping, that Tamsin had already loosened the retaining pins on the span's brake blocks, so the car could be rolled but not easily stopped. And I'd found Okonkwo in the shadow of the water tower, and put into his hand a scrap of paper on which I'd written, in my best ledger hand:

*Bramble asks: if the car stops on bay two or bay five, what happens?*

He read it twice, burned it in the fire barrel, and wrote back nothing at all. He just looked at me and tapped his hammer against his palm, twice, then five times, then laid it flat across both hands like an offering.

Two and five. Both. Not one or the other.

Both.

* * *

They brought Voss to me at dusk, or rather she brought herself, sweeping into my kitchen tent demanding coffee with the imperiousness she deploys like armor. Her hands, when she took the cup, were not steady. I have watched this woman stand on an ice shelf that calved under her and not spill a drop, so I knew what the shaking meant.

"He wants the crossing at first light," she said. "Jude refuses. Corliss has given him until dawn to sign the load certificate, or he starts shooting crew members, starting with—" She stopped. She drank the coffee like it was medicine.

"Starting with Tamsin," I said. "He's been looking at her all day. Men like him always pick the youngest."

"Yes." She set the cup down. "Bramble. I need you to get a message to Jude, and I need you to do it in a way that looks like nothing, because Corliss has a man watching my tent and another watching Underhill's, and I am about to become a very busy double agent." She said it without flinching at her own words. "I'm going to give Corliss calculations. Real ones, from my own books, with one number changed. The dead weight of the vault car. I'll show it lighter than it is. He'll believe it because he wants to believe it, and because I'm the best surveyor he's ever hired and he knows it."

"And when the bridge drops?"

"When the bridge drops, I'll have documented, in my own hand, that the load was within tolerance." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "Let them hang the collapse on me if they survive to try. But the crew crosses first. That's the condition. Jude takes the locomotive and every living soul we have over that span ahead of the gold, and I tell Corliss the engine needs a test run light. He'll allow it. He wants the line proven."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she rarely let herself. "You're the only one he ignores. Will you carry it?"

I tied my apron back on. "Mr. Corliss," I called toward the tent flap, cheerful as Sunday, "more coffee out here when you want it!"

Because that's the other thing about cooks. We're invisible right up until we're not.

* * *

I carried the message inside a loaf.

This is true, and I am proud of it. I baked that evening as I always do, four loaves, and into the second one, wrapped in oilcloth and set in the dough's heart, went Voss's note and a page torn from Okonkwo's notebook showing the fourth span's bays marked in his private shorthand — two and five circled, with the single word *both*. I set the loaf on Jude's plate myself, at the end of the table, nearest the lantern, and said, "End piece, Mr. Underhill. You look like a man who needs the crust."

Jude looked at the loaf. Jude looked at me. Somewhere behind those grey stone eyes a door opened, and I saw him understand — bread, from Bramble, unasked — and he broke it open with his hands right there at the table, in front of God and Corliss's watching man, like any hungry fool, and read the oilcloth in the ruin of the crumb.

His face never changed. He ate the rest of the loaf. All of it, deliberately, meeting my eye.

Then he stood, brushed the crumbs from his coat, and walked over to where Corliss sat by the fire, and said, loud enough for the whole camp: "First light. Test run light, engine only, then your gold. My surveyor will certify the load."

Voss, across the fire, did not smile. She was already writing numbers.

* * *

We worked through the dark hours, and this is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if the ice spares me any grandchildren: the family fought as one, and the weapons were a hammer, a notebook, a loaf of bread, and a lie.

Tamsin went up the span with a lantern hooded to a slit, and I went with her, because someone had to hold the light and because I refused to spend this night peeling potatoes while my children walked a tightrope. She pulled the retaining pins half-out on the brake blocks at bay two and dressed them back with tallow and dust so they looked seated. At bay five she did more: she cut the lashing points on the deck planking loose and re-pegged them shallow, so that any weight shifting on that bay would find nothing to grip. Okonkwo followed us both, silent as snowfall, checking our work with his fingertips, and twice he undid what Tamsin had done and did it better, and once he put his great hand on the back of her neck, gently, the way you steady a bird, and she stood still and let him.

Below us, the glacier spoke. It had been speaking for days — long groans out of the valley's throat, cracks like rifle shots rolling down the ice — but tonight it had gone conversational, muttering, restless. Above the viaduct the serac field hung in the moonlight like a row of rotten teeth, and I caught Tamsin looking up at it, and I caught her looking away, and I understood that she had done the arithmetic we had all done: the calving season was opening early, and the window was closing, and Corliss's rifles were suddenly the least of the things that could kill us by morning.

"Bramble," she whispered, on the way back down. "If it goes while we're on it—"

"Then we go together," I said, "and loudly, complaining about the food."

She laughed. In the dark, on a rigged bridge, above a waking glacier, with armed men below — she laughed, and it was the bravest sound I heard all night.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and cold, and Corliss stood on the abutment with his coat buttoned and his men spaced along the rail like fence posts, and the vault car waited on the ramp, black and patient as a coffin.

Voss handed him the calculation sheet with a steady hand. I watched him read it. I watched him believe it, because men always believe what greed has already decided. He initialled it. He waved the locomotive forward.

Jude climbed into the cab. Tamsin swung up beside him, grinning like sin. And the engine took the fourth span at walking pace, light as a promise, and the old timbers sang under her, and held.

Corliss turned to his men. "Load the car."

Above us, the glacier cleared its throat.

And I saw Jude, small in the cab on the far side of the span, look back once — not at the gold, not at Corliss, but at us, his ragged family lined along the railhead — and raise one hand.

Not a wave. A signal.

Wait for it.
