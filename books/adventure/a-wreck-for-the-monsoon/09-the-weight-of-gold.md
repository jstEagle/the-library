## 9. The Weight of Gold

The viaduct came into view at dawn, and Jude's stomach dropped the way it always did at bad bridges — except this one he had helped make bad, timber by timber, and that made it worse.

It spanned the crevasse where the glacier split itself open like a wound, eighty feet of latticed iron and borrowed wood, half the original span and half Okonkwo's charts. The morning light came up blue off the ice. Somewhere below, deep in the blue, was everything Corliss wanted.

"Load calculations," Voss said beside him, holding out the paper like it might bite. "Corliss wants them verified before crossing."

Jude took the sheet. Bramble's figures were beautiful — every axle weight, every timber rating, all of it wrong in exactly the ways Okonkwo had designed. The vault car showed at forty tons. It would actually weigh closer to sixty with the crates Corliss's men had dragged up from the tunnel mouth overnight.

"He'll check the math himself," Jude said.

"He'll check it against his own greed," Voss said. "Greed doesn't do long division."

Below them, Corliss stood on the near abutment with six armed men and a voice like gravel sliding. "Get the car moving! Window's closing!" He wasn't wrong. All night the glacier had been talking to itself — long groans from the depths, cracks like rifle shots echoing down the valley. The calving season had come early. Tamsin had timed the ice falls from the ridge: three major calvings since midnight, each bigger than the last.

Jude looked at his crew. Tamsin by the locomotive, oil to her elbows, feeding the boiler with hands that didn't shake. Okonkwo at the brake wheel, touching the iron the way he touched everything, like asking permission. Bramble on the tender, sharpening nothing, watching Corliss's men with the stillness of a man who'd decided what he was willing to lose.

*We vote,* they always said. *We vote.*

They had voted last night, four to one, and Jude had been the one. He'd lost. For once in his life, losing felt like standing on solid ground.

"Runaway slope," he said to Voss, quietly. "If the span goes mid-crossing, the far end is our only way out. I take the engine across first, light. Then the vault car follows on its own momentum — no engine attached, just gravity and Corliss's confidence."

"And if Corliss puts men on the vault car?"

"Then the math changes." Jude met her eyes. "I need you to make sure it doesn't."

Voss smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Leave Mr. Corliss's confidence to me."

* * *

The morning went according to everyone's plans, which is how Jude knew it would go wrong.

He ran the *Kestrel Maid* across the span alone, slow as walking, feeling through the cab floor for the timbers' answer. Okonkwo's rigged section sang a different note under the wheels — higher, hollower — but it held. On the far side, Jude set the brake and watched Corliss's men couple the vault car to a gravity chute they'd built overnight, a ramp of rail sloping down from the tunnel mouth to the bridge approach.

Corliss climbed onto the vault car himself. Of course he did. A man who trusted no one's arithmetic rode his own gold across the abyss.

"Loose the brake!" he shouted, and his men knocked the chocks away, and sixty tons of buried company payroll began its glide toward the crevasse.

Jude felt it before he heard it — a tremor through the rails, up through the soles of his boots, into his teeth. Then the glacier answered. Not a groan this time. A roar, deep and structural, the sound of a mountain deciding something. Up-valley, the ice face shrugged loose a wall the size of a chapel, and the whole valley filled with white thunder.

"Calving!" Tamsin screamed from the cab. "Jude, the shockwave—"

The vault car hit the rigged section at speed.

Okonkwo's timbers did exactly what they were drawn to do. They held for the first axle, held for the second, held long enough for the car's full weight to commit itself to the middle of the span — and then let go, all together, like a held breath released.

The bridge folded. The vault car went down nose-first, Corliss still on it, his scream thin and human under the avalanche roar. Crates burst their straps and spilled gold into the blue — bars catching the light as they fell, bright as fish, sinking into the crevasse the way treasure sinks in dreams.

And Elias's compass — strapped to the vault car's railing since Kestrel Pass, because Jude had wanted the old man's needle pointed home when they crossed — went down with it.

Jude didn't decide. That was the terrible truth he'd carry afterward: there was no decision, only his body already over the broken rail, already reaching down into the falling dark, fingers finding cold brass—

A hand closed on his collar and hauled him back with a strength that had nothing gentle in it. Voss. She wrapped both arms around him and threw them both flat on the bedrock as the second shockwave hit, ice dust blinding, the world roaring.

"You fool," she shouted into his ear. "You absolute fool."

"It was his," Jude shouted back. "It was all I had of him."

"You have the journal! You have *him*—" Her grip tightened. "Jude. Look at me. You have us."

Below them, the crevasse swallowed the last of the light. Somewhere in the dark, Corliss's shouting had stopped. The gold was gone. The proof of what it cost was safe in a satchel against Jude's ribs, and the compass was ice now, ice forever, which maybe was where a dead man's things belonged.

He got up. His hands were shaking. He let them.

"Tamsin!" he called. "Full head of steam?"

"Been waiting all morning!" she called back, grinning through the grime.

"Then we're going home the fast way."

* * *

They ran the *Kestrel Maid* out ahead of the mountain.

Jude drove her harder than he'd ever driven anything, regulator wide, the old girl leaning into the grades with fire in her throat while behind them the glacier came down in stages — calving walls, rockfall, the viaduct's remains vanishing under a white tide that erased the pass, the tunnel mouth, the wrecked work train, forty years of buried crime, all of it sealed under fresh ice deeper than any company could dig.

Tamsin rode the fireman's plate calling the curves. Okonkwo rode the brakes singing something low in his mother's tongue, blessing the iron. Bramble stood in the gangway with his coat flying, laughing like a man at his own funeral who'd just heard good news.

At the last curve before the valley opened, Jude looked back once. The line was gone. Not damaged — *gone*, swallowed whole, as if the company had never laid steel there, as if the forty navvies had never died for it, except that two people alive on this train carried the truth of them, and truth was the one cargo ice couldn't hold.

Voss climbed into the cab, soot-faced, breathless, and put her hand over his on the regulator.

"The backers will come after us," she said. "Corliss was theirs. They'll want to know why their gold is at the bottom of a glacier and their enforcer with it."

"Let them come," Jude said. "I'm done hiding in sidings. I've got a journal, a crew, and a witness with better handwriting than mine."

"That's not what I meant." Her hand turned, caught his, held it. The cab roared around them, fire and thunder and speed, and she had to shout it: "When you went over that rail — I have spent ten years competing with you, Jude Underhill, and I have never once been afraid until that moment. Don't you ever make me feel that again unless you mean to stay."

The whistle shrieked. The valley opened. Sunlight broke over the shoulder of the mountain and flooded the tracks ahead, all the way down to the lowlands, to junctions and juries and daylight.

"I mean to stay," Jude said.

She kissed him with coal smoke in the air and the mountain coming down behind them, and neither of them closed their eyes.

Ahead, past the last tunnel, a telegraph pole leaned crooked over the right-of-way, and a figure stood beneath it — a railway constable, hat in hand, flagging them down with papers flapping.

"Company wire," Bramble called from the gangway, reading the man's face before anyone asked. "They know we're coming."

Jude opened the regulator another notch.

"Good," he said. "We've got a story to tell them."
