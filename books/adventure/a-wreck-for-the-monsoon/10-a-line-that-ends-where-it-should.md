## 10. A Line That Ends Where It Should

The courtroom smelled of lamp oil and wet wool, and Jude Underhill sat in the witness box with Elias Wren's journal open on his knees like a man holding a live wire.

Three weeks had passed since the Kestrel Line closed behind them with a roar that still woke him some nights — not as a nightmare, exactly, but as a sound like a door shutting on a room he'd finally stopped needing to enter. Three weeks of depositions, of lawyers with company creases in their coats, of Marguerite Voss sitting two rows back with her hands folded and her chin up, giving testimony that burned her own bridges one timber at a time.

The prosecutor asked him to state his name for the record, and he did, and then she asked him what he'd found in the ice.

"Forty men," Jude said. "That's what I found. The rest was paper."

He opened the journal to the page with the water stain shaped like a hand, Elias's cramped engineer's script marching across it — *burial order countersigned, R. Halloway, third inst. Cargo to be interred at marker nine. No survivors of the tunnel crew to be retained on payroll.* He read it aloud. He'd read it so many times by now that the words came out level, almost gentle, the way you'd read a eulogy. Around him the courtroom went very still. Somewhere in the gallery a woman began to cry, and no one told her to stop.

The company's counsel stood up twice to object, and both times the judge overruled him with the weariness of a man who'd already read the manifest, the payroll ledgers, the survey notes Voss had surrendered from her backers' own files. The vault of gold under the glacier had never been treasure. It had been a grave marker, bought and paid for, dropped into a crevasse of silence along with the men who'd dug the tunnel that killed them.

When they released him from the stand, Jude's hands were shaking, and it wasn't fear. It was the specific tremor of a weight set down after years of carrying it wrong.

The debt collectors found him on the courthouse steps. He'd braced for it — old reflex, the flinch that had lived in his spine since the first letter came. But the man who approached him in the grey afternoon light wasn't carrying a ledger. He was carrying a release.

"Mr. Underhill." He said it without relish, without threat. "The instruments against you have been voided. Consideration was rendered in full." A thin smile. "In truth, apparently."

Jude took the paper and read it twice. *Paid in full* — not in coin, not in silence, but in forty names read aloud into a court record, in a journal entered as evidence, in the truth doing what truth does when it finally gets daylight: burning.

He watched the man walk away and felt nothing he expected. No triumph. Just space, where the debt used to be, like a cleared right-of-way.

Voss was waiting by the railing, hat in her hand, hair coming loose the way it always did when she'd been fighting something all day.

"They offered me immunity," she said. "Full immunity, in exchange for the backers. Halloway's correspondence, the payments, all of it." She looked out at the street, at the ordinary traffic of an ordinary town. "I took it. I want you to know I took it without hesitation, and I don't know what that says about me."

"It says you learned the same lesson I did," Jude said. "Loyalty to the wrong thing isn't loyalty. It's just a longer way of drowning."

She laughed — short, surprised, real. Then she looked at him, and for a moment neither of them said anything, and the noise of the street filled up the space where a rivalry had lived for eleven years.

"Marguerite," he said. Her first name felt strange in his mouth, like a gauge he'd never run before. "Thank you. For pulling me back."

"You were reaching for a compass," she said. "A lump of brass. I nearly let you have it."

"I know."

"I'm glad I didn't."

* * *

The coastal branch contract was waiting for them when the trial ended, and it was, by every measure that mattered, an honest one. Forty miles of light rail along the cliffs above the fishing towns, carrying herring and passengers and mail, no vaults, no sealed orders, no burial markers. The pay was modest. The timetable was sane. The company that owned it was a family concern run by a widow who interviewed every crew member personally and asked Bramble, reportedly, more questions about local history than about cooking.

They voted on it in the kitchen car, the way they voted on everything.

Tamsin went first, boots on the table, fire-scarred hands behind her head. "Coastal means salt air. Salt air means I get to teach every fireman on that line how to keep a boiler honest in corrosion season. I'm in."

Okonkwo turned his palm flat on the table, the way he did when the spirits had given a ruling. "The bridges there are old but they were built by people who expected to be held accountable. I checked the drawings. I'm in."

Bramble, who had already been corresponding with the widow about the region's whaling-era rail history, said, "I'm in, and I've already started writing a pamphlet."

Then they all looked at Voss.

She stood by the door with her survey case, the one she'd carried through the pass and over the glacier and into a courtroom, and she looked — for the first time since Jude had known her — uncertain.

"I'm not crew," she said. "I'm the one who hired you into a trap. I'm the one whose backers—" She stopped. Started again. "You should say no."

"Motion's on the floor," Tamsin said. "All in favor of Voss signing on as chief surveyor, say aye."

Four ayes. Jude's was the loudest.

Voss sat down slowly, like a woman whose legs had been holding her up on stubbornness alone for months. "I've never been anybody's family before," she said. "I've been colleagues. Rivals. Leverage. I don't—"

"You'll learn," Bramble said, setting a bowl of stew in front of her. "It's mostly just showing up. You're already good at that."

* * *

He went back to the pass alone, in the last week of autumn, before the snows closed it.

He told the crew he needed to check the marker at the tunnel mouth, and that was even true. But the real reason rode in his breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth: Elias Wren's compass, fished from the wreckage in the last desperate minutes before the calving, dented, its needle stuck pointing at a heading no map had ever used.

He climbed to the mouth of the tunnel, or what remained of it. The collapse had sealed it deeper than the company's dynamite ever had. The glacier had taken the viaduct, the vault, Corliss, and the gold, and it had taken them the way glaciers take things — completely, without malice, without memory. Somewhere under all that ice were forty navvies and a payroll that had been meant to buy their silence, and for the first time since Jude had learned the truth, that felt right. Not justice. Nothing would be justice for them. But rest. The kind of rest that doesn't need a headstone, because the whole mountain is the headstone.

He knelt at the marker and dug into the scree with his hands until he'd made a hollow the size of the compass.

"You'd have hated all of it," he said to Elias, to the ice, to the air. "The trial. The lawyers. My face in the papers." He turned the compass over. The needle twitched once, as if waking, and settled. "But you'd have liked the crew. Tamsin would have driven you to drink. Okonkwo would have fixed that bad wrist of yours. Bramble would have written you into a pamphlet." He almost smiled. "And Voss — you'd have liked her best of all. She's the engineer I tried to be and couldn't. She's the one who pulled me back."

He set the compass in the hollow and covered it, stone by stone, until the brass was gone and only a small cairn stood where a debt used to be. The needle pointed into the mountain, at the line that ended where it should, holding its heading forever.

The wind came down the valley, cold and clean, carrying the smell of coming snow. Behind him, far below, he could hear it — the thin, bright whistle of the work train the crew had run up as far as the pass road allowed, waiting to carry him home. Tamsin at the throttle, no doubt, running her hot. Voss in the cab, arguing about something. His family, idling in the cold, patient as rails.

Jude stood a moment longer. Then he shouldered his pack, turned his back on the pass, and started down toward the sound of the whistle — toward the coast, the herring boats, the forty honest miles of track, and the long, unremarkable, hard-won work of a life that finally pointed the same direction as the man living it.

The whistle blew again, longer this time, and Jude Underhill broke into a run.
