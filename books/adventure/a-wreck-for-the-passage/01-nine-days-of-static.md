## 1. Nine Days of Static

The contract said *logistics consultant*. The money said *hostage*.

Kestrel Reyes read it twice on the flight from the coast, the charter's propellers drowning out everything but her own skepticism, and signed anyway. Nine days of permits left. Nine days before the concession company's legal claim to the upper river lapsed and everything her father had found — or failed to find — reverted to mud, jungle, and whatever government ministry held the next auction. The man across the aisle, the one who'd hired her, watched her sign with the patient expression of someone who had already counted the money.

"You're the pilot's daughter," he'd said at the airstrip, as if that were a qualification. As if it explained why they needed her instead of any of the hundred competent river men in the capital.

"His daughter," she'd corrected. "He hasn't flown anything but a desk in fifteen years."

"Still. Blood knows where blood goes."

His name was Ovidio. He wore a linen shirt too clean for the river and a satellite phone he touched the way other men touched rosaries. He had a fixer's smile, all accommodation and no information, and he answered her questions the way a wall answers a thrown stone.

"How long since anyone heard from the camp?"

"Eleven days of silence. Routine equipment failure, most likely."

"Then why hire me?"

"Because your father is on that river, Ms. Reyes, and the company prefers to resolve its personnel matters with a family member present. It's better optics." He said *personnel matters* the way a coroner says *remains*.

* * *

The last fight had been in this same terminal, four years ago. She could still see the departure board flickering behind his head, the way he kept his eyes on it instead of her, as if the flight information were the more important daughter.

"You could come back," she'd said. "Teach. Write the book. You've got thirty years of material and nothing on paper."

"The river's not done with me, Kes."

"The river's never done with you. That's the whole problem."

He'd smiled at that — actually smiled, the old charm coming up like a reflex — and it had made her furious in a way she hadn't understood until much later. He wore her anger like weather. It passed over him and he remained, unchanged, already halfway upriver in his head.

"Nine days," she said now, to no one, and the charter bucked through a thermal as if the word had weight.

* * *

Ovidio spent the first hour of the flight making calls he pretended were private. The charter was loud, but the satellite phone had a headset, and Kestrel had grown up around men who talked over engines. She caught fragments. *Confirmed. No, she doesn't know the terms. The daughter is an advantage — she'll go where hired men won't.* And then, softer, in a register she wasn't meant to hear at all: *Understood. Above me. I know.*

Above him. So Ovidio wasn't the top of the food chain. Ovidio was a hand, and hands report to arms, and arms report to whatever face the company kept in the capital, behind glass, signing permits that expired in nine days.

She didn't let him see that she'd heard. She'd learned that much from her father: on a river, information is ballast. You don't dump it early.

* * *

The pilot was a heavy, sun-cured man named Bastos who had flown her father's surveys in the old days, back when her father still came home between seasons. He flew the charter the way an old man walks a known staircase — without looking, without hurrying — until they reached the second bend of the river, and then he did not fly it at all. He banked, leveled, and began to lose altitude over a wide oxbow scarred with old mining pits, and Kestrel understood with a cold drop in her stomach that this was as far as they were going.

"This isn't the camp," she said. "The coordinates are another forty minutes up."

Bastos didn't turn around. "Forty minutes is past the second bend."

"I know where the second bend is. I grew up on this river."

"Then you know what the water's doing this season." He jerked his chin at the window. Below, the river had gone the color of milk coffee, swollen and muscular, dragging whole islands of torn vegetation downstream. "Early flood. I don't land past the bend in flood season. Not since the year the water took the Cessna with two men in it."

"We have a contract."

"You have a charter to the bend. Read your paper." Bastos glanced back, and for one second his professional blankness slipped and she saw something underneath it — not fear exactly. A kind of pity. "Your father used to argue with me about that bend too. He'd say the river doesn't kill careful men. I used to believe him."

Ovidio leaned forward from the back, unruffled, as if he'd known this would happen, as if it were in the contract somewhere between the clauses. "There's a fuel cache and a boat at the oxbow," he said. "We go the rest of the way by water. It was always the plan, Ms. Reyes. The plane was just the fast part."

* * *

The boat was a long aluminum skiff with an outboard that coughed twice before it caught, and they ran the river through the gold end of the afternoon with the flood current helping them and the radio in Kestrel's lap doing nothing at all.

She called the camp frequency every twenty minutes. She called it on the emergency band, on the survey band, on the old family channel her father had kept licensed for years after he'd stopped needing one — the channel he'd used to call her mother, once, from mid-river, just to say the light was good. Static answered all of it, the same white hiss, patient as rain.

"Eleven days," Ovidio said, watching her work. "Radios die."

"Radios die. Batteries die. Men die." She keyed the mic again. "But three different bands? Somebody would have rigged a spare. My father rigged spares for breakfast."

"Your father," Ovidio said carefully, "is a man who does not always do what is expected."

No, she thought. He does exactly what's expected of him. Just never by me.

The banks went by — flooded gallery forest, a fisherman's stilt house abandoned with its pots still on the rail, a chapel with the river halfway up its door. The river was eating the year's ground already, and the season had weeks left to run. Whatever was at that camp, it was about to be underwater, along with any road back.

* * *

They made camp on a sandbar that would not exist by morning, and Ovidio built a fire and cooked rice with the competence of a man who had done fieldwork he never mentioned, and Kestrel walked to the edge of the bar and called the family channel into the dark one more time.

Static. But under the static — she couldn't swear to it, the way you can't swear to a face in moving water — something rhythmic. Three tones, repeated. Pause. Three tones.

A signal. Old-fashioned, deliberate, the kind of thing a man teaches his daughter on a kitchen table with a broken shortwave, *so you'll never be out of reach, Kes, not really.* Three tones meant *alive*. Three tones meant *stay away until I call the fourth*.

Her hands were shaking when she lowered the radio.

Behind her, Ovidio sat with his back to the fire, and the satellite phone glowed in his hand, and she watched him thumb a message into it — watched the little antenna light pulse as it went out into the dark, reporting upward to whoever lived above him — and she understood that she was not the only one who had heard something on this river tonight.

The tones came again. Three, and pause, and three.

And then, faint as a pulse under a wrist, a fourth.
