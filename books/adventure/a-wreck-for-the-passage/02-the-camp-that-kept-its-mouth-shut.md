## 2. The Camp That Kept Its Mouth Shut

The pilot would not go past the second bend.

He said it in Portuguese, then in English, then by simply pointing at the fuel gauge and shaking his head, as if the needle itself had opinions. Ovidio paid him anyway — a thick envelope, passed without counting — and the Cessna lifted off the brown water trailing spray, and then it was just the two of them and the river and a silence so complete Kestrel could hear her own blood.

"Base camp is four kilometers inland," Ovidio said. "We walk."

"You said boat."

"I said what got us here." He shouldered his pack. He was a compact man, mid-fifties maybe, with the flat calm of someone who had been paid to be calm in worse places than this. "Stay behind me on the trail. Step where I step."

She didn't step where he stepped. She hadn't followed anyone's footsteps since she was nine years old, and that had been her father's, and look how that ended.

The forest came down to the water's edge like a crowd leaning in to watch. It was the dry season's tail end — the river low, the banks exposed in ribs of red mud — but the air itself was wet, breathing on her neck. Her father had taught her names for this place once, when she was small enough to still want them: kapok, ceiba, the lianas that strangled the trees they climbed. He'd taught her everything except how to stay.

Four kilometers took an hour and a half. The camp announced itself before she saw it: the smell of woodsmoke gone cold, and beneath it something sweeter and wrong, like fruit rotting in a closed room.

Then she saw it through the trees, and stopped walking.

It was orderly. That was the first thing, and the worst thing. A base camp abandoned in panic looks like a wound — scattered gear, overturned chairs, the archaeology of flight. This looked like a photograph. Six tents pitched in a neat crescent. A mess table under a tarp with six places set. Plates with food still on them, rice gone hard, beans skinned over with a gray film. A pot of coffee boiled down to tar on the fire ring. A card game frozen mid-hand, one player's cards fanned face-down beside his plate as if he'd stepped away to look at something and meant to come back.

Nobody had come back.

"Doutor Reyes," Ovidio said quietly, at her shoulder. "Your father's camp."

"It's not my father's camp," she said. "My father's camps have dirty dishes."

But even as she said it she was moving, because there was a seventh tent set apart from the others, larger, and stenciled on its fly in fading block letters was a name she knew better than her own: R. REYES. His expedition flag hung limp from a pole beside it. Inside, his cot was made. His field notebooks were stacked and squared. On the folding table sat a satellite radio identical to the one she'd carried upriver, its battery light dead, its antenna snapped off clean and laid beside it like an amputated limb.

He hadn't lost contact. He had cut contact.

"Ovidio." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Where are the people?"

"That," he said, "is the question I am paid to answer."

* * *

They found the first body at the edge of the latrine trench, forty meters into the trees.

A man, young, in a concession company polo gone stiff with dried sweat. He lay on his back at the base of a clay bank with his eyes open to the canopy, one arm flung above his head, and there was no blood anywhere, which was the thing Kestrel kept returning to as she crouched over him. No blood, no animal sign, no broken branches above him. Just a man at the foot of a two-meter drop who had apparently fallen straight down, like a coat slipping off a hook.

"Fell," Ovidio said, looking down at him without crouching.

"Sure," Kestrel said. "People fall upward all the time."

She checked the pockets because it was what you did, what her father had drilled into her across a hundred dig sites: the dead tell you where they've been. In the breast pocket, folded twice, was a page torn from a survey report. She recognized the company letterhead from the contract they'd made her sign. The page was mostly numbers — grid coordinates, elevation readings — but along the margin, in a cramped hand, someone had written the same phrase over and over until the pen had torn the paper:

*it counts the boats it counts the boats it counts*

She put the page in her jacket. Ovidio watched her do it and said nothing, and that silence told her more than any question could have.

* * *

The survivor found them, or let herself be found, an hour later.

Kestrel heard her before she saw her — a deliberate noise, a boot heel struck against stone, three times, like a knock. Then Dr. Imani Cole stepped out of the green with a machete held loosely at her side and eyes that did the arithmetic of two strangers in about two seconds.

"You're not company," Cole said. Accent American, clipped, exhausted. Her gaze snagged on Kestrel's face. "You have his jaw. You're the daughter."

"Kestrel Reyes."

"He said you wouldn't come." Cole lowered the machete. "He said it the way people say things they're afraid of wanting."

"Where is he?"

"Inland. With Okonkwo and Vasquez. Three days now." Cole sat down heavily on a root as if her legs had been holding a verdict her whole body disagreed with. "There's a site. The drought pulled it out of the river — a temple complex, older than anything in the regional record, submerged for centuries. Your father found it. Or it found him; he was never clear on the difference after the first week."

"And the rest of your team?"

Cole looked at her for a long moment. "You signed their NDA too?"

"I signed a logistics contract."

"Then here's your logistics." Cole's voice went flat, recitation-flat, the voice of someone delivering findings. "Six weeks ago, the concession company sent a survey team up this same stretch. Four men. They came back with more money in their accounts than three years of salary. They came back quiet. One of them — Ferreira, the geologist — I knew him ten years. He sat across from me at a bar in Manaus and did not blink for the length of two beers. When I asked him what they'd found, he smiled, and it was like watching someone else operate his face. Two months later he bought a house in Brasília. Last month his wife filed a missing persons report. He walked out of it with a bag and never came back." She paused. "Rich, silent, changed. That's what comes back from upriver. Ask yourself what stays."

"The man at the clay bank—"

"Was the fourth member of that survey team. He came back to warn us. He didn't get past the latrine trench." Cole's mouth twisted. "Fell, they'll say."

"They?"

"Whoever reads the reports." Cole stood, joints cracking, and looked at Kestrel with a directness that felt like being weighed. "Your father sent us a message before he went inland. He said: if anyone comes asking, tell them the camp is fine and we are fine and do not say where he went. He said especially if the person asking has a company contract."

Kestrel felt the ground tilt slightly, some tectonic shift of loyalty. "I'm his daughter."

"You're also on their payroll," Cole said. "I know. He knows. He wrote it in the message." She turned toward the treeline, then back. "Nine days, isn't it? Until the permits lapse and this all becomes legally nobody's problem. Whatever you think you're retrieving, Dr. Reyes — decide fast what side of the water you're standing on."

* * *

Dusk came down like a lid. Cole showed them the spring, the defensive sightlines, the cache of supplies her father's team had left behind — methodical, generous, planned for exactly this. Then she melted back into the forest to her own watch post, and Kestrel was left with Ovidio and the cold mess table and the dark coming up off the river.

Ovidio built a small fire, efficient, shielded. He ate without appetite. And then he took out the satellite phone, and Kestrel — who had spent her childhood around radios, who knew instruments the way other people knew faces — watched him work it.

He wasn't typing a message. He was holding the transmit key down, lightly, in long presses. Three seconds. Pause. Three seconds.

Her skin went cold.

That was the tone pattern from the night on the river. Not a message. A beacon. He wasn't reporting to whoever lived above him — he was guiding them, laying a line of signal through the dark like bread crumbs, and every press of his thumb was a hand pointing inland, toward the temple, toward her father.

She thought of the dead man's note. *It counts the boats.*

She thought of her father, cutting his own antenna off and laying it neatly beside the radio, a man trying to go deaf and dumb to the world on purpose.

Ovidio caught her looking. He didn't stop transmitting. He just smiled, small and professional, and pressed the key again — three seconds, pause, three — and far downstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of an engine that no fishing boat makes.

Kestrel picked up her pack and started walking inland, alone, into the dark.
