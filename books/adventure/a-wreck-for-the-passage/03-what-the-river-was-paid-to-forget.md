## 3. What the River Was Paid to Forget

The drought had done what no survey team could: it had pulled the river's skirt up and shown everyone what the water had been hiding for four hundred years.

Kestrel saw it first as a geometry that didn't belong — long lines breaking the current a kilometer below the third bend, where the channel had shrunk to a brown ribbon between expanses of cracked, bleached mud. Then the shapes resolved. Steps. A terrace. The shoulders of something enormous lying on its back in the exposed riverbed like a drowned animal too big to bury.

"Stop here," she said.

Ovidio cut the outboard without argument, which told her he'd already known what they'd find. He'd been steering toward it since the second bend. She was beginning to understand that she hadn't been hired to find her father. She'd been hired to be seen finding him.

"You knew," she said.

"I knew there was a site." Ovidio kept his eyes on the temple, on the dark slot of an entrance at its base where the waterline used to sit. "I didn't know your father would still be in it."

She left him with the boat and climbed the mud on hands and knees where the crust held, following the tracks her father had left — boot prints going in two days ago, none coming out. Beside them, smaller prints. Imani Cole had said the doctor went inland with two others. So the third set belonged to someone Kestrel hadn't met yet.

The entrance exhaled cold air that smelled of stone and old water and, faintly, of woodsmoke. Someone was alive down there.

* * *

Her father sat at the bottom of a stairwell that had been a riverbed until recently, cross-legged before a wall of niches, each one stuffed with bundled documents wrapped in waxed cloth and pitch. A hurricane lamp burned beside him. He looked thinner than the man from her memory, grayer, but his hands were steady as he worked, sealing a bundle with practiced fingers.

He heard her boots on the wet stone and didn't turn around.

"They said they'd send someone," he said. "I hoped it wouldn't be you."

"That makes two of us." Her voice came out harder than she'd planned. Nine days of static, nine days of imagining him dead, and the first thing he gave her was disappointment. "You turned off your radio."

"I turned off their radio." Now he turned, and she saw the bruise-yellow exhaustion around his eyes, the way he kept one hand resting on the archive like a man touching a sleeping child. "Every transmission goes through their relay. You know that now, don't you? You've seen Ovidio's phone."

"I've seen enough." She came down the last steps into the lamplight. Behind her father, deeper in the chamber, two figures stirred — Imani Cole, rifle across her knees, and a young man Kestrel didn't recognize with a bandaged arm and the flat stare of someone who'd stopped sleeping. "There's a man dead at base camp. Fell down the embankment, they said."

"He fell," her father agreed. "After he was pushed. He wanted to radio the company that we'd found the records. They couldn't allow a transmission that mentioned them by name."

"The records." She looked at the wall of bundles. Hundreds of them. "What are they?"

"Paper." Her father almost smiled. "The most dangerous paper in this hemisphere. The concession isn't legal, Kestrel. It never was. The original grant, the surveys, the boundary treaties — manufactured. Forged in 1974 and backdated a century. The company didn't buy these rights. They invented them, and every government since has signed off on the fiction because the fiction was profitable." He pressed his palm flat against the nearest niche. "This temple was submerged when the river shifted in the 1600s. The documents predate the forgery. They're proof. Physical, datable, undeniable proof that everything upstream of us is built on a lie."

"And you've been sitting on it for two weeks."

"I've been *protecting* it for two weeks." His voice cracked open, and for one moment she saw not the father who'd chosen the work over her but a man holding a door shut against a flood. "They'll burn it, Kestrel. Or worse — they'll take it apart page by page and keep only what serves them. This is the only copy of the truth that exists, and I am the only person who knows how to read half of it, and I am *not leaving*—"

A sound rolled over the temple then, low and mechanical, vibrating up through the flooded floor: engines. Not one engine. Several, heavy, running slow.

Imani was suddenly beside them, rifle slung, moving to the entrance slit. Through it, downstream, three gunboats slid into view around the bend — gray hulls, mounted weapons, men standing in the bows like figures carved on old war monuments.

Ovidio stood in the lead boat. He wasn't looking at the temple. He was looking at his satellite phone, and he was smiling.

* * *

They came ashore in force. Twelve men fanning across the mudflat, rifles slung low and ready, while Ovidio walked ahead of them with his hands loose at his sides, a translator of violence.

Kestrel met him at the top of the steps.

"You brought them here."

"I brought the people who own this," Ovidio said. Not unkindly. That was the worst part — he sounded genuinely sorry. "Señor Reyes has something that doesn't belong to him. They're prepared to be generous. Money, transport, silence. Everyone walks away rich."

"Everyone except the man who fell at base camp."

Ovidio's face did something small and complicated. "That was not the plan."

"It never is."

Behind her, her father emerged from the stairwell carrying a single bundle — the master document, the forged grant itself, wrapped in oilcloth and clutched to his chest with both arms. He stopped on the second step, above the waterline, and looked at the line of guns and did not stop walking.

"Emiliano." Ovidio raised a hand, and the line halted. "Don't do this the hard way."

"You mean the honest way." Her father's voice carried across the flat, clear as a bell over water. "You mean give me the lie so I can hand you the truth, and everyone shakes hands and the river keeps paying you rent on stolen land. No." He lifted the bundle higher. "This goes to the press. To the ministries. To every court that will hear it. Your concession dies in the light."

One of the gunmen shifted his weight. Ovidio said, quietly, "Wait—"

Her father turned to Kestrel then, and everything he'd never said in twenty years of letters and silences was in his face at once — apology, love, instruction.

"Take them home," he said. "Not me. Them."

And he stepped backward off the terrace into the deep channel, holding the archive against his chest, choosing the weight of it over the weight of living—

The shot came from the gunboat. One round, professional, unhurried. It took him high in the chest and spun him, and he hit the shallow water at the base of the steps with a sound Kestrel would hear for the rest of her life.

She was moving before the echo finished. Down the steps, through the shallows, rolling him onto his back. His eyes were open. The bundle was still in his arms, soaked now, the oilcloth dark with blood and river.

"Kestrel." Blood at the corner of his mouth. His hand found her wrist and gripped with astonishing strength. "Listen. The flood comes tonight. The river takes the site back either way. So you choose—" a cough, terrible, wet, "—you choose what gets carried out. The paper drowns with me or it drowns alone. But Imani's hurt. Tomás can't walk fast. And you—" his grip tightened, "—you were always better at saving people than saving things."

"Shut up," she said. "Shut up, I'm getting you out—"

"No." He smiled, and it was the smile from her childhood, from before the expeditions ate him alive. "You get *them* out. Let the river have the rest. It kept it safe for four hundred years. It can keep it longer."

His hand slipped from her wrist.

Up on the bank, Ovidio was shouting orders, and the gunmen were wading toward the shallows, and somewhere above the temple the sky had gone the color of wet slate — the first clouds of flood season, arriving exactly on time.

Kestrel knelt in the water holding her father, holding the archive, holding both futures in her arms, and made her choice.
