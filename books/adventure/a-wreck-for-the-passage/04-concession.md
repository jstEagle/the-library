## 4. Concession

The river came for the temple the way debt comes for a debtor — slowly, then all at once.

Kestrel stood on the highest terrace with her father's body wrapped in canvas and the archive open at her feet, and she watched the water climb the stone steps she had climbed two days before, when the drought had laid the whole complex bare like a secret told too loudly. The brown water moved without hurry. It had all the time in the world. It had been waiting under the surface for four hundred years, patient as interest.

The archive was heavier than it looked. Oilcloth packets, wax-sealed, each one holding documents that could unmake a company: forged survey reports, backdated concessions, signatures of men who had never set foot past the second bend. Her father had carried them inland through flood season to keep them from being buried or stolen, and he had died holding them, and now they were hers, and every one of them was a weight with a name on it.

Imani Cole came up the steps behind her, favoring her splinted wrist. "You don't have to do this alone."

"I'm not doing anything," Kestrel said. "I'm letting go."

She untied the first packet and held it over the water. For a moment she let herself imagine the other ending — the one where she flew out with proof, testified, watched men in good suits lose everything. She imagined her father's face if he could see it. Not proud. She knew his face too well for that. He would look at her the way he'd looked at her across that last kitchen table, ten years ago: *you're spending your life on something that doesn't hold water.*

"Here's your concession," she said, to the company, to the river, to him — she wasn't sure which.

She let the packet fall. The water took it without a sound. Then the next, and the next, tearing them open first so the pages would scatter rather than sink intact, ink blooming and dissolving into the current like dark flowers. The river accepted everything. It had been paid to forget once; now it forgot for free.

When the last page was gone she knelt by her father's body. There was no time to dig a grave the ground wouldn't give back in three days. So she gave him to the same water, weighted with stones from the temple itself, and said the only words she had left, which were not forgiveness but were close enough to float on.

"You chose the work," she said. "I'm choosing the people. Somebody should get what they want."

* * *

Ovidio met them at the extraction point with the gunboat gone from the channel and a smile he wore like a borrowed coat. He looked at Kestrel's empty hands, at Imani's splint, at the two wounded porters leaning on each other under the tarp, and his eyes did the arithmetic.

"The site?" he asked.

"Downstream of the third bend," Kestrel said. "Submerged already. The flood's taken it." She handed him a folded chart with coordinates she had spent an hour inventing, anchored to nothing, sitting in forty meters of moving water. "Your employers can send divers. They'll find river mud and old catfish."

Ovidio studied her for a long moment. She let him. Let him believe what he needed to believe — that the find was lost, that no one had won, that his report would be received with disappointment instead of suspicion. A man who thought his employers had gotten a bad outcome tells them the truth. A man who thinks they've won tells them whatever keeps him safe.

"A pity," Ovidio said finally. "All that expense."

"Rivers are expensive," Kestrel said. "That's why nobody lives here."

He radioed it in that night from the boat, his voice flat and professional, reporting the site drowned, the concession unrecoverable, the consultant cooperative. Kestrel sat at the stern with Imani and listened to the static eat his words and thought about how easy it was to lie to someone who wanted the lie more than the truth.

The permit expired in four days. They made the second bend in three.

* * *

Home was smaller than she remembered, which is how home always is after a river.

She slept for eighteen hours, woke to rain on the window that sounded nothing like the river and everything like it, and spent a week answering nothing, seeing no one, letting the city's noise wash over her the way she'd once let the brown water take the pages. The company sent one email — a form letter closing out her contract, thanking her for her service as a logistics consultant. She read it twice, looking for a threat, and found only boilerplate. They believed Ovidio. That was the inheritance she'd arranged for them: nothing, beautifully documented.

On the ninth day she opened the box.

Her father's letters — eleven years of them, one a year, arriving on her birthday like clockwork, unanswered, unopened past the first line. She had kept them anyway. That was the thing about resentment; it was just love with nowhere to put itself.

She made coffee and started with the oldest and read straight through, and it was worse and better than she'd feared. No apologies. No explanations. Just a man writing to a daughter he didn't know how to reach, telling her about weather, about birds, about a stretch of water above the second bend where the light came down green through the canopy and the fish rose at dusk. Year after year. He had never once asked her to come. He had only ever described the place so well that staying away became its own kind of effort.

The last letter was different. Shorter. In it he wrote: *I've found something here that matters more than me. If you're reading this, I didn't come home to tell you about it, and I'm sorry, because I know what that makes me. But some things aren't meant to be kept, Kestrel. Some things are meant to be found and then given back. I think that's the whole job. I think that might be the whole life.*

She sat with that until the coffee went cold.

Then she took out paper — actual paper, because some answers deserved weight — and began to write. Not to him. There was no address for him anymore except the river. She wrote to the version of him that wrote the letters, the man who could describe light through a canopy better than he could describe love, and she told him what she'd done, and she told him she understood, and she told him, in the last line, the thing she had carried upriver and back:

*I burned nothing and saved nothing and the river has all of it now. You'd have hated that. You'd have loved it too. I'm learning the difference between keeping a thing and honoring it. It took me eleven years and nine days.*

She sealed it and did not send it anywhere. She put it in the box, on top of his letters, the last one, the reply, the correspondence closed at last.

Outside, the rain kept falling, somewhere feeding rivers she would never see again. Somewhere below the second bend, water moved over stone, over dissolved ink, over a man resting among the things he'd chosen. The permits had expired. The company had its nothing. The river had its secret.

Kestrel Reyes got up, washed her cup, and opened the window to let the sound in — that rain, patient as interest, falling on a world that kept almost none of what it was given, and gave it all away anyway.
