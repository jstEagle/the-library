## 1. The Weight of a Promise

The river gave up its dead slowly, and Freya Amberdale had learned to be patient with it.

Dawn on the mudflats at Corrow's Landing came gray and smelling of silt and rotting reeds, the water so shallow at low tide that you could walk a quarter mile out over flats that steamed like something freshly turned. Freya worked the flats alone, as always, boots sinking to the ankle in muck that sucked at every step, dragging a hand-sledge behind them loaded with whatever the current had surrendered overnight. A ship's bell green with verdigris. A crate of spoiled tallow. Three spoons, one candlestick, half a mirror that showed nothing but a smear of silver.

Salvage. That was the polite word for it. The impolite word was what the lender's men used—*mudlarking*, spat like an accusation—and they used it often, because Silas Grebe's enforcers enjoyed reminding Freya exactly how far a daughter of the celebrated archaeologist Edmund Amberdale had fallen. Digs in the southern deserts, papers published in three languages, and now his child knelt in river muck pulling spoons out of the shallows to chip away at a debt of four hundred crowns.

Four hundred and eleven, after this morning's interest.

Freya straightened, pressed a wrist against their aching back, and looked downriver toward the town. Corrow's Landing wasn't much to look at even from a distance: a spine of boardwalks built on pilings above the flood line, roofs sagging under moss, the great timber flume that carried ore barges down from the high country groaning day and night like something in pain. Beyond it, the river ran north into country nobody in town talked about except in the past tense—the gorge country, the white water, and past all of it, a hundred miles upstream where the maps went blank, the glacier. The source.

Freya didn't look at it long. Looking north was a habit their father had, and look where that had gotten him.

The tide was turning. Freya could feel it before hearing it—a change in the flat's breathing, the pools going still, the gulls lifting off the sandbars all at once. Time to drag the sledge back before the water reclaimed everything, including anyone foolish enough to still be standing on it.

That was when Freya heard the sound from the reed bank. A wet, dragging scrape, like a boat being hauled over gravel by someone who no longer believed in the boat.

* * *

He came out of the reeds on his hands and knees.

An old man, or a man oldened fast by something worse than years—coat stiff with dried mud, beard gray-streaked and crusted, one boot missing entirely, the bare foot beneath it black to the ankle. He crawled the way drowning men swim: without dignity, without choice, every motion costing him something he couldn't spare.

Freya stood very still, sledge rope wrapped around one fist, and did the arithmetic of the mudflats. No weapon visible. No one following—at least not yet, not in sight. And a man crawling toward you out of the reed bank at dawn was either running from something or carrying something, and either way he'd be dead before he could be dangerous.

"Water," the man rasped. His voice sounded like the flume groaning. "Please."

There was a jug in the sledge, half full of river water run through a cloth filter. Freya crouched, kept three feet between them, and held it out. The man drank the way he crawled—without dignity, without choice—water running down through the crust of his beard, and when he finished he lay his cheek against the cold mud for a moment as if the ground itself were kind.

"Halloway," he said. "Ezra Halloway." He said the name like he was checking whether it still fit. "You're Amberdale's kid."

The name landed like a hook set deep. Freya's face did what it always did with that name, which was nothing at all. "I don't know you."

"Never said you did." Halloway pushed himself up onto his haunches, swaying. Up close Freya could see the damage properly now: the dark bloom spreading through the side of his coat, the way his breathing caught and hitched like a mechanism failing. He'd been shot, or stabbed, or both, and he'd bound it with strips torn from his own shirt, and the binding had given up hours ago. "Knew your father, though. Knew him well enough to get him killed."

The gulls were screaming downstream. The tide was coming in. Every instinct Freya owned said *stand up, walk away, let the river have him*, because dying men on the Corrow flats drew questions, and questions drew Grebe's men, and Grebe's men drew blood.

But refusing a dying man cost nothing. That was the thought Freya would turn over later, again and again, examining it for the flaw. Refusing cost nothing—yet.

"Talk fast," Freya said. "Tide's coming."

Halloway laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing left pink froth at the corner of his mouth. "Fast. All right." He fumbled inside his coat with fingers gone clumsy, and drew out an oilcloth packet, grease-stained, tied with waxed cord. He held it in both hands the way other men held infants or knives. "You know what's upstream. Past the gorge. Past the falls nobody runs twice."

"I know what's upstream is none of my business."

"It's about to be." His eyes were bright and terrible, fever-lit. "I spent eleven years running from that river. Eleven years I told myself the map was worthless, that what's at the source stays buried, that a man can outrun a thing if he just keeps moving. You know what I learned?" He didn't wait for an answer. "You don't outrun it. It walks beside you. It waits until you're tired."

Freya glanced north despite themselves. The sky above the gorge country had that pale, hammered-metal look it got before storms. "I'm not going anywhere. I have debts."

"Grebe." Halloway spat, weakly. "I know about Grebe. Four hundred crowns, isn't it? I know what he does to people who can't pay. I watched him do it to the Ferro family." He leaned closer, and Freya caught the smell of him—blood and river rot and something chemical, lamp oil maybe, soaked into the oilcloth. "So here's my trade, Amberdale's kid. Here's the only trade I've got left."

He pressed the packet into the mud between them.

"The map goes upstream. To the source. Before calving season opens and the ice seals the route—that's six weeks, maybe less this year. You carry it there, you see what's there with your own eyes, and then—" his breath caught, held, released— "then you decide what to do with it. Not me. Not the men hunting me. You. That's the promise I need sworn. Carry it. Don't sell it. Don't trade it. Don't let it out of your hands until you're standing at the source."

"And why," Freya said slowly, "would I swear anything to a stranger bleeding out on a mudflat?"

"Because I'm not asking you to believe me." Halloway smiled, and it was the worst thing Freya had seen in a long time—not cruel, just finished, a man already halfway to being a memory. "I'm asking you to carry paper. What it costs you, you'll learn on the way. What it pays, you'll learn at the end. And because—" he tapped the packet once, with one cracked fingernail— "your father's name is written on what's inside. In his hand. And I think you've been pretending for eleven years that you don't want to know why he stopped writing."

The tide was lapping at the edge of the reeds now. Somewhere downstream, a dog barked, and a voice called out—too far to make out words, close enough to make the moment finite.

Freya looked at the packet. Grease-stained oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, sitting in the mud like a wound. Everything sensible in them said *leave it*. Sensible had kept them alive in Corrow's Landing for eleven years. Sensible had kept the debt manageable, the head down, the father's name unspoken.

Sensible had also gotten them exactly nowhere.

"If I take it," Freya said, "I'm not promising to go. I'm promising to decide."

Halloway's eyes closed briefly, as if even that much was more than he'd dared hope. "Decide fast."

Freya picked up the packet. It was lighter than it should have been, and heavier than it looked. They untied the cord just enough to confirm it was what he claimed: folded paper, dense with ink, a coastline of hand-drawn lines and markings in two different hands—one cramped and desperate, one measured and precise, and the second hand made Freya's stomach drop through the mud beneath them, because they knew it, they'd have known it anywhere, they'd grown up watching it move across ledger pages and letters that stopped coming—

They retied the cord. Their hands were not entirely steady.

"Sworn," Freya said quietly. "Carried, not sold. Decided at the source. On my word."

"On your word," Halloway echoed. He slumped back against the sledge, and some tension went out of him that was more than physical. "Good. Good. Now listen, because we haven't long. There are men coming for this. My partners—or they were, thirty years ago, before I ran. Voss. Two brothers, Henrik and Marek. They're not clever, but they're patient, and they're thorough, and they will peel this town apart looking for the map, starting with anyone who ever spoke my name." He gripped Freya's sleeve with sudden strength, fever-hot fingers. "Don't go home. Whatever you keep at home, leave it. Go north tonight. Find passage upstream—there's a surveyor who works the lower river, disgraced, takes odd jobs. Brandt. She charted the gorge. If anyone alive can get you up that water, it's her."

"You knew I'd say yes," Freya said. "You came here for me specifically."

"I came here because I was dying and you were closest," Halloway said. Then, softer: "And because your father asked me to. Thirty years ago. He said—if it ever comes back around, find my kid. They'll understand. Not forgive. Understand." His grip loosened. "I never understood him. Maybe you will."

The first finger of the incoming tide slid across the flat and touched the sledge wheels. Downstream, the dog had stopped barking, which was somehow worse.

"Ezra," Freya said—the first time they'd used his name, and it felt strange in the mouth, borrowed. "Who shot you?"

Halloway looked north, at the hammered sky, and smiled his finished smile. "Ask the Voss brothers. Or better—" his eyes found Freya's, and held them, and in them was a last flicker of the man he must have been before the river broke him— "don't let them ask you first."

Then the light went out of him gradually, like a lamp running dry, and by the time the water reached his knees he was breathing in long pauses, and by the time it reached his waist he wasn't breathing at all, and Freya stood in the rising tide holding a dead man's map and their own sworn word, both of which suddenly weighed more than the whole drowned world.

* * *

Freya did not go home.

They went instead to the lean-to behind the cooperage where they slept, moving through back lanes with the packet strapped flat against their chest beneath their coat, and took only what could be carried in one load: the tin box of crowns hidden under the floorboards—sixty-one, counted twice—the good knife, dry socks, the compass. The compass had been their father's, brass-cased, needle worn pale, and Freya had told themselves for eleven years that they kept it because brass sold well.

They packed in silence and tried not to hear how loud the silence was.

It was nearly full dark when Freya slipped out along the flume road, and the town was wrong. Freya felt it before understanding it: lamps lit in shops that should be shuttered, men standing in doorways in twos and threes, and outside Grebe's counting-house, a lantern burning where no lantern burned at night unless someone was being interrogated behind it. Word traveled fast in Corrow's Landing. A body on the flats, or the rumor of one. Questions already forming like weather.

At the edge of town, where the boardwalk gave way to the towpath and the river went black and broad under the stars, Freya stopped and allowed themselves one look back. Somewhere behind those sagging roofs was the life they'd built out of patience and small salvage, four hundred and eleven crowns of debt and a name they never said aloud. It had taken eleven years to build and one dawn on the mudflats to end.

The compass needle swung and settled, pointing north, upriver, toward the gorge and the falls nobody ran twice and the ice beyond.

Freya turned north and started walking, and behind them, faint across the water, came the sound of boots on the towpath—more than two sets, moving fast, and a voice calling ahead into the dark:

*"Find the Amberdale kid."*
