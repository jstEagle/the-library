## 2. Debt Collectors and Dead Men

The knock came at dawn, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Three blows of a fist on the doorframe of the salvage shack, hard enough to shake dust from the rafters. Freya was already awake—she had been awake most of the night with Halloway's map spread across her knees, tracing grease-softened lines by candlelight—but she went still anyway, the way prey goes still, listening.

"Amberdale." The voice belonged to Dreech, one of Marrow Quill's collectors, a man built like a dock piling and about as conversational. "We know you're in there. Open up."

Freya folded the map along its worn creases and slid it into her shirt, against her skin, where it lay warm and greasy as a second heart. Beside it she tucked the compass Halloway had pressed into her hand with his last breath—the brass case still carrying the heat of a dying man's grip. Then she looked around the shack at everything else: her father's old field tools, the coil of rope she'd hauled salvage with for three years, the tin cup, the blanket, the lamp. All of it would go to Quill now, or to fire, or to whoever picked through the wreckage after.

It was strange how little that hurt.

She went out through the back, where the wall planks gapped wide enough for a thin woman with practice, and dropped into the mud-flat dawn. Corrow's Landing was waking around her—gulls screaming over the fish racks, woodsmoke rising crooked from chimneys, the river sliding past gray-green and indifferent, carrying last night's rain toward the sea. Somewhere behind her, Dreech's fist hit the door again, then splintered it.

She didn't run. Running drew eyes. She walked, fast but even, boots squelching on the tidal flats, past the drying sheds, past the chapel with its leaning steeple, toward the north road out of town. If she could reach the treeline before Quill's men organized—

"Miss Amberdale."

The voice came from ahead, not behind, and Freya's stomach dropped through the mud.

He stood by the beached hull of a derelict skiff, a lean man in a good coat gone bad at the cuffs, holding something in both hands the way a priest holds a book. He was younger than Dreech, better dressed, and worse. This was Corven Quill himself—not the lender, but the lender's nephew, who did the collecting that required manners right up until it didn't.

"You've been difficult to find," he said pleasantly. "My uncle is owed four hundred and eleven crowns. Principal, interest, and the fee for the trouble of this morning."

"I'll have it by season's end."

"Everyone says that." He smiled. It was a nice smile, which made it obscene. "Here's what troubles me, Miss Amberdale. Last night an old man died in your camp. Crawled half a mile on his belly to get there, by the look of the trail. And this morning you're leaving town with nothing but the clothes you stand in—which means whatever he gave you, you're keeping it, and my uncle's paper isn't worth the ink." He tilted his head. "So I'll ask once. What did Halloway give you?"

The name hit her like cold water. *Halloway.* She'd never told anyone. He'd died whispering it into her ear like a confession, and she'd buried him at first light under a cairn of river stones because no one else would, and already the name was loose in the town's mouth.

"He gave me nothing," Freya said. "He gave me a job to do."

"A job." Corven tasted the word. "Dying men are generous that way. Here's my offer, since I'm feeling charitable. Give my uncle his four hundred eleven, or give me whatever's worth more than four hundred eleven, and we part friends."

"And if I have neither?"

"Then I take the shack, the tools, and the debt follows you upstream, downstream, wherever you go. My uncle's paper has long arms." He stepped aside, almost courteously, clearing her path north. "Walk away, Miss Amberdale. Everyone does eventually."

*Everyone does eventually.* She thought of her father walking away upriver twenty years ago, and something in her chest closed like a fist.

"No," Freya said, and heard her own voice come out flat and certain, surprising her. "I don't think I will."

She walked past him. He let her. That frightened her more than anything else that morning—Corven Quill never let anything go unless he intended to collect it later, with interest.

* * *

She made six miles before she found the blood.

It was on the north road itself, a dark smear across the wheel ruts, then more of it—a drag trail veering off into the alders, flies already gathering in the warming air. Freya should have walked past. Every instinct for survival she owned told her to walk past. But the trail led toward the old ferry landing, and Halloway had come *from* the north, and there was a question forming in her chest that she couldn't leave unanswered.

She found him—or what remained of him—in the shallows under the landing, snagged against a piling. Not Halloway. A stranger, a big man in a prospector's oilskins, throat opened to the bone. His pack had been cut away. His pockets turned out. Whoever killed him had been thorough and unhurried.

And pinned under a stone at the water's edge, weighted deliberately, deliberately *found*-ably, was a scrap of oilcloth with writing on it.

Freya crouched, touched nothing, read it twice.

*THE ONE WHO CARRIES IT GOES NORTH. FIND THE GIRL. FIND THE PAPER. VOSS.*

Not Quill's men, then. Someone else entirely, someone who wanted the message discovered—wanted *her* to discover it, or wanted Quill's people chasing their tails while the real hunters worked quiet. Her eyes went to the dead man's face, bloated already, unfamiliar. One of Halloway's partners? One of the men the map had made rich and then ruined?

The Voss brothers. Halloway had said the name only once, at the very end, when the words were costing him what little breath remained: *the Voss brothers will come, girl, they'll come faster than the river.* She had thought it delirium. It was a warning, delivered too late to save him and just early enough, maybe, to save her—if she was fool enough to hear it.

She stood. Up the bank, back among the alders, she caught movement—nothing definite, a wrongness in the pattern of the leaves, the sense of being watched by something patient. Two somebodies. The drag trail had been fresh. The killers were close, and they had left the note because they wanted her running scared in a predictable direction.

Fine. Let them think fear made her stupid.

Freya walked back to the road, took off her boots, and waded into the river.

* * *

The current tried to kill her twice in the first mile, and she let it believe it might succeed. She came out on the far bank a half-mile downstream, soaked, shivering, boots slung around her neck, and cut northeast through the pine dark with the compass open in her palm. The needle swung and settled, swung and settled, always finding the same truth: north-northwest, upriver, toward the glacier country marked on Halloway's map in lines no surveyor had ever drawn.

By midday she allowed herself a fire, small, in a hollow behind a fallen spruce, and laid the map out on a flat stone while her clothes steamed. In daylight it looked worse than it had by candlelight—creased, stained, annotated in three different hands, as though it had passed through years of desperate ownership. Rivers she knew. Rivers she didn't. And at its head, beyond the last named tributary, a valley drawn in careful detail with a single word repeated in the margin, over and over, in Halloway's failing script:

*SOURCE. SOURCE. SOURCE.*

Underneath, smaller, almost hidden: *They think it's gold. It was never gold. God forgive me, it was never gold.*

Freya sat back on her heels and stared at the fire.

Four hundred eleven crowns. That was the number her whole life had collapsed into—three years of hauling salvage from the riverbed, hands cracked raw, selling her father's books one by one, all of it swallowed by interest that grew like rot. Four hundred eleven crowns, and here was a map that might lead to enough gold to bury the number forever. Any sensible person would go for the gold.

But Halloway hadn't crawled a half-mile on his belly to give her gold. He'd given her a promise to carry, and promises, unlike debts, couldn't be paid by proxy. And the Voss brothers wanted the paper—which meant the paper led somewhere they feared she might reach.

Fear, she was discovering, hardened strangely. Not into courage—that was a story people told afterward—but into a kind of cold arithmetic. *If I run, they hunt me. If I hide, Quill finds me. If I go back, I lose everything slowly. If I go forward, I lose everything quickly, but I choose the losing.* There was freedom in that, ugly and bracing. The only direction nobody would expect, the only direction that cost everything, was upstream.

She fed the map back into her shirt, kicked apart the fire, and stood at the edge of the hollow looking north, where the pines climbed toward country she'd only ever seen as a blue suggestion on the horizon—the glacier's shoulder, white and remote, the place her father had gone and not returned from.

Somewhere behind her, Corven Quill was telling his uncle she'd refused. Somewhere in the alders across the river, men who signed their threats *Voss* were reading the same river she was. She was one woman with wet boots, a dead man's compass, and a debt that followed her like a shadow with teeth.

It was, she thought, the best hand she'd ever held.

Freya picked up her pack and started walking north, and did not look back at the smoke of Corrow's Landing rising thin and gray behind her—except once, just once, at the bend where the road vanished, to say goodbye to the person she had been when waking up that morning.

The river ran beside her, going the same way, and it sounded almost like company.
