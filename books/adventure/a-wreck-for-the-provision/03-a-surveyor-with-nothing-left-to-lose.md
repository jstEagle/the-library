## 3. A Surveyor with Nothing Left to Lose

Ilsa Brandt saw the girl three days before she decided to speak to her, because three days was how long it took to be sure.

She was good at being sure. It was the one thing the river had ever taught her that the licensing board couldn't take away: watch long enough, measure twice, and the water will tell you what it's going to do before it does it. People were no different. They eddied. They undercut themselves. They showed you their seams if you had the patience to read them.

The girl—Amberdale, though Ilsa wouldn't learn the name until later—had come into Corrow's Landing on the morning packet from downriver and had not left since. Three days of sleeping rough in the reed flats south of the fish shacks, of buying bread with coins counted out one at a time, of standing at the chandler's window each evening looking at gear she clearly could not afford. A compass hung at her neck on a leather cord, and she touched it the way other people touched saints' medals—not for luck but for memory.

And she kept looking upstream.

That was what fixed Ilsa's attention. Everyone in Corrow's Landing looked downstream, toward money, toward the sea, toward anywhere that wasn't here. The town sat in its own mud like a thing that had given up arguing with gravity. But the girl stood at the north landing every morning with her face turned upriver, toward the gorge country, toward the white water, toward the glacier nobody with any sense went near after the first thaw—and she looked at it the way a debtor looks at a locked door, which is to say: like there might be something behind it worth breaking for.

On the third day, Ilsa followed her into the market square and watched her try to sell a prospector's pick to a junk dealer who offered her half what it was worth and knew it. The girl took the offer anyway. That told Ilsa more than anything else had. Desperation with a deadline attached.

So did the way the girl flinched when two broad-shouldered men in oilskins came through the square asking questions of the stall-holders. Big men. Matching men. One had a scar that split his eyebrow; the other wore his hair cropped short as a convict's. They moved through the crowd the way pike move through minnows—not hurrying, because nothing needed to hurry when everything else was already afraid.

Ilsa bought a cup of coffee she didn't want from the cart nearest the chandlery and settled in to do what she did best, which was wait for the river to show its hand.

* * *

Her name had been on charts once. Not just her name—her initials, cut into brass survey plates, stamped on the official hydrographic sheets of the upper Vessel River from Two-Mile Rapid to the calving face itself. *Surveyed under the direction of I. Brandt, Royal Commission on Northern Waterways.* Eleven seasons of her life frozen into ink. She had known that river the way a mother knows a child's breathing in the next room: by feel, by absence, by the wrongness in a sound.

Then the Kestrel Narrows disaster. Nine men drowned when a rock chute she had marked as passable collapsed under the commission's lead boat. The inquiry found her markings negligent. The inquiry did not find, because nobody looked, that the commission's own director had ordered her soundings redone by a cheaper crew who'd skipped the deep-water transects entirely, or that her original field notes—which showed the chute unstable—had vanished from the archive six weeks before the accident. She had stood in that hearing room with her hands flat on the table and told eleven men in good coats exactly where their river would kill someone, and they had thanked her politely and taken her license.

The director got a knighthood. Ilsa Brandt got a leaky boat called the *Magpie*, a mooring fee she was perpetually behind on, and eleven years of drinking in towns like this one, doing salvage runs and illegal chart work for smugglers who didn't care about credentials so long as she could find the deep channel in fog.

She had thought herself finished with the upper river. Then, two nights ago, a dying man had crawled into the reed camp where the Amberdale girl slept, and Ilsa—who had been checking her own traplines along the flats—had watched from thirty yards as he pressed something into the girl's hands and made her swear to something, his cracked lips working, her face going strange and still in the firelight.

Ilsa hadn't caught the words. She hadn't needed to. Because when the man unwrapped the oilcloth bundle, even at that distance, even by bad light, Ilsa had recognized the paper.

Grease-stained. Hand-drawn. And along the river's course, in a draftsman's hand she would have known anywhere because she had corrected its errors line by line for eleven years—the old expedition style, the commission's house notation, the depth-symbols and chute-marks that only a handful of living people could still read.

Halloway. She should have guessed. Silas Halloway had been a chainman on her last survey crew, back when she'd still had a name worth putting on things. He'd gone prospecting after the commission folded and everyone said he'd found something up past the moraine and gone strange about it. Now he was dying in the mud, and whatever he'd found, he'd drawn it in a language almost nobody spoke anymore.

Almost nobody.

* * *

The enforcers found the girl's camp on the fourth night.

Ilsa heard them coming—two sets of boots on the boardwalk, heavy, unhurried—and was already moving through the reeds with the particular silence of a woman who had spent a decade learning how not to be found. She reached the edge of the clearing in time to see the scarred brother put his boot through the cold fire ring and the other one hold the girl's salvaged pick up to the moonlight, turning it, reading it like a ledger.

"Where's the paper, Amberdale?" the scarred one said. So they knew her name. "Halloway gave it to somebody. We traced him to the Landing. There's nobody else it could be."

"I don't know what you're talking about." The girl's voice was steady. Better than steady—flat, the voice of someone who had learned that fear was a luxury billed by the hour. "I never met any Halloway."

"He crawled into your camp four nights ago. Half the reed-cutters saw him."

"Then half the reed-cutters have it."

The unscarred brother laughed without humor and stepped closer, and Ilsa weighed her options with the cold arithmetic that had kept her alive through nine drowned men's worth of guilt: intervene and reveal herself to the Voss brothers, whom she knew by reputation the way sailors know a reef, or let the girl take a beating and lose her one chance at the map.

The girl solved it herself. She broke left through the reeds, fast and low, and the brothers went after her, and Ilsa did neither of the things she'd considered. She simply melted backward into the dark and let the river sort its minnows, and an hour later, from the deck of the *Magpie*, she watched the girl slip aboard a downbound barge dressed as a deckhand, pay the mate with the junk dealer's coins, and get off again two miles north where the channel forked.

Running. Badly. Toward the gorge country with no boat, no gear, and two professional hunters behind her.

It was, Ilsa reflected, the most surveyable object she'd seen in years. Everything about its future was legible. It would wreck within the week, or be wrecked for it.

Unless somebody changed the chart.

* * *

She intercepted the girl at dawn on the fifth day, at the old ferry slip above the fork, where the current ran green and fast over gravel and the mountains finally stood up out of the haze like a verdict.

The girl heard her boots on the planks and spun, a filleting knife already out of her belt—good instincts, terrible reach—and Ilsa stopped ten feet away with her empty hands visible and her voice pitched low and even, the voice she'd used on panicky green crewmen in standing waves.

"You're Freya Amberdale. Your father was Edmund Amberdale, expedition archaeologist, commissioned '09. You're carrying Silas Halloway's map, the Voss brothers are two days behind you with a rifle apiece, and you're about to walk into the gorge country on foot, which means you'll die at the first portage even if they don't catch you." She let that sit. "My name is Ilsa Brandt. I surveyed every mile of the river you're trying to reach. I'm offering you a boat."

The knife didn't lower. The girl's eyes—gray, exhausted, ferociously awake—went over Ilsa's oilskins, her salt-cracked hands, the frayed collar underneath. Cataloguing. Deciding how much of what she'd heard was salesmanship.

"How do you know about Halloway?"

"I watched him give you the map. Four nights ago, your camp, the reed flats. I was checking traps thirty yards off and I mind my own business, which is why you weren't robbed that night instead of chased this one." Ilsa tilted her head toward the water. "I also know the notation on that map, because I helped write it. Which means I know something you don't yet, Miss Amberdale, and I'll tell you free of charge, as a gesture of good faith."

Freya's jaw tightened. "Which is?"

"The route on that map closes in nineteen days. Maybe twenty. Calving season opens and the ice comes down the gorge like God dropping houses, and nothing goes up or down until autumn. Whatever promise you swore to a dying man has a deadline, and you are currently losing." Ilsa walked to the edge of the slip and pointed downstream, to where a squat, patched, ugly little vessel sat at her mooring with her paint peeling and her name half-scraped off the stern. "The *Magpie*. She leaks, she's slow, and she's mine outright. I can put you at the glacier's toe inside the window. In exchange, I want a share of whatever Halloway marked. A third. Non-negotiable."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I've lost nothing but sleep, and you've lost everything, slowly, on foot, in bear country." Ilsa held her gaze. "You've spent five days deciding whether to trust me. Don't bother. You shouldn't. You shouldn't trust anyone, least of all a disgraced surveyor who wants a cut of your fortune. Trust arithmetic instead. One boat plus one pilot plus one map beats zero plus zero plus one map. That's the whole contract."

For a long moment the girl said nothing. The river filled the silence, talking its green gravel talk, and somewhere far upstream thunder rolled off the high ice—a sound Ilsa felt in her back teeth, an old acquaintance saying hello.

"You said my father's name," Freya said at last. Quietly. Dangerously. "Why do you know my father's name?"

Because, Ilsa thought, I have spent eleven years reading everything ever written about the Amberdale expedition, because your father's disappearance and my disgrace came out of the same river and the same season, and because I have wondered every single day since whether the two events shared a root. Because I think Silas Halloway sold that expedition to men who wanted it dead, and I think the proof has been sitting in his grease-stained pocket for a decade, and I think it is now hanging around your neck on a leather cord.

But that was not a thing to say on a ferry slip to a desperate woman holding a knife.

"Your father charted sites along the upper Vessel," Ilsa said. "His expedition used my surveys. Everyone who went up that river in those years used my surveys." She shrugged, and let the shrug carry what it carried. "Do we have terms or not?"

Freya Amberdale looked at the boat, then at the mountains, then—last, longest—at the compass against her chest. When she raised her eyes again, something had closed behind them, some door shut and barred, and Ilsa recognized it because she owned the same door herself.

"A third," Freya said. "And you tell me everything you know about the expedition. Everything. Before we pass the gorge mouth."

"Everything I know," Ilsa agreed, and shook the offered hand, and felt through the girl's cracked palm the tremor she was hiding—the fear, the debt, the grief—all of it held under iron discipline, the way water holds under ice.

They cast off within the hour. The *Magpie* took the current grumbling, and Ilsa stood at her wheel with the old channels waking in her memory like scars in rain, and did not let herself think about how many years she had sworn never to go back up that river, or about the two sails of black smoke rising far behind them from the direction of Corrow's Landing.

By noon, the smoke had resolved into a boat. By one, she could see the figures at its rail.

The Vosses had found a boat of their own.
