## 4. Upstream Against Everything

The river did not care that Freya had never run water before.

That was the first lesson, and Ilsa Brandt delivered it without turning her head from the current. She stood at the stern of the *Maggie's Folly* with one hand on the tiller and the other holding a tin cup of black coffee she never seemed to drink, and she said it the way a person states the weather: "The river doesn't care. Learn that first and you might live long enough to learn the rest."

Freya sat amidships with the map wrapped in oilcloth against her chest and her hands white-knuckled on a gunwale that flexed like a living thing. Below them the water ran brown-green over drowned willows, fast and glassy on top, and underneath that glass she could feel—could *hear*—the river thinking. A low mutter of gravel bars. A hiss where the channel split around a snag. Somewhere ahead, a sound like a crowd murmuring in a language she didn't speak.

"That's the rapids," Ilsa said, following Freya's eyes upstream. "Hear the pitch? High and steady means shallow and rocky but even. If it drops to a boom-boom-boom, that's holes big enough to swallow a boat whole. That one's singing high. We'll run it."

"We'll *run* it?"

"You want to portage every riffle between here and the ice, we'll arrive at calving season next spring. The Vosses would be along before we'd cleared the second bend." Ilsa finally sipped the coffee, grimaced at it as if it had personally offended her, and set it down. "Besides. You wanted to go upstream. Nobody promised upstream was polite."

The first rapids took them an hour later, and it was nothing like Freya had imagined. She had pictured violence. What she got instead was *precision*—Ilsa walking the boat through a maze of standing waves with small corrections, the tiller never more than a few degrees off center, reading the water the way Freya read strata in a cut bank. There was a seam down the middle of the rapid, a line of smoother water where two currents braided together, and Ilsa found it and held it for forty yards while rocks the size of cottages slid past close enough to touch.

"See the V?" Freya shouted over the roar.

"There's no V," Ilsa shouted back. "There's a *downstream V*. Smooth water pointing downstream means safe channel. Pointing *upstream*—" the bow swung hard left, "—means a rock under it, every time. Water can't lie about what's under it. It just tells the truth in a language most people are too busy drowning to translate."

They came out the bottom into slack water and Freya discovered her whole body shaking, not from fear exactly, though there was plenty of that, but from something adjacent to it. She had spent six years digging salvage out of the Corrow's Landing mudflats, and she knew work, knew cold, knew the particular misery of hauling someone else's lost property out of someone else's lost river. But this was different. This was the river moving *through* her instead of her moving through the river.

"Again," she said.

Ilsa glanced back, and for half a second something moved behind her eyes that might have been approval, quickly filed away. "Again," she agreed.

* * *

They made twelve miles the first day and camped on a gravel bar below a cutbank of blue clay. Ilsa built the fire small and screened, because smoke traveled, and while Freya gutted two grayling for supper Ilsa cleaned the boat's bilge by lantern light, cursing at the seams in a low continuous monotone that Freya was beginning to understand was how the woman prayed.

"The *Folly* leaks," Freya observed.

"The *Folly* leaks at a rate I have personally calculated to the ounce per hour," Ilsa said. "She is not sinking. She is *negotiating*. There's a difference." She bailed the last of the water over the side and sat back on her heels. "Your turn. Show me the map."

Freya hesitated. It was instinct now, three days old and already hardened into habit—hands off, it's mine, it's the promise. But Ilsa had run them through water today that would have killed them both without her, and debts were debts.

She spread the oilcloth on a flat rock. Halloway's grease-stained paper caught the firelight: the river drawn in confident ink, then dissolving near its headwaters into a confusion of lines, corrections, marginal notes in a cramped hand. And there—the thing that had kept Freya awake since the trading post, since the journal they hadn't yet found but whose existence she could feel like a splinter—was a mark she almost recognized. A little symbol like a cairn with a line through it, repeated twice near the glacier's toe.

"My father used to draw that," she said quietly. "Cairns. He marked every site he trusted with a cairn symbol. Said a cairn meant *someone came back*."

Ilsa said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed register, flatter, more careful. "I charted sixty miles of this river eleven years ago. Government survey. My soundings were good. My crew was good." She fed a stick to the fire. "A flood took out our lower camp and nine hundred pounds of equipment, and the report needed someone to blame, and I was the one who'd argued against camping there. Funny how that works. Arguing correctly cost me my license."

"You think the map's wrong."

"I think the map is *old*, and rivers move, and whoever drew those upper reaches drew them from memory or from hearsay or from a dying man's wishful thinking." She looked up. "Which is why tomorrow you start learning to read water for yourself. Because one day soon I'm going to be busy keeping us alive, and you're going to need to find the channel on your own."

* * *

The learning was not gentle.

On the third morning Ilsa put Freya at the tiller through a stretch of braided shallows and stood at the bow with her arms crossed, offering nothing but the occasional word: "*Seam.*" Freya would find it. "*Rocks, left.*" Freya would swing wide. Once, when Freya misread a downstream V that was actually the tongue of a hole, the *Folly* buried her bow in green water and shipped a hundred pounds of the river in four seconds flat, and Ilsa bailed and laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for years—and said, "Good. Now you'll never trust that one again. Fear properly applied is a surveying instrument."

By the fifth day Freya could hold a seam for a quarter mile. By the seventh she could look at a stretch of broken water and hear it—not understand it, not yet, but *hear* it, the way you come to hear a language before you can speak it. Her shoulders stopped aching from clenching. Her hands learned the tiller's grammar.

And somewhere in there, without either of them announcing it, they became a crew.

It happened in small transactions. Ilsa started leaving the coffee within Freya's reach instead of hoarding it. Freya started taking the night watch without being asked, sitting up with the rifle across her knees, watching the dark water for the running lights or the campfire glow that would mean the Voss brothers had closed the gap. They talked in the evenings—carefully at first, then less so—about Corrow's Landing, about the disaster that ended Ilsa's career, about fathers, though Freya gave that subject only its narrowest possible opening before closing it again like a door against weather.

"They're behind us," Ilsa said on the eighth night, nodding at the downstream dark. "Two days, maybe three. They've got a bigger boat and a bigger engine and they don't care what it costs them. We've got knowledge and a head start, and knowledge runs out of river eventually. Every channel narrows. Every river has one canyon it must go through, and they know that as well as we do."

"Then we don't sleep at the narrows."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "We don't sleep at the narrows."

* * *

On the ninth day the trees began to change.

It came on gradually, the way all important things do. First the spruces thickened and shortened, hunching against a wind that smelled of stone and old cold. Then the cottonwoods gave out entirely, and the banks turned to gray rubble, and the river itself changed color—from the tea-brown of the lowlands to a milky, opaque green-gray, the color of ground bone, the color of rock flour ground fine beneath moving ice.

Freya stood at the bow and forgot, for a full minute, to breathe.

The valley opened ahead of them into a vast U-shaped trough walled in gray stone, and at the far end of it, filling the sky the way a cathedral fills a village, stood the glacier. It was blue-white and bruise-blue in its crevasses, ten miles distant and looking close enough to walk to by lunchtime, and even at that distance she could hear it—a groan, deep and tectonic, rolling down the valley like a struck bell.

"That's the ice," Ilsa said quietly. She had come forward, and her face, usually so guarded, was open in a way Freya hadn't seen before. "Eleven years since I've seen it. I'd forgotten it makes you feel like an insect. In a good way. Mostly."

"It's beautiful," Freya said.

"It's a machine," Ilsa said. "And in three weeks it starts calving, and when it does, the river rises twenty feet in an afternoon and everything between here and that toe becomes a churning graveyard of house-sized ice. That's our deadline. That's why the Vosses are pushing. That's why everyone pushes." She turned and looked at Freya, and the openness closed again, but not entirely; some of it stayed, like light left in a room. "You feel that bell in your chest? Hold onto it. On the bad days coming—and there will be bad days—that's what we're working for."

That night they camped within sight of the ice, and Freya took first watch with the glacier glowing faintly under the stars like something alive and slow-breathing. She thought about her father drawing cairns. She thought about a man named Halloway dying in the mud with a promise wrung out of a stranger. She thought about the Vosses somewhere behind them in the dark, running their engine too hard, burning their fuel too fast, gaining.

Near midnight she heard it: far downstream, thin across the water, the unmistakable stutter of an outboard motor being pushed past its limits, then silence, then again.

She woke Ilsa without a word. Ilsa listened for thirty seconds, her head tilted, her expression unreadable.

"Narrows," she said. "They'll reach the canyon mouth by tomorrow noon. So will we." She looked up at Freya, and there was no rustiness in her voice now, only iron. "Get some sleep, Amberdale. Tomorrow we find out what kind of crew we are."

Far above them, the glacier groaned once, long and low, like something turning over in its sleep—and did not wake.
