## 5. What Halloway Buried

The trading post announced itself by smell before sight—mold, old tallow, and the particular sourness of a place where men had once traded whiskey for furs and lies for truth. Ilsa Brandt saw it first from the bend, because Ilsa Brandt saw everything first. That was the job she'd been good at, once, before the job had been taken from her and nailed to her name like a criminal's placard.

"Post ahead," she called back over her shoulder, poling the skiff through the shallows where the river braided itself into confusion. "Left bank. Stone foundation, so it won't have rotted through."

Freya looked up from the map in her lap. She'd been staring at it for two days the way a starving woman stares at a locked pantry—hungry, furious, unable to get in. "You know it?"

"I charted this stretch." Ilsa didn't elaborate. She never elaborated. Elaboration was how you gave people handles to grab you by. "There'll be a cache cellar under the floor. Traders always kept one. Cold, dry, hidden. If Halloway stashed anything on his way upriver, it'll be there."

"You think he stashed something?"

"I think a man running from something always leaves a trail of stashes behind him like a scared animal leaving droppings." Ilsa swung the bow toward the bank. "And I think a man who drew that map—" she nodded at the grease-stained paper, its hand-inked contour lines as familiar to her as her own handwriting, because some of them *were* her own handwriting, copied, altered, defaced—"wanted someone to find what he couldn't bring himself to say out loud."

Freya said nothing to that. She'd said very little all morning, and Ilsa had noticed. Noticing was the other thing she'd been good at, once.

* * *

The post had died maybe twenty years ago. Its roof had half-collapsed into the main room, and birch saplings grew up through the split floorboards, thin and pale as ghosts. A rusted stove. A counter with a ledger still on it, swollen to twice its size by damp, its pages fused into a single block of pulp. The names of every man who'd ever traded here were locked inside it now, unreadable. Ilsa found something grimly fitting in that.

"Cache cellar's under there." She pointed her pole at the counter. "Trapdoor's behind it. Help me move this."

Together they hauled the counter aside, and there it was: an iron ring set in a hatch, the wood around it dark with old wax where someone had sealed it against weather. Freya crouched, took the ring in both hands, and pulled. The hatch groaned up, exhaling cold, dry air that smelled of paper and pine resin.

Ilsa held the lantern down into the dark.

Three things lay in the cellar. A tin of hardtack gone to stone. A rifle wrapped in oilcloth, its stock cracked but its action still slick with preserved grease. And a canvas packet, tied with waxed cord, with a single word burned into the leather flap in a careful, deliberate hand:

**AMBERDALE.**

Ilsa heard Freya's breath stop. Heard it the way you hear a rope go taut just before it snaps.

"That's—" Freya's voice came out wrong, stripped down to wire. "That's my name."

"I know." Ilsa kept her voice level, her eyes on the packet. "Your father's name was on the expedition rolls. Elias Amberdale. The Amberdale Source Expedition." She'd read those rolls often enough, in the years after her disgrace, hunting for the truth of what had happened upriver, hunting for anything that might prove the disaster hadn't been her fault. "Twelve years ago. Nine men went up past the calving line. Four came back. Your father wasn't one of them."

Freya took the packet out of the cellar like it might detonate. She turned it over twice. "Why would Halloway have this? Why would he write my father's name on it?"

"Open it and find out."

"Ilsa—"

"You took a dying man's map and a dying man's promise. This is the rest of the price." Ilsa met her eyes and didn't look away. "Better you learn it here than at the source."

Freya's jaw worked. Then she cut the cord with her belt knife.

Inside the canvas: a journal, bound in oiled leather, its pages warped from years in the cellar but legible. And beneath it, a second document—typed, official, bearing the seal of the Corrow's Landing Provision Company. Ilsa recognized the letterhead. Everyone on this river recognized the letterhead. It had signed off on the survey that blamed her.

Freya opened the journal first. Her lips moved as she read, silent at first, then not.

* * *

*They paid me in March,* the journal began, in Halloway's cramped, sloping script. *Voss and Voss and the Company men behind them. They said the expedition was carrying out more than samples. They said Elias Amberdale had found something at the source that would make the Company rich and the men who owned the Company richer, and that a share was on the table for whoever guided their claim-jumpers up the back channel before the ice sealed.*

*I said no. I said it twice. The third time they said they'd take my claim, my boat, and my name, and I'd watch them do it from a debtor's cell. So I said yes. God forgive me, I said yes.*

*I led them up the moraine on the ninth of June. I told myself they'd only take the samples, the maps, the instruments. I told myself no one would get hurt. I was a liar even then.*

*There was a fight at the icefall. I don't know who drew first. I was already going back down by then. I heard the shot. I told myself it was a bear.*

*Four men came down the river that summer. Nine went up.*

Ilsa watched Freya read that line and go white to the lips. The girl sat down hard on the counter's edge, the journal trembling in her hands.

"Keep reading," Ilsa said quietly. "There's more."

*The Company buried it. Called it a survey error, a charting fault, an accident of ice. They needed someone to blame who wasn't them, and they picked the surveyor who'd charted the route. Brandt, her name was. Ilsa Brandt. They said her maps were wrong, that she'd led the expedition into the icefall's path. I knew her maps. I'd used them. They were the best on the river. I knew, and I said nothing, because saying something meant saying what I'd done.*

Ilsa closed her eyes.

Twelve years. Twelve years of river towns closing their doors, of licenses revoked, of men spitting when her name was said, of her own family declining to write back. She had carried that disgrace like ballast, and she had believed—she had *needed* to believe—that somewhere in it there was a fault of hers, some real error, because the alternative was that her whole life had been ruined for nothing. For another man's cowardice.

For a coward who'd hidden a journal in a cellar and run upriver for twelve years, drawing maps.

"Brandt," Freya said slowly. She looked up, and Ilsa watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—the same arithmetic, she'd wager, that Freya had been running since the day they met. "Your name is Brandt."

"It is."

"You were on the expedition rolls. You were the surveyor."

"I was the surveyor they blamed." Ilsa took the typed letter from the packet, unfolded it, and read what she'd already known she'd find. The Provision Company's internal finding. *Charting irregularities attributable to I. Brandt.* Her own professional ruin, notarized. "Halloway kept a copy. Proof, he called it, in his journal. Proof he was too much of a coward to use while it could still do me any good."

Freya was quiet for a long moment. The birch saplings stirred in the draft from the broken roof, pale leaves whispering.

"Why give me the map, then?" she said at last. "Why give it to me, of all people? If he knew who I was—"

"Read the last pages."

Freya read. Her voice dropped as she did, and Ilsa caught fragments: *I'm dying anyway. The river's in my lungs. The Vosses think I kept a copy of the source chart, and they're right, and they'll tear Corrow's Landing apart looking for it.* And then, lower: *I owe the Amberdales a debt I can't pay in coin. The girl—Elias talked about her. Talked about her the whole way up the river, the year I guided him. Said she had her mother's stubbornness and his own bad habit of never leaving a thing alone once she'd started it. If she's anything like he said, she'll finish what he started. She'll get there. And she'll do it right, which is more than I ever did.*

*I'm giving her the map because she's the only one left with a reason to go that isn't greed.*

Freya set the journal down with hands that weren't quite steady.

"He knew me," she said. "He knew me and I never—" She stopped. Started again. "I was eleven when he left. He wrote twice a year, then once a year, then not at all. I spent my whole life thinking he'd chosen the river over us. Over *me*." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Ilsa could see her hating it, could see her pressing the crack shut with sheer stubbornness. "And this man—this man sold him to the Company and then sat on the truth for twelve years, and his dying gift to me was a map."

"His dying gift to you was a confession," Ilsa said. "The map's just how he wrapped it." She took the journal, turned to the last written page, and held the lantern close. "There's one more thing. He wrote it small, like he didn't want even the cellar to hear it."

*The source isn't gold,* the final entry read. *Whatever the Vosses think, whatever the Company's men whispered in the saloons, there's no vein up there worth a war. What Elias found at the source was the camp itself. What he left behind. He didn't get lost, and he didn't die on the ice. He stayed. Read his letters if you find them, girl, and then decide whether to hate him. I've had twelve years to decide and I still don't know.*

Freya stood up so fast she knocked the lantern, and Ilsa caught it by the wire handle before it swung.

"He *stayed*," Freya said. "Halloway says he stayed."

"That's what it says."

"Then the map doesn't lead to treasure." Freya's eyes had gone bright and hard, the look of a woman standing at the edge of something with no bottom. "It leads to him. To where he is—or where he was. To *why*."

Outside, the river ran on, indifferent, and somewhere downstream—days downstream, but closer than yesterday, closer than this morning—a pair of brothers were rowing hard against the current with guns wrapped in oilcloth, following the same grease-stained route to the same sealed source, for a fortune that Ilsa now suspected had never existed at all.

"We should burn it," Freya said suddenly. "The map. The journal. Burn it all and go back down and—"

"You won't."

"No." Freya picked up the journal, wrapped it in the canvas, and tied it into her pack with the same deliberate care Halloway had used on the flap. "I won't." She shouldered the pack and looked at Ilsa, and there was something new in her face—not the desperate, cornered girl who'd bargained for passage two days ago, but someone with a direction now, and a debt of a very different kind. "The Vosses will have figured out by now that Halloway's map is real and moving upriver. They'll push day and night."

"Then we push harder." Ilsa was already moving toward the door, already calculating water, current, distance—the old work, the good work, the work they'd stolen from her and that she was about to take back with interest. "There's a channel above the narrows that isn't on any chart but mine. If the river's kind, we gain a day."

"And if it isn't?"

Ilsa paused at the broken doorway, looking out at the brown water and the pale country beyond, where the air already had a knife-edge of cold in it and the mountains had begun to show their white.

"Then we'll be the ones who know this river better than the men hunting us," she said. "And I intend to make that count for everything."

Behind them, in the dead post's cellar, the empty cache lay open to the light for the first time in twelve years—too late for the man who'd filled it, and exactly in time for the people he'd spent his whole coward's life running from.

They were on the water before the sun cleared the ridge.
