## 6. The Voss Brothers Close In

The canyon announced itself a mile before we reached it—a low, grinding hush in the air, like the river clearing its throat. Ilsa heard it first. She always did.

"Narrows," she said, and cut the engine to a drift. "Half a mile of rock on both sides and nowhere to beach. If someone wanted to take us, that's where they'd do it."

"Then we go around."

"There is no around." She said it the way she said most things—flat, final, like a stone dropped down a well. "The river goes through or you don't."

We sat with that for a while, the boat turning slow circles in an eddy, brown water folding over itself. Three days upstream from Corrow's Landing and I had learned to read her silences: this one meant she was thinking, and thinking meant trouble was coming, and trouble meant I should start being afraid now to save time later.

"Options," I said, because she liked lists even when she pretended not to.

"One. We run the narrows fast, lights off, and pray they're not watching. Two. We wait for dark and drift through quiet. Three—" she squinted at the sun sitting fat and orange above the western ridge, "—we're out of time for option two, so it's one or nothing."

"The Vosses don't know what our boat looks like."

"They know what *every* boat looks like. They grew up on this water, Freya. Before they were killers they were pilots—the best I ever saw, which is the part that galls me." She spat over the side, an old habit that seemed less like contempt than prayer. "Rurik steers like he was born holding a tiller. Marek reads water the way priests read scripture. And neither of them has ever once used that gift for anything but taking."

I thought about Halloway's face when I'd found him—or what remained of his face—and pushed the image down where it couldn't reach my hands. My hands needed to be steady. In three days Ilsa had taught me enough to matter: how to feather an oar, how to read the black tongue of deep current under white water, how to lean into a turn instead of fighting it. Enough to be useful. Not nearly enough to survive alone.

"Then we run it," I said. "Now, while there's light."

"Now while there's light," she agreed, and opened the throttle.

* * *

The narrows took us the way a fist takes a fly.

One moment we were skimming flat bronze water between walls of gray shale; the next the river dropped, gathered itself, and threw us at a standing wave taller than the gunwale. Ilsa shouted something—I'd never learn whether it was instruction or profanity—and hauled the bow around a boulder the size of a chapel. Water came aboard in cold tons. The map case, strapped to my chest under my coat, went slick against my ribs. I bailed with the tin pan while she fought the wheel, and for ninety seconds the world was noise and spray and the animal certainty that we were going to die in a place with no name.

Then the walls fell back, the river widened, and we were through—shaking, half-drowned, alive.

Isla actually laughed. One bark of it, startled out of her like a cough.

"Not bad," she said. "For a mud-flat salvage girl."

"For a disgraced surveyor, you're not—"

The bullet hit the water six feet off our stern.

I didn't hear the shot at first; I heard the *whip* of it, the small vicious sound of lead parting river. Then the crack rolled down the canyon after it, doubled and redoubled by the rock, impossible to place.

Ilsa didn't scream. She did worse. She went very calm, which I would learn was the truest measure of her fear.

"Marek," she said quietly. "Left bank, high ground. Rurik will be below us somewhere, cutting the corner." She looked at me, and her eyes were doing arithmetic I didn't want to see finished. "They let us run the rapids. They wanted us tired and wet before the shooting started."

Another round kicked up a fan of spray off the transom. Ahead, the river bent hard right around a bluff, and beyond the bend I could see open water stretching to a stand of black spruce—half a mile of exposure with no cover at all.

"We can't outrun bullets," I said.

"No." Her jaw worked. "But we can outrun *them*, if we give up the thing they want."

"The boat."

"The boat."

I looked at the little vessel that had carried us three days against current, that smelled of tar and Ilsa's pipe smoke, that had become—without my permission, without my noticing—the closest thing to home I'd had since my father walked upriver and never came back. Then I looked at the map case against my chest, and the promise stitched into its grease-stained folds, and the choice made itself the way stones make themselves into cairns: one on top of another until they stand.

"Beach her," I said. "And Ilsa—we swim for the far side. Cold as that water is, it's the only cover we've got."

She was already turning the wheel.

* * *

The river took my breath before it took my footing. Cold like a hand closing around my heart, squeezing once, hard, then letting go just enough to let me panic properly. Ilsa surfaced beside me, sputtering, one hand fisted in my collar, and together we clawed across thirty yards of current toward the gravel shoal on the north bank. Behind us I heard the boat grind onto the shingle, heard boots on stone, heard voices—two voices, rough and certain, calling to each other across the water the way brothers do.

We lay in the willow scrub at the top of the shoal, soaked to the marrow, shivering so hard our teeth chattered in counterpoint, and watched them take everything.

Marek Voss came down the bank first. He was shorter than I'd imagined, built like a barrel stave, with a beard going iron-gray and a rifle held the way other men hold tools they've owned all their lives. Rurik followed, tall and stooped, turning the boat's papers over in his fingers with something almost like tenderness.

"She's a good hull," Rurik said, and his voice carried clear across the water. "Brandt always kept a good hull. Waste of her, running cargo for strangers."

"They went in the drink," Marek called. "Current'll have them a mile down by now. Or the cold'll have them under. Either way."

"Either way," Rurik agreed, and I understood with a lurch that these were men who had said *either way* over graves before.

They searched the boat thoroughly and quickly. I watched Marek hold up Ilsa's brass transit—the surveying instrument, older than either of us, the last honest thing she owned—and weigh it in his palm, and then set it back down, because it was worth nothing to him and he hadn't the imagination to know what it was worth to her. Beside me, Ilsa made a sound like a bone cracking and said nothing at all.

They pushed off in our boat within the hour, running downstream-light toward the glacier country we'd spent three days bleeding to reach. From the high water they could be at the moraine in two days. On foot, through spruce bog and scree, we'd need four.

Unless.

"I know these banks," Ilsa whispered, when the sound of the engine had faded to nothing. "There's a game trail along the ridge. Old fire road, then goat track, then moraine. It's brutal, but it cuts the distance." She turned her head, cheek pressed to the frozen mud, and looked at me with an expression I'd never seen on her—not calculation, not contempt. Something rawer. "It means leaving the river. Everything I know stops mattering up there."

"You charted the glacier country too," I said. "You told me. Years ago."

"I charted it, and then they took my name off the charts." Her laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "So yes. I can find us a path no one believes exists. That's apparently what I'm for now."

Before I could answer, the willows behind us exploded.

* * *

He came out of the brush at a dead sprint—young, gangly, maybe nineteen, wearing a deckhand's oilskin two sizes too big and carrying a canvas bag that clanked like a church falling downstairs. He hit the gravel, slipped, caught himself, and kept coming straight at us, arms pumping, face white as the shoal stones.

"Don't shoot," he gasped, before either of us had touched a weapon. "Don't—please—I'm not with them, I'm *running* from them, please—"

Ilsa had a knife out from somewhere; I hadn't even known she carried one. But she didn't move, and after a moment the boy collapsed onto the shingle ten feet away, chest heaving, hands over his head like we were the ones with the rifle.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Tam." He got it out between breaths. "Tam Oduya. I was crew on the *Marguerite*—the mail packet out of Corrow's Landing. The Vosses chartered her two days back. Made me carry their gear, cook their food, keep my mouth shut." He lifted his head. There was a bruise blooming along his jaw, yellow-green, days old. "I heard them planning. Heard them say your names. When they stopped at the narrows to set up, I slipped over the rail and swam."

"You swam *that*," Ilsa said, nodding at the river, and there was grudging wonder in it.

"I grew up on it." He sat up slowly, testing whether we were going to hurt him. Up close he was all elbows and earnestness, with river-mud dried in his curls and eyes too old for his face. "Look—I know how this sounds. Stranger out of the bushes. But you need help and I need to not be on that boat when they figure out I'm gone, and—" he hesitated, and something flickered behind his eyes, some weight he wasn't showing us, "—and I owe somebody a debt too. Everybody up this river does. That's just how it works here."

Ilsa and I looked at each other. A whole conversation happened in the space of a second: *Can we trust him? Can we afford not to? What is he hiding?*

"Can you shoot?" she asked finally.

"Yes."

"Can you keep quiet?"

"When it matters."

"Can you walk four days through country that wants you dead?"

Tam looked up at the ridge line, at the black spruce climbing into cloud, at the distant white shoulder of the glacier catching the last light like a blade. He swallowed visibly. Then he grinned—a crooked, exhausted, entirely unguarded grin—and shouldered his clanking bag.

"I can walk anywhere," he said, "if it's away from those two."

* * *

We climbed out of the river valley as the light died, following a trail only Ilsa could see, three soaked strangers strung out single file through the spruce like beads on a wire. Tam set a pace that shamed us both, young legs eating the grade, and twice he doubled back to steady me over scree I couldn't navigate alone. Ilsa led, silent, compass in her fist, and if losing her boat still lived in her face she'd buried it deeper than any of us could dig.

At the first rest stop, sharing a strip of dried fish in the blue dark, Tam asked the question I'd been dreading.

"So what's up there? At the source? What's worth all this?"

I felt the map case against my ribs, warm now from my own body, and Halloway's voice rose in memory—*swear it, girl, swear it on whatever you've got left*—and I gave the only answer that was mine to give.

"A promise," I said. "We're carrying a promise upstream."

Tam nodded slowly, as if that were the most ordinary cargo in the world. Maybe up here it was.

We walked on. And somewhere behind us, far down the darkening river, two brothers ran our stolen boat toward the ice with murder in their pockets—while ahead, unseen under the stars, the glacier waited, groaning once in the night like something turning over in its sleep.
