## 7. The Calving Window

The river stopped being a river two days before we saw the ice.

It braided out into a dozen silver threads across a valley of gray stone, each one shallow enough to wade, none of them deep enough to carry the boat even if we'd still had the boat. Which we didn't. So we walked, the three of us, hauling everything we owned on our backs through a country that looked like the world had been built yesterday and abandoned this morning. Gravel bars as wide as roads. Driftwood bleached white as bone. And ahead, always ahead, filling half the sky now, the glacier.

I'd grown up on stories of the White Spine. Every deckhand in Corrow's Landing had. Old-timers swore you could hear it from twenty miles off—a sound like a giant grinding his teeth in his sleep—and I'd always figured that was drink talking. It wasn't. We heard it at dusk on the second day, a long groan rolling down the valley like a door swinging open in an empty house, and I watched Freya Amberdale stop dead in her tracks and go pale under her sunburn.

"That's just it settling," Ilsa Brandt said, not slowing. "Ice moves. That's what ice does."

"It sounded angry."

"It's a glacier, girl. It doesn't have moods." But I caught the way Ilsa's eyes went up the valley too, measuring, and stayed measured, and said nothing more about it.

I kept my mouth shut and my feet moving, which was getting to be a full-time occupation.

Here is the thing about me that nobody had figured out yet—not Ilsa with her surveyor's eye for reading people like she read water, not Freya with her stubborn, grieving, too-honest face: I was the reason the Voss brothers were still behind us. Not chasing blind. *Following.* Because every camp we made, every fork in the trail we took, I found a way to leave word. A strip of cloth tied to a willow. A cairn built one stone higher than it needed to be. Once, a boot print pressed deep into river mud where no boot needed to go. Small things. The kind of things only a man looking for them would ever see.

They had my sister.

Maren was sixteen and worked the fish tables at the cannery in Corrow's Landing, and when the Vosses came asking after me—because I'd run cargo for them once, one season, before I understood what they were—they didn't threaten me. They didn't have to. Erik Voss just bought Maren a hot meal, sat with her for an hour, and told her how much he admired her brother's knowledge of the upper river. Then he told her she should write to me more often. She should tell me about her winter cough. She should mention how hard it was getting to find work.

That was all. That was everything.

So I'd been feeding them our trail since the canyon, and every mile upstream the lie I was living got heavier, until it weighed more than the pack on my back, and I'd started catching myself thinking terrible arithmetic. *If they catch up on the glacier, maybe they'll take the map and let the women live. If they catch up below the calving fields, nobody lives. If I stop leaving signs, Maren pays. If I keep leaving signs, everyone pays.* There was no column in that ledger with a good number in it. I checked every night by firelight. Same answer every time.

* * *

We reached the toe of the glacier on the morning of the fourth day, and I finally understood why Halloway's map had been drawn in grease pencil instead of ink. Ink would have frozen. Everything past the moraine was written in a language of ice, and the map was less a set of directions than a warning dressed up as directions.

The glacier's snout rose out of its own rubble like the prow of a wrecked ship, a wall of blue-gray ice sixty feet high, streaked black with stone, weeping meltwater down its face in a hundred cold veins. And above it—the body of the thing, climbing away into the clouds, cracked and crevassed, groaning. All morning it had been groaning. Not settling. Groaning, the way a ship's timbers groan before they give.

"Calving season opens soon," Ilsa said. She stood with her hands on her hips, head tipped back, and I could see her doing surveyor's math against the sky. "A week, maybe less. When it goes, this whole valley floor becomes a lake with icebergs in it. Nothing moves up or down the source route until freeze-up."

"So we're close," Freya said.

"We're close, and we're late, and those are the same thing right now." Ilsa turned and pointed along the glacier's flank, where a ridge of rock debris—the moraine, a long dark spine of boulders and gravel the ice had been carrying for centuries—ran up toward a notch between two shoulders of the icefall. "Trail goes around the east side. Three days, exposed, and if the Vosses are behind us they'll see us the whole way like flies on a windowpane."

Freya followed her finger. "But?"

"But there's a cut through the moraine itself. Halloway marked it." She tapped the map, folded now to a grease-smudged square. "Half a day, maybe less. Sheltered. Steep. The old routes used it before the ice shifted."

"Before the ice shifted," Freya repeated. "How long ago?"

"Twenty years." Ilsa's jaw tightened. "I charted that shift myself. It's why they blamed me for the Meridian disaster—said my route notes sent a supply party onto unstable ground. They weren't wrong about the ground. They were wrong about whose fault it was." She said it flat, the way you say a thing you've said so many times it's worn smooth, but her knuckles were white around the map case. "The cut may be gone. Or it may be fine. Ice doesn't promise anything."

"The Vosses," Freya said quietly. "If we take the long way, they catch us in the open."

"If we take the short way, the mountain might catch us instead."

I stood there listening to them weigh two kinds of dying and said nothing, and hated myself for saying nothing, because here was the moment I could have spoken. Here was the moment I could have said *take the long way, please, take any way but the one I've been leading them to*—because oh, I knew which way the Vosses expected us to go. I'd left the sign at the last fork pointing them to the moraine cut. Erik Voss knew these approaches almost as well as Ilsa did; he'd prospected the toe for three seasons. If we went into the cut, we went into the place where two men with rifles could hold the walls like a throat.

I said nothing. I told myself it was because the cut really was the better route, because Ilsa wanted it, because arguing would raise questions. All true. None of it the truth.

We went into the cut.

* * *

The moraine was a country of the dead. Not people—stones. Boulders the size of cabins tumbled together with gravel and shattered rock, a ridge of wreckage the glacier had chewed off the mountainsides and carried down and dumped, and we climbed through it hand over hand, the ice wall rising sheer on our left, blue and ancient and sweating in the sun. Meltwater ran everywhere, underfoot, overfoot, filling the gaps between stones with small treacherous ponds. Twice I heard movement inside the glacier, deep cracks opening and closing like something breathing.

Freya climbed like she did everything—jaw set, furious at the mountain for being difficult. Ilsa moved like water finding its level, testing each hold before trusting it, and I scrambled along behind them both feeling the guilt climb faster than I did.

Then the mountain cleared its throat.

It wasn't loud, at first. A tick. A crack, sharp as a rifle shot, somewhere above and to the left. Ilsa froze with one hand on a boulder and tilted her head, and I watched thirty years of experience move across her face like weather.

"Off the ridge," she said. "Now. Down, into the lee of the big block—"

The second crack came before she finished, and then the whole slope above us moved.

Not an avalanche—I want to be clear about that, because I've heard people call it that since and it wasn't. It was the moraine adjusting. A section of the rubble ridge, maybe forty yards of it, deciding all at once that it had been resting at the wrong angle for a hundred years and correcting the matter. Stones the size of barrels came down the slope in a slow-motion flood, grinding, roaring, dust boiling up gold in the sunlight, and Ilsa shoved Freya behind a house-sized boulder and I dove in after them and the world became noise.

It lasted maybe a minute. It lasted a year. Rocks hammered down around our sheltering block, some striking it with blows I felt through the ground and up through my teeth. Dust filled the cut thick as smoke. And when it thinned, when the roar faded to ticking and settling, I crawled out into a landscape redrawn.

The route ahead was gone. Buried under ten feet of fresh rubble. The route back was worse—the slide had taken the lower section of the cut with it, and beyond that, far below through the dust, I could see the valley floor, and on the valley floor, tiny as ants, two figures with packs and rifles, standing at the mouth of the cut, looking up.

Erik Voss raised one hand. Not a wave. A salute. *We see you. We know exactly where you are.*

And I knew—with a certainty that hit me harder than any falling stone—that they hadn't followed us in because they'd known the cut was rotten. They'd known. They'd been waiting for the mountain to do their work for them, expecting to walk up afterward and pick through whatever was left.

Except we weren't left. We were alive, boxed in a slot of the moraine with the ice wall on one side and a field of fresh rubble on the other, and Ilsa was already studying the ice face with her surveyor's squint, already muttering about a shelf she could see fifty feet up, a ledge running along the glacier's flank toward the notch.

"There's a way," she said. "Up and along the ice margin. It'll be exposed. It'll be slow."

"Then we take it," Freya said.

"No." My voice came out wrong, cracked, and both of them turned, and I found I couldn't look at either of them, so I looked at the glacier instead, at its beautiful terrible blue. "No, wait. Before we climb another foot. You need to know something, and if you make me keep it one more hour I'm going to be sick."

"Tam—" Freya started.

"I've been leaving signs for the Vosses." The words fell out of me like rocks going down the slope, unstoppable once started. "Since the canyon. Since before the canyon. Cloth on the willows, cairns, prints in the mud. They know our route because I've been telling them. They have my sister, Maren, she's sixteen, and Erik Voss sat next to her at the cannery and smiled at her and told her to write to me about her cough, and I knew what it meant, everybody in Corrow's Landing knows what it means when the Vosses are friendly, and I thought—if I fed them the trail, they'd get the map and leave everyone breathing. That was the whole plan. That was the coward's whole plan." My voice broke and I let it. "And I pointed them at this cut. I signed them to this cut. Whatever they were waiting for down there, I led you to the mouth of it."

The silence after was the longest silence of my life. The glacier groaned overhead, indifferent, enormous.

Ilsa's face gave away nothing at all. She might have been reading a gauge. Freya stared at me, and I braced for the fury—for the shouting, for the fists, for being left right there on the moraine—and what I saw instead was worse. Recognition. She looked at me the way you look at a mirror.

"My father chose the treasure over coming home," she said slowly. "Or that's what I believed my whole life. I built everything on that belief. And I'd have done anything—*anything*—to protect the people I decided were mine, after that." She exhaled. "You lied to keep your sister alive. I'd have lied too. I'd have lied better."

"You can't—" I swallowed. "You can't just forgive me. I nearly killed us."

"I'm not forgiving you." Freya picked up her pack and slung it, and her eyes were hard and bright as the ice. "I'm hiring you. New terms: from here on, you tell us everything, the minute you know it, especially the parts that shame you. Especially those. And when we get off this mountain, you and I are going to Corrow's Landing together, and we are going to get your sister back from the Voss brothers if we have to burn their warehouse down to do it. Those are the terms. Take them or stay here."

Above us, the glacier boomed—a deep, resonant crack that rolled down the valley and came back as an echo, and far up the icefall, a serac the size of a church leaned, shivered, and held.

For now.

Ilsa was already climbing. "Terms accepted?" she called down, not turning around. "Good. Then move, boy. The season's opening early, and I don't intend to be standing on this ice when it does."
