## 8. The Source

The river ended the way all rivers end—not with a flourish but a whisper, thinning into meltwater channels that braided across a gravel plain and vanished beneath the glacier's toe.

Freya stood at the edge of that plain with her boots in freezing water and her breath hanging in front of her like something she'd caught and couldn't release. Above them, the ice rose in a wall of blue-green shadow two hundred feet high, streaked with the black veins of old rockfall, groaning in a language older than any town downstream. The sound was not loud. It was constant—a deep, tectonic mutter, as if the mountain were talking in its sleep.

"It's real," Tam said behind her. He said it the way people say *it's real* about things they'd only ever heard of in tavern stories. "The source. It's actually—"

"Don't jinx it," Ilsa said, though she was staring too, and her voice had gone soft in a way Freya had never heard from her. Ilsa Brandt, who cursed at rapids like they were personal enemies, who had once called the Voss brothers a waste of good ammunition—Ilsa looked at the glacier the way other people looked at churches.

They had come up the last three days on foot, after the moraine nearly swallowed them, after Tam's confession still sat between them like a stone none of them would pick up again but none of them could step over either. Their supplies were down to hardtack, a tin of rendered fat, and the last of the tea Ilsa rationed out like a miser. Freya's hands were cracked from cold. Her ribs ached where she'd fallen on the scree. She had never been so tired in her life, and she had never felt so awake.

Halloway's map hung in its oilcloth wrap against her chest. She took it out now, kneeling on the gravel, and spread it flat.

The grease stains had made some of the ink bloom, but the final markings were clear enough: a bend in the meltwater, a cairn drawn as a small stacked circle, and beyond it, tucked into a notch of exposed bedrock where the glacier's shoulder met the mountain, an X drawn in a hand that pressed so hard the paper had dented.

*Here,* the map said. *Here, damn you.*

"Stay close," Ilsa said. "Ice country has rules. You don't shout. You don't run. And you never—" she pointed at the seracs clinging to the glacier's flank like rotten teeth, "—stand under those."

They walked the toe of the ice for an hour, following the map's bend, and found the cairn exactly where Halloway had drawn it. Someone had built it well—flat stones fitted tight, the kind of work an archaeologist does without thinking, hands remembering what the mind forgets.

Freya's own hands were shaking before she touched the first stone.

She knew that stacking. She had grown up watching it, on digs she'd been dragged to as a child, sites in dry countries she could barely remember except for this: her father crouched over stones, fitting them, patient as water. Edmund Amberdale built cairns the way other men signed their names.

"He was here," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "Ilsa. He was actually here."

"We knew that," Ilsa said gently.

"No. We *hoped* that." She pulled the top stone free. Beneath it, wrapped in waxed canvas gone stiff with age, lay a bundle. Not gold. Not tools. Paper. Dozens of pages, tied with cord, and on the top page, in handwriting she had spent twenty years trying to forget and could recognize blind:

*For my child, when you find what I could not bring home.*

* * *

The letters were dated across eleven months. Freya sat on the gravel with the bundle open on her knees while the wind came off the ice and Tam built a small fire behind a windbreak of stones, because even at the roof of the world, Ilsa insisted, you make tea for the dead.

The first letter was steady, almost cheerful. *The valley is everything the old stories claimed. We've opened the outer chamber of the camp-site—the expedition before ours was right about the deposit, and wrong about everything else. There are artifacts here that will rewrite three chapters of the northern histories. I have never been happier in my work.*

The second letter, six weeks later, was shorter. *Two of the party want to strip the site and sell it piecemeal. I've refused. They've gone downriver to find buyers. I've begun moving the collection somewhere they won't think to look.*

The third letter changed everything. The handwriting had begun to slant, the way writing does when the hand is cold or tired or afraid.

*My child. If you are reading this, then someone carried my words upstream, which means someone kept a promise, which means there is decency left in the world, which I confess I had begun to doubt.*

*I stayed. Understand that first. No one abandoned me; no one trapped me. I stayed because if I leave, the site dies. Men like the Vosses do not take no for an answer—they take it as a starting price. As long as I am here, as long as the entrance stays sealed and unmarked, the collection sleeps safe. I have hidden it where only a surveyor's eye could find it again, and I have burned every chart but one.*

*One more thing, and I need you to hear it the way I mean it: I do not regret choosing this. I know what it cost you, growing up with a father who chose stones over supper tables. I know what your mother told you about me, and worse, I know how much of it was true. But belonging is not a thing you inherit, my love. It is a thing you pay for. I paid for mine here. I only wish I could have paid it closer to you.*

*If you are angry—and you should be, anger is honest—then be angry, and then look around you. Whoever brought you here did not do it for money alone. No one climbs this far for money alone. Keep them. That is my last instruction, and I have the authority of a dying man to give it: keep them.*

*Your father, who loved you badly and completely,*

*Edmund*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, though the words had stopped being words and become weather, moving through her.

Behind her, the fire crackled. Tam was pretending very hard to tend it. Ilsa stood at the edge of the windbreak, facing the ice, giving her the privacy of her back—which was, Freya understood dimly, the kindest thing anyone had done for her in years.

"He died believing I'd understand someday." She heard her own voice as if from far away. "He was right. God help me, he was right."

"That's not always a comfort," Ilsa said quietly. "Being right."

"No." Freya wiped her face with a sleeve already stiff with frost. "But it's not nothing."

She looked up at the glacier's shoulder, at the notch of bedrock where the X sat on the map, and understood the rest of it now—the sealed entrance, the vault her father had built from stone and stubbornness, the treasure that would erase every debt she owed in Corrow's Landing and buy her a life with nothing owed to anyone. It was a hundred yards away. Maybe less.

And it was a grave. Her father had made it one on purpose.

* * *

Tam saw them first.

He didn't shout—Ilsa's rules held even now—but he went still in the way animals go still, and Freya learned fast to read that stillness. She followed his eyes down the gravel plain, back along the route they'd climbed, and saw two figures coming up the meltwater channel. Small at this distance. Unhurried. They moved like men who owned the ground they walked on and knew no one would dispute the title.

The Voss brothers.

"They followed us up the moraine," Ilsa breathed. She was already moving, kicking dirt over the fire, sweeping their few belongings together with the economy of a woman who had fled things before. "How—"

"Tam's messages," Freya said, and hated herself for saying it, and watched the boy flinch, and watched him stand his ground anyway.

"I sent word about the canyon," Tam said. His jaw was set. "Not about this. They must have picked up our trail after the narrows. Or they had eyes on the glacier toe the whole time, waiting for the calving window to open." He looked at Freya, and there was no apology in his face anymore, only arithmetic. "There are two of them and three of us, but they have guns and we have tea."

"Then we talk," Freya said.

"Talk to the Vosses?"

"With guns," Ilsa corrected grimly, "you listen."

The brothers arrived the way bad weather arrives—no negotiation, just presence. Erik Voss was the taller, the quieter, the one who did the thinking; Marten walked half a step ahead because he liked the shape of his own shadow. Both rifles were out, both casual, which was its own kind of message.

"Well," Erik said, and his eyes went past Freya to the letter bundle in her lap, to the map's oilcloth wrap, to the notch of bedrock above. "The prodigal daughter finds her inheritance."

"You're standing on my father's grave," Freya said. She hadn't planned to say it. It simply came out, level and true, and she saw it land—saw even Marten shift his weight.

"Graves don't pay debts," Marten said. "Gold does. Open the vault, girl. You keep whatever sentimental paper you like. We'll take the heavy stuff and be gone before the ice starts singing."

"And if I refuse?"

Erik smiled. It was the worst thing Freya had seen in weeks, and she had seen the inside of a collapsing moraine. "Then we wait for the calving season to close, and everyone up here becomes a story. The glacier doesn't care whose side it takes." He raised his rifle slightly, almost politely, and nodded toward the bedrock notch. "Open it."

Freya got to her feet. Her legs ached. Her heart was doing something complicated and enormous in her chest, and she let it, because for the first time in her life she wasn't afraid of what she wanted.

Her father had sealed the site to protect it. He had died believing she would understand.

She understood.

"The vault opens for one person," she said, "and that person is me. Alone. Your guns can watch me walk up there, but if either of you follows past the cairn, I seal it behind me and we all freeze waiting for a door that won't open. My father built it better than your greed."

Erik studied her for a long moment, weighing her the way he'd weigh a rope—testing for the strand that would snap first.

"Go, then," he said softly. "Alone."

And as Freya turned toward the ice, letter bundle pressed to her chest, she caught Ilsa's eye—and saw the surveyor's gaze flick, quick as a fish, to the overhanging serac above the bedrock notch.

To the groaning blue mass of it.

To the crack running through it like a fuse.
