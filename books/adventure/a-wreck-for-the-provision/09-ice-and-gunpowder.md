## 9. Ice and Gunpowder

Ilsa Brandt had spent eleven years learning what ice sounded like before it killed you.

The groan came first—a low, wet moan rolling down the glacier's throat like a whale grieving somewhere under the world. Then the crack, sharp as a rifle shot, and the long rumbling after it, the sound of a mountain deciding to become water. She'd heard that symphony once before, on the survey that ended her career, and she had sworn she'd never stand beneath a calving face again.

Yet here she was, flat against the moraine with Tam's shoulder pressed to hers and Freya Amberdale crouched at the lip of the expedition camp like a woman reading scripture over an open grave.

"He's coming up the east lateral," Tam whispered. His voice shook. "Both of them. I can see their lanterns."

Below them, the lost camp sat in a bowl of blue ice the glacier had held for twenty years—tents collapsed into sailcloth ghosts, a cache of supply crates fused into the frozen ground, and at the center, half-embedded in ancient ice, a vault door of riveted iron that Halloway's map had promised led to gold. It did not lead to gold. It led to letters, to records, to the truth of what Aldous Amberdale had found and why he had chosen to die beside it. And to the Voss brothers, that distinction meant nothing. A treasure was a treasure, and men who had already killed for paper would not stop for iron.

"Freya." Ilsa kept her voice level, the way she'd learned to speak in avalanches—calm words for collapsing things. "Whatever you're thinking, think it faster."

Freya turned. In the lantern light her face was tear-streaked and terrible, grief burned down to something clean. "The serac," she said. "That wall above the camp. You said this morning it was rotten."

"I said it was rotten. I also said touching it wrong would kill everyone in this valley."

"I'm asking if it can be *aimed*."

Ilsa looked at the serac—the leaning cathedral of ice, forty feet of fractured blue hanging over the cache bowl like a blade held by a thread. She had charted this glacier at twenty-six years old, before the flood, before the inquiry, before a boardroom of men in clean coats decided that a junior surveyor made a convenient grave for their own negligence. She knew this ice better than the Vosses knew their own guns. Better than Halloway had known his guilt.

She knew exactly where its spine was broken.

"Yes," Ilsa heard herself say. "It can be aimed."

* * *

It took powder, and they had powder—Halloway's old blasting kit, sealed in a tin they'd hauled over the moraine because Freya refused to leave anything of his behind. Ilsa crawled out along the ice ridge with the charge wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and every step of the way she heard the glacier talking to her: the tick of expanding frost, the sigh of meltwater running through veins in the ice, the deep arterial pulse of a river being born underneath them all.

The Vosses were fifty yards below and closing. Their lanterns swung through the ruined tents. Erik Voss's voice carried up the bowl, rough with triumph: "Leave the girl. Take the map and the iron door. Whatever's behind it, we're owed."

Owed. Ilsa set the charge against the broken spine of the serac, tamped it with snow, and thought about how many men had told her what they were owed. The company that cut the survey budget. The board that needed a name for the disaster. The river that took three of her crew while she stood on the bank holding a rope that should have held.

She ran the fuse back along the ridge, thirty feet, forty, and crouched beside Freya in the shadow of a boulder.

"When it goes," Ilsa said, "the whole face comes down into the bowl. The camp, the vault, everything. There won't be time to dig it out before calving season opens and the river takes the rest downstream."

"I know." Freya's jaw was set. Her father had sealed this place to protect it. She was going to seal it deeper. "I know what I'm giving up."

"It's not nothing, what you're giving up. That vault could buy your debt ten times over."

Freya looked at her then, and there was something in her face Ilsa recognized from mirrors—the look of someone who has finally stopped paying interest on other people's crimes. "My father died believing I'd understand someday," she said. "I understand. Light it."

Ilsa struck the match.

* * *

The explosion was small. The answer was not.

The serac shrugged off its broken spine with a sound like the sky tearing, and forty feet of ancient ice leaned, hesitated—and fell. The whole calving face went with it, acres of glacier letting go at once, and the bowl below became a white apocalypse. Ilsa hauled Freya back behind the boulder as the blast wave hit, a fist of cold air and powdered ice that stole breath and sight and sound. The world was thunder. The world was white. Somewhere inside it, the Voss brothers' lanterns vanished, and the iron vault, and the tents, and twenty years of buried secrets all became one smooth plain of rubble that no map would ever find again.

Then the ringing silence, ears screaming, and Freya clawing at her arm, shouting words Ilsa couldn't hear yet, pointing down into the settling fog of ice dust.

Two lanterns. Still burning. Two figures crawling out of the debris field near the eastern edge, where the slide had split around a granite rib and spared them by blind arithmetic.

Erik Voss was dragging his brother. Lukas's leg was wrong, bent where it shouldn't bend, and the crevasse field below them—the same field Ilsa had charted a decade ago, the one she'd warned the company about in writing, the warning that got filed and forgotten—was opening in the warming dark between the survivors and solid ground.

"They'll die down there," Tam said. He didn't sound glad about it. He sounded seventeen and horrified, which was honest, at least.

"Their rope's gone in the slide," Ilsa said. She was already moving. She didn't remember deciding to move. Her body had simply understood something her mind hadn't caught up to: that these two men had hunted Freya across two hundred miles of river, had murdered Halloway in Corrow's Landing, had held a child hostage to bend Tam's spine—and that none of it changed what the crevasse field would do to them, or what she'd be if she watched it happen.

"Ilsa—" Freya caught her sleeve. "They came here to kill us."

"They came here for money." Ilsa pulled free. "There's a difference. Most people never learn it. I intend to keep knowing it."

She went down the ice with the rope coiled over her shoulder, driving her axe pick by pick, and the crevasse breathed cold up at her like a mouth. Erik saw her coming and fumbled for a pistol that wasn't there anymore—lost in the slide, or dropped, or simply surrendered by a man too broken to pretend. He stared at her with the expression of a man watching his own ghost walk toward him.

"You're the Brandt woman," he rasped. "The one who drowned the survey."

"The one they blamed," Ilsa said, and threw him the rope.

* * *

Getting Lukas Voss out of the crevasse field took everything the three of them had. Ilsa anchored the line around a granite horn; Freya belayed; Tam—small and quick and fearless in the way of people who have already survived worse—went down the last pitch on Ilsa's hip to fix a sling under Lukas's arms. They hauled together, four hands and a winch of rope and the glacier groaning overhead like a thing undecided, until Lukas lay gasping on safe ice with his shattered leg splinted from an axe handle and Freya's spare shirt.

Erik Voss knelt in the slush beside his brother and did not say thank you. Men like him rarely did. But he looked at Ilsa for a long moment, and whatever lived behind his eyes shifted—some load-bearing assumption giving way.

"Why?" was all he managed.

"Because eleven years ago I stood on a bank and watched three people die while men in clean coats explained to me why rescue wasn't in the budget." Ilsa coiled the rope with steady hands. "I've been paying that night back ever since, one way or another. Tonight it happens to be you."

Tam stood apart, working something in his fists. When he finally spoke, his voice cracked down the middle. "The letters. To the Vosses. About my sister." He swallowed. "I don't have to send them anymore, do I?"

Erik Voss looked at the boy—at the boy who had betrayed them, who had fed them false reports and slowed their pursuit, whose treachery had very nearly cost them everything—and then at his brother's ruined leg, and at the smooth white plain where a fortune used to be.

"No," Erik said quietly. "You don't have to send them anymore. There's nothing left to sell." He laughed then, a hollow, astonished sound, half grief. "The girl buried it. She had it in her hands and she *buried* it."

"She buried what it wasn't," Freya said. She came down the slope with her father's final letter folded against her heart, and her voice carried across the ice, clear as a bell over water. "What it was, we're carrying out."

Above them, the glacier gave another long, shuddering groan, and far upstream, at the calving face, a wall of ice the size of a church let go and crashed into the newborn river, and the season's first flood-wave came whispering down the channel toward them all.

Ilsa turned uphill and started running. "Move!" she shouted. "Water's coming—everyone to the high moraine—*now!*"

Behind her, Erik Voss lifted his brother onto his back, and followed.
