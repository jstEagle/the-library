## 10. Downstream, Together

The river gave Corrow's Landing back to them the way it gave everything back—slowly, in pieces, smelling of silt and rot and old wood.

Freya stood at the bow of Ilsa's patched boat as the town resolved out of the morning haze: the mud-flat shanties on their stilts, the salvage racks strung with dripping nets, the lender's office with its green shutters like a pair of narrowed eyes. Behind her, Tam worked the sweep oar with a rhythm he'd learned in six weeks of hard water, and Ilsa read the channel aloud—*sandbar shifting left, stay right of the deadfall*—in the flat, certain voice of a woman who had charted this river when Freya was still learning to walk.

Six weeks ago Freya had left this town running from two men with guns and a debt she couldn't count that high. She came back thinner, scarred across one forearm where the moraine had opened her, and carrying nothing of value except a letter folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth.

The river knew her now. That was worth something. She was almost sure it was.

"You're grinning," Tam said. "Stop it. It's unsettling."

"I'm not grinning."

"Your face is doing something."

"It's called *arriving*," Ilsa said from the stern, not looking up from the water. "Some people do it badly."

They came alongside the salvage racks just as the tide turned, and Freya stepped onto the mud flats of Corrow's Landing for the second time in her life as a different person—or the same person, finally finished being made.

* * *

Merrick Vane kept his office cold enough to store meat, which Freya had always suspected was deliberate. He sat behind his desk with his ledgers squared and his hands folded, and he listened to her proposal with the expression of a man watching a dog walk on its hind legs.

"You want me to *hire* you," he said.

"I want you to invest in us." Freya laid the chart on his desk—Ilsa's chart, redrawn from memory and corrected by six weeks of living water, every sandbar and snag and seasonal shift marked in Ilsa's precise hand. "You've been paying divers to work the shallows blind for eleven years. Half of what they pull up is junk because nobody down there can see. We know this river now. Not the way the charts know it—the way it actually is. The channels that move. The holes that don't. Where the current drops what's worth finding and where it just drops bodies."

"That last part sounds like a confession."

"It's a résumé."

Tam, beside her, made a small choking sound and turned it into a cough.

Merrick looked at the chart for a long time. His finger traced the upper reaches, past the last of his markers, into the country no salvage crew worked because the Voss brothers' territory had made it a graveyard. "The Vosses are done," he said. "You hear that yet? Older brother took a boat south before the ice closed. Younger one's sitting in the jail at Harrow Bend waiting on a magistrate. Whatever happened up at that glacier—" his eyes came up, sharp, "—nobody's said. Nobody's asked."

"The glacier happened," Freya said. "That's all anyone needs."

"And your father?"

She held his gaze. Merrick Vane was a vulture, but he was an honest vulture; he didn't poke at wounds without reason. "He stayed where he chose to stay," she said. "I brought back what he wanted brought back. That's the whole of it."

Merrick nodded slowly, and she understood that he would never ask again—not out of kindness, but because a debt collector learns early which accounts are settled and which ones collect *you*. He tapped the chart twice. "Wages against the debt. Sixty-forty split on finds until it's cleared, then fifty-fifty after. And Brandt—" he pointed his pen at Ilsa, "—your name goes on nothing. Not yet. You work under Amberdale's name until the magistrate finishes with the Kestrel inquiry. If it clears, we talk about a partnership. If it doesn't, you were never here."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. Freya watched her swallow the answer she wanted to give and hand over the one that would keep them fed. "Understood," Ilsa said.

When they were outside in the grey light, Tam exhaled hugely. "Sixty-forty. He insulted us."

"He offered us work," Ilsa said. "In this town, that's a love letter."

Freya laughed—actually laughed—and the sound surprised her, coming up from somewhere below the ribs, somewhere the fear had been living since Halloway crawled into her camp dying. It echoed off the stilt houses, and a woman mending nets on the nearest porch looked up, and Freya realized she knew her name. Sera. Sera with the twin boys who ran the mudflats at low tide. Sera who had once given her half a loaf when the debt collectors took her food stores.

"Sera," Freya called. "Is the north rack still leaking?"

Sera stared at her. Then, slowly, she smiled. "Been leaking three years, girl. You going to fix it?"

"We're going to fix everything," Tam said grandly, and immediately slipped in the mud, and Sera laughed, and the sound carried down the flats the way sound carries in small towns—fast, and hungry for good news.

* * *

They fixed the north rack. Then the middle one. By the end of the first month, people Freya had known only as creditors and obstacles were bringing her things: a cracked compass to mend, a question about the channel below Miller's Bend, a boy who wanted to learn to read water and had no father to teach him. Tam taught him knots instead, and invented stories about the glacier country that grew more heroic each telling, and Freya let him, because some truths deserved better armor than accuracy.

The debt went down. Slowly, the way rivers cut stone—but it went down.

On the first clear night of autumn, the three of them sat on the roof of Ilsa's boat with a jug of cider and the whole spilled sky above the flats. Tam had fallen asleep mid-sentence, as he did, his head against the mast. Ilsa smoked her pipe and studied the stars the way she studied everything, like a chart she intended to correct.

"Kestrel inquiry clears next month," she said. "Witness recanted. Turns out the man who blamed me for the wreck sold his testimony to the shipping concern years ago. There'll be a formal pardon." She said it evenly, but her hand wasn't steady on the pipe. "My name back. After nine years."

"What will you do with it?" Freya asked.

Ilsa was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence. "Chart the upper reaches," she said at last. "Properly. Past the canyon, past the moraine. Put the true water on paper so the next fool with a map doesn't die on lies." A pause. "Figured I'd need a crew that knows the river."

"Figured right," Freya said.

Above them, the stars did what stars do—nothing, magnificently—and Freya felt the strange weightless grief of a wound that has finally stopped hurting and leaves only the shape of itself behind. Her father had chosen the mountain over her. She had spent her whole life reading that choice as abandonment. Now she'd stood in his camp at the source of the world's water and read his letters, and understood: belonging isn't something you're given. It's something you pay for, in full, in advance, without any promise anyone will ever know what you spent. He had paid for strangers. For the site. For her, someday, if she ever came far enough upstream to understand.

She had come far enough.

* * *

They built the cairn on the point above the Landing, where the river bent and you could see a long way both directions—downstream toward the sea he'd never reached, upstream toward the ice that had kept him.

It took them three days. Stone by stone, hauled from the high bank, fitted the way Tam fitted knots and Ilsa fitted channels. Freya set the capstone herself and pressed her palm flat against it, feeling the day's warmth still stored in the rock, and thought: *this is what a grave is when there's no body. Just proof that someone stayed put long enough to grieve.*

A surprising number of people came. Sera and her boys. Old Wren from the chandlery. Even Merrick Vane, standing at the back with his hat off, saying nothing, which from Merrick was a eulogy. The boy Tam had taken on—Piet, all elbows and serious eyes—held the lantern steady as the light went blue and then gone.

Freya unfolded the letter. Its creases had worn through in places; she knew it by heart anyway, but she read it aloud, because her father had written it to be heard someday, and someday had arrived.

*"If you're reading this, then you came all the way up. I'm sorry for the distance, little bird—all of it, every mile, and most especially the miles I put between us on purpose. I told myself it was to protect the site. That was true. But it was also because I was a coward about the other thing, the harder thing: that staying meant choosing, and choosing means someone gets left holding the cost.*

*I hope whoever you became found people. Not blood—blood is a lottery. People. The kind who carry you through narrows you can't swim alone. If you have them, then I did one thing right, because stubbornness runs in our line, and stubborn people find each other.*

*Don't dig. Don't spend. Let the ice keep what it keeps. And when you go back down the river—go slow. It's the only direction that matters, and it's better traveled in company."*

Her voice held. Mostly.

When she finished, nobody spoke for a while. The river talked instead, patient and indifferent and, tonight, almost gentle. Then Sera's younger boy broke the silence by asking loudly whether the ghost was going to haunt the cairn or the boat, and Tam said "both, obviously, he seems thorough," and the laugh that went around the point was rough and wet-eyed and real.

Freya set the letter in a tin box and walled it into the cairn with a final stone. Her father's words, kept where he'd wanted them: at the bend of the river, in company.

Ilsa put a hand on her shoulder—brief, hard, the surveyor's version of an embrace. Tam leaned his head against hers. Downstream, the lamps of Corrow's Landing were coming on one by one, little fires against the dark, and upstream the mountains stood black and patient over the ice that kept its secrets.

"Go slow," Ilsa murmured, echoing the letter. "It's the only direction that matters."

"Then we go together," Freya said.

And downstream, far off where the river met the darkening sea, a light appeared that none of them had placed—a single lamp moving against the current, upstream, toward the Landing—too steady for driftwood, too late for any honest boat.

Tam saw it first. "Freya," he said quietly. "Who's coming up the river?"
