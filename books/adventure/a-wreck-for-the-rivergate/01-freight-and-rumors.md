## 1. Freight and Rumors

The Cessna lifted off the strip at Corben's Field at first light, when the mist still lay on the river like a sheet pulled up to the chin of the world. Hazel Dunmore flew with her window cracked an inch, the way she always did, so she could smell the water coming—the green rot-and-rain smell that meant the monsoon was close enough now to have a schedule.

She liked the hour before the world woke up for a simple reason: nobody wanted anything from you before dawn. No questions, no favors, no eyes. Just freight and sky and the long brown ribbon of the Rivergate unspooling below her, braided with channels that changed their minds every season.

In the seat behind her, lashed down with cargo netting, rode four hundred pounds of flour, kerosene, quinine, boot nails, and one crate marked FRAGILE that contained, according to the manifest, sewing machine parts, and contained in fact sewing machine parts, because Hazel had learned early that the only cargo worth smuggling was cargo nobody would pay you enough to smuggle. The syndicate's men didn't search a woman flying thread and bobbins. They searched for opium, for guns, for gold dust in tinned fruit labels. They did not search for sewing machine parts, which was fortunate, because that was all there was.

She checked her watch against the sun out of habit, then checked the compass on the dash out of something older than habit.

It was a brass compass, older than she was, its face gone cloudy as cataract glass, the needle swinging lazy and true. Her father's. It sat bolted to the panel where every pilot in the territory mounted theirs, and it looked like every other compass in every other cockpit from here to the coast, which was the point. Nobody asked about a compass. It was the one thing about Hazel Dunmore that had never once been asked about, and she intended to keep it that way.

Below, the river split around a drowned island where a heron stood on one leg like a man waiting for a ferry that wasn't coming. She banked gently—gently, always gently; the old girl's airframe had opinions about enthusiasm—and set her heading for Rivergate Landing.

* * *

Rivergate Landing was not much: a gravel strip that flooded knee-deep twice a year, a fuel drum farm rusting into orange topiary, a trading post with a porch sagging like a tired horse, and the river itself, wide and slow and brown as tea left standing. There were maybe sixty people who lived there full-time and another two hundred who passed through claiming they'd leave by the dry season, none of whom ever did.

Hazel had been flying into it for three years, and in three years she had made herself exactly memorable enough to be trusted and exactly forgettable enough to be left alone. It was a narrow window and she kept it polished. She remembered names—every name, every kid's name, every dog's name—and never let anyone remember more about her than that she showed up when she said she would. She paid cash. She drank one beer at the post and never two. When people asked where she'd learned to fly, she said "around," and smiled the smile that ended conversations politely, and after a while people stopped asking, and that was the finest gift the territory ever gave her: the collective agreement not to look too closely.

"Morning, Miss Dunmore." Old Tam at the fuel pump, who had one eye and used the other one exclusively for judging landings. "That was smooth."

"They're all smooth until they aren't, Tam."

"That's why I only compliment the ones I see." He cranked the hose around. "You heard the news?"

"I've been airborne since dark-thirty. I don't hear news, I make engine noise."

He grinned through his beard, gray-shot and smelling faintly of kerosene, and began pumping. "Concession office posted notice yesterday. Permits expire end of monsoon. All of them. Every claim staked under the old concession lapses unless it's registered and worked by the last day of the rains."

Hazel kept her hands busy coiling the tie-down rope, which was already coiled, which was the trick of it—keep your hands doing something and your face does nothing at all. "How long is that?"

"Six weeks. Maybe five, if the weather comes up from the coast mean this year." Tam spat thoughtfully over the drum. "Half these prospectors are going to lose everything because they can't get downriver to file. Other half's going to find out their claims got jumped years ago and the paper was never worth spit."

"Cheerful morning, Tam."

"I'm a cheerful man in a cheerful economy." He waved the nozzle dry. "You want coffee? Marta's got some on that isn't entirely bark."

"Coffee," Hazel said, "that isn't entirely bark. Yes. That's the finest offer anyone's made me all week."

* * *

The trading post smelled of canvas, tobacco, dried fish, and the particular sweet-rot sweetness of sugar stored too near the river in a wet season. Marta Okoye ran it with her sister and a shotgun she'd never once needed but kept oiled anyway, on principle. She poured Hazel a cup that was, as promised, forty percent bark, and Hazel drank it leaning against the counter while the morning trade came in.

This was the part of the job she actually loved, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it. The talk. The river talk. Who'd run what rapids, whose boat had sunk, who'd found color in a pan up the Sable Fork, who'd married, who'd died, who'd been seen drinking with whose wife. In the city, information cost money. Out here it cost nothing but patience, and Hazel was the most patient woman on the river, mostly because patience was cheaper than attention and she could not afford attention.

"—telling you, Vann swears it's real," said a dredge hand named Colley, hunched over his cup at the far table. "Says he's had assay tags since before the war. Says the vein runs clean as a hymn."

"Says," repeated the man across from him, whose name Hazel didn't know and therefore filed away for later. "Elias Vann says a lot of things. Man's been chasing that ghost claim ten years. Syndicate's got men crawling over every square mile of that country and Vann thinks he's found what they missed?"

"He thinks they found it first and buried it. That's the difference."

The second man laughed, short and ugly. "And that's why Vann drinks alone."

Hazel sipped her bark coffee and looked out the window at her plane, and did not appear to be listening, which was a skill she'd practiced the way other women practiced piano.

Elias Vann. She knew the name the way everyone on the upper river knew it—a prospector gone half to moss, living up past the Alder Flats, coming down twice a year to argue with the registry clerk and buy lamp oil. She'd flown mail over his camp once and seen the smoke curling up through the trees, thin and stubborn, like the man himself, if the stories were anything.

Permits expire at monsoon's end. Six weeks. Five, if the weather came up mean.

Something turned over in her chest—not interest, she told herself, nothing so dangerous as interest. Just the old restlessness, the feeling she got sometimes, usually in the hour before dawn or the hour after a lie, that her careful little life was a boat riding a flood, and floods don't care how well you've tied your lines.

"You're quiet today, Miss Dunmore," Marta said, wiping the counter.

"I'm always quiet."

"Quieter." Marta leaned in, voice dropping under the argument at the far table. "You hear about the syndicate men? Two of them came through Tuesday asking after flight manifests. Not customs men. Not police. Asking who flies what, and when, and for whom." She refilled Hazel's cup without being asked, which among river people was a form of speech. "I told them you fly flour and medicine and mind your own business, which is more courtesy than they showed me."

Hazel felt the cold arrive in her stomach, punctual as a train. "What did they look like?"

"One big, one small. Big one did the talking. Polite as a Sunday sermon and about as warm. Wore his hat indoors." Marta shrugged. "I've seen the type before. They go away eventually. They always go away eventually."

They always went away eventually. And they always came back eventually, too—that was the arithmetic Hazel had learned at nineteen, at her father's funeral, when a man in an indoor hat had pressed an envelope of figures into her gloved hand and said, *your father owed us, miss, and debts pass to family like blood*, and she had been paying it ever since, in flights and silence and the slow sale of everything her father hadn't already sold.

Three more payments. Three more payments and the debt was dead and she could be anybody, anywhere. That was the deal she'd struck, the light at the end of six years of tunnel. Fly the syndicate's quiet cargo, ask no questions, keep no records, and count down.

She just had to stay invisible for three more payments.

"Miss Dunmore?" Tam's head appeared in the doorway. "Fellow out here asking if you do medevac work. Says there's a man hurt up the Alder Flats."

The cold in her stomach shifted, became something else. "Who's hurt?"

"Fella wouldn't say. Says the trading post up there radioed the mission station and the mission's floatplane is down with a cracked float. Says whoever's available, name their price."

Hazel looked at her coffee. Looked at the sky, which was building high white towers over the western hills, the monsoon's advance scouts. Looked at the compass on her dash through the window, needle steady, pointing home, wherever home was, a question she'd stopped asking herself years ago.

Every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her alive, invisible, and solvent for six years—said: *let someone else fly it. You've got cargo schedules. You've got a countdown. Don't take strangers into your cockpit.*

But the Alder Flats was eight hours' walk from any doctor, and the mission plane was down, and she was available, and somewhere underneath all the careful forgettable layers there was still the girl who'd learned to fly because her father told her the sky was the only place a person couldn't be cornered.

"Name my price," she said, setting down the cup. "Where is he?"

Tam scratched under his hat. "Like I said. Fella wouldn't say much. But he mentioned the name Vann."

* * *

She prepped the plane in twenty minutes, stripping the sewing machine crate and lashing in the stretcher frame she kept folded under the spare fuel bladder for exactly this kind of errand, the kind she took maybe twice a year and never charged full price for, because charity was also a kind of camouflage—nobody suspects the woman who flies dying men out of the bush for free.

The messenger was a lean, river-dark man named Ansel who talked with his hands and gave her what she needed in fragments: Elias Vann, gut-shot, camp burned, found by a trapper at dawn, alive as of an hour ago, fading. The trapper had done what trappers do—pressure, whiskey, prayer—and none of it was going to hold.

"Who shot him?" Hazel asked, checking her fuel.

Ansel spread his hands. "Trapper says Vann wouldn't say. Kept saying—" Ansel frowned, hunting the words. "Kept saying *they'll want the map*. Over and over. Then he'd go quiet again."

The word landed in her like a stone dropped down a well. She waited to hear it hit bottom and it never quite did.

"Well," she said lightly, climbing into the cockpit, "then we'd best get there before whoever they are decides to come back and check their work."

Ansel climbed in behind her, and Hazel ran up the engine, and the propeller washed the morning apart, and the Cessna rolled down the gravel and lifted into a sky that was beginning, very quietly, to fill with weather.

On the dash, the brass compass held its bearing north-northwest, toward the Alder Flats, toward a burned camp and a dying man and a map that somebody wanted badly enough to kill for.

Hazel told herself she was just flying a medevac. She was good at telling herself things. She'd had six years of practice.

But her hands were not quite steady on the yoke, and the restlessness under her calm had found, at last, a direction to run.
