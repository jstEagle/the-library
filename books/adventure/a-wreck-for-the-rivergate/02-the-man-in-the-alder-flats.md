## 2. The Man in the Alder Flats

The call came in on the radio at 6:40 in the morning, while the mist was still peeling off the Rivergate like wet gauze.

"Hazel Dunmore." The voice belonged to Marta Reyes at the trading post relay, and it had the flat, clipped tone of someone reading from a message she didn't fully understand. "We've got a smoke signal off the Alder Flats. Two fires, close together. That's a distress pattern."

I was halfway through my coffee, standing barefoot on the dock with the *Wren* tied alongside, her cargo netting still half-loaded from yesterday's run. Two fires meant trouble. One fire meant breakfast; two meant somebody wanted a plane.

"Anyone know who's camped out there?"

"Nobody's said. Old trapper country. You want me to pass it to the syndicate air service?"

The syndicate air service. Right. Corliss Hale's people didn't fly medevacs into the flats unless there was ore at the end of them, and even then they'd bill the corpse for fuel. I looked at the sky — high overcast, burning off by nine, wind out of the southwest at nothing worth naming. Good flying weather. Bad day to be anyone lying hurt in the alders.

"I'll take it," I said. "Log it as a supply diversion. I'll bill the district office."

"You always do this," Marta said, not unkindly.

"Do what?"

"Fly toward the smoke."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I drank my coffee instead, and twenty minutes later the *Wren* was climbing out of the river valley with her floats still dripping, headed northwest into country where the maps went thin and the rivers braided out like unraveling rope.

* * *

The Alder Flats earned their name honestly. Miles of grey-green scrub running down to a slow brown slough, the kind of ground that swallowed boots to the ankle and planes to the axle if you guessed wrong about where the firm spots were. From eight hundred feet it all looked the same — alder, sedge, standing water, more alder — until I saw the smoke.

Two columns, thin now, almost out. They rose from a clearing near the slough's edge, and as I swung wide to look at the wind sock situation — which is to say, the absence of one — I saw what was left of a camp.

It had been burned deliberately. That was my first thought, and it was wrong, or half-wrong, or right in a way I didn't yet understand. Tents gone to black skeletons. A cache stand collapsed, its contents scattered and scorched. A canoe pulled up on the bank, hull cracked open like a dropped egg. And beside the ashes of what must have been his cook fire, a man lay on his back in the mud with one arm raised, waving something white that might once have been a shirt.

He wasn't moving much. Just the arm. Just enough.

I circled twice, looking for water deep enough to set down. The slough was my best bet — maybe three feet over the sandbar at its mouth, glassy, no snags visible. Not good. Not good at all. But the man on the ground had stopped waving, and when you've flown medicine into the interior long enough, you learn the difference between someone conserving strength and someone who has run out of it entirely.

I put the *Wren* down on the slough with more faith than skill, taxied up onto the bar, and killed the engine.

The silence afterward was enormous. Water lapping at the floats. A loon somewhere, laughing at me. My own breathing, too loud inside the cockpit.

"Hello the camp!" I called, because that's what you do, even when there's no camp left to answer.

The arm moved again.

I waded ashore with the medical kit over my shoulder and my boots filling immediately, because of course they did, and crossed fifty yards of sedge to where he lay.

He was older than I expected. Sixties, maybe, though out here age is a guess you make from hands and eyes rather than faces. Grey beard matted with blood and mud. Canvas coat burned across the chest, the skin beneath blistered and weeping. He'd dragged himself, I realized — there was a furrow in the mud behind him, six feet long, from the edge of the burned tent to where he lay in the open, where a plane could see him.

And then I saw why he'd needed dragging. The front of his trousers was soaked through dark, and there was a wound low in his belly, neat and terrible, the kind of hole that doesn't bleed fast so much as it bleeds *forever*.

"Gut-shot," I said, mostly to myself, kneeling in the mud.

"Took me two days to dig the slug out," he said. His voice was a dry whisper, but it worked. "Didn't get it all. I could feel it. Still can." His eyes found mine, pale blue and terribly clear. "You the mail plane?"

"I'm whatever plane you need."

"Then you're late."

Despite everything — despite the smell of burned canvas and the wound and the cold dread already settling into my stomach — I almost laughed. "Hazel Dunmore," I said. "Who am I talking to?"

"Elias Vann." He said it like a name should mean something, and then, seeing my face stay politely blank, added: "Prospector. Twenty-two years on the Sable Fork." A pause. "You never heard of me either."

"I'm sorry. I don't—"

"No. Nobody has. That's rather the point." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for gripping my wrist with a hand that felt like a bundle of dry sticks. "Listen to me. There were four men here two nights ago. Syndicate men. They burned my camp looking for something and when they didn't find it, one of them shot me and they left me for dead. They'll be back or they won't, but either way, girl, you need to get me out of here before I stop being interesting."

Everything in me said *no*. Every instinct I'd honed over five years of keeping my head down and my name off lists said: this is not your problem, this man is a magnet for exactly the kind of attention you crossed half the territory to escape, fly him to the mission hospital at Carrow Bend, log it, forget it.

But his hand was still on my wrist, and under the dirt his pulse was fluttering like a bird in a sack, and he was looking at me with those clear pale eyes as if I were the last solid thing in a world gone to water.

"Can you walk?" I asked. Fool question. We both knew it.

"I can be *carried*," he said, "if you're strong enough. Are you strong enough, Hazel Dunmore?"

I looked back at the *Wren*, sitting pretty on the sandbar, and did the arithmetic. Him: maybe a hundred and sixty pounds, most of it dead weight. Me: five foot six and stronger than I look, but not that strong. The distance: fifty yards of sucking mud.

"No," I said. "But I've got a stretcher frame and a cargo net, and I've hauled worse than you."

"Have you?" Something flickered in his face — curiosity, or amusement, or just the effort of staying conscious. "What was worse?"

"A piano," I said. "Don't ask."

* * *

Getting him aboard took forty minutes and cost me a bruised hip, a torn sleeve, and most of my remaining respect for the structural integrity of canvas stretchers. I rigged the net between two alders, rolled him onto it with a technique I will not describe because it involved more swearing than skill, and then walked him to the plane in a kind of grim pendulum swing, ten feet at a time, resting, swinging again. Twice he passed out, and both times I stood there in the mud holding a dying man in a cargo net and seriously considered leaving him on the bank until he woke up, because my arms were shaking and the tide was coming into the slough and every minute the *Wren* sat on that bar she was settling deeper.

Both times, he woke up on his own. Both times, the first thing he said was some variation of *keep going*, delivered in that dry whisper, as if fainting were merely an inconvenience he'd scheduled around.

By the time I got him lashed into the rear cabin — on top of a crate of tinned milk, because that's what was there — the water was lapping at the float struts and my hands wouldn't stop trembling. I climbed into the pilot seat, started the engine, and let it idle while I checked him one more time. Pulse thready. Skin grey and clammy. The dressing I'd packed into the wound was already spotting red.

"Elias." I turned in my seat. "I'm going to fly you to Carrow Bend. Mission hospital. Father Anselm's got a surgical table and morphine, and it's forty minutes away. You understand? Forty minutes."

His eyes opened. For a moment they focused on me with an intensity that made me sit very still.

"Carrow Bend keeps records," he said.

"So does the morgue."

That got a ghost of a smile. "Fair." Then his gaze drifted past me, out the window, toward the burned camp sliding away behind us, and his voice changed — dropped lower, went somewhere private. "Twenty-two years I worked that fork. Everyone told me the vein played out. Everyone told me Vann was chasing ghosts. And it's there, girl. It's *there*. I had it in my hand."

"The syndicate men. They were looking for proof?"

"They were looking for my map." His hand crawled across the crate, found my sleeve, gripped. "They didn't find it. Do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because I'm not a fool." He coughed, and the cough bent him double, and for a horrible moment I thought that was the end of it, right there on the tinned milk. When he straightened, there was fresh blood on his lips and his whisper had gone thinner. "Fly me to your cabin first. Not Carrow Bend. Your cabin."

"That's insane. You need surgery. You need it *now*—"

"I need ten minutes and a lockbox," he said. "Then Carrow Bend, or the bottom of the river, whichever comes first. Ten minutes, Hazel Dunmore. I've waited twenty-two years. I can wait ten minutes."

I looked at him for a long moment. The engine idled beneath us, patient, indifferent. Somewhere behind us the Alder Flats smoked quietly, erasing itself.

Every self-preserving bone in my body voted against what I did next.

I pushed the throttle forward.

* * *

My cabin sits on a bluff above the river, thirty miles downstream of the flats, with a lean-to hangar and a graveled strip I cut myself the first summer, when I still believed in doing things properly. I brought the *Wren* in light — lighter than I should have, with a passenger bleeding in the back — touched down hard, and taxied up to the dock with the sun barely clearing the ridgeline.

Moving him from plane to cabin was easier than the mud, at least. I had a wheelbarrow. It's not dignified, wheelbarrowing a dying prospector up your path, but dignity died somewhere back in the alders, along with my better judgment.

I laid him on my bed, which meant I'd be sleeping on the floor tonight, which meant I'd already decided something I hadn't admitted to deciding. I got water heating, got clean cloth, got the bottle of laudanum I keep for emergencies and hate myself for owning.

While I worked, he talked. Not fever-talk — he was too clear for that, too deliberate. He talked like a man dictating a will.

"The claim's on the Sable Fork. Headwaters side, above the Devil's Teeth rapids. Quartz stringer in a greenstone wall — you could see gold in it with the naked eye, like flies in amber. I staked it legal, eleven years ago. Filed the paperwork. And do you know what happened?" He didn't wait for my answer. "The syndicate filed first. Or claimed they had. Their clerk in the registry office lost my filing — *lost* it, imagine that — and their permit came through on top of mine, and when I protested, a man came out to my camp and explained the economics of the situation to me. With a rifle barrel against my teeth."

I was cutting away the burned canvas from his chest, and my hands had gone very steady, the way they do when the rest of me is screaming. "Elias, I'm not a lawyer. I'm not an advocate. I fly freight."

"I know what you are." His eyes closed. Opened again. "You're the woman who flew toward the smoke."

"That's not a profession."

"It's rarer than you think." He caught his breath, and the laudanum was starting to work on him now, softening the edges of his voice. "Under the floorboards. Under the bed. There's an oilcloth packet. Don't lose it. Don't show it to anyone. And when I'm—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "When I'm done, you'll know what to do with it. I'll tell you. I just need to sleep first. Just a little sleep."

"You're not sleeping yet. Drink this." I got broth into him, and water, and another careful dose of the laudanum, and repacked the abdominal dressing while he gritted his teeth and refused to cry out, which somehow was worse than if he had.

Toward evening he drifted, and I sat in the chair by the bed sharpening my knife I didn't need to sharpen, listening to his breathing rasp and counting the intervals between it. Outside, the monsoon clouds were building up over the western ridges, fat and purple, the first real ones of the season. Another month of rain coming. Maybe less.

Near midnight he surfaced long enough to grip my hand.

"Mara," he said.

"Who?"

"My daughter." His eyes were wet, whether from pain or something else I couldn't tell. "She'll be twenty-six this year. She thinks I'm dead. Or worse — she thinks I chose the fork over her, which amounts to the same thing and costs more." His grip tightened, painfully. "Remember the name, Hazel Dunmore. Whatever happens. Remember it."

"I remember everything," I said, which was a lie, because forgetting was my profession, my art, the wall I'd built brick by brick around the person I used to be. "Sleep now."

He slept. I sat up in the chair with the lamp turned low, watching the rain start against the window, and listened to a stranger breathe in my bed, and understood with perfect clarity that my quiet life — the small runs, the forgettable name, the careful gray existence I'd built like a dam against my own history — had just developed a crack straight down the middle.

Because I'd seen the way he said *they burned my camp*. I'd heard the arithmetic underneath: four men, one old man, a slug left in the wound on purpose or through carelessness, nobody ever charged, nobody ever will. And I'd recognized the shape of it, because I knew that shape from the inside. I'd spent five years flying away from a version of that same story with my father's name written across it.

Toward dawn, his breathing changed.

I was on my feet before I was awake, lamp lit, hand on his shoulder. His eyes opened and fixed on me with sudden, urgent lucidity — the last flare of a lamp running dry.

"Floorboards," he whispered. "Oilcloth. And the promise, girl. Swear it. Swear you'll register the claim before the permits lapse — monsoon's end, no later. In Mara's name. Mara Vann. Swear it."

The rain hammered the roof. The lamp flame leaned and steadied.

"I swear," I said.

Something in his face let go — not peace exactly, but the next thing to it. His hand relaxed in mine. And outside, far off down the river, so faint I might have imagined it, an engine droned through the rain, moving upstream, moving slowly, moving like a man searching the banks for something he'd lost.

Or someone.
