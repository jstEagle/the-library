## 3. A Promise for a Map

The engine sound faded upstream, swallowed by rain, and I sat there a long moment with Elias Vann's hand going slack in mine, listening to the dark the way you listen when you know something is coming and you don't yet know what.

"Who's out there?" I asked him.

His eyes opened — not all the way, just enough to find me by lamplight. He'd spent something in the swearing. I could see it draining out of him, the last reserves of a man who had held himself together with pure spite for two days, and now that the spite had a purpose, his body was filing its resignation.

"Doesn't matter who," he rasped. "Matters what they want."

"And what do they want?"

He almost smiled. It was a terrible thing, that almost-smile, half blood and half amusement. "Same thing everybody wants, girl. What isn't theirs."

I got him a sip of water, one-handed, because he wouldn't let go of my other hand, and I didn't have the heart — or the sense — to pull it free. The water ran out of the corner of his mouth and onto the pillow. I tipped the cup back before he drowned on it.

"The map," I said. "You said oilcloth. Under the floorboards."

"Under the stove. Third board. There's a nail missing." His breath caught, held, released. "You'll want to look at it before you swear to anything else. I'm not a man who sells a pig in a sack."

"You're not selling me anything. You're dying."

"Dying's a transaction too." He coughed, wet and long, and I held the basin for him and tried not to look at what came up. "Everything gets traded, Hazel Dunmore. Time for money. Money for silence. Silence for a grave." He settled back against the pillows I'd made from a folded blanket. "I've traded all my life. This is the last trade I'll ever make, and I mean to make it clean."

I should have told him then that I was the wrong woman for clean trades. That my whole adult life had been built on the opposite principle — keep your head down, keep your ledger light, keep your name off every list that mattered. That the only inheritance I had was a debt and a compass I couldn't explain to a customs man without lying, and that I had gotten very, very good at lying.

Instead I said, "I'll get the map."

* * *

The floorboard came up easier than I expected, which told me it had been pried before. Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age and soft with damp, was a package the size of a hymnal, tied with waxed twine. I brought it to the bed and set it on the blanket, and Elias looked at it the way a man looks at a child he sent away twenty years ago and never wrote to.

"Open it."

The twine was knotted in a way that fought me, and when I finally got it loose the oilcloth peeled back in layers — three of them, each one folded with a care that said more about the man than anything he'd said out loud. Inside was a map. Hand-drawn on what looked like a surveyor's linen, the ink browned but the lines still sharp as argument. The Sable Fork ran down its length in a confident blue stroke, annotated in a cramped, patient hand: depths, channels, a drowned snag marked with a small skull, a back channel behind a place called Miller's Rise labeled *high water only, trust the herons not the charts*. And there, up where the fork split into fingers, a cairn drawn in cross-section, like an architect's plan, with coordinates written beside it in a code I didn't recognize — numbers and letters that repeated in groups of four.

"That's the cipher," Elias said, watching me. "Key's the assay tags. You'll know them when you see them. I stamped them myself." His voice was fraying at the edges now, a rope under too much load. "The claim's real, Hazel. I know what they say about me in the flats. Crazy Vann. Old Vann and his fairy gold. I let them say it. Let them laugh. A man laughing at is a man not following."

I turned the map over. On the back, in the same cramped hand, was a column of dates and names — filing dates, registry numbers, each one crossed out, each one followed by a single word in progressively angrier script: *rejected. rejected. withdrawn — pressure. rejected.*

"They wouldn't even hear it," Elias said quietly. "Twice I filed legitimate. Twice the paperwork came back with holes in it you could fly a plane through. Third time the clerk wouldn't even take my fee. You know why? Because the syndicate's assay men had already walked the ground. They know what's up there. They've known for six years. That's why my camp burned, girl. Not because I found it. Because I *kept* it."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the door seam and steadied again. Outside the rain kept up its work on the roof, patient as erosion.

"And Mara?" I said. "Why her name? Why not file it yourself, if you make it back?"

Something moved across his face — I want to say it was shame, but it was older than shame, the kind of weathered grief that stops looking like grief and starts looking like geography.

"I made my choices a long time ago, and most of them were wrong, and she paid for every one of them." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger, a stranger he disliked. "Her mother left me when Mara was nine. Took her downriver to her sister's. I told myself I'd send for them when the claim paid out. That was nineteen years ago. You know how many letters I wrote?" He held up fingers: three. Then, after a pause, corrected himself: "Wrote. Didn't send. Couldn't get the words to sit right on the page. A man can read a river his whole life and still not read his own heart."

He closed his eyes. I thought he'd gone, and my stomach dropped — but no, he was just gathering himself, spending strength he didn't have because the account was nearly empty and he knew it.

"Register it in Mara Vann's name. Not mine. Mine's a dead man's name either way — if I die here, it's a dead man's claim, and dead men's claims get jumped by whoever's standing closest with a lawyer. But a living woman, a daughter with a clean name and no enemies — they'll have to steal it from her in the open, in daylight, with the whole registry watching. That's a harder theft." His hand found mine again, and this time the grip had iron in it, the last iron in him. "And you, Hazel. Not your name on it. I know what you're carrying. Don't ask how I know — a man who flies mail into the Rivergate with a debt like yours learns to read the people who land at his strip. Your name on that filing is a lamp in a dark window. Hers is a lamp behind a curtain. Understand?"

I understood. It was the whole architecture of my life he was describing, and hearing it said aloud, plainly, by a dying man, made it feel less like wisdom and more like a cage I'd been living in and calling it a house.

"I understand," I said.

"Then hear the terms." He drew a breath that whistled. "One: the claim gets registered before the permits lapse. Monsoon's end. The clerk at Rivergate Central — Aldous Ferry — he's crooked as a dog's hind leg, but he's crooked in a *documented* way, and a documented crooked man can be levered. Two: Mara's name. Mara Vann. Spelled the ordinary way. Three—" and here his voice did something I hadn't heard it do, cracked, actually cracked, "—she gets the letters. There's a tin. Same board. She gets the letters whether the claim means anything or not. Whether she wants them or not. A daughter's entitled to know what her father was, even if what he was was nothing much."

The tin was there under the board where he said, squat and rusted, heavier than its size. I set it on the bed beside the map and didn't open it. Some things you don't open in front of the man who wrote them.

"That's the trade," Elias said. "The map and everything it touches, for the promise. And Hazel—" he waited until I looked at him, and his eyes were clear, terribly clear, the clarity that comes into a man's face when everything false has burned off, "—I'm not asking you to be brave. I watched brave men die stupid. I'm asking you to be *stubborn*. There's a difference. Brave men die for the moment. Stubborn women outlive the men sent to stop them."

Outside, far off, the engine sound came again. Closer now. Or the same distance, and only the night had narrowed.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the chair by the bed and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the flats.

Three days' flying to reach the Sable Fork by air, if the weather gave me a window, and the monsoon had not given anyone a window in six weeks. By river, longer. The permits lapsed at monsoon's end — and the monsoon's end was not a date on a calendar so much as a mood the sky fell into, but the registry clerks kept their calendars anyway, and by their reckoning I had nineteen days. Nineteen days to run a flooded river I'd never flown, past a syndicate that had already burned one man's camp over this ground, with an engine sound prowling the banks and a map written partly in cipher.

And a debt. Always, underneath everything, the debt. The number I carried in my head the way other people carried a hymn. The syndicate held my father's marker, and my father's marker held my wings — one word from their lawyers and my license went the way of his plane, into the river, into the dark. Registering Elias's claim in his daughter's name was the one version of this that didn't put my name in their ledger twice.

I looked at the old man sleeping — or not sleeping; breathing, which was not the same — and I understood that I had already decided. Somewhere between the swear and the tin, between the third board and the engine in the rain, the decision had made itself, the way rivers make their courses: not by choosing but by wearing.

The night went long. I changed his dressing at two and didn't like the color of what came away. I fed him sips of watered broth at three and he took them without waking, which was worse than refusing. At four he surfaced enough to say, "Check the map's dry," and I lied and said I had. At five the rain slackened to a whisper, and the east window went the color of dirty pewter, and I knew dawn was coming the way you know a storm's over — not by light but by the quality of the silence.

"Hazel."

His voice. Small. I was beside him before I knew I'd moved.

"I'm here."

"Is it dawn?"

"Nearly."

"Good." He worked his hand free of the blanket and lay it flat on the cover, palm up, an old man's palm, seamed and stained, the hand of a man who had dug and filed and burned and written three letters and sent none. "I want to see it. I've been up for every one since I was a boy. Never had a reason to miss one."

I opened the shutters. The rain had stopped. The river ran high and brown and silent past the alders, and the sky over the eastern hills went from pewter to ash to a thin, reluctant gold, and the mist came off the water in slow ropes.

Elias Vann watched it with his eyes half open and his mouth almost smiling.

"She used to get up for them too," he said. "Mara. Nine years old, out on the porch before anybody, sitting with her knees up, watching the light come down the water. Her mother said she got it from me. I never had the heart to say she got it from somewhere better." A breath. A long pause, and I put my hand on his chest because I needed to feel it still working, and it was, but slowly, like a clock running down. "You'll like her. She's stubborn."

"I know," I said. "You told me to be like her."

"Did I?" The almost-smile arrived all the way, for the first time, and it changed his whole face, made him young for a second, made him a man on a porch with a daughter and a river and a morning. "Well. There you go. Best advice I ever gave anybody."

The light came down the water. I watched it come, and I felt it when it reached the cabin — a warmth on the floorboards, on the bed, on the back of my hand — and I felt, at the same moment, the exact second when the breathing stopped, because the silence it left behind was a different silence, the way a room is different after a clock stops.

I sat with him until the sun cleared the hills. Then I closed his eyes, and folded his hands, and went outside into the wet gold morning, and stood on my own porch with a map in an oilcloth wrap under my arm and a tin of unsent letters in my jacket, and looked downriver.

The engine was back. Not far now — a mile, maybe less, upstream of the bend, idling. A launch, by the sound of it. Big. Patient. Holding position in the current the way a man holds position outside a door he intends to enter.

Behind me, in the cabin, Elias Vann lay still in the dawn he'd stayed up to see. In front of me, down at the strip, my plane sat in the wet grass with half a tank and a cracked windscreen seal. And between me and both of them, coming up the river with the current in its favor and all the time in the world, was the sound of something that wanted the map, the tin, and the silence of everyone who'd ever seen either.

I had nineteen days, a dead man's promise, and no plan worth the name.

I went inside to pack.
