## 4. Visitors Who Don't Knock

The rain came in off the river that morning like it had a grudge to settle, and Hazel spent it the way she spent most mornings—checking the Moth's tie-downs, wringing water out of the engine cover, and pretending her hands didn't shake.

They hadn't stopped shaking since dawn. Since Elias Vann had died in her cabin with his hand around hers and his last breath fogging the cold air between them, and she'd sat there for a long time afterward listening to the rain and understanding, slowly and completely, what she had agreed to.

The map was under the floorboard beneath her cot, wrapped in oilcloth, wrapped again in a flour sack, buried under a winter's worth of spare spark plugs she'd never gotten around to sorting. She had checked on it four times since sunrise. She told herself it was habit. She told herself a lot of things.

She was stacking split firewood under the lean-to when she heard the boat.

Not the sound of it—that came later, muffled by rain—but the absence of it. The river at Alder Flats talked all day: logjams groaning, current sucking at the pilings, the far-off complaint of some fisherman's outboard running rough. This morning there was a gap in all that noise, a hole shaped exactly like an engine cutting to idle, and Hazel had lived on this stretch of water long enough to know that holes in the river's conversation meant somebody was being quiet on purpose.

She set down the axe. Kept the hatchet in her belt instead, low against her hip where a coat would cover it.

The launch came around the bend at half-throttle, gray hull, no name painted on the bow—or rather, a name painted over so many times it had become a smudge, which amounted to the same thing. Two men at the rail in oilskins too clean for working water. And standing at the wheel, one hand light on the spokes as if he were steering more by thought than muscle, a tall man in a long coat the color of wet slate.

He brought the launch alongside her dock without a single knock of wood against wood. Fenders already out. Lines already coiled. A man who did this often, and did it well.

"Morning," he called up, pleasant as a church deacon. "Corliss Hale. Syndicate interests, upper river." He smiled, and it was a good smile, well-made, the kind that had been practiced in front of mirrors until it forgot it was ever anything else. "You'd be the pilot. Miss Dunmore."

"Hazel Thornbury," she said, because she was, and because lying about your own name to a man like this was the first domino of a very bad afternoon. "And I'm not much of a miss before coffee."

"Then I'll be quick." He came up the dock steps without hurrying, and she noticed how he placed his feet—the toes first, soft, a man who had learned to walk on gravel without announcing himself. The two men in oilskins stayed with the boat. That was its own kind of message. *I don't need them for this.*

Hale stopped a polite six feet away and let the rain fall between them for a moment, as though giving her time to admire him. He had a face built for patience. Gray at the temples, lines around the eyes that might have come from squinting into weather or from laughing at things nobody else found funny. His hands were folded loosely behind his back, and she caught herself cataloguing them—knuckles unmarked, nails clean—and hating herself for the instinct even as it saved her nothing.

"I'm looking for a prospector," he said. "Elias Vann. Old boy, white beard, half-deaf in the left ear. Camped up past the Narrows."

"Prospectors are like mosquitoes up here," Hazel said. She kept her voice bored. Bored was safe. Bored said *you are not interesting to me and I have wood to stack.* "Half of them swear they've got a claim worth dying for, and the other half are lying about the first half."

"This one wasn't lying." Something moved behind Hale's eyes—not anger, not yet. Recalculation. "His camp burned two nights ago. He wasn't in it."

"That's a shame." She bent, picked up another length of firewood, stacked it. Made him watch her work. "Fire takes what it wants this time of year. Wet wood, green pitch, everybody burning brush. You want the forestry office in Rivergate. They keep a ledger of burns."

"I've seen the ledger." He watched her stack the wood with the mild attention of a man watching a clock. "Your name isn't in it either, Miss Thornbury, and yet I'm told you flew upriver three days ago with a medevac priority filed under a name nobody at the airfield recognized."

Her spine went cold, one vertebra at a time, but her hands stayed steady on the wood. Three days ago. He'd been pulling threads for three days, patient as rot.

"Fever case at the Kettle Creek camp," she said. "Old man Sorel. You can ask him yourself if you like—he coughs loud enough to hear from the dock."

"Sorel," Hale repeated, tasting it. "Funny. The airfield clerk says your passenger weighed near two hundred pounds and complained the whole flight. Old man Sorel is supposed to weigh a hundred and forty soaking wet."

"The clerk also says he saw a mermaid off Pier Four last spring," Hazel said. "Drink enough rotgut and everything weighs two hundred pounds."

Hale laughed. It was a real laugh, or close enough to fool a stranger, warm and rolling, and for one lurching second she almost liked him—which was, she understood, precisely the point of it. Men like Corliss Hale didn't break doors down. They got invited in.

"You're quick," he said. "I appreciate that. Makes my work shorter." He tilted his head toward the cabin. "Mind if I get out of the rain? I'll take five minutes of your coffee and leave you to your wood."

"No," Hazel said.

He blinked. Genuinely, briefly, as if *no* were a word he heard perhaps twice a year and always from people who regretted it.

"No?"

"My stove's small, my coffee's terrible, and I've got a fuel run to make before the wind turns." She straightened, wiped her palms on her coat, and looked him full in the face. It cost her something. Her heart was going like a floatplane taking off, and every animal nerve she owned was screaming at her to soften, to smile, to give him the little courtesies that kept men like him content. She gave him nothing. "Whatever Elias Vann was to you, I never met him. And whatever happened to his camp, it's above my pay grade and below my interest."

For a long moment Hale just studied her, rain sliding off the brim of his hat, and she had the distinct sensation of being read like a manifest—line by line, weight by weight, looking for the entry that didn't balance.

"You fly alone," he said at last. Not a question.

"I prefer my own company."

"Mm. Most people who prefer their own company are hiding from someone's else's." He said it lightly, almost kindly, and turned to go—and then stopped, the way a man stops when he remembers something trivial, and glanced past her shoulder. Past the cabin. Toward the rise of ground behind it where the birches stood thin and dripping.

Where the fresh grave was.

Hazel had put Elias there at dawn, before the shaking started, before the coffee, before she could think better of it. She'd dug the ground soft and raw and piled the stones crooked because her arms were done and her eyes were worse, and she'd told herself the birches would screen it from the river. From the river, yes. From a man standing on her own dock with the whole grey morning to look through him—

She watched Hale see it. Watched the polite interest in his face sharpen into something older and hungrier, the way a blade sharpens on a strop—one pass, two, and suddenly it's not a tool anymore.

"Burial ground?" he asked pleasantly.

"Ground," Hazel said.

"Recent, by the look of it." He took a step off the porch, unhurried, and she moved without deciding to—came down the steps and put herself between him and the path, hatchet still hidden, pulse hammering in her teeth.

"That's my dog," she said. "Died of old age. If you take one more step toward my yard, Mr. Hale, we're going to have a different kind of conversation."

And there it was—the moment the fear turned over in her chest like a boat in rough water and came right-side up as something else. Because she was afraid, God knew she was afraid, her knees felt like borrowed equipment and her mouth had gone dry as canvas. But underneath the fear, down where she kept the compass and the debt and every other thing she never explained, something stood up and planted its feet.

*You buried a good man this morning,* that something said. *You promised him. And this well-dressed vulture is not going to dig.*

Hale looked at her. Really looked, for the first time, and whatever he found made him reassess her the way he'd reassessed the grave—as something newer than expected, and harder.

"Well," he said softly. "There she is."

"There who is?"

"The woman who flew a two-hundred-pound dead man off a mountain and lied about it to my face before breakfast." He said it without heat, almost admiring, and that was the worst part—that he wasn't angry, that he was simply filing her away, stamping her like a document. "Here's what I think, Miss Thornbury. I think Elias Vann crawled out of his burning camp with something in his pocket. I think whoever found him got an earful of dying-man talk—a lost claim, a rich vein, all the usual gospel. And I think that person, being honest and decent, probably promised to do something about it." He smiled again, and this time the mirror-polish was gone off it. "Decent people make such expensive promises."

"I wouldn't know. I mind my own freight."

"Of course you do." He stepped back, raised one hand—a small, courteous gesture, surrender-shaped—and walked to the dock. At the rail he paused. "Permits expire when the monsoon ends, you know. Six weeks, maybe less. After that, every unregistered claim on the upper river reverts to open concession, and the syndicate files first, last, and always." He settled his hat. "If a certain prospector's paperwork surfaces before then, well. Paper's only paper. But the people holding it—" he tapped the rail once, gently, "—paper people burn too. Ask anyone."

The launch pulled away into the rain, quiet as it had come, and Hazel stood on her dock until the sound of it was gone entirely and then a while longer, letting the water run off her hat brim and down her collar, feeling the fear drain out of her shoulders and the defiance settle in where it had been, dense and permanent as ballast.

*Paper people burn too.*

She went inside. Dug up the floorboard. Unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that had finally, finally gone still, and spread Elias Vann's map across her table in the grey light—every creek line drawn in a dying man's careful ink, every bearing annotated, and at the top, in letters pressed deep enough to tear the paper:

*SABLE FORK — THE VEIN THAT DOESN'T QUIT — FOR MARA*

Six weeks. A syndicate enforcer who already suspected. A leaking plane, a flooded river, and a promise sworn over a corpse.

Hazel folded the map along its old creases, tucked it inside her shirt against her skin, and began packing.

* * *

By noon she had the Moth fueled and the cabin locked, and by one she was standing on the dock at Pier Nine in Rivergate, rain streaming off the eaves, reading the hand-painted sign nailed to the pilings of the only boat in the harbor whose owner hadn't run from her reputation:

**KESTREL — ENGINE WORK & RIVER FREIGHT — INQUIRE WITHIN**

The woman welding amidships pushed up her goggles and looked Hazel over—looked at the mud on her boots, the bruise-colored exhaustion under her eyes, the way she kept one hand flat against her ribs as if holding something in place against her skin.

"You're the pilot," the woman said. Not a question either. Everyone, it seemed, was asking questions that weren't questions today. "Word upriver is you're either dead or desperate."

"Junie Okonkwo?" Hazel said.

"And you're desperate." Junie set down the torch, and her grin had a crack running through it, like ice that would hold if you crossed fast. "Good. Desperate customers pay cash."
