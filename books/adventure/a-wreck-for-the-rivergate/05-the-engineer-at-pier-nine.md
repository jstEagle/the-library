## 5. The Engineer at Pier Nine

The Kestrel sat on the mud at low water like a patient who'd stopped believing in doctors, and Junie Okonkwo knelt beside her with a scraper and no illusions.

She'd been at the hull since dawn, working the worst of the rot out of the port strake, her hands black to the wrist with old pitch and new rust. The boat was forty years of bad decisions held together by good ones—her good ones, mostly, though nobody at Pier Nine would say so out loud. Nobody at Pier Nine said much of anything to her these days. That was what a blacklist bought you. Not silence exactly. Something worse: the performance of it. Men who'd shared her table for six years suddenly finding urgent business elsewhere when she walked into the mess hall. Women she'd fished with turning their faces toward the river as if the current had grown interesting all at once.

Junie had testified against Corliss Hale and the Rivergate Consolidated Mining Syndicate eleven months ago, and she'd do it again tomorrow, and this was the price, and she paid it every morning like rent.

"Morning, Okonkwo," said the boy from the chandlery, because he was sixteen and hadn't learned yet which people were contagious.

"Morning, Teo." She didn't look up. "You got that bronze fastener stock in yet?"

"Came in on Tuesday's barge." He crouched at a respectful distance, the way you'd approach a dog of uncertain temperament. "Mister Ansel says I'm not to sell it to you."

Junie kept scraping. The blade bit into soft wood, peeled back a curl of gray rot, and underneath was heartwood still sound as iron. There was always sound wood under the rot if you were willing to dig for it. She'd built a whole philosophy on that and never told anyone, because telling people things was how they got used against you.

"He say why?"

Teo shifted his weight. "He didn't have to."

"No," Junie agreed. "He didn't." She sat back on her heels and wiped her forearm across her face, leaving a fresh stripe of pitch through the sweat. "Tell him I'll take my business to Marrow's Landing, then. Tell him I hope he enjoys selling fasteners to men who've never once needed one in an emergency."

"That's not—I can't say that."

"You can say whatever you like, Teo. That's the difference between you and me right now, and I'd hold onto it while you've got it."

He went red and retreated, and Junie went back to work, and the river did what the river always did, which was keep moving regardless of who was allowed to ride it.

* * *

The woman came down the pier at half past nine, and Junie marked her before she'd made ten steps, because that was another thing the blacklist had taught her: watch everyone, trust the way they walk.

This one walked like a pilot. Weight forward, eyes doing the constant arithmetic of distances and exits, a canvas duffel over one shoulder and something wrapped in oilcloth slung tight against her ribs like it mattered more than the duffel. She wore flying boots gone gray at the seams and a canvas jacket with a fuel stain on the cuff that someone had tried and failed to scrub out. Not rich. Not desperate either—or desperate, but the kind that had learned to dress the desperation in competence.

She stopped at the head of the pier and read the boats the way Junie read weather charts. Her gaze snagged on the Kestrel and stayed there.

"She's not for sale," Junie called, without straightening up.

"I'm not buying." The woman came closer, boots ringing on the planks. Up close she was younger than her walk suggested, maybe thirty, with a sunburned line across her forehead where a cap should be and eyes the flat gray-green of river water over silt. "I'm hiring."

"Hiring." Junie laid the scraper down with exaggerated care. "You want to hire me. Me specifically. You know who I am?"

"I know you're Junie Okonkwo. I know you ran barges through the Sable Fork narrows for six years without losing so much as a crate of nails. And I know you told a courtroom what Corliss Hale's crews do to independent claims, which is more courage than this entire pier has shown since."

The words landed somewhere under Junie's sternum and sat there glowing like a coal. She stood up slowly, making the woman wait for it, making herself wait for it too.

"And who told you all that? Because around here, my name gets you two reactions. Spitting, and crossing the street."

"The second one. Mostly." The woman almost smiled; it didn't survive the trip. "I asked around. Carefully. The spitters were the ones who confirmed you're the best engineer on this stretch of river, if that tells you anything about the quality of their judgment."

"It tells me they're right, which is annoying." Junie crossed her arms. "Who are you?"

"Hazel Dunmore. I fly freight and mail out of the upper valley."

"I know your plane. Yellow wing, engine that coughs on cold starts." Junie had watched it come down the valley a hundred times, threading weather that grounded everything else, and had privately rated the pilot better than most of the men who sneered at her name. "What does a mail pilot need with a riverboat?"

Hazel Dunmore looked out at the water for a moment. When she turned back, something had been decided behind those gray eyes, and Junie recognized the look, because she wore it herself most days: the look of a person calculating exactly how much truth a stranger could be trusted with, and deciding to gamble.

"A man died in my cabin three nights ago," she said quietly. "Elias Vann. Prospector. He was hurt and I tried to get him help and he died anyway, and before he went he gave me something he wanted delivered. Registered, actually. Downriver, at the land office, before the concession permits lapse when the monsoon ends."

Junie felt the word *Vann* go through her like a fishhook. "Elias Vann is dead?"

"You knew him?"

"Everyone on this river knew Elias Vann. Stubbornest man God ever let loose with a pick." And the syndicate had been hunting his claim for a decade—that was river knowledge, common as reeds. "How did he die?"

"Gut-shot, before I found him. His camp burned." Hazel's voice stayed level but her hand had gone to the oilcloth bundle at her ribs, unconsciously, the way you touch a wound. "There are men looking for what he gave me. Syndicate men. Possibly Hale himself."

The name dropped between them and neither woman moved.

"So let me make sure I have this straight," Junie said. "You want me to take you downriver, past syndicate territory, during monsoon, carrying something they're willing to kill for. In a boat that half this pier won't sell me a nail for."

"Yes."

"And what's in it for me?"

"A share of the claim. Elias registered it in his daughter's name, but there's room in the arrangement for whoever gets it there. He wrote it out. It's binding enough."

Junie looked at the Kestrel. Forty years of bad decisions. Her whole world, patched and repatched, worth maybe three months' honest wages and priceless as her own heartbeat. She thought about the share, and found—to her own mild surprise—that it wasn't the weight that tipped anything. She'd survived this long on spite and stubbornness alone; gold was just a different currency for the same coin.

"What else?" she said. "Because you're holding something back. Pilots always are."

Hazel Dunmore held her gaze for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, rough sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman who'd just realized she'd been caught in a lie she hadn't even told yet.

"Spite," she said. "That's my other payment. They took everything from my father. Everything, and then they sent men to collect the debt twice over, and I have spent four years being small and quiet and forgettable so they'd leave me alone. And I am done." The last word came out flat and hard as a struck flange. "I want to cost them something. I don't care what. If you're the kind of person who understands that, we'll get along fine."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere upstream thunder muttered, the monsoon clearing its throat.

Junie picked up her scraper, considered it, then set it down for the day.

"I testified against Hale because his crew burned a union boat with two men aboard," she said. "They called it an accident. The court called it insufficient evidence, except for the part where they blacklisted me for perjury I didn't commit, which tells you whose evidence was sufficient and for whom." She stepped aboard the Kestrel and patted the rail the way you'd steady a horse. "So yes. I understand spite. I've been keeping mine fed for a year."

"When can you sail?"

"High water's at two. We take the eastern channel past Gantry Point while it's deep, then run the cut before dark." Junie was already moving, pulling tarpaulins, checking the lashings on her spare fuel drums, her hands doing the work while her mouth caught up. "You'll want to stow that oilcloth thing somewhere it floats. Under the aft bench there's a compartment I sealed with two layers of canvas and tar. If we sink, it sinks slower than we do."

Hazel climbed down into the boat, careful, testing each board, and Junie noted that too—she trusted nothing until she'd tested it. A fellow survivor's habit.

"How bad is the hull?" Hazel asked.

"Bad enough that I was going to spend another week on her. Good enough that I don't have to." Junie hauled the tarp off the engine housing and stood looking at her rebuilt Marrow & Sons single-screw, gleaming with fresh grease, the one beautiful thing she owned. "We'll patch as we go. I always patch as I go. It's practically a religious practice."

"And if the syndicate comes after us?"

"Then we find out whether their boats are better than their lawyers." Junie grinned, and was surprised to find the grin was real—the first real one in months, arriving uninvited like spring. "Which, for the record, is a very low bar."

Hazel settled onto the thwart with the oilcloth bundle in her lap, and for a moment the two of them just looked at each other across the small battered deck of the Kestrel, two women who had both learned the same hard curriculum in different classrooms, sizing each other up with the particular respect of survivors.

"You could still say no," Hazel said. "Walk away. Nobody would blame you."

"Nobody would blame me," Junie repeated. "That's the problem with this pier. Everybody's so busy not blaming anybody that nobody stands anywhere." She dropped into the engineer's seat and put her hand on the starter. "Two o'clock. High water. Be here, and bring anything you can't bear to lose, because I promise you nothing else on this river."

* * *

At ten minutes to two, Hazel Dunmore came down the pier with her duffel, the oilcloth bundle, and a leather case Junie guessed held charts, and paused at the gangway because a launch was idling thirty yards downstream, black-hulled, twin engines, flying no flag at all, which on this river meant only one kind of owner.

Junie saw her see it. Saw her go very still, the way game goes still.

"Back of the boathouse," Junie said quietly, not looking up from her fuel lines. "There's a gap in the pilings where I take her out when I don't feel like being counted. We cast off from there. By the time they notice we're gone, we'll be a rumor."

Hazel moved without hurry, without looking at the launch again, and that was its own kind of skill, and Junie filed it away with approval.

By twenty past two the Kestrel slid out through the piling gap into the brown flood of the Rivergate, engine humming, rain beginning to stipple the water ahead of them like a warning. Junie put the wheel over and pointed her bow downriver, toward the Sable Fork and everything waiting there.

Behind them, faint under the rain, the black launch's engines came awake.

"Well," Junie said. "That didn't take long."
