## 6. Downstream Before Dark

**HAZEL**

The Kestrel ran the first hour like a rumor — all noise and no substance, a patched-up riverboat with an engine that talked to itself in a language only Junie Okonkwo understood.

Hazel sat amidships with the oilcloth bundle on her knees and did not open it. That was the discipline. Elias had died with the thing pressed into her hands and she had not so much as peeled a corner of it in three days, because opening it would make it real, and real things could be taken from you. She had learned that lesson twice over — once with a compass, once with a debt — and she was not eager for a third tuition.

Instead she watched the river.

The Rivergate in flood season was less a waterway than a country that had forgotten its borders. Brown water stood in the treetops on both banks, whole groves drowned to their shoulders, and the current carried a parade of debris that Junie read like a newspaper. A fence rail meant a washed-out homestead upstream. A roof thatch meant worse. A dead cow, drifting belly-up with terrible dignity, meant the levee at Two Sisters had gone, and Junie said so without looking up from her throttle.

"You've run this water before," Hazel said.

"I've run everything before." Junie stood at the wheel with her feet planted wide, barefoot on the wet boards, a woman built like the boat she loved — broad in the beam, riveted together, patched in places that showed. "Ran freight for the company for six years. Then I told a magistrate what the company does to villages that won't sign concessions, and now I run whatever pays." She glanced back. "Including you."

"Including me."

"Which is why you're going to tell me what's in that bundle before I decide which channel to trust at the Sable junction. I'm not running blind for a stranger. Spite only carries a woman so far."

Hazel looked at the water. A heron stood in a drowned willow, motionless, hunting fish that had never known a smaller world.

"His name was Elias Vann," she said. "Prospector. I flew him out of the Alder Flats with a bullet through his gut and he lasted long enough to trade me a map for a promise."

"What promise?"

"That I'd register his claim before the permits expire. Monsoon's end. In his daughter's name."

Junie was quiet for a long moment. The engine talked. Somewhere behind them, miles back, a bird screamed once and stopped.

"The Vann claim," Junie said at last. "The one that doesn't exist."

"That's the one."

"Every drunk on the riverfront claims to know a man who knew where it was." Junie's voice had gone flat and careful, the way it had gone flat when she'd first named her price. "And every one of those men is either rich, dead, or lying."

"Elias was the third kind," Hazel said. "Mostly."

* * *

**JUNIE**

Junie had buried enough men to know when a story was true by the way it was told. Liars embellished. Hazel Dunmore subtracted. She gave you the shape of the thing and let the edges stay dark, and that restraint — that careful, practiced forgettability Junie had clocked the moment they'd met at Pier Nine — was more convincing than any detail could have been.

So she opened the throttle and took the eastern channel at the Split Rock, where the current ran fast but the water was deep, and by midday the Kestrel was eating miles the way she'd been built to, back when someone at the shipyard had loved her properly.

The river rewarded them. That was the thing nobody told you about the flood country: it was gorgeous the way a fire was gorgeous. They passed a mission church standing to its eaves in brown water, its bell tower making a perfect doubled image below, and a raft of children poling a dugout through what had been a barley field, laughing at something, waving at the boat with both hands. The air smelled green and rotten at once. Sun came through the rain clouds in columns and lit the water up like hammered brass.

Junie felt the old lift in her chest, the one the river always gave her, and hated it a little, because the river had also taken her license, her livelihood, and six years of her life in a company court that had never once looked up from its ledger.

"You're smiling," Hazel said.

"I'm steering."

"You're smiling."

"Mind your bundle, pilot."

But she was smiling. The Kestrel ran clean, the weather held, and by the time the sun leaned west they had put better than forty miles behind them and the syndicate's reach — which Junie knew the way you know the reach of a bad dog — had not so much as barked.

That was when the seaplane came over.

It came low out of the west with the sun behind it, and for three seconds it was only a shape, a dragonfly silhouette against the glare, and Junie's stomach dropped through the hull before her mind caught up. She knew that airframe. Twin floats, high wing, company gray with the registration numbers painted over and repainted over, because the syndicate liked its aircraft deniable and its men anonymous.

It passed them without slowing. Climbed. Turned a wide, unhurried circle to the south, and came back.

"Get down," Junie said.

Hazel was already moving. She'd gone from sitting to flat on the deck plates in the time it took Junie to say it, the oilcloth bundle shoved beneath the thwart, her body arranged into the loose sprawl of a woman napping — and Junie thought, *she's done this before, she has rehearsed being unremarkable her whole life* — while the plane came down the channel at mast height, close enough now to see the man in the right-hand seat, not looking at them at all, looking at his chart, performing the same careful unremarkability in reverse.

The engine note swallowed them and passed. The plane climbed again, banked south, and shrank into the haze over the flooded timberlands.

Junie held course. She didn't throttle down, didn't wave, didn't do anything a guilty boat would do. Only when the drone of it had faded to a memory did she speak, low, without turning her head.

"That was a survey flight."

"That was a survey flight," Hazel agreed from the deck, "the way a noose is a necklace."

* * *

**HAZEL**

They ran until dark, because Junie said the worst thing you could do was change your pattern in front of a plane, and then they put in at a bend where a drowned orchard broke the current and the trees gave cover from above.

Hazel cooked rice on a spirit stove under the canopy while Junie lashed the boat to a branch that had been, in another life, part of an apple tree. The water stood three feet deep around the trunks. Frogs were singing in the drowned rows as if the flood were a promotion.

"Open it," Junie said, when the rice was done. "I've earned it. I ran your river all day."

Hazel unwrapped the oilcloth.

The map was drawn on the back of a linen survey chart, in ink gone brown, in a hand that had been steady right up until it wasn't. Elias Vann had drawn his river country the way a man draws a thing he has carried in his head for twenty years — every bend labeled, every depth sounded, every sandbar annotated with dates and small bitter marginalia. *Silted, '09. New channel after the levee year. Don't trust the company chart, they moved the fork on paper and not in the water.*

"There," Hazel said, and put her finger on it. The Sable Fork. Elias had drawn it splitting from the main river forty miles north of where the official survey put it, up through a gorge he'd marked with three words: *Devil's Teeth. Portage.* Beyond the gorge the lines tightened into a drowned valley, and at the head of it, circled twice, underlined, with a cairn symbol and a string of assay figures that made even Hazel's untrained eye go wide — the claim.

"Three days," she said. "Maybe four, if the flood's pushed the channels around since he drew this. He says the company charts are wrong on purpose."

"Of course they are." Junie took the map, held it to the lantern, and her face did something complicated — the look of an engineer reading another engineer's work and finding it honest. "He sounded this whole reach himself. Look — depths, dates, every year the flood changed something. This man knew the water better than the water did." She looked up. "And they burned his camp over it."

"They burned his camp over it," Hazel said, "and shot him, and let him die in my cabin, and the men who did it are going to be at the Sable junction before we are, if that plane reports back tonight."

Junie set the map down. For a moment the only sound was the frogs and the water slapping the hull and the lantern hissing.

"Then we don't sleep," she said. "We run the night water. Moon's coming up behind the cloud, and I know this stretch — there's a cut through the drowned timber that saves six miles and can't be seen from the air under canopy. We'll be at the junction before their launch is, and if the plane comes back—" she smiled, and it was not a friendly smile, "—it can watch us disappear into country its charts say is dry land."

Hazel rolled the map closed, and her hands were not quite steady, and she found she didn't mind. All day the river had been giving her the old exhilaration, the flying feeling, the sense of motion with a purpose — and underneath it now, coiling up from her stomach, was the colder thing. The plane had found them in an afternoon. The syndicate's water was wider than its paper.

"Junie," she said, as the other woman began untying the mooring line. "The man who shot Elias. If he's on that plane, or behind it — he won't stop at the map. Elias told me. He said the man wants the silence of everyone who's seen it."

"Then we'd best make sure," Junie said, "that by the time he catches us, there's too many of us who've seen it to silence."

She opened the throttle. The Kestrel slid out of the drowned orchard into the moonlit flood, and the frogs sang behind them, and somewhere to the south — Hazel could not see it, but she could feel it, the way you feel weather in an old scar — a launch was already running dark up the river with its lights out, closing the miles.
