## 7. What the Compass Remembers

The rain came down soft that night—not the hammering deluge of the coast but a fine, patient drizzle that made the whole river seem to breathe. They had tied up in a cut of deadwater behind a stand of drowned cedars, far enough off the channel that any passing launch would read them as driftwood and shadow. Junie killed the Kestrel's engine with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children, and the silence that followed was so complete Hazel could hear the water ticking against the hull, plank by plank, like something counting.

They ate cold rice and smoked fish out of the same tin, sitting cross-legged on the engine housing because the cabin floor had taken on an inch of bilge water that Junie swore was normal and Hazel refused to believe was anything less than catastrophic.

"She's a river boat," Junie said, shrugging. "River boats weep. You don't stop a river boat from weeping, you just bail faster than she grieves."

Hazel laughed despite herself, and the laugh surprised her—loose and unguarded in a way she hadn't managed in years. It occurred to her, somewhere in the middle of it, that she couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed in front of another person without first calculating what the laugh would cost her.

That was the trouble with being forgettable. You got good at it. You got so good at it that eventually there wasn't much left of you to remember.

Junie lit the lantern after dinner—a real luxury, oil being what it cost this far upriver—and hung it from the ridgepole under the tarpaulin they'd rigged over the cockpit. The light turned everything the color of weak tea. Rain dripped off the tarp's edge in a slow metronome. Somewhere across the deadwater a bird Hazel couldn't name made a sound like a hinge complaining, over and over, unhurried, as if it had all night and intended to use every minute of it.

She had the compass out. She didn't realize she'd taken it out until she felt Junie watching her do it—the small brass case sitting in her palm, lid open, needle trembling toward true north with the faint, faithful insistence it had never once abandoned, not in twenty years, not through any storm or lie or long dark stretch of country where north was the only honest thing left.

"You've been holding that thing like a rosary since Pier Nine," Junie said. Not accusing. Just noticing, the way she noticed everything—the way she'd noticed Hazel's hands shaking on the throttle their first morning out, and said nothing then either.

"It's a compass."

"Mm." Junie poured the last of the tea between them, mostly leaves. "And I'm a riverboat engineer. We're both exactly what we appear to be."

Hazel almost closed the lid. Her thumb actually moved to do it—that old reflex, the snap-shut of the brass case like a door closing, like a mouth closing. But the lantern light was warm and the rain was gentle and Junie Okonkwo sat across from her with her bad knee stretched out and her eyes steady, and Hazel found, to her considerable alarm, that she didn't want to close it.

Not tonight.

* * *

"My father's," she said.

The word *father's* came out of her like a splinter coming out of skin—all at once, with a little blood behind it. She hadn't said it aloud in eleven years. She hadn't said much of anything about him aloud in eleven years. There were entire years when she hadn't let herself think his name, because thinking it led to the debt, and the debt led to the syndicate, and the syndicate led to the night she'd stood in a wet field outside Meridian Flats while men in good boots explained to her, very reasonably, what her father's death had cost and how she would be paying it back for the rest of her natural life.

"His name was Aldous Dunmore," she heard herself say. "He flew the Rivergate before I did. Before there were concession permits, even. He flew mail, medicine, whatever paid." She turned the compass in the light. The brass was worn smooth at the hinges, polished by thirty years of a thumb worrying it the way hers did now. "And then he flew other things."

"Contraband," Junie said. Not a question.

"Contraband." Hazel nodded. "Opium paste, mostly, in the early years. Later it was guns going the other direction. He told himself he was done after each run. He told me he was done. And each time the syndicate came around with new numbers—interest, penalties, protection fees for routes he needed—he'd fly one more." She smiled, and it was a terrible thin smile, the kind that doesn't reach anywhere near the eyes. "You know how that story ends. Everybody in the interior knows how that story ends. The plane goes down in weather it shouldn't have been flying in, and six months later some prospector finds the wreck, and there's no cargo aboard, and no manifest, and nobody asks why a mail pilot was running the Sable Fork at midnight in November."

Junie was quiet for a long moment. The dripping off the tarpaulin kept its slow time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its banter.

"The syndicate owned him."

"The syndicate owned him the way they own everyone who owes them." Hazel closed her hand around the compass, and the brass was cool against her palm, familiar as her own pulse. "Except owning isn't the right word. Owning implies you're worth something. What they do is more like—leasing. You lease your life back from them, month by month, and the payments never end, and if you miss one, they take the rest of the term out of your hide. My father missed his last payment by dying. So I inherited the balance."

"How much?"

"Doesn't matter. The number changes. That's the trick of it—you can never pay it off because the number is whatever they decide it is that season. Interest compounds on interest. Penalties compound on penalties. Eleven years I've flown their freight at rates no independent would touch, kept my head down, been forgettable, and the ledger has gone *up*, not down. I checked last spring. I stopped checking after that."

Junie set down her tin. She did it carefully, the way she did everything mechanical, and Hazel understood suddenly that Junie was handling the moment itself like a mechanism she didn't want to strip the threads of.

"I testified against them," Junie said. "Four years ago. Dockworkers' union case, out of Corvane. Three of their foremen went down for moving guns through legitimate shipping. My name was on the affidavit. My face was in the courtroom." She flexed her bad knee, wincing without appearing to notice she was doing it. "The blacklist came down inside a month. Every licensed yard on the river. Every bonded outfit. I could fix engines in my sleep—I *do* fix engines in my sleep, ask anyone—but no paper worth having would touch me. So now I run freight for people who don't ask questions, in boats that shouldn't float, and I tell myself it's spite keeping me alive." She looked up. "Turns out it might be true. Spite and a leaking hull."

"They ruined you too."

"They ruined everybody who ever looked at them straight on." Junie leaned back against the engine housing, arms crossed, and studied Hazel with that frank, unblinking attention that had unsettled her from the first hour at Pier Nine and unsettled her still. "So that's why you keep your head down. That's why you're forgettable. You're not hiding from the law, Dunmore. You're hiding from the ledger."

"Yes."

"And Elias Vann dies in your care, and leaves you a map worth more than both our debts put together, and your first instinct—" Junie tilted her head, "—was what? To sell it? Buy yourself clear?"

Hazel flinched. Because yes. God help her, yes—for about four hours, alone in her cabin with the oilcloth packet on the table and Elias Vann cooling in the next room, she had sat with the map unrolled and done the arithmetic. What a syndicate front company would pay for a proven claim. What a rival concern would pay. What freedom cost, itemized.

"I thought about it," she admitted. The words tasted like bilge water. "I thought about it for half a night. And then I thought about him dying on my floor asking me for one thing—one thing, Junie, after a lifetime of getting nothing—and I thought about what my father would have done with that map."

"What would he have done?"

"He'd have registered it himself and been dead within the year." Hazel laughed again, and this time the laugh hurt. "But he'd have died free. Or he'd have believed he was dying free, which for a man like Aldous Dunmore amounted to the same thing."

The rain thickened briefly against the tarpaulin, then eased. Across the deadwater, the complaining bird finally quit. In the new quiet Hazel could hear the river proper, away beyond the cedars—moving, always moving, carrying everything downstream whether it was ready to go or not.

"Show me the back," Junie said.

"The back?"

"Of the compass. You've been opening it from the front all week like it's just a needle. But you hold it wrong for a front-opener. You cradle it. Like there's something in the base." She shrugged. "I rebuild instruments. I know how people hold things they're protecting."

Hazel stared at her. Then, slowly—wondering at herself even as she did it—she turned the compass over and pressed the recessed catch at the base of the case. The bottom plate swung open on a hinge so fine you'd never find it unless you knew.

Inside, folded into a space barely larger than a thumbnail, was a square of oilpaper no bigger than a stamp. She unfolded it with fingers that had begun, without her permission, to shake.

A photograph. Faded nearly to ghosts: a young woman in a flight jacket standing beside a battered bush plane, one hand on the propeller, grinning like the whole sky belonged to her. And scrawled across the bottom in a hand Hazel knew better than her own:

*For Haze — north is wherever you decide. — Dad*

"That's me," Hazel said. Her voice came out strange, distant, like it belonged to someone else in the lantern light. "Nineteen. First solo cross-country. He gave me the compass that morning. Said a pilot could lose the plane, the load, the route, the whole sky—but never the needle." She swallowed. "Six months later he was a wreck on the Sable Fork, and I was a debtor, and I put the photograph in the base and I never took it out again. Never showed a living soul. You don't show people the soft parts. The soft parts are where they grab you."

Junie reached across the space between them—not fast, giving Hazel every chance to pull away—and touched the edge of the photograph with one blunt finger. "Your father," she said quietly, "and my union brothers, and Elias Vann and his burned camp and his daughter who doesn't know he's dead. All different wounds. Same knife."

"Yes."

"And Hale's people are holding the knife."

"Hale's people *are* the knife."

Junie nodded slowly. Then she did something Hazel did not expect. She pulled a battered tin from her coat—the kind sailors keep tobacco in—and opened it, and inside, wrapped in wax paper, was a single brass cartridge casing, dented, empty, its rim stamped with a date four years old.

"Corvane courthouse steps," Junie said. "Somebody fired three shots over the crowd after the verdict. One of them hit Amos Ferro in the chest. He was sixty-one. He taught half the dockhands on the river their trade." She closed the tin. "I keep the casing so I remember what I'm for. You keep the photograph so you remember what you're from." She looked up, and in the lantern light her eyes were bright and hard and absolutely certain. "Between the two of us, Dunmore, we've got a memory and a reason. That's more than Hale's whole outfit has. They've only got orders."

Hazel refolded the photograph along its worn creases and slid it back into the compass base, and pressed the plate shut, and held the little brass instrument in both hands. The needle inside swung, settled, pointed north—or pointed wherever she decided north was; her father had been right about that, at least, right about the only thing that mattered.

"Thank you," she said. It was inadequate and they both knew it, and Junie had the grace not to say so.

"Don't thank me yet." Junie jerked her chin toward the black wall of the drowned cedars, beyond which the Sable Fork waited with its flooded channels and its toll stations and its three days of rising water between them and a claim that could buy all their freedoms or drown all their hopes. "Save it for when we're through the Devil's Teeth. If we're through the Devil's Teeth."

"We'll be through."

"Confidence. From the woman whose plane I've seen land like a dropped suitcase."

"From the woman," Hazel said, "who just learned she's carrying a debtor, a blacklisted engineer, a dead man's map, and a syndicate enforcer's patience—all in one leaking boat—and hasn't thrown any of us overboard yet."

Junie grinned, quick and white in the lantern light. "Night's young."

Hazel banked the lantern low and took the first watch, as they'd agreed, wrapped in a blanket in the stern with the compass in her pocket and her father's grin folded safe against her ribs. The rain kept its patient time on the tarpaulin. The river breathed. And sometime past the middle of the night, drifting at the edge of sleep with her hand resting on the brass case, she heard it—

an engine, far off, low and steady, running without lights up the dark channel they had deliberately avoided.
