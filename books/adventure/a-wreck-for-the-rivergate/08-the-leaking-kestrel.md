## 8. The Leaking Kestrel

The Kestrel had a voice, and Junie Okonkwo had spent eleven years learning to listen to it.

Every boat talked if you knew how to hear. The old rivermen said it was superstition, but Junie knew better. A hull was a drum stretched over water, and every plank and rivet had its own note in the chord. The Kestrel's idle sang low and rough through the stern, a baritone with a smoker's rasp. The wind in her rigging wires whistled a thin third above that. And underneath it all, when the engine dropped to half-throttle in the wide water below the Sable Fork junction, there came a sound Junie had never heard her make before.

A tick. Faint as a fingernail on glass. Once every four or five seconds.

She cut the throttle and cocked her head.

"What is it?" Hazel asked from the bow, where she'd been crouched over the compass with the map spread across her knees under an oilskin. She'd been like that for an hour, tracing the hand-inked contours of Elias Vann's map against the drowned landscape sliding past—creek mouths, dead snags, the ghost of an old tote road surfacing and vanishing beneath the brown floodwater.

"Shh." Junie held up one finger. "Listen."

The rain came down in the steady gray sheets that had been falling since dawn, drumming on the canvas deck cover, hissing off the river's surface. Beneath all of it: tick. Tick. Tick.

"That's not the engine," Hazel said.

"No," Junie agreed grimly. "It isn't."

She knew what it was. She'd known for twenty minutes, maybe longer, and had been telling herself she was wrong. It was the sound of water finding its way where water wanted to go—a slow seep working through a seam somewhere below the waterline, each drop striking the bilge with metronomic patience. The Kestrel's hull was a patchwork of salvaged plate and hand-driven rivets, and every inch of it had been inspected, caulked, cursed at, and loved. But boats lied about their health the way pilots lied about their hours logged, and somewhere down there, the old girl was bleeding.

Junie eased forward into the flooded timber and killed the engine entirely. The silence that followed was enormous—the kind of silence that only exists in monsoon country, when the rain fills every space that noise has abandoned.

"Bailer's under the thwart," she said. "Start looking."

Hazel found it and began hauling water over the side in long, practiced strokes. There was perhaps two inches standing in the bilge. Not fatal. Not yet. But the tick kept coming, patient as a debt collector.

* * *

They found the fence the way you find everything on a flooded river: by hitting it.

It happened forty minutes later, in what had once been a cattle paddock and was now open water dotted with the black crowns of submerged posts. The map showed nothing here—Elias Vann hadn't drawn fences—but maps were drawn in dry years, and this was not a dry year. The top rail of the boundary fencing lay six inches under the surface, invisible in the churned brown water, running perpendicular to their course like a tripwire laid across a mile of river.

The impact was small and absolute. A shudder ran the length of the hull, a sound like a giant plucking a bass string, and then the tick became a gurgle became a rush.

"Hold her!" Junie had the tiller hard over, swinging the bow toward a rise of higher ground ahead where a stand of alders marked dry land—or something close to it. "Bail! Bail like your life depends on it, because it does!"

Hazel bailed. Junie drove. The Kestrel wallowed between them, shipping water faster now, the bilge rising past Junie's ankles as she stood at the tiller, past the floorboards, the whole boat gone soft and sluggish under her hands. Two hundred yards of gray rain and gray water. The alders grew out of a low bluff that might have been a riverbank in another season, might have been somebody's front yard. Behind them, half-drowned in the murk, Junie could make out the shapes of buildings—a roofline collapsed into itself, a loading dock jutting into the flood like a broken jaw.

A trading post. Drowned years ago, by the look of it, and never reclaimed.

She ran the Kestrel up the muddy shelf of ground at the last possible moment, engine screaming in shallow water, and beached her hard. Then they both just sat there, breathing, while the rain hammered the deck and the bilge slowly drained back out through the hole they'd opened.

"Well," Hazel said finally. "That could have gone worse."

Junie looked at her. Water streamed off the pilot's cap; her wrists were shaking where she gripped the bailer.

"It absolutely could not have," Junie said. "We're taking on water in the middle of nowhere, in monsoon season, with Hale somewhere behind us, and I've got a hole in my boat the size of God knows what. Get out. We're beaching her proper."

* * *

The trading post had died a long time ago.

Junie walked its drowned perimeter while Hazel secured the boat, reading the ruin the way she read engines. The main building was timber-frame, sagging eastward, its veranda posts swallowed to the eaves by the flood. A warehouse behind it had lost its roof entirely; rain fell straight through the empty joists into standing water that mirrored the gray sky. Rusted drums lay tumbled along the old dock like spent cartridges. Somebody had painted a name across the gable once—Junie could still make out the faded ghost of letters, *HALLOWAY & SONS*, bleeding down the weathered boards.

Nobody had fought for this place. That was the thing about the Rivergate interior: the river took what it wanted, and the people who couldn't afford to lose it left, and the people who could afford to lose it never cared enough to stay. The syndicate bought the salvage rights for pennies and let the rest rot.

"There's a workbench inside," Hazel called from the doorway of the warehouse, her voice echoing strangely in the wet hollow of the building. "Tools. Some sheet tin. And—" a pause, and then a short laugh with no humor in it, "—a stack of old manifests. Someone was doing paperwork here when the water came."

"Bring the tin," Junie said. "Leave the paperwork."

She got the Kestrel hauled up on rollers of driftwood until the breach cleared the waterline, and then she lay on her back in the mud beneath the hull and went looking for the wound.

It wasn't pretty. The fence rail had struck her right where the original keel plate met a patch Junie herself had welded three seasons ago—a junction of old metal and new, the weakest seam on the boat, the one place she should have known to check. The tear was maybe eight inches long, ragged at both ends, the plate peeled back like the lid of a tin.

Junie lay there in the rain, staring up at the underside of her boat, and felt something crack open in her chest that had nothing to do with the hull.

Three seasons. Three seasons of runs, and she'd welded that patch herself, and she'd told herself it would hold because it had to hold, because the Kestrel was all she had left after the tribunals took everything else—her license, her berth at the company yards, her name on any list worth being on. Testifying against Corliss Hale's employers had cost her a career and bought her a blacklist, and the only thing the syndicate hadn't managed to take was this patched, leaking, stubborn wreck of a boat.

And now a fence had opened her up like a letter.

"You all right under there?" Hazel's voice, muffled through the hull.

"Peachy," Junie called back. "Hand me the tin."

* * *

Patching a hull in a monsoon is a conversation between a woman and the weather, and the weather does most of the talking.

Junie worked by feel more than sight, the light under the hull too poor for anything else. She trimmed the tin plate with cold-swollen fingers, shaped it to the curve of the keel, drilled new holes for the rivets while Hazel held a tarpaulin overhead to keep the worst of the rain off the work. The drill bit slipped twice. The second time it skinned Junie's knuckle to the bone, and she swore with a fluency that made Hazel raise her eyebrows.

"My father was a machinist," Hazel offered, passing her a rag. "He'd have liked you."

"He'd have liked me drunk and rich," Junie said, and set the rivet.

But something in the saying of it loosened a knot she hadn't known was tied. They worked on in a rhythm after that—Hazel holding, Junie driving, the two of them passing tools back and forth without looking, the way people do when they've started to trust each other's hands. The rain came harder. The tarpaulin filled and bellied and had to be shaken out. Somewhere in the drowned post, a length of loose roofing banged against a joist in the wind, keeping its own time against the rain.

By late afternoon the patch was in. Junie sealed it with marine glue and a layer of tarred canvas, pressed it flat with a scavenged clamp from the workbench, and lay back in the mud to admire it. It was ugly. It would hold. Those were the only two promises she ever made about her own work, and she'd never yet broken either one.

"We'll want to let it cure overnight," she said, wriggling out from under the hull. "Glue needs twelve hours minimum, and I'm not putting her back on the water with a soft patch. We're stuck here till morning."

Hazel looked out at the flooded plain, at the rain marching across it in gray curtains, and Junie watched her do the arithmetic. Every hour beached was an hour off the permit clock. Four days, maybe five, before the concessions lapsed and Elias Vann's claim dissolved back into syndicate paper.

"Then we're stuck here till morning," Hazel said simply, and Junie decided, not for the first time, that she liked the way this woman carried bad news.

* * *

They made camp in the old warehouse, on a raised platform of pallets near the front wall where the roof still held. Junie rigged the tarpaulin into a lean-to against the leaking joists, and they got a spirit stove going under it, sheltered from the wind, and heated tinned soup and strong tea while the dark came down around them like rising water.

The rain on the tin roof was a constant roar, but under it the warehouse creaked and settled, breathing its own long death. Water moved somewhere in the dark—slow, patient, finding new rooms in the building as the flood crept up the walls. Junie found herself listening to it the way she listened to engines, cataloguing sounds, sorting threat from harmless.

"Tell me something true," Hazel said suddenly, across the stove flame.

Junie glanced up. The pilot's face was half shadow, half firelight, and there was something in it that wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite a challenge.

"Like what?"

"Anything. You testified against them. The syndicate. Everyone on the river talks about it in whispers, but nobody says what actually happened."

Junie turned her mug slowly in her hands. Outside, the rain redoubled, hammering the tarpaulin, and she thought: why not. Why not, on a drowned trading post in the middle of a monsoon, with a patched boat and a stolen map and a dead man's promise sitting in the compass case between them.

"I was chief engineer on the *Corvid*, one of their ore barges," she said. "Eleven years. I knew every rivet on that boat, and I knew what we were carrying below the ore—because you can't run machinery that heavy and not know the weight doesn't add up. Mercury. Unregistered. Coming down out of claims the syndicate didn't own, going out past the assay offices so nobody counted it." She shrugged. "When the union finally grew a spine, I gave testimony. Names, dates, manifests. Enough to sink them, if anyone had wanted them sunk."

"And instead?"

"Instead two witnesses recanted, the magistrate retired mid-hearing, and I got blacklisted from every licensed berth on the river. The syndicate didn't need to win the hearing. They just needed everyone to know what happens to people who testify." Junie drank her tea. It had gone bitter. "So now I run cargo that doesn't ask questions, in a boat nobody wants, and I tell myself that's freedom."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. The stove hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the flood licked higher against the warehouse wall.

"Elias told me something, before he died," Hazel said at last. "He said the syndicate doesn't take things from people. It makes people give them up—piece by piece—until there's nothing left worth taking." Her eyes came up, firelit and steady. "I've been giving them pieces for six years. My father's debt. I keep thinking if I stay small enough, quiet enough, forgettable enough, they'll leave me the last piece."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," Hazel said. "Not anymore."

Junie nodded slowly, and found that some tight thing in her chest had loosened another turn. Two guarded women, one small fire, and the truth passed between them like a tool—here, hold this, I trust you with the weight of it.

* * *

Junie woke past midnight to a sound that did not belong.

Rain, yes. The warehouse groaning, yes. But beneath it all, far off across the flooded plain, the low diesel throb of a heavy launch under power.

She lay still on the pallets, heart counting, and listened. The sound grew. Held its course. Grew again.

Hazel was awake too—Junie could see it in the sudden stillness of her breathing. Neither of them spoke. They didn't need to. In the blackness under the tarpaulin, they did the same arithmetic at the same moment: the Kestrel was beached in plain view of the water, her silhouette unmistakable against the pale strip of the flooded field, and the launch was coming up the channel that passed not two hundred yards from the dock.

Junie rolled silently off the pallets and crawled to the broken window at the front of the warehouse, pressing herself below the sill. Hazel came beside her. Through the gap in the rotted frame, they watched.

The launch appeared out of the rain like a rumor—a long, low shape running without lights, its wake a pale scar across the dark water. Twin diesels, armored wheelhouse, a searchlight racked but unlit. Syndicate hull. Junie knew the class; she'd helped overhaul two of them in a previous life. It came on steadily, unhurried, the way men travel when they own the river and know it.

And then, horribly, it slowed.

The engines dropped to an idle, and the launch swung inward, toward the drowned dock, toward the pale shape of the beached Kestrel, and Junie heard Hazel stop breathing altogether.

Fifty yards. Forty. Close enough now to see figures on the deck, motionless, faces turned toward the shore. Close enough to hear voices carry flat across the water—one voice above the others, level and unhurried, giving quiet orders. Junie knew that voice. It had haunted a courtroom and every job she'd taken since.

Corliss Hale.

The searchlight flickered—once, twice—and swept lazily across the flooded plain. The beam crawled up the bank, through the alders, across the collapsed veranda of the old post, and stopped on the warehouse wall not ten feet from the broken window where two women lay flattened against the mud-slick floorboards, not daring to breathe.

The light held there. Five seconds. Ten. Rain streamed through the broken window and pooled against Junie's cheek, and she stared into the white blaze and thought, with strange clarity: *he can't see us. The glass is gone. He's looking at darkness inside darkness.*

The voice on the deck said something Junie couldn't catch. A short laugh from one of the others. And then the searchlight swung away, the engines climbed, and the launch gathered itself back out into the channel and ran on upriver, its wake unrolling behind it like a signature on a page.

For a long time neither woman moved. The rain filled the silence back in, drop by drop.

"He knows this stretch," Hazel whispered finally. "He slowed down because he knows this stretch. He was checking the dock."

"He was checking for us," Junie said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "And tomorrow he'll know we're behind him, not ahead. Which means—" she paused, working it through, hating the shape of it, "—which means whatever's waiting up the Sable Fork, he's already there. Or he will be by the time we arrive."

Hazel sat back against the wall, rain-dark hair plastered to her face, and in the faint gray light Junie saw her expression settle into something hard and clear. Not fear. Something past fear, on the far side of it.

"Then we leave before first light," Hazel said. "And we beat him there."

Out in the dark, the flood rose another inch against the warehouse wall, patient as everything else in the Rivergate—and somewhere upriver, closing on the claim like a noose, Corliss Hale's engines throbbed on through the rain.
