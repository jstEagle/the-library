## 9. Trading Post Ghosts

The rain let up an hour before dawn, as if it too had grown tired of the argument.

Hazel stood in the doorway of what had once been the trading post's counting room and watched the water settle back into its banks. The Sable Fork had dropped a hand's width overnight—Junie had marked it with a knife-scratch on a piling—and in the gray half-light the drowned trading post emerged around them like something surfacing from a bad dream: a sagging veranda, a roof caved in at one corner, a signboard hanging by a single chain that still read *OKELLO & SONS — GENERAL GOODS* in paint gone the color of old bone.

The Kestrel sat beached against the loading dock, patched hull gleaming wet where Junie had hammered tin over the split planking. She was under the tarp now, asleep sitting up with her cap over her eyes and her hands still curled around a wrench she hadn't put down. She'd worked until two in the morning by lantern light, swearing at the river in three languages, and Hazel hadn't had the heart to wake her.

So it was Hazel who went into the post.

Not for anything in particular. That was the lie she told herself. The truth was simpler and worse: she couldn't sleep either, and Elias Vann was buried behind her cabin four hundred miles north, and every hour she spent not thinking about him felt like a small theft.

The front door had swollen shut years ago. She went in through the loading bay instead, stepping over a rotted crate of sardine tins that had burst and fused into the floorboards, and stood a moment while her lantern pushed back the dark.

Trading posts were all the same in the interior. A long counter, shelves to the ceiling, a woodstove gone to rust, and somewhere in the back—a ledger room. Every post kept one. Licenses, manifests, correspondence, the paper trail of a hundred small businesses that the river had eventually swallowed. Okello & Sons had operated this stretch of the Fork for thirty years before the flood took it, and floods didn't burn paper if the paper was tucked away right.

She found the ledger room behind a collapsed shelf of lamp oil, the door hanging open on a room no bigger than a pantry. Dry. Or dry enough. Someone—Okello himself, maybe, or his sons—had stacked the filing boxes off the floor on bricks, and the flood had come up to within an inch of them and stopped, as though even the river had some respect for records.

Hazel set her lantern on a box and started pulling files.

* * *

She told herself she was looking for nothing. Just passing time. Just keeping her hands busy so her mind wouldn't do the work of remembering Elias's face at dawn, the way he'd gripped her wrist with the last strength he had and said *Mara* like it was a prayer and an apology both.

The first box was freight manifests, water-spotted but legible. The second was tax correspondence, dull as gravel. The third—

The third box was labeled in faded ink: **VANN, E. — CORRESPONDENCE & FILINGS — HELD FOR CLAIMANT**.

Her hands stopped moving.

*Held for claimant.* Somebody at this post had been holding Elias's papers for him. Holding them through two rejections, through whatever years had passed since he'd last come downriver, keeping them safe the way you keep a dead man's mail because you can't bear to throw it out.

She lifted the box onto her knees and opened it, and the smell of old oilcloth and older ink rose up to meet her, and for a moment she was back in her cabin with Elias dying by inches on her cot, and her throat closed so tight she had to breathe through her nose, slow, like flying through turbulence.

The top of the stack was a registration filing. Official form, Rivergate Territorial Land Office, stamped across the face in red:

**REJECTED — INSUFFICIENT WITNESS OF STAKE.**

Dated eleven years ago.

Beneath it, a second filing. Same claim number. Same coordinates—the same coordinates that waited three days up the flooded Fork inside the oilcloth map folded against her ribs. This one stamped:

**REJECTED — CLAIM CONTESTED BY PRIOR INTEREST. RIVERGATE CONSOLIDATED MINING CO.**

And beneath that, a third document, and this one wasn't a form at all. It was a letter, typed on syndicate letterhead, signed by a name Hazel recognized from the newspapers she'd read with her father's debt pinned to the icebox door: **RIVERGATE CONSOLIDATED MINING CO. — OFFICE OF THE SUPERINTENDENT**. The language was polite the way a knife is polite. *Your client's stake is noted. Your client's evidence is insufficient. We trust your office will act accordingly.*

We trust your office will act accordingly.

They'd leaned on the land office itself. Not bribed a clerk, not forged a signature—leaned on the whole institution, the way you'd lean on a fence post, casual, certain it would hold. And it had held. Twice. Elias Vann had staked a genuine vein, filed honest papers, done everything the law asked of him, and the law had looked at him, looked at the syndicate, and looked away.

Hazel sat down hard on a filing box that groaned under her.

She'd known. Of course she'd known—that was why Hale was on the river, why men had burned Elias's camp, why the map was worth killing for. Knowing a thing in the abstract was one animal. Holding the proof in your lap, feeling the weight of the paper, reading the red stamps in your own lantern light—that was another animal entirely. It had teeth.

Her fear was still there. She could feel it sitting in her chest like ballast. But underneath it, rising through it, something hotter was coming up, something she recognized from the worst year of her life, the year the syndicate's collectors had sat in her mother's kitchen drinking tea they hadn't brought and talking about her father's "obligations" in voices full of patience.

Anger. Old anger, banked for six years, catching at last.

"You son of a bitch," she said quietly, to the superintendent whose name was signed at the bottom of the letter, to the clerk who'd stamped the rejection, to the whole tidy machine of it. "You knew. You all knew."

The papers didn't answer. They just lay there being true.

* * *

She found the letters beneath the filings, bundled in string, and she almost didn't open them.

There is a privacy to the dead that Hazel had always respected. She'd flown enough medevacs, carried enough bodies, delivered enough final effects to widows with steady hands and shaking eyes, to know that some things belong to the grief alone. But these letters were addressed to Mara Vann, and Mara Vann was the reason Hazel was sitting in a drowned building a thousand miles from anywhere with a syndicate enforcer on her tail, and she owed it to the girl—not to Elias, to the girl—to know what she was carrying downriver besides a map.

She untied the string.

Fourteen letters. None sent. Each one addressed in Elias's blunt carpenter's hand: *Miss Mara Vann, c/o Mrs. Ede Ferro, Boarding House, Lower Quayside, Port Amiel.* The earliest was dated nine years ago. The most recent was dated five months before his death.

Hazel read them all. She read them by lantern light in a ruined ledger room while the river breathed against the pilings outside, and by the fourth letter she had stopped pretending her eyes were dry.

He wasn't a writer. The sentences came out crooked, over-explained, the handwriting of a man who thought better with his hands. He told Mara about the weather. About the price of beans. About a dog he'd adopted at a waystation and lost again inside a month. He never mentioned gold. Never mentioned the claim, except once, obliquely, in the seventh letter: *I've found what I was looking for, girl. Nine years I've been looking and it was there the whole time, and when it's mine proper I'm coming down to Port Amiel and I'm going to stand in your doorway and you can tell me to my face whether you'll have me back.*

And then, in the margins, in smaller writing, the things he couldn't say straight out:

*I was wrong about your mother. I was wrong about a lot.*

*I don't expect forgiveness. I'm telling you where I am so you know there was never a day I didn't think of you.*

*If anything happens to me, ask at Okello's post on the Sable Fork. Mr. Okello has held my papers true.*

The last letter was short. Dated five months back.

*Mara. There are men asking after me. Nothing for you to worry on—they can't reach past the Fork—but if a stranger comes asking you questions, you tell them nothing and you write to me care of the Alder Flats cache. I mean to see this through and then I'm coming home. — Your father, who was a fool, and is sorry, and loves you.*

*Your father.*

Hazel folded the letter along its worn creases and held it a long time without reading it again.

Five months ago, Elias had known they were coming. Had known the syndicate had caught the scent of the vein, and instead of running, instead of selling, instead of doing any of the ten sensible cowardly things a man in his position might do—he'd written to his daughter to warn her, and stayed on his claim to guard it. And they'd come anyway. And burned his camp. And shot him in the gut and left him in the alder flats to die alone, which he would have, if a forgettable bush pilot with a debt and a guilty conscience hadn't happened to be crossing that stretch of sky.

They hadn't just killed a prospector. They'd murdered a man who was trying to go home.

Hazel set the letters down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and stood up, and found that her hands had stopped shaking.

That was the strange part. Six days on the river she'd been afraid—afraid of Hale, afraid of the water, afraid of the clock ticking toward monsoon's end—and the fear had been a load she hauled everywhere, like fuel drums. Now she stood in the wrecked counting room of a drowned trading post holding a dead man's rejected dreams in her hands, and the fear had drained out through the floorboards and left something else standing in her, something level and cold and very clear.

This wasn't about gold anymore. It had never been about gold. Elias hadn't traded her the map for a share; he'd traded it for a promise, and the promise wasn't *get rich*, it was *make it true*. Make the record show what actually happened. Put his daughter's name on that vein in ink the syndicate couldn't lean on fast enough to erase.

Justice, then. Small-j justice, registry-stamp justice, the only kind the interior ever got. But justice.

"Mr. Okello," she said aloud to the empty room, "you held his papers true. I'm going to hold the rest."

She gathered the filings, the syndicate letter, and all fourteen unsent letters, wrapped them in a square of oilcloth she tore from the shelving lining, and tied it with the same string. Then she paused, looked around the little room one more time—at the boxes on their bricks, at the inch-line of high water ghosted on the wall—and took the entire VANN box as well. If the river rose again tonight, Elias's history wasn't going to drown twice.

* * *

Junie was awake when Hazel came back down to the dock, sitting on the Kestrel's gunwale with a mug of chicory substitute steaming in one hand and the wrench still lying beside her like a pet she'd decided not to kick. Her eyes went straight to the oilcloth bundle under Hazel's arm.

"Find something?"

Hazel climbed aboard and sat across from her, and for a moment neither woman spoke. The river ran brown and patient past the hull. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered—the monsoon gathering itself for another day's work.

"Elias's registration filings," Hazel said. "Both rejections. And a letter from the syndicate telling the land office to reject them." She unwrapped the bundle far enough to show the red stamps. "They didn't beat him to the claim, Junie. They didn't jump it fair. They owned the paperwork. Eleven years ago they reached into the land office and made his claim disappear on a stamp."

Junie took the papers. She read slowly, her lips moving, and Hazel watched the engineer's face do what faces did when a person's worst suspicion turned out to have been an understatement. Junie had testified against the syndicate once. She knew exactly how far those fingers reached.

"They rejected him twice," Junie said at last. Her voice was flat in the way Hazel had learned meant the opposite of calm. "Twice. And then they burned his camp rather than let him file a third time."

"And killed him."

"And killed him." Junie handed the papers back with exaggerated care, the way you hand back a loaded weapon. "You understand what you're carrying now. That's not a map anymore. That's evidence. Hale doesn't want the gold, Hazel—he wants those stamps and that letter off the face of the earth, because if they surface with a registered claim attached, it's not just Elias's name they're clearing. It's every claim they've ever jumped the same way. There'll be others. There are always others."

"I know."

"Do you?" Junie leaned forward, rain-damp braid dripping onto the deck boards. "Because there's a version of this where we run the Fork, register the claim, collect our share, and disappear, and I signed on for that version. What you're holding now says there's another version where we make noise. Noise gets people dead. I've seen it."

Hazel looked at the bundle in her lap. Fourteen letters to a girl in Port Amiel who probably believed her father had forgotten she existed. Two red stamps that said otherwise. One promise, sworn over a dying man's grip.

"There's a third version," Hazel said. "Where we do both. Register the claim in Mara's name, and file the rejections alongside it, public record, permanent. Let the syndicate spend the next ten years explaining eleven-year-old ink." She met Junie's eyes. "Elias asked me to make it true. All of it true."

Junie studied her for a long moment. Then she snorted, picked up her mug, and drained it.

"Course you are," she said. "Fine. But if we're carrying evidence, we change how we run the river. No radio calls, no waystations, no friendly faces. Straight up the Fork, quiet as driftwood." She jerked her chin downstream, toward the gray seam where the Sable Fork met the main channel. "Which means one problem. The junction at Karu Bend. Syndicate keeps a toll station there—manifest checks, 'river safety fees,' the usual shakedown. We can't shoot it and we can't sneak past it in daylight. So we talk our way through."

"You?"

"I've talked my way through worse." Junie stood, stretched until her spine cracked, and began coiling the mooring line with quick, economical turns. "Get below and stow your ghosts somewhere soft, pilot. We cast off in twenty minutes, and I want us at Karu Bend by mid-morning—before whoever's hunting us figures out we stopped here at all."

Hazel went below, lashed the oilcloth bundle flat against the hull stringer where no search would find it, and lay back on the bunk with the engine's first coughs shuddering through the planks.

Outside, the rain began again, soft at first, then harder, drumming on the tarpaulin like fingers counting down. Three days up the Fork lay a cairn and a vein and a name waiting to be written. Between here and there stood a toll station, a rising river, and Corliss Hale.

Hazel closed her eyes and saw, not the map, not the gold, but a boarding house doorway in Port Amiel, and a girl opening fourteen letters all at once.

"Hold on, Mara," she murmured to the drumming rain. "We're coming."

Above her, Junie gunned the Kestrel off the dock and into the current, and the drowned trading post slid away astern, taking its ghosts with it—all but the ones Hazel carried now, deliberately, like cargo too precious to leave behind.
