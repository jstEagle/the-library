## 10. The Toll Bridge

Junie saw the toll station before Hazel did, which was fitting, since it was going to be her problem.

The Sable Fork junction came at them out of the rain like a bad memory: a timber gantry straddling the channel where the main river split, lanterns burning yellow behind oilcloth windows, and a boom of chained logs stretched across the water with all the subtlety of a hand across a doorway. Above it, on stilts, sat the station house itself—two rooms and a watch platform, syndicate gray even under the monsoon's wash, the company's black diamond stenciled on the roof where any pilot or boatman could read it from a mile off.

"Junie." Hazel had come forward from the bow, map case strapped flat against her ribs under her slicker. Her voice was level in the way Junie was learning meant the opposite. "That wasn't on the chart."

"Charts are three years old. Tolls aren't." Junie eased off the throttle until the Kestrel was barely holding way, engine muttering below them like something sulking in its sleep. "Syndicate put stations on every junction after the strike broke. Toll, they call it. Manifest inspection, they call it when they're feeling legal. It's a filter. Everything that moves on this river moves through their hands now, or doesn't move."

"How many men?"

"Three, usually. Maybe four if the weather's kept them in." Junie squinted through the streaming windshield. One figure on the platform under an umbrella that had lost its argument with the wind. A second moving along the boom walkway, oilskin gleaming. A third—she could just make him out—sitting in the station window with a rifle laid across his knees like he was bored of holding it. "Four."

Hazel was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the cabin roof, on the deck, on everything, a sound so constant it had stopped being weather and become the world's own pulse.

"They're looking for us," she said. Not a question.

"They're looking for a leaking riverboat with two women aboard, one of them a disgraced engineer and the other a bush pilot who flew Elias Vann out of the Alder Flats the week his camp burned down." Junie wiped her palm down her thigh. "So yes. I'd say we've been discussed."

* * *

They anchored in the drowned willows a half-mile upstream, tucked into a screen of green that the flood had turned into a room without walls. Junie killed the engine and listened to the rain and to her own heart, which was doing something undignified.

Here was the thing about Junie Okonkwo that the syndicate had never understood, the thing that got her blacklisted instead of broken: she did not freeze. Fear came to her the way water came into a hull—it found the seams, it rose, it pressed—but she'd spent six years on these rivers learning that a hull only sank if you let it fill unattended. You pumped. You patched. You kept bailing until you reached shore.

She was bailing now.

"The manifests," she said, and dug them out of the chart drawer beneath the wheel: a sheaf of carbon-copy freight declarations, syndicate-printed, most of them legitimately hers from legitimate runs in the years before the trial. She'd kept them because paper was paper and paper could be made to say things. "We're the Kestrel, running machine parts and diesel compound upriver to the Marlowe dredge camp. That run is real. I did it twice last season before they pulled my ticket. The foreman there, Okafor, still owes me money and hates the syndicate more than he fears them, which makes him the rarest kind of man on this river."

"And me?"

"You're my hired deckhand and you don't talk unless someone talks to you first, and then you talk like a woman who's cold, wet, and not paid enough." Junie looked up at her. "Can you do stupid?"

Hazel's mouth twitched. "I've been doing forgettable for five years. Stupid should be within reach."

"Forgettable won't work here. They'll have your description. Forgettable is what gets you remembered—the woman trying too hard to be nobody." Junie tapped the manifest. "Stupid is safe. Stupid is invisible. Nobody writes down the name of a deckhand who complains about her boots."

"And the map?"

That was the question, wasn't it. They both looked at it. Hazel had already unwrapped it from its oilcloth back at the drowned trading post, and it lay between them now on the chart table: Elias Vann's careful ink lines on waxed linen, the Sable Fork drawn in his own hand with depths and landmarks no surveyor had ever bothered to record, and at the head of it, circled twice, the cairn. Three days up. Two, if the flood ran kind. Less than that if the permits were to mean anything.

A syndicate inspector finding that on board would end more than the trip. It would end Elias's daughter's inheritance, end the promise, end whatever it was that had started growing between the two of them in this leaking boat—because Junie had no illusions about what happened to people who got caught helping. She'd testified once, honestly, in daylight, believing the law would hold. The law had held—for the syndicate.

"We can't hide it in the freight," Hazel said. "They'll turn the cargo."

"They'll turn the cargo, the bilge, the mattress stuffing, and my spare clothes, because Corliss Hale does nothing halfway and whoever he left at this station will have orders to match." Junie turned the compass over in her hands. Her father's compass—no, Hazel's father's compass, though Hazel had handed it to her an hour ago without explanation, which in the language they'd been building between them meant *I trust you with the part of me I don't talk about.*

It was a heavy brass thing, old-style, the lid dished and scarred, the hinge long since replaced with a leather strap. And it was bigger inside than it needed to be. Junie had noticed that the first night, turning it over by lantern light while Hazel slept: beneath the needle card there was a false bottom, a brass plate sitting proud of where the machining said it should sit, a gap of nearly half an inch all around.

Somebody had built this compass to carry something. Somebody had used it before.

"Hazel," she said slowly. "Your father didn't just fly contraband. He flew *secrets*. This is a courier's instrument."

Hazel went very still, the way she did when something touched the place she kept her father buried. Then she nodded, once, and took the compass back, and did something to the strap-mount at the base that Junie hadn't seen—a quarter-turn, a lift—and the whole bottom plate came away clean.

The map, folded to the size of a playing card, fit inside with room for a prayer book.

* * *

They rehearsed it twice, then three times, Junie playing the inspector with a boredom so accurate it made Hazel laugh despite everything, and then there was nothing left to do but go and do it, which was the worst part of any plan, the part where it stopped being yours and started being the river's.

Junie brought the Kestrel around and opened the throttle to a working cruise—not hurrying, not dawdling, the speed of a woman with a delivery schedule and a grievance against the rain. The junction grew. The boom grew. The man on the platform raised a signal lamp, yellow, yellow, red, and Junie cut to idle and let the current carry them the last hundred yards like a fish being played to the net.

"Ahoy the boat!" The voice came off the boom walkway, young, carrying that particular authority of a man whose gun was doing the talking. "Stand off the boom. Kill your engine."

Junie killed it. The Kestrel drifted, kissed the logs with a wooden knock, and a boat hook caught their rail and held them against the boom while the rain came down and down.

He was younger than she'd expected, twenty maybe, syndicate gray slicker, a carbine slung rather than carried, which she chose to read as good news. Behind him on the platform stood the older one, the one with the lamp, and through the station window the rifleman hadn't moved except to lean his weapon somewhere it could be reached.

"Name and business." Young one again. He had a clipboard under his slicker and was fighting it open against the wet.

"Kestrel, out of Rivergate, bound for the Marlowe dredge camp." Junie pitched her voice right: tired, competent, mildly insulted by the whole procedure. "Machine parts and diesel compound, nine crates, declared and sealed. You want the manifest, it's on the clipboard under my chart roll, because unlike some people I keep my paper dry."

Something flickered in the young one's face—the flicker of a man who'd been braced for fear and gotten attitude instead. He took the manifest she held up. Read it. Read it again, slower, and Junie watched him check the header stamp, the consignee line, the syndicate freight seal she'd forged with a warmed knife and a steady hand at the trading post, and she smiled at him with the whole history of six years of honest paperwork behind it.

"This run's not on the schedule," he said.

"No, it's not, is it." She let that hang. "You want to radio Rivergate and ask why the Marlowe camp's dredges have been sitting dead for eleven days? You want to explain to the district office that you held up their repair parts to ask a boatwoman questions in the rain?" She jerked her chin at the manifest. "Page two. Authorization reference. Call it in if you like. I'll wait. I've got nowhere to be that isn't already underwater."

It was a gamble stacked on a bluff stacked on a truth—Okafor's camp really was late on a parts shipment; she'd heard it on the band at the trading post—and she watched the young inspector weigh it, watched the older one on the platform come down the stairs to look over his shoulder, and she kept her face set in the expression of a woman who has been delayed by idiots before and expects to be again.

"Search protocol says—" the young one began.

"Protocol says inspect inbound freight at junction stations, yes, I've read your protocol, I used to ship under it." Junie stepped back from the rail and spread her arms slightly. "So inspect. Crates are forward, seals intact, crowbar's mine if you break something you're paying for. Cargo hold's aft, and mind the bilge, it's been raining since the creation of the world. Deckhand's aboard—she'll show you anything you want to see."

And that was the moment, the hinge of the whole rotten day, when Hazel climbed up out of the cabin onto the deck.

Junie had told her: stupid, cold, unpaid. What Hazel gave them was better than acting. She came up the ladder stiffly, hunched into the rain, squinting at the inspectors with the flat resentment of somebody whose nap had been interrupted, and said, "If you're going to open the hold, tell me now so I can move the tarp off my bunk, because I am not sleeping in a puddle so a man with a clipboard can feel important."

The older inspector actually laughed. Short, surprised out of him. "Where'd you find this one?"

"Dockside. She flies planes worse than she mops decks." Junie shrugged. "You take who the river gives you."

They searched. Of course they searched—that was the point of them—and Junie stood at the wheel with her arms folded and her pulse ticking in her throat while the young one pried at crate lids and the older one swung a lamp through the hold, over the bilge, under the bunk, through the chart drawer, through the galley shelf where the tinned food sat in ranks. Hazel sat on the engine housing with her elbows on her knees and watched them with dull, patient eyes, and only Junie knew that under that dullness her right hand was resting on the pocket of her slicker where the compass rode, heavy as a heartbeat, the map folded inside it smaller than a letter never sent.

The young one checked the manifest against the crates. Nine crates, nine lines. He wrote something. The older one came back up the ladder, dripping, and stood a moment looking at the two women with the particular gaze of a man being paid to be suspicious and finding nothing to spend it on.

"Vann," he said suddenly.

Junie's blood went to ice water. She kept her shoulders loose. "What about him?"

"Elias Vann. Prospector. There's a notice out—any vessel, report sightings." He was looking at Hazel as he said it, mild, almost apologetic. "You pick anyone up out there? Swimmers, castaways, old men?"

"River's full of bodies this time of year," Hazel said, in exactly the voice of a deckhand who wanted her bunk back. "None of them asked to come aboard."

A beat. Two. The rain filled them.

"All right." The older one folded the clipboard shut. "Passage granted. Boom drops on my mark—you'll want way on before she swings. And boat-woman—" this to Junie, almost friendly, "—Marlowe camp's past the second bend. If your parts don't fix their dredge, that's between you and Okafor."

"It always is," Junie said.

The chain rattled. The boom of logs sagged, dipped, and began to swing downstream on its pivot, opening the channel like a slow eye. Junie hit the starter, the Kestrel coughed, caught, muttered, caught properly, and they slid through the gap with the current taking them, past the station, past the rifleman in the window who never even looked up, past the black diamond on the roof dissolving into the rain behind them.

* * *

For a full mile neither of them spoke. Junie held the channel down the middle of the flooded Fork, hands light on the wheel, eyes doing their work, and beside her Hazel stood at the windshield without moving, and the only sounds were the engine and the rain and the small percussive conversation of the hull with the water.

Then Hazel took out the compass, opened the hidden plate, drew out the map, unfolded it, checked it—every line, every annotation, the cairn circled twice—and folded it away again, and let out a breath that seemed to have been held since the Alder Flats.

"You called him Vann," she said. "Before he said the name. How?"

"I didn't. He said *Vann*, and I answered a question he didn't ask." Junie allowed herself a grin, wide and slightly unhinged, the grin that came after. "That's the trick, Hazel. Never lie first. Let them lie to themselves and just—don't correct them."

"That was—" Hazel shook her head. "That was the single worst hour of my life, and I once landed a plane in a hailstorm with a bleeding stranger in the back seat."

"Wait until the rapids." Junie laughed then, really laughed, giddy and loud and helpless, the relief coming out of her like steam, and after a second Hazel laughed too, both of them howling like fools into the rain while the Kestrel ran on up the Sable Fork with a forged manifest in the drawer and a dead man's claim folded inside a dead man's compass and four days, maybe less, on the permit clock.

Hazel wiped her eyes. "Second bend to the Marlowe camp. We should probably actually pass it, in case anyone checks."

"We'll pass it. We'll even wave at it." Junie steadied the wheel as the river bent north, and the grin faded into something quieter, something that felt almost like momentum. "Because here's what that boy with the clipboard told us without knowing it, Hazel. There's a notice out on Vann—*sightings*, not cargo. Which means Hale doesn't know yet whether the map survived him. He's fishing. Every station on this river is a hook in the water, and we just swam past one."

"So we keep swimming."

"So we keep swimming." Junie pointed the bow upriver, toward the second bend, toward the high country where the Fork narrowed and the water began, faintly, to make a different sound. "But swim fast. Because the next thing on this river that asks us questions isn't going to carry a clipboard."

Ahead, through the rain, the Sable Fork lifted white against the darkening hills—the first rumor of the rapids they'd have to run, or beat, before the claim was theirs to save.
