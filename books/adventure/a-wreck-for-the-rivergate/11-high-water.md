## 11. High Water

The rain had been falling for six hours before Hazel understood it wasn't rain anymore.

She stood in the Kestrel's stern under a tarpaulin that had stopped pretending to keep anything dry, watching the Sable Fork climb its banks like something escaping a cage. An hour ago the water had been brown and fast. Now it was brown and *wrong*—full of whole trees turning end over end, of fence posts and a roof and what might have been a canoe or might have been a coffin lid, all of it moving at a speed that made her stomach tighten. The river didn't rise like water. It rose like a decision already made.

"Junie," she called forward. "Junie, look at the cutbank."

Junie Okonkwo was elbow-deep in the engine housing, coaxing the old petrol motor through its third sputtering fit of the morning. She straightened, wiped her hands on trousers that had given up being any color but river-mud, and looked.

The cutbank on their left—the one they'd been using as a landmark since dawn—was gone. Not eroded. Gone. A slab of earth the size of a barn had simply detached and slid into the current, and where it had been there was now nothing but churning yellow water chewing at fresh roots.

"That's not a bank anymore," Junie said quietly. "That's a suggestion."

Hazel had grown up around rivers the way some people grow up around stoves—close enough to be burned, careful enough never to show it. But this was different. This was the monsoon doing what Elias Vann's letters had warned about, what every old-timer at Pier Nine had mentioned with a shrug: the Fork didn't flood gradually. It *jumped*. Somewhere upstream, hours away, a wall of water had been gathering behind a logjam or a collapsed gorge, and when it let go, everything downstream became arithmetic.

"How fast can we run?" she asked.

"Faster than we can think," Junie said. "Which is the problem." She dropped back into the engine well and threw herself at the fuel line, which had developed a leak somewhere near the filter—a slow weep of precious petrol into the bilge, wasted drop by drop while the sky emptied itself over them without rationing anything. "We need high ground. Now. Not in an hour. Now."

Hazel scrambled to the bow and unrolled the map from its oilcloth, weighting the corners with the compass case and a tin cup. The Fork braided ahead of them through country the mapmaker had rendered as blank green—an honest admission that nobody who drew maps came out here in the wet season. There was one mark, though. A hatched triangle on a rise above a side slough, maybe two miles down, labeled in faded ink: *Wren's Bluff — stock pen, hay shed.*

"Two miles," she said. "There's a bluff with a stock pen."

"Two miles is a long way when the river's making up rules," Junie said. But she opened the throttle anyway, and the Kestrel leaned into the current like a tired animal asked for one more mile.

* * *

They made a mile and a half before the flood caught them.

It didn't arrive. It *arrived*—there was no other word for it. One moment the Kestrel was bucking through fast water, and the next the whole river seemed to inhale, draw back, and then come down on them sideways. Hazel heard it before she saw it: a sound like a train taking a curve too fast, low and grinding and getting louder with a speed that made thinking impossible. Then the leading edge hit them—not a wave but a *shoulder*, a rolling mass of debris-choked water three feet higher than what had been there a second before—and the boat went up its face and slammed down the other side so hard Hazel's teeth clicked together and the map tore half out of her hands.

"Hold her!" Junie screamed, wrestling the tiller. "Hold her into it!"

Hazel held. It wasn't skill; it was the animal refusal to do anything else. For ten minutes—she would remember them later as ten minutes, though they could have been two or twenty—the Kestrel surfed a river that had stopped being a place and become an event. Trees passed them going faster than they were. Something struck the hull below the waterline with a boom that Hazel felt in her spine, and Junie's face went white in a way that had nothing to do with fear of drowning and everything to do with the patched hull they'd nursed since the drowned trading post.

Then, abruptly, the main channel spat them out.

The flood had done what floods do: it had found the path of least resistance, and the path of least resistance was not the Sable Fork's proper channel but a side slough to the south, a low corridor between two ridges that the map showed only as a blue thread and the river now treated as its new favorite road. The Kestrel shot into it sideways, spun twice, and fetched up against a wall of willows with a crack of branches that sounded almost apologetic.

Silence, except for rain and engine and Hazel's own heartbeat in her ears.

"Are we—" she started.

"We're alive," Junie said. "We're also forty miles from where we're supposed to be, pointed the wrong direction, and I don't want to tell you what the fuel gauge says." She looked at the gauge. It told anyway. A quarter. Maybe less, if the leak had been worse than she'd thought. "A quarter. In a flood. With a day lost already."

Hazel sat down hard on the supply crate and put her head between her knees, which was not dignified and did not help, and then lifted it because dignity could wait but ears couldn't—because underneath the rain, underneath the engine, she'd begun to hear something else.

A voice. Thin, cracked, human. Calling across the water.

* * *

Junie heard it too, and for a moment neither woman moved, because the instinct they shared—the instinct that had kept them both alive through years of keeping their heads down—said *keep moving, help is someone else's job, you have a schedule and a pursuer and a quarter tank.*

Then Hazel looked at the compass lying on the crate between them, her father's compass, the one that had pointed him toward contraband runs and debt and the bottom of whatever stretch of water he'd gone into, and she thought about what it had never once pointed toward: anywhere worth arriving.

"There," she said, and pointed.

Maybe three hundred yards off, where the flooded slough spread wide and shallow over what had been pasture, a roofline stuck out of the water at an angle like a hat knocked askew. And on that roof—clinging to the ridge beam, four small figures and one large one—was a family.

They reached the house by poling, engine off, because the water between them and the roof was full of submerged fencing and the last thing the Kestrel needed was another hole. Junie poled from the bow with a broken oar; Hazel bailed with a bucket and steered with the other oar, and the rain came down with the patience of something that intended to go on forever.

The family was a woman, gray-streaked hair plastered to her skull, holding a baby wrapped in oilskin; two boys, maybe ten and twelve, barefoot; and an old man with a rifle slung across his back who watched them approach with the flat wariness of someone who had learned what strangers cost.

"Ma'am," Hazel called, "we've got room."

"We've got nothing to pay," the woman said.

"We're not collecting."

The old man said something in a language Hazel didn't know, short and decisive, and after that the argument was over. The boys came first, leaping for the gunwale with the boneless competence of children raised on water, then the baby passed hand to hand like something sacred, then the woman, moving stiffly—Hazel saw why when she got her aboard: a gash along her calf, ugly but clean, already bound with strips of her own skirt. The old man came last, pausing on the ridge beam to look back at his house as if memorizing it, and then he stepped down into their boat and the roof of his life slid away behind them into the brown water without ceremony.

His name, the woman said, was Dario Ferro. Hers was Amaya. The boys were Tomás and Rafael. The baby had no name yet—that was the thing Amaya said that broke something open in Hazel's chest, delivered flatly, mid-crisis, as if it were ordinary: *she isn't christened, we were waiting for the dry season.* They were cattle people, or had been; the drowned pasture had been theirs, and the stock pen on Wren's Bluff had been where they'd meant to drive the herd before the river decided otherwise.

Hazel stitched the gash in Amaya's leg with the med kit she carried for exactly one passenger these days, working by lantern light under the tarpaulin while the boat drifted with the current and Junie held station against the flood. Amaya didn't flinch. She watched Hazel's hands and said, "You've done this before."

"Once or twice."

"For money?"

"No," Hazel said, and found she meant it as a kind of answer to a larger question.

* * *

They spent the night wedged in the flooded crown of a giant fig on high ground above the slough—Dario's suggestion, delivered with gestures and three words of trade pidgin, and correct, as it turned out, in every particular. The Kestrel tied off to the trunk rode out the worst of it below them, and the seven of them crowded onto a platform of branches that Dario's family had used for exactly this purpose for two generations, a platform with iron rings rusted into the wood and a tin box bolted beneath it containing matches, dried fish, and a bottle of cane spirit that Junie eyed with open longing and was denied until the baby was asleep.

It was Tomás, the older boy, who repaid them.

He spoke English the way river kids do—picked up whole from traders and boatmen, accented and absolute. He had been sitting close to Hazel while she studied the ruined map by lantern light, trying to make sense of where the flood had thrown them, and he had been watching her finger trace the blank green space south of the Fork with the frank curiosity of the young.

"You are lost," he said. Not a question.

"Temporarily mislocated."

"My grandfather knows the back channel." He pointed at Dario, who was sitting at the edge of the platform looking out at the water with the stillness of a man taking inventory of everything he'd lost. "The old channel. Before the company dug the cut for the barges. It goes around the rapids. Nobody uses it because it is shallow—but not now." He grinned, showing a missing tooth. "Now it is very deep."

Hazel went very still. "Show me."

Tomás took the pencil stub she offered and, on the blank margin of Elias Vann's hand-drawn map—which felt, briefly, like sacrilege and then like exactly right—drew a line. It left the flooded slough at a point he marked with an X, ran south along the base of the ridge, bent east through a gap he called *the Needle*, and rejoined the Sable Fork five miles below the rapids. Five miles. Five miles of rapids they would not have to shoot, portage, or die in.

"It floods first and drains first," Tomás said, with the authority of a ten-year-old reciting scripture. "My grandfather says the old channel is the river's memory. The company forgot it. The river didn't."

Across the platform, Dario was watching. When Hazel looked over, he beckoned her close, and through Tomás's translation, in the tone of a man settling accounts, he said: "You stopped. Everyone else goes past. You stopped." He tapped her once on the chest, over the sternum, with two fingers. "So I give you the channel. Fair trade."

Hazel wanted to say it wasn't a trade, that you didn't stop for people because of what they knew. But she had spent three years stopping for no one, and she knew the difference between what she believed and what she'd practiced, and the gap between them was where her father's debt lived. So instead she said, "Fair trade," and shook the old man's hand, and meant it both ways.

* * *

They ran the back channel at first light, and it was everything Tomás promised and stranger.

The old channel was a cathedral. That was the word that came to Hazel and wouldn't leave: walls of forest rising on either side, canopy closing overhead dripping monsoon, the water beneath running deep and strangely quiet after the violence of the flood—a brown mirror between green cliffs of vegetation, the Kestrel's wake the only ripple for miles. Dario stood in the bow and read the water the way Hazel read weather, lifting one hand to signal sandbars that hadn't surfaced yet, and Junie drove the engine soft and steady, and nobody talked much because talking would have broken it.

They put the family ashore at noon on the second day, at a cousin's homestead on the ridge above the Needle—a real house, on real high ground, with goats shouting from the yard and a woman in the doorway already waving before they'd finished tying off. The goodbyes were brief. River people didn't linger. But Amaya caught Hazel's arm at the gangplank, pressed something into her palm, and closed her fingers over it: a small carved figure, wood worn smooth, a bird with folded wings.

"For the boat," she said. "Kestrel. So she comes home too."

Hazel stood on the pier and watched them walk up the hill—Dario upright and unbowed, the boys racing ahead, Amaya carrying the unnamed baby—and felt something shift in her chest that she would spend the rest of the journey failing to name. Kindness as strategy, she'd told herself. The channel was worth a detour; the rescue was worth the channel. Clean arithmetic. Except the arithmetic didn't cover the carved bird in her pocket, or the way Tomás had drawn on Elias's map like the future signing its name over the past, or the fact that for one full day she had not thought about Corliss Hale even once.

"What's the tally?" Junie asked, coming to stand beside her as they cast off. "What did the mercy cost?"

"A day. Half our supplies—we fed seven mouths instead of two. Fuel's down to fumes until we reach the fuel cache at the Needle, if it's still there, if the flood didn't take it." Hazel ticked it off without flinching. "And in exchange: five miles of rapids we don't have to shoot, and—" she touched her pocket "—a patron saint."

Junie snorted. Then, quieter, watching the channel open ahead of them: "My mother used to say grace costs what it costs and pays what it pays, and you never get to audit the books." She opened the throttle. "I hated that. Still hate it. She was right."

The Needle came into view an hour later—a limestone gap barely wider than the boat, water sliding through it green-black and smooth as poured glass. Dario's channel delivered them to the far side of the Devil's Teeth without so much as a whitecap, and Hazel allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to believe the worst was behind them.

Then they rounded the bend below the rapids and saw the smoke.

Not cookfire smoke. Too black, too much of it, standing up straight in the wet air from somewhere ahead—somewhere on their route, in the direction of the only waystation between here and the claim. Beside her, Junie's hands went still on the throttle, and Hazel felt the day's warmth drain out of her like water through a cracked hull.

Nobody sold us, she told herself. Nobody could have sold us. We changed course yesterday.

But the smoke kept rising, patient and certain, and the Kestrel ran toward it with the current's blessing and Hazel's heart going cold.
