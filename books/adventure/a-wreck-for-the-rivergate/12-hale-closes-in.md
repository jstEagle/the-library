## 12. Hale Closes In

The toll station at the Sable Fork junction was built from the same rot as everything else the syndicate owned: green timber gone gray, a tin roof patched with tarps, and a ledger book swollen with damp until its pages felt like flesh. Corliss Hale stood at the counter with his hat in one hand and his patience in the other, and he gave the toll keeper all the time in the world to make a mistake.

The man's name was Dree. He was sixty if he was a day, with a walrus mustache stained amber at the fringe where tobacco juice had been running through it for thirty years, and hands that wouldn't stop moving—squaring papers that didn't need squaring, wiping a counter that was already clean. Hale had watched men like Dree for two decades. The nervous ones talked. The calm ones were dangerous. Dree was the first kind.

"Slow week?" Hale asked pleasantly.

"Monsoon." Dree shrugged. "Nobody runs the Fork in this but fools and smugglers."

"And which did I just miss?"

Dree's hands stopped. That was the tell. Not the pause before it—the stop after, the way a man's body betrays him half a second after his mouth has already decided to lie. Hale set his hat on the counter, unhurried, and smiled the smile that had opened more doors than any crowbar.

"I came up from Pier Nine," Hale said. "Talked to a man who swears he saw a patched riverboat take on fuel at midnight. Green hull, white scar along the portside bow where somebody did ugly work with a welder. Engine sounds like a sewing machine with a grudge. Ring any bells?"

"That describes half the boats on this river."

"It does," Hale agreed. "But only one of them is carrying a woman named Hazel Dunmore."

He watched the name land. Dree's face did something complicated—a flicker of recognition, then a calculation, then the careful blankness of a man deciding how much trouble he could afford. Hale let the silence stretch. He was good at silence. He'd learned it young, learned it in rooms where talking too soon got people hurt, and he had never once unlearned it.

"She came through," Dree said finally. "Yesterday morning. Paid the toll like anybody."

"With what?"

"Coin. What do you think?"

Hale turned the hat slowly on the counter, brim whispering against wood. "And her manifest. She'd have shown you a manifest."

"Standard freight. Medical supplies, dry goods."

"For a medevac pilot running supplies up the Sable Fork." Hale nodded as though this made perfect sense. "Where's the settlement on the Fork, Dree? The one that needs medical supplies?"

"There isn't one."

"No," Hale said softly. "There isn't."

He let the word sit there between them like a coin on a table. Outside, the rain came down in the long gray sheets that hadn't stopped in nine days, drumming the tin roof hard enough to make conversation feel conspiratorial. Through the fogged window he could see the junction itself, brown water braiding around a midstream island, the current carrying whole trees sideways. Somewhere up that water, in a boat held together with rivets and stubbornness, Hazel Dunmore was running his river with Elias Vann's map folded somewhere against her skin.

He'd been patient. He wanted credit for that, even if no one would ever give it. He could have burned her out of her cabin the night he found the fresh grave behind it—two spadefuls of turned earth under the alders, still dark, still soft. He could have put questions to her that don't get answered so much as survived. Instead he'd tipped his hat, thanked her for her time, and flown back downriver to do this properly. Because Vann's map wasn't paper you could tear out of a woman's hands and be done with. It was a claim. A registered claim meant surveyors, assayers, clerks, witnesses. It meant the thing the syndicate had spent eleven years burying coming up out of the ground like a corpse in a flood.

You didn't shoot your way out of that kind of problem. You dug faster.

"Here's what happens now," Hale said, and his voice stayed pleasant, which was the worst thing about it. "You're going to show me your logbook. Every boat through this junction since the rains started. And then you're going to tell me exactly what those women asked you, exactly what they bought, and exactly which channel they took. And when we're finished, you're going to forget my face, because if anyone comes asking whether a tall man in a gray coat stood at your counter, you're going to say no."

Dree swallowed. "And if I say no right now?"

Hale looked at him with genuine, almost fond curiosity. "Then I'll have to decide how much you matter," he said. "I'd rather not decide anything today. It's raining."

* * *

The logbook told him most of what he needed. The Kestrel had come through the junction at 0740 the previous morning, paid double toll in worn coin, taken on nothing but drinking water. But it was the margin note that made Hale go still: *Asked twice about the back channel past Widow's Bend. Told 'em it floods.*

Widow's Bend. The old trading post country. There was a waystation there—had been for forty years, a raised shack on pilings where runners could shelter, patch a hull, wait out weather. Syndicate policy on the back channels was simple: leave them unusable. No maintained shelters, no marked channels, no fuel. Let the jungle eat the routes so every boat had to pass through a syndicate toll gate or drown trying. The waystation at Widow's Bend had survived only because it was too far from anything to bother burning.

Until now.

Hale spread the chart across Dree's counter, weighting the corners with the toll box, a lantern, and his own forearm. His finger traced the route. Junction, up the Sable Fork two days to the rapids they called the Devil's Teeth—and they'd need the waystation to stage a portage around those, because nobody shot the Teeth loaded. Which meant the waystation was where they'd sleep. Where they'd dry their clothes. Where Hazel Dunmore would lay Elias Vann's oilcloth map out flat under a lantern and study it by the light.

He thought about that with a clarity that felt almost tender.

"Dree," he said. "Do you have a radio that works?"

"The syndicate's sets work fine. Mine's got water in it."

"Mine works fine." He carried the set in from the launch himself—a compact transceiver in a scuffed leather case, the property of the Rivergate Consolidated Mining & Concession Company, though the company name had been scraped off the plate years ago on his own instruction. He strung the antenna out the window into the rain, grounded it to the stovepipe, and waited for the set to warm while Dree watched him with the expression of a man watching a snake cross his floor.

The operator at the downriver relay answered on the third call. Hale kept his voice level, unhurried, giving coordinates and instructions the way a man reads a grocery list.

"Waystation at Widow's Bend. Burn it tonight. Take the kerosene stores, soak the pilings, light it from the water side so it drops into the channel. Nothing aboard survives. And nothing ashore either—there'll be papers, charts, maybe a cache. Ash. All of it."

A pause on the line, static chewing the edges. "There's a family camps near there sometimes. Trappers."

"Then burn it early," Hale said, "before anyone's sleeping in it. I'm not paying for funerals."

He signed off and stood looking at the chart a moment longer, at the blue thread of the back channel winding up toward the Devil's Teeth, at the small penciled square that was about to stop existing. Then he rolled the chart, tucked it under his arm, and put his hat back on.

"You've been helpful, Dree."

"I haven't been anything," Dree said. "You read my own book."

"Which is why you're going to live." Hale counted coins onto the counter—not a bribe, exactly, just toll, overpaid, the kind of money that buys silence without ever having to ask for it. "Boats come through here in bad weather all the time. You see so many faces in a season they blur together. That's not lying. That's just eyes getting old."

Dree looked at the coins and didn't touch them. "That pilot," he said. "The woman. She was polite. Asked after my knee, if you can believe it. Bad weather, flooded channel, and she asks after my knee."

Hale paused at the door, rain streaming off the eaves between them.

"They usually are," he said. "Polite. The ones worth chasing."

* * *

He sat in the launch's cabin that night with the rain hammering the canvas top and a cup of coffee gone cold in his hand, and he did not sleep, because sleep was a door he'd nailed shut years ago and some nights the nails held better than others.

Elias Vann.

Eleven years, and the name still landed in Hale's chest like a dropped tool. Everyone else in the syndicate's orbit spoke of Vann as a rumor—a mad prospector with a burned camp and a story about a vein in the high country, the kind of man other prospectors used as a cautionary tale over cards. *Don't end up like Vann.* But Hale had been there. Not at the fire itself—he'd arrived after, boots sinking in ash, the alder flats glowing orange for a quarter mile in every direction, the heat still standing off his face like an open oven door. He'd walked the perimeter of what had been a man's life: the twisted frame of a sluice box, a mule dead in its harness, the stump of a claim post sawn clean through and carried away.

Because that was the job, that night. Not the burning. The burning had been handled by men whose names Hale had made sure he never learned. His job was the paperwork of erasure. Find the claim post. Remove the claim post. Make certain that when Elias Vann crawled out of whatever hole he'd survived in, there would be nothing—no marker, no filing, no witness—to prove he'd ever staked anything at all.

He'd done it. He'd done it well. And then he'd stood at the edge of the firelight and heard, from somewhere up the slope, a man screaming a name over and over into the smoke. Not a woman's name. Not God's. A girl's name. *Mara. Mara.* And Hale had gotten back in his boat and rowed downstream and told his employers the site was clean, and he had never once, in eleven years, said the daughter's name aloud.

He'd told himself the usual things. That Vann knew the risks of staking against the concession. That the vein, if it existed, belonged to whoever had the capital to work it, and the syndicate had the capital, and that was simply the shape of the world. That a man who screamed his daughter's name into a fire had made his choices too, same as everyone.

Some nights the nails held. Tonight they weren't holding.

Because here was the arithmetic that had woken him at three in the morning for a decade: Elias Vann had survived. Had crawled out of the ashes, healed—or half-healed—and spent eleven years quietly, patiently re-staking his claim in secret, filing his papers through intermediaries, getting rejected twice on pressure Hale himself had arranged, and never once going to the newspapers, the magistrates, the union men. Never once saying *they burned me out.* Just waiting. Building his proof. Assay tags in his own hand. A map drawn from memory and verified in blood.

And now Vann was dead in a fresh grave behind a pilot's cabin, and his map was on the river, and his daughter's name was about to enter a registry book, and every year of Hale's careful, patient, well-paid work was standing in a courtroom wearing a black dress and holding a deed.

It wasn't the gold. Hale had never much cared about the gold; gold was the company's religion, not his. What he cared about—the only thing he had left to care about—was that the record stay closed. That the night in the alder flats remain what it had officially always been: an accident, a careless campfire, a madman's misfortune. He was fifty-one years old. He had no wife, no children, no country that would have him. The record was all he was. And Hazel Dunmore, polite little pilot who asked after toll keepers' knees, was sailing upriver with a lit match in her pocket.

He poured the cold coffee out the cabin door and watched the rain take it.

* * *

The relay crackled awake near dawn. Hale had the headset on before the second click.

"Widow's Bend," the operator said. "Done. Went up clean, dropped mostly into the channel. No one hurt. But—" static, then, reluctantly, "—there was sign someone'd been through ahead of us. Fresh mooring lines. They weren't there yet, but they'd planned on it. We took what paper was in the cache. Sending it down with the next run."

"Describe the mooring lines."

"Green hull paint on one of them. Weld scar on the cleat where it chafed."

Hale closed his eyes. So. They were a day behind, maybe less—the Kestrel would reach the burned waystation, find the smoking ruin, and understand that someone had read their route. And frightened, clever people who understood they'd been read did one of two things. They scattered. Or they went to ground and started hunting the leak.

Either way, the clock was now his enemy too. Four days, perhaps five, until the concession permits lapsed with the monsoon. After that, the claim couldn't be filed this season—and a season in the Rivergate was a long time to keep a pilot, an engineer, and a map alive against everything the syndicate could float, fly, or buy.

He keyed the transmitter. "Get me the seaplane at Farrow Field. Tell them weather permitting, I want eyes on the Devil's Teeth by tomorrow noon. And tell them—" he paused, choosing the words with a craftsman's care, "—tell them the pilot flies a yellow-winged monoplane and she'll likely be alone at some point. Alone is when you talk to her. Nobody touches the boat while both of them are aboard. Understood?"

"Understood."

He set the headset down. Through the canvas window the eastern sky was doing its monsoon trick, a bruised gray light leaking under the cloud deck, the river running high and fast and brown as tea. Somewhere up that water, two women were about to find a burned building and start asking each other hard questions.

Good, Hale thought, with a patience that felt almost like affection. Let them turn on each other. Fear did his best work for him; it always had. He'd simply given it a little kindling.

He banked the launch's engine, swung the bow upriver, and followed the flood.
