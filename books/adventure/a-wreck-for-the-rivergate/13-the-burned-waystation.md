## 13. The Burned Waystation

We smelled it before we saw it.

The wind had turned with the morning, swinging around out of the northwest, and it carried the smell upriver to us like a messenger running ahead of bad news—wet char, creosote, and under it something sweeter and worse, the way burned timber goes sour when it's been soaked and set alight twice. Junie caught it first. She was at the wheel with her cap turned backward against the rain, and I watched her nostrils flare the way a deer's ears turn before the shot.

"Smoke," she said.

"It's the monsoon," I said. "Everything's damp."

"Not everything." She cut her eyes toward me, then back to the channel. "Not dry stores. Not lamp oil. Not the tar paper on a roof somebody put down in June."

The Sable Fork had narrowed through the night into a corridor of drowned alder, the water standing brown and patient among the trunks like it owned them now and always had. The back channel the family at High Water had given us was supposed to thread six miles of that drowned country to the waystation at Coyle's Bend—a fuel cache, a bunkhouse, a radio mast, all of it abandoned when the syndicate bought out the last independent haulers but left standing because nobody had bothered to burn what nobody wanted. We were supposed to sleep there dry. We were supposed to take on the last of our fuel there.

The bend opened ahead of us and Junie brought the Kestrel around it slow, engine idled down to a mutter, and there it was.

The waystation was still smoking.

Not burning—smoking. The bunkhouse had gone down to its joists, black ribs standing in a puddle of ash, and the rain fell into the ruin and rose again as steam, so the whole site wore a gray shroud that moved without wind. The fuel shed had fared differently: someone had dragged its drums out onto the mudflat and split them open with an axe, methodical as a butcher, and the diesel had gone into the water in a rainbow slick that lapped at our hull. The radio mast lay across the clearing like a felled tree, snapped at the base, its guy-wires coiled where they'd fallen. On the near wall of the shed, someone had nailed a fresh board over the old syndicate stencil—the blue diamond—and painted nothing on it at all. Just bare wood. A blankness where a name used to be.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The rain did the talking, drumming on the cabin roof, hissing off the hot ash.

Junie killed the engine and let us drift. "Coyle's Bend," she said quietly. "Somebody remembered it."

"Somebody never forgot it," I said.

I went forward and took the boathook, and when we kissed the mudflat I stepped off into shin-deep water and waded ashore, because someone had to, and because my legs needed to do something other than carry the thought that had already arrived ahead of me and was sitting in my chest like a stone.

Here is the thing about being sold: you don't feel the knife go in. You feel it later, when you count who knew where you'd be.

Four people alive knew we were taking the back channel to Coyle's Bend. Me. Junie. The stranded family at High Water, who'd traded us the route for pulling their kids off a roof. And whoever the family told, or whoever listened on whatever frequency the interior still ran on when it thought no one was listening. That was the arithmetic I did while my boots sucked in the mud, and every sum came out the same: someone had talked, and the fire was the receipt.

Because this wasn't lightning. Lightning doesn't drag drums out of a shed. Lightning doesn't axe them open so the fuel can't be used. Lightning doesn't snap a radio mast at its base with a come-along and leave the wires neat. This was work done by two men, maybe three, in the dark, in the rain, with time enough to be thorough and not a minute more. And they'd left it smoking deliberately, the way you leave a calling card, or a warning, or both: *we know the channel you were given. We know where you were going to sleep.*

I stood in the ruins of the bunkhouse and looked at the ash and understood that the fog of suspicion I'd been holding at arm's length for two days had just walked up and put its arms around me.

* * *

Junie came ashore behind me. I heard her boots in the muck and didn't turn around.

"Fuel's gone," she said, stating it flat, the way you state weather. "Both drums split. There might be a gallon in the seams if I siphon the mud."

"Mast is down," I said. "Bunkhouse is down."

"Yeah." She crouched by the fire pit, which wasn't a fire pit anymore but a crater of white ash, and picked something out of it with two fingers. An hinge. Brass, half-melted. She turned it over and set it down gently, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes. Then she straightened up and looked at me, and I looked at her, and the rain came down between us like a curtain neither of us could see the other through clearly.

"Two days ago," I said, "you told me you had a contact. Back when we were loading at Pier Nine. You said there were people on the river who'd pass word if the syndicate came hunting."

Something flickered in her face. Not guilt exactly. Recognition—the look of a woman who has just heard her own words read back to her in a voice colder than she'd expected.

"I did say that."

"And last night, at the anchorage below the falls, you had the wireless out of its box. I saw the light on in the wheelhouse at two in the morning. I didn't say anything, because it was your boat and your set and I was too tired to be paranoid." I made myself keep my voice level, which cost more than shouting would have. "So I'm going to ask you once, Junie, and I want the whole answer, because Elias Vann is dead, and Hale's launch passed within a quarter mile of us yesterday, and now the one dry roof between here and the claim is charcoal. Who did you radio?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. In that silence I watched her decide—not whether to tell me, I realized afterward, but how much of herself to hand over with the telling. Guarded people do that math constantly. I know because I do it too. Every friendship I've ever had has been audited line by line before I signed anything.

"My union man," she said. "Dessalines. He ran the haulers' association before the syndicate broke it, and he kept the frequencies after. When we cleared the toll bridge I sent him a squawk—route and timetable, same as I've done on every run since I got blacklisted. It's how boats down here stay alive. Somebody always knows where you are, or you vanish and nobody ever asks why." Her jaw worked. "He's not syndicate. He testified alongside me, Hazel. He lost more than I did."

"Then either he talked," I said, "or someone heard him."

"He doesn't talk." But her voice had gone uncertain on the second word, and we both heard it, and that uncertainty sat down between us like a third passenger.

"You can't know that," I said. "You haven't seen him in what—two years? Three? People change. People get leaned on. Hale doesn't break kneecaps, Junie, that's the whole horror of him—he finds the soft thing and squeezes until it sings. Maybe Dessalines has a daughter. Maybe he has a debt. Maybe he just got tired." I heard my own voice climbing and couldn't stop it, two days of fear coming out sideways. "Everyone is tired. Everyone has a soft thing. That's how they got my father."

"Don't." The word came out of her like a piston. She stepped toward me through the ash and the rain ran off her chin and her eyes were bright and furious and hurt, all three at once. "Don't you dare put me in the same sentence as the men who killed your father. I have been leaking bilge and eating cold rice and carrying that engine over swamp on my back for you—for a dead prospector I never met—for four days, and you want to stand in a burned building and audit me?"

"I'm not auditing you. I'm counting survivors." My voice cracked on it, damn it, cracked right down the middle. "There are supposed to be two of us at the end of this. If I can't trust the only person between me and the water—"

"Then swim," she said.

And then, horribly, wonderfully, the rain chose that moment to intensify from merely torrential into something biblical, a white roaring curtain that erased the river, the ruin, the far bank, everything, and we stood there six feet apart getting hammered flat and shouting nothing, because there was nothing left to shout over the noise, and somewhere in the middle of that drowning hush I saw her shoulders drop. Saw the fury go out of her stance and leave something rawer underneath.

"Hold on," she said, quieter. "Hold on. Look at the shed."

* * *

I looked. Through the rain-haze, on the seaward face of the fuel shed—the face pointed away from the channel, the face you'd only see if you walked around the ruin on foot—there was writing. Charcoal, dragged on in haste, big block letters already bleeding in the wet:

**KESTREL — THEY KNOW THE FORK — TRUST NO WIRE — D**

Below it, smaller, almost washed away: a date. Yesterday's date.

Junie waded to it and put her palm flat against the boards beside the letters, reading it the way you'd read a pulse. "That's his hand," she said. "Dessalines writes his Ds like that, with the tail. He taught me Morse with that tail." She laughed, one short bark with no humor in it, and pressed her forehead briefly against the wet planks. "He came here. Or sent someone. They found the station already burning and they couldn't save the fuel, so they wrote on the one wall the fire hadn't taken and the channel couldn't see."

"*Trust no wire,*" I read aloud. My own voice sounded strange to me, small under the rain. "Meaning the radio."

"Meaning every radio on this river is a syndicate ear, including—" she stopped. Including hers. Including the set she'd keyed at two in the morning, certain she was whispering, not knowing—or not letting herself know—that a man like Corliss Hale doesn't need to break a code when he can simply listen to everyone and wait for the pattern to surface. Two years of Junie's little squawks to Dessalines, logged and filed and harmless, until one of them mentioned the Sable Fork and became a map drawn in someone else's blood.

"They didn't need Dessalines to talk," I said slowly, working it through the way you work a splinter out. "They needed traffic. Hale sits on the high ground with a receiver and reads the river like a ledger. Every friendly hail is an entry. We handed him our route in the clear and called it caution."

Junie was quiet a long moment. Rain poured off the brim of her cap. "My father used to say the river gives you two ways to die," she said finally. "Fast and stupid, or slow and careful. I picked careful my whole life and it turns out careful was just slow." She turned to face me fully, and whatever she saw in my face made her go on, rougher: "I should have told you about the squawks. Day one. Before you gave me the map, before Elias—I owed you that and I sat on it because telling felt like handing you one more reason to run. I'm sorry. That's the whole confession. That's all of it."

I believed her. That was the strange part—I'd spent four days believing nothing completely, and here in a burned clearing in the teeth of the monsoon I believed her completely, and I think it was because she'd said *I sat on it* instead of *it wasn't important.* Guilty people minimize. Honest people confess past the point of comfort.

"Come here," I said, which wasn't eloquent, but she came, and we stood together under the eave of the ruined shed, two soaked women in the ashes of a stranger's livelihood, and the fight drained out of the space between us and left something load-bearing in its place. Trust isn't a feeling. I'd learned that young, watching my father sign papers with men who smiled. Trust is a decision you make with incomplete information and then hold to when the information gets worse. I decided.

"Dessalines risked himself coming here," I said. "Or his runner did. Which means he's still ours."

"Which means he's exposed," Junie said. "If Hale traced our squawk, he can trace every squawk. Dessalines will be gone to ground by tonight if he's smart, and if he's not—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. The interior was full of men who weren't smart in exactly the ways that got them drowned.

I looked back at the charcoal letters, already fading, the rain erasing Dessalines's warning even as we read it. *They know the fork.* Of course they knew the fork. They'd always known the fork; they'd jumped Elias's stake on it. What they hadn't known was our timetable, and now they did, and somewhere behind us—downstream, or overhead in that seaplane waiting out the weather—Corliss Hale was doing the same arithmetic I'd done in the mud, except his sums ended in fires.

"We can't stop here," I said. "No fuel, no roof, and anyone who wrote that warning might have been followed to write it."

"No," Junie agreed. "But we're not leaving yet." She nodded upstream, past the wreck of the mast, to where the drowned alder thinned and the current quickened over a gravel bar. Half-submerged in the shallows, snagged on a fallen trunk, was a long dark shape. A pirogue. Local-built, patched with tin, riding high and empty.

And wedged upright in the gravel beside it, sheltered under a scrap of oilcloth tied down with river-cane, a jerrican of diesel. Full. With a strip of red cloth knotted around the neck—Dessalines's colors, Junie breathed, his colors since the old association days—and a note in the same tail-hooked D, weighted under a stone:

*Two drums cached at the Devil's Teeth portage head. Take the high cut above Miller's Eddy, not the channel. And missus — the seaplane went back to Rivergate this morning. Weather's got it grounded till the front passes. You have maybe thirty hours of nobody watching. Spend them well.*

Junie read it twice. Then she looked up at me, and despite everything—the ash, the leak in the hull, the bullet-shaped bruise the toll bridge had left on all our nerves—she grinned, sharp and bright as a knife coming out of a sheath.

"Thirty hours," she said. "Hazel Dunmore, I do believe the river just dealt us a hand."

I took the note from her and read it a third time, and the words that stayed with me weren't the good ones. They were the last four. *Spend them well.* Because Dessalines was a careful man, and careful men didn't write warnings unless they'd counted the hours themselves—and a man who counts your hours for you knows something about what's coming when they run out.

I folded the note into my jacket, next to the compass, next to the map, next to Elias's promise, and turned my face upriver toward the Devil's Teeth.

"Then let's not waste a minute of them," I said.
