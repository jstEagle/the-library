## 14. Portage Through the Devil's Teeth

The river told us before the map did.

I'd been running water since I was nine years old—first on my uncle's barge out of Calabar roads, then up and down every tributary the Rivergate had a name for—and I knew the sound of bad water the way some men knew the smell of rain. It came up through the hull first, a low grinding hum in the timbers, like something chewing. Then the mist ahead went white and ragged where it shouldn't have been, torn upward by falling water.

"Junie." Hazel stood at the bow with one hand on the rail, the other shading her eyes. "Is that what I think it is?"

"That depends on whether you think it's the Devil's Teeth."

She turned around. Her face did that thing it did when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like.

I cut the engine and let us drift. The Kestrel swung lazily in the current, and we both listened to the rapids talking to each other half a mile upstream—a long, broken argument of white water, maybe two miles of it by the sound, cascading down through a gorge of black granite that the flood had turned into a chute. Even from here I could see the spray standing over it like smoke off a burning field.

"No boat goes through that," Hazel said.

"No boat goes through that," I agreed. "Not this boat, not any boat, not at this water level. That's not rapids anymore, that's a waterfall wearing rapids as a disguise."

"So we go around."

I looked at the banks. On the left, the jungle rose straight up out of the water, roots like knuckles gripping the mud. On the right, there was a shelf of higher ground—still swamp, still flooded knee-deep in places, but ground. Of a sort.

"We go around," I said. "Overland. We carry everything."

Hazel stared at me. Then at the boat. Then at the engine—the Kestrel's heart, a hundred and sixty pounds of marine motor bolted to its mount, which I had rebuilt twice with my own hands and loved more than was strictly reasonable.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

* * *

We spent the rest of the morning preparing, because a portage done badly doesn't just cost you time—it kills you. I'd seen it happen once, years ago, a rubber crew up the Mokoro trying to haul an outboard through flooded forest. One man slipped on a root with a load strap across his chest. The river took him under the deadfall and we never found all of him.

So we did it right. I stripped the Kestrel to her bones: fuel drums lashed shut, crates broken down into loads no heavier than forty pounds, the engine unbolted and cradled in a frame I built from the boat's own spare planking and the last of our rope. I made two carrying yokes from a length of bamboo growing at the waterline, padded with strips of tarpaulin.

Hazel worked beside me without complaint, but I watched her favoring her left hand, and around midday I caught her flexing it behind her back when she thought I wasn't looking.

"Let me see."

"It's nothing. I landed wrong on a rock yesterday."

"Hazel."

She held it out. The wrist was swollen along the thumb side, hot to the touch, and when I pressed gently she went white and made a sound she tried to swallow.

"That's a sprain. Maybe worse."

"It's fine."

"It is not fine, and you know it, because you're a pilot and pilots know exactly what their hands are worth." I wrapped it tight with a strip of canvas and splinted it against a flat piece of kindling, the way you'd splint a bird's wing. "You carry light loads only. Small crates, the map case, the medical kit. Nothing on the shoulder."

"I can pull my weight."

"You're going to pull your weight. You're going to pull it in a way that doesn't end with both of us crippled in leech country with four days left on a permit clock." I tied off the bandage. She didn't argue again, which told me how much it hurt.

The swamp waited for us on the right bank, green and breathing.

* * *

The first mile was almost civilized. There was an old portage trail—Elias Vann must have walked it himself, years ago, because someone had blazed the trees with hatchet marks gone grey and mossed-over, and in two places there were rotted skid rollers sunk into the mud where prospectors had once hauled canoes. The flood had drowned the low sections, so we waded ankle-deep, then shin-deep, then waist-deep in water the color of steeped tea, warm as bathwater and full of things.

I found the first leech on my calf an hour in. Fat and black, already swelling. Hazel had them worse—she stopped at the second mile marker, sat down on a root, and pulled three off her ankles with the flat of a knife, one after another, grimacing.

"You know they're in your boots too," I said.

"I know they're in my boots too." She shook one out. Then another. "My father used to say leeches were honest. They tell you exactly what they want and they take it and leave."

"Your father sounds like he'd have gotten along with mine."

"He flew contraband. Yours testified against a syndicate. Probably not the same dinner table."

"Different tables," I said, hauling the engine yoke back onto my shoulders. "Same kitchen."

That was the thing about those hours in the swamp—I couldn't get away from thinking, and neither could she, and somewhere between the third mile and the fifth we started actually talking. Not the careful trading of information we'd been doing since Pier Nine. Talking. She told me about learning to fly at fifteen, standing on a crate to reach the rudder pedals, her father laughing at her from the passenger seat. I told her about the hearing—my testimony, the room full of syndicate lawyers looking at me like I was something they'd scraped off their shoes, and the blacklist that followed me out the door like a dog.

"They ruined you for telling the truth," she said.

"They ruined me for being poor and telling the truth. The first part was the real crime."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, like she hadn't meant to let it out. In the green dark of the swamp, with the leeches and the mud and the roar of the falls coming faintly through the trees, it sounded like something rare.

* * *

By late afternoon the trail gave out.

The flood had taken it—washed the blazes off the trees, drowned the skid rollers, and left us with a choice between two directions that both looked identical: black water, hanging vines, and jungle so dense you couldn't see ten feet into it. My compass said northeast. The sound of the rapids, fading behind us, said we were at least pointed the right general way.

I took the heavy loads. Engine on the yoke, then a fuel drum, then the crate of tools and spare parts. Forty pounds became eighty became a hundred and twenty, and I learned very quickly why the old prospectors had used mules. Every step sank six inches into mud that gripped like a hand. Roots grabbed at my ankles. Once I went down to my chest in a hidden hole and came up blowing, still holding the drum above the water, and Hazel was there with her good hand out before I'd even finished swearing.

"Give me the drum."

"You're not carrying a fuel drum with that wrist."

"Then hold it while I get my footing, you stubborn woman, I'm not asking you to carry it to the registry office."

I held it out. She braced it against a tree trunk until I got my legs under me. Neither of us said anything about it, but something shifted in the air between us, the way weight shifts in a boat when two people finally stop fighting the list and lean the same direction.

The light went gold, then copper, then grey. The mosquitoes arrived with the dusk in clouds so thick I breathed through my sleeve. And the leeches—God, the leeches. By the time we heard water moving ahead of us—not falling water, but moving water, the Sable Fork itself, calm and wide on the far side of the gorge—we were both staggering.

We came out of the treeline onto a gravel bar as the last light died, and I put the engine down in the shallows and sat down next to it and didn't move for a long time.

Hazel counted the loads by lantern light. Everything made it. Every drum, every crate, the map case dry inside its oilcloth, the medical kit, the tools, the engine. She read the tally off to me twice, and both times her voice cracked on the last item, and both times she pretended it hadn't.

"One hundred and sixty pounds of engine," she said, "carried five miles through hell by one stubborn engineer."

"And one stubborn pilot carried everything else on one good hand." I lay back on the gravel. The sky over the gorge was clearing for the first time in days, stars showing through rents in the cloud. "Don't make it a song, Dunmore. I'll never live it down."

* * *

We slept four hours and woke to work.

Reassembling the Kestrel on the far bank was its own kind of misery—bolting the engine mount back on by lantern light, Hazel holding pieces steady with her splinted wrist braced against her stomach, passing me wrenches with her fingers instead of her grip. But there was a rhythm to it, and somewhere in the middle of the night the rhythm stopped being miserable. I'd hand her a bolt and she'd have the washer already threaded on it. She'd point at a fitting and I'd already have the spanner moving toward it. We didn't speak for an hour and the engine went together faster than it ever had with a paid crew of two.

"There," I said finally, wiping my hands. "She'll run."

"She always runs." Hazel patted the hull, gentle, like it was a horse. "You built her to."

"I rebuilt her to. Somebody else built her cheap."

At dawn we loaded her, launched her, and I opened the throttle, and the Kestrel climbed up onto plane and ran clean down the broad brown shoulder of the Sable Fork with the Devil's Teeth roaring away behind us like a crowd we'd escaped.

Hazel sat in the bow with the map open on her knees, her splinted wrist resting on the rail, and after a while she looked back at me.

"Junie. The cairn should be two days upstream. Permits expire in—" she did the arithmetic, and I watched her do it, watched her face change. "Four days. Four days, if we don't lose any more time."

"We won't lose time. We'll spend it carefully."

But even as I said it, I was watching the banks. Because the Sable Fork was syndicate water—every prospector knew it, every toll keeper owed them, and we had come through the Devil's Teeth loud and slow and leaving a trail a blind man could follow. Five miles of muddy boot prints and blazed trees and a gravel bar with our campfire ash still warm on it.

And then I saw it, and my stomach dropped.

Smoke. A thin grey thread of it, rising off the ridge two miles ahead, dead ahead of us on the route Elias's map had drawn. There was supposed to be nothing on that ridge but a derelict waystation and empty water.

Nobody lit a fire in the Rivergate interior unless they wanted to be found—or wanted someone to find what was left.

"Junie?" Hazel had turned, following my eyes. "What is it?"

I eased the throttle forward and said nothing yet, because I needed another minute to be sure, and because I already was.

"Smoke," I said. "Straight ahead. Right where we're going."
