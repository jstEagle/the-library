## 15. The Claim

The Sable Fork narrowed all morning until the Kestrel's wake touched both banks at once.

Hazel stood at the bow with the compass open in her palm and Elias's map spread across her knees, checking one against the other every few minutes like a woman counting money she didn't yet believe she had. The river here was a different animal than the broad brown water below—narrower, faster, stained the color of strong tea by the tannin of drowned leaves, and threaded with deadfalls that appeared without warning around each bend. Junie handled them with a kind of contemptuous ease, nudging the throttle, swinging the hull wide, threading gaps that looked to Hazel like the space between a needle's eye and its thread.

"Marker should be past the twin snags," Hazel said. "Elias drew them crossed, like an X."

"Everything on this map is crossed," Junie muttered. "Crossed snags, crossed out names, crossed fingers." But her eyes were moving along the left bank, quick and hungry, and Hazel knew she wanted it found as badly as she did.

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that beaded on the oilskin of the map and made Elias's ink lines soften at their edges, as if the drawing were still settling into the paper after all these years. The flooded forest pressed close on both sides—alder and black spruce standing hip-deep in water that should have been meadow, their reflections doubling the green until it seemed they were running through a corridor built twice, once above and once below. Somewhere back in that drowned timber a loon called, long and rising, and Hazel felt the sound go through her like a wire.

*Four days.* She turned the thought over again, the way you'd turn a stone in your pocket. The permits expired at noon on the last day of the monsoon season. Four days to get downriver, through the toll station, past whatever Corliss Hale had set in motion behind them, and into the registry office in Rivergate with the paperwork in hand. Four days, minus whatever the river decided to take from them.

She looked at the compass. Her father's compass, brass-cased, glass scratched to frost by thirty years of pockets and weather. Its needle swung lazily with the boat's turns and then steadied, always, toward the same invisible point ahead. She'd stopped asking herself whether that meant anything. Some instruments you read. Some instruments read *you*.

"There." Junie's voice cracked across the silence like a whip.

Hazel followed her chin. On the right bank, where the water lapped against a shelf of gray granite, two snags leaned together over the current—two great bleached trunks fallen from opposite shores, their upper branches interlocked in a rough X against the sky. Beyond them, half-hidden in the alders, a slope of talus rose toward a shoulder of dark rock.

And at the base of the slope, just visible between two leaning stones, something pale and square.

Junie brought the Kestrel alongside the granite shelf with a bump that shivered the whole hull, and Hazel was over the side before the boat finished rocking, boots finding the submerged ledge, water to her thighs, then her waist, then her hands on wet stone hauling herself up onto rock that hadn't seen dry sun in weeks. She stood dripping, heart slamming, and stared at the thing between the stones.

It was a cairn. A marker cairn, waist-high, built of flat slabs fitted with a mason's patience—and driven into its crown, green with age but still legible, a galvanized stake with a stamped plate.

Elias Vann. Claim 4471-Sable Fork. Staked under provision fourteen.

The date stamped beneath it was eleven years old.

Hazel heard Junie come up the rocks behind her, heard her breath catch and stop. For a long moment neither of them said anything. The mist drifted. The loon called again, farther off now, up some hidden arm of the flood.

"He wasn't lying," Junie said finally, very quietly. "All those years, everyone saying old Vann was chasing ghosts up the Fork. He staked it. He actually—" She put her hand flat on the cold stones, almost gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "He staked it and nobody would let him keep it."

Hazel knelt. Her fingers were shaking so badly she could barely work the knots, but she got the oilcloth bundle open anyway, and there it was: the map, Elias's careful hand, and the little pencil annotation in the corner she'd never been able to make sense of. *Cairn bears 212 from twin snags. They moved my line posts. Counted on the cairn holding.*

They moved my line posts.

She looked up at the talus slope, and now that she knew what she was looking for, she saw them—the rotted stubs of survey stakes scattered down the hillside like broken teeth, and newer ones too, cedar-fresh, stamped with a mark she recognized with a sick drop of her stomach. The Meridian Consolidated mark. The syndicate's mark. Someone had re-surveyed this ground within the last few years and driven their own corners into Elias's claim, neat as bookkeeping, as if the old man's eleven years of sweat and dynamite and frozen nights had simply never happened.

"They jumped him," she said. The words came out flat and strange, stripped of anything. "They didn't just contest the filing. They came up here and physically moved his boundary posts and re-staked it under their own numbers."

"That's not jumping," Junie said. "That's theft with a transit level." She was already climbing, sure-footed on the wet rock, following a seam of white quartz that ribboned up the slope like a vein of light. "Come on. If he tagged assays, I want to see them with my own eyes."

The working face was forty feet up, tucked under an overhang that had kept the worst of the weather off it—a cut in the hillside tall as a barn door, timbered with hand-hewn sets gone silver-gray, and in the floor of the cut, glittering even in the flat gray light, the vein itself.

Hazel had seen ore before. She'd flown drill core out of a dozen camps, ridden shotgun over crates of samples, watched geologists tap rocks against their teeth like horses. But she had never seen anything like this. The vein ran wall to wall across the cut, a hand-span wide in places, and it wasn't the shy, disseminated sparkle of most hardrock gold. It was coarse. Visible. Yellow metal lying in the quartz like letters in a word, like someone had written a sentence in gold and then buried the page for ten thousand years.

"Oh," Junie said. Just that. One syllable, and her voice broke in the middle of it.

There were assay tags nailed to the timber sets, dozens of them, tin squares punched with holes and scrawled in Elias's blunt capitals. Hazel worked along the line of them, reading by the gray light, and the numbers made her sit down on a powder box without meaning to.

*Sample 31 — 4.2 oz/ton over 18 in.*
*Sample 44 — 11.7 oz/ton.*
*Sample 52 — free gold, visible, 22 oz/ton estimated.*

Twenty-two ounces to the ton. Camps had gone to war over a tenth of that. Men had frozen to death chasing a quarter of it. And Elias Vann had sat alone with these numbers for eleven years, filing and being rejected, filing and being rejected, writing letters to a daughter who never answered, dying on the floor of a burned cabin with the whole bright secret folded in oilcloth against his chest.

Hazel became aware that her face was wet and couldn't tell if it was mist or something else. She let it be either.

At the back of the cut, behind a slab of shale pried loose and set back in place so many times the edges were polished smooth, they found his cache. A ammunition box, rusted at the corners, inside which lay everything: the original notice of location, filled out in Elias's hand and signed by two witnesses whose names meant nothing; a leather ledger recording eleven seasons of assessment work, every day logged like a prayer—*cut 6 ft, blasted 3 charges, sluiced tailings, vein holds true*; a photograph soft with handling, showing a younger Elias squinting into the sun beside a much younger woman with Mara's stubborn jaw; and at the bottom, wrapped separately in waxed paper, a second map—not the claim, but the route to it, annotated with water levels and campsites in a hand that grew shakier year by year, the last annotations dated barely three months ago.

He'd known he was running out of time. Not monsoon time. Life time.

"He kept everything," Hazel said. "Every scrap. He knew paper was the only weapon he had left, so he built an arsenal."

"And they burned his camp anyway." Junie stood in the mouth of the cut, arms crossed, looking out at the drowned valley below with an expression Hazel had learned to read on the river: the flat stillness that came before weather. "You don't burn a man's camp over paperwork, Hazel. You burn it when the paperwork is about to beat you."

*He was there the night the camp burned.* Hale's own words, or near enough, offered like a confession he didn't know he was making. She'd carried that sentence for days now, turning it, testing its edges. Sitting here in Elias's cut, with Elias's gold shining in the wall and Elias's arithmetic in her hands, the sentence finally showed its underside. Hale hadn't come upriver for a map. Maps could be redrawn. He'd come because the claim itself was a wound the syndicate had spent eleven years pretending wasn't bleeding—and because Elias Vann, dying, had handed the knife to a bush pilot who owed them nothing and feared them only moderately.

"We have to move," she said, and was surprised how steady her voice came out. "Four days. Less, by the time we run the Fork back down."

Junie didn't answer immediately. She'd picked up one of the assay tags and was turning it over in her fingers, thumb moving across the stamped tin the way you'd touch a relic. "Eleven years," she said. "I testified against them five years ago and lost everything—my license, my berth, my name. Five years, and I told myself it was worth it because at least the record existed, at least somebody somewhere had it written down." She looked at the tag. "He wrote it down for eleven years and it didn't save him either. Paper doesn't save you, Hazel. People do. That's the whole rotten lesson."

"Then we'll need people," Hazel said. "The clerk at the registry. Whoever filed his original rejections—they'll be in the record books, with signatures. And Hale."

Junie's head came around sharply. "Hale?"

"Hale signs the corroboration, or Hale explains to a magistrate why Meridian's corner posts are sitting inside Elias's original staking. Either way, he's part of the paperwork now." Hazel gathered the cache back into its box, taking only what they needed—the notice, the ledger, the letters' return addresses, the second map—and reburying the rest exactly as they'd found it. Evidence stayed best where juries could dig it up themselves. "Come on. We've got a vein to leave unguarded and a river to outrun."

They came down the talus as the light began to fail, and Hazel paused once at the cairn, alone, while Junie walked back to the boat. She put her palm flat on the top slab, on the moss, on the cold.

"You held," she told Elias. "Now hold four more days."

The loon called again, upstream, insistent, like something urging them on—or warning them back. Hazel stood a moment longer in the mist with her hand on eleven years of another man's stubbornness, feeling the enormity of what they'd found settle over her like the weather itself: awe first, and vindication underneath, and beneath both of those, cold and clear as the tannin water, the arithmetic of four days.

Then she heard Junie shout from the boat—one word, ragged, torn out of her—

and looked up to see, far down the flooded corridor of the Sable Fork, a launch's running lights coming on through the dusk, one after another, like eyes opening.
