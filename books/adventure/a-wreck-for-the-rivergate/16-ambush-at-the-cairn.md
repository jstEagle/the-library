## 16. Ambush at the Cairn

### HALE

Corliss Hale had learned long ago that the last hour before dawn belonged to whoever wanted it most.

He crouched at the treeline above the cairn with six men fanned out behind him, and he wanted it more than anything he'd wanted in years. The fog off the Sable Fork lay thick as wet wool, pooling in the hollows, swallowing the sound of the river to a dull mutter. Perfect. In this soup a man could walk to within ten paces of his own death and never see it coming—which cut both ways, and Hale knew it. He'd chosen his men for quiet feet, not numbers.

"Vann's marker is forty yards downslope," whispered Dree, his tracker, a narrow man who smelled of kerosene and never blinked enough. "Smoke from their fire's gone grey. They're letting it die."

Of course they were. The pilot woman—Thornbury—was careful. He'd known that from the moment she'd lied to him in her cabin with that steady, pleasant voice, the lie sitting behind her teeth like a coin under a tongue. Careful people made mistakes only when they were hurried, and so Hale had spent two weeks making sure she was hurried every hour of every day. Burned waystation. Toll station. A seaplane on her horizon like a gull that wouldn't leave.

And still she'd beaten him here. He had to give her that. Elias Vann would have laughed—Elias had always laughed at Hale's expense, right up until the night the camp burned and stopped laughing altogether.

"Nobody shoots the women," Hale said quietly. "Wound if you must. I need them able to talk." He touched the revolver at his hip, the one with the worn checkering, the one he'd carried the night at Vann's camp. "The map is what matters. And after that—" he paused, choosing the words the way a surgeon chooses a blade, "—nothing else matters at all."

Dree grinned in the dark. The others checked their weapons with small oiled clicks.

Below them, through thinning fog, the cairn showed as a pale heap of stones against black earth, and beside it a lean-to of tarps, and beside that the Kestrel, patched and ugly, tied to a drowned sapling. A figure moved by the dead fire—broad-shouldered, moving like an engineer, all economy. The Okonkwo woman.

Hale felt something cold and old turn over in his chest. He'd burned one camp already because Elias Vann refused to be reasonable. He had no intention of standing in the smoke again. This time there would be no fire, no noise, no witnesses. Just fog, and quiet, and an ending.

He raised two fingers. The line began to move down through the trees.

* * *

### JUNIE

Junie woke because the river went wrong.

Not loud—wrong. The Sable Fork had been talking all night, that low gravel-voiced mutter of high water, and somewhere in the grey hour before dawn it swallowed. She lay under the tarp with her hand already closing around the wrench she kept by her bedroll the way other women kept a Bible, and she listened, and the silence told her everything: someone was between her and the water.

She rolled Hazel with her boot, gentle but not slow. Hazel came awake the way Junie had seen few people come awake—no gasp, no flail, just eyes opening flat and clear, like a lamp turned up.

"Hale," Junie breathed. "Downslope. At least four, maybe more. They've let our fire die around us—they've been watching a while."

Hazel's jaw tightened. For a heartbeat Junie watched her do the arithmetic every fugitive does: run, fight, or bargain. Then Hazel reached under the lean-to's edge and came out with the flare pistol they'd taken off the Kestrel's emergency kit, and Junie understood the answer was *none of the above*.

"Fog's our friend," Hazel whispered. "They can't see us either. We split. You take the boat side—I'm louder and slower, draw them left. Don't be a hero, don't be a corpse. We meet at the cairn or we meet downstream."

"And if we don't meet?"

Hazel's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Then one of us registers the claim and tells the story wrong on purpose. Make me brave."

Junie wanted to argue and didn't have the minutes. She squeezed Hazel's forearm once—hard, a promise in the shape of a bruise—and slid out into the fog with the wrench and the flare gun and her heart going like an unbalanced propeller.

The world had shrunk to five feet of visibility and the smell of wet stone. Junie moved along the boat line, low, counting her own breaths. Ten steps. Twenty. The fog muffled everything except—

A twig cracked to her right. Close. Too close.

She dropped as the shot came, and the world tore open.

* * *

### HAZEL

The crack of the rifle went through the fog like a bone breaking, and then Junie's voice—not a scream, worse, a bitten-off grunt of fury—and Hazel stopped being afraid and started being useful.

She fired the flare blind into the trees to her left, and the world went red. For three full seconds the fog lit up like the inside of a furnace, and in that light she saw them: men in oilskins, spread in a loose crescent, one dropping to a knee, another turning toward the glare with his rifle swinging. Three seconds is nothing. Three seconds is also everything, if you spend them well.

Hazel ran right, away from her own light, crashing through ferns, making the noise of two women where there was one. A shot snapped past her ear and clipped steam off a wet boulder. She heard Hale's voice—she knew it now, that courteous, unhurried baritone, the voice of a man reading terms aloud—calling, *"Hold your fire! Hold! I want them walking!"*

*I want them walking.* Good, she thought, lungs burning. Because I want you kneeling.

She reached the cairn and put her back against the stones, cold through her shirt, and looked for Junie. Found her—down among the boat lines, cradled against the hull, left arm hanging wrong, dark bloom spreading on the shoulder of her jacket. Alive. Swearing with impressive fluency and variety. Alive.

"Junie!"

"Still got my wrench!" Junie called back, and fired the flare gun one-handed at a shape in the fog. It missed by a mile and set the whole hillside glowing red again, and in that second red world Hazel saw Corliss Hale himself, twenty yards upslope, stepping out of the treeline with his revolver held low and patient, walking toward the cairn like a landlord come to collect.

Something happened in Hazel's chest then that she would think about for the rest of her life. Fear was there—fear was a fist around her heart—but underneath the fear, older and colder, was her father's compass in her pocket and her father's debt in her blood and eleven years of being nobody, of being forgettable, of keeping her head down while men like Corliss Hale owned the sky and the water and the law. And she thought: *he's afraid too.*

She saw it plainly, the way you see a vein of gold in quartz once it's been pointed out. Hale walked slowly because Hale was careful, yes—but also because Hale did not want this loud. He'd said *hold your fire* twice. He needed the map without noise, without bodies, without a record. A man who needed silence that badly was a man who had something to bury.

She knew what he'd buried. Elias had told her, dying, in a voice like paper tearing: *the camp didn't catch, girl. It was set. And the man who gave the order stood in the trees and watched me crawl.*

Hale came on through the last of the fog, and Hazel stepped out from behind the cairn with the flare pistol leveled at his chest and her voice level as a spirit gauge.

"That's far enough, Mr. Hale."

He stopped. Of course he stopped—a man that careful didn't gamble at ten feet. His eyes went to the flare gun, and something flickered across his face that wasn't fear exactly. Recognition. Recalculation.

"You're out of flares," he said pleasantly. "I counted."

"I'm not." A bluff with a fifty-fifty spine, and she watched him weigh it. "And even if I were, you're not going to test it. Because you need me quiet more than you need me dead, and I finally understand why."

His jaw shifted, a millimeter. "Do you."

"Elias Vann told me about the night his camp burned." She took one step closer, and the fog curled between them, and somewhere downhill Junie was groaning and one of Hale's men was shouting for orders, and Hazel heard none of it. "He told me who stood in the trees. He told me whose voice gave the word. He wrote it down, Mr. Hale. Letters. Names. Dates. Sealed and addressed, waiting for a postmark."

For the first time since she'd met him, Corliss Hale looked like a man standing on river ice and hearing it talk.

* * *

### HALE

The letters.

The word went through Hale like a bullet of its own. There were no letters—he would have known; he'd gone through Vann's effects himself, twice, after the fire, before the syndicate's lawyers had finished pretending the claim had never been filed. But the pilot said *letters* the way a card player says *full house*, and her eyes were steady, and steady eyes had ruined better men than him.

Behind him, Dree was calling softly, wanting orders. Downhill, the engineer woman was propped against her boat, bleeding, cursing, alive—alive because he'd ordered it, and now that mercy stood up and pointed a weapon at him.

"Those letters are fiction," Hale said. He kept his voice at conversational pitch. A man who raised his voice admitted the stakes. "Vann was a drunk and a fantasist. Whatever he told a stranger in his last hours—"

"He told me the name of the man who ordered the fire." She took another step. Insane, calm, inevitable. "He told me the man stood close enough that he could smell his cigar. Cuban. He told me the man wore a ring on his right hand and tapped it against his revolver grip when he thought no one could see."

Hale's right hand, of its own accord, went still against the checkered grip.

Fog drifted between them. Somewhere below, the river kept up its idiot muttering, indifferent as God. Hale ran the arithmetic he'd been running his whole career: shoot her, and the letters—if they existed—went to a lawyer, a newspaper, a registry clerk, anyone; lose her, and the map stayed lost but the grave stayed quiet. Two outcomes. Only one of them ended with Corliss Hale still being a man who could walk into a room and have it go silent out of respect rather than disgust.

"Put the gun down, Miss Thornbury," he said, and heard, to his own ears, how much the sentence cost him.

"Come and take it," she said. "Or better—walk with me. Slowly. Toward the boat. Where my partner can keep an eye on you, and your men can watch you decide what kind of story ends here."

"This is madness. My men will—"

"Your men will do exactly what you tell them, because you're the kind of man who leaves no witnesses—including, today, yourself." She smiled, and it was Elias Vann's smile, the one that had infuriated him for fifteen years, the smile of a person who has found the one thing you can't buy. "You taught the Rivergate how you operate, Mr. Hale. I'm just using the lesson."

Ten feet. Eight. He could rush her. He might even survive it.

But he could smell, faintly, on the fog, woodsmoke—and fifteen years fell away, and he was standing in burning alder scrub watching a prospector crawl through the mud, and he understood with perfect clarity that he could not survive it twice.

Hale raised his hands. "Dree," he called, without turning his head. "Weapons down. All of you. That's an order."

* * *

### JUNIE

From the boat, Junie watched Hazel march Corliss Hale down the slope at flare-pistol point, hands raised, six armed men standing in the fog like fence posts nobody had nailed up yet, and she started laughing—wet, disbelieving, painful laughter that pulled at the hole in her shoulder and she didn't care.

"You," she wheezed when Hazel got close, "are the single most dangerous woman on this river, and I include the rapids."

"Save it." Hazel's hands were shaking now that it was done—the delayed tremor, Junie knew it well, the body sending its bill after the meal. She knelt by Junie, tore open the med kit with her teeth, and started cutting the jacket sleeve away from the wound with hands that shook and did not miss. "Through and through. You're lucky."

"I'm blacklisted, shot, and holding a hostage on a mountain. Define lucky."

"Lucky is what we call it when the alternative gets a name." Hazel packed the wound, wrapped it, cinched it tight enough to make Junie see colors, and then looked up—at Hale, standing bound-wristed at the cairn under the grey first light, watching them with the expression of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

"What do we do with him?" Junie asked quietly.

Hazel picked up the compass from where it had fallen, thumbed it open, closed it. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, steadier, the voice of a woman who had stopped running somewhere in the last five minutes and hadn't told anybody yet.

"We make him a bargain he can't refuse," she said. "Because the river's rising, the permits expire in four days, and I'm done letting men like him write the endings."

Above the cairn, the fog was lifting, and through it, far upstream, came a sound that made every one of them look up: a deep, grinding roar, like the mountain clearing its throat.

High water. Coming fast.
