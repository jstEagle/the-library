## 17. Bargaining with the Flood

The rain came down like a verdict.

Hazel stood at the Kestrel's stern with the mooring line wrapped twice around her good wrist, watching the Sable Fork climb its banks in brown, muscular surges. The river had swallowed the sandbar where they'd fought at dawn. It was working on the willows now, tearing at them in patient handfuls, and by nightfall it would come for the high ground where she'd dragged Junie out of the fog with a bullet through her shoulder.

Behind her, in the hold, Corliss Hale sat bound to the engine mount with his own tow rope, and he was humming.

She'd heard it all morning through the deck boards — low, tuneless, unhurried, like a man passing time in a train station. It should have frightened her. For an hour it did. But somewhere around midday, listening to that flat drone under the drum of the rain, Hazel understood what it actually was: patience. Hale wasn't humming because he was comfortable. He was humming because he believed the situation was temporary. Men like him never doubted the river would deliver them back into their own hands.

She meant to prove him wrong before dark.

* * *

Junie lay propped against the wheelhouse bulkhead on a nest of life vests, her left arm strapped tight across her chest, her right hand still capable of holding a wrench or — Hazel had no illusions — a gun. The bullet had gone through clean, above the joint, missing everything that mattered by the width of a prayer. She'd dressed it herself with the field kit and half a bottle of Elias's medicinal brandy, biting down on a leather strap while Hazel poured, and she hadn't made a sound until it was over, and then only one word: *damn*.

Now she watched Hazel coil the mooring line with the appraising eye of an engineer assessing a load-bearing member.

"You're going down there," Junie said. Not a question.

"He knows things we need."

"He knows how to make people need things they don't." Junie shifted, winced, kept talking anyway. "Hazel. I've seen him work a room. Toll keepers, clerks, men who owed him nothing — he walks out owning them. You go into that hold alone, you're walking into his workshop."

"Then come with me."

"I can barely hold a cup."

"Then you'll sit behind me and hold one badly, and he'll know I'm not alone." Hazel hung the coiled line on its peg. "That's not nothing, Junie. Alone is what he does best."

Junie looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — the same recalculation Hazel had seen at Pier Nine, when two guarded women had measured each other across a patched hull and decided, provisionally, to live. She got up. Slowly, using her good arm, swearing at the boat, at the river, at the general architecture of the world. But she got up.

They went down together.

* * *

The hold smelled of bilgewater, engine oil, and the cold iron smell of a man who'd been sitting in wet rope for ten hours. Hazel had given him water twice and food once, because whatever else he was, he was her prisoner now, and there were rules even outside the law. Especially outside the law.

Hale sat exactly where she'd left him, back against the engine mount, legs stretched out, wrists lashed behind him. His coat was ruined. His collar was still crisp. That was the thing about Corliss Hale that no one ever believed until they met him: he looked like a bank manager who'd wandered onto the wrong boat, right up until you saw his eyes, which were the color of a frozen river and about as warm.

He stopped humming when her boots hit the ladder rungs.

"Miss Thornbury." His voice was pleasant. Conversational. "I wondered when you'd come down. The waiting is always worse than the conversation — I find people imagine me far more frightening than I actually am."

"People don't imagine it," Hazel said. She lowered herself onto the ammunition crate across from him, knees apart, hands visible, the way Junie had told her — *never give a bound man a reason to think you're nervous*. "Elias Vann didn't imagine it either. Neither did whoever burned his camp while he was gut-shot in the brush watching."

Something flickered behind the frozen-river eyes. Brief. Controlled. But it was there, and Hazel marked it the way she'd mark a wind shift.

"I read your file," Hale said, as if she hadn't spoken. "Before I came to your cabin. Did you know that? Hazel Dunmore. Licensed pilot, cargo and mail concessions, interior routes. Father deceased — flying accident, though the syndicate's ledgers tell a more interesting story about what he was carrying and for whom. Debts inherited, debts accrued. A woman with excellent reasons to stay invisible." He tilted his head. "Invisible women don't tie enforcers to engine mounts. Something changed you."

"Elias changed me."

"Ah." He said it softly, almost kindly. "Yes. The map."

"The claim."

"They're the same thing, Miss Thornbury. That's rather my point." He shifted against the ropes, testing them without appearing to — she caught it, the small flex of the shoulders, the turn of the wrists, cataloguing slack. "You're sitting on a piece of paper that says a dead man owned a hole in the ground. I'm sitting on a rope. But in four days, when the permits lapse, that paper is worth exactly what this rope is worth, and you'll have spent a week running flooded rivers, bleeding your friend, and making an enemy of the only organization in Rivergate that can make a woman disappear between registry stamps. So let's be practical. Give me the map. Take the Kestrel downriver. I'll report that I lost you in the flood — which will even be true. Everyone walks away. Even Miss Okonkwo, whose name is already in enough of our books."

It was well done. Hazel gave him that. Reasonable, specific, generous-sounding, every threat wrapped in cotton wool. Ten days ago it would have worked. Ten days ago she'd been a woman whose whole strategy was being forgettable, and a man like Hale could have talked her out of her own shadow.

But she'd spent a night in a drowned trading post reading a dead man's rejected filings, and something in her had burned down along with the waystation, and what grew back was different timber.

"No," she said.

Hale sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a professional confronting an amateur's stubbornness. "Then we'll both be uncomfortable."

"The river's rising," Hazel said. "By tomorrow morning this anchorage is gone. We'll be running white water with a wounded engineer and a prisoner in the hold, and if we ship water, I'll cut you loose and let you swim for it, because I won't have a drowning man dragging my boat down. That's not a threat. That's ballast arithmetic." She leaned forward. "But here's what I came down here to say, so listen, because I'll only say it once. There's a version of the next four days where you walk away. Not free of us — free of *this*. Free of the fire at Vann's camp. Free of the jumped claim. Free of the letters."

His stillness changed quality. Hazel had spent years reading weather, and she read him now the same way: the pressure dropping, the air going strange before the storm.

"What letters," Hale said.

And there it was. Not *do you have letters*. Not *what do you mean*. *What letters* — the question of a man who already knew which ones existed and needed to know how many had survived.

"Elias wrote to his daughter," Hazel said. "For eleven years. Every letter unsent, bundled in oilcloth at the trading post, hidden behind the assay office wall. He wrote about the claim. He wrote about the syndicate jumping his stake. And he wrote about the night his camp burned." She held his gaze. "He named names, Mr. Hale. He was very thorough. He was a prospector — noticing things was his trade."

The hold was silent except for the rain on the deck above and the river grinding at the willows outside.

"You're bluffing," Hale said. But his voice had lost its pleasantness, and they both knew it.

"Am I?"

"If such letters existed, Vann would have used them. He had eleven years."

"To what end? Send them to whom? The magistrate in Rivergate takes syndicate money to buy his cigars. The land office rejects his filings before the ink dries. Who was he supposed to show them to?" Hazel shook her head slowly. "He wasn't keeping them as a weapon. He was keeping them as a confession — to Mara. So his daughter would know he hadn't failed. That he'd been robbed, and by whom, and that he'd never stopped digging. They weren't ammunition, Mr. Hale. They were an inheritance." She paused. "Until now."

Hale looked at her for a long time. Then he did something she hadn't expected: he laughed. Quietly, without humor, shaking his head at some private joke between himself and the universe.

"Do you know what I was doing the night Vann's camp burned?" he said.

"Tell me."

"I was twenty-six. Two years in the syndicate's service, mostly collections, mostly men who'd signed papers they couldn't read. My superior — a man named Brack, since promoted, since retired to a villa on the coast — ordered a demonstration. Vann had filed a claim on the Sable Fork that assayed rich enough to frighten the board. The order was to burn the camp empty. Scare him off the river entirely." Hale's voice was flat as slate. "Vann wasn't there. We waited two days. Brack lost patience. He sent four men back with orders that had changed, and he sent me to watch and report, because he said I was too soft to do it and he wanted me to see what resolve looked like." His jaw tightened. "I sat on the ridge with a lantern shuttered, and I watched, and I reported. That's my crime, Miss Thornbury. Not the fire. The watching. And I've carried it up every river in this territory for fifteen years, and I will carry it to my grave, and no bundle of letters changes the weight of it."

Hazel hadn't prepared for this. She'd prepared for denial, for negotiation, for menace. She had not prepared for a man handing her his guilt like a man laying a card face-up.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked why I'm telling you, and lying to you would cost more than the truth." He smiled thinly. "Also because you should understand your position. If those letters surface, they don't destroy me. I was a witness. Brack gave the order; the four men struck the match. The letters destroy *them*, and the organization that protected them, and the magistrate, and half the comfortable machinery of Rivergate. Which means every one of those people has the same interest I do in seeing those letters burn — and unlike me, they won't wait politely in your hold while you decide."

"So you admit they exist."

"I admit that Elias Vann was a thorough man who wrote constantly and hid things well. I searched that trading post myself, three years ago, after his second filing was rejected. I found assay tags. I found receipts. I did not find letters." His eyes narrowed. "Which makes me curious how a mail pilot found in one night what I missed in three days."

"Maybe you weren't looking for paper," Hazel said. "Maybe you were looking for gold."

The silence stretched. Outside, the river took another willow with a crack like a rifle shot, and the Kestrel strained at her lines.

* * *

Junie spoke from the foot of the ladder, where she'd settled herself with the brandy bottle and a pistol resting openly on her knee. "He's good," she said to Hazel, conversational, as if Hale weren't there. "You notice? He gives you a true thing so you'll trust the false thing wrapped around it. Confession as bait. Union organizers called it the honeyed hook."

"And what would you call it?" Hale asked her, with something almost like respect.

"Tuesday," Junie said. "I testified against your outfit with three lawyers in the room trying to make me sound crazy, and I still put two of your foremen in front of a commission. You want to talk about what I know, Mr. Hale, I'll talk all night. But my friend here has a proposal, and I'd hear it before you spend any more of your charm."

Hazel drew a breath. This was the part she'd turned over all morning, the part that had woken her from a thin sleep at dawn with her heart hammering. It was insane. It was also the only door she could see.

"Here's the bargain," she said. "Not the map. The map isn't mine to trade — it goes to the registry in Mara Vann's name, and then it's public record, and no one burns public record fast enough to matter. What I'm offering is your testimony. A sworn statement about the night of the fire. Brack's orders, the four men, what you saw from the ridge. Filed alongside the claim registration, witnessed, dated before the permits lapse."

Hale stared at her. "You want me to testify against my own employers."

"I want you to stop being their instrument. There's a difference, and I think you felt it the night you sat on that ridge and knew you were watching something you'd never get back out from under." She leaned forward. "In exchange: we release you at the fork junction, whole and unharmed, and I file nothing about your part in any of this. No ambush at the cairn. No shooting of my friend. As far as any record shows, Corliss Hale came downriver as a witness, not a hunter. You walk away — not from justice, but into it, on your own feet, choosing it. Which is more than you gave Elias Vann."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow we run the flood with you in the hold, and the day after that we reach Rivergate, and I put those letters in front of the first honest clerk I can find, and your name is in them whether you swear to anything or not. Refusing doesn't protect you, Mr. Hale. It just means history happens *to* you instead of *with* you."

He was quiet for a long moment. The rain filled it.

"You understand what you're asking," he said finally. "If I sign anything, the syndicate doesn't send another enforcer. They send men who don't ask questions. I'd be a dead man inside a season."

"Probably," Hazel said. "Unless you're gone first. Downriver, over the mountains, wherever men like you go when they stop being useful. You know the routes better than I do." She shrugged. "I'm not offering you safety. I'm offering you a choice. It's the only currency I have, and frankly it's worth more than the gold."

Hale looked down at his bound wrists. When he raised his head, the frozen-river eyes had thawed into something more dangerous and more human: a man doing arithmetic he'd been avoiding for fifteen years.

"Show me one letter," he said. "One. In Vann's hand, naming the fire. Show me that, and I'll believe the rest exists, and we'll discuss terms."

Hazel rose. At the ladder she paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at him.

"You'll see them when the ink's dry," she said. "Faith, Mr. Hale. Elias died on faith. Seems fitting you'd live on it."

She climbed into the rain, and Junie followed, and below them the enforcer of the Rivergate syndicate sat alone in the dark with the engine ticking and the river rising, and for the first time in fifteen years, Corliss Hale had no idea what happened next.

Up top, Junie spat over the side and grinned through the pain. "Four days to noon," she said. "Let's go register a dead man's fortune."
