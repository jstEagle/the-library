## 18. The Long Run Downriver

The rain came down like the sky had finally lost its temper, and Junie Okonkwo loved it.

Not the rain itself—she'd cursed it for three days straight, cursed the river it swelled, cursed the way it turned the Kestrel's patched hull into a drumhead for the whole storm to play. But the rain meant cloud, and cloud meant the syndicate's seaplane couldn't fly. She'd watched it circle twice that morning, a gray shape worrying at the gray sky like a fly at a wound, and then the ceiling had dropped to nothing and the pilot had done the arithmetic every bush flyer knows by heart and turned back east.

"Your friend's gone home," she shouted over the engine.

Hazel was wedged against the portside gunwale with her splinted wrist cradled in her lap, looking like a woman who'd been assembled from spare parts and forgotten on the workbench. "He's not my friend."

"He's your problem, though." Junie jerked her chin aft, toward the hold, where Corliss Hale sat bound to the engine mount with his own belt and a length of rope Junie had tied with the particular spite of a woman who'd been blacklisted for six years and had opinions about it. He sat very still, the way men do when they've decided stillness is a strategy. Water ran off the tarpaulin and pooled around his boots. "And your problem's about to get wet."

"Then he'd better learn to hold his breath."

Junie laughed—a short, ugly, wonderful laugh—and put the Kestrel's nose downriver.

The river had stopped being a river somewhere in the night. It was a brown ocean with opinions now, a mile wide in the flats, choked with debris, whole trees sailing past like drowned giants with their arms out. The Sable Fork had come down off the hills in a hurry, and everything it had torn loose on the way was riding with them: fence rails, a dead pig, half of somebody's roof, spinning slow in the eddies like a hat someone had dropped.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Hazel called.

"Because the registry office closes at noon tomorrow, because the permits die with it, and because I have not spent six years eating river fish and hating myself to watch you get polite at the last minute." Junie wiped rain out of her eyes with the back of one glove. "Also because the alternative is Hale's employers, and I'd rather drown."

"Fair."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

The rapids showed up the way bad news always does—suddenly, and with a sound you feel in your teeth before you hear it in your ears.

Junie had run water her whole life. She'd grown up on a stern-wheeler her father kept alive with wire and stubbornness, and she'd learned to read a river the way other people read faces: the lie in the smooth water, the truth in the foam. But this was different. The last rapids before Rivergate were a mile of granite chaos that the charts called, with colonial delicacy, the Devil's Teeth, and the monsoon had turned them from bad to biblical. The channel she knew was gone. There was just water now, and water's mood.

She throttled down and let the current carry them in sideways, because that was the only way in.

"Hold onto something that loves you!" she shouted, and heard Hazel's laugh—high, cracked, real—over the roar.

The first wave hit them like a freight train that had decided, late in life, to become a wall of water. The Kestrel's bow punched through it and the whole boat stood up on her tail like a rearing horse, engine screaming, and for one long, glittering moment they were pointed at the sky. Junie saw the next wave coming over Hazel's shoulder—a green hill with a white mane—and steered into it with her whole body, hip against the wheel, boots braced, the way her father had taught her: *you don't fight the river, girl, you argue with it, and you never let it think it's won.*

They came down hard enough to knock the breath out of everyone aboard. Somewhere in the hold, Hale swore with feeling. Junie chose to find that encouraging. A man who swore was a man who still expected to live.

"Left!" Hazel screamed. "Left channel—there's a hole—"

"I see it!"

She didn't, not really. She saw white water and brown water and a rock the size of a chapel wearing a wig of spray, and she steered by faith and fury, threading the gap with maybe a boat's width on either side, the Kestrel's patched hull groaning like a widow at a funeral. The patch held. She'd have kissed it if she could reach it. Six hours in the drowned trading post with a sheet of canvas, a pot of pitch, and Hazel holding the lamp while the rain tried to put it out—and the patch held.

The river tried everything else. It threw a log at them that missed by an arm's length. It spun them broadside in a chute and held them there, trembling, while Junie stood on the throttle and argued, and argued, and the engine smoked and gave, and gave, and finally the Kestrel broke free and ran.

The last quarter mile was almost gentle, the way the worst days sometimes end with a kindness nobody meant. They shot out of the Teeth into a long brown reach of flooded forest, water over the banks, the current still fast but sane, and Junie realized her hands were aching because she'd been strangling the wheel for a mile without breathing.

She breathed.

"Junie." Hazel's voice, small and strange. "Junie, look at your arm."

Junie looked. There was a gash along her forearm, shallow, ugly, bleeding freely into the rain, and she had absolutely no memory of getting it. "Huh," she said. "I've been shot at, drowned, and portaged through the Devil's Teeth, and a *log* got me."

"Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm the pilot. Pilots don't sit down."

"Pilots who bleed into the bilge sink the boat. Sit. *Down.*"

There was something in Hazel's voice that hadn't been there before—command, and under the command, worry, and under the worry something Junie had spent the whole trip pretending not to notice. She sat. Hazel rummaged one-handed in the med kit, wrapped the arm with the brisk, ungentle competence of a woman who'd patched up prospectors in a leaking cabin, and tied the knot with her teeth and her good hand, and Junie watched her do it and thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the river: *oh. Oh, that's what this is.*

"You're staring," Hazel said, not looking up.

"Rain in my eyes."

"Liar."

"River in my ears, then."

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar that had been dry land a week ago, high enough to hold, low enough that the engine could be worked on. The rain had settled into a steady, businesslike drumming, the storm's long middle age after its furious youth. Hazel rigged the tarp. Junie rebuilt the fuel line with numb fingers. In the hold, Hale sat in the dark and said nothing, which Junie had decided was the most honest thing he'd ever done.

They ate cold rice and smoked fish, and Hazel produced, from some interior pocket of her flight jacket, a dented flask, and they passed it back and forth without ceremony.

"To Elias," Hazel said.

"To Mara," Junie said. "Who gets the gold and never has to know what it cost."

"She'll know. I'm going to tell her everything."

"That'll be a first. Truth-telling. Dangerous habit." Junie took another pull and looked out at the river, black and enormous and going somewhere, same as them. "You know what my father used to say? He'd say, *Junie, the river doesn't care what you deserve. It only cares what you can carry.*"

"Cheerful man."

"Riverboat man. Same thing." She handed the flask back. "We're carrying a lot tomorrow."

"We're carrying enough."

They sat with that. Somewhere upstream, thunder grumbled like an old man asked to move his cart. The patched hull ticked and settled as it dried. And from the hold, unexpectedly, Hale's voice, level as a clerk reading a manifest:

"The syndicate will send men to Rivergate. Not the plane. Men. They'll be at the registry by noon whether you make it or not."

Junie was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, lantern in hand, glaring down into the hold. Hazel got there a half-step behind her, and the two of them stood over the bound man like a jury of two.

"You've got a lot of opinions," Junie said, "for a man tied to an engine."

"I've got a bargain," Hale said. "I made it with Dunmore. I keep my bargains. Ask anyone who's survived me." Something moved in his face that might have been, on a more expressive man, humor. "But I keep them exactly. Not one minute more. Noon tomorrow, and whatever happens at that registry desk happens with the whole world watching."

"Then we'd better not be late," Hazel said quietly.

Junie looked at her—at this woman she'd known six days and would know the rest of her life, this woman who'd shot the rapids with a busted wrist and a dead man's map and a prisoner in the hold, this woman who'd chosen, against every instinct Junie herself had ever trusted, to be *seen*—and she felt the grin come up out of her like the river coming down.

"Noon," Junie said. She spat in her palm and held it out. "We make it by noon, or we die trying, and either way, Hale buys the drinks."

In the hold, Corliss Hale said, with what was unmistakably, finally, a smile: "I don't drink."

"Then you can watch."

Hazel spat in her palm and took Junie's hand, and the rain came down on the gravel bar, and the river ran on toward Rivergate, carrying them and the morning and whatever came after.

Junie was up before first light, arm bandaged, engine warm, wheel in her hands.

"Noon," she told the river. "You hear me? Noon."

The river, being a river, said nothing—and ran.
