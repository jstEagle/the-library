## 19. Registry Office, Last Day

The Kestrel came down the last reach of the Rivergate with her hull singing and her seams weeping, and Hazel stood at the bow with the compass in her pocket and counted church bells that weren't ringing yet.

Half past nine. The town rose out of the rain in pieces—first the smoke, then the tin roofs, then the long gray face of the customs shed, then Pier Nine with its pilings black as teeth. Somewhere behind them, miles back upriver, the syndicate seaplane had turned tail into the weather, and somewhere behind that, Corliss Hale sat bound in the hold of a boat he could have sunk with a word, going to do a thing no enforcer had ever done.

"Ten minutes to the pier," Junie called from the wheelhouse. Her voice was rough. She'd been awake thirty hours on a shoulder that should have put her flat, and she steered like she didn't feel it, which Hazel knew was a lie, because she'd watched her change the bandage at dawn by the light of a storm lantern and seen what the wound had done overnight.

"Take ten minutes," Hazel said. "We have until noon."

"We have until noon," Junie agreed, "if nothing else goes wrong."

Something went wrong.

It announced itself as a smell first—hot metal and scorched oil threading through the rain—and then as a sound, a knock from deep in the engine's chest like a fist on a door. Junie's head came around. For three full seconds neither of them said anything, and the Kestrel ran on toward the pier with her heart failing.

"No," Junie said, to the engine, in the tone of a woman who had been betrayed before and recognized the pattern. "No, you don't. Not today."

She got them in anyway. It was not pretty. The engine died twice in the last hundred yards and caught again both times, coughing, and then they were sliding along the pier wall and Hazel had the bow line in her hands and Junie killed the throttle for good, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Junie stood over the open engine hatch with her good arm and looked down into it the way you look into an open grave.

"Bearing," she said. "Maybe. Maybe worse than a bearing." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist and left a smear of grease across one cheekbone. "How long to noon?"

Hazel checked her father's watch. "Two hours and eleven minutes."

"Then go." Junie already had the toolbox out. "I'll keep her alive or I'll die trying, but the registry closes at noon and you are not standing on this pier when it does. Go. Run."

Hazel hesitated—one breath, two—with the rain coming down and the pier swaying under her boots and the whole journey suddenly balanced on the question of whether she could walk away from this boat, this woman, this fight, even for two hours.

"Hazel." Junie looked up. Under all the grime and exhaustion her eyes were absolutely steady. "I have been blacklisted, shot, flooded, portaged, and held together with baling wire for six days. One bearing will not finish me. *Go.*"

Hazel went.

* * *

The registry office occupied the ground floor of the land commissioner's building on Cordwainer Street, behind a door that had been painted green so many times the panels had gone soft, and Hazel ran the four blocks from the pier through water shin-deep in the low places with the oilcloth packet pressed against her ribs under her coat.

The streets were running with monsoon. Shopkeepers were sandbagging their thresholds. A boy ferried passengers on a raft made of oil drums through what had been the market square two weeks ago. Nobody looked at her twice; everybody in Rivergate was running somewhere in the rain, and that was the whole trick of being forgettable, she thought wildly—it worked right up until the day you needed to be remembered.

The office door was locked.

She stood there with her hand on the handle and her heart slamming, and then she saw the sign, the little card in the wire frame, hand-lettered: *CLOSED FOR FLOOD. REOPEN WHEN RIVER FALLS.*

No.

She hammered on the glass with the side of her fist. "Hello! Hello, is anyone—" Hammered again. "Registry business! It's urgent, it's a filing, the permits expire *today*—"

From somewhere inside: footsteps. Slow ones. Then a shape resolved behind the fogged glass—a small round man in shirtsleeves and suspenders, gray-haired, spectacles pushed up on his forehead, holding a storm lantern against the gloom. He peered out at her without unlocking anything.

"We're closed," he said through the glass. "River's over Cordwainer. Come back when it falls."

"The concession permits expire at noon," Hazel said. "Today. If I don't file before noon, a claim dies. A man died for it. Two men, if you count the one who's about to help me file it against his own employers."

The man behind the glass was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the awning above them both.

"You're the pilot," he said. "Thornbury. Freight runs, upper river." Of course he knew her. Clerks knew everyone; that was the whole architecture of a place like Rivergate. "You've never once come in here."

"I try not to need things from offices," Hazel said. "Please. Open the door."

He studied her a long moment—the soaked coat, the split lip she'd forgotten she had, the packet clutched to her chest like a child. Then he turned the key.

Inside smelled of paper and lamp oil and damp. Filing cabinets stood in ranks ankle-deep in shadow, and someone had stacked sandbags across the back wall where the river had already breathed through the foundation once. The clerk lit a second lamp, turned the sign on his desk clock so she could see it—10:04—and said, "Mabry. Deputy registrar, these twenty-two years. What are we filing?"

"A mineral claim." She unwrapped the oilcloth on his desk with hands that would not quite stay still. The map came out first, Elias Vann's careful ink on waxed linen, the Sable Fork drawn in his own hand, the cairn marked with a cross and coordinates. Then the assay tags, strung on wire. Then the letters, bundled and tied. Then the affidavit pages she and Junie had written by lantern light on the boat, blotched with river water, signed in three hands.

Mabry read. He read the way old clerks read, completely, his lips moving, his finger tracing the coordinates, and the clock ticked, and outside the rain came down on Cordwainer Street, and Hazel stood on wet floorboards and tried to breathe.

"This claim," Mabry said at last, "was filed before. Twice." He pulled a ledger off the shelf behind him without looking, opened it to a ribbon marker, and turned it toward her. Elias Vann's name, in Elias Vann's blunt capitals, dated seven years back, and beside it in the registrar's column: *REJECTED — INSUFFICIENT PROOF OF WORKING INTEREST.* And again, four years later: *REJECTED — DISPUTED TITLE.*

"Disputed by whom?" Hazel asked, though she knew.

"The Rivergate Consolidated Mining and Concession Company," Mabry said, "filed a counter-claim both times. They have a man whose whole job is disputes." He closed the ledger gently, as if it were a coffin lid. "This is going to be contested, Miss Thornbury. Before the ink dries. You understand that."

"I understand that better than you know," Hazel said. "Which is why my third signature is coming through that door within the hour, and why you're going to want to be sitting down when he does."

Mabry looked at her. "Who?"

"Corliss Hale."

* * *

At 10:40, Junie came through the door of the registry office carrying a canvas tool bag, soaked to the waist, grease to the elbows, and grinning like a woman who had beaten the devil at cards.

"Bearing," she announced. "And a cracked fuel line, which I patched with the inner tube off my spare hat. The Kestrel lives. Barely. She's sulking, but she lives." She dropped onto the bench by the door, saw the map spread on the clerk's desk, and her grin faded into something quieter. "That's it, then. That's everything."

"That's everything," Hazel said. "Where is he?"

"Walking up Cordwainer Street unbound, unarmed, and unaccompanied, because I told him if he ran I'd radio every launch on the river with his description and a reward." Junie flexed her bandaged shoulder, winced, kept flexing. "He said—" She stopped. Shook her head. "He said he gave his word on a gravel bar in a flood, and a man who breaks his word in a flood will break it anywhere. His words. I don't know what to make of him, Hazel. Six days I've been trying to make something of him and I can't."

"You don't have to," Hazel said. "Just watch."

Corliss Hale came in at 11:12.

He looked, Hazel thought, exactly like what he was: a big, deliberate man in a good coat gone wrong at the hem, with river mud dried gray on his boots and a bruise along his jaw from the fog fight at the cairn. He carried himself the way he always had, unhurried, like a man who owned the room's schedule—but there was something different in his face now, some load set down or picked up, she couldn't tell which. He stopped inside the door. He looked at the map on the desk for a long time.

"I drew a line through those coordinates once," he said. "Seven years ago. Told the company the ground was worthless." He took off his hat. Held it. "It wasn't worthless. I knew it wasn't. That's why they sent me."

Mabry had gone very still behind his desk. "Mr. Hale. You are—I know your name, sir. Your name is on half the dispute filings in this ledger."

"Yes," Hale said. "It is." He stepped forward and laid a folded document on the desk beside the map: typed, letterheaded, stamped with the syndicate's seal. "Affidavit of Corliss Hale, agent of Rivergate Consolidated. In it I swear that the Vann claim was staked and worked in good faith in the year of the first filing; that the company's counter-claims were filed on my false report and the directors' knowledge of it; that the original stake markers were destroyed by company crews under my supervision; and that the camp on the Alder Flats was burned on company orders, the night Elias Vann was shot." His voice did not change at all. That was the terrible thing about it, Hazel thought. It did not change at all. "Signed and witnessed. Witnessed by Hazel Thornbury and June Okonkwo, who may consider themselves witnesses whether they like it or not."

"I don't like it," Junie said. "But I'll sign it again."

"Why?" Hazel asked. She had to. She had bargained with him on a rising river with the letters in her hand, and he had given his word, and words from Corliss Hale were currency she still didn't trust herself to count. "Why now? You spent years burying this man's claim. You were *there*."

Hale looked at the map a while. At the cross marking the cairn. At the neat inked hand of a prospector who had died on a cabin floor trading a promise for a piece of linen.

"Elias Vann pulled me out of the Sable Fork flood of '09," he said finally. "Carried me a mile on his back with a busted leg of my own. Never told anyone. Never asked anything." He put his hat back on. "I burned his camp anyway, because orders, and I told myself orders were the same as necessity. Then a pilot fished me out of a boat hold and offered me the river instead of a rope, and I found I couldn't tell myself the story anymore." He glanced at her, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind his eyes that might have been grief, or its twin. "Your bargain holds, Miss Thornbury. I testify to the jumped claim and the fire, and then I'm gone. But I keep my word. Ask anyone who's ever bought me. It's the only thing I ever sold that was real."

Mabry cleared his throat. "Gentlemen—ladies. It is 11:31. If this filing is to be entered before the permit lapse at noon, I need names. Whose name goes on the claim?"

"Mara Vann," Hazel said. "Elias's daughter. Estranged. She doesn't know yet—that's mine to fix after. But it's hers. It was always meant to be hers."

Mabry wrote it. The nib scratched. Outside, the rain eased for the first time in days, thinning to a whisper on the awning, and the light through the fogged window went briefly silver.

Signatures: Hazel Thornbury, pilot, witness to discovery. Junie Okonkwo, engineer, witness to the cairn and the vein. Corliss Hale, for the defense of the title no one expected him to defend. Mabry blotted each page, checked the coordinates against the survey rolls, checked the permit expiry against the wall calendar, and muttered the whole time in the manner of clerks everywhere, and Hazel stood with her hands flat on the edge of the desk and watched the clock.

11:44.

11:51.

Junie found her hand under the desk and squeezed it, once, hard enough to hurt, and Hazel squeezed back.

11:56. Mabry filled in the registration number in the great ledger, the same ledger that had rejected Elias Vann twice, and dipped the stamp, and looked up.

"For the record," he said, "the Rivergate Consolidated will contest this by nightfall."

"Let them," said Junie.

"Let them," said Hale.

"Stamp it," said Hazel.

Mabry brought the stamp down.

The sound it made was very small—a soft, decisive thump of rubber on paper—and it was the loudest thing Hazel Dunmore had ever heard. *REGISTERED. MARA VANN. CLAIM No. 4471, SABLE FORK.* Eleven fifty-eight and forty seconds by the deputy registrar's clock, and the claim that had been buried twice was alive, on the books, in the name of a daughter who didn't yet know her father had kept writing to her for nineteen years.

Nobody cheered. There was too much rain in the room for cheering, in a manner of speaking. Junie laughed once, a short cracked sound, and pressed her bandaged arm against her ribs. Hale stood by the door with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral. Mabry blotted the page, poured four cups of tea from a kettle Hazel hadn't noticed until now, and said, "Twenty-two years at this desk. First time anyone's beat the clock by ninety seconds. Drink your tea."

Hazel drank hers. Her hands shook around the cup, and she let them.

* * *

They came out of the registry office just past noon into a street gone strangely bright, the clouds torn open overhead in ragged strips with sun falling through in columns, steam rising off the flooded cobblestones like the whole town was exhaling. Down on the river the water was high and brown and running fast, but running *down*, and somewhere on it the Kestrel waited at Pier Nine with a patched fuel line and a bad temper.

Hale paused at the bottom of the steps. He settled his coat, put his hat on properly, and became, visibly, once more, a man the syndicate would be looking for.

"They'll know by tonight," he said. "By morning there'll be questions in three towns and men on the river. You should take Mara Vann and get ahead of it—or don't, and stand. Your choice. You've earned choices." He looked at Hazel. "The letters. You'll deliver them?"

"Tonight," Hazel said. "Every word."

He nodded once, turned, and walked away up Cordwainer Street through the steam and the light, a broad straight back getting smaller, and Junie watched him go with her head tipped.

"You think he runs?" she asked.

"I think," Hazel said slowly, "that a man who keeps his word in a flood keeps it other places too. But I think he's done being found." She turned toward the pier, toward the river, toward the last thing left undone, and her stomach tightened with a fear entirely new—not of syndicate men, not of rising water, but of a stranger's face when she heard how her father died. "Come on. We've got a boat to limp home and a daughter to find."

"She's not hard to find," Junie said. "I asked at the customs shed while you were hammering on doors. Mara Vann teaches school. Greenfield mission, up the coast road." She grinned, tired and crooked. "There's a mail plane that flies out tomorrow morning. Pilot's supposed to be forgettable, but I hear she's been leaving a name in ledgers lately."

Hazel looked at her, and the sun came through the broken clouds and lit the whole flooded street gold, and for the first time in longer than she could measure, Hazel Dunmore was not afraid of being seen.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "One more flight."

"One more flight," Junie agreed, and spat in her palm, and held it out.
