## 20. Mara

The registry clerk in Corvane had given me the address the way he gave out everything—reluctantly, and only after I'd shown him the stamped filing with his own office's seal still wet enough to smudge. *Mara Vann, seamstress, 14 Lantern Row.* He'd written it on a slip of paper and slid it across the counter like it might bite him.

"Family of claimants come around asking for trouble," he'd said. "You want to be the one carrying it to her door?"

"I already am," I told him.

It took me two days to get to Corvane. The mail run went as far as the river junction; from there it was a rattling bus with a floor full of chicken crates and a driver who stopped three times to argue with people he owed money to. I sat in the back with my satchel between my knees and felt every mile of the road through my spine. The sprained wrist had stiffened into a dull ache that flared whenever I braced against a turn. Junie had splinted it properly before we parted at Pier Nine—she'd wrapped it with the same methodical care she gave her engine, muttering that I flew with my hands and ought to treat them like moving parts.

"You'll go see her alone?" she'd asked.

"She lost one parent to the Rivergate. She doesn't need two strangers standing over her."

Junie had considered that, then nodded once. "I'll be at the Kestrel. Patching what Hale's men didn't manage to sink." A pause. "Take your time. Some cargo you can't rush."

Corvane was a mill town pretending it wasn't dying. The sawmill's whistle still blew the hours, but half the windows on Lantern Row were papered over, and the ones that weren't held the particular darkness of rooms where nobody had replaced a curtain in years. Number 14 sat at the end of the street, a narrow house with a narrow garden, and in the garden—this surprised me—a riot of beans and marigolds growing like they were trying to make up for everything else on the street.

A woman was kneeling among the bean rows when I came up the path. Dark hair pinned back with a pencil. Sleeves rolled. Hands in the dirt.

"Mara Vann?" I said.

She turned, and I saw Elias in her face—not the ruined man who'd died in my cabin, but the younger version from the photograph he'd kept folded inside the map's oilcloth. Same jaw. Same set to the mouth, like she was permanently braced against bad news.

"Who's asking?"

"My name is Hazel Dunmore. I fly mail and medicine out of Rivergate." I stopped at the edge of the garden because it seemed wrong to step on something she'd grown. "Your father sent me."

The pencil slipped from her hair. She caught it before it hit the ground, which told me she was quick, and then she stood very slowly, which told me she'd learned not to trust quick answers about Elias Vann.

"He's dead," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she wiped her hands on her apron, walked past me without a word, and left the front door open behind her.

* *

The house smelled of starch and cedar. Mara made tea because her hands needed something to do—I recognized that too—and set two cups on a table scarred with needle pricks. Bolts of cloth stood against one wall like a congregation. A dress form wore half a coat, one sleeve finished, the other hanging empty.

"Elias Vann," she said, sitting across from me with her spine straight as a tent pole. "He wrote twice in nineteen years. Once when I was twelve—a birthday card with a riverboat on it. Once last spring." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands closed around her cup like it was warm in a cold room. "Both times he said he'd found something. Both times he said he'd explain soon. So." She lifted her chin. "Tell me how he died. All of it. Don't arrange it for me."

So I didn't.

I told her about the smoke column I'd spotted off the Alder Flats and the medevac call that shouldn't have been mine. About landing in the ash and finding him gut-shot beside his burned camp, propped against a wheel hub with his rifle across his knees, still trying to guard a pile of cinders. About loading him into the plane while he cursed me for wasting fuel, and the flight back with him bleeding through my spare shirts, telling me about a vein of ore so rich the syndicate had spent years making sure no registry would ever hear its name.

I told her about the cabin. Three days of fever and lucidity trading places. How he'd talked to her more than to me in those three days—apologies mostly, and instructions, and once, near midnight, a story about teaching a girl of six to pan gravel in a creek while her mother pretended not to worry.

Mara's hands had gone white around the cup. I kept going.

"The third night he knew. He asked me for my word that I'd register his claim before the concession permits expired at monsoon's end—in your name, not mine. In exchange he gave me this."

I took the oilcloth bundle from my satchel and unwrapped it on the table: the hand-drawn map, soft as cloth from handling, the Sable Fork rendered in Elias's careful surveyor's hand. And beneath it, the letters. Nine of them, unsent, rubber-banded together, each addressed in the same hand: *Mara.*

"And this." I set down the registry certificate, the clerk's stamp still crisp. "Registered four days ago. The Vann Claim, upper Sable Fork. In your name."

For a moment she didn't move. Then she reached out and touched the map with one fingertip, the way you'd touch something you suspected might be hot.

"He registered it? It went through?"

"It went through. With corroboration from a witness I'd rather not describe over tea." That was a problem for another chapter, and I meant to keep it that way until I understood it myself. "The permits expire at noon tomorrow. It's done. It's yours."

Mara picked up the letters. She didn't open them. She just held the bundle against her chest and looked at the map, and I watched something move through her face that had no name I knew—grief and fury and a kind of stunned inheritance all tangled together.

"Nineteen years," she said quietly. "He had this the whole time. And instead of coming to me, he—" She stopped. Started again. "Why didn't he send these?"

"Because every time he got close, the syndicate took it away from him. His first filings were rejected under pressure. Men burned his camp twice. The second fire is the one that killed him—or started the chain that did." I hesitated, then decided she deserved the whole weight of it. "There's a man named Corliss Hale. He works for the syndicate. He was there the night your father's camp burned—the first time. Your father named him in these letters. That's part of why they were never safe to send."

She opened the top letter then. Just the first one, dated eleven years back. I watched her read it, and I watched her jaw do exactly what Elias's jaw had done in my cabin when the fever broke—set hard against whatever wanted to come through it.

"He writes like he talks," she said finally. "Like apologizing costs extra."

"It did, for him. Everything cost extra. That was the disease of the place he ended up." I leaned forward. "Mara. I'm not going to pretend I knew him well. I knew him for four days, and most of those he was dying. But I know what he traded to keep this claim alive, and I know what he refused to trade. When Hale's men had him shot and bleeding, he could have bought his life with the map. He chose to spend his last breaths making sure it reached you instead. Whatever else he was—whatever nineteen years of silence cost you—that's also true, and it's yours to weigh."

The tea went cold between us. Outside, a mill whistle blew the hour, and somewhere down the street a dog started up and thought better of it.

"Why you?" she asked at last. "Why would a stranger do all this? Running rivers, getting shot at—" her eyes flicked to my splinted wrist, "—for a dead prospector's promise. What did you get out of it?"

And there it was. The question I'd been turning over since the registry stamp came down, the one I'd been avoiding in the honest dark of the Kestrel's hold.

"A debt," I said. "Not his. Mine. My father flew contraband for the same syndicate to pay what they claimed he owed. He died doing it, and they transferred the debt to me. I've spent six years being small and forgettable and paying them in silence." I turned the compass over in my hands—my father's compass, the one that had carried Elias's map hidden in its casing for three hundred miles of river. "Elias was the first man who showed me there was another way to settle accounts. You pay with truth or you don't pay at all. So I registered his claim, and I put my own name in the syndicate's ledger where they can see it, and I'm done being forgettable."

Mara studied me for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, cracked sound with tears right behind it.

"You're both the same," she said. "Stubborn as riverbed rock, both of you. He'd have liked you." She set down the letters and pressed her palms flat against the table, steadying herself. "What happens now? They won't just let it stand. The syndicate."

"No. They won't." I pulled the last item from my satchel—a copy of the filed claim, and beneath it, a sworn statement in Hale's own hand, extracted by bargain and rising water, corroborating the jumped stake. "But the claim is registered, the record is public, and there are men in the territorial office who've been waiting years for a reason to look at the syndicate's permit books. Your father's claim gives them that reason. What you do with it is yours. Sell it, work it, burn the paperwork—it's yours. That was the promise. The rest is just weather."

Mara looked at the deed, at the map, at the letters stacked in their rubber-banded column of nineteen silent years. When she spoke again her voice had gone rough.

"There's a room upstairs I've kept locked since I was twelve. Everything he ever sent me is in it. Birthday cards. A creek stone he mailed once, wrapped in newspaper, no letter." She laughed again, wetter this time. "I used to tell people my father was dead. It was easier than explaining." Her eyes came up to mine. "Now I have to decide if he was dead and this is a ghost, or if he was alive all along and just couldn't find the road home."

"Maybe both," I said. "Rivers fork. People do too. Doesn't mean they stop being rivers."

She stood, gathered the letters, and held them a moment longer. Then she crossed the little room and put her hand on my shoulder—brief, firm, the grip of a woman who mended things for a living and knew exactly how much pressure torn fabric could take.

"Thank you," she said. "Not for the gold. For finishing it. For carrying him the last mile he couldn't walk." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Stay for supper. I won't hear no. You look like you've been eating riverboat food for a month, and I owe you a meal I can actually cook."

I stayed. We ate beans from her garden and bread that tasted like someone's actual life, and she read the letters aloud after dark, one by one, laughing and swearing and crying in roughly equal measure, while the mill whistles marked the hours of a town that didn't know yet what had landed in its midst.

It was nearly midnight when she looked up from the last letter and went very still.

"Hazel," she said slowly. "This one's dated three weeks ago. The night before he died." She smoothed the page flat with both hands. "It's not to me. It's addressed to *you.*"

She turned it toward me across the table, and Elias Vann's handwriting waited on the page like a man holding out his hand.

*To the pilot who finds this—*

*I know you're reading this because I'm dead, and because you kept your word. So here's mine...*
