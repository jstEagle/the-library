## 21. Settling Accounts

### Hazel

The registry office had a public ledger, and it turned out the clerk kept a second one — an unofficial book of grievances that men like Corliss Hale preferred never to see daylight. Hazel asked for both.

She stood at the counter on a Tuesday morning with rain still dripping off her flight jacket and read her father's name aloud into the room: *Amos Thornbury*. The debt. The flights he'd run for the syndicate when no legitimate carrier would hire him, the cargo he'd stopped asking about somewhere over the Sable Fork, the crash they'd called weather and she'd always known was something else. She'd spent eleven years paying for it in silence, flying forgettable routes into forgettable strips, keeping her head down so well she'd nearly disappeared herself.

The room was half-full — clerks, a couple of freight agents waiting on stamps, a woman with a dog tied outside under the awning. Hazel's voice came out steadier than she expected.

"Eleven years ago the Rivergate Consolidated Mining Syndicate held a debt over my father's head. Not a debt of money. A debt of fear. They told him if he ever talked about what he carried, or what he saw, they'd take his license, then his plane, then whatever else they decided he owed. When he died, they transferred the account to me. I've been flying their errands and their silences ever since."

A pen scratched somewhere and stopped. The dog barked once outside.

"I'm talking now," she said. "Because a man named Elias Vann died in my cabin with a bullet in him, and because his claim was jumped by these same people twenty years ago, and because Corliss Hale stood in the smoke of Vann's burned camp and did nothing — worse than nothing. I have letters. I have assay tags. I have Hale's own testimony, given freely, that the fire was set to clear Vann off ground the syndicate wanted. And I have eleven years of ledgers showing exactly how a debt like mine gets built, flight by flight, until a pilot can't tell anymore which cargo is hers and which is theirs."

She laid the papers on the counter one at a time, like dealing cards she'd finally learned to stop hiding. The clerk — old Mbeki, who'd stamped Mara Vann's claim with four minutes left on the permit clock and hadn't blinked once while doing it — took each page without comment and entered it into the record.

When she finished, the room was very quiet. Then the woman with the dog came inside, dripping, and said, "My husband flew for them too. Before you were born." And after her, another voice, and another. It went on past noon.

Hazel walked out into the gray drizzle feeling lighter than she had any right to feel. Not clean — nothing about it was clean. Her father was still dead. Elias was still dead. Junie still carried a slug in her shoulder that would ache every cold season for the rest of her life. But the debt was void now, struck from the books by disclosure instead of payment, and when she'd imagined this moment over the years — usually at altitude, alone, talking to the compass — she'd always pictured it costing more than it did. That was the trick of silence, she understood finally. You paid interest your whole life on a principal that evaporated the moment you said its name out loud.

### Junie

Junie heard about the disclosure secondhand, from a dockhand at Pier Nine who'd heard it from a customs man who'd heard it from God knew where, and by evening it was all over the riverfront in the way news traveled there — fast, embellished, mostly true. By the next morning, three separate boat owners had offered her work.

That was the strange part. Not the offers themselves but the manner of them: casual, sideways, as though the blacklist had been a rumor everyone had agreed to stop repeating all at once. Nobody said *I'm sorry* or *we were wrong about you.* One man just handed her a work order for a boiler refit and said, "Can you start Thursday?" as if the intervening two years had been a long weekend.

She'd testified against the syndicate at twenty-six, fresh out of engineering school and full of fire, and she'd watched the fire get answered with a quiet, administrative kind of ruin. No threats. Just names crossed off lists, references gone lukewarm, jobs given to other people with apologetic smiles. She'd learned that a blacklist doesn't need enforcers. It only needs everyone to keep being polite.

Now the politeness had shifted, the way river ice shifts before it goes out — all at once, with a groan nobody wants to claim responsibility for. The syndicate's lawyers were busy enough with Hazel's filings and the provincial inquiry into the Vann claim that nobody upstream had the time or the appetite to punish a mechanic for taking honest work.

Junie took the Thursday job. She also took a moment alone in the Kestrel's engine well, sitting on the deck plates with her bad shoulder propped against the bulkhead, and let herself feel it: not triumph, exactly. Something smaller and harder-won. The right to be ordinary again. To be a woman with a trade instead of a woman with a cause.

There was one piece of unfinished business, and she'd been turning it over for two days. Corliss Hale.

He'd walked out of the inquiry chamber on Friday morning, given his corroborating statement about the jumped claim and the fire, refused the follow-up questions, and simply — gone. No hotel bill, no forwarding address. The provincial police had a warrant drawn up for the syndicate's directors, not for him; his cooperation had bought him a strange, gray liberty. Some people on the docks said he'd taken the northbound steamer. Others said no boat had taken him anywhere, that a man like Hale didn't leave, he just became less visible until he was a story.

Junie had hated him for weeks — properly, thoroughly, with the clean heat of it — and then she'd sat across a table from him during the bargaining while Hazel laid out terms, and she'd seen what Hazel saw: a man more afraid of being named than of being shot. Fear like that wasn't strength. It was just a smaller cage.

Still. He'd been there the night the camp burned. He'd watched Elias Vann bleed into the dirt of his own claim and done the arithmetic that let him walk away. Junie could forgive a lot of things, but arithmetic like that stayed with her.

She was still deciding what she felt when Hazel came down the pier with a paper bag of rivets and a look on her face that meant work.

"You going to sit in that hole all day," Hazel said, "or are you going to help me pull this engine?"

Junie grinned despite herself. "Both. Hand me the wrench."

### Hazel

They worked on the Kestrel for nine days.

It shouldn't have taken nine days. A hull patch, a rebuilt fuel line, a new propeller shaft bearing — Junie could have done it alone in five, six if the weather turned. But neither of them hurried. There was something in the slow rhythm of it that neither wanted to end: Hazel holding plates while Junie drove rivets, Junie reading torque specs aloud while Hazel torched out the warped strut, the two of them eating lunch on the pier with their boots hanging over the water, arguing amiably about whether the Kestrel deserved a new coat of paint or whether paint was vanity and caulking was character.

On the fourth day Hazel asked, quietly, about the shoulder.

"It'll ache in the cold," Junie said, not looking up from the fuel line. "Doc says the range comes back most of the way. Enough to handle a wheel, anyway." She glanced over. "You're doing the math. Don't. I'd do it again. I'd do it slower and duck sooner, but I'd do it."

Hazel nodded and passed her the soldering iron, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

On the sixth day, a letter came for Hazel care of the registry office. No return address. Inside, a single sheet, typed:

*I've said what I'll say. The rest belongs to the men above me, and they'll answer for it or they won't — that's no longer mine to carry. Tell the engineer she was right about me, which will annoy her more than anything else I could send. Tell Vann's daughter nothing, because she has everything she needs and it isn't mine to add to. The compass your father carried — I remember it. He showed it to me once, at Alder Flats, the year before. Said a man ought to know where home points even when he can't go there. I thought it was sentiment. It took me thirty years to learn it was instruction.*

*Don't look for me.*

*C.H.*

Hazel read it twice on the pier and then fed it to the stove in the Kestrel's cabin. Some debts got settled by disclosure. Some got settled by a man walking north into the trees with nothing but his fear and his one good act, and she found she could live with that arithmetic too — not happily, but honestly, which was more than she'd managed in eleven years.

On the ninth day, they launched her.

The Kestrel slid off the ways at high tide with her patched hull riding sweet and level, and Junie took her upriver a mile and back with the engine singing the way engines sing when every tolerance is true, and when she brought her alongside the pier Hazel was standing there with a can of gray paint after all.

"Vanity," Junie said.

"Character," Hazel said, and handed her a brush.

They painted her together as the light went long and gold over the water, two coats, the whole hull, working in easy silence broken only by Junie humming something tuneless and Hazel laughing at the wrong moments. When it was done they sat on the pier and looked at her — the wrecked boat, salvaged, riding high against the pilings with her new paint going silver in the dusk.

"So," Junie said. "What does a pilot do when she's paid her debts, cleared her name, and got nothing left to outrun?"

Hazel looked down the river, toward the strip where her plane waited, toward the mail route she'd fly again come Monday, toward the registry books where her name now appeared in her own right — *Thornbury, H., licensed carrier* — and felt the strangest thing, which was that the horizon ahead looked less like escape and more like arrival.

"I hear the mail runs better," she said, "when the pilot's actually glad to be seen delivering it."

Junie snorted. "Sentiment."

"Instruction," Hazel said, and reached into her jacket pocket, where the compass sat warm against her ribs, its needle steady, pointing — for the first time in eleven years — nowhere she needed to flee from. Somewhere upriver, a girl named Mara Vann was learning to read her father's map. Monday would come soon enough.

But tonight the tide was high, the paint was drying, and Pier Nine had never once, in all her years on this river, looked so much like home.
