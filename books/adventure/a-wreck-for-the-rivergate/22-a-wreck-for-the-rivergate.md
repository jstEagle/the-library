## 22. A Wreck for the Rivergate

The mail plane lifted off the Rivergate strip at first light, the way it always did, and for once Hazel didn't check her mirrors.

That was new. That was the whole of it, really—six months of small new things stacked up like cordwood until she looked back one morning and realized she'd built herself a different life without quite noticing when the work got done. The mirrors still got checked, out of habit and good sense; pilots who stopped checking mirrors ended up as cautionary tales told over bad coffee at every bush strip from here to the coast. But she checked them now the way you check a door you know is locked. Not because something was coming. Because it was good practice.

Below her the interior unrolled in its dry-season colors—the monsoon long gone, the flooded waterways shrunk back into their channels, brown water running fast and clean between banks of green so dense it looked poured rather than grown. The Sable Fork glittered off to the east, and she let herself look at it for three full seconds, which was two more than she'd allowed herself all spring.

Three days up that fork, past the Devil's Teeth, there was a cairn of stacked stones with a legal description attached to it now. *Mara Vann, registered claimant.* The ink on that filing had been dry since before the rains ended, stamped by a clerk whose hands shook so badly he'd had to press the seal twice, and every time Hazel flew over this stretch of country she felt the weight of it settle somewhere behind her sternum—not heavy, exactly. Just present. The way a healed bone is present. You notice it in cold weather. You're glad of it anyway.

She banked south toward the river settlements, mail sacks rustling behind her seat, and caught herself humming. Actually humming. Junie would have laughed herself sick.

* * *

The run itself was ordinary, which was its own kind of miracle. Medicine to the Alder Flats clinic—a crate of penicillin and a letter bundle, and old Marta Osei met her at the strip with her usual complaint about the landing ("You come in low over my beans every time, Miss Thornbury") and her usual embrace afterward, which contradicted the complaint entirely. Feed seed to the Kessler brothers, who were rebuilding their dock for the third time in two years and had stopped blaming the river and started blaming their own carpentry. A sack of schoolbooks up to the mission, where the children ran out to meet the plane like it was a carnival ride, because to them it was—they'd been born after the syndicate's seaplanes stopped ruling these skies, or near enough after, and an airplane meant letters and medicine and not much else.

Hazel signed the delivery book at each stop. Signed it *Hazel Dunmore*, full name, no hesitation, the letters coming out of her hand round and certain in a way they hadn't been a year ago.

There had been a season—she could admit it now, from altitude, from distance—when she'd practiced being forgettable like other people practiced scales. She'd kept her voice level and her answers short and her name off every list she could. She'd paid cash. She'd flown under weather and under radar and under the general attention of the world, and she'd told herself it was prudence, and some of it had been. But underneath the prudence had been the debt, and underneath the debt had been her father, and underneath him had been the simple animal conviction that if anyone ever looked at her too closely, they'd find the wreck of him there and take it out of her hide.

Corliss Hale had looked at her closely enough. And what he'd found—or what she'd made him find, in a fog above a cairn with a bound man in the hold of a leaking boat—had turned out to be worth more than silence ever was.

She thought about Hale sometimes. Less than she used to. He'd walked out of the registry office that last day with his testimony given and his employers' names on the record, and by nightfall he'd vanished the way men like him vanish—into the coast cities, or the territories beyond them, or simply into the general fog of men who'd spent their whole lives being useful to people who'd never once been useful back. The union lawyers said his deposition had cracked the concession case wide open. The papers said less, which suited everyone. Junie said, once, drinking coffee on the Kestrel's patched deck: "Men like that don't get redeemed. They just get redirected." And Hazel had said, "Good," and meant it, and neither of them had spoken of him again.

The syndicate itself hadn't fallen so much as sagged—concessions voided, permits reissued, the toll stations on the Fork abandoned to the river rats and the herons. There were still men upstream wearing white shirts that cost too much, Hazel suspected. There always would be. But they wore them further away now, and quieter, and the registry books in Rivergate town had a clerk who answered to the law instead of the payroll.

Small things. Stacked like cordwood.

* * *

She came down the river approach to Pier Nine just past noon, and there it was.

The Kestrel sat at her mooring looking, frankly, worse than she had a right to. Junie had rebuilt her through the dry months with the patience of a woman performing surgery on a family member—new planking below the waterline, a recaulked hull, an engine block Hazel had personally helped haul three miles through leech-haunted swamp and therefore regarded with a mixture of respect and resentment. From the air the boat looked like what she was: a vessel that had drowned once and decided against it. Her paint was three colors because Junie bought whatever was cheap. Her name had been repainted across the stern in letters slightly too large, because Junie said a boat that had earned her name twice deserved to wear it loud.

And standing on the pier beneath her, one boot up on a bollard, waving both arms in a windmill circle that could be seen from altitude by the legally blind—Junie Okonkwo.

Hazel brought the plane down onto the water beside the pier with a flourish she'd deny under oath, taxied in, and killed the engine to the sound of Junie's voice carrying across the flat calm water: "Took you long enough! I've been standing here since eleven like a fool waiting on the mail!"

"You said noon."

"I said I'd be here around noon. There's a difference. It's called anticipation." Junie caught the bow line Hazel tossed and made it fast, then peered into the cockpit with theatrical suspicion. "Where's my package?"

"Your package is four crates of engine parts, two sacks of rice, and a letter from your union contact that I did not read even though it was sitting right there on top looking extremely readable."

"That's why you're my pilot." Junie hauled the first crate up onto the pier, grunting. "Integrity. Also you owe me for the rice, I'm charging freight."

"You're charging me freight on rice I'm delivering to your own boat."

"Business is business, Dunmore."

It was the same rhythm they'd found somewhere on the third day of that first run downstream, somewhere between the seaplane shadow and the lantern-lit map, and it had held through everything since: the leak, the flood, the ambush, the portage, the long white-knuckle shoot downriver with a prisoner in the hold and Junie gray-faced from the shoulder wound, cracking jokes through her teeth while Hazel flew the boat through water that wanted them dead. Some partnerships are built in boardrooms. Theirs had been built in bilgewater, and it showed, and it held.

They worked the cargo in comfortable silence for a while. Then Junie straightened, wiped her hands on her trousers, and nodded at the compass sitting on the instrument panel where it always sat.

"Still pointing home?" she asked.

It had started as a joke, back in the weeks after the registry. Hazel's father's compass—an old brass thing, dented, its needle given to a lazy drift that had driven him half mad and that Hazel had learned to read like handwriting. During the run she'd hidden Elias's map inside its casing, and after the map went to Mara, the compass had come back to the panel empty, and somehow it had never left. Somewhere in the months since, Junie had started asking, every few flights, whether it was still pointing home—and the answer had gone from *nowhere in particular* to *downriver* to *toward the Fork* to, lately, simply *yes.*

Hazel glanced at it now. The needle swung lazily, settled, held.

"Yeah," she said. "Still pointing home."

Junie grinned and said nothing else, because she was a woman who knew when a thing had been said enough times, and climbed aboard the Kestrel to put the kettle on.

* * *

They drank tea on the boat's deck in the afternoon sun, and Hazel told her about the flight—the Alder Flats, Marta's beans, the schoolchildren at the mission—and Junie told her about the week's work: a salvage job on a swamped barge upriver, a dispute with the harbormaster over mooring fees that Junie had won through sheer refusal to lose interest, and the news that the ferry company had offered her a contract.

"A real contract," Junie said, turning her cup slowly. "Legitimate work. My name in their book and everything." She snorted. "First time in six years anybody's offered me paper instead of a handshake and a warning."

"What'd you tell them?"

"Told them I'd think about it." Junie looked out at the river, at the light going long and gold across it. "Part of me wants to say yes just to see the look on their faces when Okonkwo walks in with references. Part of me likes being my own boss on a boat held together with spite and caulking." She shrugged. "We'll see."

"We'll see," Hazel agreed, and the *we* sat there between them, unremarked upon, entirely understood.

Because that was the other thing that had happened, the thing Hazel had been circling all day without naming: she wasn't leaving. She'd assumed, for most of her life, that she was the kind of person who was always about to leave—that the next strip, the next territory, the next clean start was always one bad month away, and that staying anywhere was just the interval between departures. She'd planned her whole existence around the assumption of eventual flight, the way you plan around a flood season.

But the flood season had come and gone, and she was still here. Her name was in the registry books—not as a claimant, though Mara had tried, God love her, had tried to sign over a share of the claim until Hazel refused hard enough to end the argument. No: her name was in the books as a licensed carrier, a mail contractor, a pilot in good standing with a route and a strip and a mooring arrangement at Pier Nine that Junie had negotiated without telling her, presenting it as a fait accompli along with a bill for the rice.

Her name was in the books. She'd gone down to the office last month to renew her carrier's license, and the clerk—young fellow, new since the purge—had pulled her file and said, "Ah, Miss Dunmore, yes, we have you down from the spring filings," as if she were a person whose history was a matter of record rather than a matter of rumor, and she had stood there in the government-smelling room and felt something in her chest unlock that she hadn't known was locked.

Being seen, it turned out, wasn't the same as being hunted. She'd spent years confusing the two.

* * *

Toward evening she walked out to the end of the pier alone, the way she did sometimes, and looked upriver.

The Sable Fork junction was visible from here on a clear day, a dark seam in the green where the waters met, and today was clear. Somewhere up that seam, three days' hard travel, Mara Vann was living on her father's claim—had moved out in the spring with two hired hands and her father's unsent letters in a oilcloth packet, and had sent word downriver once a month since. The vein was producing. Not rich, not yet, but honest, and hers, and worked by people she chose. Last month's letter had included a photograph, grainy and sun-bleached: Mara standing at the marker cairn, one hand resting on the stones, squinting into the camera with an expression that was half grief and half defiance and entirely her own. On the back she'd written: *He would have hated how long this took and loved every minute of the fight. Thank you for fighting it.*

Hazel had read that sentence maybe forty times. It still landed the same way each time, like a hand briefly pressed flat against the sternum.

Elias Vann had died in her cabin at dawn with the promise fresh on his lips, and for one black moment after, she'd understood the inheritance as a burden—a map, a deadline, a syndicate, a grave she'd have to dig with her own hands. She hadn't known then that it was also a key. That keeping his promise would mean breaking hers to herself, the old one, the quiet one, the one where she stayed small and unseen and safe. That a dying man's last request would be the thing that finally taught her the difference between hiding and living.

She owed him that. She'd never gotten to tell him so. She'd told Mara instead, the whole truth, out on the porch of Mara's rented room in town—the death, the deal, the fear, the fire, all of it—and Mara had listened without interrupting, and at the end had said only, "He picked the right stranger." Hazel had cried then, finally, months too late and exactly on time, and Mara had sat with her and said nothing, because some debts are paid in tears and don't need witnesses beyond the one.

Out on the water, the light was failing gold. Behind her she heard Junie's footsteps on the planks, unhurried, and then Junie settled onto the pier beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and handed her a mug of tea without being asked.

"Heard from the ferry people again," Junie said.

"Mm."

"They want an answer by Friday." A pause. The river ran. "Also, I've been thinking about the Kestrel."

"She's the finest wreck on this river and you know it."

"She is." Junie's voice went softer, which for Junie meant almost audible. "But she's been through a drowning, a shooting, a flash flood, and carrying a prisoner in her hold, and she's still floating. You know what I keep thinking? Most boats never get tested. They live their whole lives tied up pretty and never find out what they can survive." She took a sip of tea. "Wrecks that keep running. That's the whole trick, isn't it. Not staying pretty. Staying afloat."

Hazel looked at her. "Is that philosophy or engineering?"

"It's both. It's why I'm expensive." Junie bumped her shoulder. "Anyway. Friday. Ferry contract. And there's talk of a mail subcontract up the coast—regular runs, guaranteed pay, your name on the paperwork. Thought you might want in. Partner rates."

Partner rates. Hazel turned the words over and found they fit.

She looked down at the compass in her pocket—she'd taken to carrying it on her walks, an old habit repurposed, the needle drifting and settling, drifting and settling, pointing nowhere and everywhere and, lately, home. Her father had died flying contraband for men in white shirts, and she had spent half her life paying his debt and hiding from his ghost, and none of that was undone, none of it could be. But the debt was voided on the record and the ghost had a granddaughter with a working claim and his compass pointed at a pier where a woman was offering her partnership on a boat named for a bird that falls and flies again.

"Partner rates," Hazel said. "I'll want it in writing."

"Naturally."

"And I'm still not paying freight on my own rice."

"That part's non-negotiable."

They sat together as the sun went down behind the hills and the river turned to hammered copper, and far up the Sable Fork a claim lamp was being lit in a window by a woman named Vann, and here at Pier Nine a kettle was going back on the stove, and tomorrow there would be mail to fly and cargo to haul and a river that rose and fell and rose again, indifferent and generous, wrecking whatever wasn't built to bend.

Hazel Dunmore finished her tea, watched the first star come out over the water, and stayed.
