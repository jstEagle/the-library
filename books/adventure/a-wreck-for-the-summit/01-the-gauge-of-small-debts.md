## 1. The Gauge of Small Debts

The foreman's name was Hobb, and he had the kind of face that had been arguing with weather for forty years and losing on points.

"You've done chain work before," he said. It wasn't a question, which was a problem, because Petra Zhou had prepared three lies about it and now none of them would fit.

"Enough," Petra said, which was true in the way that a man who has once swum the channel has "been in water before." They'd run surveys across two provinces and one that no longer existed on any map worth owning. But the Crown's new summit railway did not hire disgraced engineers. The Crown's new summit railway hired people who could hold a chain taut and keep their mouths shut, and it paid them accordingly — eleven coppers a day and a bunk in a tent that smelled of pitch and other men's boots.

Hobb looked at their hands. Petra had thought about this. They'd rubbed dirt into the calluses that morning, but dirt sits on top of an engineer's hands like a bad wig; the calluses themselves were in the wrong places, earned from instruments instead of shovels.

"Chain work," Hobb said again, slower, filing the word away somewhere. "Fine. You're on the river stretch. Don't drown. Paperwork's worse if you drown."

Petra took the stakes and the chalk and went down to the water, and felt, as they always felt at the start of a job, the brief clean relief of being useful at something small enough not to matter.

* * *

The summit railway was the Crown's great vanity: a line that would climb out of the river valley and over the shoulder of the mountains to reach the mining towns on the far side, cutting four days off the freight route and, not incidentally, driving a spike through the last stretch of country the Crown hadn't gotten around to naming. There were posters in the river towns. A locomotive rendered in heroic profile, smoke curling in a way locomotives do not actually smoke, and beneath it: *THE FUTURE DOES NOT WAIT FOR THE VALLEY.*

Petra had read that line six times and each time heard Ovid's voice behind it, dry as old paper: *The future doesn't wait, it just sends a bill.* Ovid Zhou had taught survey the way some men teach scripture — with marginal notes, with jokes that turned out to be load-bearing. He'd been dead eleven months now, and Petra still caught themselves turning to say something to him whenever a benchmark came up clean on the first try.

Eleven months, and Petra was still carrying his map.

That was the thing they could not explain to anyone, least of all to themselves in the honest hours. Rolled in oilcloth at the bottom of their pack was a survey map of every closed rail line in the valley provinces — the branches abandoned when the Crown consolidated, the spurs starved of funding, the whole rusting skeleton of a network that had once been somebody's future. Ovid had drawn it. Not for any client. For himself, the way a doctor keeps a list of patients he failed to save. And when he died — alone, in a rented room, with his affairs arranged with a tidiness that Petra had found unbearable — the map had come to Petra along with two boxes of instruments and a debt ledger with more red in it than ink.

Petra kept the map the way you keep a letter you're afraid to reread. They did not look at it. They knew where it was the way you know where a bruise is.

* * *

The river stretch ran below the old trestle at Marrow Bend, a timber skeleton standing in the water like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride. The Crown's plans called for the new line to cross here on stone piers, which meant someone had to establish the existing grade, which meant Petra spent their days wading in water cold enough to make philosophy unnecessary, chaining distances between points that had been chained before by men whose names were lost, and thinking, in the wordless way of craft, *this is the only conversation I'm still good at.*

They were good at it. That was the trouble. Invisibility, Petra had learned, was not a matter of doing poorly. It was a matter of doing exactly well enough, in exactly the way expected, and then going home. Do too badly and you get noticed. Do too well and you get noticed. The window was narrow and Petra kept drifting out of it, because their hands would not cooperate with mediocrity. On the fourth day they corrected a stake placement without being asked, and the young rodman, a gap-toothed boy named Willet, said, "How'd you know that was off?"

"The bank slopes," Petra said. "Water cuts the near side. Your datum drifts."

Willet wrote this down as though it were gospel. Petra went back to their tent that night feeling the particular chill of a person who has been interesting in public.

You want to stay invisible, they told themselves over the camp stove, heating beans with the patience of long practice. So stop being the best chainman on the river. Stop knowing things. Be dull. Dullness is a discipline. You have failed at everything else; fail at this too.

But there was a loneliness worse than solitude, and it was the loneliness of pretending to be less than you were, hour after hour, day after day, until the pretense got so heavy you couldn't tell whether you were hiding or disappearing. Petra had been disappearing for eleven months. Some evenings they checked their own hands to be sure they were still solid.

* * *

It was the map that undid them, because of course it was. Maps are honest objects. They betray everyone eventually.

It happened on a rest day, rain hammering the tent canvas, the whole crew confined to cards and complaints. Petra had the pack open at the foot of their bunk — a mistake, a carelessness born of eleven months of no one caring what was in anyone's pack — and they'd taken the oilcloth roll out just to hold it, the way you take out a photograph. Not to read it. Just to confirm it was still there, still real, still the one true thing in a life currently made of wet canvas and eleven coppers a day.

Hobb came in out of the rain looking for a dry pipe.

He stood dripping. Petra sat with the map half-unrolled across their knees, and there was a moment — Petra would replay it for weeks — where the right move was obvious and simple: roll it up, stuff it away, grumble about old paperwork. Anyone could have done it. A man with no past could have done it in his sleep.

Instead Petra froze. And Hobb, being a foreman, and therefore a man professionally trained to notice when something is frozen, came closer.

"That's a grade survey," he said. "Of closed lines."

"It's nothing."

"It's the Marrow branch." Hobb's finger came down on the map, on a line Petra knew by heart, and stopped. "And that's—" He bent. His eyebrows did something complicated. "That's Ovid Zhou's hand."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere outside, a mule expressed an opinion about the weather.

"You knew him?" Petra said, which was the wrong thing to say, because it confirmed everything, and some part of Petra watched itself say it with the detached horror of a passenger watching the driver fall asleep.

"Knew him?" Hobb straightened up. He wiped his nose with the back of his hand, leaving a streak of rain and tobacco. "Everyone on the old lines knew Ovid Zhou. Man surveyed half the valley before your Crown bought it all up and padlocked it. Best instrument hand I ever saw, and I include myself, which costs me something to say." He looked at Petra with new attention, the way a man looks at a lock he suddenly suspects is worth picking. "He had an apprentice. Years back. Clever kid. Quiet. Then the Vellmark business blew up and the kid vanished off the registers same week."

"I don't know anything about that."

"No," Hobb agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

Petra rolled the map. Their hands were steady, which surprised them; inside, something was running downhill fast. "It's an old document. I keep it for sentimental reasons. It's worthless."

"Nothing with Ovid Zhou's name in the margin is worthless," Hobb said. "Man couldn't write a grocery list without putting a benchmark in it." He shook rain off his hat, settled it, and paused at the tent flap. "Crown surveyor comes through next week. Brandt, his name is. Sharp as a chisel and twice as likely to leave marks. If I were a man with a worthless sentimental map, I'd keep it out of sight while he's about."

And he left, whistling, leaving Petra to understand that they had just been threatened, warned, and possibly befriended, in an order they couldn't determine.

* * *

That night Petra did the thing they never did. They unrolled the map all the way, weighted the corners with stones, and read it by lamplight like a letter from the dead.

Ovid's hand marched along every margin: elevations, dates, small dry jokes. *Grade holds. Ballast stolen by farmers, can't blame them.* Along the upper river, above Marrow Bend, the lines converged toward a blank space where the paper showed no contours at all — the forbidden source, where the river came out of the mountain, land closed by Crown order for longer than Petra had been alive. Ovid had drawn the closed lines right up to the edge of it and stopped, and in the margin beside the blank space, in letters smaller than anywhere else on the sheet, he had written:

*Paid in full. Ask me someday.*

Petra stared at that for a long time. Ask me someday. Ovid had been dead eleven months, and the someday had expired quietly, unpaid, like everything else he'd left behind.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the river talked to itself in the dark, low and constant, the way it had talked for a hundred years under the trestle, keeping whatever counsel rivers keep.

Petra thought about Brandt coming next week. About the map, which could not be burned — they'd tried, twice, in weaker moments, and both times their hands had refused at the match — and could not be shown, and could not be carried, and could not be put down. About eleven coppers a day and a life measured in wet canvas. About how invisibility had seemed like safety once, and now felt like drowning in slow motion, in water shallow enough to stand up in, if only you were willing to stand.

The river dropped a little more every day. Everyone said so. Dry season coming early this year, the ferryman said; lowest water in a decade, he said, things showing up in the riverbed that hadn't seen sun since the old days.

Petra rolled up the map, and lay down, and did not sleep, and listened to the water getting lower.
