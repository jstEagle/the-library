## 2. What the River Keeps

The river had been lying about its depth all summer.

Petra Zhou knew this the way they knew most things—by measuring it when nobody asked them to. The Crown's survey contract said to take soundings at the old trestle crossing and record them in triplicate, and Petra took the soundings and recorded two copies and kept the third, because a person who kept no private records was a person who could be erased by anyone with a stamp. The river at the trestle was supposed to run eleven feet at its throat. It ran four and a half, and dropping, and the Crown's engineers upstream were already arguing in their tents about whether the summit railway's ballast barges could make the season or whether they'd all be writing apologetic letters in October.

Petra did not care about ballast barges. Petra cared about what four and a half feet of water failed to cover.

They'd noticed it on the second sounding—a dark shape below the abandoned trestle, wedged where the current curled around a shelf of limestone. Most people would have called it a rock. Petra had spent eleven years reading rivers for railroads, and rocks did not sit at angles that defied hydraulics. Rocks did not have corners.

So on the third morning, while the Crown's crew was still frying its breakfast and complaining about it, Petra waded out under the trestle with a pry bar and a rope tied around their waist, and told themselves—firmly, the way one tells a dog not to chase carts—that they were only going to look.

* * *

The water came up to their sternum and pushed like a drunk uncle who wanted to be friends. The bottom of the riverbed was smooth stone worn glassy by forty years of current, and Petra's boots skated on it until they found the seam of gravel below the shelf and dug in. The shape resolved as they got closer: iron-banded oak, the size of a sea chest, furred with a grey beard of algae. One band had rusted through. The lid sat proud of the body by the width of a knife blade, propped open by a stone that had worked itself in sometime in the last few decades, which meant the river had been quietly emptying it for years, and whatever remained inside was whatever the river hadn't wanted.

"Right," Petra said to the water, which gurgled back without sympathy. "Just looking."

The chest weighed as much as a dead horse. It took twenty minutes, three rest breaks, and one moment of genuine humiliation in which Petra lost their footing entirely and went under, coming up blowing like a kettle, before they wrestled it onto the gravel bar downstream of the trestle where the sun could get at it. They sat there dripping, heart slamming, and looked at it.

It was a good chest. That was the first thought, and it was an engineer's thought: dovetailed corners, bands riveted not nailed, the kind of work you only saw from shops that expected their product to outlive the buyer. There was no lock left—the hasp had been pried long ago, probably by the same flood that wedged it here—but across the lid, where a less careful maker would have put a nameplate, someone had stamped a crest into the oak itself, deep enough to survive everything the river had done.

A heron. Two herons, actually, joined at the neck so their heads faced away from each other, wings spread into a single fan.

Petra knew the mark. Everyone past forty knew the mark; everyone under thirty knew it only as a shape in history primers, next to a paragraph beginning *Vellmark, formerly a principality of the upper valley, dissolved in the Reconciliation of—* and then a date nobody remembered because nothing on the page after it mattered. Forty years ago Vellmark had been a country. Then it wasn't, and its railways—which had been, if you believed the old men in the taverns, better than anything the Crown ever laid—were pulled up or sealed, its maps redrawn, its name sanded off signposts so thoroughly that some towns changed their names twice just to be safe.

And now here was its bird, sitting on a gravel bar, wearing forty years of algae.

Petra should have put it back. They knew this with the flat certainty of a man who has touched a stove before. Unclaimed property in a Crown survey corridor belonged to the Crown; found property bearing the arms of a dissolved state belonged to the Crown *and* to whoever reported it, which meant clerks, which meant questions, which meant a paper trail leading directly to a surveyor named Zhou whose references were thin and whose real name appeared in exactly one place Petra very much wished it didn't.

They opened it anyway. Of course they opened it. Telling Petra Zhou not to open a thing was like telling the river not to run downhill.

Inside: water. A finger's depth of it, cold and clear, sloshing in the bottom like the chest had been keeping a small aquarium. Silt. A coil of something that had once been leather. And, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically and paid for it, a book.

Not a book. Petra got the oilcloth off with fingers gone clumsy from cold and found themselves holding a brass-bound ledger, the metal green with age but sound, the clasp still working on the second try. Stamped into the brass cover: the two-headed heron again, smaller, finer, with a border of chevrons around it like railway track seen from above.

"Well," Petra said. "You're worth something."

That was true, and it settled the matter, and Petra closed the ledger and stowed it in their pack and rowed the chest's remaining contents back into the river with a clear conscience, and that evening they sold it to a dealer in Marrow Bend for enough to eat through winter.

None of that happened. But it was the story Petra told themselves while they carried the pack back to camp, and they believed it right up until sundown, when they sat on their bunk with the lamp turned low and told themselves they were only going to check the condition of the paper. For valuation purposes.

* * *

The ledger opened to a title page in a hand so precise it looked printed:

*Survey Ledger of the Upper Line, Vellmark Principality Rail. Source Extension, Mile 0 to Mile 41. Kept by order of the Office of Ways.*

Below it, columns. Elevations. Bearings. Cut depths. Fill volumes. Water tables noted in a shorthand Petra recognized instantly because it was the shorthand every surveyor learned and no two used quite the same way—and this one used it the way Petra's own mentor had.

Petra sat up straighter.

They turned pages. The hand stayed perfect for miles of entries—readings taken in weather, in seasons, in the kind of terrain that made the Crown's current crew weep—and then, somewhere around Mile 12, another hand joined it. Rougher. Faster. Margin notes crowding the formal columns the way weeds crowd a fence line.

*Spring seep at cut 14 — drain before winter or she'll heave.*

*Foreman says the heron gate is bad luck. Bad luck is a foreman who won't shore his own cuts.*

*Paid the ferryman in nails. He preferred it.*

Petra read that last one three times. Not because it was remarkable—it was the sort of joke every survey crew in the world had made—but because of the letterforms. The way the *f* crossed low. The way numbers leaned slightly left, like tired men against a wall.

Ovid Zhou had taught Petra drafting. Had taught them leveling, chaining, how to read water, how to lie to a landowner with a straight face and a transit. Ovid had died two winters ago owing money to everyone in three counties, and Petra had spent those two winters paying down what debts they could and hiding from the rest, and in all that time Ovid had never—not once—mentioned Vellmark. Never mentioned working it. Never mentioned that his surveyor's shorthand, the little private hieroglyphs he'd drilled into Petra over six years of apprenticeship, matched the house style of a drowned kingdom's Office of Ways.

*He learned it somewhere,* Petra thought. *Shorthand doesn't come from nowhere.*

The lamp burned lower. Outside, the Crown camp snored and a mule complained about philosophy. Petra turned another page and stopped breathing for a second, because there, in the margin beside Mile 19, in Ovid's left-leaning hand:

*Source line sound. Better than the summit route. They'll seal this anyway. Maps are politics, P. Remember that when they tell you a line is closed. Ask who closed it, and what it cost them to say so.*

*P.*

Petra set the ledger down on their knees and looked at the tent wall for a long time.

They had been nine years old when Vellmark dissolved. They had no memory of it at all—no flags, no soldiers, nothing but the word itself, which adults said the way you say the name of a dead relative at dinner: carefully, and then changing the subject. And now here was Ovid, forty years young in this margin, joking with a ferryman about nails, calling the source line *sound*, predicting its sealing with the weary accuracy of a man who had watched it happen before.

The map of closed lines lived in the bottom of Petra's pack, rolled in oilcloth, and had lived in various packs for eleven years. It was the one inheritance Ovid had left that couldn't be seized for debt: a survey map of every line shut down, abandoned, or "rerouted" in the upper valleys over sixty years, annotated in that same left-leaning hand. Petra had never known why Ovid kept it. They'd assumed sentiment. Ovid had seemed like a sentimental man the way a river seems calm—you only find out otherwise by falling in.

Petra unrolled it now, weighting the corners with boots and the brass ledger itself, and held the lamp close.

Mile 0 to Mile 41. The source extension. On Ovid's old map it was drawn as a solid line struck through with three careful slashes—the surveyor's mark for *closed, deliberately*—and dated the year of the Reconciliation. On the ledger's route sheets, the same line ran mile by mile, station by station, with elevations that matched Ovid's annotations to the foot. Not similar. Matching. The margin notes weren't commentary on the ledger.

They were part of it. Ovid Zhou had surveyed the source line *for Vellmark*, and the ledger in Petra's hands was the master record, and somehow—through whatever tangled arrangement of debts and favors and silence a man like Ovid collected over a lifetime—it had ended up in a chest under a trestle, and Petra had pulled it out because they couldn't leave a corner unexplained.

"Ask who closed it," Petra murmured, "and what it cost them to say so."

The lamp guttered. Petra trimmed the wick and kept reading, telling themselves the whole time that this was due diligence, that a person needed to know what they were selling, that the dealer in Marrow Bend would ask provenance questions and it paid to have answers.

It was a good story. Petra was almost able to believe it by the time they reached the last written page, near Mile 41, where the formal columns stopped mid-entry—the survey simply ending, unfinished, as though the pen had been set down between readings and never picked up. In the margin, alone, one more note in Ovid's hand, older-looking, the ink browned:

*If this finds anyone: the debt stands. Tell them the engineer's heir pays it.*

Petra read it four times. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, and the river outside the tent went on saying the only thing it ever said, and somewhere in the dark below the trestle the chest sat empty on its gravel bar, holding nothing but river water and the shape of everything it used to contain.

"The engineer's heir," Petra repeated softly. "Well. That's flattering. I didn't agree to inherit anything."

But even as they said it, they were doing the arithmetic they could never stop doing—forty-one miles of sealed line, one unfinished survey, a deadline called the dry season dropping the river a half-foot a week, and a nation's whole surviving claim to anything, possibly, riding in a brass-bound book currently being warmed by a disgraced engineer's knees.

Petra closed the ledger. Opened it again. Read the last margin note a fifth time, as if a sixth reading might produce a different sentence.

It didn't. Somewhere out in the dark, a boat horn sounded on the river—one long note, upstream-bound—and Petra found themselves listening to it far longer than a person with any sense would, wondering who moved on this water at night, and what else the low season was going to uncover.

In the morning, they told themselves, they'd ride to Marrow Bend and sell it.

In the morning, a Crown surveyor named Brandt would be waiting at the trestle with a notebook of his own, asking polite questions in a voice like a level bubble finding center. But that was tomorrow's problem, and tonight Petra Zhou sat up late with a dead kingdom in their lap, learning its handwriting.
