## 3. A Man Who Counts Rivets

The Crown surveyor arrived on a Tuesday, which Petra would later decide was the most insulting part of the whole affair. Tuesdays were nothing days. Nobody important traveled on a Tuesday.

Petra was elbow-deep in the river shallows at the time, re-sighting a benchmark that had shifted — or rather, that Petra had nudged back into agreement with the ledger's figures the night before, in a fit of conscience they were still arguing with themselves about. The morning was doing its best impression of warmth: thin sun, mist burning off the water, a heron standing downstream with the patient insolence of a creature that owned the river and merely tolerated everyone else on it.

Boots crunched on the shingle behind them.

Petra did not turn around immediately. This was a policy. People who turned around too fast looked guilty, and people who looked guilty got questions, and questions were the one commodity Petra Zhou could not afford to buy. They finished their note, capped the pencil, and stood up slowly, like a person with nothing in the world to hide except, perhaps, a mild disappointment in the weather.

The man on the bank wore a Crown surveyor's grey coat, brushed clean, and boots that had never once been sorry. He was perhaps fifty, built like something that had been assembled rather than born, and he held his hat in one hand as though he'd read somewhere that rivers respected manners.

"Morning," he said. "Zhou, is it? The contract surveyor?"

"That's me." Petra kept their voice at the pitch of a man mildly bored by his own existence. "You'll be wanting the foreman. He's up at the cut."

"I'll want him eventually." The man smiled. It was a good smile, well-maintained, like everything else about him. "Elias Brandt. Crown Office of Works. I'm told you found something interesting under the old trestle."

*There it is.* Petra felt the words go through them like a cold current finding a boot seam.

"Found?" They climbed the bank, unhurried, brushing gravel off their knees. "I've found mud, mostly. Mud and a dead eel. If the eel's interesting to the Crown, you're welcome to it."

Brandt laughed — a short, genuine-sounding bark, and Petra noted with professional alarm that it *did* sound genuine, which meant either that it was, or that this was a man who practiced. "The eel is safe from me. But someone moved stone under the third trestle pier. A fair amount of it. And someone left drag marks going down to the waterline that stop at a hole in the silt roughly the size of a strongbox."

"You've been busy," Petra said.

"It's my job to be busy. Yours, according to your contract, is benchmarks and gradients." Brandt said it pleasantly, the way a man says *your station in life*, and Petra felt the old itch between the shoulder blades, the one that said *run*. They ignored it. Running confirmed things.

"The trestle's been a fishing spot for forty years," Petra said. "Half the valley's dragged hooks under it. Kids dive there in summer. If the riverbed looks rearranged, blame the youth of the district."

"Mm." Brandt set his hat on his head now, which Petra understood as a signal: the pleasant part was over; the precise part was beginning. "Except the disturbance is fresh. Silt hasn't resettled in the scour pocket. And whoever dug left tool marks consistent with a crowbar and a winch strap, not a fishhook." He paused, letting the silence do some of his work for him. It was very effective silence. "Also, the water dropped two feet last week. Which means anything wedged out there has spent the last decade invisible and is suddenly not."

Petra looked at him. He looked back with the mild, bottomless patience of a man counting rivets in a bridge while pretending to admire the view.

"You've given this a lot of thought for an office man," Petra said.

"I used to be a field man." Something crossed his face then — quickly, gone before it could be named. Regret, maybe. Or memory. "The office is where they send you when you're too accurate for somebody's comfort."

That, at least, Petra understood completely.

* * *

They ended up at the work camp's cook shack because it was the only place with coffee, and the coffee was bad enough that both of them could use it as an excuse to talk slowly. Petra watched Brandt take his first sip without flinching and revised their estimate of him upward. A man who could drink this coffee without complaint had survived worse than bad interviews.

"So," Brandt said, setting the tin cup down. "Tell me about yourself, Zhou. Where'd you train?"

"Here and there." Petra wrapped both hands around their own cup, mostly so they'd have something to do that wasn't reaching for a weapon. "Apprenticed on the river works. Gradients, drainage, trestle repair. The unglamorous trades."

"No formal schooling?"

"Some." *Careful.* "A tutor, when my father could pay him. He couldn't often."

It was a good lie. It had texture, a little shame folded in, just enough detail to be checked against nothing. Petra had learned lying the way other people learned music — by ear, by practice, by performing for audiences who wanted to believe.

Brandt nodded along, and then, gently, like a man laying a card face-up on the table: "Your hand is very fine, though. For a self-taught man. I saw your field notes on the foreman's desk. That's a draftsman's hand. Trained. Probably guild-trained." He took another sip. "And your benchmark sightings are done in a notation I haven't seen outside the old Vellmarki corridor surveys. Crossed sevens. Fraction bars instead of decimals."

The coffee turned to lead in Petra's mouth.

"Regional habit," they said. "River towns trade methods. My teacher picked things up wherever—"

"Ovid Zhou," Brandt said.

Petra went very still, and hated themselves for it, because stillness was itself an answer.

"He trained half the engineers on this stretch of river, before his license was revoked," Brandt continued, conversational, terrible. "Brilliant man, by all accounts. Sloped his culverts wrong exactly once, they say, and after that he sloped everything twice. You write like him. You sight like him. You even stand like him — I've seen the photographs in the Office archive, the ones from the Vellmark line, before the dissolution." He set the cup down with a small click. "So here's my difficulty, Zhou. Either you're his nephew, his student, or his ghost, and two of those three interest me very much."

"And the third?"

"Ghosts don't drink coffee this bad voluntarily."

Despite everything — despite the cold walking up their spine — Petra almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was a shrug they'd been rehearsing since the chest came out of the river. "He taught me. Years ago. Before the revocation. I inherited his notebooks, his habits, and his handwriting, apparently. None of it's a crime."

"No," Brandt agreed. "None of it's a crime." He turned the cup a slow quarter-turn on the table, aligning its handle with the table edge, and Petra thought: *this is a man who cannot leave a crooked thing crooked, and I am currently the crookedest thing in this valley.* "What *would* be a crime is removing Crown property from a Crown-adjacent site without reporting it. Salvage law's dull reading, but it's clear. Anything of historical value recovered from navigable waters belongs to the Office of Works. There's a form. It's a short form. I keep copies in my coat."

"Do you often carry forms to rivers?"

"I carry forms everywhere. Forms are how civilization apologizes for itself." Brandt smiled again, and this time Petra caught the machinery of it, the gears behind the teeth. "So. Did you find anything under the trestle, Zhou?"

Here was the moment. Petra had run it a hundred times in the night, lying awake under the lean-to with the ledger's brass corners pressing through their pack into their spine like a question. Lie flat: *no, found nothing.* Lie big: *found an old crate of rotten nails, burned it.* Lie sideways: *found something worthless.*

They chose sideways, because sideways was the only direction Brandt hadn't already mapped.

"A box," Petra said. "Rotted to lace. Inside was sludge and a book that fell apart when I touched it. Paper pulp. Ink gone to soup. I threw what was left back in the hole." They met his eyes and held them, which was the hard part, because holding a liar's gaze steady requires believing your own lie for exactly as long as the look lasts, and Petra's belief had a leak in it. "Worthless. I didn't even report it. Should I fill out your form retroactively?"

Something flickered across Brandt's face. Not triumph. Worse — *disappointment*, faint and courteous, like a host noticing a guest has declined dessert.

"Pulp," he repeated.

"Wet paper does that. Forty years underwater."

"Forty years," Brandt said softly. "Interesting number. The trestle's older than that. The dissolution was forty-one years ago this autumn." He let that sit. Then he stood, unhurried, and put out his hand. "Well. Nothing worth a form, then. I'm glad we've cleared it up."

Petra shook the hand. His grip was dry, exact, calibrated — a handshake conducted to three decimal places.

"I'll be in the valley another fortnight," Brandt said, settling his hat. "Checking the summit line's foundations. The Crown's very concerned about foundations lately." He paused at the door of the cook shack and turned back, and for one unguarded second the courtesy dropped away and Petra saw the actual man underneath: tired, sharp, and watching. "For what it's worth, Zhou — if you *had* found something, and it *were* pulp, I'd advise you to sleep well. Men who find pulp always do. It's the men who find ledgers who lie awake."

He touched his hat brim and walked out into the sun, boots crunching evenly on the shingle, pausing once — only once — to look down at the third trestle pier, the way a man looks at a page he intends to memorize.

* * *

Petra sat with the dregs of the bad coffee for a long time after he was gone.

The mistake, they decided, was the pulp. Pulp was too neat. A man like Brandt didn't catch lies by catching details; he caught them by catching *convenience*, and nothing was more convenient than evidence that destroyed itself. Petra had handed him a story with no loose ends, and honest stories were full of loose ends — the dead eel, the kids' fishhooks, the rotted strap that wouldn't quite burn. Loose ends were what truth smelled like. Petra's story smelled like varnish.

They packed up the theodolite with hands that wanted to shake and didn't permit it, and thought about the ledger rolled in oilcloth inside their bedroll, thirty feet away in the lean-to, radiating its quiet impossible weight. Two-headed heron. Ovid's margin notes in the corners, cramped and urgent, written by a man paying off a debt in the only currency he had left: *measurements.*

*Men who find ledgers lie awake,* Brandt had said, and Petra thought: *he knows. He doesn't know what, but he knows, and worse — he wants to be wrong.* That was the thing Petra kept circling back to, the detail that wouldn't file itself away. A stupid hunter would have arrested them on the spot, or blustered, or left. Brandt had done none of those. He'd given Petra a shape to escape through — *pulp, worthless, no form needed* — and then told them, plainly, that he knew the shape was empty. It wasn't a trap. It was an invitation to be honest, extended by a man who clearly hadn't been offered honesty himself in a very long time.

Which made him more dangerous, not less. Stupid men could be outrun. Patient, lonely, accurate men had to be *outlasted*, and the dry season was coming, and the river was dropping, and somewhere upstream was a source the maps weren't allowed to name.

Petra shouldered the pack — ledger and all, because leaving it hidden now would mean explaining later why they'd hidden it — and started up the bank toward the next benchmark.

Behind them, downstream, the heron finally moved, lifting off the water with a sound like a sail filling, and flew — Petra noticed — not south, but upstream. Toward the closed lines. Toward the source.

Even the birds, apparently, were keeping secrets this year.
