## 4. The Heron Recognizes the Heron

The market at Lowbridge smelled of wet wool, frying onions, and the particular despair of a town that had once expected to be important and was still waiting for the news to arrive otherwise.

Petra moved through it the way they moved through everything these days: head down, hands in pockets, a person shaped exactly like nobody worth noticing. It was a skill. They'd spent three years perfecting it—shoulders slightly rounded, stride just short of apologetic, eyes fixed on the middle distance where nothing interesting ever lived. Invisibility, Petra had learned, was not the absence of being seen. It was the careful management of what people saw and immediately forgot.

They were there to buy rope. That was the whole errand. Rope, and if the price was right, a tin of that bitter tea the river towns drank because proper coffee cost more than dignity allowed. Simple. Boring. The kind of morning that left no marks.

The satchel rode against their hip as always, the ledger inside wrapped in oilcloth, the brass corners of the thing pressing through the canvas like knuckles through a glove. Petra had considered leaving it behind at the lodging house. Had actually gotten as far as the door with it sitting on the table, looking innocent, which brass survey ledgers stamped with the crests of dead nations should never be permitted to do.

But leaving it felt like leaving a wound unbandaged in a room full of dogs. So it came along, pressed to their side, warm from their body heat, heavier than its four pounds by approximately the weight of everything it meant.

The rope seller wanted too much. The tea seller wanted even more. A woman selling eels out of a barrel told Petra, unprompted, that her husband had died in March and she'd never liked him, and did Petra want eels. Petra bought no eels but heard about the husband anyway, which seemed to be how markets worked here. Everyone in Lowbridge had a story and no one had anyone to tell it to.

It was almost pleasant, in a grim way. Petra was beginning to relax—which was, of course, the exact moment the universe chose to make its move. The universe, in Petra's experience, had the timing of a bad practical joker and none of the charm.

* * *

It happened at the ferry landing, where the market spilled down toward the water in a crooked staircase of stalls. Petra had gone there for no good reason except that the rope seller's cousin kept a stall near the landing and supposedly had better line at better prices, and Petra had learned long ago that in river towns all commerce eventually flowed downhill to the boats.

The ferry was a flat-bottomed thing called the *Patient Wife*, painted green once and now painted mostly by weather. Its ferrywoman stood on the deck coiling line with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand coils and intended to do ten thousand more without wasting a single motion.

She was tall, broad-shouldered, with dark hair tied back and a sunburn that suggested she'd stopped negotiating with the sun years ago and simply accepted the terms. She looked, Petra thought, like a person who knew exactly where every object on her boat was at all times, including objects that had been thrown overboard.

Petra wasn't looking at her particularly. They were haggling with the cousin over rope, using the local technique of looking pained and walking away slowly until the price followed them.

Then the wind shifted.

It was a small wind, barely a breath, the kind that comes off the water in the late morning and lifts loose things. It caught the flap of Petra's satchel—the buckle strap had worked loose somewhere between the eel barrel and the stairs—and lifted the folded cloth Petra used to pad the ledger's brass corners.

Just for a second. Just enough.

The stamp showed: two-headed heron, wings spread, facing both directions at once, the way herons in old Vellmarki carvings always faced, as if refusing to choose a future.

The coil of line dropped from the ferrywoman's hands.

Not slipped—dropped, deliberately, the way a person sets something down when their hands are suddenly needed for other work, like keeping their knees locked. She went white. Not pale. White, the way paper is white, the way a bedsheet is white, the specific bloodless white of a person who has just seen a ghost wearing a very familiar face.

Petra saw it happen. Saw the color leave her face in real time, saw her hand come up and grip the rail, saw her mouth form a word that had no business existing in a Crown river town—a word in a language Petra didn't speak but recognized anyway, from forty-year-old ballads and the margins of banned books, a word that meant roughly *mercy* or possibly *ancestors* depending on who was doing the translating.

Then she looked up, straight at Petra, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once, and she said, in accented but perfectly functional Common Tongue:

"You. Ferryman. Come here."

"I'm not a ferryman," Petra said, because apparently when confronted with the impossible, their instinct was pedantry.

"Come *here*."

There was something in her voice that made argument feel like a luxury. Petra paid for the rope—it was good rope, the cousin had been right, and the price had followed them downhill exactly as predicted—and crossed the planks to the *Patient Wife* with the gait of a person approaching a dog of unknown temperament.

Up close she was younger than she'd looked from the stairs. Thirty, maybe thirty-two. There was a scar through one eyebrow, old and clean, and her hands were rope-hard and river-soft at the same time, which Petra had only ever seen on people who'd grown up on the water and never fully left it.

"Your bag," she said. "Open it."

"No," said Petra.

Her jaw tightened. "You carry the heron into a Crown market with the wind blowing and you say *no*?"

"I carry a lot of things," Petra said. "That's a padding cloth. For brass instruments. Survey instruments." Which was true, technically, in the way that a coffin is a box for sleeping.

"Survey instruments," she repeated. Her eyes did not blink. Petra had met people who could lie with their whole bodies; this woman could apparently see through lies with hers. "Stamped with the two-headed heron of Vellmark. On a padding cloth. For your instruments."

"The stamp is decorative."

"Decorative."

"It's an old design. Very popular. Forty years ago."

Something flickered across her face at *forty years ago*—something that might have been grief or might have been rage, and Petra suspected it was both sharing a room and splitting the rent. She stepped closer. She smelled of engine oil and river water and, faintly, of some spice Petra couldn't name and would spend the rest of the day failing not to think about.

"Forty years ago," she said quietly, "they took my grandmother's house and gave it to a man who couldn't read. Forty years ago they put my father on a barge with two hundred others and told them it was relocation. He waved at us from the rail. He was smiling. He thought we'd follow within the month." She looked down at the deck. "We did not follow within the month."

Petra said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't either be a lie or open a door Petra had spent three years nailing shut.

"So you will understand," she went on, lifting her chin, "when I ask you—one time, politely—to show me what is in that bag."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I will assume you stole it," she said simply. "Because whoever carries the heron either stole it or inherited it, and you have the look of a man who inherits debts, not heirlooms."

*Man*, Petra noted. Everyone assumed. It was convenient, usually. Today it stung in a way Petra filed away to examine never.

"I'm sorry about your father," Petra said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded.

"I'm sorry about nothing," she said. "I'm not asking for sympathy. I'm asking for the truth, which is rarer and harder to carry." She held out her hand, palm up. Rope-callused. Steady. Waiting.

And here was the thing—the thing Petra would turn over later, at night, in the dark, like a stone with something alive under it. Petra had planned for Brandt. Had planned for thieves, for customs men, for the Crown's clerks and the Crown's questions. Every plan ended the same way: deny, deflect, disappear. The ledger was merchandise. Four pounds of brass and paper with a street value that would buy a boat, or a new name, or a year of being no one in a town where no one asked questions. Petra had carried it for eleven days telling themselves exactly that story, and the story had held, mostly, the way a tarp holds in weather that hasn't tested it yet.

This woman wasn't offering money. She wasn't threatening arrest. She was standing on her own boat with her hand out and her eyes shining, asking to see a stamp the way other people asked to see the faces of children they'd given up.

Nobody had ever wanted anything Petra carried for reasons that weren't money. It knocked something loose. Something structural.

"No," Petra said again, and heard their own voice come out wrong—too fast, too thin, the voice of a person closing a door on their own fingers.

The woman lowered her hand slowly. She nodded, once, as if confirming a diagnosis.

"All right," she said. "You keep it. But hear me, stranger, because I will only say this once." She leaned in, and her voice dropped to something low and level and absolutely certain. "That seal belongs to a country that is not dead. It is only sleeping, and it sleeps scattered—in Kell's Ferry, in Marrow Bend, in the high valley where the river starts, in cellars and back rooms and the memories of grandmothers who refused to forget the anthem. And every one of us, every single one, has dreamed of seeing that stamp again." She straightened. "So whatever you think you're carrying, you're not carrying it alone anymore. The moment the wind lifted that cloth, you stopped being invisible. I hope you understand that. I hope you understood it before you walked into this market."

Petra's mouth was dry. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a person filing a document. Then, abruptly, the fury drained out of her posture and something almost gentle replaced it, which was somehow worse. "You look tired. You look like a man who hasn't eaten a hot meal since the last town fined him for something. There's a cookshop behind the chandlery. Tell them Sefa sent you. The stew is terrible but there's a lot of it."

"How do you know I haven't eaten?"

"Because you bargained for rope for six minutes and bought eels for no one." She turned back to her coiling, dismissing him with the fluid cruelty of the truly busy. "Go on. The wind will shift again by afternoon. Things get seen."

Petra walked away. Made it four steps. Five.

Stopped.

* * *

Here is what happened in those five steps, at a speed Petra's rational mind could not match:

Eleven days ago, a chest had come up out of the riverbed with a dead nation's crest on it, and Petra had thought: *money*. Six nights ago, Petra had read the ledger cover to cover by candlelight and thought: *Ovid, what did you do?* Three days ago, a Crown surveyor with polite eyes had asked questions over bad coffee, and Petra had thought: *run*. And now a stranger with her grandmother's stolen house in her voice had looked at four pounds of brass like it was a resurrection, and Petra's entire architecture of self-preservation—

—felt like a sealed tunnel. Sound. Watertight. Built by someone competent.

Built by someone who had sealed it from the inside.

Petra turned around.

Sefa Idrizi—for that was the name she'd given, and names were currency Petra had learned to count—was watching them over her shoulder mid-coil, and she did not look surprised. That was the humiliating part. She looked like a woman who had bet on a horse and was mildly pleased to see it running.

"One look," Petra said. "Not here. Not with the wind doing whatever it wants. Somewhere with a door."

The coil stopped. "A door," she repeated.

"A door and your discretion. You see it, you tell me why it matters to you, and then we both go on with our lives and pretend this market never happened."

"That's a terrible bargain," she said. "You're showing me the most dangerous object on this river and getting nothing."

"I'm getting to stop feeling like this," Petra said, before their brain could intervene, and then wished very much for the eel barrel to swallow them.

Sefa studied them for a long moment. Whatever she saw seemed to settle something.

"The boathouse," she said. "End of the landing, past the net racks. When the market thins. An hour." She hesitated, and for the first time her composure cracked—not into anger but into something rawer, younger. "You'll really show me? You swear it? Because I have waited—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "My grandmother died in February. She was ninety-one. She sang the anthem every night into the end, off-key, in a language the Crown fined people for speaking when I was a girl. If that stamp is real, and you're lying to me, I want you to know that I will find you. I'm very good at finding people on rivers. It's most of my job."

"It's real," Petra said. "I'm not lying about that part."

"*That* part," Sefa repeated. But she almost smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and Petra looked away first, which was becoming a pattern they resented.

An hour later, in the dim of the boathouse, among nets and tarps and the green underwater light that comes through plank gaps, Petra unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be.

Sefa Idrizi looked at the two-headed heron for a long time. She didn't touch it. She pressed her knuckles to her mouth instead, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and when she finally spoke her voice was wrecked and fierce and quiet all at once:

"Do you have any idea," she said, "what you're holding?"

"Yes," Petra said. And for the first time in eleven days, it wasn't a lie.

Which was when they both heard boots on the landing outside—heavy ones, official ones, the kind that belonged to a man who counted rivets—and Sefa blew out the lamp without either of them agreeing to it, and the dark closed over the heron like water.
