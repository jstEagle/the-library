## 5. Debt, Inherited

The rain came in off the river that evening the way it always did in Harrow Bend—sideways, with an opinion. Petra sat in their rented room above the chandler's shop with the ledger open on a crate that served as a desk, and read Ovid Zhou's handwriting for the first time in three years.

It stopped them like a hand on the chest.

Everyone else's notes in the ledger were in the tidy Vellmarki survey script, all upright strokes and careful fractions, the handwriting of people who believed the kingdom they measured would go on measuring forever. But in the margins, in pencil gone soft and brown with age, ran another hand entirely. A slanted, impatient, brilliant hand. A hand that had once written *Petra — check your own arithmetic before you check mine* across a hundred returned problem sets.

*He surveyed this,* Petra thought, and then, more precisely: *He surveyed this for them.*

The margin notes began eleven years back, by the dates. Ovid had been younger then—or at least his pencil had been. He'd walked the source line from the mouth of the gorge to the spring itself, and he'd annotated everything: rock strata, water tables, load-bearing estimates for tunnel works that had never been built. And beside each entry, small calculations in a shorthand Petra knew intimately, because they'd stolen it shamelessly as an apprentice.

But it was the note on page forty-one that made Petra set down the lamp.

*Paid in full,* Ovid had written, underlined twice. *Not in coin. Never ask me what I owe or to whom; you will not like the answer.*

Petra stared at it until the letters swam.

They remembered that year. The year Ovid had come back from a contract job upriver with new boots, a new coat, and a look Petra had never been able to name—a look like a man carrying something heavy in a place no one could see. He'd refused the next three Crown contracts, which had been financial suicide, and taken instead a string of miserable drainage jobs nobody wanted. When Petra asked why, Ovid had said only: "Because some lines you draw for money, and some lines you draw because you said you would."

Petra had thought it was just Ovid being Ovid, cryptic as a closed switch.

Now the cryptic old man was dead, and his debts had apparently been left to whoever inherited his map of closed lines. Which was Petra. Who had never agreed to inherit anything except a toolbox and a bad habit of checking other people's arithmetic.

"Paid in full," Petra muttered. "In what?"

The ledger didn't say. Ledgers never do; they only record that a debt exists, not its weight. But the margin notes kept going, page after page, Ovid walking the source line again and again over six years, each survey more detailed than the last, as though he were paying something down installment by installment. And on the final page, dated four months before his death, one last note:

*If anyone reads this, tell them the valley is not empty. It is holding its breath.*

---

Petra slept badly and woke to hammering on the street door at an hour when honest people were still pretending to sleep.

They knew before they opened their eyes what it was. You learn the sound of official knocking the way you learn the sound of your own name. Three raps, pause, three raps—the knock of men who have a warrant and want you to know they have time.

Petra moved fast and quiet, years of habit taking over where panic would have slowed them. Ledger into the oilskin wrap. Oilskin into the false bottom of the survey case—the one compartment every engineer builds and every searcher knows about, which is exactly why Petra kept the real hiding place elsewhere: wrapped in the spare fuse cord, coiled in the bottom of the tool roll, because nobody in the history of searching has ever wanted to uncoil a stranger's fuse cord.

Then they opened the door downstairs with the expression of a man inconvenienced before coffee.

There were three of them. Two big ones flanking the stairs, and between them a thin clerk with a paper that had Petra's name spelled correctly, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't been.

"Crown survey office," the clerk said. "We're executing a search warrant regarding materials removed from a site of historical interest below the old trestle."

"Materials," Petra repeated. "You mean river rocks. I pulled up some lovely river rocks."

The clerk did not smile. "You'll stand there while we look."

And look they did. They went through Petra's belongings with the thoroughness of men paid by the hour and the delicacy of men who had never owned anything themselves. They shook out the bedroll. They emptied the pack. One of them found the map of closed lines rolled in the lining and spent a long, frowning minute with it while Petra's heart performed quiet gymnastics.

"That's a training exercise," Petra said. "I copy old alignments to keep my hand in. Closed lines are good practice—they don't argue."

The big one grunted. The clerk wrote something down, which meant nothing good.

They found the false bottom in the survey case inside four minutes. Petra watched them do it with the mild disappointment of a craftsman watching amateurs find the obvious trick. The compartment was empty, of course. The clerk looked at it, looked at Petra, and Petra gave him a shrug that said *every case has one, mate, it's where I keep my sins*, and the clerk wrote that down too, probably.

Forty minutes. That's how long they took. At the end of it the room looked like a laundry explosion, the ledger was still coiled in the fuse cord, and the clerk stood at the door with his paper and delivered the sentence Petra had been waiting for since the trestle:

"You'll remain in Harrow Bend. Surveyor Brandt will wish to speak with you further."

"Wish to," Petra echoed. "How friendly."

The door shut. Petra listened to the boots recede, then to the rain, then to their own pulse, and allowed themselves exactly ten seconds of relief before reaching for the tool roll—

—and finding it light.

The ledger was gone.

For one long moment Petra simply stood in the wreckage of their room, wet wool smell and scattered papers, and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from betrayal arriving ahead of schedule. Because there was only one person who'd known where things were kept. Only one person who'd been in the room. Sefa Idrizi, who'd sat on that very crate two nights ago eating bread and talking about her grandmother's ferry routes, while Petra's back was turned boiling water—and who had, apparently, uncoiled a stranger's fuse cord.

"I'm going to drown her," Petra told the empty room. "I'm going to find her, and I'm going to drown her in the river she loves so much, and it'll be poetic, and everyone will understand."

* * *

Finding Sefa in Harrow Bend took twenty minutes, because Harrow Bend is one street and a dock, and Sefa Idrizi was at the dock, sitting on a bollard like a woman waiting for a ferry that wasn't coming, with a satchel at her feet that Petra recognized down to its buckle.

"You," Petra said.

"Me," Sefa agreed. She didn't get up. She had the composure of someone who had already rehearsed this argument in three languages.

"You went through my tools."

"I went through your tools."

"You stole from me."

"I protected you." She said it without heat, which was infuriating. "They came with a warrant, Petra. Warrants end in cells, and cells end in questions, and questions end in answers you can't take back. You were going to hide it in the room and hope. Hoping is not a plan. Hoping is what people do instead of plans."

"It was MY—" Petra caught themselves, lowered their voice as a fisherman passed with his net. "It was mine. I found it. I carried it. You had no right."

"No right." Sefa finally stood, and up close her eyes were furious in a way her voice wasn't, which was somehow worse. "You want to talk about rights? Forty years ago my grandmother's nation was erased from the maps by the same government whose clerks just turned your socks onto the floor. My family has been paying rent on our own language ever since. And you found the one document in the world that says we existed—that says we *measured*, we surveyed, we built, we counted—and your plan was to sell it."

"To buy time. To get clear of—"

"To sell it." She let that sit. Rain dripped off the bollard between them. "So yes. I stole it. Unasked. Without apology." Then, quieter, in a different voice entirely: "They'd have found it, Petra. The fuse cord is clever, but the thin one has searched forty rooms this month and he reads engineers the way I read water. You'd be in a cell by now, and the ledger would be in Brandt's safe, and in five years it would be a rumor, and in ten it would be nothing."

Petra opened their mouth to argue and discovered the argument had collapsed somewhere during the speech. That was the humiliating part—not losing the fight, but realizing mid-fight that you'd already lost it yesterday, maybe the moment you'd shown a stranger the seal on your satchel in a market full of strangers.

Because here was the thing Petra kept circling and refusing to land on: Sefa hadn't stolen the ledger *from* them. She'd stolen it *for* them. There was a difference, and the difference had a weight, and the weight had consequences.

"They'll search again," Petra said finally. "Brandt doesn't send clerks. He sends clerks first, then himself."

"Then we shouldn't be here when he does."

"*We.*"

Sefa looked at them—looked the way she'd looked at the seal in the market, white-knuckled and certain—and said, "You've been saying 'we' since the trestle. You just haven't noticed yet."

"I have not."

"'My room got searched.' 'They'll come back.' 'We shouldn't be here.'" She ticked them off on her fingers. "Engineer, you count rivets for a living. Count those."

Petra counted them. The terrifying thing about arithmetic is that it doesn't care what you wanted the total to be.

Somewhere up the street, a door slammed, and both of them turned toward the sound at exactly the same instant, with exactly the same flinch, and then looked at each other having seen each other flinch, which was its own kind of signature on its own kind of document.

"The dry season starts in five weeks," Sefa said. "When the river drops, the upper reaches close to boats, and the elders' claim dies with the water. I need to be upstream. And you—" she almost smiled, and it was the almost that undid him, "—you need to not be here when Brandt knocks again. Those happen to point the same direction."

Petra looked at the satchel. Looked at the woman holding it hostage. Thought of Ovid's pencil in the margins—*paid in full, not in coin*—and of a valley holding its breath, and of a debt that apparently traveled with the toolbox.

"If I say no?" Petra asked.

"Then I give the ledger back and we both stay and get arrested together, which is at least efficient."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then you inherit the rest of whatever your mentor owed," Sefa said, "and I stop owing you a theft."

The rain kept coming sideways, with its opinion. Downstream, the river ran low and getting lower, patient as a creditor.

Petra heard themselves say, "Two tickets upstream, then," and understood, with the sensation of a bridge settling under load, that the decision had been made some days ago—by hands that weren't theirs, in margins they hadn't written, in a debt they were only now learning to count.
