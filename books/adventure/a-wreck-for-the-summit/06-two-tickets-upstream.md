## 6. Two Tickets Upstream

Sefa Idrizi had been ferrying people across the Vell for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read passengers the way other people read weather. There was the nervous kind, who gripped the rail and asked about depth. There was the drunk kind, who sang. And there was Petra Zhou, who stood at the stern of her boat like a man auditing it — checking the gunwale joints, the wear on the oarlocks, the way the hull sat in the water — and said nothing at all.

That was three days ago, back when Petra still had the ledger and Sefa had only seen its seal for one white-hot moment in the market before being refused like a beggar at a wedding. Three days ago Sefa had gone home to her room above the chandlery and lain awake staring at the ceiling beams, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

Now the ledger was back under her floorboard, because she had stolen it, and Petra was sitting in her kitchen pretending not to know that, and the river was dropping an inch a day, and everyone at this table was lying about something.

It was, she reflected, pouring the coffee, a very Vellmarki sort of morning.

"You put sugar in it," Petra said, tasting, suspicious.

"Vellmarki coffee has sugar in it."

"This isn't Vellmarki coffee. This is Crown coffee, bought with Crown coin, in a Crown river town."

"And yet." She pushed the honey pot toward them anyway. "Sugar is sugar. My grandmother used to say the Crown can tax everything except what you put in your own cup."

Petra looked at her over the mug with those flat surveyor's eyes, the ones that measured everything and admitted to nothing, and Sefa thought: *this is going to be difficult.* Good. Difficult things were usually worth it.

* * *

She had rehearsed the speech on the walk over from the chandlery. She'd rehearsed it again while hauling water. She had, in fact, rehearsed it so many times that by the time she opened her mouth, the whole careful structure collapsed and what came out was:

"The elders have been waiting forty years."

Petra blinked. "Good morning to you too."

"They've been waiting since I was six years old. Since the Proclamation. Do you know what the Proclamation says? It says Vellmark never existed. Not conquered — *never existed*. You can't inherit land from a country that was never there. That's the whole trick of it. They didn't take our nation, they took our *past tense*." She heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, breathe, start again lower. "Except paper survives. Paper always survives. The old surveys survive. If the ledger reaches the elders at the source before the dry season closes the gorge, they can file the claim under the Border Water Act — the one law the Crown's own lawyers wrote that cuts against them. Original survey documentation. Continuous occupancy. It's all in there, Petra. Every line Ovid Zhou ever ran."

Petra set down the mug. "You stole my ledger."

"I protected your ledger."

"You went through my—"

"I went through your *satchel*, which you left unattended at a public dock, which any Brandt man could have done with less apology." She folded her arms. "And if you're going to be angry, be angry quickly, because we have perhaps nineteen days and I intend to spend every one of them arguing."

Something flickered behind Petra's eyes — not quite amusement, but its cousin. "Nineteen days until what?"

"Until the river drops below the shallows at Graymarl Bend and nothing bigger than a coracle gets through. Until the gorge path dries into dust and the patrols can track us by footprint. Until the elders' window closes for another year, and then another, until everyone who remembers waiting is dead and the children—" she stopped herself. "The children at Kell's Ferry have never seen the source, Petra. Never. It's two weeks' walk and it might as well be the moon. The anthem, yes. The recipes, yes. The heron on every doorpost. But the actual water? The actual stones?" She spread her hands. "We are a nation of people describing a house we were born outside of."

Silence. Petra drank the oversweet coffee without complaint, which Sefa noted was itself a kind of concession.

"Why me?" Petra said finally. "You have a whole community upstream. Boats, cousins, people who owe each other favors. Why does it need a disgraced Crown engineer who won't even show me their face when they smile?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"You do. I've watched you try three times. It's like watching a door open onto a wall."

Sefa laughed despite herself, then pressed on before the laugh could soften anything. "Because the ledger is half yours. Because Ovid Zhou's hand is in the margins of every page, and the elders will want his heir standing next to it when it's presented. Because—" she hesitated, then decided honesty was the better weapon, "because you're the best engineer on this stretch of river and the first closed line hasn't been opened in forty years, and I have rowed past that tunnel mouth my whole life and wondered what's behind it."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll take the ledger myself and go alone, and probably drown at the second weir, and it'll be your fault posthumously."

"That's not how fault works."

"It's exactly how fault works in my family. We have a very sophisticated system."

* * *

Petra didn't say no. That was the thing. Petra also didn't say yes. Petra did what Petra apparently always did, which was stand up, walk to the window, and look at the river as though it might offer terms.

Sefa let them look. She used the time to make eggs, because in her experience no negotiation survived an empty pan, and because cooking for someone was a way of saying things she hadn't earned the right to say plainly yet.

"You should know what happens if the Crown gets it instead," she said, cracking shells one-handed. "Brandt recovers the ledger. It goes into an archive in the capital. In twenty years some clerk misfiles it. In fifty it's pulp. The valley gets blasted for the summit railway foundations, the aquifer goes wherever aquifers go, and the last legal thread connecting us to that water snaps quietly in a drawer." She slid the eggs onto two plates. "My mother crossed the border with nothing but a spoon and a hymnal. She used to tell me: they can burn the house, but check what the fire leaves in the ash. The ledger is what the fire left, Petra. It's ash-shaped and it weighs four pounds and it's everything."

Petra came back to the table. Sat. Looked at the eggs the way they'd looked at her boat — professionally, hunting for flaws — and ate them anyway.

"If I agree," Petra said slowly, "it's not for your elders. Understand that. I'm not sentimental about nations. I've seen what nations do to engineers who build their bridges."

"Then why?"

Three reasons, Sefa suspected, and Petra would admit to maybe one and a half.

"Ovid," Petra said. "There are margin notes in that book in his hand, and I haven't finished reading them. He owed someone something — the way he wrote, it reads like a debt he meant to pay and didn't. If the debt is to your elders, then it's mine now, and I don't carry debts I can name." A pause. "Also, Brandt has men watching the docks. Staying in this town another week is a choice between arrest and running. Running upstream happens to be the direction I was already considering."

"And that's all?"

"That's all."

"Liar," Sefa said warmly, and passed the bread.

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and it was during the preparation that Sefa learned the most useful thing about Petra Zhou, which was that Petra could not lie with their hands. Their mouth could produce a perfectly smooth sentence — *I'm just a contract surveyor, I've never touched a sealed line in my life* — but give Petra a task and the truth came out through the fingers. They coiled rope like someone who'd been flogged for bad coils. They checked her boat's caulking and found the one seam she'd been ignoring since spring and re-caulked it without asking, muttering figures under their breath, and when she teased them about it they flushed to the ears and pretended to be checking something else.

"You love this," she said, watching them true the tiller fitting with a borrowed file.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're *happy*. Your shoulders come down when you work. Did you know that? You walk around up here—" she indicated the general region of Petra's ears, "—but the moment you have a tool in your hand, you live in your hands instead."

Petra looked at their own hands as if seeing them for the first time, then looked away. "Pack the spare fuse cord," they said. "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

"We're doing this properly," Sefa agreed, and felt something in her chest unknot that had been knotted since the market, since the moment she'd seen the two-headed heron on a stranger's satchel and understood that the past tense could be revoked.

* * *

By dusk the boat was loaded: tools, rope, the good lamp, hard bread, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and hidden in the false bottom of the water cask, because Sefa trusted no lock and no satchel and very few people. Petra argued for hiding it in the tool chest. Sefa argued that Brandt would search a tool chest and nobody searches a water cask because nobody wants to get wet. Petra conceded with the expression of someone filing the argument away for later review.

They stood at the dock in the blue hour. The river murmured its evening murmur. Somewhere upstream, invisible, the first closed line waited behind forty years of stone.

"One condition," Petra said.

"Name it."

"When this is done — whatever happens at the source, whoever keeps the ledger — I want the margin notes copied. All of them. Ovid's hand. I want them for myself."

Sefa studied them for a long moment. Here it was, she thought. The real cargo. Not the paper — the handwriting. Everyone on this river was carrying somebody's handwriting; hers carried her mother's spoon-and-hymnal story, worn smooth from handling. She nodded.

"Copied fair, in ink, on good paper. And Petra — when the elders ask who brought the ledger home, I'm telling them the truth. Ovid Zhou's heir. You can scowl about it the whole time."

"I don't scowl."

"You're scowling right now."

"That's my face."

"It's a scowl with ambitions."

And there — briefly, badly, like a lamp behind a curtain — Petra smiled. Actually smiled. Sefa decided not to remark on it, the way you decide not to move when a wild bird lands near you.

She cast off the bow line. The boat swung its nose upstream, toward the darkening gorge, toward the tunnels and the dropping water and the elders who had waited out a lifetime of springs.

"Nineteen days," she said, taking the oars.

"Eighteen and a half," said Petra, who had clearly checked the gauge marks that morning, and settled into the stern with the map of closed lines across their knees — and began, quietly, to count rivets on the old trestle pilings as they passed, like a person greeting old friends whose names they weren't ready to say aloud.

Two miles upriver, on the far bank, a lantern blinked twice. Held steady. Blinked twice again.

Petra saw it too. Neither of them said a word. But Sefa pulled harder on the oars, and Petra moved the ledger deeper into the cask, and the boat slipped into the shadow of the gorge like a secret returning to the place it had been kept.
