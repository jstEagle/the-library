## 7. The Dead Line Breathes

The first closed line announced itself the way old men announce themselves at funerals: with a cough of loose stone and an air of having been waiting a very long time.

Petra heard it before they saw it. The boat rounded a bend where the gorge narrowed to a throat, and beneath the river's mutter there came a low, hollow note—wind moving through something man-made. A tunnel breathes differently than a cave. Caves sigh. Tunnels whistle. Forty years of engineering instinct sat up in Petra's chest like a dog hearing its name.

"There," Sefa said, pointing with the pole.

Above the waterline, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble and one aggressively optimistic birch tree, stood the portal. It should have been rubble. Every other abandoned line Petra had ever surveyed ended in collapse—the mountain always won eventually, that was the whole quiet joke of the profession. But this portal stood square. The masonry was intact. And across the arch, someone had laid a course of dressed stone in a pattern Petra knew intimately: the Vellmarki bond, alternating headers and stretchers, the signature of masons who'd been taught by the same school Ovid Zhou had learned from secondhand.

"It's not collapsed," Petra said, in the tone of a person reporting a miracle they distrusted.

"No," Sefa agreed, hauling the boat toward a shelf of gravel below the portal. "It's *closed*. There's a difference. My grandfather used to say the Crown didn't destroy our lines—they locked them. Like a house you leave when you're angry but still own."

She made it sound casual. It wasn't. Her knuckles were white on the pole.

They beached the boat and climbed the gravel shelf, and Petra put their hand against the stone, because they always did, because stone told you things your eyes lied about. Cold. Dry. No frost-heave in the joints. Whoever had sealed this door had done it properly, with drainage weeps left open at the springline so water wouldn't build pressure behind the wall and crack it. That wasn't spite-work. That was care. Somebody had closed this tunnel the way you'd close a house for winter, expecting to come back.

"Somebody loved this line," Petra murmured.

"Everybody loved this line," Sefa said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The seal itself was a wall of coursed stone set two feet back from the portal face, mortared with a lime mix that had cured hard as porcelain. Petra circled it twice, running their fingers along the joints, reading the work the way other people read letters.

"Whoever built this wanted it strong," they said, "but not permanent. See here—" they tapped a course of stones about chest height, slightly different in color from its neighbors, "—this is a plug, not a foundation. Different batch of lime, probably mixed on-site in small quantities. And these weeps—" three clay pipes set into the lower courses, "—they're sized to drain seepage but not floodwater. If someone had meant never to return, they'd have flooded the backfill and been done with it."

Sefa watched them with an expression Petra couldn't parse at first. Then they realized what it was: recognition. The particular look of a person watching another person speak fluently in a language they'd thought dead.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you touch a wall and it tells you its whole life story." She leaned against the portal frame, arms crossed, entirely too comfortable. "My uncle Tomas did that. He was a bridge warden on the upper line. He could put his palm on a pier and tell you the year it was built and whether the mason had been paid in full or in grudge."

"Was he right?"

"Always. It drove my aunt mad." She smiled, and then the smile went somewhere more careful. "Can you open it?"

Petra looked at the wall. Behind it, presumably, ran a mile or more of tunnel climbing toward the interior, toward the next closed door, toward the source. Around them, the gorge held its breath. Somewhere behind them, hours downstream and gaining or not gaining, Elias Brandt was doing arithmetic.

"I can open it," Petra said. "But I want to be honest with you about what opening it means."

"Go on."

"If I take this plug out wrong—if I go at it like a wrecker instead of an engineer—I could bring the portal down, bring half the hillside down, and close this line forever in the most literal sense available. This isn't a door. It's a load-bearing decision." They crouched, checking the weeps again. "But if I do it right, it comes out clean, and the tunnel behind it has had forty years to prove whether it holds. Which is information we need anyway."

"That's not honesty," Sefa said. "That's a lecture. I asked if you could open it."

Petra blinked. "Yes."

"Then open it." She was already unloading tools from the boat. "I'll make tea. You'll want tea after. Everyone wants tea after; it's a law of nature where I'm from."

* * *

It took four hours, and Petra would remember all of them for the rest of their life.

The problem was elegant, which is engineer-speak for horrible. The plug was keyed into the surrounding masonry—not just stacked but bonded, so you couldn't simply knock it out without loading the arch above. The correct approach was to cut the keying joints, relieve the load with temporary timbering, and draw the plug forward in controlled pieces. Textbook work. Work Petra hadn't done since before the inquiry, since before the name Zhou became a cautionary tale taught to apprentices over lunch.

Their hands remembered anyway.

That was the thing nobody warned you about disgrace—that the body keeps faith when the profession doesn't. Petra's hands found the chisel angles without being asked. Their ears sorted the ring of good stone from the dull knock of a joint already cracked. When they set the first acrow prop—a improvised affair of boat spars and wedges, because they'd packed light and lived poor—the load transferred with a sound like a long-held note finally released, and some muscle deep in Petra's shoulders unclenched for the first time in six years.

They worked. Sefa watched.

She tried to be subtle about it, which failed completely. She'd position herself "checking the tea fire" at an angle that commanded the entire worksite. She'd ask questions that were transparently excuses—"Is that tool called a gad or a jumper?" (a gad), "Do engineers talk to the stones?" (only the difficult ones)—and between questions she simply watched, chin on her knees, with an openness that Petra found harder to bear than suspicion. Suspicion they knew how to deflect. Admiration had no handle.

"You're staring," Petra said, somewhere in hour three, after drawing out the first full stone and setting it aside intact for reuse.

"Yes," Sefa said, without apology. "You're very good at this."

"It's just masonry."

"Mm." She poured tea into a tin cup and held it out. "My grandfather said the Crown took everything except what people were *good at*. They can't confiscate competence. He said that's why they burned the guild rolls—couldn't stand knowing exactly what they'd stolen." She pressed the cup into Petra's hand. Their fingers were still vibrating faintly from the chisels. "Drink. You've earned it. You're earning it."

"I haven't opened anything yet."

"You've been talking to that wall for three hours like it's an old colleague, and it's been answering. That counts."

Petra drank the tea. It was terrible—river water, scorched leaves, a pine needle that had fallen in and been committed to—and it was the best cup they'd had in years, and they hated how much that revealed about the state of their life.

* * *

The last stone came out at dusk.

Petra had planned the extraction for the day's end deliberately—one final cut, one pull, and then whatever was behind the wall would be behind the wall no longer, and there'd be no daylight left to work by, forcing a rest. Rest mattered. Tired engineers made doors into tombs.

The stone slid free with a grinding whisper, and the tunnel exhaled.

There was no other word for it. Forty years of held air moved past them in a slow current, cool and mineral-smelling, carrying the particular scent of dry limestone and old creosote and, underneath it, something almost sweet—decades of dust settling into chemistry. The draft stirred the bramble at the portal and carried it inward, and Petra stood in the doorway of the dark with their hat in their hand like a man entering a church.

"Well?" Sefa whispered, though there was no reason to whisper.

Petra struck a match and held it through the portal.

The flame bent inward, steady, fed by the breathing draft. Beyond it: track bed, intact. Rails, present—rusted to a dull chocolate but seated true on sleepers that looked, even at match-distance, sound. Drainage ditch along the east side, clear. And on the west wall, every twenty meters, a white-painted survey station, numbered in stenciled characters that forty years hadn't managed to blur.

"It's alive," Petra said. Their voice came out strange. "The whole line. It's not a ruin, it's—it's asleep. Someone shut it down properly. Drained the culverts, greased what needed greasing, left the stations marked." They turned to look at Sefa, and the match died between them, and in the dark her face was a pale shape and her eyes caught the last of the sky. "Sefa, this line could run. With real work—months of it—but it could actually run."

"My grandmother said it would," Sefa said quietly. "She said the Crown would need it again someday, because they were too proud to build anything as well as we did, and too cheap to tear ours down right." She laughed, a small wet sound. "We used to say that at gatherings. *The line sleeps.* Children's talk. I thought it was a story we told ourselves."

"The line sleeps," Petra repeated. It sounded different in their mouth than it must have in hers—less like folklore, more like a condition report.

They camped that night in the portal mouth, backs to the sealed darkness, facing the river. Sefa slept quickly, the way river people do, dropping off mid-sentence. Petra lay awake listening to the tunnel breathe behind them—in, out, slow as a sleeper's ribs—and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at inconvenient hours, exactly what was wrong with them.

They belonged to this. Not to Vellmark, not to the exile community, not to any flag or anthem or two-headed bird. But to the craft itself—to the grammar of loads and drains and keyed stone, to the pleasure of a wall that told the truth. For six years they'd survived by being nobody: no title, no history, no handwriting anyone could recognize. And now they'd spent a day being *extremely* somebody—an engineer, in full, hands and ears and judgment—and it had felt like coming home to a house that had never been theirs. Warm rooms. Familiar stairs. Someone else's name on the deed.

Ovid's name, probably. Ovid's debt, certainly. The thought should have frightened them. Mostly it did. But underneath the fear was something worse: relief. The relief of putting down a weight they'd convinced themselves was a personality.

Beside them, Sefa murmured something in her sleep. Not Vellmarki—or not entirely. Petra caught the shape of it: *the line sleeps,* in a child's cadence, a rhyme.

In the morning, they'd shoulder packs and walk into the dark, following rails toward the source and whatever waited there. In the morning, Brandt's arithmetic would be one day closer to catching them. In the morning—

Petra sat bolt upright.

The draft had changed.

All night the tunnel had breathed in and out, slow and tidal, the way sealed spaces breathe when temperature shifts. But now the intake was stronger. Steadier. Coming from deeper in, pulling past them with purpose, the way air moves when somewhere ahead of it there's an opening—a second door, a shaft, a chimney to the surface.

Or a way through that didn't require opening anything at all.

"Sefa," Petra said, and shook her shoulder. "Wake up. The tunnel's breathing wrong."

She surfaced instantly, hand going to the boat knife. "Wrong how?"

"In. Only in. There's airflow from inside—deep inside. Which means either the far end's open—" Petra paused, listening again, and the hair rose on their arms, "—or something in there is moving enough air to matter. Get the lamp."

From the dark behind them, faint as a pulse, came a sound neither of them could explain: metal on stone. Rhythmic. Patient.

Like a winch, turning.
