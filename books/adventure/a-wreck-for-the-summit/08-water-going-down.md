## 8. Water Going Down

The river told you things if you were willing to listen, and lately it had been telling Sefa the same thing over and over in a voice that got smaller every morning: *hurry*.

She'd grown up on the Vellmarki stretch of this water, back when it had a name in two languages and a ferry schedule her grandmother kept in her head like a psalm. She knew its moods the way you know a relative's — the spring temper, the autumn sulks, the way it went quiet and mean before a flood. So when she woke on the third morning above Kell's Ferry and heard the gravel bar downstream crunching instead of hissing, she didn't need a gauge.

"The river's dropped a hand-span since yesterday," she said, crouching at the bank while Petra drank their terrible coffee. "Maybe more."

Petra looked at the water the way they looked at everything — as though it had filed incorrect paperwork and was about to be asked to explain itself. "You can tell that by sound?"

"You can tell it by sound." She flicked a pebble at their boot. "The shallows stop whispering and start clicking. Gravel moving under less water. Also," she added, standing and pointing upstream to where the *Vela's Daughter* sat tilted against her mooring line like a tired horse, "she's listing to port, and yesterday she wasn't."

Petra squinted at the boat. Then at the river. Then at Sefa, with an expression she was beginning to recognize as the engineer's version of grief: the world refusing to obey its own specifications.

"How many days do we have?"

"That depends on how honest you want me to be."

"I've recently developed a taste for honesty. It's very new. Don't ruin it."

Sefa laughed despite herself — she'd been doing a lot of that lately, laughing when she'd sworn she wouldn't, and she suspected it was Petra's fault. "Four days, maybe five, before she won't float the gorge shallows. After that we walk, and walking means hauling everything, and hauling everything means we arrive at the source after the elders have packed up for the season." She let that sit. "Or never."

Petra said nothing for a long moment. They did that thing where they turned their cup slowly, watching the coffee dregs settle like sediment in a core sample. Then they nodded once, sharp, and started breaking camp without another word, and Sefa understood that the nod meant *yes*, and that Petra Zhou would rather chew gravel than say yes out loud where someone could hold them to it.

Fine. She could work with nods.

* * *

They ran the first reach hard through the morning, Sefa at the sweep oar reading the water, Petra amidships with the ledger chest lashed between their boots, calling out depths off the old survey figures whenever Sefa demanded them, which was often, and occasionally when she hadn't, which was better.

"Twelve feet past the point," Petra said at one bend, unprompted.

"Thank you."

"You didn't ask."

"You're learning."

It was, Sefa thought, a strange kind of conversation — numbers passed back and forth like coins, no wasted words, and yet somehow the most fluent either of them had been in weeks. On the second day out from the market town, somewhere around the burned pilings of the old toll bridge, something shifted. She called "*Left!*", and Petra was already shifting weight before the word finished; she leaned into the current and found Petra leaning with her, not against her, and the boat came through the chute so clean it felt less like steering than like being agreed with.

Afterward neither of them mentioned it. But Petra handed her the coffee first that evening without being asked, and Sefa caught herself smiling at the fire like an idiot, and decided that competence, apparently, was a language, and that she and this impossible railroad ghost were becoming conversational.

Then the sky over the western gorge went the color of a bruise, and the river stopped being a metaphor for anything.

* * *

She heard it before she saw it — a sound upstream like a door slamming underground, deep and wrong, the sound of water finding a channel it hadn't been invited into. Flash flood. The high country must have caught rain that hadn't reached them yet; up there it would be falling still, gathering, looking for the fastest way down, and the fastest way down was the gorge ahead of them and every low shelf of bank beside it.

"Off the water," Sefa said, already hauling for the north bank. "Now. There's a shelf above the scree — get us there."

Petra didn't argue, which told Sefa everything about how bad it sounded even to ears that hadn't been raised on this river. They ran the *Daughter* in against a gravel fan, jumped into thigh-deep water, and hauled her up by the mooring line while the first brown tongue of the flood came licking down the far channel. The chest went up the rocks first — of course it did; Petra carried it like a grudge — then the tools, then the bedrolls, then the two of them, soaked to the ribs, sitting on a ledge of limestone forty feet above a river that had doubled its width in the time it took to swear about it.

And that was when Sefa saw the work crew.

Downstream, wedged on a gravel spit that the flood was eating from both ends, stood six men in Crown gray, waving their arms in the universal language of people who have made a serious error. Their punt was gone — she could see it wrapped around a boulder like wet paper — and the spit was maybe thirty paces long and shrinking.

"Oh no," Sefa said.

"No," Petra agreed.

"They'll drown."

"They'll drown," Petra confirmed, in the tone of a person cataloguing facts they disliked.

Neither of them moved for exactly three seconds. Sefa counted. Then Petra said, "The rope. Do we have enough rope?" and Sefa was already uncoiling it, and whatever either of them had intended to feel about Crown employees — the same Crown that had erased Vellmark, the same Crown whose surveyor was presumably sniffing along their wake like a dog after a sausage cart — got set aside, because the water didn't care whose side anyone was on. Water only cared about arithmetic.

The problem was the eddy. Below the spit the current folded back on itself in a slow revolving hinge, and anything thrown from their ledge would swing wide and miss. Unless it didn't. Unless someone read the fold right and put the rope into the one lane of slack water that curled back toward the spit like a hand beckoning.

Sefa had grown up throwing lines into that exact kind of hinge. Petra had spent twenty years calculating where loads would swing. They looked at each other, and Sefa watched the same thought land behind Petra's eyes that was already running through hers, and neither of them said a word about it.

"Your throw or mine?" Sefa asked.

"Yours. My arithmetic." Petra was already tying off the coil end around a limestone horn, testing it with their whole weight, muttering figures under their breath — swing radius, current speed, the drop of the line's arc — and Sefa realized with a small private shock that she trusted those numbers completely, absolutely, the way she'd never trusted any figure any official had ever quoted her.

She threw. The coil went out flat and low, kissed the hinge just where Petra's finger had pointed a half-second before, and swung back in a lazy arc across the slack lane until it landed at the feet of the youngest man on the spit, who stared at it as though it were a snake.

"PICK IT UP," roared Petra, in a voice Sefa had not previously known they owned, and the young man picked it up.

Getting six men across a flood-swollen river on one rope is not a rescue scene from a ballad. It is cold, and loud, and repetitive, and it takes forever, and every single crossing you are certain the next one will be the one where the rope snaps or the ledge gives or somebody panics. Petra anchored and counted; Sefa belayed and pulled; the men came across one at a time, white-faced, clutching the line, and the spit behind each of them got smaller. The last one across was a boy — truly a boy, nineteen at most, in a clerk's coat too big for him, with ink-stained fingers and a satchel he refused to drop even when the water took him to his chin and Sefa had to lean out and haul him by the collar.

He came up the rocks coughing, clutched his satchel to his chest, and gasped, "The — the manifests —"

"Let go of the manifests or I'll add them to the river myself," Sefa said, and something in her tone made him let go of everything except the rope.

* * *

By dark the flood had crested and begun its long rude withdrawal, leaving the banks smeared with mud and the air smelling of drowned willow. The six Crown men huddled around a fire they were allowed, barely, at the edge of the ledge, close enough to share its warmth and far enough to make clear it wasn't theirs. Their foreman, a slab-faced man named Dree, had tried twice to ask questions — who were you, what's your business on this water, that's a fine boat for civilians — and had received, respectively, a shrug from Sefa and a look from Petra that made him decide he was cold and tired and needed to sit down.

But the young clerk came and sat near their gear, dripping, wrapped in his own coat, and watched Petra work.

Because Petra was working. Of course they were. While everyone else shook and shivered, Petra had produced the little brass gauge from their kit and was taking readings off a stake they'd driven into the receding waterline, logging figures by firelight in a notebook, lips moving. Every quarter hour. Unasked. Because the river was dropping fast now, faster than it should, and Petra wanted the curve.

"You're surveying," the clerk said finally. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't an accusation either. It came out almost reverent.

"Someone has to know how much river is left," Petra said, not looking up.

"My father was a lock-keeper. Before." The boy hesitated on the word *before* the way exiles' children always did, the word a door to a room nobody was allowed to enter. "Before the dissolution. He used to take readings like that. He said a river with no one measuring it is a river that belongs to whoever's lying about it."

Sefa went very still. Petra's pen stopped for half a heartbeat, then continued.

"What's your name, boy?" she asked.

"Jory. Jory Ansel." He swallowed. "Clerk, third grade, Office of Summit Works. Or — I was. That punt had our season's supply ledgers on it, and Mr. Dree says we're all dead men when we report losing them, and—" He stopped, looked at the fire, at the gray coats, at the rope still coiled where it had saved his life. "You didn't have to pull us out. You especially didn't. We'd have been a problem for you."

"We noticed," Petra said dryly.

"So." Jory turned to face them fully, and Sefa saw that under the shivering and the ink stains there was a spine in the boy after all. "I owe you my life, and I'm not going to pay it by telling the first patrol I meet that a surveyor and a ferrywoman with a Vellmarki seal on her satchel are running the dead line upstream. Whatever you're doing. Whoever you are." His chin wobbled slightly, and he steadied it. "That's mine to give. Nobody ordered me to keep quiet. Nobody ever orders clerks anything; they just assume we'll write what we're told."

Silence, except the river, dropping audibly now — a long mineral ticking as the shallows gave up their water, the sound Sefa had woken to that morning, sped up and merciless.

Petra looked at the boy for a long time. Sefa knew that look; she'd been on the receiving end of it. It was the look of a person checking a weld.

"All right," Petra said at last. "Here's what you'll write, then, when they find you. You were swept downstream past the gorge mouth. A fisher family pulled you out. You saw no boat, no seal, no surveyor. Can you remember a lie that size?"

Jory considered. "Sir, I copy out figures for a living. Lying is just figures that don't add up. I can carry one."

Something happened to Petra's face then — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would be built, if Petra ever authorized construction. "Fishers," Petra said. "Give them a name."

"Halla," Sefa said quietly, before she could stop herself. Her grandmother's name. The boy wrote it down.

* * *

Later, when the Crown men slept in a row like spoons and Jory sat awake on watch out of some clerkly instinct toward order, Sefa stood at the edge of the ledge and listened to the river shrink.

It was worse than she'd estimated. The flood had been a thief as well as a bully — it had scoured the upper channel and stolen the snowmelt reserve all at once, and now the water was paying it back in a hurry. By her ear, they'd lost a day. Maybe two. The *Daughter* would float the lower gorge tomorrow and the day after, and after that the gravel bars would come up like bones.

"Four days of boat," she said to the dark, knowing Petra had drifted up beside her without hearing a footfall. "Three, if we push. Then we walk."

"Six days walking," Petra said. "With the chest and the tools. Five if we abandon the tools, and I'm not abandoning the tools."

"I wasn't going to suggest it."

"I know. I just wanted to say it out loud so the universe knows where I stand."

Despite everything — the lost day, the flood, the six sleeping witnesses ten feet away, the surveyor named Brandt whom she could feel in her hindbrain like weather coming — Sefa laughed. Quietly, so as not to wake Dree and his gray ghosts, but she laughed, and she felt Petra almost laugh too, a small seismic event, quickly suppressed.

"We'll make it," she said. "We have to. The elders break camp when the source shallows go dry, and after that the claim dies with the season, and forty years of waiting dies with it."

Petra was quiet a moment. Then, in a voice lower than the river: "We'll make it."

Not *yes*. Not a promise. But from Petra Zhou, it was a signed document, and Sefa found she could live on that.

She was about to turn in when she saw it — downstream, far off, where the gorge bent toward the old toll road: a light. Not a campfire's sprawl. A lantern, held high and swept side to side, deliberate, patient, the way a man searches a riverbank when he is looking for something specific and has all night to find it.

Beside her, Petra went rigid.

"Do you see it?" Sefa whispered.

"Yes," Petra said. And then, with the weariness of a person doing arithmetic they'd hoped to avoid: "That's not a fisherman's sweep. That's a measured grid. Someone's searching in sections."

The lantern paused. Swept again. One section closer.

"Brandt," Sefa breathed, and the name dropped into the dark between them like a stone into the shrinking river, and the ripples hadn't finished spreading when Petra reached for the map of closed lines.
