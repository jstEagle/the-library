## 9. Brandt's Arithmetic

Elias Brandt had three rules for fieldwork, and he kept them written on the inside cover of his notebook in his own small, square hand: *Count everything. Trust nothing you didn't count yourself. And never, under any circumstances, drink the coffee at a Crown waystation.*

He was currently violating rule three, which was how he knew he was tired.

The coffee at the Wrenlow Toll Station tasted like it had been brewed from a tarpaulin and then apologized for. He drank it anyway, standing at the counter while the toll clerk — a boy of perhaps nineteen with the unfortunate name of Pim and the even more unfortunate habit of talking — dug through a month of ledgers.

"Nobody comes through here worth writing home about," Pim said. "Mostly gravel haulers. Two surveyors from the summit office, back in— no, wait, that was June. Oh! There was a boat crew. Bought rope."

"When?"

Pim squinted at the ledger. "Fifteenth."

Brandt wrote *15th — rope* in his notebook, because rule one said count everything, and because a party buying rope at a river station was either going somewhere difficult or planning to pretend they'd been somewhere difficult. Both were interesting. Interesting was his business.

Officially, his business was simpler than that. His orders, signed by the Deputy Commissioner of Railways and stamped twice for emphasis, read: *Recover the Vellmarki survey ledger removed from Crown waters near the old Trestle Nine. Apprehend and question the party responsible.* There was a line about discretion, and another line about the sensitivity of matters touching the dissolved territories, which was Crown language for *we would prefer this never happened.*

Brandt had read the order four times on the ride out, and each time he noticed something new that bothered him. The first time, it was the word *removed*, as though the chest had climbed out of the riverbed on its own legs and walked off. The second time, it was the absence of any mention of what the ledger contained. The third time, it was the date of the order, which predated the report of the disturbance by two days.

The fourth time, he stopped reading and started thinking, which was the most dangerous thing a man in his position could do.

Because an order written before the crime is reported is not an order to investigate. It is an order to conclude.

***

He spread his materials out that evening in the waystation's back room: the toll ledgers he'd copied, the river charts, the survey plates for the new summit railway, and — folded into a folio he was not supposed to have — his own field notes from three years ago, when he had been a junior surveyor on the very same summit line, before his promotion moved him from measuring ground to measuring men.

His wife used to say he thought in columns. She had meant it kindly. Marta had been dead six years now, and he still caught himself arranging grief into tidy rows, as if sorrow could be reconciled like accounts. It could not. But arithmetic was the one prayer he knew, so he prayed it.

Column one: the disturbance at Trestle Nine, reported by a gravel crew, dated the eleventh.

Column two: the engineer who had been working that stretch of river. Petra Zhou. Hired on the Crown's own books, which meant there was a file, and Brandt had read the file twice. It was thin in the way files are thin when someone has scraped them clean. Survey work, seasonal contracts, references that checked out exactly far enough and no further. A mentor listed among the referees: Ovid Zhou, deceased. No relation, or none admitted.

Brandt had interviewed Zhou's protégé at the work camp, over coffee that violated rule three even by camp standards. He remembered the interview with professional discomfort, because he had enjoyed it. That was the trouble. The suspect had lied to him — he was nearly certain of it, certain enough to write it down — but had lied *well*, with the specific competence of someone accustomed to being believed, and had poured the coffee without being asked, and had answered the dangerous questions a half-second slow and all the other questions instantly, which is the signature of an intelligent person managing a conversation rather than having one.

And there had been the moment with the map. Brandt had asked, idly, whether Zhou carried closed-line surveys on personal jobs. And Zhou had said — smoothly, reasonably — *"Everyone in this trade keeps old maps. They're how you know where the bodies are buried."*

A joke. A good one. And underneath it, a confession delivered with such confidence that Brandt had almost applauded.

He had let them go. He told himself it was procedure: no warrant, no evidence beyond instinct, and instinct did not survive contact with a magistrate. But he knew the truer reason, and the truer reason sat in his chest now like a swallowed stone. He had let them go because he had wanted to see where they went.

Rule two said trust nothing you didn't count yourself. Very well. Count.

Column three: the route. Toll stations along the upper river, in order. Wrenlow, the fifteenth — rope purchased. Harrow Bend, the seventeenth — nothing purchased, but the ferry log showed a passenger crossing against the current, upstream, paying in coin too old to be common. Brandt turned one of those coins under his lamp. It bore a bird. Two heads, one body, wings spread like a man surrendering or a saint ascending.

He knew the bird. Every surveyor of his generation knew the bird, though officially it appeared nowhere. The two-headed heron of Vellmark, kingdom dissolved forty years past, its borders absorbed, its maps pulped, its name surviving only in the sort of documents the Crown preferred burned and in the memories of people the Crown preferred scattered.

The coin was forty years old at least. Someone had been keeping it. Keeping it bright.

Column four: the old line. Here Brandt opened his own field notes from three years ago, and here his handwriting — usually so neat — went ragged, because this was the page where the trouble lived.

The summit railway's charter route crossed the high valley above the river's source. He had surveyed that crossing himself, young and eager and flattered to be given the section every senior man called *the widow-maker* and declined. He had measured the rock, tested the water table, filed his figures. And then his figures had come back from the ministry changed.

Not wrong. Changed. His numbers had shown the valley sitting on water the way a drum sits on a skin — an aquifer under pressure, sealed by limestone, holding more water than the valley held sky. His recommendation had been a deep-drainage scheme, expensive, unglamorous, the kind of thing that wins no ribbons. What came back from the ministry, bearing his name in the header, recommended shallow footings and a celebratory note about schedule savings.

He had protested. Once. In writing. He still had the reply, though he no longer carried it: a courteous paragraph informing him that the hydrological question had been *resolved at a higher level*, and a raise.

He had taken the raise. That was the whole of his villainy, if villainy it was, and he had spent three years telling himself it wasn't — that a junior surveyor does not halt an empire's railway, that the figures might have been re-examined by better men, that the valley would hold. He had gotten very good at not doing the arithmetic. That was the trick of it. Not lying, exactly. Declining to count.

Now the count was walking up his river in a hired boat, buying rope, paying with heron coins, following a line that had been closed for forty years straight toward the source of everything he had declined to add up.

Brandt put down his pen. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the river ran low over its stones, dropping toward the dry season with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

***

He did the final sum at midnight, because some sums will not wait for morning.

If the ledger recovered from the riverbed was what the orders implied — a Vellmarki survey document — then it predated the dissolution, which made it older than the Crown's claim to the source valley. Claims, in law, were a matter of paper and priority. The Crown's paper was forty years younger than whatever Zhou was carrying.

But paper alone wouldn't send a careful person running upstream through closing water. Paper needed a destination. And the only destination on the old Vellmark source line — the line that matched the toll records, the ferry crossings, the rope, the coin — was the source itself.

Which meant the ledger was not merely a relic. It was a key, and the lock was at the head of the river, and someone intended to turn it before the season shut the door.

And beneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, the third fact: if the aquifer figures in that ledger agreed with the figures Elias Brandt had measured three years ago and watched get erased, then the Crown's summit railway was scheduled to blast its foundations directly into a reservoir of water, and the flood would run downhill into every settlement on the lower river, including — he checked the gazetteer, knowing what he'd find, finding it — a cluster of unregistered homesteads upriver of Harrow Bend that the census had politely failed to notice for two generations.

Vellmarkis. Of course. The Crown erased a nation and then built its railway on the grave without checking whether the grave was hollow.

Brandt sat with that until the lamp burned low. He was not a sentimental man; sentiment, in his experience, was arithmetic done badly, rounding up what you hoped for. But he found his eyes had gone to the window, where the stars over the eastern ridge sat in their ancient, unsurveyed positions, and he thought: *Marta would have known what to call this.* She had been a schoolteacher. She had believed people were legible, given patience. He had never managed to believe it about institutions.

Here was the war, then, laid out on the table with all its pieces counted:

His orders said recover the ledger. His oath said obey his orders. His superiors had falsified his survey, which made his oath complicit, which made obedience a kind of lie, which made him — he searched for the word and settled on it without pleasure — *accessory*. And yet if he set the orders down and picked up his conscience instead, he had no idea what the conscience instructed. Warn the exiles? Confess? A man could not arrest his own government. There was no cell for that. He had looked, once, in the regulations. The regulations were silent on the subject, which told him everything.

There was also, he admitted, the matter of the suspect themselves. Petra Zhou. He turned the interview over again in his mind, the way he'd turn the heron coin. An engineer hiding an engineering past. A protégé carrying a dead mentor's debts. Someone who had stood in the rain of Brandt's polite questions and lied only about the things that mattered, which was, in Brandt's long experience of liars, the behavior of an honest person in an dishonest situation.

He wrote one last line in the notebook, under the columns, in his small square hand:

*They are not fleeing the Crown. They are outrunning the season. Follow the water, not the law. The law will catch up on its own; it always travels lighter.*

Then, after a moment, because rule one said count everything:

*Rope. They bought rope. Nobody buys rope to read a ledger.*

***

Dawn came up gray and practical. Brandt paid Pim for the room, overpaid for the coffee on principle, and stood a moment on the toll station's porch watching the river.

It was down a hand's width since yesterday. He could see it in the exposed stone along the bank, a pale seam like a scar healing upward. Six days, maybe seven, before the shallows above Harrow Bend became unnavigable even for a determined boat with good rope. After that, anyone bound for the source would be walking, and walking meant gear, and gear meant weight, and weight meant they would move slowly and leave a trail a competent man could follow at his leisure.

Brandt was a competent man. He mounted his horse.

He did not send the telegram waiting at the station office — the routine one, *trail cold, continuing inquiries* — until he had rewritten it three times to remove anything that might tell his superiors precisely how warm the trail was. Let the ministry think he was floundering. Floundering men were left alone. Men who found things were given orders, and he had had quite enough of orders written before the facts.

As he rode out onto the north road, he passed the ferry landing, and a woman there — broad-shouldered, river-worn, mending a net with the ferocity of someone sewing a flag — looked up at him and then away, too quickly, the look of a person who recognized a uniform and had opinions about it she was saving for later.

He noted her. Rule one. He did not stop. Some counts, he was learning, you let finish themselves.

The road bent east toward the old line, and the ridge ahead held the shape of a country that no longer officially existed, and Elias Brandt rode into it carrying two sets of orders — one signed in ink, one unsigned in his chest — and for the first time in three years, he allowed himself to do the arithmetic all the way to the bottom.

The total frightened him less than he expected.

What frightened him was how much he hoped the engineer got there first.
