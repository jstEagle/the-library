## 10. The Town That Isn't on Maps

The turnoff from the river was not a turnoff at all. That was the first thing Petra noticed, and the thing they kept noticing all evening: Kell's Ferry did not want to be found.

Sefa took them up a side channel so narrow that willow branches combed through the boat like fingers through hair, then poled across what looked like a dead-end marsh until a curtain of reeds parted on hinges — actual hinges, oiled and quiet — and they slid into a boathouse built into the bank itself.

"You've done this before," Petra said.

"Once or twice." Sefa tied off with a knot that had no name in any language Petra knew. "Rule one of being erased from maps: build your doors where cartographers don't look."

"And rule two?"

"Feed anyone who finds you anyway."

* * *

The town — Petra refused to think of it as anything smaller than that, though it was maybe forty roofs folded into a river bend like a hand cupped around water — announced itself by smell before sight. Woodsmoke, bread, hot iron. A dog trotted out to inspect them, decided they were acceptable, and escorted the whole way in like an honor guard of one.

People came out of doorways the way water comes out of cracks: gradually, then everywhere. An old woman with flour to her elbows. Two men mending a net who didn't stop mending but didn't stop looking either. And children — six, eight, a dozen — materializing along the fence line with the universal posture of children who have been told not to stare and are staring anyway.

"Sefa Idrizi," said the old woman, wiping her hands. "You were supposed to come at midsummer."

"I was supposed to come never, Aunt Besa. Plans changed." Sefa put a hand on Petra's shoulder, and Petra felt it land like a stamp on a document. "This is Petra Zhou. Engineer. They're bringing something home."

Every eye went to the satchel. Petra's hand went to the satchel. The two motions happened at exactly the same speed, which told Petra something about themselves they didn't enjoy.

"Bread first," Aunt Besa said, as if the ledger were a matter for after dinner, which — Petra would realize later — was precisely how these people survived forty years of erasure. Everything enormous got handled after bread.

* * *

The blacksmith was called Dario Vuk, and he had hands like bridge abutments and a voice he clearly wasn't used to raising, because when he sang, it came out careful, like a man carrying something full to the brim.

It happened at supper, in the long low hall that served as everything — meetinghouse, schoolroom, dance floor, refuge. Someone asked Sefa if she'd heard news from the coast settlements, someone else made a joke about Crown tax collectors and their inability to find their own boots, and then Dario, half in his cup, started humming.

Petra knew within four notes that it was an anthem. You can't fake the architecture of one — the way the melody climbs like it's climbing stairs toward something.

Dario sang it low, three verses, and the hall went quiet in a way that wasn't sad, exactly. It was the quiet of people keeping something alive by not disturbing it. The two-headed heron, flying both ways over one river. Petra caught perhaps one word in five — the exile dialect had drifted upstream from whatever Petra had read in Ovid's old correspondence — but the tune lodged under their ribs and stayed there.

"That's the anthem," Petra said afterward, quietly, to Sefa. Not a question.

"Dario's grandmother taught him. He's the only one left who has all of it." She turned her cup. "The children know the chorus. They've never seen the source, Petra. Some of them have never seen the old markers. They sing about a country that exists entirely in a song and a seal and—" she nodded at the satchel between Petra's feet, "—whatever you're carrying."

Petra looked down the table at the children, who had finished eating and were now engaged in the ancient sport of seeing how close they could get to the strangers without being caught at it. One girl, maybe seven, with a scowl far too large for her face, had established a forward position approximately four feet away and was conducting surveillance.

"Hello," Petra said.

"You talk funny," the girl said.

"So I'm told."

"What's in the bag?"

"Halina!" her mother hissed from the kitchen doorway.

"A book," Petra said. "An old one."

"Is it worth a lot?"

"Halina."

"It's worth a lot," Petra said, "but not in money," and the girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate, decided it was either very wise or very stupid, and retreated to report to her confederates.

Sefa was smiling at Petra in a way that made them want to check whether they'd spilled something.

* * *

The trouble with warmth, Petra thought, lying awake that night in the guest room above the smithy, is that it doesn't announce itself as a trap. Traps are supposed to be hidden. This one had lit lamps and set out bread and given them a room with a quilt that smelled of lavender and somebody's whole life.

They'd been claimed. That was the word for it. Somewhere between the anthem and the girl named Halina, the town had simply — annexed them. Aunt Besa had already called them "our engineer" twice. Dario had asked, shyly, whether Petra might look at his forge bellows, which had been wheezing since spring. Someone had mentioned, casually, terribly, that the footbridge needed real engineering if it was ever going to take a cart.

And Petra had heard themselves say *I could take a look at that* — twice. Three times.

They sat up in bed and looked at the door. Their bedroll was still rolled, still strapped, sitting by the door where it always sat, where it had sat in every lodging for eleven years, since Ovid died and Petra learned the arithmetic of departure: keep the bedroll packed, and leaving costs nothing.

Except it wasn't nothing anymore, was it. Leaving now would cost a girl with a scowl. It would cost a blacksmith's wheezing bellows. It would cost Sefa, who slept somewhere down the hall and who had, without asking, without apology, made Petra's war her own.

Petra lay back down and stared at the beams.

The trap wasn't the town. The trap was that some traitorous chamber of their chest had already answered the door.

* * *

Morning brought repairs, because mornings in Kell's Ferry apparently brought nothing else.

By nine, Petra had the forge bellows open on Dario's bench, finding a cracked leather gusset and a hinge worn to lace. By ten, a small crowd had gathered to watch, because in a town of forty roofs an engineer at work was better than a festival. By eleven, Halina had appointed herself assistant, handing tools with the precision of a surgeon's nurse and the commentary of a sports announcer.

"Small wrench."

"This one?"

"The other small wrench."

"There's only one size of small."

"Then yes, that one."

Dario watched from the doorway with his enormous arms crossed, and when Petra got the bellows breathing again — a long, even, satisfied exhale, like the forge itself had been holding its breath for a season — the big man laughed, a sound like a pile driver falling over, and clapped Petra on the shoulder hard enough to relocate something.

"Our engineer," he said, beaming, and Petra didn't correct him, and hated themselves a little for not correcting him, and loved it a little too, and filed all of it under *later*, the drawer where they kept everything they couldn't afford to feel yet.

The footbridge took the afternoon. It was honest work — bad joinery, worse pilings, a load path that insulted everything Petra had ever learned — and fixing it put them in the cold shallows up to the knees with Sefa holding the far end of a beam, and for an hour there were no nations and no ledgers and no surveyor named Brandt presumably tracking them downstream even now. There was just the beam, and the count — *lift on three* — and Sefa's laugh when the count went wrong and the beam went into the water and Petra went in after it.

"You're doing that on purpose," Sefa accused, hauling them up.

"Engineers test structures," Petra said, dripping. "Sometimes destructively."

"You tested it with your whole body."

"Thoroughness."

* * *

Supper that second night was fish and flatbread and a bean stew that Aunt Besa refilled with the relentlessness of a woman who measured love in bowls. And it was there, warm and fed and surrounded, that Petra made the mistake of relaxing.

Because that was when the elder came.

His name was Tomas Idrizi — Sefa's uncle, it emerged, though the family tree here seemed less a tree than a woven basket, everyone related to everyone by blood or by shared erasure. He was old the way bridges are old: still standing, visibly bearing weight. He sat down across from Petra without ceremony, folded his hands, and looked at the satchel.

"You've read it," he said.

"Some."

"Then you know what my brother's generation waited for." Not accusatory. Just placing the fact on the table between them, like a coin.

"I know what's in a ledger," Petra said carefully. "Lines and figures and claims. I don't know what it is to wait a generation."

"No," Tomas agreed. "But you know what it is to carry a debt that isn't yours." He must have seen Petra flinch, because he almost smiled. "Sefa talks in her sleep. Well — she talks constantly; sleep is no barrier."

Petra looked down at the stew. "Ovid Zhou surveyed for Vellmark. Whatever he was paid, I inherited it. I understand that's why you're feeding me."

"We'd feed you anyway," Tomas said. "But yes. That's why we're *patient* with you." He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and paused. "One more thing, engineer. The elders go up to the source country every dry season. Every year, they wait there a week, at the old markers, in case someone comes. Forty years, they've gone. My sister went nineteen times before her knees quit. This year the water's dropping early." His eyes were pale and steady. "If the ledger doesn't reach them before the ford dries, the claim dies in a courtroom instead of living on the land. So eat your stew. Rest your hands. Because in two days, we'll need those hands, and I'm told they remember things."

He walked off. Petra sat very still.

Across the hall, Halina was teaching three smaller children the anthem's chorus, getting most of the words wrong with tremendous confidence. Dario hummed along, supplying corrections between mouthfuls. The fire cracked. Somebody laughed at something.

Petra reached down and moved the satchel from the floor to their lap, and held it there, and let the hall's warmth press in from all sides like deep water.

A trap, they told themselves. Remember it's a trap.

But their hands, resting on the brass-stamped cover, had already stopped believing it.

* * *

Later, in the smithy loft, Sefa found Petra sitting up with the ledger open on their knees, the lamp turned low, not reading — just looking at the margin notes, Ovid's cramped, slanted hand crawling up the edges of pages full of another man's figures.

"He wrote in the margins the way other people breathe," Petra said, without looking up. "Constantly. Involuntarily."

"What does this one say?" Sefa sat beside them, close enough that their shoulders touched, and pointed at a note Petra had been staring at for ten minutes.

Petra read it aloud, slowly, translating the shorthand: "*Source valley — see aquifer note, final page. Do not let them blast here.*"

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"Do not let them blast here," Sefa repeated softly. "Blast where? What aquifer note?"

"I haven't gotten to the final page," Petra said. Which was true. Which was also, some part of them observed with cold professional clarity, a sentence they had been carefully making sure remained true for two days.

Sefa turned and looked at them — not angry yet, just reading, the way she read water — and outside, far downstream toward the Crown works, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound of blasting. The summit railway, chewing through the mountains, one charge at a time, heading for something it didn't know was there.

Petra closed the ledger.

"Tomorrow," they said. "Let me read the final page tomorrow."

Sefa kept looking at them. The blasting echoed again, and faded, and the silence it left behind was the loudest thing in the room.
