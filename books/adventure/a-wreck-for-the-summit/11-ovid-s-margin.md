## 11. Ovid's Margin

The rain came in off the gorge that evening like a debt collector—no knocking, just arrival—and drove us under the tarp with the boat turned on its side and the ledger between us like a third passenger who took up too much room.

Sefa Idrizi had grown up reading by lamplight in a house where lamplight was rationed, so she knew how to make a little light go a long way. She held the candle stub close to the page and tilted it until the old ink threw its shadows sideways, and that was how she saw them: not the surveyor's neat columns of figures, but the margin notes. Small ones. Slanted ones. Written fast, by someone who had been thinking faster than he was writing.

She had read those margins before, or thought she had. In Kell's Ferry, by better light, with Petra hovering at her shoulder like a man watching someone else hold his own child. She'd read the headings, the chainage numbers, the elevations. The bones of the thing.

Tonight, with the rain hammering the upturned hull and Petra asleep—or pretending to be, curled in his bedroll with his back to her, which for Petra was the same posture either way—she went back to the margins properly.

They were in two hands.

One hand was Vellmarki script, old-fashioned, the letters formed the way her grandmother had formed them, with the little tails on the *r* that the Crown schools had beaten out of everyone under forty. That was the original surveyor, whoever he'd been. The other hand—

She knew that other hand.

Not from the ledger. From the map of closed lines that lived rolled in oilcloth in Petra's pack, the map he pretended he didn't have and guarded like a wound. She'd seen his mentor's name penciled on the title block once, when Petra wasn't looking: *O. Zhou, asst. eng.* And she'd seen the way Petra wrote his own figures—tight, economical, every stroke doing double duty—and she'd seen this second hand in the margins doing exactly the same thing.

Ovid Zhou had written in this book. Ovid Zhou, dead these six years, drowned in a river not so very different from this one, had sat somewhere upstream with a candle of his own and written notes in the margins of a Vellmarki survey ledger that officially did not exist.

"Petra," she said.

He didn't move. Rain drummed the hull. Somewhere downriver an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Petra. I know you're awake. You breathe differently when you're asleep. Quieter."

A long pause. Then, from inside the bedroll, muffled: "Nobody breathes quieter asleep."

"You do. You breathe like you're hiding it." She turned the page. "Come look at this."

"I'm sleeping."

"You're listening. It's better than sleeping. Come listen with your eyes."

He grumbled the way a door grumbles—protesting, but opening—and came to sit beside her with the bedroll still around his shoulders like a shell he hadn't decided to leave. He looked at the page upside down first, because he was Petra, then reached over and turned the ledger toward himself without asking, because he was also Petra.

His face did something complicated.

"That's his writing," Sefa said.

"Yes."

"You knew."

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence for him. "I knew he worked a contract he never talked about. The year before I apprenticed proper. He came back thin and strange and wouldn't say where he'd been. Said it was drainage work." His jaw tightened. "Drainage work. He taught me everything about water and he called the most important job of his life *drainage work*."

"And you never wondered what he surveyed?"

"Of course I wondered. I wondered for six years. Wondering isn't knowing." He tapped the margin note with one finger, careful, the way he touched anything old. "What does it say? The Vellmarki parts are beyond me."

So Sefa read them aloud, translating as she went, slowly, because some of the terms were surveyor's terms and some were older than surveying—the words her people used for underground water, for the stone that holds it, for the way a valley drinks.

*The spring is not a spring,* the first note said. *It is a window. Below the source valley lies the aquifer—the deep water, the held water—limestone to a depth no chain will reach. The whole valley floor sits on it like a lid on a pot.*

The next note, further along:

*Crown party arrived today. They are blasting the summit cut. I told their engineer the mountain is hollow below. He wrote it down. Whether he believed it is another matter.*

And then, cramped into the bottom corner of the final page, in handwriting gone hurried and small, as if the writer had heard footsteps coming:

*If they blast the lower gallery the lid cracks. The water will find the new road down. Everything downstream of the source goes under—the ferry crossings, the low fields, the town at the bend if there is still a town at the bend. Forty years from now nobody will remember why the valley flooded. They will call it weather.*

Sefa stopped reading.

The candle flame leaned in a draft and straightened itself. Rain ran off the hull in ropes. Downstream—two days' walk downstream, three days' poling, depending on the water—was the bend in the river. Was the exile town. Was her mother's cousin's forge, and the schoolhouse with no roof yet, and the children who sang the heron anthem off-key because only the oldest among them had ever heard it sung right.

"They're going to drown us," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "The summit railway. The blasting. It's not a risk. It's a schedule."

Petra didn't answer right away. He was staring at the last margin note with an expression she couldn't read, and she was learning, slowly, to distrust her ability to read him precisely because she'd gotten good at it.

"Petra," she said. "Did you know?"

The silence went on one beat too long, and that was the answer before any word was.

"You *knew*." She pulled the ledger toward herself, out of his reach, absurdly, as if distance from his hands would change what was already true. "You've known since when?"

"Since the chest," he said quietly. "Since the first night. I read it through. All of it. The aquifer notes are in his hand and I know his hand better than my own."

"Since the *first night*." She stood up under the tarp, which meant bending nearly double, which somehow made the fury worse, having to fold herself around it. "At the trestle. Before Brandt. Before me. You read that my town sits under a flood schedule and you said nothing."

"What was I going to say?" He was standing too now, and his voice stayed level, which she knew was effort, which made it worse in a different way. "'Hello, stranger I met yesterday, your community is doomed, also here's the evidence that would get us both hanged'? You'd have run straight upstream to warn them. Which is exactly what should happen, but—" He stopped. Started again. "You'd have taken the ledger and gone, and Brandt would have had me, and the ledger, and none of it would have reached anyone."

"So you decided for me."

"Somebody had to decide something!"

"And that's the part you don't hear, Petra. That's always the part." She was shaking now, and not entirely from cold. "You decide. What I get to know, when I get to know it, whether I'm trustworthy enough to be trusted. You carried this alone because you didn't think I could carry my share. My own town's death sentence, and you didn't think I could carry it."

"I thought—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. When he spoke again it was slower, dragged up from somewhere deeper. "Everyone leaves, Sefa. Everyone always leaves, or gets left, and the leaving is easier if you've already packed. I told myself if you knew, you'd leave, and I'd deserve it, and at least I'd see it coming. That's not a reason. I know it's not a reason. It's just the shape of me."

It was, infuriatingly, the most honest thing he'd ever said to her, and she hated it, because honesty is very hard to stay angry at and she wanted, badly, to stay angry.

"The elders," she said instead, forcing her voice down to business, because business was safer. "The claim dies with the season. That was the mission. That's what we tell them at the source—that the ledger proves the line, proves the land, proves we were here before the maps forgot us."

"It's more than that now." Petra crouched and turned the ledger back around, and his finger found the figures beneath the margin notes—not Ovid's hand, the original surveyor's, the columns of depth and flow. "Look. Your people didn't just measure the water. They measured what happens to it. Flow rates, storage, the pressure head in the limestone. This isn't a land claim with a warning attached. It's a warning with a land claim attached. Whoever built this ledger built it to survive exactly this—to sit in a drawer for forty years until somebody needed proof of both things at once."

"Ovid finished it," Sefa said. "Your mentor. He added his notes to theirs. He tried to stop the blasting and failed, and then he—" She looked at the last line again. *They will call it weather.* "Then he hid the book where the Crown would never dredge, and he went home and never told even you, and he died with it."

"He paid a debt," Petra said. "To your kingdom. And I'm holding the receipt."

Outside, the rain eased from roar to steady murmur, the way it does when it has decided to stay all night rather than make a scene. Sefa sat back down. The anger was still there—it would keep, anger always keeps—but underneath it something else had moved in and rearranged the furniture.

This had been an escape. A race against the dry season, a ledger delivered, a claim filed, a debt cleared, two people who happened to be rowing the same direction. That was the story she'd told herself at the market stall when she'd seen the heron on his satchel and gone white as milk. *Get the book to the elders. Save the claim. Go home justified.*

But the ledger wasn't only a deed. It was a forecast. And forecasts come with obligations.

"We're not running ahead of the dry season anymore," she said. "We're running ahead of the blasting. Do you know when the Crown crews start the lower cut?"

Petra shook his head. "Brandt might. Brandt's men were measuring something at the second line—I assumed it was grade."

"They were measuring where to put the charges." She blew out the candle stub, because there was nothing more to read by it and because the dark made it easier to say the next part. "Every day we spend being careful is a day closer to the lid cracking. We can't take the safe route anymore. We can't wait out weather or dodge patrols. We go straight up the gorge, fast, and we bring them the whole truth—the claim *and* the warning—before the first charge goes off."

"Including the part where I lied to you," Petra said. "By omission. For a week."

"Including that." She lay back on the hull boards and stared at the underside of the tarp, where the rain shadows moved like fish. "I haven't decided what I'm going to do about it. I want that on the record. You don't get to decide that either."

"Fair," he said. And then, after a while, so quietly she almost let it pass: "For what it's worth. I'm glad it's you who found the note. If it had been me, alone, I'd have found a way to make it smaller. You make things bigger. It's the most annoying thing about you."

"It's the only thing about me worth the fare," Sefa said, and did not smile, because she was still angry, mostly, and anyway he couldn't see her in the dark.

But sleep didn't come easily after that, and when it finally did, it brought her the sound of water where water shouldn't be—a knock under the floor of the world, patient, scheduled—and she woke before dawn with her heart going and Petra already up, already packing, already watching the river gauge stick they'd driven into the shingle two days ago.

The water had dropped another hand's width overnight. He read it without touching it, the way he read everything, and she watched his face do the arithmetic.

"How bad?" she asked.

He didn't answer at first. He looked upstream, toward the source, toward the elders, toward the town at the bend that was downstream of all of it, and then he looked at her, and whatever he was going to say, she could see him deciding whether to say it or pack it away in the oilcloth with everything else.

"Sefa," he said. "There's something else in the margins I didn't tell you."

* * *

The quarrel that followed lasted until the rain came back, and the rain did not stop it, and neither of them came out of it unchanged—but that is the next part of the story, and it began, as most of their worst and best hours did, with a map.
