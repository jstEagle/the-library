## 12. The Quarrel at the Weir

The map came out of the oilcloth the way it always did—slowly, with the particular reluctance of a thing that has been rolled too long and knows its own creases by heart.

Sefa stood across the fire from him, arms crossed against the drizzle that had begun to sift down through the gorge's throat, and watched him unroll it on the flat top of an old weir stone. The weir itself was a Vellmarki relic, she'd told him that morning—a fish ladder built before the Crown had a name for the river—and its stones were worn smooth in the shapes of hands that had hauled nets up them for a hundred years. Now it just held back a shallow pool full of rain and regret.

"Show me," she said. "All of it. Not the parts you've been feeding me since Kell's Ferry. All of it."

Petra put his finger on the source valley. He did it the way he did everything he was ashamed of: precisely, without flourish, as though accuracy were a kind of apology.

"Ovid surveyed it twice," he said. "Once for the line, once for himself. Look at the margin notes here—the ones in the second hand, the smaller one. He mapped what's under the valley. Limestone, karst channels, a standing body of water the size of a lake, all of it held up by rock that's been dissolving since before anyone drew a border through here." He traced the contour lines Sefa had assumed were decorative. "The aquifer."

"The aquifer," Sefa repeated. She said the word carefully, like a coin she was testing between her teeth.

"And the summit railway," Petra said, "is blasting its cut through the ridge directly above it. Here." His finger moved to the ridge, to the little crown-shaped marks Ovid had used to denote planned demolition charges. "When they fire those shots, the vibration goes down through the limestone. Karst doesn't forgive vibration. It opens. Channels connect. And everything—" he lifted his finger off the map, and let it hover over the river downstream, over the bend where a town sat that wasn't on maps, over a blacksmith who remembered anthems and children who'd never seen the water their grandparents sang about—"everything goes downhill from there."

The rain thickened. Neither of them moved to shelter.

"How long," Sefa said.

"Ovid estimated the first rupture within two seasons of the blasting starting. The blasting started last spring."

"Two seasons." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible part, Petra thought later; it didn't rise at all. It went somewhere else entirely, down into a register he hadn't heard from her, flat and cold as the pool behind the weir. "So we have until next spring. And you've known this since—when? Since the ledger? Since the night you read it instead of selling it?"

"Yes."

"Since Kell's Ferry?"

"Yes."

"Since I fed you fish soup in my mother's cousin's kitchen and you watched my niece fall asleep against your shoulder while I explained to the elders' messenger that the ledger was coming home, that help was coming, that we had time?" Her voice cracked on *time*, one hairline fracture in all that flatness. "You sat there and you knew."

"I knew," Petra said.

* * *

Here is what must be understood about Petra Zhou, and what Sefa understood only afterward, and what Petra himself could not have articulated at gunpoint or at fireside or anywhere in between: he had spent thirty-one years constructing a life whose central engineering principle was that no structure should bear weight it cannot survive losing.

Mentors left. Ovid had taught him rivets and gradients and the load-bearing poetry of stone, and then Ovid had gone into the river country one autumn and not come out, and the letters that followed had been fewer and shorter until they stopped, and the debt Ovid owed Vellmark had passed to Petra like a mortgage on a house he'd never agreed to buy. Parents left; his had left early and politely. Crews left; that was what crews did when a job ended. Every person he had ever trusted had eventually become a name on a manifest, a signature in a margin, a handwriting he recognized on someone else's map.

So he had developed, over the years, a doctrine. It was not cowardice—he could face rockfall, floodwater, Elias Brandt's coffee. It was arithmetic. If you never let anyone close enough to be lost, you were never lost yourself. If you kept your bedroll packed by the door, departure cost nothing. If you withheld the aquifer survey—if you told Sefa about the claim and the elders and the deadline but not about the drowning waiting underneath it all—then when the truth came out, and it would come out, because truths in his experience behaved exactly like water under pressure, then at least he would already be gone. He would have left first. Leaving first was the only dignity the doctrine allowed.

He tried to explain some of this at the fire, and it came out worse than he feared.

"I was going to tell you," he said.

"When?"

"Soon."

"*When,* Petra."

"After the source. After the elders had the ledger and it was out of our hands and there was nothing either of us could do about it anyway. What difference does it make if you know two weeks earlier? You can't stop the blasting. I can't stop the blasting. Knowing sooner just means being afraid longer."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and the rain ran off the brim of her hat, and when she spoke again her voice had come back up out of the cold register into something far more dangerous, which was heat.

"You want to know what difference it makes," she said. "I'll tell you what difference it makes. At Kell's Ferry, old Marta asked me straight out—is the town safe, girl, is the water safe—and I said yes. I said yes because you'd read the ledger and you hadn't said otherwise, and I thought, here is a railroad engineer, here is a man who reads ground the way I read current, and if there were something wrong with the water he would say so. I staked my word on your silence. Do you understand that? Your silence has a price and other people paid it."

"That's not fair," Petra said, and heard even as he said it how small it sounded.

"No," Sefa agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair. The Crown isn't fair, the dry season isn't fair, Ovid Zhou taking payment from a drowned kingdom and leaving his debts to a stranger who won't even spend them honestly—that isn't fair either. But you don't get to stand in the rain and talk to me about fairness when you've been rationing the truth like it's the last of the drinking water." She stepped closer. The firelight caught her face and there was fury in it, but underneath the fury, worse, there was hurt, and the hurt was older than this quarrel, older than the ledger, the hurt of a woman whose whole nation had been erased by people who also found it more convenient not to mention things. "You keep a map of closed lines," she said, "because you closed them yourself."

The words went into him like a spike driven true.

Because it was accurate. That was the unbearable thing. He had spent years telling himself the map of closed lines was a keepsake, a professional curiosity, a memento of Ovid—but Sefa had seen through it in a market, in a heartbeat, the day she'd gone white at the seal. The closed lines weren't lines the world had shut on him. They were lines *he* had shut, door by door, tunnel by tunnel, sealing each one deliberately from inside, the way the Vellmarki had sealed their tunnels—not to keep anything out, but to keep something in. Grief. Hope. The memory of every crew that had ever dispersed and every friend who had ever become a manifest entry. He carried a map of his own abandonments and called it a survey.

"Sefa," he said.

"Don't." She held up a hand. Rain dripped off her fingers. "Don't do the thing where you apologize in complete sentences and then pack up and go anyway. I've watched you do it for three hundred miles. You think I haven't noticed the bedroll? You think I haven't noticed that you sleep facing the door, that your boots are laced before dawn, that you've never once unpacked anything deeper than the top layer of your pack? I'm a ferrywoman, Petra. I know cargo that's ready to be offloaded when I see it."

"I'm not—"

"You are. You're cargo that's ready to be offloaded. You've been ready since the day we met. The only question is whether you'll have the decency to admit that what you're really doing is jumping ship before the wreck, so you can tell yourself afterward that you saw it coming." She turned away from him, toward the river, toward the dark upstream where the source waited with its cathedral of limestone and its lake of held-back water, and her shoulders were shaking, and whether from cold or the other thing he couldn't tell and didn't dare ask. "My people lost a country because powerful men decided the truth was inconvenient. I will not lose the rest of it because one stubborn engineer decided his feelings were."

Petra stood by the fire with the map open between them and the rain coming down harder now, drumming on the weir stones, filling the pool, and every instinct he owned—the whole doctrine, the whole architecture of a life designed to fail safely—was screaming at him to roll up the map, douse the fire, lace his boots, leave first, leave now, leave while leaving was still his idea.

So that is what he did.

That is the shameful mechanics of it, and he never pretends otherwise in the account he gives later, not even to himself: he rolled the map along its old creases, and he slung his pack, and he picked up the lantern, and he walked out into the rain, upstream along the towpath, alone, in the dark, because everyone leaves and it is better to leave first and he had believed this for thirty-one years and belief does not surrender just because a ferrywoman has finally found the flaw in the design.

Behind him, he did not look back, so he did not see Sefa sit down on the weir stone beside the dying fire and put her face in her hands. He did not hear her say, to the rain, to the river, to nobody: "Come back, you idiot." He especially did not hear the way she said it, which was not angry anymore at all.

* * *

He walked four miles before he stopped.

He knew it was four miles because he counted, because counting was what he did instead of feeling, paces and chain-lengths and gradient estimates, the litany of his trade recited against the noise in his chest. The towpath climbed along the gorge wall. The rain came sideways off the river and found every gap in his coat. Somewhere below him the water ran loud and low, dropping by inches every day, the dry season eating the river the way it ate everything, patiently, without malice, and he thought: four days of water, six days of walking, and now minus one ferrywoman, minus one boat, minus the only person in forty years who had ever looked at the map of closed lines and known what it actually was.

He thought about the doctrine. He made himself think about it properly, the way he'd audit a suspect bridge: load by load, member by member.

Everyone leaves. True. Empirically unimpeachable. Ovid had left. His parents had left. Crews had left.

Better to leave first. And here the inspection found the crack, the hairline fracture running through the whole span, because leaving first had never once saved him anything. It had only guaranteed that he arrived everywhere alone. Thirty-one years of departures and not one of them had prevented a single loss; they had merely converted losses into self-inflicted wounds, which bled longer. Ovid had still died in the river country without him. The crews had still scattered. The only thing leaving first had ever produced was the map of closed lines, and Sefa was right, gods, Sefa was right, he had closed them himself, every one, and a man who seals tunnels from inside should not be surprised to find he cannot hear anyone knocking.

He stood on the towpath in the rain with the lantern hissing and thought about her niece asleep against his shoulder, and Marta's question—*is the water safe*—and the answer he had let Sefa give, and the fact that somewhere downstream a town full of blacksmiths and anthem-singers was sitting on top of a bomb with a two-season fuse, and that the man holding the fuse diagram was currently hiking away from it in a sulk because a woman had accurately described his emotional architecture in front of a fire.

"Well," he said aloud, to the rain, because there was no one else to say it to. "You're a wreck, Petra Zhou."

It was, he would maintain forever after, the single most productive sentence of his career.

He did not go back that night. Pride is a heavy cargo and does not offload quickly, and besides, the sensible thing—the engineer's thing—was to let the storm pass, to let them both sleep on it, to return at dawn with the aquifer figures laid out properly, with a plan, with the third path already half-drawn in his head: the old tunnel works, the sealed doors, the chance to channel the water instead of letting it find its own way out. He sat under a cutbank ledge with his coat over his knees and worked the problem by lantern light until the wick burned low, and the work steadied him the way it always had, and somewhere around the third hour he realized he was sketching not just the channel works but a small platform, a landing, at the bend above the town, and labeling it in Ovid's shorthand, and that he had drawn it without deciding to, the way you draw a house you intend to live in.

Dawn came gray and dripping. He packed the lantern, shouldered the pack, and walked back down the towpath with his boots squelching and his doctrine in ruins and something unfamiliar and terrifying sitting in his chest where the doctrine used to be.

The camp was empty.

Fire scraped over, ashes wet. Bedroll gone. Boat gone. And weighted under a stone in the middle of the cold fire ring, folded once, precise as everything she did: a page torn from the back of her river book, marked in charcoal with the one symbol every Vellmarki child learned before their own name.

The two-headed heron. One head looking forward. One looking back.

And beneath it, in her quick ferry-hand, six words:

*Gone ahead. Brandt crossed the weir.*
