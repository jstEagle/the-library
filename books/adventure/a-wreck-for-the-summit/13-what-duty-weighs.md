## 13. What Duty Weighs

Elias Brandt had been a surveyor long enough to know that the land always told the truth eventually. It was people who lied, and they did it in ink.

He stood at the mouth of the second closed line at half past five in the morning, when the mist came off the river like breath off a kettle, and he watched the two of them work. That was the thing that undid him first—not the trespass, not the warrant folded in his breast pocket like a knife he kept forgetting he carried. It was watching them *work*.

Petra Zhou was up on the old winch gantry with a lantern between their teeth—actually between their teeth, which was either madness or efficiency, and Brandt suspected both—and their hands moved over the rusted mechanism with the unhurried certainty of someone greeting an old acquaintance. Below them, the ferrywoman Sefa Idrizi hauled on a chain in a rhythm so perfectly matched to Petra's that it took Brandt a full minute to realize neither of them was counting aloud. They didn't need to. They'd been counting together for weeks.

*They don't even know they're doing it,* he thought. *That's the worst part.*

Brandt had three men behind him in the alder brush and a warrant in his pocket and orders that read, in the Undersecretary's own hand: *Recover the ledger by any means consistent with discretion.* He had read that sentence perhaps four hundred times since leaving Kell's Ferry, and every reading had taught him something new about the word *discretion*, none of it comforting.

He stepped out of the brush.

The chain went slack. Petra turned on the gantry, lantern still in their teeth, and for one absurd moment the three of them regarded each other across forty years of rusted iron—the Crown's man below, the trespassers above, the river muttering its way through the gorge.

Petra took the lantern out of their mouth. "You're early," they said.

"You're loud," said Brandt. "Winches are loud."

"We oiled it."

"I heard it from the bend." He came forward into the gray light, hands visible, hat pushed back—an old habit of his, showing empty hands before anything else. His men stayed in the trees because he'd told them to stay in the trees, and because Corporal Voss was twenty-two and thought warrants were exciting, and Brandt wanted no excitement before coffee. "I've been following your toll receipts for eleven days, Miss Idrizi. You pay cash, you overtip, and you argue about bridge fees, which is how I know the ferrywoman is genuine, because nobody fakes arguing about bridge fees."

Sefa set down the chain slowly. She looked, Brandt thought, less like a caught criminal than like a woman doing arithmetic she'd already finished. "And the engineer?"

"The engineer," said Brandt, "doesn't pay tolls. The engineer walks the banks and reads cut-stone like scripture, and leaves boot prints on trestles that were sealed before either of us was born." He looked up at Petra. "Zhou. Ovid Zhou's apprentice. The one who walked out of the Meridian collapse inquiry before they could decide whether you were a scapegoat or a suspect."

Something crossed Petra's face—not guilt, exactly. More like the flicker of a lamp in a draft. "You've done your reading."

"It's my trade. Reading things." He patted his breast pocket, where the warrant sat. "I have a document here that says I should put irons on you both and carry you back downriver."

"And?" said Sefa.

"And," said Brandt, "it's cold, and I haven't eaten, and I'd like to see what's behind that door before anyone closes anything forever." He nodded at the tunnel mouth, where the morning light had begun to find the edges of a stone-and-iron seal, mortared from the inside, deliberate as a signature. "May I?"

Petra looked at Sefa. Sefa looked at Petra. Between them passed one of those conversations that takes a second and would take Brandt a page to transcribe, and then Petra said, "You'll want to stand clear of the counterweight," which was not a yes, but was not a no, and in Brandt's experience was the most honest answer anyone gave before breakfast.

* * *

They made coffee on a spirit stove in the lee of the gantry, and it was terrible, and Brandt drank two cups of it out of politeness and a third out of genuine despair. Sefa produced hard bread and a jar of honey that she guarded with her elbow while doling it out, and Petra sat on a rusted drum with the ledger open on their knees, and for a while nobody said anything at all, which Brandt found more companionable than most conversations he'd had in the capital.

It was Petra who broke the silence, and they broke it sideways, the way engineers do—not with a confession but with a question dressed as a complaint.

"Your summit line," Petra said. "Kilometer thirty-one through forty-four. Who drew the grade figures?"

Brandt felt the coffee go cold in him, which was impressive, given it had started cold. "Why?"

"Because the figures in the public prospectus say the summit shelf sits on granitic basement. And granite doesn't do this." Petra tapped the ledger. "Limestone does this. Karst does this. Limestone under a summit shelf does—" they flipped pages, found the margin note in Ovid Zhou's cramped, patient hand, and held it out, "—this."

Brandt didn't take the ledger. He looked at the page from where he sat, because taking it would make it evidence, and evidence had procedures, and procedures had superiors, and superiors had the Undersecretary's handwriting in them.

"Read it to me," he said.

Petra raised an eyebrow. "You want me to read you my own stolen property."

"Your mentor's stolen property, technically, and I want to hear whether you flinch on the numbers." He set down his cup. "Indulge me. I'm a man who counts rivets."

So Petra read. And Brandt listened the way he'd once listened to his own instruments, back when he'd trusted them: completely, hungrily, with the whole of his attention arranged like furniture against a wall.

Ovid Zhou's margin notes laid it out plain. The source valley—the forbidden source, the one the maps had stopped naming forty years ago—sat above a limestone aquifer the size of a cathedral's conscience. The old Vellmarki surveys had mapped its pressure, its seasonal rise, its seepage paths. And the Crown's summit railway, as designed, intended to blast a cutting directly through the aquifer's crown sometime in the next eighteen months. When it did, the water wouldn't trickle out politely. It would find the new fracture, prefer it, follow it down, and come out—Brandt did the arithmetic in his head, and made himself do it twice—somewhere very near the valley floor. Somewhere near a town of exiles who weren't on any map, which meant that when the flood came, there would be no map to tell anyone they had ever been there at all.

"That's why the tunnels were sealed from inside," Sefa said quietly. "Not to keep people out. To keep the water's memory in. The old engineers knew what was under there."

"They knew," Petra said, "and they wrote it down, and then the country ended, and the writing survived, and now it's sitting on a rusted drum being read to a Crown officer over bad coffee." They closed the ledger. "That's the whole story. You can arrest us now."

Brandt looked at the tunnel mouth. He looked at the ledger. He looked at his own hands, which were steady, which had been steady for thirty years of service, and which he suddenly distrusted precisely because they were steady.

"My survey," he said.

Both of them looked up.

"The summit shelf transect. Kilometer thirty-one through forty-four. I ran it myself, four years ago." He spoke carefully, the way a man walks on ice he has personally tested and found wanting. "My figures said karst. Fractured carbonate, high seasonal water table, unsuitable for deep cutting without drainage works costing more than the cutting itself. I filed the report." He paused. "The report that reached the Minister's desk said granitic basement. Stable. Suitable."

Sefa's voice was very soft. "Who changed it?"

"A clerk, presumably. Or a committee. Or the Undersecretary, who had already promised the railway's completion date to three newspapers and a princess." Brandt laughed—a short, airless sound with nothing in it. "I appealed. Formally. Twice. The second time I was invited to consider my career trajectory, which is a phrase I recommend to no one. Then I was promoted, which is how the Crown says *be quiet*, and I accepted the promotion, which is how I said *yes*."

The stove hissed. Down in the gorge, the river ran low and clear over stones it hadn't shown all summer, dropping, always dropping, the season closing like a door.

"So," Petra said at last, and there was no cruelty in it, only the flat kindness of one professional to another, "you've known for four years that the figures were false, and you've spent eleven days chasing two people who can prove it, under orders to bury the proof."

"Yes."

"And now you've heard the numbers read aloud over breakfast."

"Yes."

"What," said Petra, "do you intend to do?"

And there it was. The question he'd been carrying upriver alongside the warrant, heavier than the warrant, heavier than his kit, heavier than the promotion that had been sitting in his chest for four years like a swallowed stone. Three people around a spirit stove with the same evidence and three duties that could not be reconciled by any geometry he knew. Sefa's duty ran upstream, to elders and children and a nation that existed now only in anthems and claims. Petra's duty—to whom? To a dead mentor, to a debt, to craft itself, maybe; Brandt genuinely couldn't tell, and suspected Petra couldn't either. And his own duty, which wore a uniform and signed his pay and had just asked him, in effect, to help drown a town to protect a lie he had personally disproven.

"I intend," Brandt said slowly, "to finish my coffee."

"Sir?" Corporal Voss's voice from the treeline, young and eager and carrying.

"Voss. Walk the downstream bank to the bend and back. Count the gauge markers. Take your time."

A pause. "All of them, sir?"

"All of them. There are dozens. Some may require sitting down near."

Boots crunched away. Brandt waited until the sound faded, then took from his satchel—not the warrant pocket, the other one, the honest one—a leather folder. Inside was his own copy of his own survey, four years old, annotated in his own hand with the corrections he'd never been permitted to file. He'd carried it upriver without admitting to himself why. Now he admitted it, silently, to a rusted drum and a spirit stove.

"If I arrest you," he said, "the ledger goes into a Crown archive, the aquifer goes into the Minister's too-hard tray, and in eighteen months a blasting crew makes a hole in the world and a town stops existing. If I let you go, I am guilty of dereliction, and possibly treason, and certainly unemployment." He turned the folder over in his hands. "I've been trying for eleven days to find the version of duty that doesn't end in one of those. I haven't found it. But I've noticed something."

"Which is?" said Sefa.

"That the Crown's claim to this valley rests on my falsified survey. Which means the claim isn't false because you stole a ledger. It's false because my superiors lied, and I let them." He looked up, and found Petra watching him with an expression he recognized from mirrors—the look of a person recalculating. "Duty's supposed to weigh something. That's the whole point of it. A duty that weighs nothing isn't duty, it's habit."

Petra said nothing. But they opened the ledger again, to the aquifer pages, and turned it to face him, and that was answer enough.

Brandt took out his pen. For a long moment he held it over his own report copy—the one true copy left in the world besides the ledger—and considered annotating it, filing it, fighting one more round through channels that had already eaten him twice.

Then he took out his matches instead.

The paper caught quickly, being dry and being, in the end, only paper. He held it until the flame reached his gloves, and let the last corner go into the stove, where it curled among the coffee grounds. Four years of careful figures, rising as heat. Somewhere downstream a town full of unrecorded people was waking up, and the water under the mountain was minding its own business, and Elias Brandt had just committed his first act of honest bookkeeping in four years.

"You understand," he said, as the ash settled, "that I now have no report, no warrant I'm willing to serve, and no idea what I'm going to write in my log tonight."

"Liar," said Sefa, almost gently. "You know exactly what you're going to write."

He did. *Surveyed second seal. Found parties engaged in legitimate hydrological assessment. No grounds for detention.* Every word of it true, which was the luxury of it, and the trap of it, and the strange lightness of it too.

He stood, brushed off his coat, and put his hat back on, because a man needed some kind of armor and his had a brim. "The third seal," he said. "Past the gorge narrows. It'll be flooded this time of year—the old drawings show a winch chamber, but the winch will have been stripped for salvage decades ago." He watched their faces change. "You'll need a diver. And you'll need to move today, because the river won't wait for your conscience and it certainly won't wait for mine."

Petra stared at him. "You're giving us route intelligence."

"I'm giving you *hydrological* intelligence," Brandt said. "There's a difference, and if anyone asks, we're all going to insist on it." He clicked his satchel shut. "Voss will be back in ten minutes. When he asks, I interviewed you, you cooperated fully, and I released you pending further review. Further review is a real procedure. It can take years."

Sefa was smiling now, small and sharp as a fishhook. "And what will you do, Mr. Brandt? After?"

"After," said Brandt, looking once more at the sealed door, at the sleeping water behind it, at the whole dishonest beautiful weight of what came next, "I believe I'm going to need to see a tunnel blown up properly. I've read the theory for years." He settled his collar against the morning chill. "I'd hate to die without the practice."

Behind them, far up the gorge, thin as a whistle, the river kept falling—and somewhere past the narrows, the third door waited with its lungs full of water.
