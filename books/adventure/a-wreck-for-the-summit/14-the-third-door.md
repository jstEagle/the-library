## 14. The Third Door

The tunnel mouth breathed cold at them like a held grudge.

Petra had known it would be flooded before they rounded the last bend of the gorge — you could hear it, if you'd spent enough years listening to dead lines the way Petra had. A sealed tunnel full of air makes a certain hollow note when water moves against its door. A sealed tunnel full of *water* says nothing at all. It just sits there in the mountain, patient as a debt.

This one said nothing.

The third door was a slab of riveted iron set into the limestone like a coin pressed into bread, and above the waterline — which came up to Petra's knees where they stood on the old loading platform — the Vellmarki crest was still legible: two herons, head to head, beaks almost touching. Someone had painted it fresh once, long ago. The paint had gone the color of weak tea.

"Winch housing's there," Sefa said, pointing to a rusted frame bolted over the door. "Or was."

"Or was," Petra agreed. The winch itself was gone. Not broken — *gone*. Bolts sheared clean, mounting plate pried up, and no wreckage anywhere on the platform. Somebody had taken it out of this tunnel deliberately, and carried it away through country that didn't have roads.

Sefa crouched at the edge of the platform and put her palm flat on the black water, the way she did when she was thinking with her hands. "Flooded from inside," she said. "The spring found a new path after they sealed it. It's been filling ever since."

"Forty years of spring," Petra said. "It'll run the whole length of the bore. Maybe farther." They did not say the rest, which was: *maybe all the way to the source*, which meant maybe all the way to everything they'd walked six days to reach.

They had come four days up the gorge on foot, hauling the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against Petra's chest like a second heartbeat, hauling the tools, hauling the arithmetic that got worse every morning. Four days of water left. Six days of walking, by the last honest estimate. The numbers had stopped being a plan somewhere around yesterday noon and had become instead a kind of shared prayer neither of them said aloud.

Behind them, somewhere down the gorge, Brandt's patrol was also walking. Also counting days. Petra tried not to think about that either, and failed, and gave up trying, which was nearly the same as peace.

"We go around?" Sefa asked, though they both knew the answer. The gorge walls here rose sheer for two hundred feet, and the ridge above was scree and slide-country, and the clerk — Tomas, who had caught up to them two days ago with a stolen patrol rationsack and an expression of a man jumping off a cliff into a river he hoped was deep — was already favoring an ankle.

"We don't go around," Petra said. "We go through."

Tomas looked at the water. Then at Petra. Then at the water again, with the particular dread of a young man who had recently learned that following engineers was hazardous to his health. "Through *that*?"

"The tunnel runs level for three hundred meters before it climbs toward the source gallery. If the far end isn't submerged, we walk through an air pocket and come out the other side." Petra heard their own voice doing the thing it did — flat, competent, invulnerable — and hated it a little. "If the far end *is* submerged, then we've lost nothing but an hour."

"And if the middle is blocked?" Sefa asked quietly.

"Then we've lost an hour and gotten wet."

She looked at them for a long moment. She had gotten very good, these past weeks, at hearing what Petra didn't say. It was one of the things about her that unnerved them most — that and the way she could read water the way Petra read rails, instantly, totally, without showing any work.

"You can't dive," she said. Not cruelly. Just as fact.

"I wasn't proposing to dive."

"You were proposing to stand on this platform looking at the water until I proposed it for you."

Petra opened their mouth to object and closed it again, because the objection available to them was *no I wasn't*, and they had never once in their life won an argument by saying something untrue in front of Sefa Idrizi. She always knew. It was like lying to a river.

* * *

They made camp on the platform because there was nowhere else, and ate cold rice while Tomas dozed against the winch frame with his bad ankle propped on a crate. The gorge darkened around them by degrees, the way it did — not dusk so much as the sky giving up on them early.

Petra worked by lamplight. This was the part of the trade they still loved without reservation: the figuring. They measured the door, sounded the water depth with a knotted cord (four feet at the lip, then unknown), checked the seal gaskets, and mapped the tunnel from memory — Ovid's original drawings lived in Petra's head now as surely as in the ledger, and possibly more reliably, since the ledger had spent forty years underwater.

Ovid had surveyed this line. Ovid had stood on this exact platform, probably, with this exact lamplight problem, and written in the margin in his cramped hand: *Third door seals true. If ever reopened, mind the sump below the rise — the spring does not forgive.*

*The spring does not forgive.* Petra had read that line a dozen times since Kell's Ferry and each time it had felt less like engineering advice and more like a warning sent forward through forty years specifically to them.

"You're doing the face," Sefa said.

"What face."

"The face where you've decided something terrible and are waiting for me to agree to it."

"I haven't decided anything."

"Petra." She sat down across the lamp from them, cross-legged, forearms on her knees, and waited. She was very good at waiting. She had grown up in a community that had been waiting forty years; patience was practically her mother tongue.

Petra turned the ledger page toward her. "Air pocket theory holds only if the bore stays intact. But there's a sump — a low section — about eighty meters in, where the grade dips under the old drainage cut. Ovid flagged it. If the water's filled past the sump, the air pocket doesn't connect. There's no walking through. There's only—" They stopped.

"Diving," Sefa said.

"Eighty meters of pitch-black flooded tunnel, at altitude, in snowmelt-cold water, with no idea what's collapsed on the far side of the dip. No. Absolutely not. That's not a dive, that's a coffin with a current."

"So we lose four days going over the ridge, and the river dies, and the elders' claim dies with it, and Brandt catches us on open ground where he doesn't need a warrant, just rope." She said it evenly, without drama, laying the sums out like a hand of cards. "That's the alternative."

"There's always another alternative. There's always—"

"Petra." Her voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went soft. "I grew up on these rivers. Before we came to Kell's Ferry my family ran freight through gorges that would scare you sober. I have been holding my breath professionally since I was six years old."

"That was twenty years ago and it was *surface* water—"

"It was the Drown Road," she said, "and my uncle died on it, and I know exactly what moving water takes and what it gives back. And I am telling you, engineer, that I can make eighty meters in a straight flooded bore if the far end has air behind it. I can't promise. But I can try. And you—" she pointed at them with the rice spoon, "—cannot. You swim like a filing cabinet. I've seen you."

"That was *one* river crossing—"

"You went in upright and came out upright. It was the most disturbing thing I've ever seen."

Tomas, half-asleep, mumbled: "It was pretty disturbing."

"Thank you, Tomas."

"*Nobody asked you,*" Petra told him, and he grinned and pretended to sleep again, badly.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere back in the flooded dark, water moved against stone with a sound like slow applause.

"Sefa." Petra made themselves say it plainly, because she deserved plain. "If you go under and something's wrong — a blockage, a snag, the air pocket's not there — I can't come get you. Do you understand? I would drown next to you and accomplish nothing except making it a pair."

"I know," she said.

"That doesn't frighten you?"

"Of course it frightens me." She reached across the lamp and took their hand — she did that sometimes now, casually, as if it were nothing, as if it weren't the single event of Petra's entire day, every day, for weeks. "But I've been frightened since the ferry landing, and the frightened feeling hasn't once been about the water."

Petra looked at their own hand inside hers and could not produce a single sentence. Forty years of Vellmark's silence, six days of gorge, one drowned kingdom's ledger against their ribs, and the hardest thing they'd done all year was sit still while a woman held their hand over a lamp.

"First light," they said finally. "We rig the line. I anchor you from here. Two hundred feet of cord, knots every ten. You count your knots aloud on the return if you can speak. If you're not back in twelve minutes, I'm—" They stopped. Swallowed. "I'm going to do something stupid. Let's both hope I don't have to define it."

* * *

First light in the gorge arrived sideways, a gray rumor leaking down from the rim, and by the time it reached the platform the water at the door looked less black and more like the inside of a bottle of ink held up to a window.

They rigged for an hour. Cord anchored to the winch frame, tested to Petra's whole weight plus anger. Sefa stripped to her shift, bound her hair, and rubbed goose fat on her arms and shoulders against the cold while Tomas held the lamp and asked questions he immediately regretted ("Do you breathe *before* or—", "Before, Tomas.").

Petra checked the knots twice, then a third time, hating every knot for existing.

"Twelve minutes," Sefa said.

"Eleven. I'm rounding down in your favor."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

And she kissed them — quick, on the mouth, matter-of-fact, the way she did everything involving risk, as if to say *this too is just work* — and stepped off the platform into the water, and was gone.

The cord paid out. Petra fed it hand over hand, counting knots, saying them aloud because Tomas needed to hear them and because Petra needed to hear their own voice doing something other than screaming.

"Ten. Twenty. Thirty."

The lamp threw a yellow circle maybe eight feet wide. Beyond it the tunnel mouth was a perfect absence.

"Forty. Fifty."

The cord slowed. Stopped. Started again, slower.

"Sixty. Seventy." Petra's hands had begun to shake. Eighty meters was the sump. Eighty meters was the dip, the low place, the place where the spring did not forgive. "Seventy-five. Eighty."

The cord went slack.

Not taut-slack, not snagged-slack. *Loose*. Drifting. The horrible loose of a line whose far end is attached to nothing, or to someone who has stopped pulling.

"Nine minutes," Tomas whispered. He was standing now, ankle forgotten, both fists in his collar.

Petra counted seconds in their head, the way you count between lightning and thunder, except there was no thunder coming, there was only the water and the loose cord and the arithmetic of lungs. Ten minutes. Ten and a half. At eleven minutes Petra stood up, and later they would not remember deciding to, and Tomas grabbed their arm and Petra shook him off with a strength they didn't know they had—

—and the cord snapped taut. Twice. Three times. The agreed signal: *pull*.

They hauled together, Petra and Tomas, hand over hand, falling backward across the platform, and the water broke, and Sefa came up coughing in the lamplight with her eyes wide and white-rimmed and her hands locked on the cord like it was the last true thing in the world.

Petra had her by the shoulders, then under the arms, dragging her onto the stone, and she was breathing — ragged, whooping, *breathing* — and Petra discovered they were shouting something, words without shape, and that their hands were pressing her hair back from her face over and over as if checking she were real.

"The sump," she gasped. "There's a — collapse at the sump, a gap, I had to — squeeze through, lost the cord end, had to find it blind—" She coughed, laughed, coughed again. "There's air. Past the sump. Three hundred meters of dry bore, Petra, it's *dry*, the seal held, the old devils built it to hold—"

"You lost the cord," Petra said. Their voice came out wrecked. "You were eleven minutes under and you lost the cord and you *squeezed through a gap*—"

"I found it. Blind. By the knots." She was shivering violently now, the cold arriving all at once, and Petra wrapped her in the blanket and then in their own arms over the blanket, and she let them, and leaned in, wet hair against their jaw.

For a moment neither of them said anything. The lamp burned. Tomas, with the exquisite tact of the young, became intensely interested in repacking a rationsack that did not need repacking.

"When I couldn't feel the knots," Sefa said quietly, into Petra's shoulder, "I thought about your face. The one where you've decided something terrible. I thought — I can't leave him alone with that face. Nobody should have to live with that face unsupervised."

Petra laughed, or sobbed, or produced some sound that contained both and belonged entirely to this woman and this platform and this gray morning. "You came back for my *face*."

"I came back for you," she said. Plainly. The way she said everything that mattered. "The face is incidental."

Petra held her tighter. There were declarations available — big ones, true ones, ones they'd been not-saying since the weir, since the rain, since the first time she'd watched them work and Petra had felt *seen* like a struck match. But none of them were big enough, and Sefa was shaking with cold, and some things, Petra understood suddenly, are not said so much as survived together. This was one. They had pulled her out of the water and she had come back for them, and the ledger against Petra's chest seemed, for once, like the second-most-important thing aboard.

"Never again," Petra said into her hair. "No more dives. We go through together or not at all. I mean it. I'll rig a raft, I'll drain the sump myself with a spoon—"

"Together," she agreed. "Now give me the brandy Tomas is pretending we don't have."

"We don't have brandy," said Tomas, producing the brandy.

* * *

They went through at midmorning, one at a time along the re-rigged line, Sefa first to guide, then Tomas, then Petra, who dove the sump gap with Sefa's hand locked around their wrist the whole way — *together or not at all* — and surfaced gasping in the dry bore beyond, where the air tasted of stone and forty years and, faintly, impossibly, of pine.

Three hundred meters of tunnel, intact. Old lantern brackets. Chalk marks on the wall in a hand Petra knew better than their own: Ovid's survey ticks, marching toward the source like footprints waiting to be followed.

At the far end, daylight. Real daylight, falling through a ventilation shaft, and beneath it, carved into the lintel of the inner door, the two herons — and under them, in old Vellmarki script that Sefa read aloud with her voice breaking:

*The water remembers the way home.*

Beyond the door, the gorge climbed toward the source, and the light up there was wrong — too bright, too golden, moving.

Smoke, Petra realized. Campfire smoke. Someone was already at the spring.

"They came," Sefa whispered. "After forty years — they *came*."

And behind them, far down the flooded bore, faint but unmistakable, came the sound Petra had been dreading for six days: boots on stone, and voices, and the clipped cadence of a man giving orders in a tunnel he had no right to be in.

Brandt had found the third door too.
