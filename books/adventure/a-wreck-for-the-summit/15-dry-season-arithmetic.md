## 15. Dry Season Arithmetic

The river told us before either of us said it aloud.

I had grown up reading water the way other children learned letters — where it deepened, where it lied, where it went quiet in a way that meant trouble. And this morning, standing on the gravel bar where we'd hauled the boat the night before, I read a sentence I didn't want to finish.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* sat three fingers higher out of the water than she had two days ago. Her hull showed a pale stripe of drying wood below the waterline, like a man whose trousers had shrunk in the wash and was too proud to mention it.

"Sefa," Petra said, crouched at the bow with their hand flat on the stone, "how much draft does she need?"

"Sixteen inches loaded." I waded out to the channel marker — an old Vellmarki stake, one of the heron posts that still stood along this stretch like sentries nobody had relieved in forty years. The current curled around my ankles, thin and apologetic. Where the post's base should have been hidden, it stood exposed a full hand's width, bleached stone gleaming in the sun like a bone.

Petra did the arithmetic without paper, because of course they did. Their lips moved slightly. I had learned by now that when Petra Zhou's lips moved, someone downstream was about to have a bad day.

"Drop's been running two inches a day since the weir," they said. "Maybe two and a half once we're past the narrows and out of the shade. Sixteen inches of draft. That gives us—" They stopped. They looked at the river. They looked at me.

"Say it," I said.

"Four days. Maybe five if we carry her over the shallows, which will break our backs and possibly the keel."

"And the gorge?"

"Six days walking. Seven if we're careful. Eight if the rockfall at the Widow's Teeth has come down further since the last survey."

"So we are four days of boat and six days of gorge," I said, "and the river will not wait for us to finish our sums."

Petra stood up, wiped their hands on their thighs, and gave me the look I had come to think of as their honest face — the one that wasn't performing competence or hiding behind it, but just a tired person telling another tired person the truth.

"No," they said. "It won't."

* * *

We spent the morning unloading her.

There is a particular grief in taking a boat apart. Every knot untied is a small admission. I'd run the *Mercy* for eleven years — first as a ferrywoman under Vellmarki colors that officially didn't exist, then under no colors at all, which is what you call a nation that has been dissolved but refuses to inform its boats. She had carried grain, goats, contraband hymnals, one extremely indignant tax inspector, and now a brass ledger with a two-headed heron stamped on its lid, and I had never once asked her to do anything she couldn't.

Now we stripped her to the ribs and hid her under brush above the high-water line, wrapped in oiled canvas, with a prayer to whatever saint looks after abandoned vessels. My grandmother would have left an offering. I had nothing but half a wheel of hard cheese, so I left that, wedged under the canvas where the foxes couldn't argue with it.

"She'll keep," Petra said.

"She'd better."

"You could sell her when this is done. Buy something bigger."

I looked at them. They were hauling the tool roll onto their back — the good tools, the ones they touched the way other people touch letters from home — and they had said it lightly, the way they said everything that mattered, sideways, watching the middle distance instead of me.

"I'm not selling her," I said. "She's coming home with us. Somebody has to teach her the way back upriver."

Something moved across Petra's face — quick, quickly put away, like a coin slipped back into a pocket. *Us.* I had used the word deliberately. I had been using it deliberately for three days now, one syllable at a time, the way you feed a shy animal.

They picked up their pack and started walking toward the gorge mouth and did not answer, which from Petra was practically a sonnet.

* * *

The arithmetic got worse at noon, when we stopped in the shadow of the first gorge wall and took inventory properly, sitting cross-legged on a flat rock like merchants at a very grim market.

"Water," Petra said, laying out the skins. "Four full, two half. At four people's ration—" They stopped. Corrected themselves. "At two people's ration, four days. Five if we're disciplined."

"We are never disciplined."

"We were disciplined that time with the flash flood."

"That was terror, not discipline. Terror doesn't scale."

A sound escaped Petra then that I'm going to insist on calling a laugh, though a stricter witness might have called it a cough with ambitions. It was progress. Three weeks ago, Petra Zhou would have simply written *terror doesn't scale* down in some internal ledger as a fact about the universe and moved on.

"Tools," they continued, tapping items as they named them. "Pry bar. Two chisels. The winch handle off the second door — don't ask why I kept it, I don't know. Powder for one more door, if there is one more door. Rope, sixty feet. The ledger." They rested their hand flat on the satchel, and I watched their thumb find the embossed heron through the leather without looking, the way you find a light switch in your own house.

"And food?" I asked.

"Nine days, if we eat like engineers. Twelve if we eat like exiles."

"Eat like exiles, then. We've had practice."

The gorge walls rose on either side of us, red and gold in the afternoon light, close enough in places that I could have thrown a stone from one side to the other. Beautiful country. Cruel country. The kind of place that gives you exactly what you need and charges you double for carrying it.

Behind us, somewhere downriver, Elias Brandt was doing his own arithmetic. I knew it the way you know weather coming — not from evidence, just from the shape of the air. He had let us go at the second door. He had burned his own report. But letting us go and giving up are different animals, and Brandt struck me as a man who kept both on a short leash.

"He'll be a day behind us," Petra said quietly. I hadn't realized I'd spoken aloud. Or perhaps they'd simply been thinking the same thing; we did that more and more, finishing each other's inventories. "Maybe less. His men are fresh. Ours are—" They glanced at their own legs. "Ours are us."

"Then we walk tonight," I said. "Cool hours. Rest at midday when the rock bakes. We make the gorge give us six days' distance in five."

Petra looked at me for a long moment. "You've done this before."

"My grandmother crossed these gorges twice with the elders' records sewn into her coat hem." I shrugged, though it wasn't a shrug kind of story, and we both knew it. "You learn to love the night hours. Nobody can see what you're carrying, including yourself."

* * *

He caught up with us at dusk.

I heard him before I saw him — a scrabbling on the scree above the trail, entirely too much noise for anyone who meant harm, followed by a yelp, a slide, and a young man in a ruined Crown uniform fetching up against a boulder with all the grace of a dropped sack of turnips.

"Tamsin Brakke," Petra said, without turning around. "Crown survey clerk. Third grade."

"Second grade," the young man said breathlessly, from the ground. "I passed the examination in spring."

"Congratulations," Petra said dryly. "You've fallen down a gorge for it."

I turned and looked at him properly. He was maybe nineteen, all elbows and earnestness, one bootlace trailing, a survey satchel banging against his hip. The same clerk we'd pulled from the flood at the narrows — the one who owed us his life and, apparently, had decided to repay the debt in person, at the least convenient possible moment.

"You shouldn't be here," I said.

"No," he agreed readily. "I absolutely shouldn't. Surveyor Brandt ordered me to stay with the main patrol. So I waited until they made camp and then I left." He said it with the bright, uncomplicated pride of a man announcing he has committed his first crime and found it agreeable.

"Why?" Petra asked. Just that. One word, laid down like a rail spike.

Tamsin scrambled upright, brushed scree off his uniform — a hopeless project, like mopping a waterfall — and looked at each of us in turn. I watched him gather himself, watched the words line up behind his eyes.

"Because I ran the aquifer figures again," he said. "The ones from the source valley. Off your margin notes, ma'am — I mean, off the notes in the ledger, which I definitely did not see, except that I did, during the flood, when you were both busy not drowning." He swallowed. "And because I checked them against Surveyor Brandt's original field book. And because somebody falsified the numbers, and it wasn't Brandt, and if the summit blasting goes ahead the way the Crown plans it, the water table under Kell's Ferry—" His voice cracked, just slightly, on the town's name. He'd heard us say it. He'd remembered. "Somebody has to tell the elders. And Brandt can't, because he's Brandt. And you two are going anyway. So."

Silence, except for the river muttering in its sleep far below.

"You understand," Petra said slowly, "that what you're describing is desertion. From a Crown survey crew. In the field."

"Yes, sir."

"They hang people for less."

"Do they?" Tamsin considered this. "Only if they catch them, I'd think."

And there it was again — that cough-with-ambitions from Petra, worse this time, nearly a real laugh. I felt something loosen in my chest that had been tight since the boat came out of the water.

"We have four days of water for two people," I told him. "That's two days for three. And six days of walking."

Tamsin's face fell, then firmed. He reached into his satchel and produced, with the flourish of a magician revealing the trick, four more water skins, full, sloshing gently.

"I filled them at the patrol camp," he said. "I also took the salt pork nobody was guarding, a spare fuse kit, and Surveyor Brandt's copy of the gorge survey, which he will notice missing, which is rather the point, because it means he'll know where we've gone and roughly how fast, and—" he faltered here, the first doubt crossing his face, "—he won't shoot at us. I think. He let you go once."

"He did," Petra said.

"So he'll let you go again. And if he doesn't—" Tamsin lifted his chin, which trembled slightly, ruining the effect entirely and improving it at the same time. "Then he'll have to go through me, and I'm very annoying."

I laughed out loud then, full and unguarded, the first true laugh since the river began dying under us. Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the whole thing. You spend forty years being a nation nobody can see, and then one evening in a gorge a boy in a stolen uniform stands on a rock and says *I'm very annoying* like an oath of citizenship, and suddenly you are not scattered anymore. Suddenly there are three of you, and three is a congregation, and a congregation is the seed of everything my grandmother had sewn into her coat hem.

"Rationing starts now," Petra said, but their voice had gone soft around the edges. "Two liters a day each. We walk nights. Sefa leads — she knows the gorge. Tamsin, you're on the survey book; I want distances and elevations logged every hour, because if we live through this, those numbers are going to matter more than anything the Crown ever paid for."

"Yes, sir." Tamsin was already digging out his notebook, already writing, already ours.

"And Tamsin."

"Sir?"

"Stop calling me sir. I'm not your superior. I'm barely employed."

"What should I call you?"

Petra opened their mouth. Closed it. Looked at me, of all people, as if I might hold the answer — as if after twenty-two chapters of their life they still needed someone else's permission to be called by their own name.

"Engineer," I said. "Call them Engineer. They've earned it twice over, even if the Crown tore up the certificate."

Petra stared at me. The dusk was deepening, the gorge walls going purple, the first stars pricking through above the rim. And I watched something settle in their face — not a smile exactly, but the ground a smile builds on.

"Engineer," Tamsin repeated, reverently, writing it down, and I swear the boy underlined it.

* * *

We walked into the dark.

The gorge at night is a cathedral with the roof taken off — black walls a thousand feet high, stars in a narrow bright river overhead, our three shadows thrown huge and wavering by the single lantern we allowed ourselves. Tamsin counted paces in a whisper. Petra's boots found the old Vellmarki roadbed under the scree, stones laid by hands forty years dead, still holding their line, still level, refusing to admit the nation that built them had ever been dissolved.

Four days of water. Six days of walking. Behind us, a day back or less, a man who had burned his own report. Ahead of us, the Widow's Teeth, the third door, and the source.

But that night, for the first time since the chest came out of the riverbed, the arithmetic balanced. Not comfortably — nothing in this story balances comfortably — but it balanced: three walkers, enough water if we were disciplined, and a road under our feet that somebody had built to last.

Near midnight, Tamsin stumbled, and I caught his arm, and Petra stopped and waited without being asked, lantern raised, until we were all three moving again together.

Neither of them noticed what I noticed: that Petra had stopped. That waiting for other people was becoming a habit.

I filed it away, the way I file everything worth keeping, and led us deeper into the gorge — toward the sound, faint and growing, of water falling somewhere in the dark ahead, where no map said any water should be.
