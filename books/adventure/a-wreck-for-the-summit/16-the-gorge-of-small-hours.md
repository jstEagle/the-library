## 16. The Gorge of Small Hours

The moon set at midnight, and after that the gorge belonged to us and to whatever lived in it.

We walked in single file because there was no other way to walk. The clerk—Tomas, though he still flinched when I used his name, as if I might be laying a trap with it—took the middle position on the theory that the middle was safest. Sefa took the front because she always took the front, and I took the rear because someone had to count rivets, so to speak: the ledger in its oilcloth against my chest, the map of closed lines rolled tight in its tin, and forty pounds of tools that had seemed essential at the last waystation and now seemed like a personal failing.

"Tell me again," Tomas said, for perhaps the ninth time since dusk, "why we didn't take the boat."

"Because the boat would float approximately four inches above the riverbed," Sefa said, without turning around, "and require approximately six."

"And why we're walking instead of waiting for the rains?"

"Because the rains are eleven weeks away and the elders' claim dies in nine days." She stepped over a slab of fallen limestone. "Also because Brandt's patrol is behind us, and Brandt's patrol has horses, and horses cannot do this."

She said *this* with a certain satisfaction, gesturing at the gorge entire—the walls rising sheer and black on either side, the path a rumor of gravel along the river's edge, the water itself running low and quiet and treacherous, the way a polite person can be treacherous.

I will say this for the dry season: it made the going honest. In high water you'd have been wading to your knees, guessing at the footing. Now every stone showed its face, and every stone had a face like a liar's.

We made good time for three hours. Good time by gorge standards, which is to say we covered ground that a healthy goat would have covered in half the time while looking bored about it. Tomas complained on a schedule—every twenty minutes, precisely, like a clock with grievances—and I found I'd begun to look forward to his complaints the way you look forward to a church bell. They meant time was passing and nothing had gone wrong yet.

It was somewhere past the third hour that I noticed the sound.

Or rather, noticed the absence of one. The gorge had been talking all night—the river muttering, loose scree ticking down the walls when our boots disturbed them, wind worrying through the narrows ahead with a sound like a man whistling through his teeth. And then, between one step and the next, the wind stopped.

Sefa stopped too. Of course she did. She'd grown up reading rivers the way I read ledgers, and she'd heard what I heard, or failed to hear.

"The narrows," she said quietly.

"I know."

"There's a bend ahead where the wall comes down close to the water. Overhanging. The old survey called it—" she paused, hunting the word, and I supplied it from Ovid's margin notes, from memory, in the old tongue:

*The Throat.*

"You've been studying," she said, and I could hear the smile in it even in the dark.

"Somebody left excellent marginalia lying around."

Tomas, catching none of this, whispered: "Why did we stop? Is it a rest? I would accept a rest."

"It's not a rest," Sefa said. "Keep your voice down and your feet quick. We go through the Throat fast and quiet, no talking, no stumbling. If anything starts to move up there, you don't look up. You run for the far side. Understood?"

"What starts to move up where—"

"*Understood?*"

He understood. We went.

The Throat was exactly what its name promised: a place where the gorge narrowed until the walls leaned together overhead, leaving a slot of sky scattered with stars, and the path squeezed down to a ledge barely wide enough for two abreast if the two were friends, which we were not quite, being instead something more complicated that neither of us had found words for. The rock overhead was layered limestone, pale even in the dark, tilted at an angle no mason would forgive. I put my hand flat against it as I passed, the way you'd check a fevered brow.

Cold. Stable. Old.

Old, I told myself, does not mean patient forever.

We were most of the way through—I could see the ledge widening ahead, the walls stepping back, the river spreading out into a broad silver ribbon—when Tomas sneezed.

I want to be clear about this. It was not his fault. It was dust, gorge dust, the fine white flour of ten thousand years of limestone wearing down, and any human nose would have objected. But a sneeze in the Throat of a mountain gorge at three in the morning carries like a rifle shot, and the echo hadn't finished bouncing off the far wall before something answered it from above.

Not a sound, exactly. A *shift*. A deep, mineral groan, the sound of a very large thing deciding, after a very long time, to change its mind about staying put.

"Run," Sefa said, and then the mountain said something louder.

I don't remember deciding to push Tomas. I remember my hands doing it—flat against his back, hard, sending him stumbling out onto the wide ledge ahead—and I remember grabbing Sefa's wrist because she had turned toward me instead of away, because of course she had, because she was the kind of person who turned toward trouble the way other people turned toward their names being called.

And then the world came down.

* * *

Here is what a rockslide is, from underneath: it is not an event. It is a weather. Stone falls as rain falls, everywhere at once, and the air goes white with dust so thick you breathe it more than air, and the noise is not a noise but a condition, the way deafness is a condition, total and without edges.

I got Sefa behind a boulder the size of a cart—there was one, providentially, at the inner edge of the ledge, and my hands knew it was there before my eyes did, the way hands know things in the dark—and we crouched inside its shadow while the Throat emptied itself above us. Rocks the size of fists. Rocks the size of sheep. One the size of a hay wagon went past close enough that I felt its wind, and struck the path where we'd been standing, and kept going into the river with a boom I felt in my teeth.

Then, gradually, the weather ended. Stones tickled down. Dust sifted. Somewhere in the white murk, the river resumed its muttering, sounding, I thought, slightly embarrassed.

"Sefa." My voice came out sanded flat. "Sefa. Talk to me."

"I'm here." Coughing. "I'm here. Tomas! TOMAS!"

Silence. Then, faintly, from beyond the slide, thin as a thread: "*Here!* I'm here! I'm—" a coughing fit—"I'm on the other side! The path's gone! There's just—there's just rock, all the way down to the water!"

The dust began to settle, and the moonless dark reassembled itself around us, and I saw what he meant. Where the Throat had been there was now a talus slope—a great fan of broken stone, fresh-faced and sharp, pouring from the shattered overhang down into the river. It wasn't impassable. Nothing is impassable; that's a word civilians use. But it was hours of climbing, maybe more, hauling ourselves up and across loose rock that would shift under every step, in the dark, with the overhang still groaning occasionally overhead like an old man turning in bed.

And on our side of it—behind us—the way back lay open. Clear. Downstream. Toward Kell's Ferry, toward the boat, toward safety, toward every sensible thing.

I stood in the dark with the ledger against my chest and did arithmetic.

Four days of water, we'd calculated. Six days of walking. That math had assumed the gorge path. With the Throat blocked, the detour upstream—climbing out of the gorge, cutting over the shoulder of the mountain, dropping back in past the slide—was two days, maybe three, in country with no water we knew of. Which meant arriving at the source with nothing, or not arriving, or arriving dead. The elders' claim died in nine days regardless. The season did not negotiate.

But here was the thing my hands understood and my heart was slow to admit: I could go back. Not upstream. *Back.* Downstream, alone, tonight, in the dark, and by dawn be ten miles gone, and by noon be a rumor, and by week's end be nobody, which was the thing I had spent seven years being and had gotten very good at. The ledger was worth money to the right buyer. The map of closed lines was worth more. I had every chance. The mountain had handed me my invisibility back like a coat at a party's end, and all I had to do was put it on and walk away from the two people shouting my name—

"—Petra!" Sefa's hand found my arm in the dark, gripped hard. "Petra, you've gone somewhere. Come back."

"I'm here," I said. And heard myself say it, and understood that it was true in a way it had never quite been before.

Because here was the other half of the arithmetic, the part I'd been trying not to do: Tomas was alone on the far side with a quarter of our food, no tools, and a Crown warrant with his name adjacent to ours. Sefa was standing next to me with scraped palms and a bruise blooming on her jaw where a pebble the size of an apple had caught her, and she was not saying *go back*, she was already uncoiling rope from her shoulder, already looking up the talus slope with her head tipped the way she looked at water, reading it.

They were not my family. I had never planned for a family. Families were things that happened to people who stayed, and I had built a whole life on the principle of not staying, on doors left oiled and bedrolls kept packed, on being the man who was useful right up until the moment he was gone. Ovid had taught me the trade and then died and left me a debt I hadn't agreed to. Sefa had stolen a ledger to protect me and I'd yelled at her for it. Tomas had defected from the Crown with nothing but a clerk's arithmetic and a sneeze.

None of it was mine. None of it had ever been mine.

And I stood there in the settling dust, holding the ledger that proved a drowned kingdom had once paid my mentor in obligations, and I thought: *that's the trick, isn't it. Debts don't ask permission. You inherit them the way you inherit a face.*

"Give me the rope," I said.

Sefa paused. In the dark I couldn't see her face, but I felt the whole shape of her attention turn toward me like a lamp.

"You're sure?" she said. Not *are you capable*. Not *is it wise*. *Are you sure*—which is a different question entirely, and the only one that mattered.

"No," I said. "Anchor's that boulder. Two wraps, tension hitch. I'll lead; you feed slack. And when we get to Tomas, somebody is going to have a serious conversation with him about covering his mouth in gorges."

"That conversation," Sefa said, pressing the rope into my hands, "is going to include the words 'thank you,' or I'll want to know why."

* * *

Climbing fresh talus is like climbing a staircase that hates you personally. Every hold is honest until you commit your weight to it, and then it files for divorce. I went up on all fours mostly, testing each block, listening to the slope talk back—the small ticking conversations of stone finding new arrangements—and behind me Sefa fed the line with perfect evenness, never a jerk, never a sag, so that the rope felt less like a tether than a hand on my shoulder.

Halfway up, a slab the size of a door let go under my left knee.

I don't remember the fall. I remember the rope going taut—the beautiful, brutal arithmetic of it, my weight transferred through forty feet of hemp to a boulder and a woman who did not let go—and I remember swinging, pendulum-slow, my boots skating down the fan of stone, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that has stayed with me since: *she has me. She actually has me. When did I become someone who could be held?*

I found a new line. I climbed. At the crest of the slide, where the broken stone met the surviving ledge, Tomas materialized out of the dark with both arms out and hauled me over the lip like a fish, and then held on longer than the rescue strictly required, shaking slightly.

"You came back," he said. "You—you were on the other side, and you climbed *into* it—"

"Yes, well." I sat down hard on a rock that had recently been part of a mountain and was taking the demotion well. "Someone has to teach you about sneezing."

Sefa came up the rope last, fast, effortless, the way she did everything that involved rivers or heights or risk, and stood over us both with her hands on her hips and dust sifting off her shoulders like flour off a baker.

"Well," she said.

"Well," I said.

"You went back."

"I went back."

She nodded once, sharply, the way she acknowledged a river gauge reading, and turned to Tomas, and the moment folded itself away unspoken—but not unfelt. Some things you don't say because they're too big for the hour. You bank them. You carry them forward with interest.

* * *

Dawn found us on the far side of the Throat, walking again, slower now, all three of us moving like people who had been introduced to mortality overnight and were still negotiating terms. The gorge opened ahead into country I recognized from the map—not Ovid's margins this time, but the original Vellmarki survey, the ink older than I was: the valley widening, the walls stepping down, and far off, where the morning haze thinned, a line of peaks with snow still on them though the season was dry everywhere else.

The source. Or the roof of it, anyway.

Tomas limped beside me, one ankle wrapped in Sefa's spare shirt, and asked, in the small voice of a man who has decided the answer matters more than his pride: "How much farther?"

I unrolled the map of closed lines across a flat stone, weighted its corners, and put my finger on the blue pencil mark Ovid had drawn thirty years ago and I had traced over a hundred times since without knowing why.

"One day," I said. "If the path holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

I looked at the map. Every line on it closed, crossed out, erased—except the ones we'd opened, and the one we were walking now, drawn in by a dead man who'd known the debt he was handing me.

"Then we make it hold," I said. "That's the whole trade, Tomas. That's all engineering is."

Behind us, faint but unmistakable, carried up the gorge on the morning air: a sound I had hoped not to hear for another day. Hooves. Many of them, and the thin metallic note of tack, and beneath it—when the wind turned—the unmistakable cadence of a man giving orders in a voice trained to be polite about it.

Brandt had found the slide. And a rockslide, I thought grimly, rolling the map and shouldering my pack, slows a horseman for an hour. Maybe two.

Sefa was already walking.
