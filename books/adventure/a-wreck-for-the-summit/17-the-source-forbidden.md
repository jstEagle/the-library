## 17. The Source, Forbidden

The gorge ended the way a held breath ends.

One moment we were climbing through the last of the narrows—four days of hauling the ledger and Petra's tools on our backs, drinking water by the thimbleful, the clerk (Tomas; he'd finally told us his name on day two, as if it were a confession) counting our remaining water like a man counting down to his own execution—and the next, the rock simply opened, and there it was.

I had heard my grandmother describe the source. I had heard her mother's letters read aloud at every Vellmarki gathering of my childhood, in a rented hall above a fishmonger's in a city that had never wanted us. *A cathedral of limestone,* they always said, *with a spring for an altar.* I had assumed, the way you assume about anything holy, that the descriptions had grown in the telling. Forty years of exile will inflate a memory like dough left too long near a warm stove.

It had not grown. If anything, the telling had been modest.

The valley opened above us like a cupped hand. The walls rose sheer and pale on three sides, limestone gone gold in the morning light, and the spring came out of the mountain's heart in a column so clear it was nearly invisible—a rope of water standing in the air before it broke into mist and fell into a pool that glowed the particular green that only deep, cold, patient water achieves. Stalactites hung from the overhangs like the pipes of some enormous drowned organ. The air tasted of stone and cold and, faintly, of iron—the taste of water that has traveled a long way underground to reach you.

And the markers.

I stopped walking. Tomas walked into me and apologized. Petra, ahead of us with the survey pack cutting grooves into their shoulders, stopped too, and turned, and I watched them see what I saw.

Brass posts. Set into the living rock at intervals around the pool, each one stamped with the two-headed heron, each one still bright enough to catch the light because brass does not forget what it is even when nations do. Forty years of rain and snow and no one to polish them, and still they stood at their stations like soldiers who had never received the order to disperse.

"They're intact," Petra said. Their voice did something strange on the second word, cracked down the middle like ice under a boot.

"Of course they're intact," I said, and my voice did something stranger, because I was crying, which I had not planned to do and would deny later. "Nobody knew to come tear them out."

* * *

We came down into the valley properly, and that was when we saw the smoke.

Not wildfire smoke—cooking smoke, thin and deliberate, rising from behind a screen of birch at the valley's eastern edge. Petra's hand went to my arm. Tomas made a small sound like a kettle deciding whether to whistle.

"That'll be them," I said.

"You don't know that," Petra said.

"Sefa." It was Tomas, very quietly, pointing at the nearest brass post. Someone had polished it recently. The heron gleamed. There was a strip of blue cloth tied around its base—faded, weathered, replaced often enough that the newest knot was still bright. "Somebody's been here a long time."

I untied my boots and waded into the shallows of the pool, because there are protocols, even for a girl who grew up two hundred miles from home, and one of them is that you greet the water before you greet the people. The cold went through me like a bell being struck. I said the greeting my grandmother taught me, the one she said her own mother had said at this exact spring, and my voice shook on every word and I said them anyway.

When I turned around, there were people at the treeline.

Eleven of them. Old, most of them, though not all—a woman perhaps sixty with a blacksmith's forearms, a man leaning on a staff who might have been eighty or might have been fifty-five and worn down by waiting, two younger ones whose faces had the careful blankness of people who have learned not to hope in public. They wore ordinary clothes. Mended, patched, ordinary. But the woman at their center wore a shawl worked in the old pattern—the herons facing away from each other, the way grief faces—and when she saw the ledger satchel across my chest she put her hand over her mouth.

"You're Idrizi," she said. Not a question. "Yllka Idrizi's granddaughter. You have her chin."

"My grandmother," I managed.

"She wrote to us. Every year, until her hands failed. She wrote—" The woman stopped, gathered herself. "She wrote that someone from our line would bring it back. We thought she meant her son. Her son died in the mines. Then we thought she meant no one, and that the letter was just an old woman's kindness." She looked at the satchel again. "What is your name?"

"Sefa."

"Sefa." She said it the way you set down something heavy you've been carrying a long way. "I am Elder Marta. Come and sit. You look like a river that has forgotten how to be still."

Behind me, I heard Petra make a sound that was almost a laugh, strangled at birth.

* * *

They fed us first. This is also protocol, and I could have wept again at the rightness of it: nettle soup with barley, flatbread baked on a stone that had clearly been baked on for years, tea from pine needles that tasted like the idea of forests. The elders' camp was not large—a dozen tents and shelters tucked against the birch screen, a smokehouse, a rack of drying herbs—but it was arranged with the geometry of permanence. Paths swept. A well-tended fire circle. A school bench, small, child-sized, sitting empty.

"How long?" Petra asked. They asked it the way they asked all their questions, sideways, eyes on the fire rather than the person.

Elder Marta understood anyway. "Thirty-one years. My husband and I came first, when the Crown's surveyors declared the valley worthless. Worthless!" She laughed, and it was not entirely a kind laugh. "No timber worth taking. No ore worth digging. Too far from anywhere. So they wrote it off, and we moved into the write-off, and we have kept the markers polished and waited." She looked around at her people. "Dorin was born here. He has never seen Vellmark. None of us have seen Vellmark, because Vellmark is—" she gestured at the valley, the water, the brass posts, "—here. It was always here. It was only ever waiting for paper."

"The ledger," I said, and my hand was already on the satchel.

"In a moment." Elder Marta held up one finger, and I swear to you it was exactly my grandmother's gesture, the same angle of the finger, as if the two of them had rehearsed it across forty years and two hundred miles. "First. Tell me about the engineer."

Every head turned to Petra.

Petra, to their credit, did not try to become furniture, which is their usual strategy. They sat up straighter instead. "Petra Zhou. Railroad engineer. Formerly." A pause. "Currently between employers."

"Ovid Zhou's student."

Something crossed Petra's face like weather. "You know that name."

"He surveyed our source line when I was a girl," said the blacksmith woman, whose name was Brene. "He ate at my mother's table. He argued politics with my father until both of them were hoarse and happy. He was paid in nothing, you know. He refused coin. Said he'd take payment in—" she smiled at some old memory, "—in 'the pleasure of being right.' Arrogant man. Wonderful man."

"He never mentioned any of you," Petra said quietly. "Not once. Twenty years working together. He never said a word."

"No," Elder Marta said gently. "He wouldn't have. After the dissolution, association with us was dangerous—for him and for us. So he carried it alone. That was his way. Carrying things alone." She looked at Petra with an expression I couldn't read. "You carry things alone too, I think."

"I carry tools," Petra said. "Tools are lighter."

Brene laughed out loud. Dorin, the young man born in exile, leaned forward and said, with the directness of the young, "Is it true you opened the second door? At the Kell's Ferry line? Nobody's opened those doors in forty years."

"It wasn't locked," Petra said. "It was sealed. From inside. Which is worse, engineering-wise, and better, sentiment-wise."

"What does that mean?"

"It means whoever closed them wanted them found again someday, but only by someone who'd earned it." Petra shrugged, and I saw Dorin decide, on the spot, that this grumpy, mud-caked, sleep-deprived stranger was the most interesting person alive. I recognized the expression. I was wearing a version of it myself, and had been for some weeks now, and had been pretending otherwise.

Then I opened the satchel, and took out the ledger, and the camp went silent in a way I had never heard a room go silent—not even the night my grandmother died, which had been quiet but full of breathing. This was silence with no breathing in it at all.

Elder Marta took it in both hands. She turned to the first page, where the two-headed heron was struck in gilt, and she pressed her thumb below it, on the blank space where the archivist's seal of receipt should have been, and she said words in old Vellmarki that I only half knew—words for *returned*, and *witnessed*, and *owed*—and then she began, very quietly, to cry, and eleven other people cried with her, and Tomas blew his nose with tremendous dignity, and Petra stared very hard at the fire with their jaw tight, doing their level best to be somewhere else, and failing, because there was nowhere else to be.

* * *

An hour. We had one hour.

I want to record what that hour contained, because later—after everything—it seemed impossible that so much could fit inside sixty minutes, the way it seems impossible that a whole valley fits inside a gorge until you walk through it.

In that hour: Elder Marta read aloud from the ledger's final pages, the source surveys, Ovid's margin notes in his cramped, slanted hand, and the elders bent over them like people warming themselves at a fire. In that hour: Dorin touched the brass heron on the cover and said, "This is real. I thought—I thought it was just stories." In that hour: Brene fed us more bread, on the theory that grief and joy are both improved by bread. In that hour: Petra, unable to help themselves, got into a technical argument with the eldest elder about the original tunnel gradients, and lost, and looked delighted about losing in a way I had never seen on them, and I thought, *oh no, oh no, this is what they look like when they're happy, and I have known them for weeks and I am only now seeing it.*

In that hour, I sat beside Petra on the school bench by the empty fire circle, and I said, "You could stay."

"Don't start," Petra said.

"I'm not starting anything. I'm observing. There's a difference. Observation is a surveyor's trade."

"Sefa."

"You keep your bedroll packed by the door," I said. "Even here. Even after the gorge. I've seen you do it every morning since Kell's Ferry. You pack it like you're going to need to leave in the dark without saying goodbye."

Petra was quiet for a while. The spring filled the silence, that low continuous sound, water speaking its one long sentence.

"Everyone leaves," Petra said finally. "Or everyone gets left. In my experience the second one happens more, so I've taken up the first one. It's more dignified."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard, and I once heard a man argue that a ferry could be steered by shouting at it."

That got the almost-laugh. Almost. "Sefa. Look where we are. Look at this place. People waited thirty-one years for a piece of paper, and I'm the one carrying it, and the minute it changes hands I'm—what? Extra? The courier? Couriers leave. That's the whole job."

"You're not a courier."

"Aren't I?"

"You're the only person alive who can read half these margins," I said. "You're the only person who knows what the aquifer figures mean. And—" and here I had to stop, because the next part was true and terrifying and I had promised myself I'd say it before Brandt arrived, whenever that was, because a man like Brandt is always arriving eventually. "And you're mine. Or I'm yours. Whichever direction the debt runs. And I don't intend to find out by waking up to an empty bedroll."

Petra looked at me. Really looked, the way they looked at a structure before trusting it with weight.

Before either of us could say anything else—which was probably fortunate, because whatever Petra was assembling behind their eyes looked load-bearing—Tomas came around the birch screen at a dead run.

He didn't say anything at first. He just stood there, hands on knees, breathing like a landed fish, and pointed back the way we'd come, down the gorge.

"Boats," he gasped. "Crown boats. Blue pennants. Six men and—" he swallowed, "—and a warrant. I saw the seal. It's a recovery writ. Signed by the Surveyor-General."

The camp moved the way water moves—fast, silent, finding every channel at once. Brene banked the fire. The elders folded the ledger pages back along their original creases with hands that shook only slightly. Elder Marta did not run. She stood holding the ledger against her chest and looked at me.

"One exit," she said. "This valley has one exit, and it's the one they just sailed up. We chose it for the water. We forgot to choose it for the war."

"We could hide it," Dorin said. "Bury the ledger—"

"And let them dig it up with a shovel and a smile?" Brene snapped. "A writ of recovery means they can turn over every stone legally. No. It stays in sight. Things hidden look guilty. Things held openly look owned."

"By whom?" Dorin demanded. "That's the entire question!"

"Not the entire question," said a new voice, dry as old paper.

Elder Yovan, the man with the staff, had come to stand beside us, and he was looking not at the gorge but at Petra, with an expression of terrible, gentle amusement.

"The entire question," he said, "is what the engineer intends to do. Because I have read that young man's margin notes for an hour, and Ovid Zhou did not send his student here to bury a book. He sent them here to build something. Am I wrong?"

Petra opened their mouth. Closed it. And I watched them do the thing they do—turn inward, consult the maps, weigh the loads, calculate the gradient—and then look up at the pale gold walls of the valley, at the spring standing in the air like a rope thrown down from the mountain, at the one narrow throat of the gorge through which six Crown men were even now climbing with a warrant and a shovel and a smile.

"I need an hour," Petra said. "And everyone's blasting powder."

Down in the gorge, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a boat horn. One long note. The note, in any navy in the world, that means *we have arrived, and we are not leaving.*
