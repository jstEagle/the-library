## 18. Two-Headed Heron

The source chamber had a way of making voices small.

Petra felt it the moment they stepped inside: a cathedral of limestone, ribbed and pale as the inside of a shell, water sliding down its walls in silver threads that caught the lantern light and scattered it into a hundred trembling coins. The spring rose from a basin at the far end, deep and green-black and perfectly still, the kind of stillness that made you want to whisper even when there was nothing to disturb.

The old Vellmarki survey markers stood around the basin like patient sentries—brass studs set into the rock, each stamped with the two-headed heron, each polished by forty years of exile hands that had come here in secret to touch them. Sefa's fingers were on one now. She hadn't said anything since they'd arrived. She didn't need to.

The elders' camp occupied the chamber's wide eastern shelf: six low tents, a cookfire vented through a crack in the ceiling that drew the smoke away like it had been engineered for exactly that purpose—which, knowing Vellmarki engineers, it probably had. There were eleven elders. The oldest was a woman named Maren Idrizi, Sefa's great-aunt, who walked with two sticks and an expression that suggested she had been waiting for this argument since before Petra was born.

And then, an hour after the ledger came home, Brandt came too.

He arrived at the mouth of the chamber with four Crown troopers, his warrant case under one arm, his survey satchel under the other, and the particular set of his shoulders that Petra had learned to read across three hundred miles of river: the shoulders of a man carrying something heavier than either.

"Petra Zhou," he said. His voice echoed off the limestone and came back sounding like three men agreeing with him. "Sefa Idrizi. Elders of Vellmark." He looked around the chamber, and Petra watched him do what he always did—count. Rivets, men, exits, seconds. "By authority of the Crown Survey Commission, I am placing you all under detention pending—"

"Aunt Maren," Sefa said mildly, not turning around, "there's a man at the door reading from a paper."

"I can hear him," said Maren Idrizi. "Forty years, and they still knock the same way."

* * *

It should have been a disaster. By every reasonable measure—the warrant, the troopers, the eleven elders who had spent a generation learning how to disappear—it was the moment everything ended.

Instead, Maren Idrizi said, "Sit down, all of you. It's nearly supper, and nobody negotiates well hungry," and the strangest thing happened: everyone sat down.

Brandt hesitated at the threshold for a long moment. Petra watched him weigh it—duty on one side, soup on the other—and thought, not unkindly, *you poor man, you never stood a chance.* He sat. One of his troopers sat. The other three remained standing by the entrance in a posture that suggested they'd been ordered to look menacing but had been given no guidance on how to sustain it past the first course.

The soup was barley and river fish and something green Petra couldn't identify, served in tin bowls that had been mended so many times the mending was most of the bowl. It was the best thing Petra had eaten in a year, and they ate it in the hostile silence of people who all knew exactly why they were in this room and none of whom were willing to say it first.

Maren let them finish. Then she folded her hands on the table—a plank across two crates, scarred with old survey marks—and said:

"Now. The Crown man will tell us we are trespassing on Crown land. I will tell him the land has never stopped being ours; only the maps changed. He will tell me maps are law. I will tell him law is a story the strong tell until someone stronger tells another. And then we will both be very dignified while nothing happens." She turned her head toward Brandt. "Am I close?"

"You've done this before," Brandt said.

"Twice a decade, since your government dissolved mine." Maren's voice was dry as August gravel. "You'll forgive me if I skip to the part where we're stuck."

Brandt opened his warrant case. "The Commission's position is simple. The ledger is Crown property, recovered from Crown waters. The claims it contains are void. The aquifer survey—" he paused, and Petra saw the flinch, small and fast, the flinch of a man about to lie in front of witnesses he respected, "—is unsubstantiated."

"The aquifer survey is *mine*," Petra said quietly.

Everyone looked at them. Petra hadn't meant to speak. But the word *unsubstantiated* went through them like a spike through a rail joint, and their mouth moved before their pride could stop it.

"I ran the figures myself. Off Ovid's margins and my own soundings. The summit line's blasting pattern puts its deepest charge within eleven meters of the aquifer crown. When it fires, the ceiling goes, and the water goes with it—all of it, down the fracture lines, into the gorge. This valley drowns. The town downstream drowns. And the railway—" Petra met Brandt's eyes. "The railway loses its foundations inside a year, because the springs that feed the cut are the same springs. You don't get to call that unsubstantiated. You get to call it inconvenient."

The silence afterward had texture. Sefa's hand found Petra's under the table—not gripping, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on something to confirm it's real.

"That," said Maren Idrizi, "was more interesting than my speech. Continue."

* * *

What followed was three hours of the hardest talking Petra had ever done, and most of it wasn't theirs.

The elders wanted what they had always wanted: the ledger recognized, the claim honored, Vellmark restored—or if not restored, then at least *admitted*, written back onto the world. Old Tomas, the blacksmith's brother who'd made the crossing with them, kept saying the word *nation* the way other men said *home*, and every time he said it, the chamber seemed to grow a little smaller around the possibility of losing it again.

Brandt wanted—Petra watched him struggle to say it, and finally he did say it, standing up, warrant case forgotten on the bench—"I want my arithmetic to be true." That was all. "I surveyed that summit line. I certified the figures I was given. If those figures are false, then everything I've done for nine years is a load-bearing wall over empty space, and I would rather blow it out myself than wait for it to come down on someone else." He looked at Maren. "My orders say recover the ledger and deny the risk. My orders are wrong. I know they're wrong. I don't know what's right, and I'm asking—" and here his voice did something Petra had never heard it do, which was waver, "—I'm asking if there's a version of this where I don't have to choose between my oath and my conscience. Because I've been doing that math since Kell's Ferry, and it doesn't balance."

"And me," Sefa said, when the quiet got long enough. "I want the town to stand. I want the children—who have never seen the source, who sang the anthem off-key in a schoolhouse that isn't on any map—I want them to have somewhere to be *from*. Not a nation. A place. I'd trade the whole argument for that."

Petra sat with it. All of it. The ledger open on the table between the soup bowls, Ovid's margins marching down the pages in that cramped, certain hand, the map of closed lines spread beside it, and beneath all of it, humming like a live rail, the thing Petra had been carrying since the third tunnel—the thing they'd known and feared and finally, here, in front of everyone, had to say out loud.

"There's a third path," Petra said. "It's impossible. I want to be clear about that up front. It's expensive, dangerous, and it requires everyone in this room to trust at least one person they have excellent reasons not to trust."

Maren raised an eyebrow. "Those are usually the only plans worth having. Go on."

Petra stood and unrolled the map of closed lines across the table, weighting the corners with tin bowls, and put their finger on the source chamber itself.

"The old Vellmark line doesn't end here. It ends *below* here. Ovid's last survey shows a service gallery running under the spring basin—forty meters of tunnel, sealed from the inside when the line closed, which means it's intact, because nobody seals a tunnel from the inside unless they mean to reopen it." Their finger traced down, through the second door, the flooded third, to a junction marked in Ovid's hand with a symbol Petra had spent weeks decoding: a circle with a channel cut through it. "This is a diversion works. He designed it. The gallery runs to a spillway above the gorge—if we open it, controlled, we can give the aquifer a second throat. Water goes where we send it instead of where the blasting sends it. The valley keeps its springs. The town downstream keeps its river. And the railway—" they looked at Brandt, "—the railway gets its foundations, because the springs keep feeding the cut at a regulated flow instead of draining out through a blast fracture. Everyone lives. That's the plan. That's the whole plan. Build the thing that lets everyone live."

"Nationhood," said Tomas flatly. "You're proposing plumbing."

"I'm proposing proof," Petra said. "Here's the part nobody wants to hear. The Crown will never honor the ledger because it's a claim. Claims can be buried—your whole history is proof of that. But the Crown cannot bury a working piece of infrastructure that saves its own railway. Once the diversion runs, once their own engineers certify that the summit line stands *because* of the Vellmark gallery, the ledger stops being a document and becomes a set of drawings that demonstrably work. You can't erase a thing that's holding up your railroad." They looked around the table. "I'm not offering you a nation. I'm offering you indispensability. It's less than you deserve. It may be more than you'll ever otherwise get."

Nobody spoke. The spring filled the silence, that low continuous voice it had, older than any of the arguments.

Then Maren Idrizi began, very quietly, to laugh.

"Oh, you *are* Ovid Zhou's," she said. "He sat in that exact spot—" she pointed at Petra with one stick, "—thirty-eight years ago, and told the council of Vellmark that the mountain didn't care whose flag flew over it, and that anyone who planned around flags instead of water deserved what the water did to them. We thought he was being cynical." Her eyes were bright and wet at once. "He was being *kind*. He was trying to build us something no army could take away, and we were too busy wanting our name back to notice." She looked at the ledger. "We owe him a debt. Apparently his apprentice has come to collect it."

"It's not—" Petra started.

"It absolutely is," Sefa said, squeezing their hand. "Let her have it."

Brandt had been staring at the junction mark on the map for a full minute. Petra knew that stare. It was the stare of a man re-running numbers he'd already run, hoping they'd come out different, and watching them come out the same—except this time the same was survivable.

"The diversion works," he said slowly. "If the gallery's intact. If the seals hold. If the charge placement can be timed to the aquifer pressure without—" He stopped. Looked up. "This is a demolition. A controlled demolition, under a living spring, with the valley's entire water supply on the other side of the rock."

"Yes," Petra said.

"And you're the one directing it."

"I'm the one directing it."

Brandt exhaled through his nose, the longest exhale Petra had ever witnessed from a man of his economy. Then he picked up his warrant case, opened it, took out the warrant, and did not tear it up—that would have been too easy, and Brandt did not do easy things—he simply set it face-down on the bench and sat on it.

"Technically," he announced, to the room, to the troopers, to the limestone, "you are all detained. For your protection. Pending resolution of an engineering emergency. Nobody leaves the chamber. Nobody touches the charges except under Engineer Zhou's direction. Idrizi—" he pointed at Sefa, "—you're on fuse lines; you're the steadiest hands on this river and I'd rather have you inside my perimeter than outside it. Elders—" he faltered slightly, confronted with eleven people old enough to be his grandmother, "—please stay behind the survey line I'm going to draw. Troopers, you're laborers now. Congratulations on the promotion."

One of the troopers, the young one who'd been glaring heroically at the soup earlier, said, "Sir, does this make us traitors?"

"It makes you a crew," Brandt said. "Traitor is a question for lawyers. Crew is a question for engineers, and the engineer says we work tonight."

* * *

They worked until the lanterns burned low.

Petra stood at the basin's edge with the map of closed lines and Ovid's ledger open side by side, and for the first time since pulling that chest from the riverbed, the two documents agreed with each other completely, page against page, margin against margin, like two halves of a conversation resumed mid-sentence after forty years. *He knew,* Petra thought, tracing the junction mark. *He knew what the Crown would build. He knew what the water would do. He left the answer underground and waited for someone to come find it.* The chest in the riverbed hadn't been lost. It had been *posted*—addressed to whoever the river chose.

Sefa appeared at their elbow with a tin cup of tea, pressed it into their hands, and stayed there, shoulder against shoulder, looking at the spring.

"My aunt thinks you're him again," she said.

"Your aunt needs new spectacles."

"My aunt sees fine." Sefa turned, and in the lantern light her face was doing several things at once, most of them dangerous. "Tomorrow you're going to light a fuse under a mountain, and if the seals hold, my town lives and your railway lives and maybe—*maybe*—my people get their names back. And if they don't hold, we all learn to swim very fast." She smiled. "I've decided I'm not worried. Do you know why?"

"Because you grew up in these rivers?"

"Because you count rivets when you're scared, and you've counted every rivet in this chamber twice, and you only do that when you intend for the thing to still be standing tomorrow." She touched the brass heron stud on the wall, gently, the way you touch a sleeping person. "Goodnight, Engineer Zhou."

She went to her bedroll. Petra stood a while longer at the water's edge, tea cooling in their hands, listening to the spring practice its one note.

Then, from the mouth of the chamber, a shout—one of Brandt's troopers, young and breathless, scrambling down the shelf with his cap in his fist.

"Engineer! Sir! There's a boat coming up the gorge—Crown colors, commission pennant—and sir, they're flying the *inspector general's* flag, and Brandt says—" the boy swallowed, "—Brandt says somebody's come to check the arithmetic."
