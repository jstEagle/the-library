## 19. Blast in B Minor

Elias Brandt had been a surveyor long enough to know that the most dangerous moment in any operation was the moment just after you committed to it. Before that, you could still count rivets, check figures, redraw the line. After it, there was only the thing you had done, and the arithmetic of living with it.

He stood on the shelf of rock above the source chamber with his commission warrant folded in his breast pocket and felt the exact weight of that arithmetic. It was roughly the weight of a folded warrant. Which was to say: nothing at all, and everything.

The inspector general's boat had come up the gorge an hour after dawn, all brass and bunting, carrying Deputy Inspector General Corven Hale and two clerks and a trooper escort of eight. Hale had stepped onto the stone shelf in polished boots that had never once been wet, looked at the spring, the old Vellmarki markers, the elders' camp, the two fugitives, and said, with the mildness of a man reading a menu, "Brandt. You were ordered to recover a ledger. Instead I find a picnic."

"It's a very informative picnic, sir," Brandt had said.

Now Hale stood at the chamber's mouth with his arms crossed, watching Petra Zhou pace out the fuse lines on the far shelf like a conductor tuning an orchestra nobody else could hear yet.

"Explain to me again," Hale said, "why I should not arrest every person on this rock."

"Because in forty minutes," Brandt said, "either this works, and the Crown's railway keeps its foundations, or it doesn't, and the valley drowns, and the railway drowns, and the inquiry that follows will want to know why the deputy inspector general was standing close enough to be blamed. Sir."

Hale looked at him. Brandt had served under men who could end his career with a sentence, and he had learned to read them the way he read contours on a map—by what was *not* drawn. What was not drawn on Hale's face just now was conviction. What was drawn was arithmetic. Brandt had spent three chapters of his own conscience learning the difference, and he recognized the arithmetic the way you recognize a family resemblance.

"You've read the aquifer figures," Hale said. It was not a question.

"I surveyed this valley, sir. Eight years ago. My figures were corrected before they reached the ministry." He let the word *corrected* sit in the cold air between them. "The engineer's figures were not."

* * *

The strange thing—the thing Brandt would find himself turning over for years afterward, the way you turn a stone that shouldn't have been where you found it—was how quickly it had all been arranged once it had been agreed to.

Petra Zhou had laid it out the night before with chalk on the wet limestone, and Brandt, who had come up the gorge intending to arrest them, had found himself holding the chalk lamp instead. That was the trick of Zhou, he was beginning to understand. They never asked you to betray anything. They asked you to hold a light.

"Two charges here and here," Zhou had said, tapping the chalk marks. "Not to break the tunnel open—that's the mistake the Crown's blasting crews would make. We're not demolishing. We're *persuading*. The old Vellmarki engineers cut a relief channel into the east face when they built this line. It's in the ledger, page sixty. They sealed it because they never needed it. We need it. We put the charges where the rock is already weakest, along the seam, and the aquifer does the rest of the work itself. The water wants out. We're just giving it a better door."

"And if your figures are wrong?" Brandt had asked.

Zhou had looked at him with the flat honesty of a person who had already asked themselves that question every night for a week. "Then I'll be standing closest to the door when it opens the wrong way. That's the trade. It's always been the trade. That's why they pay engineers in blame instead of money."

Sefa Idrizi, coiling fuse at his elbow, had snorted. "They don't pay you at all. I've seen your contract."

"It's a *symbolic* contract."

"It's a napkin."

It had been at that point—watching the two of them bicker over fuse lengths like an old married couple who had met a month ago—that Brandt had quietly taken his copy of his own report out of his satchel and put it in the cook fire, and had not mentioned it to anyone, and had felt lighter than he had in eight years.

* * *

Now the morning of it. The elders had withdrawn to the high shelf with the children, which had taken some doing, because the eldest of them—a woman named Maren Idrizi with Sefa's same stubborn jaw—had informed Brandt that her people had been moved off this land once already and would not be moved by a man with a warrant and clean boots. Brandt had shown her the figures instead of the warrant. She had read them for a long time. Then she had gathered the children without another word, which Brandt understood to be the highest compliment a Vellmarki could pay a piece of paper.

Petra Zhou stood at the center of it all with a speaking trumpet they had produced from nowhere and a calm that Brandt recognized as manufactured, because he manufactured the same one himself every morning. Under it, Zhou's hands were not quite steady. Under it, nobody's were.

"Charge one, wired," came Sefa's voice from the east shelf, thin with distance. "Charge two, wired. Detonator to me. Petra, if you say *check your work* one more time I am going to check your work into the river."

"I said it once."

"You said it twice. I count rivets too."

Brandt walked the line one last time, because it was his duty, and because if the rock came down he intended to be able to say at the inquiry that he had walked the line. The charges sat snug in the seam like seeds in a furrow. The fuse ran true. The relief channel's old seal—a wall of fitted stone laid by hands forty years dead—waited behind it, patient as everything else in this valley.

He found Zhou at the firing point, and beside them, to his mild surprise, the young clerk Toma, the one who had defected at the gorge with a satchel of Crown stationery and the expression of a man who had jumped and was still deciding whether to enjoy the fall. Toma had appointed himself timekeeper and was holding a pocket watch with both hands as though it might bolt.

"Engineer," Brandt said.

"Surveyor." Zhou didn't look up from the detonator box. "You should be with your inspector."

"My inspector is currently asking the elders if the spring has always made that sound. I believe he is stalling. I believe I taught him that." Brandt paused. "Zhou. Whatever happens in the next ten minutes—"

"Don't." Zhou looked up then, and there was something in their face Brandt hadn't seen before—not the armor, not the competence, but the person underneath both, tired and hopeful and braced. "Don't make a speech. I've read your reports, Brandt. You're a terrible speechmaker. You once described a landslide as *a material redistribution event with casualties*."

"Two casualties," Brandt said. "I remember the number."

"So do I," said Zhou, more quietly. "That's the whole job, isn't it. Remembering the number."

* * *

"Two minutes," said Toma, in a voice that cracked on both words.

Sefa crouched at the detonator with her hand on the handle, and Brandt watched her look at Petra across forty yards of wet rock, and watched Petra nod, and understood that the nod contained an entire conversation he was not party to and probably never would be. There was a whole language there. He had spent his life in the company of people who spoke in reports. It was good, he thought, to witness a language with actual speakers.

"Fire in the hole!" Sefa called, and it was the old Vellmarki formula—he knew it from the archive files, *fire in the hole, and the heron keep the hole*—and then she brought the handle down.

Brandt had heard a hundred blasts. Quarry shots, demolition charges, the Crown's summit works tearing at the mountainside with all the subtlety of a debt collector. He had thought himself prepared for the sound.

He was not prepared for the *note* of it.

Later, Sefa would insist it was in B minor, and Toma would write it down in the margin of the official record, and no one would ever correct it. It was a low, doubled tone—the first charge and the second arriving half a heartbeat apart, the rock answering in the same key—and then a silence so complete that Brandt could hear the spring behind them change its mind.

The seam had opened exactly where Zhou had drawn it. Not a collapse. A *door*. And through it, the aquifer came—not in the killing flood Brandt's nightmares had been rehearsing, but in a long, silver, rising breath, pouring through the relief channel the old Vellmarki engineers had cut and sealed and left like a letter to the future, filling the channel, finding its grade, running.

Running *clean*.

And doubled. That was the part that undid him. The original spring kept its note, and now a second voice joined it from the east face, and the two streams met in the old stone basin and braided together, and the sound they made together was—Brandt searched his vocabulary, the whole careful bureaucratic vocabulary of a lifetime, and found nothing—was *right*. Like a chord resolving. Like a debt paid.

"Figures," Zhou said. Their voice was shaking. Brandt pretended not to notice, because that was a courtesy surveyors extended one another. "Flow rate's—Toma, write it down—flow rate's holding at projected. It's holding. It's *holding*—"

"It's holding," Sefa confirmed, from the east shelf, and her voice was shaking too, and then she laughed, one short, disbelieving note, and sat down on the rock like her strings had been cut.

* * *

Deputy Inspector General Corven Hale stood at the water's edge for a long time with his polished boots an inch from the flood line, and Brandt watched him do the arithmetic. He could see it moving behind the man's eyes: the falsified surveys, the summit railway's foundations sitting on ground that would have liquefied within two seasons, the inquiry, the names, the *his* name, in a report he could no longer control because the evidence had just come out of a mountain in front of forty witnesses and was currently running clean and doubled and extremely public.

"You understand," Hale said at last, without turning, "that this changes nothing legally. The ledger is still Crown property. The claim is still void. These people are still—" he gestured at the elders on the high shelf, at the children, at the whole stubborn resurrected nation of them, "—still what they are."

"Yes, sir," Brandt said.

"And you are still under my commission."

"Yes, sir."

"And I am still obliged to detain the fugitives Zhou and Idrizi and recover the ledger."

"Yes, sir." Brandt paused. "Although I'd note, sir, that the valley is unstable following a demolition event, that the gorge road is compromised, and that the safe custody of the engineer and the ferrywoman—and the ledger—would be best ensured by detaining them *here*, under supervision, until such time as terms can be formally discussed with all parties. For their protection."

Hale turned and looked at him for a long, long moment. Somewhere behind them the spring ran its new doubled note, and one of the Vellmarki children on the high shelf started, badly and beautifully, to sing the heron anthem, and was shushed by her grandmother, and started again.

"Your protection," Hale said. "You're detaining them for *their* protection."

"For everyone's protection, sir. It's a very unstable valley."

Hale looked at the water. He looked at the railway's distant grade, visible down the gorge, saved now by the very claim his ministry had spent forty years burying. He looked at Brandt, and Brandt watched the arithmetic finish, and watched the man arrive at the only sum that kept his boots dry.

"Draft the detention order," Hale said. "All parties. Cite the valley instability. And Brandt—" he straightened his cap, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like an inspector general and more like a tired man who had once, long ago, wanted to be an engineer, "—when you draft the terms. Make them generous. I find I've developed a sudden professional interest in *stability*."

* * *

That evening Brandt sat on the shelf with his warrant in his pocket, unsigned, and watched Petra Zhou and Sefa Idrizi walk the new channel by lamplight, arguing about flow rates, holding hands when they thought no one was looking, which was to say: constantly, and badly, and in full view of the entire valley.

Toma sat down beside him with two tin cups of the elders' terrible tea and handed him one.

"Sir," the clerk said. "The detention order. Do I cite the engineer by rank or by name?"

Brandt considered. Forty years of records called this valley nothing. Forty years of records called these people gone. He was holding the pen that would write the next line, and for once in his careful life, he wanted the line to be true.

"By name," he said. "Both of them. And Toma—leave a space under the charges."

"A space, sir?"

"For the terms." Brandt sipped the terrible tea and looked up at the high shelf, where the elders had lit their lanterns, and at the chamber mouth, where the deputy inspector general was waiting, and at the spring, which ran on in its new chord like it had never once been silent. "Something tells me," he said, "we're going to need the room."
