## 20. Terms of Water

The spring cathedral had never been built for negotiations.

It was built, as far as anyone could tell, for nothing at all—water rising through limestone in slow columns, light falling through a crack in the vaulted rock like something poured rather than shone. The Vellmarki elders had arranged their camp at its mouth with the practical grace of people who'd waited forty years: a table of split planks, a lantern hung from a survey marker so old its brass had gone green, and a kettle that had been boiled and reboiled until it had opinions.

Petra stood at one end of the plank table with ink on their fingers and the ledger open between them like a patient on an operating cloth.

Across from them sat Elder Maren Idrizi—Sefa's aunt, though "aunt" covered roughly half the exile community upstream—and beside her, three other elders whose names Petra had learned over two days of being fed until they could barely stand. On the Crown side sat Elias Brandt, out of uniform for the first time Petra had ever seen him, wearing a plain grey coat that somehow made him look more like a surveyor than his commission ever had. Behind Brandt, two clerks from the summit railway office who kept looking at the spring like it might invoice them.

And behind everyone, on the rocks, sat half the town of Kell's Ferry, because there was no force on earth that would have kept them away, and everyone present knew it.

"Read it aloud," Elder Maren said. "All of it. We have waited a generation to hear our valley named in a Crown document. We will not have it mumbled."

Petra cleared their throat. The document was four pages long, drafted overnight by lamplight, argued over until dawn, and rewritten twice when Sefa pointed out that the first version gave the Crown the water rights and the second gave the exiles the tunnel and nobody the railway, which Brandt said flatly would mean war, and Sefa said flatly that they'd survived worse than a railway, and Petra had spent an hour in the middle of the night drawing a diagram until both of them stopped talking and started listening.

"The Crown of—" Petra began, and stopped. "Whose name do we use? The old King is dead. The Regency signs things now."

"The Regency," Brandt said, "will sign whatever keeps the summit line out of a flooded cutting. Use whatever title makes them feel grand."

"*The Crown,* then," Petra said, "grants to the inhabitants of the valley of the source—the valley heretofore unrecorded, unsurveyed, and unadmitted to exist—a charter of settlement, in perpetuity, with rights to the waters of the source, the works of the old Vellmark line within the valley bounds, and the governance thereof by council of the charter families."

A sound went up from the rocks—not a cheer, not yet. Something quieter and older than cheering. Elder Maren closed her eyes. One of the other elders, a blacksmith with forearms like bridge piers, took off his hat, which Petra suspected he did perhaps twice a decade.

"In exchange," Petra continued, and here their voice went carefully level, "the charter families undertake the aquifer diversion works, maintaining the channel by which the source waters are carried clear of the summit railway's foundations, in perpetuity, the Crown supplying materials and the charter families the skill."

"And the third clause," Elder Maren prompted, because she had read the draft four times and trusted no one else to summarize it honestly.

Petra hesitated. This was the clause that had cost the most ink.

"The parties agree," Petra read, "that the surveys of record for the summit railway, as filed in the years prior, shall be... superseded."

Brandt made a small motion with one finger. *Say it properly.*

"That is to say," Petra amended, "the falsified figures shall be replaced by true ones, and no party shall pursue the matter of how false figures came to be filed, or by whose hand."

Silence, except for the spring, which had been making its opinion known all morning in a language of water on stone.

Elder Maren opened her eyes. "No nation," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a stone she had been turning over for two days and had finally set down. "No flag. No restoration. A charter. A valley on a map, and our children born under someone else's crown, but *born here.*"

"Yes," Petra said. Because lying to this woman had become impossible somewhere around the second bowl of stew.

Maren looked around at the other elders. The blacksmith put his hat back on, which seemed to be the formal mechanism of assent. An old woman who had spent the entire negotiation shelling beans into a bowl without missing a rhythm said, "My mother walked out of Vellmark carrying me and a spoon. I always thought we'd go back." She looked up at the crack of light in the cathedral roof. "Turns out we were going forward the whole time. Just slowly." She resumed shelling.

"It carries," Maren said, "if the water clause holds. If the diversion fails—if the railway floods anyway, or if the Crown decides in ten years that a charter is cheaper to revoke than to honor—then everything signed today is paper."

"Charters can be revoked," Brandt agreed, in the tone of a man who had checked. "But a working aqueduct that saves the Crown's most expensive engineering project cannot be revoked without the Crown admitting it prefers its railway drowned. Bureaucracies fear many things. They fear above all being seen to punish their own rescuers in public." He paused. "I know. I was one for twenty years."

"You're resigning," said the bean-shelling elder, not looking up.

"I am." Brandt set a folded letter on the table, already written, already sealed. "Effective upon signature. I'll stay through the diversion works as a civilian engineer—Petra will need someone to hold the other end of the tape, and my handwriting is better." He glanced at Petra. "Also, if I return to the capital unemployed, certain persons will ask me questions I've agreed not to answer. It's safer for everyone if I'm visibly busy here."

"He's staying because he likes us," Sefa called from the rocks, where she had been sitting with the young clerk, Tam, going over fuse-line calculations on a slate. "He'll deny it. Note it in the minutes."

"Note that the ferrywoman is a menace," Brandt said, without heat.

* * *

The signing itself was almost comically small for what it was.

There was no pen of state. There was the kettle-adjacent pencil stub, rejected; there was Tam's good pen, produced from inside his coat where he had been protecting it through flash floods and rockslides like a relic; and there was an ink bottle that Elder Maren produced from a cedar box along with a folded Vellmarki flag so old its two-headed herons had faded to ghosts.

"Ovid's," Maren said quietly, when Petra went still at the box. "He left it with us the last time he came down the river. He said he was holding too much of ours already." She slid the ink across the table. "You're holding some of it now. That's how debts work in families. They don't get paid. They get carried."

Petra dipped the pen. Their hand, which had not shaken through a rockslide or a standoff, shook now, over a signature.

They signed first—as engineer of record for the diversion works, a title that would have been unthinkable six months ago and now fit like a coat they'd owned all along. Then the elders, each in turn, the blacksmith pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages. Then Brandt, who wrote his name in the witness line in a hand so precise it looked typeset, and then, beneath it, without ceremony, drew a single line through his commission number and wrote *resigned* after it.

"There," he said, capping the pen. "Twenty years undone in four seconds. I expected it to hurt more." He considered. "Or less. I genuinely don't know which I expected."

"What will you tell them in the capital?" Tam asked. He was still, formally, a Crown clerk, though he had been functionally defected since the gorge and everyone had stopped pretending otherwise around the second day of hauling gear.

Brandt looked at the spring. "The truth," he said, "in the smallest possible quantity. The diversion works were designed by a local engineer of great ability. The original surveys contained errors. Errors were corrected. The railway is saved. Any further questions can be directed to the flooded cutting they'll have if they push the matter." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Bureaucrats respect a man who has resigned. He can't be promoted, demoted, or transferred. He becomes weather. You plan around him."

"And the ledger?" Elder Maren asked.

That was the last item, and everyone knew it. The brass survey ledger sat at the center of the table, its two-headed heron stamp worn nearly smooth, Ovid's margin notes crowding every edge in a hand Petra had known longer than any face.

"It stays here," Petra said. They had decided this at dawn, alone, and had told no one, partly from resolve and partly from cowardice about the argument it might start. "In the schoolhouse, when there's a schoolhouse. Open. Anyone who wants to read it can read it—including the parts in Ovid's hand, including the debt page. A claim does you no good locked in a chest. And a debt—" their voice caught, just slightly, and they pushed through it— "a debt isn't paid by burying the account book. It's paid by leaving it where the next person can see what was owed, and decide what they owe."

Maren studied them for a long moment. "Your mentor hid this ledger in a river," she said, "to keep it from the Crown. And you're putting it on a shelf."

"He hid it because he thought the story was finished," Petra said. "It wasn't. It was just between chapters." They tapped the margin notes. "Besides, he wrote in the margins expecting readers. Ovid never did anything expecting no one would look. He was the least secretive secretive man I ever met."

Sefa came down from the rocks then, slate under her arm, and stood at Petra's shoulder—not touching, but near enough that Petra could feel her there, the way you feel a load-bearing wall.

"One amendment," she said. "Before we close." She looked at the elders. "The charter says the valley belongs to the charter families. Fine. But the river doesn't stop at the valley. And neither do we. I want it minuted that the waystations stay open—Kell's Ferry, the weir camp, all of them. Not Vellmark restored. Just... doors that aren't closed."

Elder Maren smiled for the first time in two days, and it transformed her face into Sefa's face, thirty years on. "Add it," she said. "In your hand. Family amends family's documents. That's tradition."

So Petra added the clause themselves, printing carefully in the margin, right beside where Ovid's notes ran thin and then stopped—the place where his account ended and theirs began.

* * *

They signed in the late afternoon, when the light through the roof-crack went gold and lay across the table like a benediction nobody had ordered. When the last signature dried, the blacksmith stood, and—without any announcement at all—began to hum.

It took Petra a moment to recognize it: the heron anthem, the one the smith had been humming brokenly at Kell's Ferry, the one the children sang off-key. Here, in the spring cathedral, with the charter signed and the ink still wet, the elders picked it up in fragments—an alto line from the bean-shelling woman, a bass rumble from Brandt, of all people, who claimed afterward he was only clearing his throat. From the rocks, the exiles of Kell's Ferry joined in, and the song rose into the limestone vault and came back down doubled, the way the spring would run tomorrow.

Nobody sang it well. Everybody sang it completely.

Petra stood at the edge of it, holding the pen, not singing, doing what they always did—which was standing slightly outside the warmth, checking the structure, confirming it would bear weight.

Sefa found them there. She didn't say anything for a while. The anthem rolled on overhead.

"You're doing the thing," she said finally. "Where you audit the happiness for defects."

"Somebody has to. Nobody else here reads load tables."

"Mm." She leaned against the rock beside them, shoulder just brushing theirs. "Find any cracks?"

Petra considered the scene: the elders, the faded flag, Brandt pretending not to sing, Tam conducting with a slate pencil, the ledger open on the table catching the gold light, Ovid's margins legible as speech. A valley that existed again. A debt, if not paid, then at last *acknowledged*, which was maybe what payment had meant all along.

"No," Petra admitted. "Structurally sound. Embarrassing, but sound."

"Good." Sefa took the pen out of their fingers and set it down. "Because tomorrow we start the diversion works, and I'm told the engineer of record is a tyrant with a tape measure."

"I'm told the fuse-line chief dives headfirst into flooded tunnels and gives her crew heart failure."

"Allegedly." She was quiet a moment. The anthem ended, and in the hush the spring spoke up again, louder than before—or maybe it only sounded louder, now that everything else had been said. "Petra. The bedroll. By the door. Are we done with that?"

Petra looked at the spring, at the charters and clauses, at the strange accidental family scattered across the cathedral floor. Somewhere downstream, a town that wasn't on maps was waiting to learn it had a name. Somewhere below their feet, an aquifer waited to be given a new road. And somewhere in their own chest, a door that had been sealed from the inside for twenty years was, apparently, open.

"Yeah," Petra said. "We're done with that."

Above them, at the mouth of the old line, Tam's voice rang out against the rock, high and delighted: "The gauge matches! Petra—the old track gauge—it *matches the new one!* Somebody planned this!"

Petra closed their eyes. Of course somebody had. Ovid had planned everything, right down to the margins.

"Tomorrow," they said, and reached for the tape measure.
