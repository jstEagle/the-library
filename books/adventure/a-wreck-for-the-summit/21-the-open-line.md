## 21. The Open Line

The first train came through on a Tuesday, which Sefa considered a small miracle of bureaucracy, since Tuesdays were traditionally the day the Crown remembered it owed people things and then forgot again by Wednesday.

She stood on the new platform at Kell's Ferry—*Kell's Ferry Charter Station*, the sign said, painted by the blacksmith's daughter in letters that leaned slightly eastward because she'd insisted the town was heading that way—and watched the engine come down the valley like something being let out of a long confinement. It moved slowly. The ballast was still settling, and Petra had refused to let anything faster than a walking pace over the aquifer cut until they'd personally checked every culvert twice, which Sefa knew meant three times, because she'd counted.

The whistle blew when it reached the platform, one long note that echoed off the gorge walls and came back changed, and the whole town cheered, and Sefa found herself cheering too, which embarrassed her, and then she decided not to be embarrassed, because forty years her people had whistled that same two-note call to each other across this valley and been answered by nothing but rock.

Today the rock answered back with steam.

Petra stood at the far end of the platform with their surveyor's chain coiled over one shoulder, watching the wheels the way other people watched sleeping children. The clerk—Tomas now, no longer *the young Crown clerk*, though he still stood like one, spine straight, notebook ready—stood beside them holding the timetable board upside down until Petra turned it over without looking at him.

"He'll do," Sefa heard an elder say behind her, meaning Tomas, meaning the apprenticeship, meaning the whole improbable arrangement whereby a Crown clerk had defected, been forgiven, and now learned rivets from a disgraced engineer who was disgraced no longer, or at least re-branded: *Charter Engineer, Valley Works*, stamped on paper Brandt himself had signed before resigning his commission with the same pen, which Sefa privately thought was the most dramatic thing a man who counted rivets had ever done.

The passengers got off. There were only eleven of them, mostly surveyors and one very confused merchant who'd expected the end of the line to be the end of civilization and instead found a schoolhouse, a forge, and a woman selling honey cakes who charged him double and called it charter rates.

Sefa found Petra at the edge of the platform, past the last lamp, where the old Vellmarki mile-marker stood re-erected—the two-headed heron worn nearly smooth, one head looking upstream toward the source, one head looking down at everything the water carried away and brought back.

"You didn't ride it," she said.

"It's my railway. I know how it ends." Petra said it dryly, but their hand was on the mile-marker, thumb tracing the heron's second head, the way they did when they thought no one was looking. They'd been doing it for weeks. Sefa had started keeping track.

"Everyone watched you today," she said. "The elders. The children. You stood in front of the whole town and read the charter terms aloud in Vellmarki."

"My accent is terrible."

"Your accent is a crime. The children love it. They've started copying it specifically to annoy me."

Something moved through Petra's face then—not quite a smile, more the place a smile kept trying to land and couldn't find permission. "They asked me yesterday what the anthem means. The third verse. I told them I didn't know."

"You don't?"

"I told them I'd ask someone who did." Petra finally looked at her. "I'm asking."

Sefa looked out at the tracks running down the valley, silver in the late light, following the route Ovid Zhou had surveyed for a kingdom that no longer existed, carrying trains for a Crown that had very nearly erased it, built on engineering that belonged to neither and both. The ledger sat in the schoolhouse now, under glass, open to the page with Ovid's final margin note. The children filed past it every morning. One of them had asked if the heron was dead, and the teacher had said no, it was molting, and Sefa had laughed until she cried in the middle of a arithmetic lesson.

"Come to dinner," she said. "I'll teach you the third verse. It's about rivers. Everything in our songs is about rivers; we're not subtle people."

Petra came to dinner. This was itself remarkable, and Sefa noted it the way she noted water levels: carefully, with an eye toward trend.

For six weeks Petra had eaten at the communal table, worked from dawn past dark, taught Tomas, argued with the elders about drainage grades, slept in the station house—and kept their bedroll packed by the door. Rolled tight, strapped, positioned for a departure measured in seconds. Sefa had seen it every time she came to go over figures. She'd never said a word about it. Some things you named too early and they died of the naming.

But tonight Petra arrived with clean hands and, notably, without the pack. Just the bedroll, unrolled—actually unrolled, spread flat under one arm like a thing being surrendered—and a bottle of plum brandy that Sefa suspected had been purchased from the honey-cake woman at a markup that would have made the merchant weep.

"What's this?" Sefa asked, taking the bedroll.

"Laundry," Petra said.

"It's a bedroll."

"It's laundry that happens to be a bedroll. It needs washing. I thought—" Petra stopped. Started again, which Sefa had learned was what honesty looked like on them: a person changing trains mid-sentence. "I thought it could stay here. While it's being washed. And after."

Sefa set it down on the chair by the window, next to her own bedroll, which had lived there unrolled for weeks because she'd stopped packing hers the day the spring ran doubled and clear.

"After," she repeated.

"After," Petra confirmed, and then busied themselves with the brandy cork as if it had personally wronged them.

They ate on the narrow porch because the evening was warm and the river was loud in the way it got in autumn, fuller now, fed by the diverted aquifer, running the color of good tea. Sefa taught Petra the third verse. It was, as promised, about rivers—a song about a river that agreed to carry a traveler's name to the sea so the traveler wouldn't have to keep walking. Petra learned it badly and hummed it anyway, off-key, and somewhere across the water someone in the town heard it and picked up the harmony, and then a second voice, and Petra went very still, listening, like a man hearing a language he'd been speaking alone his whole life suddenly answered in chorus.

"They're singing along," Petra said. "To my terrible humming."

"They're singing along to the song. Your humming is just the vehicle."

"That's generous."

"I'm a generous person. Ask anyone. Ask the merchant I overcharged."

Petra laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, and Sefa realized she could count on one hand the times she'd heard it, and that she intended to change the denominator substantially.

Later, when the brandy was half gone and the lamps across the river had gone out one by one, Petra grew quiet in the particular way they did before saying something true. Sefa had catalogued this too. She waited. She was good at waiting; you had to be, on a river.

"When I pulled the chest out of the riverbed," Petra said, "I meant to sell the ledger. I want that in the record. Before the schoolchildren get any ideas about me."

"Noted."

"And when you stole it back from my lodgings, I was furious."

"You threw a boot at my boat."

"I missed on purpose."

"You absolutely did not. You have the best throw I've ever seen. You hit a moving fuse-line at eighty paces during the demolition."

"I was furious," Petra repeated, "because you'd decided for me. That someone else wanted what I had, and wanted it for reasons bigger than mine, and I hadn't chosen it." They turned the cup in their hands. "That's been my whole life, Sefa. Things happening to me. Mentors dying and leaving debts. Kingdoms dissolving and leaving chests in rivers. Crowns building railways over graves. I got very good at being somewhere I could leave. The bedroll by the door wasn't caution. It was—I don't know. A door I could pretend was mine."

Sefa said nothing. The river said plenty.

"And then the gorge happened," Petra went on, "and I turned around. Back into the rockfall. And I remember thinking, standing there in the dust, that I had finally done something no one made me do. Nobody ordered me back into that slide. There was no debt in Ovid's margins about it. No warrant. I just—" Their voice did something small and rough. "I just went and got you."

"You did," Sefa said quietly. "I was there. Mostly under a rock at the time, but present."

"And I've been unpacking ever since. Slowly. One thing at a time." Petra looked at her then, straight on, the way they read gauges: honestly, because a misread number killed people. "I'm stating it plainly because you deserve plain. I'm not staying because the work is here, though the work is here. I'm not staying because of the charter, or the debt, or because Brandt would arrest me out of sheer habit if I left. I'm staying because of you. I chose the rockfall. I choose this. Every morning I'm going to choose it, and some mornings it will be hard and I will want the door, and I'm telling you now so that when that happens you can point at the bedroll on your chair and remind me whose choice it was."

The river ran. Somewhere up-valley a night bird made its argument and lost.

Sefa reached over and took the cup out of Petra's hands, set it down, and took the hands instead. They were engineer's hands, scarred and precise, and they shook slightly, which she pretended not to notice and Petra pretended not to know she noticed.

"For the record," she said, "I also meant to sell you out. Early on. To Brandt. For the amnesty. I drafted the letter in my head twice."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"You whistle when you lie. You've whistled less every week. I've been keeping count."

Sefa stared at them, and then she laughed, hard enough that a light came on across the river and went back off, disappointed. "You insufferable gauge-reading—"

"I'm also staying because of you," Petra said, over her laughter, plainly, the way they laid rail: level, true, built to hold weight. "In case the record needed both entries."

It did not need both entries. But Sefa gave it both anyway, later, in the way of people who had spent a season learning each other's languages—hers spoken in rivers and patience, theirs in grades and tolerances—and who had finally, on a platform they had literally built with their own hands, found the dialect they shared.

The bedroll stayed on the chair. Unrolled. Unpacked. Home, the way a line is home once the spikes are set: not because nothing will ever shake it, but because someone checks every culvert three times, and stays.

Morning came gray and soft, rain threatening and not delivering. Sefa woke to the sound she'd already learned to love better than the anthem: Petra humming the third verse, off-key, downstairs, putting water on for coffee and pretending not to rehearse.

She was still smiling about it when the knock came at the door—heavy, official, three beats.

Tomas stood on the step, soaked to the knees, notebook clutched to his chest, and his face wore an expression Sefa recognized from a season ago: the look of a man carrying news he wished belonged to someone else.

"There's a telegram from the capital," he said. "It's addressed to the Charter Engineer." He swallowed. "It's about the summit tunnel. The Crown's original bore, north of the pass. Petra's going to want to see the figures."

"Why?" Sefa asked, though her stomach had already begun to drop, the way rivers do.

Tomas held out the telegram. "Because according to this, the old bore isn't sealed. It never was. Somebody's been using it—and the traffic runs *toward* the source."
