## 22. A Wreck for the Summit

The chest sat in the corner of the schoolhouse like an old dog that had earned its place by the fire.

Petra noticed it first thing every morning now, because the schoolhouse was on the way to the survey shed, and the survey shed was where Petra spent most of their waking hours, and the waking hours had a way of starting whether you wanted them to or not. The lid stood open. The brass fittings had gone soft with handling—children's hands, mostly, dozens of small palms learning what a two-headed heron felt like under their fingers. The seal itself had faded from gold to something like old honey, and nobody seemed to mind. Sefa said that was the point of things: they were supposed to be touched.

Inside, on a bed of river-worn cloth, lay the ledger. It was propped open these days to no particular page—the elders had argued about which page mattered most and settled, in the way of committees everywhere, on rotating it weekly. This week it showed the source line as Ovid Zhou had drawn it forty years ago, his margin notes marching down the edge in that cramped, certain hand Petra would know anywhere, even now, even after everything. *Aquifer bearing strata begins here. Do not blast above this mark.* The Crown's engineers had copied those notes into their own books without saying where they'd come from. Petra let them. Credit was a currency Petra had never learned to spend well anyway.

Through the schoolhouse window came the sound of eleven children attempting the heron anthem.

"Attempting" was generous. The anthem had a range. The children treated the range as a suggestion. Somewhere around the second verse—*the heron flies on two heads' breath, one for the water, one for the land*—a boy named Tam hit a note that had previously existed only in the dreams of cats, and the teacher, Mistress Anka, who had survived worse than this if the stories were true, simply raised her voice and carried on. She always carried on. That was the whole of her method and it worked better than most methods Petra had seen employed by people with actual training.

Petra stood at the window longer than the walk to the shed required.

It was strange, the things that got you. Not the big moments. The big moments—the standoff at the source, the blast, the signing—you carried those like freight, heavy and expected. It was the small ones that ambushed you. A faded seal. Eleven off-key voices singing a nation back into existence one mangled verse at a time. The fact that there was a schoolhouse at all, in a town that six months ago had not been permitted to appear on maps, teaching children who six months ago had been taught not to say where they were from.

Tam's terrible note hung in the air. Another child laughed. Mistress Anka shushed them without breaking stride.

Petra went to work.

* * *

The summit line ran through the valley now instead of over its grave.

That sentence deserved unpacking, and Petra unpacked it every single day, because every single day they walked the grade and checked it. The original Crown alignment—the one Brandt's superiors had approved, the one built on falsified surveys—had called for the line to climb out of the valley on a high embankment across the aquifer's crown, blasting through limestone that Ovid's margins had marked in letters practically shouting. That line would have drowned Kell's Ferry within a decade. Maybe sooner. The aquifer didn't keep calendars.

The new alignment dropped into the valley floor, crossed the spring run on a low stone viaduct—Petra had designed the arches themselves, and had enjoyed designing them more than any work in twenty years—and climbed out the far side through the reopened tunnel works of the old Vellmark line. The tunnel Brandt had formally detained them outside of while the charges spoke. The tunnel that had flooded to the ceiling once upon a time and now ran dry and clean, its drainage galleries doing exactly what Petra had promised the negotiating table they would do, which was the only promise Petra had ever made to a government and kept.

The crews called the viaduct the Heron Bridge. Nobody had officially named it anything. Names just happened to things here, the way weather happened. Petra had stopped correcting people about it around the third week.

This morning's task was simple: final grade check on the last kilometer before the summit portal, the stretch where the new line met the old. The clerk—no, not the clerk anymore; Toma had been apprenticed to Petra since spring, and apprentices did not get called clerks, Toma had made that rule himself—Toma ran the level while Petra walked the sightline, and the two of them argued about cut depths in the comfortable, repetitive way of people who had argued about cut depths a hundred times before and would argue about them a hundred times more.

"You're reading high," Petra said.

"I'm reading correct."

"You're reading optimistic. There's a difference and it's four centimeters."

Toma grinned and re-shot the sight. He was right, as it turned out. Four centimeters of optimism, exactly. Petra noted it in the book and pretended this proved nothing either way.

The boy had come a long way from the soaked, terrified clerk they'd hauled out of the flash flood, the one who owed them silence and then paid it forward into loyalty. He still wrote everything down—that hadn't changed, some habits were load-bearing—but what he wrote down had changed. Surveys instead of reports. Figures instead of accusations. Last month he'd asked Petra, very seriously, over dinner at Sefa's table, whether it was true that engineers could hear water underground. Petra had said yes. Toma had pressed his ear to the kitchen flagstones for a full minute, heard the cistern, and declared himself gifted. Sefa had laughed so hard she'd had to sit down. Petra had pretended not to smile and had failed completely, which Sefa pointed out, which led to further laughing, and eventually the dishes went undone, which was how you knew an evening had gone well.

The summit portal stood ahead, its face dressed in new stone cut to match the old. Above the arch, someone—Petra suspected the blacksmith, who remembered the heron anthem and had strong opinions about public art—had carved a small bird. Two heads. Facing opposite directions, the way the seal did. One for the water. One for the land.

Petra looked at it for a while.

Then they went back to arguing with Toma about centimeters, because the dead did not need monuments from Petra Zhou. They needed the drainage to work.

* * *

Evenings belonged to the house.

It was still strange to say. *The house.* Two rooms and a lean-to off the charter road, built by the community in the Vellmarki fashion—stone below, timber above, the roof pitched steep against mountain snow—with a door wide enough for a person carrying tools, because Sefa had specified that dimension herself and the builders had nodded like it was the most natural specification in the world. Which, Petra was learning, it was, here. Doors sized for the lives that used them. What a concept.

Sefa was on the porch when Petra came up the path, mending a net she no longer strictly needed, because the ferry ran on schedule now and nets were for fish and she claimed she fished for the quiet of it. Petra knew better. She fished because her mother had fished, upstream, on water that had a different name then, and some inheritances you kept in your hands rather than your head.

"Well?" she said, not looking up. "Is the summit line going to fall down?"

"Not today."

"That's what you said yesterday."

"And I was right yesterday too. My record is unblemished."

"Your record includes a collapsed weir, a flooded tunnel, and one rockslide you personally walked back into." She bit the thread clean. "Unblemished is a strong word for a man who keeps a map of closed lines."

"A map of *open* lines," Petra said, and sat down beside her, and felt her shoulder come to rest against theirs the way it did every evening, automatic as tide.

Because that was the other thing. The map.

* * *

The map of closed lines lived in the survey shed now, flat in a proper drawer instead of rolled in a pack, because Petra had finally—finally—unrolled it all the way and pinned it to a backing board, and looking at it flat had been its own small ceremony. Forty years of closures. Every line the Crown had struck, every trestle pulled down, every tunnel sealed from inside by people who loved the railway enough to bury it rather than let it be used as a weapon. Ovid's marks. The exile communities' marks. Marks in three hands and two alphabets, a palimpsest of grief drawn in ink and rail-steel.

For years Petra had carried it rolled tight, the way you carry a wound. Evidence of a past they'd fled. Proof of a trade they'd disgraced. A map of everything shut.

Tonight, after supper, with Sefa reading by lamplight and the house holding its warmth around them like cupped hands, Petra took out a pen.

Not the survey pen. The good one, the one with the nib Sefa had bought at the market in Kell's Ferry the week the charter was signed, "for signing things," she'd said, "you'll have things to sign now, everyone does," and she'd been right, there had been forms, so many forms, charters generate paperwork the way rivers generate silt.

The map spread on the table between them. Sefa looked up from her book. She didn't say anything. She just watched, the way she'd watched Petra work the first sealed door, with that open admiration Petra had once not known how to receive and had since learned, slowly, awkwardly, to stand inside of without flinching.

There was a blank space on the map. Down-valley, past Kell's Ferry, along the route the new charter road would take when the summer crews arrived—a spur, surveyed but unbuilt, running from the valley junction up toward the high pasture settlements where half the exile families had wintered for a generation. On the old Crown maps that corridor was marked with the same notation as everything else Petra had ever erased: *closed.*

Petra drew the line.

Not dashed, the way planned lines went—not yet surveyed to final grade, not yet committed. Not crossed out, the way closed lines went. Just drawn. Solid, plain, a single confident stroke from junction to pasture, the kind of line you put on a map when you intend to walk it yourself.

"What's it called?" Sefa asked quietly.

"It doesn't need a name yet. Lines get names when they're real. Ask any child in that schoolhouse—they'll name it something ridiculous by autumn."

"The Heron Road," Sefa said immediately. "They'll call it the Heron Road. Tam will start it. You watch."

"Tam couldn't carry a tune in a bucket."

"Tam will absolutely start it." She set down her book. "You drew it solid."

"I did."

"Not dashed."

"No."

She studied him across the lamplight, and whatever she saw there made her reach over and turn the lamp up a little, so the new line caught the light properly, so it could be read.

"For years," Petra said, and found the words coming slowly, the way hard words did, "this map was a list of everything I'd failed to save. Every line on it was a door I'd helped shut, or watched shut, or ran from. I kept it rolled up because—" They stopped. Started again. Sefa waited; she was good at waiting; she'd waited out a dry season and a gorge and a man's entire armored heart, she could wait out a sentence. "Because I thought that was all it would ever be. A record of closings."

"And now?"

"Now it's a record of openings that haven't happened yet." Petra tapped the new line. "That's what a plan is, when you get right down to it. A closure in reverse."

Sefa laughed—her real laugh, the one that started somewhere below the surface and came up like the spring at the source, doubled and clean. Then she leaned across the map, careful of the wet ink, and kissed him, plainly, the way she'd started doing some months ago and had never once apologized for, and Petra had stopped expecting her to.

Outside, the valley held its evening sounds: the spring run, doubled now, talking to itself in the stone channels; the far-off clank of the night crew at the summit portal; and from the schoolhouse, where Mistress Anka was running a late lesson, one more attempt at the anthem, slightly less off-key than the morning's. Progress, measured in semitones.

The chest sat open in the corner of the schoolhouse. The map lay open on the table. And the man who had spent thirty years keeping his bedroll packed by the door fell asleep with both feet under the blanket, in a house with his name on the charter deed next to hers, while outside the window the water ran high and clear toward a town that was, at last, on the map.

In the morning there would be grades to check and centimeters to argue and a road to build that didn't have a name yet but already, unmistakably, did.

The heron flew on two heads' breath. One for the water. One for the land.

And for the first time in Petra Zhou's long, closed-map life, both heads were pointed home.
