## 1. The River Keeps What It Is Owed

The river had been rising for three days, and by the fourth it had begun to eat the road.

Sable Fairweather watched it happen from the deck of the *Marrow*, poling the ferry across a channel that hadn't existed last week. The Kestrian side of the crossing — a mud shelf where goats grazed in dry summers — was now a brown tongue of current licking at the roots of a willow stand, pulling soil away in soft, silent mouthfuls. Somewhere under that water was the cart track that had brought farmers down to market. Somewhere under it, too, was the ferry landing's old stone footing, which meant Sable had to nose the flat-bottomed boat against a fallen alder and let passengers climb across like cats on a fence.

They paid badly for it. Two coppers a head, three if they had goods, and half of them haggled as if drowning were a service Sable had invented personally to inconvenience them.

"Four coppers," said the woman with the goose, "and I'll throw in the egg she laid this morning."

"The goose rides free if you hold her yourself." Sable took the coppers because four was four, and held out a hand not to shake but to point. "Aft. Hold the rail. If she panics, you go over with her."

The woman went aft clutching the bird. The rest of the morning's cargo followed: two boys with fishing poles, an old man carrying a crate of what smelled like fermenting plums, a pair of sisters who stared at Sable's scarred hands the whole crossing and whispered behind them.

Let them whisper. Whispering was cheap. It cost nothing to be the strange ferryman with the weathered face and the accent that didn't sit right anywhere, the one who never drank with the dock crowd and never answered questions about where they'd learned to read water like scripture. Anonymity was a trade like any other, and Sable had gotten good at it. Six years good. You built it the way you built a wall — stone by stone, lie by small lie, until even you sometimes forgot what was buried on the other side.

The *Marrow* was thirty feet of patched oak and stubbornness, poled or rowed depending on current, licensed out of a river office whose clerk spelled Sable's name differently every season. That was fine. Names were ballast. You kept just enough to stay upright and dumped the rest when the water got rough.

The crossing took twenty minutes. The current wanted forty, shoving the hull sideways, and Sable worked the pole hard off the bottom, feeling through the shaft the way the flood had rearranged everything — old channels silted shut, new ones torn open, the whole grammar of the river rewritten overnight. Monsoon season came early this year. The high country snowpack was still melting into it, and every tributary from the Kestrian hills ran fat and mean.

On the return run, empty, riding light, Sable saw the chest.

It hung in the drowned willows maybe two hundred yards downstream of the landing, snagged where the flood had bent the trees flat and left them combing the current like fingers. At first it was just a shape — dark wood, pale binding — turning slowly in the eddy behind the trunks. Driftwood, Sable thought. A dead animal. Then it rolled, and brass caught the gray light and threw it back, a clean yellow flash in all that murk.

Sable shipped the pole and drifted, watching.

The rule was simple, and Sable had written it themselves after the second year of running: *the river keeps what it is owed, and gives back only what nobody wants.* Bodies, mostly. Wreckage. The occasional drowned lamb. Things that belonged to grief, not to you. You reported them if there was a reward posted; otherwise you let them hang in the willows until the water dropped and someone else found them and made them somebody else's problem.

But brass fittings were worth money. Brass, decent oak, iron hinges — a chest that size could mean a week of ferry fares, and the monsoon was going to strangle the crossing within days anyway. Nobody would miss salvage pulled from a flood. Salvage wasn't touching anything that mattered. Salvage was what any sensible scavenger would do.

Sable told themselves that twice, which was once more than usual, and swung the *Marrow* toward the willows.

* * *

Getting to it was work. The willows stood in waist-deep water laced with submerged branches, and Sable had to strip off boots, wade in with a boat hook, and lean out over the gunwale while the current tried its patient best to peel the ferry away and keep it. The chest was wedged tight between two trunks, half-submerged, its lid pressed down by a mat of debris. Waterlogged, then. Heavy. Whatever was inside had either sunk it or been packed to survive it.

The lock was broken — torn, not picked, the hasp ripped out of the wood as if something downstream had hit it hard enough to spring it. Flood damage, probably. Probably.

Sable hesitated, hands flat on the wet lid.

Here was the thing about curiosity: it was a door, and doors opened both ways. Sable had spent six years learning to walk past doors. A sealed box in a flood was a story, and stories had owners, and owners had questions, and questions had teeth. The smart move was to strip the brass, burn the box, and be poorer but invisible.

The smart move. Yes.

Sable pried the lid open anyway.

Inside, cushioned in oilcloth gone slick with river water, lay flat sheets of metal — thin plates, each about the length of a forearm, stacked like pages. Not tin. Something denser, darker, with a fine grain to it. Sable lifted the top one and turned it to the light.

Engraved lines covered its face. Contours, hatched slopes, a river system drawn with a sureness that made Sable's stomach tighten in an old, unwelcome way — that was surveyor's hand, real surveyor's hand, the kind of linework you only got from someone who'd walked every step they drew. Numbers ran along the margins in a cramped cipher. And stamped deep in the lower corner, repeated on plate after plate as Sable fanned through the stack, was a seal: a mountain split by a star, crowned with a broken circlet.

Sable knew that seal. Everyone past forty knew it, and everyone younger knew it without knowing how. Vhalen. The mountain-and-star of a country that had been on maps when Sable was a child and was on none of them now — erased so thoroughly that schoolchildren in Kestria learned the borderlands as if they'd always been empty, as if the valleys upriver had never had names, roads, kings. Twenty years gone. Not conquered, exactly. *Dissolved*, the treaties said, in the way powerful neighbors dissolved things they wanted cut up quietly.

And here was Vhalen's crown stamp, pressed into metal, riding a flooded border river like a message in a bottle.

Sable set the plate down on the thwart very carefully, the way you set down a snake you weren't sure was asleep.

The plates numbered better than thirty. Most showed terrain — ridgelines, glacier outlines, elevation figures in that ciphered script. One near the bottom of the stack was different: denser, worked over, with corrections inked and re-inked beneath the engraving, as if its maker had changed his mind and then changed it back. Sable couldn't read the cipher. But the shape of the thing was legible enough to anyone who'd ever guided a party over high ground. This was a route. A pass, maybe. Drawn, hidden inside a larger survey, corrected, and then sealed in a chest that ended up in a river.

People didn't seal valuable things in chests and lose them in floods. People *hid* valuable things in chests, and floods were excellent at hiding.

"Right," Sable said aloud, to the willows, to the rain starting again on the water. "Brass fittings. That's all this is."

The willows said nothing. The river said nothing. Downstream, the ferry landing waited with its mud and its geese and its two-copper fares, and above it all the hills stood gray under the monsoon's first real weight, holding their snow, holding their secrets, letting the water carry down whatever they were done with.

Sable bailed the chest out, wrapped the plates back in their oilcloth, and stowed it under the stern bench beneath a coil of rope and a tarp — not hidden, exactly. Just not obvious. There was a difference, and Sable had built six years on differences like that.

Then they poled back to the landing, and the afternoon's crossings began, and the chest rode under the bench like a stone in a boot.

* * *

By dusk the rain had settled in for the night, a steady gray drumming on the tarp, and Sable sat in the *Marrow*'s little cuddy with a lamp turned low and the chest open on the floorboards.

They'd spent the day being a ferryman. Hauling cargo, arguing fares, minding the current as it climbed another handspan up the landing posts. All the while the plates had sat under the rope coil, radiating presence, the way a sleeping dog radiates heat. Twice Sable had caught themselves thinking about them mid-crossing and had to pull their attention back to the water — dangerous, that, on a flood river. Attention was survival. Attention was the whole trade.

Now, alone, Sable gave in and studied them properly.

Lamplight brought the engravings up sharp. Whoever had cut these lines had been a master; the contours flowed like handwriting, confident, unhesitating, with tiny conventions Sable half-recognized from the old imperial surveys — the kind of training that died with countries. The cipher in the margins wasn't random either. Sable had seen enough coded freight manifests on the smuggling routes, back in another life, to know structure when they saw it: repeating groups, position-weighted characters, a key somewhere waiting to be applied.

One plate, though, needed no key. Near the stack's middle, a sheet bore not terrain but text, engraved large and formal around the central seal, in old Vhaleni script. Sable couldn't read the words. But the layout was unmistakable — a title line, a signature block, the flourishes of officialdom. A cover plate. A document's face.

And beneath the signature, smaller, almost an afterthought, a second line of script that someone had tried to efface — chiseled shallow, then abandoned halfway. As if the engraver had started to remove it and decided, at the last, to leave it.

Sable sat back on the bunk and rubbed their eyes.

Sell the brass. Melt the plates. That was still the play. Scrap value, a week's money, no history attached. The seal meant nothing good to anyone — dead nations attracted living vultures, and vultures had long memories and longer knives. Somewhere upstream, presumably, there were people who wanted this chest, and people who wanted the people who wanted it, and Sable wanted no part of either queue. Six years of invisibility said: *melt it down tonight, be done by morning.*

But.

But the corrections on that one dense plate. Someone had fought over that route in ink, changed their mind, changed it back. Someone had signed a document and then tried to scratch away part of their own name-work and stopped. Those were human marks, the marks of a person cornered by circumstances, doing the best careful work they could under pressure — and Sable knew what that looked like from the inside, knew it the way you know your own hands.

The lamp guttered. Rain hammered the deck. Out on the black water, the river kept moving, hauling its load of mountains toward the sea, keeping what it was owed and offering up, once in a great while, something nobody wanted.

Something nobody wanted. Sable laughed quietly at that. Nothing sealed in royal brass and hidden in a flood was unwanted. It was *unclaimed* — for now — and the difference between those two words was exactly the width of the trouble coming to collect it.

Sable closed the chest, slid it deep under the bunk, and put the rope coil back on top.

One night, they told themselves. Keep it one night, sell the brass tomorrow, be a ferryman again by noon. Simple. Sensible. The same voice that had talked them into opening the lid, wearing a different coat.

Through the cuddy's fogged glass, downstream where the landing lamps burned low, a figure stood at the end of the dock in the rain — too far off to make out details, wrong hour for a passenger, standing the way people stand when they're watching a specific boat instead of the river.

Sable turned the lamp down to nothing and sat in the dark, listening to the water, and did not sleep well.
