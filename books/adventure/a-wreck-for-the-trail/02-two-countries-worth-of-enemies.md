## 2. Two Countries' Worth of Enemies

The harbor clerk's office at Vessick Landing smelled of wet paper and lamp oil, the way every harbor clerk's office smelled, and Sable stood in line with their ferry manifest held loose in one hand like it was nothing — like paper had never once ruined them.

Rain came off the river in gray sheets. The monsoon was still a week out, maybe two, but the water was already up, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees past the pilings like it was clearing its throat before speaking. Sable had been working this stretch of the Sorrel for eleven days now: hauling grain sacks, ferrying a wool merchant who tipped badly, running children across to the school on the far bank for coins that bought bread and lamp fuel and not much else. Honest work. Small work. Work that asked no questions and answered none.

That was the trick of it. You made yourself small enough that the world forgot to look.

"Next."

Sable stepped up to the counter and slid the manifest across. The clerk was a heavy man with ink-stained cuffs and spectacles fogged from the damp, and he took the paper without looking up, which was how Sable liked it. Eyes down. Ink down. Everything down.

"Ferry *Kestrel's Mercy*," the clerk read aloud, in the flat voice men used for things they didn't care about. "Owner-operator, one crew." He squinted at the signature block. "You're listed as Ashen. First name?"

"Sable Ashen."

"Ashen." He said it again, tasting it, and something in the way his mouth moved around the second syllable made the back of Sable's neck prickle. "There was a guide named Ashen once. Up north. Ondmark side."

"There are Ashens everywhere," Sable said. "It's a common name."

"Mm." The clerk looked up.

He was older than Sable remembered — but then, Sable had been remembering him young, because you remember your enemies young, you keep them frozen at the moment they hurt you. This was Harl Voss, who had worked the customs desk at Coldharbor six years ago, who had stamped the expedition permits with his own hand, who had read the incident report afterward and looked at Sable across a table while four names were read aloud into the record. Four dead climbers. One guide who signed a false name on the recovery affidavit and walked out of Ondmark before the inquiry convened.

Voss's eyes went over Sable's face the way a man reads a river: looking for the deep channel, the thing under the surface.

"You've got a familiar look," he said.

"I have a common face," Sable said. "It's why I ferry people instead of guiding them."

Something flickered behind the spectacles. Not recognition — not yet. Suspicion without a shape to hang it on. Voss turned the manifest over, checked the toll stamp, checked the insurance mark, and Sable stood there breathing like a man standing in a field during lightning weather, knowing the strike could come from anywhere and pretending to watch birds.

Six years ago there had been snow. That was the thing about the memory — everyone expected fire, blood, screaming. What Sable carried instead was snow, and silence, and the particular blue color of a rope end hanging off a cornice where a rope end should not be.

* * *

They had been seven. A client party out of Kestria, wealthy, impatient, recommended to Sable by reputation — *the best guide on the northern ranges, Fairweather, they call them; nothing gets past Fairweather.* Sable had been thirty-one and careless with pride, the way you are when you've never been tested where it counts.

The route was the Gray Stair, a ridge-and-couloir line above the Ondmark border, technically legal, technically sane, in the season when it was usually climbed. But the client — Aldous Renner, timber money, three generations of it — wanted the summit on his fiftieth birthday, and the weather window was thin, and Sable had looked at the sky and looked at the man's hungry face and said yes when the honest answer was *wait.*

Wait. One word. Six letters of alphabet and a lifetime of difference.

The storm came in early. That happened; storms lied about their schedules. What didn't happen — what shouldn't have happened — was the fixed line failing at the third pitch. Sable had placed those anchors personally, or believed they had. Later, much later, an Ondmark rigger would find that two of the pitons had been pulled and reset shallow, hammered into ice that was already rotting, by someone who knew exactly what a shallow piton looks like in a guidebook inspection and exactly what it does at load.

Sabotage. That word never made the official record. What made the record was this: *guided party overtaken by storm; anchor failure at pitch three; four members lost; guide survived.*

Survived. There was the poison in it. Sable had been below, roped to the last client, and when the line failed the mountain took the four above and left the two below, and Sable had spent nine hours in whiteout holding a dying man's hand through his mittens because there was nowhere to go and nothing to do but be present while the cold did its arithmetic. Aldous Renner died at dawn asking about his daughter. His daughter, Mira, was seventeen, and she was the one who hired Sable's lawyer, and she was the one who stood up at the inquiry and said, in a voice like a struck bell, *I don't blame the guide. I want to know who checked those anchors.* And the inquiry did not want to know, because the inquiry had been paid not to know, and the fastest way to close a wound like that is to hand it a guide.

So they handed it Sable. And Sable — exhausted, half-frozen, advised by lawyers who were being paid by the same timber money that owned the mountain — signed the recovery affidavit as *Ashen,* a name borrowed from a grandmother, and let the record say what the record needed to say, and walked south across the border into Kestria with a blacklist waiting on both sides of the line.

*Fairweather* was finished. *Ashen* could carry a ferry.

Some nights Sable told themselves it had been cowardice. Other nights — the honest ones — they knew it had been mercy, of a kind: Mira Renner had needed someone to blame who wasn't her father's company, and Sable had needed to disappear more than they needed to be right. Two needs, one false name. It had felt, at the time, like the only move on the board.

It had felt like camouflage. It had taken years to understand it was also a cage.

* * *

"—Ashen? I said, do you want the seasonal stamp or not?"

Sable blinked. The office. The rain. Voss watching them with that slow customs-house patience, the patience of a man whose entire profession was noticing what people tried to hide.

"Seasonal," Sable said. "And the cargo rider."

"Cargo rider costs extra in flood season."

"I know what it costs."

Voss stamped the manifest. Once, twice. Then he didn't slide it back across the counter. He held it, flat under his palm, and looked at Sable over the spectacles, and the lamplight caught the ink stains on his fingers and turned them black as frostbite.

"You know," he said conversationally, "there was a business about anchors, some years back. Ondmark side. Gray Stair. A guide who walked away from the inquiry before it sat." He tilted his head. "The description in the circular was vague. Height wrong. Build wrong. But faces—" He tapped the bridge of his nose. "Faces stay. I've seen ten thousand faces come through this office and I forget most of them by supper. Yours I wouldn't forget by supper."

"Then you have a better memory than my mother," Sable said. "She forgot mine twice."

Voss almost smiled. Almost. He slid the manifest back, and Sable took it, and the paper trembled — barely, a shiver a stranger would read as cold, because the office *was* cold, because the rain *was* coming down — and Sable turned for the door with every muscle performing calm the way a puppet performs life.

At the door, Voss said, "Ferryman."

Sable stopped. Did not turn. Turning was admission; admission was a door opening onto a room with no other exits.

"The river's high this year," Voss said. "High water brings up old things. Chests, bodies, papers." A pause long enough to walk through. "If anything interesting comes up out of that river, a man in your position would be wise to bring it straight to the customs house. Wise, and rewarded."

"I pull dead trees out of the willows and sell them for firewood," Sable said. "That's all the river gives me."

"All it's given you *yet,*" Voss said pleasantly, and went back to his ledger.

* * *

Outside, the rain hit like a decision. Sable crossed the plank dock with the manifest rolled inside their coat, heart going hard and stupid against the ribs, and made themselves look at the boat — the *Kestrel's Mercy,* forty years old, patched twice below the waterline, smelling of grain dust and bilge — and made themselves see it as a stranger would see it. Just a ferry. Just a tired man or woman or whatever the stranger decided to call them, because Sable had spent six years sanding the edges off even that question, letting each dock town draw its own conclusion and never correcting anyone. Ambiguity was armor. A person nobody could describe accurately was a person nobody could find.

Except Voss had found the shape of them in under a minute. Faces stay.

Sable crouched at the stern and pretended to check the rudder fittings, which gave them an excuse to look at the deck where the chest sat under an oilcloth, lashed beside the spare oar. Brass corners green with river patina. Lock rusted shut. They'd hauled it out of the drowned willows two days ago intending to strip the fittings and sell the brass, and hadn't, and hadn't examined too closely why they hadn't, and now a customs clerk was talking about what the river brings up and what a wise man does with it.

Inside the chest — Sable had forced the hasp just far enough to see, that first night, by lamplight, hands shaking in a way they hadn't shaken since Ondmark — inside the chest were survey plates. Etched metal, wrapped in oilskin, precise as scripture. And pressed into the lead seal on the wrapping, a crest: a mountain split by a star.

Vhalen. A country that had been erased so thoroughly that schoolchildren in Kestria learned the word only as a cautionary tale about borders, and the maps showed open tundra where the nation had been, as if sixty thousand people had simply evaporated off the plateau one winter.

Sable had put the hasp back down and sat in the dark for a long time, listening to the rain, feeling the old hunger rise — the cartographer's hunger, the mountaineer's hunger, the *what's-up-there* hunger that had gotten four people killed and one person erased — and had strangled it the way they strangled it every day. Curiosity was the luxury of people with clean records.

But Voss knew. Or suspected, which in practice was worse, because suspicion could be sold to whoever paid best, and Sable had a fair idea who paid for things in this stretch of the border. There were men who bought dead countries the way other men bought timber rights.

The smart play — the play six years of survival had drilled into the bone — was to row out tonight, mid-rain, mid-darkness, and drop the chest into the deepest hole in the Sorrel. Let the river keep what it kept. Go back to grain sacks and schoolchildren and being nobody.

Sable untied the oilcloth instead. Just to look. Just to confirm it was worthless, which it surely was, which would make dropping it easy.

Under the oilcloth, the topmost plate caught the gray light, and Sable saw etched contour lines climbing into country they recognized — the high glacier country north of the border, the country no map in Kestria drew honestly — and along the plate's margin, in a fine surveyor's hand, a notation in cipher, and beneath the cipher, small enough to miss:

*Route viable. Sealed against seizure. — E.V.*

E.V. Sable didn't know the initials. But the hand was steady, and the lines were confident, and the confidence of it reached across however many years and spoke directly to the part of Sable that had once loved mountains more than it feared them.

"You're going to get me killed," Sable said quietly, to the plate, to the chest, to the dead country that owned it.

"Probably," said a voice from the shadow of the covered skiff moored next door. "Or me. Depends which of us is smarter."

Sable came up fast, knife already in hand — six years of habit — and found themself looking at a kid. Young, hooded, soaked through, sitting cross-legged on the neighboring skiff's deck as if they'd grown there, watching Sable with eyes that had gone straight past the ferry and the ferryman and locked onto the crest like a compass needle finding north.

"That seal," the kid said. "Where did you get it?"
