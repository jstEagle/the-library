## 3. What the Seal Means

Wren had been living on the covered skiff for nine days, and in that time she had learned three things about the dock at Marrow's Landing: the fishmonger's boy stole from his own father, the night watchman could be bought with a bottle and a compliment, and nobody — nobody — looked twice at a soaked kid sitting still under an oilcloth.

She'd survived worse docks than this. She'd survived her father's death, which was a dock of a different kind, one you fell off once and never climbed back onto. She'd survived the Guild's inquiry, the questions about missing folios, the way Master Ellery's voice had gone soft and terrible when he said *apprentice, do you know what happens to people who steal from the archive?* She hadn't stolen anything. That was the joke of it. She'd only taken what was already hers — her father's field notes, his cipher key, the letters he'd written her mother in a hand so small it looked like weather — and the Guild had called it theft because the Guild believed everything belonged to it. Every coastline. Every river. Every name.

So she sat under the oilcloth with her knees drawn up and her father's satchel between her feet like a second heartbeat, and she watched the ferryman.

The ferryman interested her. Not because there was anything remarkable to see — a broad-shouldered figure in a rain-dark coat, working the morning crossing with the economy of someone who hated wasted motion — but because of how they moved. Wren had grown up around surveyors, and surveyors learned to read terrain before they read words. The ferryman read water the way she read contour lines: constantly, unconsciously, eyes flicking to the current seams, the boil lines behind the pilings, the place where the flood-swollen river folded over something submerged. When a log came down fast on the starboard side, the ferryman had already shifted weight before Wren heard it. That wasn't a boatman's instinct. Boatmen respected the river. This person *distrusted* it, personally, the way you distrust a former employer.

And the ferryman never looked at anyone's face longer than a breath. Wren knew that trick too. It was her own trick.

So when the ferryman hauled the chest out of the drowned willows — when Wren saw the brass catch flash, saw the crest stamped into the lid as the thing swung up dripping onto the deck — she felt the recognition go through her like a fishhook set behind the sternum.

The crowned antler. Split by a single vertical line.

Vhalen.

Her father had drawn that crest four hundred times. In the margins of his surveys, in the corner plates of his maps, in the flyleaf of the atlas he'd made her when she was seven — a hand-bound book of blank maps titled *Places You Will Go, If Anyone Asks You To.* He'd told her once, tapping the antler with a pencil worn down to a stub: *This country is going to die, little wren, and when it does, every map that carries this mark will be worth more than the paper beneath it. Remember the seal. Seals are promises, and dead countries keep their promises better than live ones.*

She had thought, for six years, that all the promises were burned. The Guild had inventory lists of Vhaleni materials — seized, catalogued, redistributed to the archives of Kestria and Ondmark like spoons from a dead grandmother's house. Her father's own master atlas, the glacier survey, the whole northern folio: gone. She'd assumed pulped or locked away, and either way lost to her.

And now here it was, swinging off a ferry hook in the rain, held by a stranger who looked at the crest the way most people look at a snake.

Wren should have stayed under the oilcloth. Nine days of stillness had kept her alive; stillness was the first rule of being erased. But her body was already moving, quiet across the gap between skiffs, and by the time the ferryman said *you're going to get me killed* to the plate in their hands, she was sitting cross-legged on the neighboring deck with the hood back, watching them come up fast with a knife already open.

Six years of habit, that draw. She noted it. Filed it. Kept very still.

"That seal," she said. "Where did you get it?"

* * *

For a long moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked on the oilcloth above the ferryman's deck. Down the dock, the fishmonger shouted at his thieving son, and the sound broke over them and passed, and still the knife stayed where it was, steady, pointed at nothing in particular and at her entirely.

"River," the ferryman said finally. "Flooded willows, north bend."

"I can see that. There's weed wrapped around the hasp." Wren tilted her head. "It didn't wash down from upstream. Someone sank it shallow, meaning to come back, and the flood beat them to it. Which means whoever owns it thinks it's still down there. Which means—" she stopped herself, because the rest of the sentence was *they'll be looking,* and saying it aloud would only make the knife-hand twitchier.

"Which means," the ferryman said, "it's none of your business."

"It's the most my business has been in six years."

Something shifted behind the ferryman's eyes — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. Wren recognized that too; she did it herself a dozen times a day, weighing strangers like cargo, deciding what each one could be trusted to carry. She decided to make the arithmetic easier.

"My name's Wren," she said. "That's not the name I was born with, but it's the one I answer to, and it's more than I expect you to give me in return, so we're even from the start."

The knife lowered maybe two degrees. "Generous."

"I'm a generous person. Ask anyone who's ever met me and then never speak to me again." She nodded at the plate in the ferryman's fist. "You're holding a survey plate. Copper-faced, if I'm any judge, which I am. That's not decoration — that's data. Engraved line work, probably forty, fifty sheets in a chest that size, and the chest alone is worth more than this entire dock."

"You know a lot about chests."

"I know a lot about maps." She let that sit. Let the ferryman look at her properly for the first time — the ink stains she'd never quite scrubbed from her fingers, the callus on her right middle finger where a ruling pen rested, the way her eyes had gone, involuntarily, greedily, to the plate again. "I was apprenticed to the Kestrian Cartographers' Guild. Before."

"*Before* is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"Isn't it just." She smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile, and she saw the ferryman recognize that too — the smile of someone whose lies were load-bearing. Two liars, she thought, looking at each other across three feet of wet deck and an open knife. Like recognizing like. It was almost cozy.

"Here's what I think," Wren said. "I think you pulled that chest up to sell the fittings. Brass catches, corner strapping, maybe the lock if it's good iron. Am I close?"

The ferryman's silence was confirmation enough.

"And I think that when you opened it and saw what was inside, you didn't feel richer. You felt *found.*" She leaned forward slightly. "Because that crest isn't just a crest. It's a signature. Everything stamped with it was logged, registered, archived — and everything archived gets hunted when it turns up missing. You didn't pull treasure out of the river. You pulled a question out of the river, and the question is going to follow you until somebody answers it."

"Or until I throw it back."

"You won't." Wren said it flatly, no challenge in it, just observation. "You've been holding that plate for ten minutes and your thumb keeps finding the engraved lines. You're reading it without knowing how. People don't throw away things their thumbs like."

The ferryman looked down at their own thumb as if betrayed by it. Wren nearly laughed. Nearly.

"So here's my offer," she said. "Passage upstream. Whatever's left of your contract, whatever run you're working — I ride along, I keep low, I pay in the only coin I have, which is this: I can read those plates. All of them. The engraving style, the notation, the marginal ciphers — that's late-Vhaleni royal survey work, and there are perhaps five people alive who can parse it without the key, and I'm one, because the man who invented half those symbols taught me my letters. You have a chest full of a dead country's secrets. I am the closest thing to a translator you will ever find, and I am currently free."

"Why upstream?"

"Because downstream is Kestria, and Kestria is where questions get answered with rope." She shrugged. "Also because I need to go upstream, and I've been sitting on this dock for nine days waiting for a boat that isn't smart enough to refuse me. You're the least smart candidate so far. Congratulations."

* * *

The ferryman didn't put the knife away. Wren respected that. Trust offered too quickly was just another kind of lie, and this person — Sable, she'd heard the harbor clerk spit the name yesterday evening, heard the clerk's face change when the ferryman walked past, heard the word *blacklisted* muttered like a curse and a warning both — this person understood the price of names as well as she did.

Yes, she'd known since yesterday. She'd watched the clerk watch the ferryman, and she'd seen the recognition pass between them like a current, and she'd thought: *there's someone else running.* The borderlands were full of runners, but runners had a gait. The ferryman had it. Shoulders angled away from being looked at. Eyes that counted exits.

"What's your play?" the ferryman asked at last. "You decode the plates, and then what? Sell them? Because if you're planning to sell—"

"Sell them to whom?" Wren laughed, short and hard. "Every government on this river wants Vhalen to stay dead. That's why it stays dead. That's why the maps got confiscated instead of copied. A whole country erased isn't an accident, it's a *policy*, and policies have budgets." She shook her head. "No. I want them published. Copied, distributed, scattered so wide no one can ever gather them up again. My father spent his life mapping a country his own employers were busy forgetting, and when he died they treated his life's work as contraband. I want it back in the world. That's the whole of my ambition. It's modest. It's also going to get us both killed, probably, so I understand if you decline."

The rain thickened. Out on the river, the current had turned the color of strong tea, carrying branches, a drowned fence post, a hat that might have belonged to anyone. The calving-season window, the high passes, the brief weeks when the ice gave up its dead and its roads alike — all of that was upstream, all of that was north, and Wren watched the ferryman look north the way she'd watched them look at the river all morning: distrustfully, expertly, unable to stop.

"One condition," the ferryman said.

"Name it."

"You don't touch the chest without me present. You don't copy anything without me seeing it. And when we're done — if we're done — I decide what happens to the originals." The knife finally closed, slid away somewhere under the coat with a whisper of oiled leather. "They came out of my river."

"Your river," Wren repeated, tasting it. "All right. Agreed. Though I'll note for the record that 'my' is a dangerous word to use about anything on this stretch of water."

"Noted." The ferryman extended a hand — calloused, rope-scarred, honest about what it was. "Contract's simple, then. Passage for translation. Either of us breaks it, the other throws them off at the next landing."

"Witnessed by the rain," Wren said, shaking it. Her hand disappeared inside theirs entirely. "Very binding. Rivers hold grudges."

"They do," the ferryman said, and for the first time something moved through the flat wariness of their face that might, in better light, on a luckier day, have been the ghost of humor. "Welcome aboard, Wren-who-was-apprenticed-before. Stow your bag aft. We cast off within the hour, ahead of the worst of it."

"The worst of what?"

The ferryman looked north, at the sky over the hills, which had gone a strange bruised green-black, the color the monsoon sky went when it stopped raining and started *arriving.*

"The storm," they said. "And everything else."

Wren gathered her satchel — her father's satchel, her father's cipher, her father's unfinished war with every archive in the world — and stepped aboard, and as she did, she caught sight of the plate again where the ferryman had set it down, and the crowned antler gleamed up at her through the rain like a promise kept past the grave.

Down the dock, unnoticed by either of them, a man in a gray traveling coat closed the ledger he'd been pretending to read, and began, unhurried, to follow.
