## 5. Leaking

**Sable**

The stranger talked fast, and Sable let them.

That was the trick of it — you learned in the borderlands that the first minute of any conversation told you everything. Who needed what. Who was lying about needing it. This one — soaked to the ribs, maybe nineteen, maybe younger, with ink stains worked so deep into the knuckles they looked like bruises — needed passage, and was willing to trade the one thing Sable couldn't price: knowledge of the chest.

"Vhalen," the kid said again, when Sable didn't lower the boathook. "The crest on those plates. You've seen it now, so you know it's not Kestrian, not Ondmarkish, not anything on your charts. Because there *are* no charts. They scrubbed it."

"Scrubbed it how?"

"How do you think? The same way anything gets scrubbed." The kid's jaw tightened. "Paperwork. Men like Corvane's buyer. Men who pay for dead things and then make sure they stay dead."

Sable weighed the boathook a moment longer, then set it down against the gunwale — not sheathed, just down. A message in itself. "You were at the dock. Last night. When Corvane came."

"I was under the pier." No shame in it. "I heard him offer you four hundred crowns for an unopened box. Do you know what I'd have done with four hundred crowns?"

"Died rich?"

Something flickered across the kid's face — surprise, then a grudging almost-smile. "Wren," they said. "My name's Wren."

"Sable." And then, because the name had already been spoken aloud once too often this week, because the harbor clerk had said it like a knife going in: "Just Sable."

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had lied about bigger things than names, but never to someone whose eyes were this tired. Tired people saw through craft; they'd seen every trick already, usually by having them used.

The boat was worse than advertised. Wren had expected river scow, and got something older — a flat-bottomed ferry hull with patched seams and a tiller worn smooth by hands that weren't Sable's anymore, if the way they avoided touching it meant anything. But the engine turned over on the third pull, and the deck was dry-ish, and it floated, which put it ahead of most things Wren had relied on lately.

"You can bunk aft," Sable said. "There's oilskin. Don't touch the chest."

"It's my father's work in that chest."

"It's brass fittings and scrap metal until you prove otherwise. Aft."

Wren went aft. Through the curtain of rain hammering the river into white noise, they watched Sable cast off — efficient, economical, a person who had clearly done ten thousand small tasks exactly this way and resented each one slightly less than dying. The mooring line came free with a flick. The bow swung upstream.

Toward the mountains. Toward, though Wren hadn't said it yet and Sable hadn't asked, the glacier.

The monsoon arrived properly an hour later.

It didn't begin so much as arrive — a wall of water walking up the valley, swallowing the far bank, then the near bank, then the sky's distinction from either. Sable killed the lamp, ran the boat by feel and memory of the channel markers, and shouted over the roar that Wren should hold onto something that was bolted down, of which there was very little.

Wren held the chest instead.

* * *

**Sable**

By midnight the pump was losing.

Sable knew the sound their boat made when it was healthy — a hollow knock-and-slosh, honest as a heartbeat. What it made now was quieter, deeper, water finding seams that the summer drought had opened and the autumn rains had never tested. The bilge pump coughed, spat, kept coughing. The water in the well rose past the second plank. Past the third.

"Wren!" No answer over the storm. Sable fought the tiller through a standing wave, felt the current grab the stern like a hand, and screamed again. "*Bail!*"

A shape crawled forward out of the dark, and then small hands were in the water beside Sable's, hauling with a dented pot that had no business being aboard and every sign of having been stolen from somewhere. Good instincts, Sable thought grimly. Bailing side by side, shoulders bumping, breath ragged, the two of them throwing the river back at the river in the oldest war humans had ever fought and mostly lost.

It should have been miserable. It was. But there was something else underneath it, something Sable distrusted immediately: rhythm. The kid bailed on the off-beat without being taught. Matched the roll of the hull. Didn't panic when a wave came over the bow and filled the pot faster than it could be emptied — just dumped it and dipped again.

"Where'd you learn boats?" Sable shouted.

"Didn't!" Wren shouted back. "I'm learning *you*!"

Which was such an absurd, infuriating, accurate thing to say that Sable laughed — actually laughed, a short ugly bark torn out of them by the storm — and bailed harder.

* * *

**Wren**

Dawn found them wedged in a backwater behind a drowned gravel bar, engine dead, hull riding low, the rain gone from torrential to merely biblical. Everything Wren owned was wet. Everything Sable owned appeared to be wet too, which gave Wren a small, mean comfort.

The water in the well had won the night's argument: six inches deep, steady, refusing to drop no matter how long they took turns at the pot. Sable knelt in it, running fingers along the seam below the waterline, and came up with a look Wren recognized from mirrors — the look of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"She's taking it faster than we can throw it out," Sable said. "We need to beach her proper, pull a seam, re-caulk. That's half a day if the weather breaks. Longer if it doesn't."

"Will it break?"

Sable looked up at the sky as if asking it directly. The sky declined to comment.

"We're exposed here," Wren said. "Corvane's man saw us cast off. He'll have a boat by now."

"He'll have a *better* boat. Everyone has a better boat." Sable wrung out a sleeve with mechanical calm. "Better boats draw more water, though. This channel's barely a forearm deep. We go shallow where they can't follow, and we leak where they wouldn't dare."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy with extra steps."

Sable's head came around sharply — and Wren braced for anger, for the door closing, for the particular coldness of people who survived by letting nothing in. Instead Sable studied them for a long moment, rain dripping off their chin, and said:

"You're not afraid."

"I'm terrified. I'm also right. There's a difference."

"There is." Sable turned back to the seam. "Most people never learn it."

* * *

**Sable**

They ran the shallows all morning, Sable reading the water the way other people read documents — every boil and slack patch a sentence, every V of current a warning. Wren watched from the thwart with the ferocious attention of someone memorizing, and asked questions. Too many questions. Good ones, though, which was worse.

"Why does the water pile up there before the drop?"

"Pressure wave. Water doesn't like to change its mind quickly."

"And that green patch?"

"Deep and slow. Deceiving. Green water drowns more fools than white ever does."

Wren wrote it down. Actually wrote it down, on some kind of wax tablet pulled from an oilskin wrap, sketching the channel in quick confident strokes while the real channel tried its best to kill them. Sable found it unsettling, being observed that closely. Being *recorded*. It was like watching someone take notes on a fire.

But when the hull struck a submerged fence post around midday — a glancing blow, a shudder, a new voice added to the chorus of leaks — it was Wren who moved without being told, jamming a wad of oilcloth into the sprung seam while Sable held the boat off the obstacle. No discussion. No hesitation. Just two people who had known each other thirty-six hours operating like crew.

Sable hated how much that helped.

* * *

**Wren**

They made camp that night on a shelf of rock above the flood line, boat hauled halfway out and tied to a tree that had decided, against all evidence, to keep standing. Sable built a fire under a tarp with the sour competence of someone who had done it in worse places, and heated a pot of something gray and nourishing-adjacent.

Wren ate, and watched the firelight move on Sable's face, and made a decision.

"The plates," Wren said. "You should know what you're carrying."

"I know what Corvane thinks they're worth."

"Corvane knows what they're worth to *him*. Not what they are." Wren set down the pot. "My father drew those plates. Aldous Wren, royal surveyor of Vhalen. He died the year the country did. The chest came downstream the same year the archives burned. Someone saved it. Someone carried it a long way before the river took it back."

Sable said nothing, which Wren was learning was Sable's way of listening hard.

"There's a pass," Wren went on. "Through the high glacier. My father mapped it and hid it inside a routine survey — buried it in plain sight, ciphered into contour lines, because he knew what would happen to it if anyone official ever saw it. A route north that belongs to no government. That's why men like Corvane exist. That's why the seal gets scrubbed off maps. Not because Vhalen mattered. Because the *route* matters."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the flooded valley, thunder rolled along the ridgeline like furniture being dragged upstairs.

"So," Sable said finally. "You want to go there."

"I want to *publish* it. Openly. Everywhere. A pass that can't be owned can't be closed." Wren hugged their knees. "You want to sell it or sink it, I don't care which. But neither of us gets anywhere without the other. You have the boat and the water. I have the cipher and the map. And we both have Corvane coming upriver behind us."

Sable stared into the fire for a long time. Long enough that Wren started composing retreats.

Then: "Sleep," Sable said. "First watch is mine. We run at first light."

And that was all. But as Wren rolled into the oilskin, they heard Sable murmur something else, half to the fire, half to themselves, quiet enough to be deniable:

"A route nobody owns."

A pause.

"Hell of a thing to be carrying in a leaking boat."

* * *

**Sable**

The sound woke Sable an hour before dawn — not thunder, not rain, but a low grinding groan from the hull, like a ship clearing its throat before saying something final.

Sable was aboard in three strides, lantern up, and the light showed what the sound had promised: the old patch below the waterline, the one the previous owner had glassed over with tar and prayer, had peeled back along a hand's width. Water wasn't seeping anymore. It was *entering*, with the confidence of something invited.

"Wren! Up! Now!"

They got her off the bank and into the current with the well already ankle-deep, and Sable made the call they'd been avoiding since the gravel bar: no more shallows, no more caution. Straight upriver, engine flat-out, toward the smelter town lights someone had told them about in another life — forty miles of floodwater between them and a dry dock, and a hull that would give them maybe thirty.

So they bailed. Both of them, all of it, the whole gray roaring morning — pot and bucket and finally a cut-down jerrican, working in shifts that stopped being shifts because neither would take one. The rain came sideways. The river climbed the banks and took fences, sheds, a whole orchard of drowning trees sailing past bow-first. And through all of it the two of them threw water overboard and the water climbed anyway, patient as arithmetic, until Sable's arms burned and Wren's hands were rubbed raw and the lantern at noon showed eight inches in the well and rising.

"We're not making it," Wren gasped.

"We're making it."

"You always like this?"

"Only when I'm right."

The boat struck something unseen at midafternoon — a soft, terrible, underwater collision, the whole hull shuddering like a struck bell — and the new sound that followed was not a drip but a hiss, a long insistent whisper of water under pressure, and Sable knew with cold certainty that the arithmetic had changed.

Ahead, through the rain, a smudge of smoke on the left bank. Roofs. A rusted crane arm leaning over the water like a question.

"Smelter town," Sable shouted, hauling the tiller over. "Half a mile. Bail like the devil owes you money."

Wren bailed. Sable steered. The boat settled lower with every hundred yards, the river reaching hungrily over the gunwales, and the last thing Sable registered before the bow ground onto the mud below the ruined crane was the feel of Wren's shoulder pressed hard against theirs — not falling, holding — two liars keeping each other upright in six inches of freezing water.

The hull sighed beneath them, half-drained, half-drowned, done.

Sable looked at the wreck that was home, income, and escape all at once, and then at the kid still bailing out of pure stubbornness, and said the words they'd sworn off for seven years:

"Grab the chest. We're walking."
