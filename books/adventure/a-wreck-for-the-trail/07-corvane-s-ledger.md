## 7. Corvane's Ledger

The ledger had no name on its spine. That was deliberate. Corvane had learned, over thirty years in the trade of forgetting, that the most dangerous documents were the ones that announced themselves — the treaties with wax seals thick as coins, the maps with gold-leaf borders, the royal decrees filed in triplicate and archived in fireproof rooms. Those things shouted *I matter*. They invited attention. They made men curious.

His ledger was a plain merchant's book, bound in undyed calfskin, its pages ruled in faint gray lines, its entries written in a shorthand of his own devising that would read as inventory figures to anyone who stole it. *Twelve bales, river wool, delivered.* It had never once contained wool.

He sat with it open on the desk in his rented house above the Kestrian harbor town of Vell's Landing, a lamp turned low because the monsoon humidity made the paper swell and he preferred to work before the afternoon rains came up the valley. Outside, the river ran brown and swollen past the pilings, carrying with it the season's usual debris: fence rails, drowned goats, the occasional roof. Somewhere downriver, a ferryman was hauling in whatever the flood offered up for salvage.

Corvane did not know yet that this was relevant. He would know by nightfall.

He turned to the current page and reviewed his accounts the way other men reviewed their consciences — thoroughly, without flinching, and only alone.

*Item: the Ondmark survey, 1189. Three plates, one atlas, two witnesses. Settled.*

*Item: the Ashvale boundary dispute. The marsh reclassified as tidal flats; eleven families relocated under terms they believed were their own idea. Settled.*

*Item: the Vhalen materials. Outstanding.*

He dipped his pen, considered, and wrote nothing. Some entries deserved more thought than ink.

* * *

Corvane had been born in a country that no longer existed, which he regarded as the single most useful fact of his life. He was a child of Vhalen's last decade — old enough to remember the royal cartography house with its smell of size and cedar, young enough that when the annexations came he had no land to lose, no title to mourn, no grave to tend. His father had been a minor functionary in the survey corps, a man who spent his whole career measuring a nation and died owing money to it.

What Corvane inherited was not property. It was an understanding.

He understood that geography was not a fact. It was a claim. A mountain range meant nothing until someone drew it, and once drawn, it could be redrawn. Rivers moved; borders pretended not to notice. Whole peoples lived and died inside lines that some clerk had ruled with a straightedge after dinner. And every so often — rarely, but inevitably — a piece of terrain became so inconvenient that three governments would independently arrive at the same conclusion: *this must never be found again.*

That was when they came to him. Not with threats. With money, and with the particular relief of powerful men who have finally found someone willing to do the thing they cannot order done through channels. You cannot put an erasure in a budget line. You cannot commission it, or audit it, or deny it under oath. You can only pay a private man who keeps no records — or rather, who keeps records no one can read.

Corvane's genius, if he permitted himself the word, was patience. Amateurs burned things. Amateurs shot people and left bodies that became stories, and stories became investigations, and investigations became martyrs. Corvane had watched a hot-blooded colleague in Ondmark hang for exactly that error. No — you let time do the cutting. You bought the archive. You pensioned off the clerk. You waited for the last man who remembered the pass to die of age, or drink, or a bad winter, and then you bought the estate sale, and what remained you scattered so finely across the world's paper markets that no scholar could ever assemble it. Erasure was not destruction. Erasure was *dispersal*. A thing destroyed left a hole, and holes showed. A thing dispersed left nothing at all.

For twenty-two years, the Vhalen high pass had been his masterpiece.

* * *

It had begun, as these things did, with a treaty.

Three of them, actually, signed in the two years after Vhalen fell — the Kestrian–Ondmark Accord, the Borderlands Demarcation, and the quiet little understanding between Kestria and the southern principalities that no one called a treaty because calling it one would have required publishing it. Together they divided the former Vhalen frontier into a sensible arrangement of buffer zones, tariff corridors, and mutually convenient ambiguities. The arrangement held because none of the signatories wanted the mountains. The mountains were expensive. The mountains were full of people who had not agreed to be divided.

And threaded through those mountains, known to perhaps forty living souls and mapped in detail by exactly one, was the pass.

Corvane had seen the surveyor's preliminary notes before the end — his father had brought them home, wrapped around bread, during the last winter of the war. Even then, half-finished, they were extraordinary. A route through the high glacier, timed to the calving season, that bypassed every checkpoint, every garrison, every customs house on the entire frontier. A road out of Kestria that no government controlled, because no government knew it existed. The surveyor — a careful, brilliant man named Aldous Wren, though Corvane had not learned the name until years later — had hidden it inside a legitimate glacier study, encoded, folded into measurements of ice that no minister would ever bother to read.

If that route became public, the treaties dissolved. Refugees would move where the checkpoints weren't. Smugglers would price the garrisons out of existence. Ondmark would discover that its western border was a suggestion, and Kestria would learn the same about its north, and within five years there would be a war, or worse — there would be *no* war, just a slow unravelling, borders going soft as wet paper while three chancelleries screamed at each other about whose fault the softness was.

So the client states paid. And Corvane collected the surveyor's papers, plate by plate, year by year, from estates and dead men's trunks and the descendants of the forty souls, most of whom had forgotten what their grandfathers' documents even depicted. He had gotten all but a handful. He had gotten, he believed, everything that mattered.

Then, six days ago, a harbor clerk in Vell's Landing — a man on Corvane's modest retainer for exactly this kind of watching — sent word that a salvaged chest bearing the Vhalen crest had come up out of the flood, and that a ferry guide had taken it aboard, and that the guide had then done something strange.

The guide had refused to sell it.

* * *

"Refused," said Master Hesk, the buyer, "outright. I offered four hundred crowns for the lot unopened. Then six hundred." He sat across the desk from Corvane, drying his soft hands, looking like a man who had been personally insulted by arithmetic. "Nobody refuses six hundred. Not ferrymen. Ferrymen are the greediest creatures on water."

"What did he look like?" Corvane asked.

"Tall. Weathered. Kept his hood up even below decks. Hands like rope." Hesk frowned. "There was something in the way he stood. Like a man standing at attention who hates standing at attention."

Corvane turned a page of the ledger, though he wasn't reading it. "Did he give a name?"

"Gave a false one. I've handled enough false names to know the cut of them." Hesk hesitated. "There's more. I had a look at his boat registration. The clerk pulled the harbor rolls. The boat's registered to a Sable Marsh."

"And?"

"And the registration is nine years old, and the man on the roll is described as having a scar through the left eyebrow, and our ferryman has that scar, and here is the interesting part—" Hesk leaned forward, enjoying himself, which Corvane forgave him because Hesk was useful, "—the harbor master at Greywater keeps a blacklist, unofficial, of guides barred from the mountain routes. Nine years back there was an incident. A guided party lost on the Serel Ridge. Seven dead. The guide signed the incident report under the name Marsh and was struck from the rolls in both Kestria and Ondmark. The description matches. The scar matches. And a guide who's been blacklisted for nine years has exactly one reason to be working a flood-season ferry run with his hood up."

Corvane set down his pen.

A disgraced guide. A man erased — not by Corvane's craft, but by his own misfortune, which was almost elegant. Such men were predictable in one respect only: they wanted nothing to do with anything that mattered. Which meant that if he was holding Vhalen plates and refusing to sell, he either didn't know what he had —

— or he suspected, and was frightened, and fright made men do one of two things. Run, or bargain.

Either way, the situation had passed beyond the competence of Master Hesk, whose talents ended at offering sums of money to greedy people.

"You've done well," Corvane said, and Hesk visibly swelled. "One more task. I want eyes on the boat tonight. Quiet ones. If the guide moves, I want to know the direction before his wake settles. Nothing more. Do not approach him, do not board the boat, do not speak to the harbormaster. If he runs, let him run. Running tells us more than talking would."

Hesk left. Corvane sat for a long moment with the lamp guttering, listening to the rain begin against the shutters, and allowed himself the small pleasure of admiration. A blacklisted guide pulling a Vhalen chest out of a flooded river — the world had a sense of humor, occasionally, though it was a cruel one, and Corvane had long ago stopped laughing at it.

* * *

The hunters arrived after dark, two of them, dripping rain onto the flagstones of his study like a pair of ill-omened dogs.

They were brothers, though no document said so — Dree and Anselm, raised in the borderlands, deniable in every jurisdiction that mattered. Dree was the patient one: a lean, gray man who could sit in a market square for four days watching a door and come away knowing everyone who used it. Anselm was the other kind of patient, the kind that looked like violence waiting for permission. Between them they had retired three archivists, a defrocked priest, and an entire family of map-sellers whose stock in trade had grown inconvenient. None of it had ever been traced to anyone, because there was no one to trace. They worked for a ledger that did not exist.

Corvane laid out the facts for them without hurry: the chest, the crest, the guide, the probable identity, the blacklist. He watched their faces as he spoke. Dree's went still and attentive, the stillness of a man loading a crossbow behind his eyes. Anselm simply smiled, which Corvane noted and did not return.

"The guide may already have disposed of the plates," Dree said. "Sold them piecemeal. Brass fittings first, paper after."

"He hasn't. He refused an offer for the unopened chest, which means he understands the contents are worth more than the container, which means he's opened it, which means he knows what the seal is." Corvane rose and stood at the window. Below, the river churned past the pilings, brown and muscular, dragging the drowned season toward the sea. "But knowing what a thing is and knowing what it means are different educations. He is a guide, not an archivist. My guess is fear, not ambition. He'll run upriver, away from the ports, away from the rolls. Small towns. Backwater moorings. Places where a man with a false name can buy fuel and silence."

"And when we find him?"

Corvane turned from the window. Rain crawled down the glass between himself and the lamplit room, breaking the hunters into pieces and putting them back together.

"Recover the plates. All of them. Every scrap of Vhalen paper in that boat, and anything copied from it — I want his belongings searched for tracing paper, for notes, for charcoal rubbings. If he has told anyone, I want to know who, and I want to know it before you act on it. A man alone is a problem. A man with a confederate is a thread, and I pull threads until the spool comes apart."

"And the guide?" Anselm asked. There was nothing eager in his voice, which was the frightening thing about it. Just procedure.

Corvane considered. Nine years ago, a party had died on the Serel Ridge, and a guide had signed a report and vanished into the borderlands, and somewhere in that story there might be a loose end — a client, a family, a truth that a clever investigator could use. But that was secondary. What mattered was simpler.

"If he gives up the plates without trouble, he becomes a poor ferryman with a bad memory, and we leave him his memory and his poverty. If he runs with them—" Corvane paused, weighing the sentence the way he weighed all his sentences, for accuracy rather than mercy. "Then he has chosen to be a holder of Vhalen materials, and holders of Vhalen materials do not survive the season. Make it look like the river. The river is doing excellent work this year. No one will question another drowning."

Dree nodded slowly. "And if he's sold a plate already? To a smelter, a junk dealer, some dockside collector?"

"Then buy it back at any price and burn the seller's memory of the transaction. Money first. Only if money fails—" Corvane returned to his desk, opened the ledger, and took up his pen. "Only if money fails does any of the rest become necessary. We are not butchers. We are editors."

He wrote the entry in his gray shorthand, unhurried, the rain keeping time against the shutters:

*Item: Vhalen materials, partial recovery, one chest, one custodian. Dispatched: two. Expected settlement: before the melt.*

* * *

They left within the hour, upstream, in a flat-bottomed skiff with no name painted on it, running without lights through the rising flood. Corvane watched from the window until the dark took them, then sat back down and did what he always did after dispatching work: he planned for failure.

If the guide fled alone, the matter was simple. If the guide had help — and there had been a second set of footprints on the dock mud, Hesk reported, small ones, light, belonging to someone who boarded the boat and did not register as crew — then the matter grew a thread, and threads were pulled.

A companion. Perhaps a lover, perhaps a partner, perhaps a child. Someone who knew the plates existed. Corvane added a line to the entry, small and neat:

*Custodian possibly not alone. Identify second party.*

He blotted the page, closed the ledger, and banked the lamp. Outside, the monsoon settled in for the night, hammering the roof, swelling the river, doing the patient destructive work that Corvane had built his life upon and never once been paid for directly. The flood gave up its dead to whoever was watching. By morning, somewhere upriver, a disgraced guide would make a decision about speed and direction, and that decision would be legible to men trained to read wakes the way priests read scripture.

Corvane slept well. He always did. Guilt, in his experience, was a luxury of people who believed the world could be put back together.

Upstream, in a leaking boat with a stolen name and a dead nation's secrets in its hold, the guide was already moving.
