## 8. Salt and Rust

The smelting town of Karrow's Reach had died twice: once when the furnaces went cold, and once again when the river changed course and left it stranded a half-mile from the water like a beached whale nobody wanted to claim. What remained was rust and geometry — the skeletons of foundries, chimneys standing like broken fingers against a bruised sky, and streets paved with slag that glittered black-green when the rain hit it right.

Sable had known about the place for years, back when knowing things was their trade. Guides kept mental maps of every ruin along the waterways because ruins meant shelter, and shelter meant options, and options were the only currency that never devalued.

They brought the boat in through a breach in the old canal embankment where the floodwater had cut itself a shortcut into the town's drowned lower quarter, and they tied off beneath the ribs of a collapsed crane house whose roof still held, mostly, over a slip that was mostly dry.

"Charming," Wren said, looking up at the crane house with its ceiling of hanging moss and bird nests.

"It's dry," Sable said. "That's the whole review."

The hull had been complaining for two days — a soft, wet insistence from below the waterline that the pump answered only grudgingly. In the gray light under the crane house, Sable pulled the floorboards and found what they'd feared: three seams opened along the port side, the caulking gone to stringy rot, one plank sprung enough to slide a knife blade under. The monsoon hadn't done this alone. The boat was old, and old boats failed the way old people did — slowly, then all at once, usually at night, usually somewhere inconvenient.

"We're not making the upper river on this," Sable said. "Not without patches. Real ones, not rags and pitch."

Wren crouched beside them, sleeves pushed up, hair stuck to her forehead in the way it got when she'd been worrying without admitting it. "There's nothing between here and the weir towns. Nothing with a smith, anyway."

"There's here." Sable nodded toward the far bank of the dead canal, where a long low building squatted under a sagging roof — the old plate-works, and beyond it, if Sable remembered right, a salvage yard run by a woman named Odile Ferrant who had once been the town's chief roller and now was its entire population, give or take the gulls.

"You know someone here?" Wren asked.

"I knew someone here. Eight years ago." Sable climbed out onto the slick stone. "She'll remember me or she won't. Either way she'll want payment."

"With what? We have almost nothing."

Sable stood in the shallows a moment, water pulling at their boots, and did not look at the chest. That was the trick of it — you could hide anything as long as you never looked directly at it.

"We have materials," Sable said.

* * *

Odile Ferrant had grown broader and grayer and no softer. She met them at the gate of the salvage yard with a pry bar held casually, the way other people held opinions, and studied Sable's face for a long ten seconds before anything moved in it.

"Well," she said finally. "I thought you were dead. Or worse, famous."

"Neither took."

"Hm." She lowered the pry bar and jerked her chin toward the yard. "In. Don't touch the copper stock. And whoever's skulking behind you can come too, if they've got hands."

Wren had hands. Within an hour she had them black to the wrist, hauling scrap iron while Odile argued price with Sable across a workbench scarred by forty years of cutting torches. The arithmetic was ugly. Hull patches meant copper sheet or lead, properly annealed, plus oakum, plus pitch, plus Odile's time — and Odile named her prices the way she'd once rolled plate: flat, heavy, and non-negotiable.

"That's robbery," Sable said.

"That's the market. You want cheap, float your leak down to Vessing and wait out the season. If it floats that far." Odile spat a stream of brown leaf-juice into a bucket. "You always were tight with coin, Fairweather. I remember that about you. Tight with everything."

The name landed the way it always landed — like a hand pressed briefly on the back of the neck. *Fairweather.* They'd given Odile the name eight years ago, back when using it had still been safe, or had seemed so. Sable let it pass without flinching, which was its own kind of skill.

"I can pay part in labor," Sable said. "And part in trade goods."

"What trade goods?"

Sable reached into their coat and drew out the fragment wrapped in oilcloth. Not the key plate — never the key plate; that one lived sewn into the lining of the coat itself, flat against Sable's ribs where it rode like a second heartbeat. This was a corner piece, cracked diagonally, bearing perhaps a third of a contour field and the edge of a grid line. A fragment. Enough to show what it was. Not enough to matter.

Odile unwrapped it on the bench and went very still.

For a moment the only sound in the yard was rain ticking on tin roofing and the gulls arguing out over the dead canal. Then Odile said, quietly, "That's Vhalen work."

"You recognize it?"

"I rolled survey plates for eleven years before the furnaces died. You don't forget the hand." She turned the fragment toward the light. Her thumb hovered over the engraved contours without touching them, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping animal. "This is pre-Fall engraving. Fine cut. Nobody cuts like this anymore — it costs too much patience." She looked up. "Where did you get it?"

"The river gave it to me."

"The river," Odile repeated, "gave you a sealed chest stamped with a dead nation's crest, and you're selling me a corner of it for hull patches." She laughed, but there was no humor in it, just a kind of weary respect. "You always did undersell yourself, Fairweather."

"Is it enough?"

"For the patches, the oakum, my time, and my silence?" Odile rewrapped the fragment with surprising gentleness. "It's enough. It would be enough if I sold it on, and I might, someday, when I'm desperate — but I'm not desperate yet, and you're not worth betraying yet." She pushed off the bench. "Come back tomorrow evening. Bring your girl. She's got good hands and bad posture, and both are fixable."

"She's not—"

"I've got eyes," Odile said. "Whatever she is to you, bring her."

* * *

They worked through the next day and half the night. Odile ran the forge herself, feeding it scrap and salvaged charcoal, and the three of them fell into a rhythm that felt older than any of them: heat, hammer, quench, fit. Wren proved quick at the fiddly work — driving oakum into seams with a caulking iron, her small hands better suited to it than Sable's rope-hardened ones. She worked in silence mostly, but it was a different silence than the one she'd carried aboard. Less guarded. Once, driving a seam near the bow, she started humming something tuneless and stopped herself as if embarrassed, and Sable pretended not to have heard, and after a minute she started again.

Sable hated how much they noticed that.

The patches went on true. By lamplight, with the boat hauled clear, the new copper gleamed along the port side like scales on a mended snake. Odile walked the length of it twice, arms folded, and pronounced it good for five years or one bad season, whichever came first.

While Wren cleaned tools, Odile drew Sable aside under the eave, out of the rain, and spoke low.

"A man came through yesterday," she said. "Before you. Asking after traffic on the river."

Sable kept their hands still. "People come through."

"This one wasn't people. He was polite the way a bill is polite. Soft voice, good boots, clothes too clean for the road. Said he was looking for a ferryman and a young woman traveling together, said he represented parties interested in recovering lost property." Odile's eyes stayed on the middle distance. "He showed me a sketch. Ink drawing, well done. Your face, Fairweather. Older than the last time I saw you, but yours."

The rain filled the pause.

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I see maybe four boats a month and none of them carry anyone worth describing." Odile finally turned her head. "I'm not doing it for love. But I'll tell you what I told him, and then I'll tell you what I didn't, and you can do your own sums. He offered me money for word of you. Good money. He'll offer more next time, and there will be a next time, because men like that don't stop at one town." She shrugged, vast shoulders rising and falling. "I'm telling you because you paid fair and because I hate clean boots. That's the whole of my virtue. Spend it wisely."

"Which way did he go?"

"Upstream. Toward the weir." She paused. "He asked one more thing. Asked if anyone had come through selling old metal. Survey metal." Her gaze flicked, brief and sharp, to the shed where the rest of the chest sat under sacking. "I said the river gives nothing to nobody."

Sable breathed out slowly. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Move." Odile clapped them on the shoulder hard enough to stagger. "And Fairweather — whatever you're carrying that a man with clean boots wants this badly — I've seen that look before. On people carrying less than you. Most of them made it further than you will, because they told the truth to the person sharing their boat."

Then she went inside and shut the door, and the conversation was over.

* * *

They cast off at first light, running the flooded canal back out through the embankment breach into the main channel, the river high and brown and muscular with the week's rain. The patched hull held. Sable felt it holding — the pump silent for the first hour, then working lazily, then not at all — and something in their chest unclenched that had been clenched since the day the chest came out of the willows.

Wren sat amidships with the decoy plates spread on the thwart under an oilskin, copying cipher notes into her father's little book, cross-referencing symbols with the fierce quiet concentration she brought to everything that mattered to her. Sable steered and watched the water and did not think about clean boots.

Except they did think about clean boots. All day. The hunter was upstream, Odile had said. Upstream was where they were going. Which meant either the man had passed through Karrow's Reach and continued on, guessing wrong about their route — or he knew exactly what lay upstream worth guarding, and was waiting.

Either way, Sable reasoned, there was no version of events where telling Wren helped. Wren would want to turn back, or confront him, or — worse — start asking why a stranger was hunting them, and questions had a way of walking backward down the trail to the chest, and from the chest to the plates, and from the plates to the one Sable kept hidden against their ribs. Wren trusted them. It was a fragile, new-built trust, mortared with bailing water and shared bread, and it would not bear the weight of *a man is hunting us and I decided not to mention it.*

So Sable said nothing. It was, they told themselves, protection. It was, some colder part of them admitted, habit. You hoarded what mattered. You kept the true map sewn in your coat and handed people corners. You survived alone because alone was the only arrangement that couldn't be betrayed from inside.

They told themselves this all day, and almost believed it by dusk, when they made camp in the lee of a shattered lock-gate and ate rice with dried fish and Wren fell asleep mid-sentence over her cipher book, pencil still in her hand.

Almost.

Because Wren woke in the gray before dawn, as she always did, and Sable was already awake, as they always were, and the river mist lay thick enough to drink, and into that mist Wren said, without preamble:

"When were you going to tell me about the man?"

Sable went cold from the boots up. "What man?"

"The man asking after us at Karrow's Reach." Wren sat up. Her voice wasn't angry. That was the worst part — it was level and careful and awake, the voice of someone who had been lying awake doing arithmetic. "Odile told you last night. I saw her pull you aside. I saw your face after. And this morning you checked the treeline twice before I'd finished lashing the cargo, and you've been steering us through the slack water on the far bank all day instead of the current, which is slower, and you only do that when you don't want to meet anyone coming downstream." She hugged her knees. "I grew up with a cartographer, Sable. I read routes the way you read water. You've been avoiding something since we left the yard, and last night is when it started."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere upstream a bird cried out, thin and questioning.

"Wren—"

"Don't." The word cracked, just slightly, the levelness failing for one syllable. "Don't do the thing where you decide what I can survive knowing. My father did that. He hid his work inside a glacier survey because he decided what the world could survive knowing, and the world erased him so thoroughly that his own daughter spent six years thinking he was a minor clerk who died of fever." Her eyes were bright and furious in the pre-dawn gray. "I am not asking you to tell me everything. I know you keep things. I've watched you keep the best plate hidden even from me — yes, I know about that too, I've seen you sleep with your coat on in a hot cabin, don't insult us both. I'm asking you to tell me when we're hunted. Because I'd rather die informed than live managed."

The river ran past them, brown and patient, carrying everything downstream eventually.

Sable found that their mouth was dry. "A man came to the yard the day before us," they said at last. "Clean boots, soft voice. Looking for a ferryman and a young woman. He had a sketch of my face. Odile sent him upstream and lied to him, and then told me, and I—" They stopped. Started again, because Wren deserved the whole shape of it, ugly parts included. "I decided you'd be safer not knowing. And I decided that partly because it was true and mostly because it was easier. If you knew, you'd ask questions, and the answers lead to places I don't want you poking. So I hoarded it. Like I hoard the plate. Like I hoard everything." They looked out at the mist. "It's not a virtue. I know that. It's just the only way I've stayed alive, and I don't know how to put it down."

Wren was quiet for a long time. The sky lightened by degrees, the mist turning from gray to pearl.

"He had a sketch of your face," she said finally. "Of *your* face. Not mine. Not ours." She unfolded from her blanket, moving with sudden purpose, and began striking camp. "Corvane doesn't care about me. I'm a loose thread he hasn't bothered to clip. He cares about the ferryman who pulled the chest out of the river — which means he knows about the chest, which means he knows what's in it, or close enough." She threw Sable their pack. "Which means the question isn't whether you should have told me. The question is what he knows that we don't, and we're floating straight toward him."

Sable caught the pack. "The weir's a day upstream."

"Then we have a day to decide whether we go through it or around it." Wren stood at the bow, small and sharp-eyed in the growing light, and for the first time since the dock at Vessing she looked less like a stowaway than a navigator. "And Sable — next time something hunts us, you wake me. Whatever you think I can't survive knowing, I promise you I've already survived worse. I survived being erased."

She turned upstream. Sable pushed off from the lock-gate, and the river took them, and ahead, somewhere in the mist, the weir waited — and, Sable suspected, so did the man with clean boots, patient as rust, certain as salt.
