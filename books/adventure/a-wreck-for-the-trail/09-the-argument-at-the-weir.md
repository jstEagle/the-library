## 9. The Argument at the Weir

**Sable**

The weir announced itself an hour before they saw it — a long, low thunder rolling up the valley like a wagon of stones being unloaded forever. Sable had run this stretch of the Vessing twice in better years and remembered it as a staircase of water: three stone steps dropped across the river's throat, built by some dead empire to hold back floodwater, now held together mostly by habit and moss.

They tied off below a leaning alder while the light was still gray, and Sable went forward on foot to look.

The portage path was gone. Not overgrown — *gone*, drowned under two feet of brown water that poured through what had once been a towpath cut. The old fisherman's stair on the far bank still stood, but its lower steps were slick with foam and half-submerged, and above them the carrying trail vanished into a wall of bramble that no one had cut back since the last war, whichever that had been.

"Bad?" Wren asked, coming up behind with the plates wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a sleeping child.

"The river's high. We can carry around if we climb the bluff on the left — half a day lost, maybe more, and we'd be visible from the road the whole way." Sable crouched, touched the current with two fingers, reading it the way Wren read her father's cipher. "Or we shoot the middle step and pray."

"You've done it before."

"In lower water. With a boat that didn't leak."

Wren looked at the weir the way she looked at everything — as if it were a document to be interpreted. "The middle step," she said. "We'd lose too much time going around. And time is the one thing Corvane's men can't make more of either."

Sable didn't answer right away, because there was something else — something that had been sitting in their chest since the smelting town like a swallowed hook, and the sight of the drowned portage path pulled it up into their throat. They were out of river. Whatever happened next happened here, or not at all.

"Wren." They turned. "Before we go through anything, you should know I traded a plate fragment at Rusthollow."

"I know."

Sable blinked. "You—"

"I counted them before we left the lock-gate. You think I haven't been counting since the dock? I grew up in a survey office. Numbers are how I pray." Her voice wasn't angry. That was somehow worse. "I want to know which one you gave away."

"A corner plate. Marginal survey marks. Nothing legible without the key plates."

"Nothing legible *to you*." Wren set the oilcloth bundle down on a flat stone with exaggerated care, the way people do when they are trying very hard not to throw something. "Sable. My father spent eleven years hiding a pass inside a glacier survey so carefully that three governments' worth of archivists never found it. Do you imagine he made the marginal plates worthless? The corner fragments are where he hid the *checksums*. If Corvane's man has even one, and he has my father's published work — which he does, because Corvane owns every archive worth owning — then he can start narrowing down which of these plates matter."

The river roared below them, patient as rust.

"Why didn't you tell me?" Sable said.

"Because I watched you lie about it for two days. Because I wanted to see how long it would take." She laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "That's the cartographer in me. I map things. Apparently I map people too, and you are the most dishonest piece of terrain I have ever surveyed."

* * *

**Wren**

She hadn't meant to say it like that. Or maybe she had. There was a version of the fight she'd rehearsed in the dark hours of the night watch — measured, reasonable, the kind of argument her father would have approved of, all evidence and proportion. And there was the version that came out instead, because she was tired, and scared, and because she had spent nine days watching Sable Fairweather hoard trust the way other people hoarded coin, giving out exactly as much as survival required and not one grain more.

Sable took it without flinching. That was the infuriating thing about them. Every accusation slid off that weathered face like rain off waxed canvas.

"You're right," Sable said. "I lied. I told you the trade was for rope and pitch. It was for rope, pitch, and hull patches, and I kept the key plate back because—" They stopped. Started again. "Because everything I've ever carried that mattered got someone killed."

"There it is," Wren said quietly. "Say the rest."

"The rest of what?"

"The rest of the story. The party that died. The report you signed under another name. You've fed me crumbs for nine days — enough to keep me loyal, not enough to let me actually know who I'm bailing water beside." She stepped closer, boots sliding on wet stone. "I gave you my father. I gave you the cipher. I gave you the truth that I'm a runaway apprentice with a forged travel writ and a bounty notice in three provinces. And you've given me a name that might not even be yours and a past you ration out like hardtack."

"That's not fair."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is for people who aren't being hunted." She gestured upstream, toward the thunder of the weir, toward whatever waited beyond it. "Corvane doesn't care whether you're honest, Sable. He cares whether the plates burn. So let me ask you the question you've been avoiding since the dock: when this is over — if we live — what happens to my father's map?"

Sable was silent for a long moment. The mist moved between them.

"It gets destroyed," Sable said finally. "Or buried. A route like that, uncontrolled, unguarded — do you know what it becomes? A smuggler's road. An army's shortcut. Every desperate person in Kestria funneling through a glacier pass that kills half of them, and every government on the map with a reason to close it permanently. Your father hid that route to protect it. Publishing it would be the one thing he'd never forgive."

"He hid it to *finish* it!" The words tore out of her before she could shape them. "He died with the survey incomplete — do you understand? Eleven years of work and the last plates were never verified, never walked end to end. He used to tell me a map isn't finished until someone stands on every line of it. He never got to stand on his own pass. If we destroy those plates, he stays erased forever, same as Vhalen, same as—" Her voice cracked. She hated that. "Same as everyone Corvane has ever been paid to delete."

"And if we publish it, people die on it."

"People die on *every* route! People die staying home! My mother died of a fever in a city that no longer exists because a treaty needed her country to be a memory!" Wren heard herself shouting over the roar of the weir and couldn't stop. "You talk about survival like it's a religion — stay small, stay silent, touch nothing that matters. But silence is just a slower way of dying, Sable. Silence is how whole nations get erased. One quiet decision at a time."

"And your loud decisions get us both killed by Thursday."

"Better than dying of caution at eighty!"

They stood chest to chest on the wet stone, breath ragged, and the strange thing — the thing Wren would remember afterward — was that underneath the fury there was relief. Nine days of polite cooperation, of two liars circling each other with sharpened courtesy, and now at last the knives were out, and it felt almost like honesty. Almost like home.

* * *

**Sable**

Later — much later, in the bad hours — Sable would try to work out when they'd first seen the glint on the bluff. Probably during the shouting. Probably while Wren was telling them that silence was a slow death, some part of Sable's old guide-mind had already registered the wrongness: a patch of green on the eastern bluff that didn't move with the wind, then did move, then stopped again.

Hunters. Two of them, maybe three, working along the ridgeline with the unhurried confidence of people who knew their quarry was busy arguing.

"Down," Sable said, and hauled Wren behind the alder.

"What—"

"Bluff. East side. Don't look."

Wren went very still. Sable watched her do the arithmetic — the same arithmetic Sable had done — and saw her arrive at the sum: the argument had been loud, the mist was thinning, and the only escape routes were the drowned portage, the bramble-choked carry, or the weir itself.

"They followed the river," Wren whispered. "How?"

"Rusthollow. The hunter asking questions — he didn't need answers. He just needed to know which direction we went when he left." Sable peered through the alder's curtain. The figures on the bluff had stopped moving. Waiting, or signaling. "Going around means climbing into them. The carry trail is a killing ground — single file, blind corners, nowhere to run."

"So we go through."

"We *shoot* the middle step. In this water. In a boat that leaks." Sable closed their eyes and read the river one more time, the way they used to read mountains, back when reading them well had meant something. Three steps. The top two were broken water — survivable, ugly. The third was the killer: a twelve-foot drop into a recirculating hole that had eaten at least two barges in Sable's memory, its boil line marked by a fence of pale foam and one dead cow turning slowly, with terrible patience, ten feet below the crest.

But the hole was fed from the left. The right third of the drop, where the old sluice-gate had collapsed, ran steeper and cleaner — a chute of green water no wider than the boat, punching straight through into the tailrace below.

There was a line. There was always a line. That was the whole of guiding, the secret Sable had spent seven years pretending wasn't true: not that danger could be avoided, but that it could be *read*.

"Sable." Wren's hand found their sleeve. "Whatever you're deciding — decide fast. They're coming down the bluff."

She was right. The green patches were descending now, quick and competent, angling for the carry trail. Ten minutes, maybe less.

Sable looked at Wren — this furious, frightened, brilliant girl who had called them a liar to their face and been entirely correct — and heard their own voice from minutes ago: *everything I've ever carried that mattered got someone killed.* But that wasn't quite true either, was it. What had gotten people killed was the report signed under another name, the silence bought with a client family's safety, the years of small cowardly erasures dressed up as survival. Wren was right about that too, damn her. Silence was a slow death. Sable had been dying of it for seven years and calling it prudence.

"New rule," Sable said, hauling the painter free. "No more lies between us. Starting now, starting ugly. I signed that report because the man who hired me begged me to — his daughter was on the mountain, his company was finished otherwise, and I was young and flattered and broke. Four people died because the route was sabotaged, and I let the world believe it was my error because the truth would have ruined a family that had already lost enough. That's the whole ugly ledger. Judge it however you like."

Wren stared at them. "That's—"

"The truth. Late, but true. Now bail like your father's name depends on it, because it does." Sable shoved the boat out into the current. "Middle step. Right-hand chute. When I say *lean*, you lean like your life is the ballast, because it is."

The first step took them before Wren could answer — a lurch, a wall of brown water over the bow, the world reduced to noise and cold. Sable worked the sweep with both hands, feeling the river's weight shift across the blade, reading the second step's chaos for the seam of smoother water that threaded between two submerged stones. Found it. Lost it. Found it again.

The third step rose ahead like a verdict.

"Right!" Sable roared. "*Lean!*"

The boat swung, hung for one impossible moment on the crest with the dead cow turning below and the whole roaring weight of the flooded Vessing pressing at its stern — and then the chute took them, green water, clean water, a drop that lasted three seconds and a lifetime, and they punched through the boil line sideways, shipping half the river, and came out spinning in the calmer water below with the weir thundering behind them like applause.

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Wren started to laugh — a wild, soaked, disbelieving sound — and Sable realized they were laughing too, both of them drenched and gasping and alive, and that somewhere in the last hundred yards the argument had stopped mattering and the honesty had started.

"They'll come down the carry and be stuck above the weir till they find a bridge," Sable said, steering for the far bank. "A day behind. Maybe two."

Wren wrung out her hair, still laughing shakily. "So we bought ourselves a day by nearly drowning."

"We bought a day by trusting each other with the oars. The drowning was incidental." Sable glanced at her, and found the words rising before they could stop them — words seven years overdue. "Wren. About the map. When we cross the pass — if we cross it — I'll help you publish it. All of it. Under your father's name."

She went still. The mist drifted between them.

"Not because I've stopped believing it's dangerous," Sable said. "It is. Everything worth doing is. But because you were right — silence is how things get erased, and I've been erased long enough for one lifetime. I won't do that to him. Or to you."

Wren looked at them for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Sable's face seemed to settle something. "Then we'd better survive the monsoon," she said, "because the melt season opens in nineteen days, and my father's cipher says the pass window is shorter than that."

Behind them, faint through the roar of the weir, came a sound that froze the laughter out of both of them: a whistle. Two notes, repeated. The hunters, signaling — not downstream, where the carry trail lay.

Upstream. Toward the ridge. Toward a crossing they shouldn't have known existed.

Someone had told them about the bluff path. Someone ahead of them, on this side of the weir, already knew exactly where the river was taking them next.
