## 10. Monsoon Country

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, sideways and in bulk, as if the sky had decided to relocate its entire contents into the river valley at once. Wren sat under the boat's patched canvas awning with her father's cipher pages spread across her knees, weighting the corners with brass fittings and a tin cup, and watched Sable Fairweather steer through weather that should have stopped them three days ago.

"You're holding it upside down," she said.

"I'm holding it however I want." Sable didn't turn. Their hands moved on the tiller with the small constant corrections of someone who had made peace with chaos long ago. "It's a code. Codes don't have an up."

"They do when they're my father's codes." Wren turned the page back around. "He drew everything from north-up because he said a mapmaker who loses her bearings once will lose them twice. The cipher follows the same rule. The key glyph always points to the top of the plate."

Sable glanced back — just long enough for Wren to catch the expression, that particular flat skepticism they wore like oilskin — and then returned to the river.

"Show me again."

That was the thing about Sable that Wren was still learning how to hold: they complained about everything and learned everything. They called the cipher witchcraft and then stayed up past midnight tracing glyphs by lamplight until their eyes went red. They called Wren's father a dead man with too many secrets and then asked questions about him with a gentleness they never used on anything else.

Wren smoothed the page. Rain drummed the canvas overhead like fingers on a desk, impatient, counting.

"Start here. The anchor mark. In his survey work it means *fixed point* — something that doesn't move no matter what the season does. In the cipher it means *this is true*. Everything after it is built on it. If you find an anchor mark, you can trust what follows."

"And if you don't?"

"Then it's misdirection. He hid things inside other things. A false route drawn beautifully, so anyone who stole the plates would follow it into a crevasse field and never be heard from again." She said it lightly. It wasn't light. Her father had designed traps for men like Corvane before Corvane had ever heard of Vhalen, and now those traps were the only part of him still walking around in the world, wearing Wren's hands.

Sable was quiet for a while. The river ran brown and swollen, carrying whole trees, half a fence, something that might once have been a roof. On either bank the drowned willows stood to their shoulders in water, and beyond them the hills had gone soft and green and dripping, country that looked like it had been invented specifically to kill travelers.

"Your father," Sable said finally. "Did he teach you this on purpose? The cipher?"

"He taught me letters first. Numbers. How to hold a chain and count paces without losing my place. The cipher came last — he said I'd know when I needed it." Wren looked at her own hands, ink-stained, rope-scarred from the last week of hauling lines in flood current. "I thought needing it would mean he was gone. I didn't think it would mean everyone else was gone too."

The tiller creaked. Sable said nothing, which from Sable was nearly a kindness.

* * *

By the fourth day the monsoon had stopped being weather and become a country of its own. They traveled in it the way you travel through hostile territory: short runs between shelter, long hours anchored in the lee of bluffs or ruined bridges, sleeping in shifts because the river rose at night and didn't always rise politely.

It was during one of those anchored hours — rain hammering, the boat tied to a stone mooring post that had belonged to some empire or other, both of them wedged under the awning sharing a pot of tea that tasted mostly of the river — that Sable began teaching Wren water.

"Look at the surface," Sable said. "Not the waves. Under the waves."

"It's all waves."

"Underneath them. See where the current goes smooth and glassy in a line, there?" They pointed with two fingers. "That's fast water over deep channel. That's your road. Now look left — where it goes rough and choppy with no wind to blame."

"Shallows?"

"Rocks just under the surface. Water tells you everything if you stop looking at what it's doing and start asking why it's doing it." Sable took a swallow of tea. "Same as people."

"That's the most words you've said all day."

"Don't get used to it."

But Wren had already gotten used to it, which frightened her more than the hunters did. She had spent a year running — from the guild hall, from the men who'd come asking about her father's papers, from every town where someone might recognize the surname — and in a year of running she had learned to need nothing from anyone. Need was a hook. Need was how they caught you.

And yet here she was, learning to read water from a disgraced guide who hoarded trust like a miser hoarded coin, and finding that she slept better when Sable was awake. That she'd started leaving the good biscuit on Sable's side of the tea tin without deciding to. That when Sable laughed — rare, brief, usually at their own expense — something in Wren's chest unclenched that had been clenched since the night her father's study burned.

She didn't name any of this. Naming things gave them edges, and edges could be cut.

* * *

The near-drowning happened on the sixth day, at a place Wren would later think of only as the bend, though it had probably had a hundred names in a hundred languages, none of which appeared on any map worth trusting.

They were running a stretch of flooded gorge because the alternative was a portage through country where, two days earlier, they'd found fresh hoofprints and a cold fire ring. The gorge was narrow, fast, and — Sable had said, jaw tight — survivable if the water behaved. The water did not behave.

It began well enough. Wren worked the pump while Sable steered, the hull groaning around its new patches, spray coming over the bow in sheets. Then the river bent hard against a cliff wall and the whole current leaned into the turn like a animal shouldering a gate, and the boat swung wide, and Wren saw it a half-second before Sable shouted: a strainer. A full-grown tree, swept down in some earlier flood, lodged against the rocks with its branches combing the surface — the kind of obstacle that didn't stop boats, it ate them.

"Stern! Hard over—"

Sable threw their weight against the tiller. The boat answered, but the current didn't care; it took the stern and swung it, and the canvas awning's forward line — the one Wren had been meaning to re-tie for two days — snapped taut around her ankle as the boom of the awning frame whipped across.

She went over the side the way you go over the side: completely, instantly, with no opinion in the matter.

The river closed over her head and became a single enormous hand. Cold went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. She was underwater, then above it, then underwater again, tumbling, and the line around her ankle hauled taut and dragged her backward toward the tree — toward the strainer, toward the branches that held everything they caught and let nothing go.

She got one breath. She used it to scream, which was stupid, which wasted it, and then the water took her under again and she felt the branches rake her shoulder, snag her jacket, begin the slow patient work of keeping her.

*Later*, she would remember thinking, absurdly, *later I'll map this reach and mark it in red.* Later. As if there were going to be one.

Then the line around her ankle went slack — cut, she'd learn afterward, Sable hacking through it with the boat knife in two strokes while the current tried to take them both — and hands closed on her collar, and she was hauled up through the brown roaring water into air and rain and Sable's face, white-lipped, furious, alive.

They were both in the water now. She realized that dimly: Sable had come in after her, tied to nothing, in flood current, which was the kind of thing guides died doing. Sable got an arm around her chest and swam for the downstream eddy behind the strainer's root mass, kicking hard, and the eddy caught them — slow water, spinning gently, a pocket the river had forgotten — and Sable dragged her onto the slick rock shelf at its heart and collapsed beside her, coughing, shaking.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The gorge roared past six feet away. Rain fell. Wren lay on her back on wet stone and breathed in great ragged pulls and listened to her own heart argue with itself.

"You cut the line," she managed finally.

"You were tangled in it."

"You came in after me. You weren't roped. That's—" Her voice cracked. "That's the first thing they teach you not to do. You told me. Go after one drowning person and you make two."

Sable stared up at the rain. Water ran off their face and into their collar. "Yes," they said.

"Why?"

A long silence. Long enough that Wren thought they wouldn't answer, that the question would go into the same locked room as everything else about Sable Fairweather's past.

"Because the boat doesn't row itself," Sable said at last, roughly, "and I wasn't going to sit in it alone."

Which was a lie. Wren knew it was a lie the way she knew a forged contour line — not because she could prove it, but because she'd spent her whole life around people who lied professionally, and she knew what lies sounded like when they were being used as doors instead of walls. Sable hadn't come into the water for the boat. Sable had come into the water because Wren had been in it, and because somewhere in the last week — in the bailing and the cipher lessons and the shared tea and the wordless watch rotations — something had grown between them that neither of them had planted on purpose and neither of them was willing to name.

So she didn't name it. She rolled her head sideways and looked at Sable, soaked and shivering and pretending not to be, and said:

"The good biscuit's yours from now on."

Sable huffed — almost a laugh, strangled halfway — and covered it with a cough. "Don't make it strange."

"Too late."

* * *

They recovered the boat a mile downstream, beached in a willow stand with half the river aboard and the pump working like a condemned prisoner. It took until dark to bail her out and rig a jury patch over the gouge the strainer had torn in the hull, and they worked side by side in the failing light without speaking much, passing tools and rags and the bail bucket back and forth with the easy rhythm of people who had done this together often enough to stop counting.

Wren thought about that rhythm while she worked. About how her father used to say that a survey team was measured not by its instruments but by whether the two members could hand each other a pole without looking. She and Sable had passed that test days ago. Maybe weeks. Maybe somewhere back at the smelting town, or the weir, or the first gray morning they'd woken cold in the same boat and split the last dry blanket without negotiating.

After dark they ate under the awning, dried to dampness at best, and Wren brought out the cipher pages because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Again," Sable said, before she offered. "From the anchor mark."

Wren blinked. "You hate the cipher."

"I hate not knowing things." Sable settled cross-legged opposite her, lamplight catching the hollows of their face. "If we're going to die on a glacier in two weeks, I want to die reading your father's handwriting properly. Not guessing."

So she taught. And this time Sable learned fast — faster than they pretended to, faster than pride allowed them to admit — and by midnight they were reading simple phrases aloud, halting but true, their finger moving down the columns of glyphs: *fixed point. This is true. The way is hidden in the ice. Trust the surveyor.*

Wren's throat tightened. *Trust the surveyor.* Her father had written that into the cipher key itself, buried in the practice passages, waiting years for someone to read it. Waiting, maybe, for exactly this: a stranger with nothing left to lose, and his daughter, reading his voice out loud in the rain a thousand miles from anywhere he'd ever stood.

"He'd have liked you," she said quietly. "I think. He liked stubborn people who pretended not to care."

Sable was silent a moment. "Nobody likes me."

"He would have. It's a very short list. You should feel honored."

Something moved across Sable's face — too quick to name, gone before it landed — and they looked away toward the black sheeting rain, and said, in a voice carefully stripped of anything at all:

"Get some sleep. We run the next reach at first light, before the water comes up."

Wren rolled into her blanket. But sleep came slowly, and when it came, it came easily, which was new. Somewhere in the dark the river kept talking to itself, and the boat rocked, and Sable sat up on watch with the cipher pages still open on their knees, studying them by lamplight long after they needed to.

In the morning, when they raised the anchor and pushed out into the brown flood, Wren spotted it first: far upstream, on the bluff above the gorge, a thin thread of smoke rising where no village was marked — and beneath it, small with distance, figures. Watching them go.

Three of them. Not moving. Just watching.

Sable saw them a moment later and said nothing, and Wren said nothing, and the boat slid on into the monsoon country with the current pulling hard toward the mountains — and the mountains, when the clouds tore briefly apart at midday, showed them their first glimpse of white: high, blue-shadowed, and waiting.
