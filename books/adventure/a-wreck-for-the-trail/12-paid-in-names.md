## 12. Paid in Names

Maren Oduya had buried three husbands, outlived two flood years, and been lied to by every kind of traveler the river carried, so when the tall guide and the thin girl came up her bank in the gray hour before dawn, dripping and asking for nothing, she knew at once they were lying about the nothing.

"You're Sable Fairweather," she said, before either of them had finished their first sentence.

The guide went very still. That stillness told her more than a name would have. People who were innocent of their reputations flinched; people who had made peace with them shrugged. This one did neither. This one stood like a door that had been barred from the inside for so long the hinges had rusted shut.

"I don't know what you've heard," the guide said.

"Everything," Maren said. "And half of it twice." She turned to the girl, who was shivering in a coat two sizes wrong for her, soaked to the collar, clutching a satchel like it held her own pulse. "And you'd be the cartographer's whelp. Wren."

The girl's chin came up. Good. Fear was useful; pride was more so.

Inside, Maren fed them rice porridge with river fish and did not ask a single question, which she knew from long practice would loosen their tongues faster than any interrogation. The guide ate like someone who had learned to eat fast and had forgotten how to eat slowly, and kept glancing at the door. The girl ate like someone who had not seen food in two days and was trying to pretend otherwise. Between them, on the bench, they left exactly one hand's width of space, and Maren, who had watched people fall in love and out of it for seventy years, marked that space and waited to see what would fill it.

"You know who we are," the guide said finally. "So you know what it costs to shelter us."

"I know what it costs to shelter anyone," Maren said. "The river doesn't care whose name is on your papers. It drowns us all at the same rate." She set down her bowl. "But you're right that I didn't take you in out of charity. Sit. Listen. Then decide whether you're the person I think you are."

* * *

The village of Serel's Landing was not on any map, and Maren had spent forty years making sure of it. Its people were the river's leavings: a deserter from the Ondmark cavalry who now kept bees; three families who had fled the Kestrian tithe wars with false names so worn they had become true ones; a midwife struck from the guild rolls for the crime of delivering a magistrate's bastard and refusing to drown it. They farmed the floodplain in the dry months and moved to the ridge in the wet ones, and no census-taker had ever found them, because no census-taker had ever thought to look for a village that did not want finding.

But the monsoon this year was a hungry one. The river had come up a hand's width every night for nine days, and the low trail to the ridge — the trail they used to move the children and the goats and the grandmother who refused to be left behind — would be under water within the week. After that, the only way up was the high path over Kettle Spine, and the high path was not a path. It was a rumor with rocks in it.

"We have six children with the lung-rot," Maren said. "The fever-medicine is at the mission station over the ridge, and the mission boat stopped coming when the water got rough. If they don't get the medicine and the dry air both, I will bury them by the next dark moon, and I have buried enough children for one life." She looked at the guide across the fire. "You came up my bank with rope on your shoulder and a boat that leaks like a confession. You've been guiding since before you could grow that beard, whatever name you've been hiding under. So. You'll take them over the Spine."

It was not a question, and she watched the guide understand that it was not a question, and she watched something else move under the stillness — something old and frightened that had nothing to do with the Spine at all.

"I'm out of the trade," Sable Fairweather said quietly.

"No one is out of the trade," Maren said. "The trade is out of you. It's in how you stepped onto my floor — heel first, testing the boards. It's in how you sat with your back to the wall and your eyes on both doors. You can bury a skill under ten years of ferry runs and fake names, but it doesn't die. It just waits." She leaned forward. "I don't care what you did or what they say you did. I care that my grandchildren live. Take them over the Spine, and you and the girl can stay until the water drops, and no one in Serel's Landing will ever have heard of Sable Fairweather. Refuse, and you can go back to your leaking boat and the river can have you, and I will still not have heard of Sable Fairweather, because you'll be at the bottom of it."

The girl — Wren — had gone rigid at the name, and Maren noted that too. The girl knew. The girl had known for some time, and had stayed anyway. That was its own kind of testimony.

"I'll need to see the Spine first," the guide said at last. "In daylight. Before I promise anything."

Maren smiled for the first time that morning. "Now that," she said, "is what a guide sounds like."

* * *

They went up to look at Kettle Spine in the afternoon, in the gap between rains, and Maren went with them because she was too old to pretend she wasn't curious and too proud to pretend she wasn't needed.

The Spine was a hogback of black shale and rotten limestone that ran two miles between the floodplain and the high meadow where the mission trail began. In dry years, goats crossed it and made it look easy. In wet years, it wept water from every seam, and the shale let go in slabs the size of coffin lids, and the locals called the crossing "paying the toll" and did not joke about it. There was a chute halfway along — the locals called it the Kettle's Spout — where all the seepage funneled into a single roaring vein, and below the Spout the trail crossed a slope of loose talus that had killed two strong men in Maren's memory, both of them proud of how well they knew mountains.

Sable Fairweather stood at the base of it for a long time without speaking, and Maren watched the guide's eyes do a thing she had seen only in the very best of the guides she had known in her ferry-running days: they did not look at the mountain. They looked at the water coming off it. Reading the seepage the way a priest reads scripture, tracing every vein, weighing every trickle, and somewhere behind those eyes a route was assembling itself out of nothing, line by line, the way it had done ten thousand times before the world decided this person was finished.

"There's a line," the guide said finally. "Left of the Spout, through the gray band — the shale there is bedded flat, it'll hold. Then we climb out of the drainage entirely and traverse above the talus on the limestone rib. It's exposed, but the rock's sound, and we stay out of the runout the whole way." A pause. "How many of the children can walk?"

"The two eldest can walk. The little ones will have to be carried or roped."

"Then we go slow, and we fix handlines on the rib before the children touch it. Three days of rigging. Four days of crossing, six children at a time." The guide turned and looked at Maren, and for a moment the rusted hinges of that door seemed to strain. "I can do it. But I want to be honest with you about what you're buying. I haven't led anyone over anything in nine years. The last party I led—"

"I know about the last party you led," Maren said. "I told you. Everything, and half of it twice." She put a hand on the guide's arm, and felt the muscle underneath flinch and then, slowly, decide not to pull away. "The mountain doesn't care about your report. The mountain cares whether you read it true. And you just read my Spine true in four minutes, standing in the rain, with your hands shaking." She nodded up at the gray band. "Go rig your lines, guide. The children leave the day after tomorrow."

* * *

Maren watched the first crossing from the meadow below the rib, because she was the one who had to tell the mothers, and she refused to describe a thing she had not seen with her own eyes.

It was, she would say later, like watching a person remember a language they had been forbidden to speak.

The guide went first, always first, testing each hold before trusting it to a child, and the guide's hands had stopped shaking by the second morning. The handlines on the limestone rib were textbook — anchors doubled, angles backed off, the rope positioned so a slipping child would swing into the rock instead of out over the void — and when the eldest girl, Tam, froze halfway across with the wind pushing at her back and started to cry, the guide did not shout at her and did not coddle her. The guide simply came back along the line, stood beside her, and said something Maren could not hear, and Tam laughed through her tears, and crossed.

By the third day the children had stopped being afraid of the guide and started competing for the guide's attention, which Maren knew was the truest measure of a guide there was. The little one, Osk, who could not walk the rib and had to be roped and lowered like a sack of meal, howled the whole way the first time and giggled the whole way the second time, because the guide had turned his lowering into a game, counting the ledges aloud in a voice like a festival announcer, and by the third crossing Osk was shouting the numbers before the guide could.

Wren worked the lines below, quick and tireless, and the two of them had developed a whole vocabulary of tugs and whistles that needed no words at all. Maren watched them at the evening fire, sitting in that same hand's width of space, and noticed on the fourth night that the space had closed to the width of a finger, and that neither of them had noticed, and that both of them would be mortified to notice, and she smiled into her tea and said nothing.

On the last crossing, the rain came early, a hard gray squall out of the west, and Maren's heart climbed into her throat — but the guide had read the sky an hour before the sky had made up its mind, and had the whole party moving an hour faster than planned, and they came off the Spine into the meadow soaked and laughing and alive, all six children, all three trips, not one broken finger between them.

That night the mothers wept on the guide's shoulders, and the guide stood stiffly under the weeping like a post under a garland, accepting it without believing any of it belonged there.

Maren found the guide alone at the fire afterward, cleaning rope.

"You realize," she said, sitting down uninvited, "what you've done."

"I carried some children over a hill."

"You guided." Maren let the word sit there. "Not ferried. Not hauled. Guided. You read the mountain, you rigged the line, you put a scared child's fear down and picked her courage up, and you did it nine years after the world told you your hands were finished. That's not a favor you did me. That's a door you opened." She looked at the guide's face, at the war going on behind it. "You can close it again. Most people do. It's easier. But you should know it's open now, and you opened it yourself, and no one — not the Kestrians, not the Ondmarkers, not whatever that soft-voiced man is who's been asking after you downriver — can un-open it for you."

The guide's head came up sharply. "What soft-voiced man?"

Maren shrugged. "Came through the low country ten days ago, asking at the ferry landings. Well-dressed. Paid in new coin. Asked about a boat that leaked, and a guide, and a girl with a satchel. The ferryman at Two Fords told him nothing, because Two Fords is ours, though he didn't know it." She studied the guide. "He's behind you. How far behind, I couldn't say. But he's coming, and he's patient, and patient men on a river are worse than floodwater, because at least you can see the flood coming."

The guide was already on their feet, and Wren was already awake — Maren noticed the girl had been awake the whole time, listening from the dark of the doorway, satchel already in her arms.

"How long to ready the boat?" the guide asked.

"An hour, if you don't say goodbye. Two if you do." Maren rose, joints cracking, and looked at the two of them — the disgraced guide and the runaway apprentice, firelit and cornered and, she thought, not nearly as doomed as they believed. "You paid your toll, guide. Both directions. The children are over, the medicine is in their lungs, and Serel's Landing will forget your name the moment you're gone — that was the bargain, and we keep our bargains." She paused. "But I'll tell you what I told the last traveler who passed through here running from something. The river takes what it's owed, but it also gives back what it's borrowed. You pulled something out of that water, didn't you? Something with a seal on it?"

She watched them both go still as stone.

"Then it wasn't the river that pulled you in," Maren said. "It was that. And things like that don't stop pulling until they're done with you." She jerked her chin toward the dark water sliding past the landing, high and brown and fast, carrying whole trees on its back. "Whatever you're carrying in that satchel, the man behind you wants it more than you want to be free of it. So you'd best decide, before he catches you, whether you're going to keep running from what you are — or start running as it." She turned for her house, then stopped. "The boat's yours at dawn. And guide — the line through the gray band. Left of the Spout. If you ever come back this way, the river's shifted, but the rock remembers. So do we."

They left before first light, and Maren did not watch them go, because watching people go was a habit that made old women foolish.

But she did note, with the satisfaction of a woman who had bet correctly her whole long life, that the guide had left payment on the table anyway — more than the bargain asked, in coin that was clearly the last of what they had — and beside the coin, folded small, a page torn from a survey plate's protective wrapping, marked in a dead nation's cipher.

A map. Or the start of one.

Maren Oduya put it in the box under her floor with the other things Serel's Landing kept that were not on any map, and went to wake the children, and did not think about the soft-voiced man again until the floodwater brought his boat to her landing, three days later, flying no colors at all.
