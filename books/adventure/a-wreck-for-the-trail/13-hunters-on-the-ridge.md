## 13. Hunters on the Ridge

The elder's name was Maren, and she did not ask Sable to guide her children so much as she told them the price and waited for them to pay it.

"Four of mine," she said, standing in the doorway of the longhouse while rain sheeted off the eaves like a curtain being shaken out. "And two from the Teller family downstream. The fever's in the low village now. Dr. Ilsa says they need the high air, the sulfur springs past the ridge, or we bury them by next moon." She looked at Sable the way a woman looks at a horse she's thinking of buying — not unkindly, but with arithmetic behind her eyes. "The flood will cut the trail in three days. Maybe four."

Sable had said yes before the sentence finished. That was the strange part. Somewhere between the village's lamplight and Maren's flat gray stare, something old had reached up through Sable's chest and answered on its own.

Now, at first light on the second day, that same something was asking whether it had made a mistake.

The ridge trail climbed out of the river valley in switchbacks so steep the children had to be roped together like cargo. Six of them: the eldest maybe fourteen, a boy called Tam with his jaw set like he'd already decided not to complain; the youngest a girl of seven, Pip, who had named Sable's pack "the mule" and refused to be corrected. Behind them came Wren, pale under her hood, and two village women strong enough to carry a child if it came to that.

Sable walked point, reading the mountain the way other people read a letter from home.

It was good country. That was the problem. Good country meant exposure — long traverses across open scree where anything above could see anything below, and where anything below could see you coming for an hour before you arrived. Sable had chosen this route over the forest line precisely because the forest hid ambushers as easily as travelers. Up here there was nowhere to hide.

Which meant if Corvane's men were coming, Sable would see them.

*When,* said the voice that had kept Sable alive through two countries' worth of enemies. Not *if.* When.

"Pip," Sable called back without turning. "Feet wide on the loose stuff. Walk where I walk, not where it looks easier."

"It looks exactly the same."

"That's what the mountain wants you to think."

A small, indignant silence, then small boots scrabbling obediently along Sable's own boot prints. Wren caught Sable's eye as she passed on the switchback below, and something passed between them too — not words, nothing so cheap. Just the shared knowledge that sat in both their stomachs like a swallowed stone: *they're coming.*

They'd seen the smoke the night before. A cook fire in the valley, three ridges back, burning green wood in the rain because someone cared more about speed than concealment. Corvane didn't send men who crept. He sent men who arrived.

Sable had spent the evening doing what Sable did best, which was lying with geometry. The decoy chest — the one bought in Salt and Rust days ago, weighted with stones and packed with worthless plate fragments and a forged survey bearing Wren had drawn in an hour of furious concentration — now rode in the middle of the party, slung between the two village women where a distant watcher would read it as precious cargo guarded at the center. The real plates were wrapped in oilcloth against Sable's spine, flat as a psalm book, riding under the pack frame where no one would think to look.

If the hunters took the bait, they'd take it and go. Corvane wanted the chest. He didn't know yet that the chest was a lesson.

If they didn't take the bait —

Well. Sable had been planning for that too. All night, by feel, in the dark of the longhouse loft, running the ridge in memory the way a gambler runs a hand of cards.

There was a place. Two hours up, where the trail crossed beneath a shoulder of the mountain called locally the Old Man's Chin — a hanging face of fractured limestone, hundreds of tons of it, held to the slope by habit and ice. Any guide worth the blacklist against their name knew faces like that. Knew which ones were sleeping and which ones were only pretending.

The Old Man's Chin was pretending. Sable had spotted it yesterday: a seep line running dark down the rock, frost-heaved blocks perched at angles that offended the eye, a crack behind the face wide enough to lose a forearm in. The monsoon water was working at it day and night. In a week, maybe two, the mountain would drop that whole shoulder on its own.

In a week, the children would be dead of fever and the trail would be underwater anyway.

But a mountain could be hurried. That was the secret nobody put in the guidebooks. Rockfall wasn't luck; it was leverage. You found the keystone, the one block holding its neighbors up out of vanity rather than physics, and you persuaded it. Everything else followed gravity's own argument.

Sable had done it once before, years ago, on a route in Ondmark that no longer appeared in any company's catalog. It had worked perfectly. That was the part nobody believed afterward — that the thing had worked *perfectly*, and the deaths had come from somewhere else entirely.

Don't think about that. Think about the children. Think about the geometry.

* * *

The fevered children slowed them, as fever does. By midmorning they'd made the traverse below the Chin, six small figures strung along a ledge barely wider than a cart track, the drop on their left falling away in gray-green steps into cloud. One of the sick girls rode on a village woman's back, burning hot even through the rain, mumbling about her mother's kitchen. Tam carried the younger Teller boy's pack along with his own and hadn't mentioned it.

Sable stopped at the apex of the traverse, where the ledge met the base of the Chin itself, and let the column bunch up in the shelter of an overhang while checking the backtrail.

Cloud below. Cloud ahead. And then — a flicker, there and gone, like a fish turning in murky water — movement on the switchbacks two ridges down.

Three figures. No. Four, spaced like professionals, moving fast, not bothering with cover because they didn't believe anyone was watching. The lead one carried a crossbow slung across his back and moved with the flat economy of a man who had killed enough people to stop performing it.

Sable counted heartbeats. Four hundred paces of open ledge between the hunters and the overhang. At the children's pace, forty minutes. At the hunters', twelve.

"Wren." Keep the voice level. Children hear fear the way dogs hear whistles. "How are you at climbing?"

Wren followed Sable's gaze up the Chin — the seep line, the perched blocks, the black crack behind the face — and Sable watched understanding arrive in her expression like weather. "You want to bring that down."

"I want to *ask* it to come down. Politely. On our schedule instead of the mountain's."

"And us?"

"The ledge holds. It has to hold — that face sheds onto the slope, not straight down. We stand under the overhang, tight in, and we let it fall where it falls." Sable heard how it sounded and hated it. There was no way to say *I've done this before and mostly everyone lived* that didn't sound like a confession. "Get them under the rock. All of them. Now."

Wren didn't argue. That was the thing about Wren that kept surprising Sable — she argued endlessly about principles and never about survival. She turned and started moving the children with a calm, bright lie about a rest stop and a game involving who could stand flattest against a wall, and within two minutes all six kids and both women were pressed into the shallow cave of the overhang, packs overhead, Pip giggling because the game was silly.

Sable started up.

Not the face itself — nothing that direct. A chimney on its flank, wet and ugly, hands and feet finding holds the way a blind man finds furniture: by faith and repetition. Forty feet up, the seep line crossed the chimney, and Sable traversed left onto the broken apron beneath the hanging blocks, boots testing each one, weight fed in slow as water.

There. The keystone. A slab the size of a coffin lid, wedged nose-down against two smaller blocks, holding back a shelf of rubble the way a finger holds back a flood. Water ran out from under it in a thin brown ribbon, patient as everything else the monsoon did.

Sable knelt, got fingers into the crack behind it, and pulled.

Nothing. Of course nothing. Ten thousand tons of mountain don't move for fingers.

The pry bar came off the pack — the same bar that had opened the flooded chest a lifetime ago, back when Sable still believed the chest was scrap brass. Sable set it into the crack, leaned weight onto it, and felt the mountain consider the offer.

Somewhere below, faint through the rain-hiss, a shout. The hunters had found the empty switchbacks. Found them faster than expected. Twelve minutes had just become eight.

*Sable Fairweather,* Sable thought, driving the bar in another inch, *blacklisted in two countries, dead in every way that matters, is about to pull a mountain down on purpose.* If the old guild examiners could see this, they'd fail the technique and pass the nerve.

The slab groaned. Shifted a finger's width. Water gushed from the new gap, cold as a grave across Sable's wrists.

That was when the crossbow bolt hit the rock a foot from Sable's head.

The sound was enormous — a wet stone crack, chips stinging Sable's cheek — and the world snapped into the narrow bright place it went when dying became possible. Down-slope, the lead hunter stood on the last switchback, cranking the windlass with unhurried arms, his companions fanning out across the traverse. Between him and the overhang: the children.

He wouldn't shoot at the children. Even Corvane's men needed the villagers' goodwill gone — no, that was hope talking. Hope was a bad guide. He'd shoot whoever was in the way, and right now the only thing between his bolts and that huddle of small bodies was forty feet of vertical wet rock and one disgraced guide with a pry bar in a mountain's teeth.

Sable pulled.

The slab came free with a sound like the world clearing its throat — a deep grinding boom that Sable felt in the sternum more than heard — and the shelf behind it went with it. Hundreds of tons of limestone, hurrying after a week of patience compressed into ten seconds of obedience. The whole face seemed to lean, hesitate with theatrical cruelty, and then pour.

Sable jumped.

Not down the climb — down *inside*, into the slot between the moving rock and the stable wall, the kind of choice that isn't a choice, tucking the pry bar against the chest, arms over the head, boots braced on walls that shuddered like the flanks of a galloping animal. Stone thundered past close enough to feel the wind of it. A block the size of a barrel struck the lip of the chimney mouth and burst apart an arm's length above Sable's head, and something struck back — a glancing blow across the shoulder blade that lit the whole left side white and drove the breath out in a single ugly grunt.

Then the mountain was done talking, and the dust rolled down over everything like a gray tide, and the silence underneath it rang.

* * *

Sable came back to the world in pieces. Rain, first — rain on an upturned face. Then pain, inventorying itself: shoulder screaming, ribs on the left side reporting in with every breath, one boot missing entirely and the foot inside it bruised but whole. Then voices, high and frightened, and small hands hauling with more courage than strength.

Pip had Sable's collar in both fists, dragging with the entire force of seven years. "Wake up, wake up, the mule fell down—"

"I'm awake." It came out as gravel. Sable coughed dust, tasted blood and limestone, and got an elbow under herself. The chimney was half gone, choked with fresh rubble. Above, where the Old Man's Chin had hung its jaw for a thousand years, there was now a raw scar and a talus fan spilling across the switchbacks below — across the switchbacks where the hunters had been standing.

Sable looked down the slope and felt nothing that could be named. Four figures had become, at distance, impossible to count among the settling dust. One was moving — limping, dragging a leg, vanishing back down into the cloud. The others might have run. Might have been buried. The mountain kept its own ledger and showed no one.

The crossbow lay on the trail fifty feet down, bent double under a block.

"They were going to shoot you," Tam said. He was fourteen and trying very hard to sound older, and his voice cracked on *you*. "We saw. From under the rock. They were aiming at you and then the whole — the whole hill—" He stopped, swallowed, and looked at Sable with something that Sable recognized and had no defenses against, because it was the look Sable used to get from clients on their first summit morning. Trust, freshly minted. "You pulled the mountain down."

"I asked it nicely." Sable got upright, swayed, was steadied by two small hands and one firm grip from Wren, who was looking at Sable with an expression that had too many layers to read in the rain — worry and awe and, underneath, the beginning of a question Sable dreaded. *Where did you learn to do that? What else have you survived?*

Later. Let her ask later.

"Everyone accounted for?" Heads nodded around the overhang. The sick girl still burned, still murmured, but breathed. The springs were four hours on, the trail beyond the rockfall clear — the slide had taken the hunters' route, not theirs. Cost paid, account balanced.

Except.

"The chest," Sable said, and the word came out wrong, too sharp.

The village women exchanged a look. The elder sister pointed down-slope, at the talus field, at a scatter of splintered wood visible among the fresh stone where the slide's edge had clipped the ledge — where the decoy chest had been lashed to the pack of the younger woman, who had dropped it in the scramble for cover, and the mountain had collected it the way the river once had.

Gone. Buried under a hundred tons of deliberately murdered geology, mixed with the actual rockfall until no digger on earth could sort treasure from tailings. Wren stared at it, and Sable watched her do the same arithmetic: the forged plates, the false survey bearing, weeks of careful misdirection — all of it entombed with the hunters who'd chased it, unreadable, unrecoverable, *unprovable*.

Which meant Corvane would learn, sooner or later, that he'd been chasing stones. And when he learned it, he would stop sending four men with a crossbow.

"He'll know," Wren said quietly, under the children's noise. "Sooner or later, he'll dig, and he'll know."

"Then we make sure 'later' is long enough." Sable tested the ruined shoulder, hissed, and settled the pack strap on the good side. The real plates pressed flat and patient against Sable's spine, and for the first time since the river, carrying them felt less like smuggling and more like stewardship. "Springs are four hours. Move."

They moved. And three hours on, wrapping the bad shoulder by a sulfur spring while steam rose around them and the fevered children soaked in the shallows under the women's watch, Sable found Wren sitting apart with something in her lap that stopped Sable's breath.

Paper. Old paper, water-stained, folded and refolded many times — Sable knew that document the way a scar knows the knife. The incident report. The one signed with a name that wasn't quite Sable's. Wren must have lifted it from the false bottom of the pack during some night watch, and now she sat in the steam with it open on her knees, reading, her lips moving slightly, her face changing as she read.

She looked up. Met Sable's eyes across the rising mist.

"Sable," she said, and her voice was not angry, and that was somehow worse than anger. "This isn't what happened. This isn't what happened at all."
