## 14. What Sable Signed

The steam hung between them like something alive.

Wren had read perhaps half the report when she understood that she was reading two documents at once — the one on the page, and the one beneath it, pressed flat by omission, the way a footprint survives in dried mud long after the foot has gone. The official account was clean. Too clean. A guided party of six had entered the Serpent's Tooth in late autumn. Weather turned. Three clients died. The guide, one *S. Fairweather*, had signed a statement accepting full responsibility for route error.

Route error. Wren knew her father's trade too well to believe those words meant anything. Route error was what you wrote when you didn't know yet, or when you weren't allowed to say what you did know. Her father had taught her that much before he'd taught her anything else: *Every report is an argument, little bird. Ask who benefits from winning it.*

She looked up at Sable across the steam. "This isn't what happened. This isn't what happened at all."

Sable didn't move. That was the first strange thing. Wren had expected the document to be snatched from her hands, or for Sable to stand and walk out into the rain with that terrible fluid grace they used when a conversation became a place they refused to be. Instead Sable sat very still, hands loose on their knees, watching her read as though watching a wound being cleaned.

"Where did you get that," Sable said. It wasn't a question. It was a door held shut.

"The false bottom of your pack. When we patched the hull at the smelting town." Wren didn't apologize. Apologizing would have been a lie, and they had agreed — hadn't they, up on the weir, shouting over the water — that they were done lying to each other. Mostly done. She was still holding back the cipher's last key, but that felt different. That felt like hers. This paper had never been hers, and she'd taken it anyway, because that was what apprentices of cartography learned first: *the truth is always filed somewhere inconvenient.*

"You went through my things."

"I did." She let the steam carry it. "And now I'm going through this. Because there are four pages here and only one of them is signed, and the three that aren't don't match the one that is."

Sable's jaw tightened. "Wren—"

"Listen to me." She stood, and the wet floorboards gave under her bare feet, and she crossed the small room until she was close enough that the steam couldn't hide either of them. "I grew up in survey offices. I've read incident reports since I could read at all. My father wrote them. He rewrote them, when the ministry told him to, and he hated every word he changed, and he taught me how to see the seams." She held up the report. "Look at the dates. The party entered the Tooth on the ninth of Frostmonth. The weather system came through on the eleventh. But this—" she tapped the third page, "—this says the guide *chose* the high line on the tenth, *knowing* conditions were deteriorating. Knowing how? From what observation? There's no barometric record. No wind log. Nothing from the low stations. If Fairweather made that call blind, fine, that's negligence. But if Fairweather had information the report doesn't include—"

"Stop."

"—then someone removed it, and someone made sure the only person left to blame was the person who—"

"*Stop.*"

The word cracked across the room like ice letting go. Wren stopped. In the silence she heard the rain hammering the roof of the bathhouse, the river working at its banks below, and Sable breathing — slow, deliberate breaths, the kind climbers take when they're managing fear instead of air.

"You want to know what happened," Sable said quietly. "Fine. But not here. Not where anyone can walk in."

* * *

They took the report to the boat.

It was still moored in the flooded willows downstream of the village, hidden under netting and cut brush, and they reached it by wading the last hundred yards through water warm as blood and brown as tea. Inside, the cabin smelled of pitch and damp wool and the ghost of the monsoon they'd survived together. Sable lit the small oil lamp, shuttered it, and sat down across from Wren with the whole boat between them, as if distance were a courtesy.

"Read it again," Sable said. "Out loud. The parts that bother you."

So Wren read. Her voice filled the little cabin, steady, professional, the voice she'd used reciting contour intervals to her father while he checked her against the plates. *"...the guide elected to ascend via the eastern couloir despite visible deterioration..."* She stopped. "There were no visible conditions. The eastern couloir can't be seen from camp — I've looked at the plates. You can't 'visibly' assess a couloir you're standing underneath."

"Go on."

*"The guide failed to consult the party regarding the change of route."* Wren looked up. "Did you?"

Sable was quiet a long moment. The lamp flame leaned and righted itself.

"No," Sable said finally. "Because the change of route wasn't mine. And the man who ordered it asked me not to tell his family why."

The rain filled the pause.

"His name was Aldric Voss," Sable said. "He was the senior client. Wealthy Kestrian family — shipping money, the kind of money that buys mountains the way other people buy boots. He hired me privately, off the guild rolls, which should have been my first warning. Six of them total. His brother-in-law, two business partners, a nephew, a family friend. They wanted the Tooth in late season because Voss wanted to say he'd done it in late season. I told him the window was wrong. He paid me triple to stop saying so."

Wren said nothing. She had learned, in the past weeks, that Sable talked the way ice moved — slowly, and then all at once, and you did not stand in the path of it.

"On the tenth morning, one of the porters came to me before dawn. Shaking. He said he'd found cut anchors at the lower camps. Fixed lines severed — not frayed, *cut*, clean, with a blade, and re-tied badly so you wouldn't notice unless you weighted them. Someone had gone ahead of us up the mountain and sabotaged the route." Sable's eyes stayed on the lamp. "There were only seven people on that mountain who knew our line. Me, six clients, and the porters who'd already turned back. Which meant one of the six."

"One of the clients," Wren whispered.

"Aldric Voss." Sable's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Or someone acting for him. I never proved which, and I've had twenty years to try. What I know is this: Voss came to me on the tenth and ordered the high line. Ordered it. Told me the eastern couloir was closed anyway — which was true, if you believed the sabotage was real — and that the high line was faster. And then he looked at me with those calm shipping-money eyes and said, *'My brother-in-law has been asking questions about my accounts. My nephew gambles. Accidents happen on mountains, Fairweather. Make sure this one happens high.'*"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the river shifted a snag against the bank with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"And you refused," Wren said.

"I tried. I told him I'd take the party down. He reminded me that he'd hired me off the books — that I had no guild protection, no contract, no witnesses, and that a disgraced guide accusing a Voss of murder would be laughed out of every court in Kestria before the bodies were even cold." Sable's hands had gone still on their knees, perfectly still, the stillness of someone holding something heavy without letting it show. "Then the storm came in early. A day early. And Voss took three of them up the high line himself, at night, without waking me, taking the good rope and leaving the cut ones behind for the descent."

"Oh," Wren said. It was a very small word for a very large pit.

"They fell in the whiteout. All three. Voss, the brother-in-law whose questions he feared, and the nephew who gambled." Sable's voice didn't change at all, and that was the worst thing Wren had ever heard. "The two who lived were the ones who stayed in camp. The family friend and the business partner. And when the recovery teams came, the family friend — who owed Voss everything, who'd been kept by that family like a pet for thirty years — told the inquiry that *I* had chosen the high line. That I'd been drunk. That I'd abandoned the party."

"And you signed the report."

"I signed a version of it." For the first time, Sable looked up, and met Wren's eyes across the lamplight. "The guild investigator was honest with me, in his way. He said: sign, and the inquiry closes, and the Voss family stops looking for someone to ruin beyond you. Fight it, and they would bury me so deep no glacier would find me — and they'd start on the porters, too. The porter who warned me had a wife and a new baby in the valley. So I signed. I signed *S. Fairweather, guide, accepting responsibility for route selection.*" A breath. "I let them make it my mountain. Because the alternative was a war I couldn't win, fought with people who'd burn anyone standing near me."

Wren set the report down on her knee. Her hands were trembling, and she watched them tremble with a kind of distant curiosity, the way she'd once watched her father's compass needle swing wildly near the iron ore deposits above Halven's Reach — not lost, just responding honestly to forces too large to see.

"You let them erase you," she said.

"I let them *use* me. Erasing came free." Sable almost smiled. "Turns out I was good at it. Twenty years of practice."

"That's why the name on the chest plates mattered. That's why you wanted to throw them in the river. Every time something of yours gets found, it gets used against you."

"Are you asking or telling?"

"Telling." Wren folded the report closed, carefully, along its old creases, and then — before she could think better of it, before caution could catch up with her heart — she reached across the space between them and put her hand flat on top of Sable's hands. They were colder than she expected. Guide's hands, rope-worn, scarred at the knuckles. They did not pull away.

"Sable. Listen to me. I spent years watching my father sign things he didn't believe. Reports trimmed to fit treaties. Passes left off maps because a minister's cousin owned the toll road. He used to say the worst part wasn't the lying — it was that after a while, he couldn't remember which parts of himself he'd sold and which he'd kept." Her throat tightened. "You didn't sell yourself. You paid with yourself to keep someone else's family safe. Those aren't the same thing. I know the difference. I've *read* the difference, in a hundred reports, and this—" she tapped the folded paper, "—is the first one I've ever read where the signature was the most honest line on the page."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't forgotten the way out. "That's a generous reading, apprentice."

"It's an accurate one. I'm a cartographer's daughter. Accuracy is the family religion." Wren didn't move her hand. "You've been carrying this for twenty years like it was yours. It isn't. It never was. It belongs to Aldric Voss and whoever sharpened the knife on those anchors, and I think—" she hesitated, then committed, the way her father had taught her to commit ink to a finished plate, no erasures, "—I think Corvane knows. I think that's partly why he wants the chest. A guide with a buried scandal is easy to destroy. A guide with a *provable* one is dangerous."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes — not hope exactly; hope was a country Sable had renounced citizenship in. More like a border crossing. A long hesitation at the checkpoint, papers being examined.

"Why are you telling me this?" Sable asked. "Why now? You could have used it. Blackmail works both directions — you could have made me take any line you wanted to the pass."

"I could have," Wren agreed. "But you'd have known, eventually, that I knew. And then you'd have been guiding me the way you guided that report — watching every word, guarding every flank, alone inside your own expedition." She squeezed the cold hands once and let go. "I don't want a guide who's protecting themselves from me. I want a partner. Partners don't hold hostages."

The lamp burned. Outside, the rain eased by degrees, the way it did sometimes in the smallest hours, the sky catching its breath between storms. Wren became aware, suddenly and completely, of how tired she was — bone-tired, monsoon-tired, tired in the way that only truth makes you, because lies are light and truth has mass.

"Forgive me for going through your pack," she said.

"There's nothing to forgive."

"Yes, there is. And I'm offering it before you ask, because I know you'd never ask." She smiled, small and crooked. "Consider us even. You took the blame that wasn't yours; I took the paper that wasn't mine. We're both thieves of other people's weight."

For a moment Sable said nothing. Then, quietly, in a voice Wren had never heard them use — unguarded, unarmored, almost young — Sable said:

"Nobody's ever read that report and come out on my side."

"Well." Wren began rolling the report and sliding it back toward the false bottom of the pack — then paused, and instead tucked it into the inner pocket of her own coat, next to the cipher key. "Get used to it. You're stuck with me until the pass."

Sable looked at her for a long time. Then nodded, once, the way guides nod at a route they've decided to trust.

It was nearly dawn when the sound came — faint, upstream, carried on the thinning rain. A bell. The village's flood-bell, ringing fast and urgent, the pattern Wren had been taught on her second night there: *not rising water, not sickness.* Something else.

Boots hit the deck outside. The elder's granddaughter, breathless, hair plastered to her face, hauling open the cabin door without knocking.

"The ice," she gasped. "Up-valley — the sentries heard it an hour ago. The glacier's started to calve. Your window's open."

Wren looked at Sable. Sable was already reaching for the lamp to kill it, already moving, twenty years of running converting in a single heartbeat into twenty years of climbing.

"How long do we have?" Wren asked.

The girl swallowed. "Days. Maybe three. And there are riders on the south trail — Corvane's colors. He's a day behind you. Maybe less."

Sable killed the lamp, and in the sudden grey pre-dawn light, Wren saw something she had never seen on that face before: not fear, not shame, but readiness — the look of a guide standing at the base of the mountain they were born for.

"Then we climb," Sable said.
