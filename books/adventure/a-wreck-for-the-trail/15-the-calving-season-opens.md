## 15. The Calving Season Opens

*Wren*

The mountain spoke first.

Wren heard it before she saw anything — a sound like a door slamming somewhere deep inside the world, rolling down the valley and across the grey-green water of the melt lake, arriving in her chest as much as her ears. She was on the shingle spit below the moraine, filling the water skins, and she stood very still with the skins dripping from her hands, listening.

Then came the second sound: the long groan, the ice shifting its weight like an old man turning over in bed.

"Sable." Her voice cracked on the second syllable and she had to try again. "Sable! It's starting."

Sable came out of the tent fast — too fast, catching the ridgepole with their shoulder so the whole thing lurched like a boat in weather. They'd been sleeping in shifts for three days now, ever since the hunters' fire had appeared on the far side of the weir, a day's travel back and closing. Sable looked wrecked: frost-scoured lips, eyes red-rimmed from wind and sleeplessness, hands that hadn't stopped trembling since the rockfall even though they both pretended not to notice.

"Where?" was all they said.

Wren pointed up-valley to where the glacier's snout shouldered into the lake — a wall of blue-white ice two hundred feet high, streaked with the black bands of old ash falls, calved into a sheer face by the water gnawing at its base. For six days it had sat silent and sullen, giving nothing. Now, as they watched, a section of that face the size of a chapel shrugged loose and fell.

The splash reached them a full breath later, a white wall of spray that climbed, hung, and collapsed back into the lake. The wave it sent across the water lifted the shore ice and dropped it again with a sound like crockery breaking. Somewhere under the surface, Wren knew, bergs the size of houses were already drifting, nine-tenths hidden, waiting to punch holes in anything foolish enough to sail among them.

"It's early," Sable said. "The survey said—"

"The survey said mid-month." Wren was already scrambling up the moraine to the cairn where they'd cached the plates, her father's cipher-plate wrapped in oilcloth against her skin the way she'd carried it since the smelting town. "It's eight days early. The winter was warm. My father wrote about that — warm winters, the glacier wakes hungry."

She knelt at the cairn and unwrapped the plates with fingers that wouldn't quite obey her. There were eleven of them, etched zinc, each one dense with her father's hand: contour lines fine as hair, elevation notes in his cramped cipher, and threaded through all of it — visible only if you knew the key, only if you'd spent a childhood learning to see what he hid in plain sight — the route. The pass of Vhalen, drawn as a fault line through the ice, a way over the spine of the mountains that no government owned because no government knew it existed.

Her father had died before she was old enough to ask him why he'd hidden it. She'd spent four years and one long terrible river figuring out the answer anyway: because some things are only safe while they're secret, and some secrets are only worth keeping until someone needs them.

Someone needed them now. Half a valley away, refugees were gathering on the Kestrian side — the village elder's word had traveled, the way word always traveled among people who lived off every map — waiting for a route that would let them cross without papers, without tolls, without disappearing into Corvane's ledgers. And behind Wren and Sable, a day back and gaining, came the man who erased geography for a living.

She spread the first plate on a flat stone and weighted its corners with pebbles. The morning light came off the glacier and made the etched lines glow.

"We have to go today," she said. "Not tomorrow. Today."

Sable crouched beside her. They didn't touch the plate — they never touched the plates, some superstition Wren had never broken them of — but they read it, and Wren watched them read it, watched the guide come up behind their eyes like a lamp being lit in a dark house. It was the look she'd started to trust more than words. Sable Fairweather lied about everything except terrain.

"Window's short," Sable said. "Once the calving starts proper, the melt comes off that ice in a flood by midday, every day. We cross the lower moraine before dawn, hit the ice field while it's still frozen hard, be off the glacier by—"

"Noon," Wren said. "He says noon. Third note in the margin. *Off the ice by noon or the ice will make its own decision.*" She touched the tiny ciphered characters. "I used to think he was being poetic."

"He wasn't?"

The glacier answered before she could. Another section went, further along the face, and this time the crack that preceded it was long and shrieking, a sound like the whole mountain tearing linen. The boom rolled over them. Pebbles jumped on the stone beside the plate.

Sable stood. "Pack everything. Leave the tent."

"The tent is our—"

"The tent is forty pounds of canvas that'll get us killed on the moraine. We've slept in worse." A ghost of the old dry humor, there and gone. "Move, cartographer."

They moved.

* * *

*Sable*

The lower moraine was exactly as bad as Sable remembered it, which is to say it was a mile of loose stone stacked by a glacier's bad temper, unstable as a drunkard's promise, and the only way forward.

They climbed it in the last hour of dark, headlamps — no, not headlamps, Sable corrected themself, *storm-lanterns*, hooded, the light cut to a blade — picking out the next three steps and nothing beyond. Behind them the lake boomed at intervals, each report a little louder than the last, the glacier clearing its throat. Ahead, above the black shoulder of the ridge, the upper ice field lay pale under the stars, a frozen river poured down the throat of the pass.

Sable counted their own breathing and put one foot where the ground agreed to hold it. Left foot on the embedded slab, weight tested, right foot onto the gravel lens between boulders, never straight-legged, never committed until the stone committed first. Twenty years since anyone had paid them to do this and the body hadn't forgotten a single lesson. That was the bitter joke of it: the skill had stayed loyal when nothing else had.

Wren climbed well. That surprised Sable still, after all these weeks. The girl had river-legs now and mountain-sense growing fast; she read slope the way she read plates, patiently, letting the terrain tell her things. Twice she froze at a sound behind them and twice Sable talked her through it without stopping — *that's just the lake, keep moving, we don't stop on scree* — and both times she obeyed, and both times something in Sable's chest that had been clenched since the rockfall loosened another turn.

They topped the moraine as the sky went the color of dirty pewter, and the wind found them, and the cold found them through the wind, and there was no more time to be gentle about any of it.

"There." Sable pointed with their chin, hands busy re-lashing the rope coil. Where the ice field met the lateral moraine there was a ramp of old avalanche debris, compacted, climbable, feeding onto the glacier itself maybe half a mile up. From there, if her father's lines were true, the route bent left around a serac field, crossed two ice bridges marked in cipher, and gained the col in a long diagonal that avoided the crevasse bands entirely. Eleven plates' worth of a dead man's love for his daughter, drawn in invisible ink.

"And behind us?" Wren asked, not looking back. She never looked back anymore. Sable had noticed that. She'd decided looking back was a luxury.

Sable turned anyway, because someone had to.

Far below, at the mouth of the valley where the trail from the weir came down to the lake, there was smoke. Not campfire smoke — that rose straight and lazy in still air. This was a signal fire, built big, fed green, and even at this distance Sable could read the grammar of it: *we are here, we are coming.*

A day behind, Corvane had said the ledger would allow. But Corvane's men didn't rest like other men rested, and the meltwater trails were firming as the season dried, and a day could become half a day could become nothing at all if Sable and Wren lost an hour arguing with a crevasse field.

"They're at the lake," Sable said. "That buys us the morning. Maybe."

"Maybe isn't much."

"Maybe is what guides sell." Sable swung the pack up. "Come on. Ice's hardest before the sun touches it. After that, every minute costs double."

* * *

*Wren*

By mid-morning they were on the glacier itself, and Wren understood for the first time why her father had needed eleven plates.

Nothing here held still. The ice field was a landscape having a slow argument with itself — pressure ridges shouldering up overnight, meltwater streams appearing between breakfast and noon where none had been, seracs leaning at angles that changed when you weren't watching. Her father's cipher accounted for all of it. *If the left-hand serac has fallen, take the right channel. If the bridges are flooded, retreat to the moraine and wait for freeze.* He had written instructions for a country that refused to be mapped, and Wren followed them with her heart in her mouth, translating line by line while Sable translated terrain, and between the two of them they stitched a path across the moving white.

The calving followed them up the valley like weather.

Every twenty minutes, sometimes less, the snout of the glacier below them let go of another piece of itself, and the sound rolled up the ice — *crack, then the pause where the world holds its breath, then the thunder* — and each time Wren felt it in her teeth. The meltwater swelled accordingly. The stream they'd crossed easily at dawn was by ten o'clock a brown torrent churning blocks of ice downstream like a child playing with toys.

"Your father picked his season well," Sable shouted over the noise. They were roped together now, thirty feet apart, crossing the first of the cipher-marked bridges — a snow bridge over a crevasse wide enough to swallow the tent they'd abandoned, sagging in the middle like a tired hammock. Sable crossed first, testing, spreading weight flat-footed, then paid out rope while Wren came across with her eyes fixed on Sable's face instead of the blue darkness under her boots. "Another week and this route closes till autumn. Another month and it closes for good — the melt undercuts the whole lower field."

"He knew," Wren said. "He wrote that the window was *four days, no more, and pray for cold nights.*" She stepped off the far lip and sat down hard, legs shaking, and let herself shake. "Four days. Today's day one."

"Then we spend them well."

They spent them badly and then well and then badly again, which is how days on glaciers go. The second bridge was flooded — a stream running across its surface, eating at it — and they detoured an hour around through broken ground Sable navigated by instinct alone, muttering to the ice like an old adversary. The sun came out at noon and the surface turned to porridge and they post-holed to the knee, then the thigh, then wised up and roped tighter and moved to the shaded flank where the crust held. Wren's hands went numb inside her mitts and she worked the cipher-plate by memory instead, reciting her father's margins like prayers.

And through all of it, at the edge of hearing, the glacier kept talking. *Crack. Pause. Thunder.* Getting closer, somehow, though that was impossible — the snout was miles behind and below. Except it wasn't the snout. By late afternoon Wren realized the sounds were coming from above them now, from the serac bands flanking the route, the whole glacier shedding its winter weight in a long rolling conversation with gravity.

"It's not just the terminus," she said, stopping. "Listen. It's the whole ice field. It's letting go everywhere."

Sable listened, head cocked, and Wren watched the assessment move across their face like cloud-shadow. "Warm night coming," they said finally. "Cloud cover. It won't refreeze. Tomorrow the ice will be rotten from the top down." They looked at her, and for once Sable Fairweather didn't dress the truth in guide's calm. "Tomorrow is the last good day. Day after that, we're climbing a waterfall."

"Corvane?"

"A day back. Maybe less, if they climbed the moraine at night like we did." Sable's jaw worked. "They'll have seen the calving too. They know the clock same as us."

"Then they'll push hard."

"They'll push past sense. Men who work for Corvane push past sense; it's in the pay." Sable shouldered the rope. "Which means tomorrow we race. Real race. No margin, no mercy, no stopping to argue about whose fault the weather is."

"That was one time."

"It was a memorable time."

Despite everything — the thunder, the burning in her legs, the smoke she could no longer see but could feel rising behind them like a hand on her neck — Wren laughed. It came out ragged and surprised her. Sable almost smiled, that narrow private thing that was all they ever gave away for free.

They made camp at dusk on a shelf of old ice at the glacier's shoulder, sheltered behind a wall of blocks the calving had thrown up years ago. No tent, no fire — nothing that could be seen from below. They ate cold oats and split chocolate Wren had been hoarding since the village, and above them the col showed as a black notch full of stars, impossibly close and impossibly far.

"Day one," Wren said. "Three left."

"Two usable." Sable lay back on the rope coil and put an arm behind their head. In the starlight their face looked younger and older at once. "You know what happens if we make it."

Wren did know. Beyond the col, her father's final plate showed the descent into country no treaty mentioned — open valleys, unnamed rivers, the far side of every map either of them had ever been hunted across. Freedom. And also the end of the road that had made the two of them a *them*. She didn't say any of that. Instead she said:

"What happens if we don't?"

Sable was quiet long enough that she thought they might actually answer honestly, which from Sable was rarer than the pass itself.

"Sleep," they said. "Dawn start. We climb before the sun can rot the crust."

But Wren lay awake a long time after Sable's breathing went slow, listening to the glacier talk in its sleep — *crack, pause, thunder* — and thinking about the smoke at the valley mouth, and about her father walking this same ice thirty years ago with his secret folded against his chest, and about how the window was open, finally, after everything, and how windows that open also close.

Sometime in the black hours, the glacier gave a crack longer than any before it, and the pause afterward lasted so long she sat up, and the thunder, when it came, came from directly overhead.

High above the col, lit by starlight, a serac the size of a cathedral leaned, groaned, and began to fall.
