## 16. Ice Underfoot

The glacier announced itself before we saw it — a sound like a door slamming somewhere far below the world, then a long groan that traveled up through the riverbed and into my boots. Wren stopped on the moraine ridge and went very still, the way she did when she was listening to something the rest of us couldn't hear.

"That's it," she said. "That's the calving."

I'd heard ice move before. I'd crossed the Serpent's Tongue twice in my guiding years, back when my name opened doors instead of closing them. But this was different. This glacier wasn't settling. It was *shedding*.

"Window's open," I said. "Which means it's also closing."

Wren unrolled her father's plates across a flat boulder, weighting the corners with stones, and I watched her do what she'd been doing for three weeks now: reading. Not the way I read water or weather or the set of a stranger's shoulders — she read lines and numbers and a cipher her father had built into the survey like a smuggler's false bottom. The pass wasn't drawn on any plate. It was *encoded* in them, folded into contour intervals and benchmark notations that meant nothing unless you knew the key.

"Here." Her finger traced a line up the icefall. "He marked safe passage with these double-ticks. See? Every third contour line has them. It's a thread through the maze."

"And Corvane?"

"A day behind. Maybe less." She rolled the plates with hands that didn't quite tremble, and I gave her credit for that. "The meltwater's rising behind us too. If we don't cross in the next four days—"

"We cross in three." I shouldered the pack. "Come on."

* * *

Rope teams of two are a lie you tell yourself. A proper team is four, maybe six, spread wide enough that when the snow gives way under one of you, the others can arrest the fall. Two people tied together twelve meters apart isn't safety. It's a shared grave with better odds.

But two was what we had, so at dawn we roped up at the base of the icefall, and I put myself on the front end because that was the whole point of me.

"Rules," I said, cinching the harness I'd rigged from salvage line and two carabiners bought off a dead man in Salt and Rust country. "You don't step where I haven't stepped. You don't talk while I'm probing. And if I go down, you drop flat, dig in, and do *not* try to be brave. Bravery kills rope teams faster than crevasses do."

"What do I do instead?"

"You stay boring. Boring keeps people alive."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she looked up at the icefall — a hundred meters of shattered blue chaos, seracs leaning against each other like drunks holding each other upright — and the smile died of natural causes.

We climbed.

Probing with the pole, reading the snow bridges by their color and their sound — white and hollow means thin, blue and dull means thick enough to trust your weight, and anything that *rings* means get off it now. My body remembered all of it. That was the strange cruelty of those first hours: the mountain hadn't forgotten me, even though everyone else had. My feet found the rhythm. My eyes caught the sag in a bridge, the shadow-line where a crevasse breathed under fresh snow. Ten years of disgrace, and the skills had just sat there waiting, patient as stone.

Wren followed exactly as told, stepping into my prints, calling out the plate references when I needed them. Her father's ticks led us left around a leaning serac the size of a chapel, then up a ramp of old ice between two walls of newer, dirtier stuff.

"Third tick ends here," she called. "After this he wrote—" she squinted at the margin note she'd memorized, "—'the glacier has eaten the lower route. Trust the ridge shadow.'"

"He wrote that?"

"In the year Vhalen fell. He was mapping while his country burned."

I thought about that longer than I should have, mid-icefall with my pole sunk to the plate. A man documenting an escape route for a nation that no longer existed, knowing most of the people who'd need it were already dead. There were easier ways to go mad.

The crevasse field above the icefall was worse than the fall itself. Bridges everywhere, some real, some liars. Twice the pole punched through and I rerouted us along a line that made Wren inhale sharply — traversing above a slot you could have dropped a house into, our boots on ice no thicker than a promise.

"You're sure?" she said, once.

"No," I said. "But sure is for people with maps. We've got something better. We've got *this* map, and me."

She didn't answer, but when we stopped to rest in the blue shade of a serac wall, she passed me the water flask first without being asked. Small thing. On a rope team of two, small things are everything.

* * *

It happened at midafternoon, on a bridge her father had marked with a triple-tick — his highest confidence rating, Wren said. The kind of marking a royal surveyor only gave to ground he'd tested with his own weight.

The bridge held for me.

It held for Wren until her third step. Then the world made a sound like a struck bell, and the snow under her dropped away, and she fell.

The rope snapped taut and nearly took me with her. I'd anchored without thinking about it — boot-axe buried, body low, the whole instinctive architecture of arrest — and the line burned across my shoulder as her weight came onto it in one long sickening surge. She fell maybe eight meters before the stretch in the old salvage line ate the shock, and then she was hanging in the blue dark, turning slowly, and I was flat on my stomach at the lip with the cold breathing up at me.

"Wren. Talk to me."

Her voice came up thin and echoing. "I'm — my leg hit something on the way down. I think it's just bruised. Sable, the walls here — they're *blue*. Real ice. I can see the layers."

"Don't look at the walls. Look at me. Can you move?"

"I can't climb eight meters of vertical ice with a bruised leg, Sable."

No. She couldn't. Which meant prusik loops, which meant hauling, which meant every minute she hung there was a minute the afternoon sun worked on that bridge lip, undercutting it, getting ready to drop its own load of rotten snow down the slot and cut her loose or cut me in half or both.

I hauled. God, I hauled. Three-to-one purchase off the anchor, hand over hand, the rope sawing my palms raw through the wraps, Wren kicking her good leg against the wall to help, the two of us grunting and swearing in two different dialects of desperation. It took forty minutes. It took a year. When her hand finally came over the lip and clamped my wrist, I pulled her the last meter onto solid ice and we lay there side by side, shaking, close enough that I could feel her breath.

"You stayed boring," I managed.

"I stayed *boring*," she agreed, and then, horribly, she started to laugh — the high, cracked laugh of someone whose body has just learned it gets to keep living — and after a second I laughed too, lying on a glacier with bleeding hands, laughing like idiots while the ice groaned beneath us.

Then the light shifted, and I looked west, and stopped laughing.

* * *

The storm came out of the north like it had a grudge. No build-up, no courtesy of warning clouds — just a white wall rolling over the upper peaks, and within twenty minutes the wind was flinging spindrift horizontally and the temperature had fallen through the floor.

We couldn't go up. The next section was a ridge traverse her father's notes described as "exposed; do not attempt in wind." We couldn't go down. Eight meters of vertical ice and a storm stood between us and the retreat. And we couldn't stay where we were, because where we were was a snow slope with a broken bridge in it.

There was one option. I'd seen it on the way up and dismissed it as desperate: a shelf of old ice tucked under the flank of a serac wall, maybe three meters deep, floored with ancient compacted snow. A cave, sort of. A coffin, possibly. In a storm on a glacier, the difference doesn't matter much.

We dug it out with the axe and the pole basket and our hands, enlarging it, cutting a bench, stacking a snow wall against the wind. By full dark we were packed into it side by side with the packs between our boots, sharing body heat because there was no other heat, the storm screaming past our snow wall like a freight train that never finished passing.

Neither of us slept, not really. You don't sleep on ice at altitude in a storm; you doze and shiver and wake to check that your toes still belong to you. Somewhere in the small hours, Wren spoke into the dark.

"My father would have liked you."

"He never met me."

"He met plenty of guides. He wrote about them." A pause. The wind filled it. "He wrote that a guide is the only person who stands between you and the mountain, and that you can tell everything about a person by how they stand there. Whether they put themselves between you and the fall, or whether they position themselves to run."

I didn't say anything. My hands throbbed where the rope had burned them.

"You put yourself between," she said. "Twice today. Once on the bridge, once hauling me out. You didn't calculate it. I watched your face. There was nothing to calculate *with*."

"It's reflex. It doesn't mean anything."

"It means everything. It's the one honest thing about you, Sable Fairweather. Everything else is camouflage, but that—" her voice was slurring with exhaustion now, "—that's the load-bearing part."

I should have argued. Instead I sat with it in the dark, in the roaring cold, and found I couldn't.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the storm spent, the glacier wearing thirty centimeters of fresh snow that hid every bridge we'd trusted yesterday. The world had been reset overnight. Nothing we'd learned on the ascent could be relied upon again.

We probed everything twice. It cost us three hours and most of my remaining patience, and then we reached the place where yesterday's route simply ended: the ridge shadow her father had trusted was gone, collapsed into a jumble of blocks, and the alternative was a long traverse under a wall of seracs that were now freshly loaded with storm snow and warming in the new sun.

Wren read the plates. Read them again. Her face did something complicated.

"There's another line. Lower. He marked it 'untested.'"

"Untested meaning what?"

"Meaning he saw it from a distance and wrote down what he saw. He never walked it."

I looked at the untested line. It threaded below the serac wall, across a slope that would funnel anything the ice above decided to shed, and up onto a shoulder that might — *might* — connect with the col.

"How fast can you move?" I asked.

"On a good leg? Faster than you'd think."

"It's not a question of speed. It's a question of *spacing*. We go one at a time. I cross, I signal, you cross fast, no stopping, no looking up. If the wall lets go while either of us is in the open—"

"I know how avalanches work, Sable. I grew up with a surveyor."

"Then you know the rule." I clipped her onto the anchor point and looked her in the eye. "If it comes down and I'm in the path, you don't come digging. You mark the spot, you take the plates, and you finish the crossing alone. That's not bravery talk. That's arithmetic. One of us carrying the map beats zero of us."

For a long moment she stared at me, and I watched her want to argue and decide not to, because she'd grown up with a surveyor and she knew the arithmetic too.

"Cross fast," she said finally. "And Sable — the wall doesn't get a vote. Come back."

I crossed fast.

The slope was silent except for my own heartbeat and the squeak of my boots, and the serac wall rose above me on the left, blue and beautiful and loaded with fresh snow, and I moved through its kill zone in ninety seconds that felt like ninety years, counting my steps, refusing to look up, refusing to run because running breaks crust and running invites the slide. At the far shoulder I turned, planted the axe, and waved her on.

She came fast, light for all her bruising, eyes fixed on me the whole way — and she was ten meters from safety when the mountain cleared its throat.

Not the wall. Below us. A deep boom, then another, rhythmic, gathering — the glacier calving somewhere out on the face, huge masses of ice letting go into the meltwater, and the vibration came up through the slope under Wren's feet like the skin of a drum.

She stumbled. Caught herself. Kept coming.

And then the snow under her right boot sighed and began to move — not an avalanche, just a shallow surface release, a slab of storm snow the size of a table peeling loose and starting to carry her sideways toward the edge of the shoulder, toward the drop into the calving basin below.

I had the rope. I had three meters of slack and a buried axe and arms that had hauled her out of a crevasse yesterday and had not yet forgiven me for it. I threw myself flat, wrapped the line twice around my forearm, and braced.

The jerk nearly dislocated my shoulder. She slid, screamed, stopped — half over the edge, held by eleven millimeters of salvage rope and whatever her father had put in the plates that kept her believing we'd make it. I walked the rope backward hand over hand, my boots plowing furrows, until her fingers found the rock of the shoulder and she dragged herself over the lip and onto solid ground beside me.

We lay there gasping. Below us, out on the glacier face, a serac the size of a cathedral leaned, groaned, and fell, and the sound of it rolled up the valley like the closing of an enormous door.

"That's the window opening," Wren whispered, watching the ice dust rise. "Sable. That's the calving. We're in it now."

Above us, the col stood clear against the sky, one day's climb away.

Behind us, far down-valley, something smaller moved — a dot on the moraine, deliberate, unhurried, climbing.

Corvane was done being a day behind.
