## 17. Corvane's Offer

We climbed through the night because stopping had become a thing other people did.

The glacier above the ice shelf rose in long gray staircases, broken every few hundred yards by crevasse fields that Wren read from her father's cipher like a priest reading scripture — *bridge at the third fracture, cross at dawn light, never trust blue ice after noon* — while I read the mountain itself, the old way, with my boots and my gut and thirty years of listening to what snow says before it kills you. Between us we made a kind of competence neither of us owned alone.

By the time the sky went the color of dirty pewter, we'd gained the shoulder of the col and could see it: the notch in the ridge, maybe four hundred vertical feet above, rimmed with wind-plume blowing off into Kestria like smoke from a chimney. Beyond it, nothing I had ever seen. Beyond it, the country that wasn't on any map.

Wren sat down hard on a boulder of ice-embedded granite and laughed — a thin, cracked sound, more exhaustion than joy.

"We're going to make it," she said. "Sable. We're actually—"

"Quiet."

I'd heard it before I saw it: the particular rhythm of crampons on ice that isn't hurrying. A climber who is chasing you climbs fast, ragged, loud. This was someone climbing well. Someone conserving.

He came up out of the moraine fog below us at a steady, unhurried pace, a lean figure in gray wool with an ice axe slung across his back like a scholar's satchel, and when he reached the base of our slope he stopped, planted his axe, and looked up at us with the mild expression of a man arriving slightly early for an appointment.

Corvane.

I'd imagined him a hundred ways since the burned ferry contract — huge, scarred, flanked by hunters. In person he was none of those things. He was small and neat and somewhere past fifty, with spectacles fogged at the edges and the soft hands of a man who paid others to do violence. He looked like a provincial tax assessor. He looked harmless.

That was the most frightening thing about him.

"Miss Vhalen," he said pleasantly, to Wren. "Master Fairweather." The name landed on me like a dropped stone. He smiled at whatever crossed my face. "You'll forgive the intrusion. I've been climbing for two days and I confess I'm not built for it." He patted the ice beside him as if inviting us to sit. "Might I have a quarter hour? You can keep your axes drawn. I came alone — you may search me if it would ease your minds."

"Your men are behind you," I said.

"My men are a day and a half behind me, and I told them to stay there." He removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with maddening calm, and replaced them. "What I have to say, I prefer to say without witnesses. Including mine."

Wren's hand was white-knuckled on her axe. Mine wasn't much better. But there was truth in what he said — the valley below was empty, the wind plume the only movement on the ridge — and something in his stillness said he knew exactly how this looked, and had chosen it deliberately.

"You have your quarter hour," I said. "From over there." I pointed to a spot ten yards off our line, where the slope fell away toward a crevasse field. If he moved wrong, gravity would do my work for me.

"Sensible," he said, and walked there, unhurried, and sat down on his pack as though it were a park bench in a civilized square.

* * *

"I want to begin," he said, "by complimenting you. Genuinely. The rockfall on the ridge — textbook. I lost two good men to it, one permanently, and I want you to know I bear no grudge. They were warned. I pay for risk; they accepted it. That's the whole of our arrangement, and it always has been." He folded his hands. "Which brings me to why I'm here."

"You're here to take the plates," Wren said.

"No. I'm here to buy them. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." He turned his head and looked out over the glacier, at the serac fields leaning in the middle distance like drunks against a wall. "Do either of you know what I actually do? Not what the river towns say. What I do."

"Nobody knows what you do," I said. "That's rather the point of you."

"What I do," Corvane said, "is forget things. Professionally. A border shifts; a village stands on the wrong side of it; the village becomes an embarrassment. I am retained, and the village ceases to appear on maps, in censuses, in the memories of clerks. A surveyor draws a road through a duke's timber claim. I am retained, and the road becomes a rumor, then a fable, then nothing. I don't burn books, Master Fairweather. Burning leaves ash, and ash can be read. I simply arrange for the world to have never bothered writing anything down." He smiled faintly. "It is quieter work than you'd imagine. Most of my career has been paperwork."

"And Vhalen?" Wren's voice shook. "Was that paperwork too?"

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man meeting a colleague's child.

"Vhalen was my masterpiece," he said quietly. "Three treaties rest on the fiction that no pass exists through the Serpent Glacier. Kestria's eastern garrison, Ondmark's tariff wall, the Federation's mining concessions — all of them priced on the assumption that the high country is impassable. Your father proved otherwise, Miss Vhalen. He mapped a route that would let a loaded mule train cross in six days instead of sixty around the coast. And then he hid it inside his own survey, ciphered it, and died before he could decide what to do with it." He spread his hands. "I've spent eleven years making sure no one else decided for him. Until you pulled a chest out of a river."

The wind moaned off the col. Somewhere deep in the glacier, ice settled with a sound like a giant shifting in sleep.

"So here is my offer," Corvane said. "Not violence. Violence is what I use when buying fails, and buying almost never fails, because most people want what I'm selling." He reached into his coat and drew out a slim ledger, bound in oiled leather, and held it up so we could see it was real. "In this book are three names. One is a harbor clerk in Kestria who will swear he never recognized you. One is a guild master in Ondmark who will withdraw the testimony that blacklisted you. One is a woman in the lowlands whose family died on a mountain you didn't sign the report for — she believes you murdered her husband, Master Fairweather, because someone very competent made sure she does. All three names. All three corrections. Signed, witnessed, delivered, whether or not you accept my offer — because I find that gifts open negotiations better than threats."

My breath stopped. Wren looked at me sharply, but I couldn't look at her. The woman's name. Eleven years I'd carried that name like a stone sewn into my coat, and this soft little man had just picked it up and weighed it in front of me like a grocer weighing flour.

"And if we sell?" Wren asked. Her voice was flat now. Careful.

"If you sell," Corvane said, "the plates come with me, the cipher dies with your father's memory, and the world goes on believing the Serpent Glacier is a wall. In exchange: fifty thousand crowns, split however you like, deposited wherever you like, under names of your choosing. New papers. New histories. I am, among other things, extremely good at new histories." He paused, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "And anonymity. Real anonymity. Not the poor man's version you've been living — running, hiding, working ferries under other men's contracts. I mean the genuine article. No blacklist. No hunters. No clerk in some river town squinting at your face. You could disappear so completely, Master Fairweather, that even I would lose you. And I promise you—" he tapped his temple, "—no one else alive could say the same."

* * *

He let the silence sit. That was his craft, I realized. Not the ledger, not the money. The silence. He'd been doing this for thirty years — sitting on cold ground across from frightened people, offering them their own hearts back at a price, and waiting for them to notice that what he was holding out was exactly what they'd already told themselves they wanted.

Because it was. That was the obscene thing. It was everything I wanted.

Fifty thousand crowns was a number so large it didn't mean money; it meant never hauling another drunk merchant's barge through floodwater. The names meant the stone gone from my coat, the woman in the lowlands able to bury her hate somewhere softer than my throat. And the anonymity — gods, the anonymity. I had built my whole life since the mountain into a shape whose only purpose was to be forgotten. Corvane wasn't offering to change my life. He was offering to perfect it.

Beside me, Wren had gone very still.

"You're thinking about it," she said. Not accusing. Worse — she sounded like she'd expected it.

"I'm thinking about the fact that he knows things about me I've never told anyone," I said. "Including you."

"He's lying about some of it," Wren said. "Or all of it. That's what people like him do."

"People like me," Corvane corrected mildly, "keep excellent records. Check the ledger yourself, Miss Vhalen. I insist. Distrust is healthy in negotiation." He set the book down on the ice between us, equidistant, and stepped back from it with his empty hands visible. "Take it. Read it. Burn it if you like. I'll climb down and leave you the mountain either way."

Neither of us moved toward it.

"Here is what I cannot offer," Corvane went on, and now his voice changed — dropped its warmth, became almost clinical, a lecturer's voice. "I cannot offer you the pass. Understand what the pass *is*. It is not freedom. It is a wound in three countries' borders, and wounds attract armies. Publish your father's map and within five years there will be a fort at the col, a tariff station in the valley, and a garrison road where the glacier was. Refugees will flow through it until the states seal it, and then it will be sealed, and the sealing will be done by men with less imagination and worse manners than me. You will have traded a secret for a road, and roads," he said, "are how governments eat mountains."

"That's not yours to decide," Wren said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It's yours. That's rather why I'm sitting on the ice talking instead of letting my men do what I pay them for." He stood, brushed off his coat, retrieved his axe. "I'll be at the moraine camp below until tomorrow midday. After that, my associates come up, and I won't be able to hold them — I never could, that's the fiction we all maintain, that the dogs are someone else's. Think on it. Sleep on it, if you can sleep at altitude." He turned to go, then paused, and looked back at me with an expression I hadn't seen on him before — tired, almost wistful.

"For what it's worth, Master Fairweather, I read the original incident file. All of it, not the redacted copy. I know what you signed and why." He resettled his spectacles. "I erase things for a living. So believe me when I tell you: you are the only man I have ever met who erased himself better than I could have. It was beautiful work. It would be a shame to undo it now."

Then he was gone, down-slope, unhurried, fading into the moraine fog like ink into water.

* * *

We didn't speak for a long time.

The wind off the col filled the silence, and beneath it the glacier talked to itself — cracks, sighs, the deep percussion of something calving far away. The window was open. Somewhere below, Corvane's hunters were climbing, and tomorrow midday was a door swinging shut.

Wren finally sat down on the granite and put her face in her hands. When she looked up, her eyes were red-rimmed but level.

"The woman," she said. "The one whose family died. Is it true? What he said — that you signed the report to protect someone?"

I looked at the ledger lying on the ice between us, close enough to touch, radiating warmth like a stove. Fifty thousand crowns. Three names. A life with the stone taken out of it.

"It's true," I said.

"Then he's not lying."

"He doesn't need to lie. That's what nobody understands about men like him. Everything he said is probably true. The fort at the col. The armies. Maybe even the part about the map becoming a road." I crouched, slowly, and picked up the ledger. It was lighter than it should have been. "He offered me everything I've spent eleven years telling myself I wanted. And the worst part, Wren — the worst part is that standing here holding it, I can't tell anymore whether that's what I wanted, or just what I settled for because wanting anything bigger got people killed."

Wren watched me turn the ledger over in my hands.

"What are you going to do?"

I looked up at the col — the notch in the ridge, the wind plume streaming off it into the unmapped sky, four hundred feet of ice between here and the other side of everything. Behind us, down-valley, the fog where Corvane had vanished. Ahead, the crevasse fields, the seracs, the calving glacier, the window already narrowing.

I opened the ledger to its first page and began, methodically, to feed it into the little brass spirit stove.

"Ask me at the top," I said.
