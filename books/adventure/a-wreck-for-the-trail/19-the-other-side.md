## 19. The Other Side

Wren had imagined this moment a hundred times during the crossing — the last crevasse behind them, the col at their backs, and the world opening out beyond the pass like a held breath finally released. In her imaginings there was always music, or light, or some grand gesture of arrival. What she got instead was wind, and gray scree running down into a valley no one had drawn, and Sable Fairweather sitting down hard on a boulder as if their legs had simply resigned without notice.

They had come through.

The thought arrived in pieces, the way the glacier had calved around them — in slabs, in thunder, in the long white silence after. Somewhere below them, on the Kestrian side of the ice, Corvane was gone. Wren kept turning that over too, the way you probe a bruise. She had watched Sable hold out a rope across forty feet of shivering air. She had watched the man on the other end look at it — look at *her*, at the plates strapped to Sable's pack, at everything he'd crossed a country to erase — and step back instead of forward.

She still didn't know what to call what she felt about that. Grief for a man who would have killed them both, maybe. Or grief for whatever he'd been before he became a ledger with legs.

The valley below was real. That was the thing she couldn't stop looking at. It ran north and west into haze, braided with meltwater streams, unmarked by so much as a boundary stone. Her father's cipher had described it: *beyond the shoulder, green going gold, water talking all the way down.* He had written that twenty years ago, from memory, from a survey he knew would never be printed. And here it was, exactly as he'd left it, waiting.

Wren sat down next to Sable before her knees could make the decision for her.

Neither of them spoke for a while. The wind came up the valley in long slow pushes, smelling of stone and snowmelt and something greener than either of them had breathed in weeks. Below, a stream caught the low sun and broke it apart.

Sable's hands were shaking. Not badly — a fine tremor, the kind that comes after adrenaline spends itself and the body presents its bill. They were wrapped around a tin cup of tea Wren didn't remember brewing, which meant Sable must have made it, which meant somewhere in the last hour Sable had gone through the motions of living while Wren stood at the edge of the shelf staring back the way they'd come.

"You should eat," Wren said.

"In a minute."

"You said that an hour ago."

"Did I." Sable drank anyway. The tremor settled by degrees, like sediment dropping out of river water.

Wren unwrapped the last of the flatbread and split it without asking. This was how they'd learned to feed each other over five weeks of monsoon and ice — not with offers but with assumptions, food appearing in the other's hand, water held out wordlessly. She handed half over and watched the mountains behind them instead of watching Sable's face, because some things were easier to say to a mountain.

"My father stood here," she said. "Or near here. The bearing in his notes puts him maybe two hundred meters east, where the rock's more stable."

"He surveyed it in summer?"

"Late summer. Calving season, same window we used. He wrote that you get three days, maybe four, and then the meltwater turns the lower icefield into soup." She turned the bread over in her fingers without eating it. "He never crossed. Did you know that? He mapped the pass from both sides, but the middle — the part where the glacier moves — he only ever did from a distance. With lenses and triangulation. He drew a route through ice he never set foot on."

Sable was quiet a moment. "And you walked it."

"We walked it." Wren looked at her own boots, crusted white, the stitching abraded to threads. "On his map. On guesses he made twenty years ago from a hillside. Every safe line we took today, every place we stepped where the ice held — that was him. He's been dead eleven years and he carried us across that glacier today."

The wind filled the silence. When Sable finally spoke, their voice was rough, scraped thin by altitude and cold air.

"He'd have been proud of you. If that means anything, coming from someone who knew him four days."

"It means everything." Wren hadn't meant to say it that plainly. It came out anyway, the way truth does when you're too tired to build walls around it. "It means — nobody's ever said anything like that to me. About him. Everyone who talks about my father either calls him a traitor or a ghost. You called him a guide."

"I called it what I saw."

*What I saw.* Wren almost laughed. Four months ago, in another life, on a dock in Kestria, she'd have given a month's rations to hear Sable Fairweather claim to see anything honestly. Back then every sentence out of them had been a door closing — deflect, diminish, deny. She'd recognized the craft because it was her own craft; she'd lied her way onto that dock and she'd lied her way into Sable's boat, and the two of them had spent the first hundred miles lying in parallel lines that never touched.

Somewhere between the weir and the icefield, the lines had crossed. She couldn't name the day. Maybe the day Sable went under the meltwater after her and came up coughing with a fistful of her collar and an expression of pure fury, as if Wren had personally arranged the flood. Maybe earlier. Maybe the night in the unmapped village, when Sable had sat by the fire with the sick children asleep against their coat and looked, for one unguarded hour, like a person who belonged somewhere.

"Sable." She made herself turn and face them. "What happens now?"

Sable looked out at the valley. The light was failing, gold going to copper going to blue, and it moved across their face in bands as cloud shadows ran down the slopes. For a long time they didn't answer, and Wren let them not answer, because she understood this was the first time anyone had asked them that question and meant it as an open door rather than a trap.

"I don't know," Sable said at last. It sounded like a confession. "I've known what I was doing next for — I don't know how long. Since the party died. Next was always just: farther away. Farther from Kestria, farther from Ondmark, farther from the report with my signature on it. I got very good at *next*. It's the only skill I've practiced in six years." A dry sound, not quite a laugh. "And now I'm standing on the far side of everything, and there's no *farther*. There's just here."

"And here is?"

"Unmapped." Sable said it like tasting the word. "No treaties. No borders. No clerks who know my face. Nobody hunting us — Corvane's employers have no reach past the pass, not yet, not for years. We could walk down into that valley and be anyone. Be no one. Start a farm. Disappear so completely that even we'd lose track of ourselves." Their hands tightened around the tin cup. "That's what I wanted. Six years, that's all I wanted. To be a person nothing stuck to."

"But?"

Sable was silent so long that Wren thought they wouldn't answer. Then:

"But I keep thinking about the village. The elder's face when we brought the children back over the ridge. And I keep thinking about your father drawing a route through ice he never touched, knowing no one would ever print it, doing it anyway. Because the map was true, and true things shouldn't die just because powerful people find them inconvenient." Sable turned, and their eyes in the last light were steady in a way Wren had rarely seen — the wariness burned down to coals, something underneath it finally warm. "I spent six years learning how to vanish. Turns out vanishing is easy. It's staying that costs."

Wren's throat went tight. She looked away, back at the valley, because if she watched Sable's face while saying what she had to say next she'd lose her nerve entirely.

"The plates," she said. "You know what I want. I've wanted it since the dock. My father's work, published, under his name. The route open. Every exile, every erased family, everyone the treaties forgot — a way out that no government can close, because it was never theirs to close." She made herself go on. "But I need to ask you straight, because we've earned straight. If you want to burn them — if being free of Kestria means being free of the plates too — I'll grieve it, and I'll do it. You pulled me out of a flood. You carried me across a glacier. You don't owe my father anything."

"No," Sable agreed. "I don't."

The word hung there, cold and clean as the air. Wren braced.

"So I'll do it because I choose to," Sable said. "Not owed. Chosen."

Wren's head came around. "What?"

"The plates. Copies go to the papers — the free ones, the ones across the water that Kestria can't lean on. Your father's name on the survey. Yours beside it as decoder. Mine as guide, if the route needs verifying, which it will, because no editor prints a glacier crossing off one set of plates." Sable shrugged, and the shrug cost them something; Wren could see it in the set of their shoulders, the old camouflage trying to reassert itself and failing. "Anonymity was a good servant and a terrible master. I'm done paying rent on a grave I dug for myself."

Wren stared at them. Of all the endings she'd rehearsed for this conversation — argument, compromise, a truce of mutual silence — she had never once imagined Sable Fairweather volunteering to be seen.

"They'll reopen the inquiry," she said quietly. "The disaster. Your name will be in every paper in Kestria within a season. Everything you ran from will find you, because you'll have stopped running and told it your address."

"I know."

"And you're sure."

Sable looked down into the empty cup for a moment. When they raised their head, the exhaustion was still there — carved deep, permanent as glacial striations — but under it was something Wren had never seen on that face before. Not peace exactly. Readiness.

"I signed a false name once to protect someone else," Sable said. "Cost me everything. Seems right that the next signature I give is my own, and it buys something for everybody." They passed the cup back to her, and their hand had stopped shaking. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we walk down into a country that doesn't know we're coming. Let's keep it that way till morning."

* * *

Wren didn't sleep. Not really. She dozed against the packs through the short violet dusk and woke when the cold sharpened, and lay listening to the glacier groan behind them like a ship settling, and watched stars come out over a valley that existed on no chart in any archive in the world.

Her father had a word for blank space on a map. He used to tap the empty margins with his pencil and say, *that's not nothing, Wren, that's room.* Room for rivers not yet traced, for roads not yet argued over, for names not yet given. As a child she'd thought it was just something surveyors said. Lying awake under alien stars, she understood it differently. Room was what you got when you stopped hiding. Room was terrifying. Room was the whole point.

Near midnight she heard Sable move. Not toward sleep — toward the eastern edge of the shelf, where the drop fell away into darkness. Wren sat up and watched, unnoticed, as Sable knelt there in the starlight and took something from an inner pocket: a small square of oilcloth, worn soft with handling. She knew what was inside it without seeing. The key plate. The one Sable had hidden even from her, back in the smelting town, back when trust was still a currency they traded in fractions.

Sable unwrapped it and held it up against the sky, turning it, reading the etched lines by starlight the way Wren had taught them — *the ridge lines are the letters, the watercourses are the spaces* — and after a while, softly, in a voice pitched for no one, Sable began to speak. Wren caught fragments on the wind. Bearings. Elevations. A description of the descent route into the valley, spoken aloud like a lesson, like a man teaching himself a language he intended to live in.

Teaching himself, Wren realized, to be a guide again.

She lay back down and let her eyes close, and for the first time since the flooded river took her old life and gave her a chest full of dead nation, sleep came easily. Whatever waited down in that unmapped green — inquiries, hunters, governments, the whole grinding machinery of being *known* — it would arrive tomorrow.

Tonight there was room.

And far below, at the valley's mouth, where the meltwater streams braided together and ran north toward country no treaty had ever named, a light flickered once — small, orange, deliberate. A campfire. Then two. Then a third, strung along the dark like beads on a thread, a line of fires where no line of fires should be.

Someone was already using the route.

Someone had crossed before them.
