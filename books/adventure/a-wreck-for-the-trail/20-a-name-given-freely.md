## 20. A Name Given Freely

The town was called Merrow's Landing on the Kestrian charts and nothing at all on any other map, which suited everyone who lived there. It sat where the river widened and slowed after its long fall from the high country, a sprawl of drying nets and tar-paper roofs and one printing house that had once produced hymnals and now produced whatever paid — broadsheets, bills of lading, obituaries written by people who preferred their dead unexamined.

Sable had been in towns like it before. Had been *in* this one, maybe, years ago, back when anonymity was still something you fell into rather than built. The trick of such places was that nobody looked up from their work long enough to memorize your face, and if they did, they'd already forgotten why.

Wren stood at the window of the rented room above the chandlery, watching the rain come off the river in gray sheets. The plates were spread across the bed between them, wrapped in oilcloth, each one wiped clean of glacier grit for what felt like the hundredth time. Eleven survived of the original fourteen. Two had gone to the village elders as promised — copies, pressed in wax and ink at the smelting town, faithful enough to guide anyone who could read them. One had shattered against the ice below the col, along with the man who'd wanted it badly enough to die there.

The key plate lay on top. Her father's cipher ran down its margin in his small, patient hand: *the pass is real. I hid it where only weather would think to look.*

"You're sure," Wren said. Not a question. She'd asked it four times since they'd come down off the ice, and each time Sable had given her the same answer, and each time she'd asked again anyway, as though repetition might wear a different groove into the stone.

"No," Sable said. "But I'm doing it."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had taken shape somewhere on the descent, in the white silence below the serac field, when Sable had knelt beside Corvane's scattered papers and understood — with the strange clarity that comes only after nearly dying — that the man had been right about one thing. The plates were worth a fortune. Three governments would pay it. Corvane's own employers would have paid triple to see them burn.

And that was exactly why they couldn't be sold or buried. Anything worth that much would be hunted forever. A secret doesn't die; it just changes hands until someone less careful holds it. The only way to make the pass safe was to make it worthless as a secret — to give it to everyone at once, so loudly and so publicly that no archive, no ledger, no soft-spoken buyer with a taste for dead things could ever gather it back in.

"You can't publish a map," Wren had said on the second night downriver, huddled under the tarp while the rain did its monsoon arithmetic against the canvas. "Newspapers can't print survey plates. They don't have the presses."

"They don't need the presses." Sable had turned the key plate over in their hands, feeling the fine engraving of the Vhalen crest under one thumb. "They need the story. A dead nation. A hidden pass. A royal surveyor who outfoxed three governments from inside his own survey office. And proof." They'd looked up at her. "We copy the plates. We write the account. We send it everywhere at once — Merrow's Landing, Port Callas, the Ondmark gazette if we can reach it. Three sets, three cities, same day. After that it isn't ours anymore. That's the point."

"And your name?"

That question had taken longer to answer.

* * *

Now, in the chandlery room, Sable laid out the materials on the floorboards: three copies of the full account, written out twice over in Wren's precise cartographer's hand and Sable's blunt ferryman's scrawl, then recopied until the words sat steady on the page. Three sets of wax-pressed plate facsimiles. Three envelopes, heavy paper, sealed with plain tallow because fancy seals invited curiosity.

The account itself had taken six drafts. Wren had argued for including everything — the sabotage, the old disaster, the report Sable had signed under another name. Sable had argued for leaving all of it out. They had compromised the way exhausted people do: the account told the truth about the map and said nothing about the messenger beyond what the signature required.

Because that was the whole of it, wasn't it. The signature.

Sable picked up the first envelope and held it a long moment, weighing nothing, weighing everything. For eleven years the name *Fairweather* had been a debt collector. It arrived before you did, in harbor ledgers and guild registries, in the flat voice of clerks who checked lists before they checked faces. *Blacklisted. Kestria and Ondmark both. The guide who walked nine clients onto a rotten cornice and signed the incident report as Rell Marsh.* Sable had spent a decade being no one, and no one had kept them alive — no one got hunted, no one got recognized, no one got blamed. No one also got to stop running, but that had seemed, for a long time, like a fair trade.

Then Corvane had found them anyway. The hunters had found them anyway. The river had handed them a chest stamped with a dead country's crest, and every attempt to stay no one had cost more than being someone ever had.

"Say it out loud," Wren said quietly. She'd turned from the window. Rain-light made her look younger and older at once. "Once. Before you seal it."

Sable looked at the envelope. At the line at the bottom of the enclosed account, where the attribution waited:

*Copies of the attached survey plates are deposited with this paper and with two others, this day, in duplicate. The route is real. The pass is open. Let no government buy what has already been given away.*

— Sable Fairweather, guide.

"Fairweather," Sable said. The word came out rough, like a stone turning over in a shallow stream. "Sable Fairweather. Guide."

Not *former* guide. Not *disgraced* guide. Just the name and the work, set down side by side, the way a carpenter signs a doorframe or a mason a wall — not an apology, not a boast. A claim. *I made this. I stand behind it.*

It should have felt like surrender. It felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had forgotten its absence was possible.

"The blacklist," Wren started.

"Is a list," Sable said. "Lists get read by clerks. Clerks read newspapers too. By the time anyone in a guild hall decides what to do about me, the story will have crossed three borders and been reprinted in a dozen languages, and I'll be a footnote to my own name instead of the other way around." A breath. "Corvane understood something I didn't. He knew information is only power while it's scarce. He spent his whole life keeping things scarce. I'm going to spend one afternoon making this thing common as rain."

Wren came and sat across from them, cross-legged on the floorboards, and for a moment neither spoke. Outside, a barge horn sounded low and mournful through the rain.

"He'd have liked this," she said finally. Her father. "He used to say a map isn't finished until someone uses it who wasn't supposed to. He hid the pass hoping — he never said hoping what, exactly. But I think it was this. I think he hoped someday the map would matter more than the men who buried it." She touched the key plate, light as touching ash. "Sign it however you want. But put his name in the account. Aldous Wren, Royal Surveyor of Vhalen. He earned the byline. I just carried it."

"It's already there," Sable said. "First line. Second draft onward."

Something moved across her face — grief and gratitude tangled past separating — and she nodded once and looked away toward the window, blinking hard, and let Sable pretend not to notice.

* * *

They sent the envelopes separately, which was the whole architecture of the plan.

Wren took the first to the *Merrow's Landing Courier*, walking in through the front like any traveler with news to sell, letting the editor's boy carry it back while she lingered over a map of shipping lanes on the office wall long enough to be remembered as ordinary. The second went by mail packet to Port Callas, addressed to the *Callas Herald*, dropped in the shipper's box on the quay with a dozen other letters bound north. Sable carried the third personally, two streets and a bridge away, to a scrivener's shop that doubled as a relay for the Ondmark gazetteers — a gray-bearded man with ink-stained cuffs who accepted the packet, weighed it in his palm, and asked no questions, which cost Sable three times the normal rate and was worth every coin.

By mid-afternoon it was done. Three cities. Three sets of plates. Three signatures in the same blunt hand.

There was no ceremony in it. That surprised Sable most. They walked back across the bridge in the thinning rain and stopped halfway, at the highest point, where the river ran green-gray and swollen beneath them, carrying snowmelt from the very glacier they'd crossed. Somewhere upstream, behind all that gray distance, the pass stood open — a corridor through the ice that no treaty named and no army owned, real now in a way it had never been, because within days strangers would be reading about it over breakfast in three capitals, and within weeks someone would be planning to walk it, and within a season the route would belong to refugees and traders and the merely curious, and no ledger anywhere would be able to call it back.

"You're grinning," Wren said, coming up beside them.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible. Do it more."

Sable hadn't grinned in eleven years, or hadn't noticed doing it, which amounted to the same thing. They looked at the water instead, and found the grin wouldn't leave.

* * *

The blacklist became irrelevant faster than either of them guessed.

Two days later the *Courier* ran it above the fold — *THE PASS THAT THREE EMPIRES BURIED* — with a woodcut of the Vhalen crest copied crudely from the plates and a leader column speculating, breathlessly and mostly wrongly, about which ministries had paid to keep the route dark. By the fourth day the Port Callas papers had picked it up, adding details the originals hadn't contained and inventing several more, and by the sixth day a Kestrian border ministry spokesman was denying everything in language so carefully vague that it confirmed everything.

And on the seventh day, a clerk from the Kestrian Guide's Guild found Sable at the chandlery.

They heard him on the stairs — the particular deliberate tread of a man who wants to be heard coming — and Sable had time to stand, and to decide, strangely calmly, whether to go out the window or the door. They chose the door.

The clerk was young, damp, and visibly uncomfortable, holding his hat in both hands like a shield. "Mister—" He consulted a paper. "Marsh? Or—"

"Fairweather," Sable said. Said it plainly, in a public hallway, in daylight, with Wren watching from the doorway behind. "You'll find both names lead to the same person. What does the Guild want?"

The young man blinked, recalibrating. Whatever speech he'd prepared, it hadn't accounted for being met head-on. "There's — an inquiry, sir. Reopened. Regarding the Ondmark expedition, the one from — eleven years ago. The Ministry's reviewing the incident report. There are... questions about the route assessment. New questions." He swallowed. "You're requested to appear. As a witness, not — not a defendant. The wording matters, sir. Someone insisted on the wording."

Someone. Sable thought of a report signed under another name, of a client's family paid off and silenced, of eleven years of carrying a weight that had never entirely fit the shoulders it was stacked on. Of Wren reading that same report by lamplight two nights ago and saying, *this wasn't your fault, and you know it wasn't, and you carried it anyway.*

"Tell the Guild I'll appear," Sable said. "Under Fairweather. Both names, if they like. In writing."

The clerk wrote it down, thanked them twice, and left looking like a man who'd expected a locked door and found it standing open.

Wren waited until his footsteps faded. "You could run," she said. "Boat's fueled. Tide turns at dusk. We could be forty miles upriver before anyone—"

"I'm done running from paper," Sable said. "Paper's all it ever was. And besides—" they nodded at the table, where the newspapers lay in drifts, the story already growing legs in three countries, "—I've got an alibi now. I'm the person who gave away the pass. Everything else I am is a smaller story than that."

Wren smiled slowly. "Alder Wren, Royal Surveyor of Vhalen, and Sable Fairweather, guide."

"That's us."

"That's us," she agreed, and went to start supper, and the room filled with the smell of rice and river fish and rain, and it was, absurdly, almost home.

* * *

On the ninth evening, the reply came.

Not from a ministry or a guild — those would take weeks, and would arrive wearing official seals and official menace. This came by river runner, a lean girl of perhaps fifteen who climbed the chandlery stairs two at a time and held out a scrap of oilcloth without a word.

Inside was a strip of birchbark, written on in charcoal in a hand Sable knew — the elder's hand, the one from the village, the woman who had traded shelter for a ridge crossing and paid in trust.

*Children over the ridge safe. Hunters gone. Word travels even here. Three families ask: when the route opens lawful, will the guide who signed the map come show us the way? We cannot pay much. We can pay in names — ours, freely given, which is more than maps usually get.*

Below it, a list. Nine names. Then a tenth, added in different charcoal, newer: *and mine — Wren, daughter of Aldous, who kept the map.*

Sable read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you read a thing you intend to keep.

Outside, the rain had stopped for the first time in days, and the river ran bright under a rinsed sky, running north toward the pass, toward the open country beyond, toward spring. Down at the quay, Sable could hear hammering — someone building, always someone building, boats and sheds and lives that didn't appear on any register.

"Well?" Wren said, drying her hands. "What do we tell them?"

Sable folded the birchbark carefully and put it inside their coat, over the heart, where the important cargo rode.

"We tell them yes," Sable said. "And then we'd better find a bigger boat."
