## 22. Where the Trail Starts

The river was lower in spring.

That was the first thing Sable noticed, standing on the bank at Greywater Landing with a mug of chicory going cold in one hand — how much of the world the flood had been hiding all those months. The drowned willows where the chest had snagged stood dry and ordinary now, their roots exposed like knuckles. A year ago that water had been a brown animal with teeth. Today it ran clear and green over gravel bars, chattering about nothing, and Sable found it almost insulting how harmless it looked.

Almost. Rivers never forgot what they were owed. Sable just no longer flinched at the reminder.

Behind them, the new boat sat on the slipway taking on the last of her fittings — a proper boat this time, not the leaky coffin they'd fled upriver in during the monsoon. Forty feet of Kestrian larch on an Ondmark hull, shallow-drafted for the upper reaches, with a covered hold big enough for twelve passengers and their bundles. Wren had drawn the lines herself. She'd argued with the shipwright for three days about the freeboard, won, and then spent another two days painting a small blue compass rose on the transom with a Vhalen star at its center — not the royal crest, she'd been careful to say, because the crest belonged to a dead country, but the surveyor's mark, the one her father had cut into every benchmark from the lowlands to the ice.

*Routes outlive nations,* she'd said, kneeling there with paint to her wrists. *That's the whole point of them.*

Sable hadn't argued. Sable didn't argue much anymore. It wasn't softness, exactly — the old instincts were still there, coiled and ready, the ones that said *never touch anything that matters, never be seen, never stay.* But somewhere between the weir and the pass and the long grey weeks after, those instincts had been joined by others, quieter ones, and the quiet ones were winning.

Stopping no longer cost more than staying. That was the whole of it. That was the miracle nobody wrote ballads about.

* * *

The clients arrived on a Tuesday, as refugees tended to do — in ones and twos, in borrowed carts, carrying more than they owned and less than they'd lost.

There were nine of them for the first run. A grandmother from the Ondmark side whose village had been rezoned out from under her by some treaty clause written in a language she didn't speak; two brothers, fourteen and eleven, sent ahead by parents who couldn't afford passage for all; a young couple with a baby who cried only when the wind dropped, which everyone agreed was backwards; a stonemason, a seamstress, a man who'd been a schoolteacher until his school was declared administratively nonexistent. They came up the landing road blinking in the morning light, clutching papers stamped with the new transit seals — the Vhalen Route Authority, established by international accord eight months after the plates went public, its charter signed by three governments that had once paid Corvane good money to make sure it could never exist.

Sable read each set of papers carefully anyway. Old habits. Then handed them back with a nod.

"Rules are simple," Sable told them, gathering on the dock. "Life vests on when I say. You bail when I say. You don't touch anything marked in red on the boat, and you don't ask me how I know the river." A pause. "You can ask Wren anything you like. She likes being asked."

From the wheelhouse, without looking up from her charts, Wren said, "I do not."

But she was smiling, and the grandmother caught it, and something in the little group loosened — that first unclenching of people who have been afraid so long they've forgotten their shoulders used to sit lower.

They cast off at midmorning. The boat took the current sweetly, and Sable stood at the helm feeling a thing they had no clean name for: the river familiar under the hull, the passengers' weight distributed right, the day unfolding exactly as planned. For years a plan had been a liability. Plans got people killed. Plans got names signed to reports under other names. But this — this was just work. Good work. Work done in daylight, under a name that fit.

"Sandbar shifting off Miller's Point," Wren called from the bow, where she stood with her father's brass sighting instrument, an heirloom she'd carried through everything. "Favor the left channel."

"Favoring."

"You're favoring too early."

"I'm favoring *confidently.*"

The eleven-year-old brother laughed, then clapped both hands over his mouth as if laughter might get him deported. Sable glanced back at him. "It's allowed," they said. "On this boat, it's allowed."

* * *

They made the first camp that night on a gravel shelf below the old smelting town — Salt and Rust, Wren still called it, though the town had a name again now, and half its population had come back since the route opened. Trade did that. Maps did that. A line on a chart, honestly drawn, turned out to be worth more than any treaty seal.

Sable got the cook fire going while Wren checked the mooring lines, and the passengers settled into the rhythm of strangers becoming temporary crew — the stonemason splitting kindling without being asked, the seamstress mending a torn spray cover, the grandmother telling the boys a story in a language Sable half-recognized from border villages that had also lived unregistered, unnamed, off every official chart. The village that had sheltered them had sent word again last month, third time since winter: children safe, ridge trail holding, elder's regards. Sable had written back. That was still strange — writing back, signing the letter, trusting the post.

Wren came and sat close to the fire, shoulder nearly touching Sable's shoulder, and unfolded the chart she worked on evenings: the full Vhalen survey, redrawn, annotated, corrected against everything they'd learned on the crossing. Her father's cipher decoded line by line in her neat hand. Someday it would hang in a cartographers' hall in the capital. For now it hung from a wooden roller in the wheelhouse, salt-stained at the corners, and every passenger aboard looked at it at least once, the way people look at a door.

"The glacier's calving early this year," Wren said quietly. "I had a letter from the survey station at the col. They want us to run an escort trip for their supply team in June. Official contract. With our names on it."

"Our names."

"Well. Yours. Mine's already on things." She tapped the chart. "Yours is the one that took effort."

Sable fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. There had been a time — not even two years gone — when the name *Fairweather* had been a debt collector's knock, a blacklist entry, a report signed in someone else's hand to protect a client's family from a sabotage Sable had seen coming and been powerless to stop. The inquiries had reopened it all last autumn. Some doors had closed permanently; a certain guiding guild in Ondmark would rather dissolve than admit Sable Fairweather through its doors, and that was fine, that was theirs to carry. But other doors had opened, heavier ones, and stayed open. The truth had turned out to be load-bearing.

"Yeah," Sable said. "Put my name on it."

Wren looked over, firelight in her eyes, and grinned like a girl catching her father's star in a brass instrument. "Knew you'd say that."

"You didn't."

"I hoped loudly."

* * *

The second morning brought fog off the water, thick as wool, and Sable did what fog demanded: anchored, waited, taught. The passengers gathered in the wheelhouse out of the damp, and Sable found — to their own mild surprise — that teaching had become part of the job and part of the pleasure. Reading water. That was the lesson today, the same one Sable had once taught Wren in the monsoon dark: how current writes its intentions on the surface, how a smooth tongue of water can mean depth or can mean a rock just beneath wearing a disguise, how eddies lie and seams tell the truth.

"See that line?" Sable pointed through the wheelhouse glass at a faint ripple threading the fog. "That's the river deciding where it wants to go. My whole job is agreeing with it before it insists."

The schoolteacher raised a hand like he was still in a classroom. "And if you disagree?"

"Then the river wins, and we find out what we're made of." Sable let that sit a beat. "I've disagreed twice in my life. First time cost me everything. Second time—" they glanced at Wren, who pretended very hard to be studying her chart, "—second time the river was on my side, and I still nearly lost the person who matters most. So now I agree with rivers."

The grandmother said something soft in her own language, and the stonemason translated: "She says a guide who respects the water is a guide who respects the people in the boat."

"Tell her she's hired."

"She says she's ninety-one."

"Everyone's hired. That's rule four."

By noon the fog burned off and they ran the middle reach clean, and in the afternoon they passed the drowned willows — dry-topped now, ordinary, just trees — and Sable looked at them longer than they meant to. Wren noticed. Wren always noticed.

"That's where it started," she said, coming to stand at the rail.

"That's where it started."

"You ever wish you'd left it in the water?"

Sable considered it honestly, the way they'd learned to consider most things. The chest had cost them a leaking hull, a burned ferry contract, a season of hunters, a night on an ice shelf with the temperature dropping and the bridge gone, a serac fall that still visited Sable's sleep sometimes wearing Corvane's face. It had cost anonymity, which for years Sable had called freedom and knew better now.

"No," Sable said. "I wish a lot of people hadn't died for it. I wish her father had lived to see the charts printed. I wish the world needed less rescuing. But that chest?" They watched the willows slide past. "It was the first true thing I ever pulled out of a river. Everything else I'd ever hauled aboard was just salvage."

Wren said nothing for a while. Then: "My father used to say a map is a promise the land makes to whoever reads it honestly." She bumped her shoulder against Sable's arm. "He'd have liked you. He distrusted everyone, too."

"He'd have hated me first."

"For about a week," Wren agreed. "Then forever after."

* * *

They delivered the nine at the upper terminus on the fifth day — a rough stone quay below the glacier's moraine, where the Route Authority had built a waystation out of the bones of an old Vhalen watchpost. The transit officer who met them checked papers, stamped them, and then shook Sable's hand in front of everyone, plainly, by name, because that was simply what happened now.

The passengers disembarked into their new lives. The grandmother paused at the gangway, gripped Sable's forearm with surprising strength, and said something long and formal in her own tongue. The stonemason translated, a little awkwardly: "She says: the river kept what it was owed, and gave back more. She says your family travels light but rows deep."

Sable watched them go up the trail toward the pass — the pass, *their* pass, open now to anyone with honest papers and honest intent — until the fog took them, and felt the strangest ache, the good kind, the kind that means a thing has finished properly.

The run home was quiet. Wren took the helm for the middle stretch and Sable sat on the roof of the wheelhouse in the last of the sun, mending a frayed line, hands busy, mind pleasantly empty. Below, faintly, they could hear her humming — a tune from Vhalen, one of the few songs that had survived the erasure because it had survived in her father's head, and then in hers, and now, apparently, in the acoustics of a wheelhouse on a boat called the *Fair Weather*, because Wren had painted the name on the transom without asking and Sable had looked at it for a long moment and said nothing, which they both understood to be the loudest yes ever given.

Halfway down the reach, Wren killed the engine and let her drift.

"Sable." Her voice came up through the deck boards. "Letter came while we were loading. Forwarded from the waystation."

"Oh?"

"There's a family. Six of them, from the flooded districts past the delta. They need to go up next month — the youngest is sick, needs the mountain air, the doctors say. And there's..." she hesitated, and Sable could hear her choosing words, which was unlike her, "...there's a second letter too. From the cartographers' hall. They want me to come lecture. The capital. A whole season."

The line went still in Sable's hands.

"They said I could bring a co-instructor," Wren added, too casually. "Someone with field experience. Someone who reads water." A pause. "Someone whose name opens doors now instead of closing them."

Sable looked downstream, toward Greywater Landing, toward the slipway, toward the small house they'd leased in March with a view of the water and room enough for two desks. Staying. The word didn't snag anymore. It didn't cost. It just sat there, warm as the sun on the deck, waiting to be picked up.

"Tell the family yes," Sable said. "We'll take them up."

"And the hall?"

Sable coiled the mended line, slow, and smiled at the river — at the whole shining, ordinary, promised length of it.

"Tell them their guide is available."

Below, the compass rose gleamed on the transom, the Vhalen star at its center pointing steadily north, and the *Fair Weather* turned for home on a current that finally, at long last, was going the same way they were.
