## 1. Fuel, Debt, and Weather

The Beaver started on the fourth try, which Paloma Kavanagh considered a good morning.

She let it cough, catch, cough again, then settle into that ragged idle that sounded like an old man clearing his chest before telling a story he'd told a hundred times. Outside the windscreen, mist hung over the strip at Little Current Bay in the way it always did before seven—low, gray, patient. The kind of weather that didn't threaten anything. The kind that just waited to see what you'd do.

"Come on, girl," she said, patting the panel the way other people patted dogs. "We've got medicine and a schedule."

The schedule was a fiction. There was no schedule in the interior. There was only weather, fuel, and whatever the communities needed when they needed it, and Paloma had learned years ago that pretending otherwise was how pilots ended up dead or broke. Usually both.

She ran her checks by feel. Mixture rich. Flaps set. Fuel gauges lying to her in their usual optimistic way—she'd dipped the tanks herself at dawn, so she knew the truth: three hours and change, maybe four if she flew her like a grandmother. Above the instrument panel, clipped to the frame with a binder clip gone brittle with age, hung the ledger page.

*Kavanagh, T. — outstanding balance.*

Her father's name in someone else's handwriting. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars, owed to Meridian Air Services for a plane he'd bought on credit two seasons before the river took him, along with everything else he'd ever promised anyone. Paloma had inherited the debt the way you inherit eye color or a bad knee—not chosen, not earned, just there, part of the machinery of being who she was.

She'd crossed out nothing on that page. She'd never once written *paid* beside any figure. But every month the number shrank, and every month she let herself believe, briefly, in the arithmetic of escape: two hundred runs, three hundred, and then one day she'd pull the page off its clip, fold it into a paper airplane, and throw it out over the water.

She hadn't decided yet whether she'd actually do it. Some mornings it was the only thing that got her out of the bunk.

The Beaver lifted off the strip at 7:12, wheels leaving the gravel with the reluctant grace of something too heavy for its own good, and the bay fell away beneath her—black water, black spruce, the thin silver thread of the inlet road where maybe nine people lived year-round. She banked south toward Kettle Landing and felt the old familiar settling, the moment the ground stopped mattering and the machine became the whole of her address.

This was the part nobody understood unless they'd done it. Flying supply wasn't romantic. It was weight-and-balance math and weather guessing and knowing which of your customers would have the dock cleared and which would make you wade. It was insulin packed against ice packs, dog food, chain oil, a coffin once, a bride twice. It was being useful in a way that required you to be present and invisible at exactly the same time.

Paloma had gotten very good at invisible.

* * *

Kettle Landing smelled like woodsmoke and fish guts, which meant home to roughly forty people and meant nothing at all to the government maps, which showed it as an unpopulated point labeled with an elevation figure. She backed the Beaver up to the dock—no brakes worth trusting, so she did it with the prop wash and a prayer—and Mrs. Okonkwo was already waiting with the manifest and a thermos.

"You look tired," Mrs. Okonkwo said, handing up a crate marked FRAGILE in letters that had been rewritten twice, each time larger.

"I look like a woman who's been flying."

"That is what I said." The old woman watched her unload the insulin into the cooler without being asked, because Mrs. Okonkwo had been watching Paloma since she was nineteen and had never once offered help unasked, only presence. "Your father used to tie off wrong on this dock. Every time. I told him."

"He mentioned." He hadn't. Her father hadn't mentioned much of anything in his last year, except the debt, once, drunk, and the river, twice, sober, in a voice she still thought about more than she wanted to.

"You eat?"

"I'll eat."

"You won't." Mrs. Okonkwo pressed the thermos into her hands anyway. It was the closest thing to love either of them could afford, and both of them knew it, and neither of them named it.

By ten she was loaded again—outbound freight, mail, a teenager heading to Fort Renner for dental work who slept in the back seat the entire leg—and by noon she was back on the strip at Little Current Bay, tying down against a forecast that couldn't decide whether it wanted to be rain or nothing. The radio crackled through the afternoon with the usual traffic: float operators, a forestry patrol, somebody's cousin lost near the narrows and found again before anyone launched a boat.

It was almost four when the call came through on a frequency she didn't recognize.

"Any station, any station, this is Survey Unit Six, government charter request."

Paloma's hand hovered over the mic. Government charters paid well. That was the trap of them—they paid like people who had never once worried about money, and they filed paperwork, and they logged routes, and within a season there were men in clean jackets standing on your strip asking questions about your maintenance records and your flight paths and your father's outstanding balance, which was somehow public knowledge to anyone with a badge.

She keyed the mic. "Survey Six, this is Kavanagh. What do you need?"

"Charter service, Ms. Kavanagh. Personnel and equipment transport into the upper interior. We understand you're rated for the canyon strips."

"We heard wrong."

A pause. When the voice came back it had smoothed itself out, the way voices did when they were reading from something. "We're conducting a hydrological survey of the upper watershed. Eleven personnel. We'd need recurring service, weekly at minimum. The compensation would be—"

"No."

Another pause, longer. "Ms. Kavanagh, we haven't quoted a figure."

"Don't need one." She watched the mist start to come back over the water, earlier than it should have, thicker than it should have. "I fly private runs. Medicine, mail, freight. I don't do official work."

"The interior is underserved. Your neighbors would benefit from—"

"My neighbors," Paloma said, "do fine." She let go of the transmit button before she could say anything else, because the truth was uglier than *I don't want your money*. The truth was that eleven strangers with instruments and clipboards and a ministry behind them were exactly the kind of eyes she'd spent five years making sure never landed on her routes, her ledger, her name. Official work meant official scrutiny, and official scrutiny meant the debt page above her panel becoming a file in somebody's drawer, and files had a way of turning into levers.

The radio hissed. Then, quieter, almost conversational: "We'll be in the region some weeks yet, Ms. Kavanagh. If you reconsider."

"Safe surveying," she said, and switched the frequency over to weather, and told herself the small cold feeling in her stomach was just hunger, like Mrs. Okonkwo said.

* * *

Evening came down gray and windless. She sat in the shack she rented from the band office—two rooms, a stove, a window that looked out on the tied-down Beaver rocking gently at its moorings like a horse dozing at a fence—and ate canned stew and listened to the lake work at the shore.

Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars. She'd checked the number that morning the way she checked it every morning, a superstition dressed as bookkeeping. At her current rate she'd be clear in four years. Four years of flying careful, charging fair, keeping her head down and her routes dull and her name off every list that mattered.

She was twenty-nine years old and her entire future fit on one side of a piece of paper.

From the window she could see the whole settlement: the dock lights, the generator hum, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys in the still air. People who trusted her with their insulin and their mail and their dead. People who called her by her first name and knew nothing else about her—not the debt, not the nights she lay awake doing arithmetic, not the fact that she could not remember the last time she'd eaten a meal across a table from another human being on purpose.

That was the trade. That was the deal she'd signed without signing anything. They got a pilot who showed up; she got a life small enough to hide inside.

The radio on the shelf clicked and hissed—the overnight channel, mostly static this time of year. She was reaching to switch it off when a voice came through, thin and broken by distance, the words arriving in pieces like cargo dropped from height.

"—Survey Six, position—" A burst of noise. "—canyon, coordinates—" Static swallowed it whole, then gave back a fragment: "—anyone copy, we are—"

Then nothing. Not silence exactly. The carrier wave stayed open, a long empty breath of signal, as if someone on the far end had set down the microphone and walked away from it mid-sentence.

Paloma stood with her hand on the dial for a long time, the stew going cold in the bowl, the mist pressing against the window glass.

The coordinates, when the regional office read them back to her an hour later, were inside the canyon. The canyon no chart marked correctly. The canyon she'd refused to fly for, that same afternoon, for eleven strangers with instruments and clipboards.

She looked at the ledger page above the panel, visible even now through the dark window, pale in the moonlight like something pinned under glass.

Then she went to get her boots.
