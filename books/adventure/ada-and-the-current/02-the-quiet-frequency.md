## 2. The Quiet Frequency

The radio woke her before the alarm did.

Paloma had slept in the bunk room behind the charter office, as she did most nights when a run was scheduled before dawn, and the set on the shelf above her head crackled to life with the particular static of a frequency nobody was supposed to be using. She lay still for a moment, listening, one hand already reaching for the volume knob out of habit. In the interior you learned to sort radio noise the way city people sorted traffic—by texture, by urgency, by what it meant for your morning.

This was not weather talk. This was not the freight office or the fishing boats or old Marta at the trading post checking whether anyone had seen her husband's skiff.

This was the survey camp.

"—repeat, grid nine, we're getting—" A man's voice, clipped and professional, then a sound Paloma couldn't name. Not wind. Not feedback. Something between a hum and a held breath, as if the air itself had leaned into the microphone. "—instruments are reading off the—"

The transmission folded in on itself and went quiet.

She sat up in the dark. The bunk room smelled of coffee grounds and engine oil, the two constants of her life, and the ledger hung where it always hung—pinned above the chart table in the main room, her father's debt written out in his own careful hand, every line item, every interest charge, every month he'd borrowed against the Beaver to keep flying. Forty-one thousand, three hundred and six dollars. She'd stopped counting the days since she'd inherited it. The number counted itself now, automatically, the way she checked the fuel gauges.

The radio stayed silent for eleven minutes. She knew because she watched the clock, sitting on the edge of the bunk with her boots half-laced, telling herself it was nothing. Camps went quiet. Generators failed. Men turned their sets down to argue about dinner.

Then the mayday came through, and it was wrong in every way a mayday could be wrong.

"Mayday, mayday, mayday—" The voice was different this time. Younger. Frayed at the edges like rope dragged over rock. "Kavanagh—if anyone—coordinates—" And then a string of numbers that made her stomach drop, because she knew that country, and those numbers did not sit where anything should sit. They put the caller inside the canyon. Not above it. Not along the rim where the survey had been granted its permits. Inside it, down where the walls closed to less than two hundred feet and the river—the one the locals called the Current, said with a flatness that had always struck her as deliberate—ran green and slow and deep.

No chart marked the canyon correctly. She'd complained about it for years. The government sheets showed the river bending east; it bent west. They showed the canyon floor at four hundred feet elevation; her altimeter disagreed every single pass. She'd learned to fly it by eye and memory and the stubborn conviction that her own instruments were worth more than Ottawa's cartography.

The mayday cut off mid-word. No "over." No sign-off. Just gone, like a door closing on a lit room.

* * *

By seven she was standing in the regional office, dripping lake water off her boots onto the linoleum, arguing with a man named Dussault who managed airspace the way other men managed parking lots—badly, and with a clipboard.

"You're not rated for that strip," Dussault said, though they both knew he was wrong.

"I'm the only one rated for that strip," Paloma said. "Name another pilot who'll take the Kettle into that canyon in October. Go ahead. I'll wait."

Dussault rubbed his face. He was tired in the way bureaucrats get tired—not from work but from responsibility arriving without resources. "The survey team has its own aircraft support contracted out of Yellowknife. Their plane's down for maintenance."

"So send a helicopter."

"It's three hours out and the weather window is closing. You're here. You know the approach. The regional director signed off an hour ago." He slid a paper across the desk: a search authorization, her tail number already typed into the blank, as though someone had known it would be her before she'd agreed. That detail bothered her more than she let show. "Visual sweep first. Land only if there's a reason to land."

"There's a reason to land," Paloma said. "They called my name."

That stopped him. "Your name?"

"In the mayday. 'Kavanagh—if anyone.'" She shrugged, making it smaller than it felt. "Maybe they've got my charter card from the fuel dock. Maybe somebody recognized the Beaver. Doesn't matter. They asked."

Dussault looked at her for a long moment, and something in his expression shifted—not warmth exactly, but the recognition of a person who understood debts. "Bring them out, Kavanagh. All of them."

"All of them," she repeated, and hated how easily the promise came, how much it sounded like her father, who had once promised a widow he'd bring her son home from a trapline and had flown into whiteout trying.

* * *

The flight north took ninety minutes under a sky the color of wet slate. Paloma flew with the window cracked despite the cold, needing the smell of the lake country in the cockpit, needing something real against the memory of that hum on the radio. Twice she keyed the survey frequency and called. Twice the channel answered with open-carrier silence—that dead hiss of a set transmitting to no one, which is somehow worse than static, because static at least means weather.

The Current appeared the way it always did: too late, too sudden, a seam of green splitting the black spruce. She dropped toward the canyon mouth and felt the familiar wrongness settle over her—the charts said left, the river said right, and her hands followed the river because the river had never lied to her yet.

The camp sat on a gravel bench above the waterline, six tents in two neat rows, and from two hundred feet up it looked fine. Too fine. Smoke would have been reassuring; there was none. But the tents were pitched taut, guy-lines straight, gear stowed in the manner of people who expected to come back before dark.

She circled twice. No movement. No figures on the beach. The floatplane strip below the bench—a stretch of calm water locals used for landing, the only place within fifty miles a Beaver could put down—lay empty and glass-still.

Paloma set the Beaver down anyway.

The water took her floats without a sound. She taxied to the gravel, shut down, and stepped out into a silence so complete she could hear the river moving under its surface, a low continuous note like a finger circling the rim of a glass. The locals said the Current hummed sometimes. She'd always assumed it was water over gravel. Standing there, she wasn't sure.

"Tents were warm," she said aloud, just to break the quiet, because pilots talked to themselves and everyone knew it.

Inside the first tent, the kettle on the camp stove was still warm. Not hot—warm, the way metal stays after someone has stepped away minutes ago, not hours. There were eleven bedrolls. Eleven mugs, eight of them washed and stacked, three still holding tea gone cold and skin-thick. A card game lay abandoned mid-hand, cards face-down, as if the players had stood up all at once.

Eleven people. The survey manifest Dussault had given her listed twelve names. She counted the mugs again. Counted the bedrolls. Twelve names, eleven places set, and no note anywhere explaining the arithmetic.

She walked the camp perimeter slowly, doing the thing searchers do—calling out, listening, marking what didn't fit. What didn't fit was nearly everything. Boots lined up outside the tents in tidy pairs. A theodolite on its tripod, capped and lashed, ready for transport rather than use. And near the supply tent, a hard case stamped with a government crest, sealed with wire and lead, the kind of container you don't issue to a party measuring river gradients.

She left it. Dussault had said visual sweep, land only if there's a reason. She was already past that line. She wasn't going to start opening sealed cases on her own authority.

The tracks started at the edge of the gravel bench and ran toward the river. Boot prints, dozens of them, overlapping—twelve sets, maybe, walking abreast in a way that no field team walks, bunched together like children crossing a schoolyard. The prints were even. Unhurried. Nobody running. Nobody dragged.

They ended at the waterline.

Not faded. Not lost in gravel wash. Ended. The last clear print was a full boot-depth in wet sand, toes pointing at the water, and beyond it the sand lay smooth as poured concrete, as if nothing had ever crossed it. As if the river had reached up and wiped the page.

Paloma stood at the bank for a long time. The Current moved beneath her, green and unhurried, and that low note rose again through the soles of her boots, fainter than hearing, closer to feeling. She thought of the garbled transmission, the coordinates inside the canyon, the voice fraying mid-word. She thought of twelve names and eleven mugs.

Then she saw him.

Downstream, maybe eighty meters, a figure stood at the waterline where the tracks ended—or where tracks like these had ended—and he was swaying slightly, a man standing in wind that wasn't blowing. He wore survey coveralls with the name tape torn off. He wasn't calling for help. He was looking at the water the way you look at a door you've forgotten how to open.

Paloma's hand went to the radio on her hip, and for the first time in years—since her father's funeral, since the ledger, since she'd learned that asking for help costs more than it returns—she hesitated before speaking.

"Dussault," she transmitted finally, keeping her voice level. "I've found one of them. One survivor. Sending position." She paused, watching the man sway at the edge of the green water. "There's something else. I don't know what to call it yet."

The river hummed. The man turned, slowly, and looked at her across eighty meters of empty sand, and his expression was not the expression of a rescued man.

It was the expression of someone who had been about to go back in.
