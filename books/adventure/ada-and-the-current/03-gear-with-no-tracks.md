## 3. Gear With No Tracks

The canyon didn't want her there. Paloma felt it before she saw it—a pressure in the ears, a tightening in the chest, the Beaver wallowing in air that moved wrong, like wading through water that had opinions about her.

She'd been flying the Current's drainage for six years and had never once come up this tributary. No reason to. The charts showed a dead-end box canyon with a creek too shallow to matter, terrain nobody surveyed because there was nothing in it worth surveying. But the mayday coordinates put the camp two miles past where the chart said the walls closed in, and when she crested the ridge at nine hundred feet, the canyon kept going—wide, green, unhurried, a river running through it that no chart gave a name to except the one the locals used, spoken quietly: the Current.

Her mouth went dry. She'd grown up on stories about this water. Her father had flown freight over it twice and refused a third time, and when she was eleven he'd told her, half-drunk on cheap rye, *some rivers you don't look down into, Paloma. They look back.*

She'd laughed then. She wasn't laughing now.

The strip came up fast—a gravel bar the survey team must have graded themselves, smooth enough, long enough, wind straight down the canyon. She flared the Beaver onto it and rolled out with the river sliding past her left wingtip, dark and glassy even in full daylight, moving without a single riffle, as if it were flowing under something instead of over anything.

Silence after shutdown rang in her ears. Then the ordinary sounds: cooling engine ticking, wind in the spruce, water making no sound at all.

That was the first wrong thing. A river that size should be loud.

* * *

The camp was intact. That was the part that crawled under her skin and stayed.

Eleven tents pitched in a neat row, guy-lines taut, boots outside three of them arranged side by side like men who'd stepped out expecting to come back. The mess tent's stove held a kettle—she touched it through her glove and pulled back. Warm. Not hours-warm. Minutes-warm, maybe, though the air here did strange things to her sense of time, and she couldn't swear to it.

A radio sat on the folding table, handset cradled properly, frequency still set to the regional channel. Beside it, a logbook, open, entries in four different hands. The last one read:

*Day 9. Grav readings anomalous again. R. says stop logging them. We are not stopping.*

No signature. No date beyond that.

Paloma stood in the doorway of the mess tent and let herself feel it fully for three seconds—the animal certainty that she should get back in the plane and leave—then made herself move, because somewhere out here were eleven people, and if any of them were alive, they'd been alive longer than her fear had.

She worked the camp methodically, the way her father taught her to work a preflight: hands busy, mind quiet. Food stores untouched. First-aid kit complete. A crate of rock samples, sealed, stenciled MINISTRY OF LANDS AND RESOURCES. Two aluminum instrument cases near the surveyor's tent, latched shut, padlocked, each stamped with the same stencil and a serial number. Whatever was inside, somebody had decided it shouldn't be opened by just anyone—and had then walked away from it entirely.

And on the table beside the radio, a field recorder, small, black, scuffed. Its tally counter read 71:22:06 and was still counting. Still recording. For nearly three days it had been sitting here in an empty camp, listening to nothing.

She reached to switch it off and stopped. Some instinct older than procedure said: don't touch what's still working. She left it running.

The tracks started at the edge of camp and went to the river. Eleven sets of boot prints in the mud, overlapping, unhurried—no sprinting, no staggering, no drag marks. People walking toward the water the way you walk to supper. At the gravel bank the prints simply stopped, all of them, in a clean line, as if the river's edge had been drawn with a ruler and everyone had agreed to end there.

Below the line, wet stone. Nothing else.

Paloma crouched at the bank a long time. The water was clear near the edge and utterly dark past three feet, and when she leaned close she caught something—not a smell exactly, more a feeling in the sinuses, a hum she seemed to hear with the bones of her face rather than her ears. Low. Patient. Like a generator running under the world.

She stood up too fast and had to steady herself against a spruce.

"Hello!" Her voice cracked flat against the canyon walls. "Survey team! Anyone!"

The echo came back wrong—delayed a half-beat too long, doubled faintly, as though the canyon were repeating her to itself before giving the word back.

Then, downstream, movement.

* * *

He was walking the shoreline like a man looking for his own footprints.

She saw him from two hundred yards and her heart did something complicated—relief braided tight with dread, because one survivor meant ten unaccounted for, and because he was walking *toward* the water, slow, angled, like the river had a pull on him he hadn't noticed yet.

"Elian?" She'd read the manifest twice on the flight in; names were the only thing she had of these people. "Elian Voss?"

He turned. He was maybe thirty, lean, sunburned, wearing a survey jacket with the sleeves shoved up and no hat, and his eyes found her with an expression she couldn't parse—recognition arriving ahead of memory, the way you know a song before you can name it.

"You're the pilot," he said. His voice was scraped raw, like he'd been talking to someone for days. Or something. "You came."

"I came." She closed the distance carefully, hands visible, voice level. "How many others? Where is your team?"

He looked at her, and then past her, at the river, and his face did something terrible and quiet—it emptied.

"I don't—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "There were eleven of us. There were eleven of us and I can't—I was standing right there." He pointed at the bank, at the clean line where the tracks ended. "Right there. And now it's—how long has it been?"

"Your mayday went out yesterday morning. It's been—" She counted. "Better part of two days since anyone heard from your camp. Maybe three since whatever happened, happened."

"Three days." He said it like a man testing ice. "I've lost three days."

"Not lost," Paloma heard herself say, gentler than she intended. "Misplaced. We'll find them."

It was a lie and they both knew it, but he took it anyway, the way drowning people take rope.

She got him walking, back toward camp, asking small questions—name, rank, next of kin, what he'd eaten, could he run if he had to. He answered the logistics fine. Everything else was static. He remembered the flight in. He remembered setting up camp. He remembered, with sudden vividness, the taste of the coffee on day eight and an argument about gravimeter calibration. Then a gap, smooth-edged, total, and then waking up alone on the shoreline this morning with wet cuffs and no idea how he'd gotten there.

"Wet cuffs," Paloma repeated.

He pushed his sleeve back. The fabric was stiff with dried river water, and beneath it his forearm bore a faint mark—not a bruise, not a cut. A pattern, like a watermark, like something printed briefly on the skin and mostly faded. She looked at it for one second too long and he covered it with his hand.

"Don't," he said quietly. "I know. I've been looking at it all morning."

They reached the mess tent and he stopped dead in the doorway, staring at the logbook, at the warm kettle, at the recorder still counting its silent hours. "This is our camp," he said. "I know every knot on those tents. I tied that one." He pointed. "But it feels like walking through a photograph of my own life."

Paloma put the kettle on anyway, because some rituals hold people together at the seams, and while it heated she inventoried him the way she'd inventoried the camp: pupils even, gait steady, oriented times-three but not times-four. Shock, probably. Something worse, possibly. She'd deal with worse later. Right now she needed him sitting, drinking, and away from the river.

He sat. He drank. And then, over the rim of the tin cup, he looked at her with an intensity that had nothing to do with shock.

"I know you," he said.

"No, you don't. I've never flown a survey outfit in my life. I'd remember."

"You do." He set the cup down. "Not your name. Not your plane. But you. There's—" He struggled, hands opening and closing. "There's a hangar. Rain on the roof. You were arguing with someone about a fuel bill, and you had grease on your jaw, right here"—he touched his own chin—"and you laughed at something and then pretended you hadn't. Two years ago. Maybe more."

The kettle began its low climb toward boiling, and Paloma stood very still inside the sound.

Two years ago. A fuel stop at a strip outside Fort Nelson. Rain. A charter geologist waiting on weather, watching her argue with the FBO about a fuel bill she couldn't afford, and yes—grease on her jaw, and yes, she'd laughed at some dry remark of his and then hated herself for it, because laughing cost money somehow, always had. She'd thought about that stranger for a week afterward, which was unlike her, and had never told a soul.

"That's not possible," she said.

"I know," Elian said. "Believe me. I know." He rubbed his eyes. "Three days of my life are gone and the only thing that survived them whole is you. Isn't that the strangest thing you've ever heard?"

It wasn't. But she didn't tell him what was.

Because while he talked, she'd drifted to the window of the mess tent, and out past the tents, past the treeline, high up where the canyon opened to the south, she'd seen it: a glint, moving. Then another. Aircraft, high and slow, flying a search grid far too neat to be a search. They weren't looking for survivors.

They were looking at the river.

One of them turned, and for a moment the sun caught its underside, and Paloma's stomach dropped through the gravel bar, through the water, through whatever was underneath the water, because she knew that silhouette. Everyone in the interior knew it. Government. Here within a day, when the regional office had told her no one else was rated for this strip, no one else could come, no one else would.

Nobody had asked her to keep the camp secure. Nobody had asked her to collect the instruments. Which meant somebody expected her to, and whoever that somebody was already knew exactly what was down here.

Behind her, Elian said, "What happens now?"

Paloma watched the government planes circle once more and slide away north, and the hum in her sinuses rose so faintly she might have imagined it—or might have answered it.

"Now," she said, "we pack up everything that matters, and we get out of this canyon before someone else decides it belongs to them."

She picked up the recorder, still running, 72 hours and change of whatever-it-had-heard, and slid it into her jacket pocket instead of the crate with the rest.

Some instincts you don't explain. You just obey.
