## 4. What the River Keeps

The water was still humming.

That was the first thing Elian understood, lying in the back of the Beaver with a blanket over him and the drone of the engine in his teeth: the sound hadn't stopped. It had only changed key. Out on the riverbank it had come up through the soles of his boots, through his knees, through the marrow of him, a note too low to hear and too large to ignore. Now it lived in the engine's rhythm, folded inside it, and when he closed his eyes he could feel it keeping time with his pulse.

He kept his eyes open.

Paloma flew the way she did everything—economically, without wasted motion, her hands resting on the yoke like they'd grown there. She checked him twice in the mirror, quick glances, and each time he arranged his face into something convalescent and harmless. Sick man. Shocked surveyor. Nothing more.

*You walked into the water,* said the part of his mind that wouldn't shut up. *All of you. You walked in willingly, and you came out alone.*

He didn't know if that was true. That was the problem. The flashes came without context, without sequence, like film spliced by someone who hated him.

* * *

The first flash had hit him on the shoreline, before she landed—before he even heard the plane. He'd been standing at the edge of the Current with no memory of getting there, and the river had been doing something rivers don't do. It had been lit from below. Not sunlight scattered off stones. Light from underneath, moving against the current, pale green-white, organized. It had pulsed once, slow as breathing, and the pulse had gone through him like a word spoken directly into the chest.

And then—the team. Maren and Osei and the twins, Dr. Vasquez with her sealed cases, all eleven of them minus himself, wading out into water that barely reached their waists but swallowed them anyway. No panic. No struggle. Osei had turned back at the last second—not to call for help, but to smile. A smile of recognition, of homecoming, and that was the image Elian couldn't shake, the one that sat in his stomach like a stone: not terror. *Relief.*

They had wanted to go.

He must have made a sound, because Paloma's voice came through the headset she'd fitted over his ears. "Stay with me. We're twenty minutes from Fort Meridian. You got a name?"

"Elian." His voice sounded borrowed. "Elian Reyes."

"Paloma Kavanagh." A beat. "You said we've met."

Had they? He searched for it and found nothing—nothing except a strange, warm certainty, the way you know a room you dreamed once. "I think so," he said. "I can't place it."

"Don't worry about it." But something in her voice tightened, a wire drawn one notch tauter, and he understood that she could place it, or almost, and that this unsettled her more than it unsettled him.

* * *

The second flash came somewhere over the ridge line, and this one he nearly couldn't hide.

Humming water. He was under it—that much he knew, though his body remembered no drowning, no cold—and the light beneath the surface was all around him, and it wasn't light at all, it was *attention*. Something down there in the green dark had looked at him. Not with eyes. With memory. It had opened him like a file and read him, and what it found—

What it found was a kitchen. Yellow wallpaper. His mother's radio playing a station that didn't exist anymore, some frequency long decommissioned, and her voice singing half a bar of a song he'd forgotten he knew. He had been eight years old. He had been eight years old and whole, before the accident, before the foster rotations, before geology and distance and the careful professional emptiness he'd built over the hole where his family used to be.

The Current had shown him his mother's kitchen.

And then it had shown him—this was the part that made his breath go ragged, this was the part he would take to his grave—a woman's face, framed in the window of a floatplane, laughing at something he'd said, two years ago, in a place with rain on the glass. Her name had been written on a coffee cup. *Paloma.* He hadn't known then why he memorized her. Now he did. The river had shown him her because the river showed everyone what they'd lost, and apparently, two years before he ever stood on its bank, he had already lost her—or failed to find her, which amounted to the same thing.

"Hey." Paloma's voice again, sharper. "Breathe. You went white."

"I'm fine." The lie came easily, smoothly, and he hated how easily. "Just—cold. How long has it been? Since your team lost contact."

"Six hours, give or take." She adjusted something. "You're telling me you don't know where the other ten are."

"No." True, as far as it went. "I woke up on the shore. Alone."

She was quiet for a moment. Through the windshield the canyon fell away behind them, and he watched her eyes flick to the mirror, checking it the way she'd been checking it since takeoff—not for him. For something else.

"What is it?" he asked.

"Probably nothing." But her jaw was set. "There's aircraft back there. Two of them, high, holding pattern over the canyon. They're not bush planes. They're not mine."

Government. The word arrived in his head fully formed, wearing its own uniform, and with it a feeling he couldn't source: *they will want to know what you remember, and they will not want you to remember it.* He kept his face slack. He was growing good at this, and the speed of his own improvement frightened him.

"Search and rescue?" he offered.

"At six thousand feet, in a holding pattern?" She shook her head once. "Nobody searches from up there."

* * *

Fort Meridian resolved out of the haze an hour later—a gravel strip, a fuel tank, a scatter of roofs—and with every mile Elian felt the flashes settling into him like sediment, layering, organizing. He began to understand their shape. They weren't random. They were a record. Somewhere in the drowned architecture of those three missing days, he had seen everything his teammates had seen, felt what the Current had given each of them: Maren's brother who drowned when they were kids, alive and waving from the shallows; Osei's childhood house, whole, doors open; Vasquez's father calling her name in a language she'd stopped speaking at nine years old.

He knew this because he had watched them receive these gifts. He had stood knee-deep in humming water and watched ten people be handed their hearts' contents, and he had understood, in that moment, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like gravity, why none of them had come back out.

Why would you? Why does anyone come back from the thing that gives them back everything?

He had come back because of the woman in the window. Because the Current had shown him her face and then let go of him, and he still didn't know if that was mercy or a mistake or an instruction.

Paloma brought the Beaver down soft as a held breath. As the wheels kissed gravel she killed the engine and the silence rushed in, and in that silence the hum was suddenly, horribly absent—and he missed it. God help him, he missed it.

"You okay?" she asked, turning in her seat, and up close her eyes were gray-green and tired and searching, and he thought: *I know you. I have always known you. I saw you before I ever met you.*

"I'm okay," he said.

Three lies now. He was keeping count. There would be men here soon—men in government jackets, asking questions with answers pre-printed behind them—and every lie would need another lie under it, load-bearing, until the whole structure of him was concealment.

Paloma stepped out onto the gravel and shaded her eyes, scanning the sky. He watched her through the window. Watched her go still.

"Mr. Reyes," she said quietly, without turning around. "How do you feel about people in suits?"

Down the strip, a black SUV was kicking up dust, coming fast.

He looked at the vehicle, then at the canyon far to the north, invisible now but humming in his bones, and said the fourth lie of the day:

"I've never met one I liked."
