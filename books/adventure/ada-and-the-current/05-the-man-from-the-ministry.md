## 5. The Man From the Ministry

The strip at Fort Meridian was gravel and mud, one fuel drum, a windsock that had given up on standing straight. Paloma brought the Beaver in low over the treeline the way she always did, watching for moose, and set it down soft enough that Elian didn't wake in the seat behind her.

He'd slept most of the way, curled against the window with his jacket balled under his head, and every time he stirred she'd caught herself checking him in the mirror like he might dissolve. Three days missing from a man's mind, and he'd looked at her on the shoreline with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—not gratitude, not confusion. Recognition, maybe. Or the ghost of it.

She taxied to the drum and shut down, and that was when she saw the car.

It was wrong the way a heron standing in a cattle field is wrong. A dark government sedan, city clean, parked at the edge of the gravel with its wipers still wet from some rain that hadn't reached here yet. A man leaned against the fender with his hands folded, wearing a coat too good for the interior, and he watched her land the way other people watch weather come in.

Paloma sat with the engine ticking down and felt the old instinct rise—the one her father had bred into her without meaning to. *Official eyes.* She'd refused the survey outfit's charter money for exactly this reason, and now here was the government anyway, delivered to her doorstep like a bill.

She woke Elian gently. "We're here."

He blinked at the sedan. "Who's that?"

"That," she said, "is what I'd like to know."

* * *

The man introduced himself as Corrigan—no first name offered, no ministry named—and shook hands like a man who'd practiced until the practice wore off. His grip was dry and brief. Up close he was younger than his coat suggested, forty maybe, with the kind of face that would still be forgettable at sixty.

"Ms. Kavanagh." He said her name correctly, which meant he'd done his reading. "Glad you made it out. The canyon weather can turn fast this time of year."

"You knew where we were."

"We knew where you *were going*." He smiled without warmth. "May I see the instruments?"

Elian stood at her shoulder, swaying slightly, three days of nothing sitting on him like wet cargo. Corrigan glanced at him once—a flicker, cataloguing—and returned to her.

"The cases are in the back," Paloma said. "Sealed. I didn't open them."

"No," Corrigan agreed pleasantly. "You didn't."

There was something in the way he said it that made her want to open them just to spite him. She hauled the two instrument cases out of the Beaver instead and set them on the gravel between them. They were heavier than they looked, or maybe everything felt heavy after the last two days.

Corrigan crouched, checked the seals with a fingertip, and nodded as if confirming a delivery of produce. Then he straightened and looked at her directly, and his voice changed register—warmer, confiding, the voice of a man about to do you a favor.

"Your father's estate carries a debt with the regional air service board. Fourteen thousand, six hundred and change, accruing interest since before he passed." He let the number sit there. "I can make that disappear today. This afternoon, even. One phone call from a payphone in Meridian, and the ledger is clean."

Paloma kept her face still. Above the instrument panel of her plane, pinned with a bent nail, that ledger lived—her father's handwriting, the columns of what he owed and why, the debt she'd flown four hundred hours a year to outrun. Fourteen thousand six hundred. He knew it to the change.

"What do you want for it?"

"Very little." Corrigan spread his hands. "Silence. You found an empty camp, you found Mr.—" a small pause, consulting memory he already had, "—Voss, and you flew home. The river is unremarkable. The survey was routine. If anyone asks, and no one will, that's the whole story. And the recorder stays with me."

Her stomach dropped half an inch. "I don't have a recorder."

"It's a small orange box," Corrigan said, "about the size of a lunch pail, with a running tape inside. It was logging at the camp when the team went off-grid. It wasn't among the recovered equipment." He watched her. "You searched the site thoroughly, Ms. Kavanagh. I'm sure it's in your aircraft."

She thought of the box wedged under the cargo netting behind the rear seats, where she'd put it without deciding to. She'd told herself she was keeping it safe for the team. She'd told herself a lot of things since the riverbank.

"I'll need a minute," she said.

"Take two." Corrigan checked his watch. "I have a call to make regardless."

* * *

She walked the length of the strip with her hands in her pockets, past the fuel drum, out to where the gravel gave up and the muskeg began. Behind her, Elian sat on the Beaver's wheel strut, watching. Corrigan stood by his sedan making his phone call, and whatever he said into it, he said quietly, and twice she caught him glancing her way while he talked.

The recorder. She turned it over in her mind the way she'd turn a stone on the riverbank. On the tape: eleven voices, presumably, up to the moment the chatter died mid-word. Whatever the Current was, whatever had walked ten people into the water and spat one back out with holes in his head, some part of it lived in that little orange box. Corrigan wanted it because it mattered. Things that didn't matter got left in the mud.

And if it mattered—if it was worth fourteen thousand dollars and a phone call and a two-day drive to a gravel strip—then it was worth more than that, somewhere else, to someone else. She didn't know who. She only knew that handing it over felt like paying a debt she hadn't incurred, and she had spent five years learning exactly how much she hated that.

But the ledger. God, the ledger. She could see it already: the paper unpinned from the panel, the nail pulled, the empty square of bright wood where it had hung. Flying with nothing owed. Waking up and owing nobody.

That was the trap of it, she understood then. Not that the offer was cruel—it was generous, almost tender. That's what made it work. Corrigan wasn't buying her silence with money. He was buying it with the exact shape of her longing, measured to the change.

She walked back.

"The cases are yours," she said. "The recorder isn't on my aircraft. Maybe the river has it. Rivers keep things."

Something moved behind Corrigan's eyes—not anger; disappointment, filed away for later. "Rivers do," he said. "Well. The offer stands regardless. Consider the debt handled either way—we prefer our pilots solvent and cooperative, and solvency often leads to cooperation." He handed her a card, cream-colored, with a single phone number and no name. "If anything surfaces. Tapes, notes, dreams. Anything."

"Dreams," Paloma repeated.

"People say interesting things when they're tired," Corrigan said mildly. He shook her hand again, that same dry practiced grip, and looked past her at Elian. "Mr. Voss. There will be paperwork for you in town. Medical, mostly. Take your time with it."

Then he was gone, the sedan grinding east along the gravel with its wipers going against rain that never came, and the strip was just a strip again, and Paloma discovered her hands were shaking.

* * *

They took a room above the Northern Lights Hotel because it was the only room, and because Elian couldn't be left alone, and because neither of them said any of that out loud. Two beds, a radiator that knocked like a polite ghost, a window overlooking the main street where the evening came down blue and cold.

Paloma bought him dinner at the hotel café—stew, bread, coffee he held in both hands like something rescued from a fire. He ate slowly, methodically, and she watched the food bring color back into him by degrees.

"They cleared the debt," he said finally. "Didn't they. That's what the man in the coat was offering."

"He did more than offer. It's done." She turned her cup. "Fourteen years of my life, erased by a phone call. I should feel lighter."

"But you don't."

"I feel bought."

Elian nodded slowly, as if that were a familiar currency. Outside, a truck went past with its chains jingling, and the light in the room shivered.

"Can I tell you something strange?" he said. "Among the strange things."

"Go ahead."

"I remember you." He set his coffee down. "Not from the riverbank—I mean before. Two years ago, maybe. A stopover—" he frowned, reaching, "—a strip south of here. Little place, one hangar, rain coming down so hard it sounded like applause. Your plane had a cracked cowling and you were arguing with a mechanic about it, and you were—" he stopped, embarrassed. "You were laughing. Furious and laughing at the same time. You had grease on your wrist, right here." He touched the inside of his own forearm. "You bought coffee from a machine that only worked if you hit it, and you hit it, and it gave you two cups, and you gave me one because I was the only other person fool enough to be flying in that weather."

Paloma had gone very still.

Because she remembered it too. Rain like applause, the mechanic named Doucette swearing in two languages, the coffee machine she'd struck like a stubborn engine. She remembered giving the cup to a young geologist waiting out the weather, and she remembered thinking he had kind eyes and a tired smile, and she remembered—this was the part that made her skin prickle—remembering it *vividly*. Not the way you remember a stranger. The way you remember a hinge point, a day things turned.

Two years ago. One hour, maybe, in a rainstorm, and both of them had carried it intact ever since, unmentioned, unexplained.

"That's not normal," she said quietly.

"No," Elian agreed. "It's not."

"And you're telling me now because—"

"Because that man took my team and offered you your father's debt, and we both shook his hand, and I want one thing in my life right now that isn't transactional." He looked at her across the table, and the exhaustion in his face was real but underneath it was something steadier. "You could have handed him the recorder. I saw you decide not to. I don't know why you did it. But I know why I'm glad."

Paloma looked at the window, at the blue dark coming down over Fort Meridian, at the gravel strip where her Beaver sat with an orange box hidden under the cargo netting and no debt pinned above its panel anymore.

"Get some sleep," she said. "First thing tomorrow, we're going to figure out what's on that tape."

She said *we*, and heard herself say it, and didn't take it back.
