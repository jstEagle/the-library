## 6. Debt Paid in Full

The envelope was waiting on the counter of the air services office when I came down from the strip, still smelling of avgas and river fog. Heavy cream paper, my name typed on it—no handwriting, no return address, just a franked stamp from an office that didn't exist on any letterhead I'd ever seen.

I stood there with it in both hands for a long moment before I opened it. The kettle ticked behind me. Somewhere out front, Marsh was arguing with a fuel truck driver about water in the lines, and the argument had the comfortable, endless quality of arguments that will never be resolved and don't need to be.

Inside the envelope: one page. My father's name at the top. A figure beneath it—the full balance, principal and interest, eleven years of compounding that had hung over me since I was nineteen years old. And beside the figure, in a different typeface, two words.

*Paid. In full.*

Below that, a line for a signature. Mine.

I read it four times. The numbers didn't change. The debt didn't shrink or waver or reveal itself as a trick of the light. It was simply gone, the way a mountain is gone when cloud comes down over it—not moved, not settled, just hidden, and you're expected to fly as though it were never there.

Marsh stuck his head through the door. "Truck driver says our fuel's wet. I say his gauge is wet. You want coffee?"

"Marsh." I held up the page. "You ever see anything like this?"

He came over, wiped his hands on a rag that had been black since the previous administration, and read it. His eyebrows did something complicated.

"That's your old man's ledger?"

"Every cent."

"And they're calling it square." He tapped the signature line with one cracked fingernail. "You know what this is, Paloma?"

"I know what it looks like."

"It looks like a receipt," he said. "Receipts mean somebody bought something." He set the page down on the counter with surprising gentleness, like it might go off. "Question is what."

I didn't sign it. Not yet. I folded it into my jacket pocket, where it sat against my ribs all day like a second heartbeat, and I flew the afternoon mail run to Two Rivers and back thinking about receipts and purchases and what exactly I might have sold without knowing it.

* * *

Elian was staying in the spare room above the office. Doc Ferro had cleared him—bruised, dehydrated, a crack in two ribs he said would heal fine if the man would stop trying to carry things—and beyond that there was nowhere for him to go. The rest of his team had been flown out, he'd been told. Medevac, day after the incident. He'd missed them by hours.

"They didn't wait?" he asked me that evening, sitting on the edge of the cot with his ribs taped and his boots unlaced. "Eleven days in the field and they didn't wait for me?"

"You were missing three days yourself," I said. "Maybe they thought—"

"I know what they thought." He said it flatly, without heat, and looked at his hands. They were better than they'd been on the shoreline, when they'd been shaking so hard he couldn't hold the thermos I gave him. Steadier now. But he kept looking at them like they belonged to someone whose habits he hadn't fully learned.

Downstairs, the radio murmured its evening traffic. Weather, positions, the usual litany. And underneath it, fainter, the sound of Marsh locking up.

"Elian." I sat down across from him, on the crate I used for a chair. "The man from the ministry. Corrigan. What did he say to you? Exactly?"

"He was very kind." Elian's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "That's how you know, isn't it. In my line of work. The kinder they are, the worse the news." He leaned back against the wall, careful of the ribs. "He told me my team was safe. He told me I'd been through a traumatic event and that memory loss was a normal response. He told me there'd be paperwork—a standard confidentiality acknowledgment, nothing sinister, everyone signs them—and that once I signed, there'd be transport home and a commendation in my file." He paused. "And then he shook my hand and said, 'Mr. Voss, the government is grateful for what you can't remember.'"

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out wrong, too sharp, and Elian looked at me with something that wasn't surprise—he'd heard that laugh before, he insisted, though I couldn't place where, and the not-placing itched at me like a stone in a boot.

"He said the same thing to me," I managed. "More or less. Minus the handshake."

"What did he offer you?"

I took the envelope out of my pocket and handed it across. He read it slowly, and I watched his face do what mine had done: the arithmetic, the disbelief, the slow settling of understanding that a thing this clean is never free.

"Your father's debt," he said quietly. "This is real?"

"As real as it's been for eleven years. It's why I fly medicine into places the charter companies won't service. It's why I keep my head down and my routes boring." I took the page back. "Corrigan found out about it in about four minutes of conversation. Which means he knew before he landed. Which means I was researched."

"Which means the offer was prepared before you landed at that camp," Elian said. "Before you found me."

We looked at each other. Outside, the last floatplane of the day droned in over the ridge, and the light went the color of tin.

* * *

It was Marsh who cracked it open, two nights later, because Marsh heard everything that moved through that strip the way other men hear weather.

He came up to the office after closing, didn't take off his coat, and stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands, which was how I knew it was bad. Marsh's hat only came off for funerals and apologies.

"Paloma. You going to want to sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"The survey team." He turned the hat in his hands. "The ten that got medevacked out. They didn't come through here."

"So? There are other strips."

"Not other strips in range of that canyon, there aren't. Not for a bird big enough to lift ten passengers plus gear." He set the hat on the counter, finally, like even it was tired. "I've been asking around, quiet-like. Fuel logs, flight plans, the works. That medevac never filed. No strip within six hundred klicks logged an arrival matching their description. No hospital in the region took ten survey personnel with exposure." He looked at me with flat, unhappy eyes. "They vanished off the books the same day they vanished off that riverbank."

The room got very small. I could hear the stove ticking, the wind working at the eaves, my own pulse doing something unprofessional.

"Recovered," I said. "Corrigan said recovered."

"I'm telling you what the logs say. And the logs say nothing, which is worse than saying something." Marsh put his coat back on. "One more thing. Girl at the regional office—good kid, talks too much—says Corrigan's outfit filed a passenger manifest out of here three days ago. Ten names. All ten signed something at the bottom. She read me the form number." He shrugged into his collar. "Secrecy oaths, if I had to guess. The kind you sign when you've seen something, not when you haven't."

After he left I sat in the dark office for a long time, the ledger receipt in one hand and the whole shape of it assembling itself in front of me like terrain emerging from fog.

Ten survivors, recovered days ago. Flown out quiet. Signed, sealed, thanked for what they couldn't remember. And one man left wandering a shoreline for three days because the Current, whatever it was, hadn't finished with him—or because Corrigan's people hadn't gotten to him first, and now he was the loose thread on a garment the government needed seamless.

And I was holding the scissors.

Because I had the recorder. It had been instinct, that moment on the riverbank—Corrigan's men cataloguing instrument cases, and my hand closing around the little steel box in the tent and sliding it into my jacket like it was warm. I'd told myself it was evidence, insurance, habit. Now I understood it was leverage, and that leverage cuts both ways.

If Elian remembered too much—if the recorder surfaced, if anyone outside the circle heard what was on it—then Elian stopped being a loose end and became a problem. And problems, in Corrigan's world, got handled. I thought about the word *handled*, the way officials use it, the way you'd use it about cargo, about freight, about something that doesn't get a vote.

I climbed the stairs to the spare room. Elian was asleep, or nearly—one arm flung over his eyes, breathing shallow around the cracked ribs. On the table by the cot lay the secrecy acknowledgment Corrigan had left for him, unsigned, corners already curling.

I stood there in the doorway longer than I meant to, watching him breathe, and the trap closed around me so gently I almost admired it. The debt was gone. I was free. Free to fly anywhere, owe no one, start clean—and every road out of that freedom ran directly through the silence Corrigan had purchased, and the price of the silence was a man asleep in my spare room who trusted me, and whom I was starting to—

I stopped the thought there. Filed it. Some cargo you don't declare.

He stirred. "Paloma?" Sleep-rough, half-gone. "Everything okay?"

"Fine," I said. "Go back to sleep."

But downstairs, I took the recorder out of the lockbox where I'd hidden it, weighed it in my palm—small, cold, patient—and understood that the receipt in my pocket wasn't a receipt at all.

It was a bill. And it had come due.
