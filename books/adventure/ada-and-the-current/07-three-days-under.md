## 7. Three Days Under

The oath arrives on a Tuesday, in a manila envelope with no return address, and it is the most beautiful document Elian Vasquez has ever been asked to destroy.

He reads it four times at the kitchen table of the ministry guesthouse, the one they've moved him to from the clinic, the one with the good coffee and the locked windows. *In consideration of services rendered and in exchange for full reinstatement of duties*, it says, *the undersigned agrees to non-disclosure of all observations, recordings, and experiences occurring between the dates of—*

The dates. That's where his pen always stops.

Three days. The 14th, the 15th, the 16th. He knows them now like coordinates, like a fracture line running through himself. Everything before the 14th is solid: the flight in, the camp on the gravel bar, Marta arguing about core samples, Dev's terrible guitar. Everything after the 16th is solid too: waking on the shoreline, cold to the bone, a woman with dark hair crouched over him saying his name like she was checking whether it still worked.

Between—the 14th, the 15th, the 16th—is water.

*Sign here.*

His signature would be two words long. His whole career has trained him for this moment: observe, record, report, comply. He signed oaths before, smaller ones, confidentiality clauses, safety waivers. This one just asks him to agree that three days of his life are nobody's business, including his own.

It should be easy. It is not easy, because every night since the shoreline, the water has been coming back to him in pieces, and each piece makes the signature heavier.

* * *

The first piece comes back in the shower, which is fitting, because it comes back as sound.

He has the water too hot, steam climbing the tiles, and somewhere under the drumming there's a hum. Low. Below hearing, almost—more felt than heard, a vibration in the sternum, in the fillings of his teeth. He turns the tap off and stands there dripping and the hum keeps going, faintly, the way a bell keeps going after you've told it to stop.

And he remembers: standing on the bank of the Current at dusk on the 14th, the whole team behind him, and the river humming. Not flowing—humming. Like a struck string the length of a canyon. Marta had said *do you hear that* and Dev had said *it's the generators* and Marta had said *we're fifty meters from the generators and that's not them,* and then nobody said anything, because the hum had climbed into their chests and made speech feel rude.

That's the first day. He's sure of it now. The 14th was the day the river started humming.

* * *

The second piece comes back as light.

He's asleep—or he thinks he's asleep—and he's standing on the water. Not on ice. On the Current itself, black and slow and holding him up the way a held breath holds you up, and beneath his feet there is light. Not reflected. Emitted. A soft green-gold glow rising from far down, deep past any depth the surveys had sounded, and moving in patterns too deliberate to be current and too fluid to be machinery.

On the 15th, he remembers thinking, we went down to it. Or it came up to us. The memory won't fix the direction, and he suspects the direction doesn't exist.

And he remembers the others walking toward the bank—not summoned, not dragged. Willing. Marta first, boots in her hands so she wouldn't make noise, the way you approach something sleeping. Dev carrying his guitar case, which made no sense until suddenly it made perfect sense, because Dev had lost his brother to a flood when they were boys and had never once spoken of it, and what do you bring to meet the thing that keeps your dead? You bring the song you used to play for him.

They walked into the water and the water took them gently, the way you take a letter from someone who's waited years to hand it to you.

Elian stands in the guesthouse shower until the hot water runs out, shaking, understanding two things at once. First: the other ten didn't wander off. They went. They were given something.

Second: he was given something too, and he can't remember what it was, and the not-remembering is starting to feel less like damage and more like theft.

* * *

The third piece comes back on Thursday, and it comes back as her.

Corrigan visits that morning—all teeth and smooth wool, setting the pen down on the table like it's a gift. "Just formality, Dr. Vasquez. Your colleagues signed days ago. They're home. Careers intact." A pause, calibrated. "You were under the longest, we understand. Whatever you think you remember, the stress of exposure can fabricate quite convincing—"

"I remember the hum," Elian says.

Something flickers behind Corrigan's eyes, gone before it lands. "There is no hum."

"There is no canyon on your charts either," Elian says, "and yet I've flown over it."

Corrigan smiles and leaves the pen.

That afternoon, trying to exhaust himself into sleep, Elian walks the town's one long street, and that's where the third day finds him. Because he passes the general store, and there's a bulletin board, and pinned among the snowmobile ads is a photograph of a floatplane on a grey lake, and leaning against the strut of that plane, laughing at whoever held the camera, is Paloma Kavanagh.

He stops so hard a woman behind him mutters and goes around.

Not the photo—that's new. What's new is what rises with it: the 16th. The last day. He's on the bank again, alone this time, the others already gone into the water or into whatever the water gave them, and the Current shows him—

Shows him a stopover two years ago. A fuel stop at a strip he'd have sworn he couldn't name. Rain on the canopy of a hangar. Coffee from a dented pot, terrible, burnt. A pilot with sawdust on her sleeve and grease to the wrist, complaining about a carburetor with the flu, and he'd sat on an oil drum and listened for forty minutes because listening to her was better than any silence he'd ever paid for. She'd laughed at something he said—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and she'd looked at him afterward like she was angry about it.

He'd forgotten her. Completely, criminally forgotten her, the way you forget a dream by noon.

But the Current hadn't. The Current had gone down through everything he was and brought that up—of all the things he'd ever lost, all the people, all the small warm moments ground under by work and distance, the river had chosen *her*. Held her up to him in the green-gold light and said, in whatever way a thing like that says: *this. You let go of this.*

And then the river had put him back on the shore with three days missing and her face waiting above him, and he hadn't known her, and she had looked at him with something worse than grief—with recognition, patient and hurt, because she remembered the hangar and he didn't.

Now he does. Now he remembers everything, standing in front of a bulletin board in the cold, and the oath in his jacket pocket weighs as much as an anchor.

* * *

He understands the trap fully now, walking back. The others signed because they got their memories back and traded them away clean—sold the river's gift for their careers, their pensions, their untroubled sleep. Maybe it was worth it to them. Maybe Marta tells herself the hum was hypoxia.

But Elian got handed one specific thing, out of all the things he's ever lost, and the ministry wants him to sign a paper agreeing he never received it. Signing means the hangar goes dark again. Signing means the laugh on the oil-drum afternoon gets repossessed. Signing means Paloma becomes a stranger twice.

Duty says sign. Duty has fed him, housed him, funded every core sample and contour map of his adult life. Duty is Corrigan's smooth voice saying *just formality.* Duty is a pen on a table.

Love—he tests the word, finds it holds—love is a woman who flew into an unmarked canyon alone because eleven strangers stopped answering their radio, and who looked at him on a shoreline and said his name like a question she was afraid to finish.

He gets back to the guesthouse before dusk. The pen sits where Corrigan left it. The oath sits on the table, *sign here* patient as a tripwire.

Elian picks up the pen. Uncaps it. Holds it over the line long enough to watch his own hand start to shake.

Then he sets the pen down, picks up the oath instead, and tears it in half. Then halves into quarters, quarters into confetti, methodical, almost calm, until the manila envelope's beautiful language is a small white snowfall in his cupped hands. He carries it to the sink and burns it over the burner flame, piece by piece, watching each fragment curl and go.

No one knocks. No alarm sounds. The room is very quiet, and he understands that the quiet is temporary, and that he has just become the loose end Corrigan mentioned without mentioning.

He shrugs into his coat. There's only one place in this town he needs to be, one person he owes three days' worth of truth, and he walks fast through the blue evening to the boarding house by the strip where she's been staying, heart hammering not with fear but with the strange lightness of a man who has finally stopped lying to himself.

Her door is the second on the left. Light underneath. He raises his fist to knock—

—and freezes, because down the street, parked in the shadow of the fuel shed, a government sedan has just turned its headlights on.
