## 8. Running the Canyon

The knock came at 4:40 in the morning, three soft raps that were meant to sound polite and didn't.

Paloma was already awake. She'd been awake since two, lying in the narrow bed above the charter office, listening to the town's one taxi make loops it had no reason to make, watching the ceiling go grey with the first suggestion of dawn. Some part of her had known this night would end with a knock. She'd spent the evening after Elian tore up the oath not celebrating but calculating—fuel loads, fog burn-off times, the weight of a Beaver with two souls aboard and no cargo.

She looked through the curtain gap and saw two men on the stairs below, coats buttoned to the chin, standing in the lamplight like they'd been issued the hour along with their instructions. One of them held a folded paper. The other held nothing at all, which was worse.

Elian was already dressed. He sat on the edge of the bed with his boots on, hands loose between his knees, and when she met his eyes he nodded once—the nod of a man who has decided something and is only waiting for the world to catch up.

"Back window," he said quietly. "You said there's a back window."

"There's a back window."

They went down through the storeroom, past crates of engine oil and a wall of dusty charts, and out into the alley where the cold hit them like a hand pressed flat against the chest. Behind the building she could hear the front door opening, voices pitched low and reasonable, the particular gentleness of men who have been told to be gentle right up until they aren't.

The strip was a ten-minute walk if you hurried and didn't care about noise. They cared about noise. Paloma led them along the drainage ditch behind the fuel depot, moving the way her father had taught her to move around things that could hurt you—slowly, without apology, keeping your weight over your feet. The fog was coming off the river in long white ropes, thickening as the sky lightened, and she thought: good. Fog was the one thing the government's fast airplanes couldn't buy their way out of.

The Beaver sat where she'd left it, tied down at the far end of the apron, dew silvering the fabric of its wings. She pulled the chocks, untied the tail, and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin in her chest—not fear exactly, but the focused calm that arrived whenever the world got simple enough. There was a plane. There was a sky. Everything else was weather.

"Elian." She handed him the preflight checklist, though he barely needed it anymore; three days of sitting in her hangar had made him useful with his hands. "Fuel caps. Check for water in the sumps. If anyone comes down the taxiway, you don't run, you don't shout—you walk to me and tell me like you're telling me the wind direction."

He looked at her. "And if they don't stop walking?"

"Then I start the engine," she said, "and we find out how much of this airplane is bluff."

* * *

They were airborne at 5:31, wheels up into a sky the color of unpolished pewter, and Paloma turned north before she turned anything else, because north was the canyon and the canyon was the only place on earth where she knew the ground better than any man chasing her could know his instruments.

Behind them, the town shrank to a scatter of lights going out one by one, and then the fog took even that.

She flew low. Lower than the regulations allowed, lower than she'd ever flown with a passenger who wasn't family—which was its own kind of statement, she realized, and decided not to examine it. Two hundred feet over the black spruce, skimming the tops of the ridges where the fog pooled thickest, running the valleys like a fox runs a fence line. The Beaver wasn't fast. It had never been fast. But it was honest, and it would fly all day on what other planes burned in an hour, and in fog at treetop height none of that mattered except knowing your machine and knowing your river.

Elian sat in the right seat with the chart unfolded across his knees, tracking their progress with a pencil stub. He'd been quiet since takeoff, and she'd assumed it was fear until she glanced over and saw his face—not fear at all. Recognition. He was looking down at the terrain the way a man looks at a room he's been dreaming about.

"The Current bends east here," he said suddenly. "Before the narrows. It doesn't bend east on any map I ever drew."

"No," Paloma said. "It doesn't."

"And you knew that."

"I've been flying supplies up this valley for six years. The maps are wrong about the river the way a photograph is wrong about a person." She eased the yoke left, following the fog corridor along the water. "My father used to say the interior keeps its own ledger. You just have to read it yourself instead of trusting somebody's copy."

Something moved in her peripheral vision—a flicker high and to the south, gone as soon as it came. Her stomach dropped through the floor of the aircraft.

"What?" Elian said.

"Nothing." But her eyes went to the mirror, and the mirror told her she hadn't imagined it: a shape above the cloud deck, sleek and dark, holding position the way a predator holds position—patiently, at a distance it can close whenever it chooses. Government paint. Twin engines. Probably radar that could pick a loon off a lake at forty miles.

"They're up there," Elian said. Not a question.

"They're up there."

"Why haven't they come down?"

Paloma watched the fog stream past the struts, white and endless, and understood the answer before she finished asking herself the question. "Because they can't see us. And because they think we're going somewhere. A plane that's running always goes somewhere—they'll follow our heading, wait for us to cross open country or climb for weather, and take us over some flat piece of nowhere where there's no witnesses and no wreckage worth explaining." She flexed her fingers on the yoke. "So we don't go somewhere. We go *here*."

She put the Beaver down into the canyon.

* * *

The walls rose on either side like the pages of a closing book. Fog filled the gorge to within eighty feet of the water, and below that ceiling the air was still and cold and smelled of wet stone and something older, something mineral and faintly sweet that Paloma had never been able to name and had stopped trying to. The Current ran beneath them, wide and slow-seeming and utterly wrong, the way deep water is always wrong—too smooth, too dark, moving without any visible reason to move.

The radio crackled. A voice, clipped and official, calling a registration number that happened to be hers.

*"Beaver zero-seven-kilo, you are entering restricted airspace. Turn to heading one-nine-zero and climb to nine thousand. Acknowledge."*

She reached over and switched the radio off.

"You can't just—" Elian started.

"I can. Watch me." But her hands weren't steady, and she let him see that they weren't, because some part of her had decided he deserved the truth of her along with everything else. "Corrigan doesn't want a fight, Elian. He wants paperwork. He wants us to land somewhere with a runway and a hangar and men with clipboards, and he wants it done so cleanly that ten years from now nobody can prove it happened. That's why we're still alive. We're not a threat yet. We're an errand."

"And when we stop being an errand?"

She looked down at the water sliding past beneath the keel, black glass, holding the grey light without reflecting it.

"Then we'd better already be whatever we're going to be."

The recorder sat in the map case between their seats. She'd strapped it there herself at 4:15 that morning, before the knock, before she'd fully admitted to herself that the morning would need it. Forty-one hours of audio from the survey camp. Every reading, every transmission, every silence. The full record of what the Current had found them—and, though neither of them said it aloud, the full record of what it had shown them. Elian's three missing days lived in that box as surely as they lived in his head, and Corrigan wanted it the way a man wants the last copy of a document that could hang him.

It was the only thing they had. She'd known it since the strip, since the two men on the stairs, since the moment her debt had been paid by people who expected gratitude instead of questions. Money bought planes and patience and faster aircraft. But it couldn't buy back a recording once the recording was gone, and it couldn't survive a recording that got loose.

"Leverage," she said aloud, mostly to herself.

"What?"

"That's all we've got. That box is the whole hand." She banked gently around a rock fin that no chart admitted to, the wingtip kissing the fog. "Corrigan can't shoot us down over the canyon—too many questions, and the Current eats evidence in ways that make even ministries nervous. So he'll wait. He'll herd us. He'll try to make us so tired and so scared that handing it over feels like mercy." Her jaw tightened. "I've been merciful my whole life because I couldn't afford anything else. Debt does that to a person. It teaches you that every choice has already been priced."

Elian was watching her with an expression she couldn't read—something between grief and wonder.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now I'm deciding what I'm worth."

Above them, muffled by the fog deck, the government plane passed—once, twice, a shadow crossing the pale ceiling of the gorge like a bird over ice. Hunting by pattern. Waiting for them to run out of canyon, out of fog, out of fuel.

Paloma checked the gauges. Three hours and change. Enough to reach the coast, if the coast were an option, which it wasn't. Enough to keep flying the canyon until the fog burned off and the sky opened and the mathematics became final.

Enough, maybe, for one more decision.

She leveled the wings over the widest reach of the Current, where the water lay flat as poured metal, and cut their speed to a crawl, and began—to the certain fury of whoever was listening upstairs—to simply circle. Slowly. Over the place where eleven sets of tracks had ended at the riverbank, and one man had walked back out carrying three days he couldn't remember and a face she couldn't forget.

Below them, faint through the hull, through the fog, through everything, the river seemed to hum.

"Do you feel that?" Elian whispered.

Paloma did. And for the first time since the knock on the door, she wasn't sure whether the terror in her chest was about the plane overhead—or about the patient, listening dark beneath her wheels, waiting to see what she would do with the truth in her lap.

The fuel needle began, very slowly, to fall.
