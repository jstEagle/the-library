## 9. Over the Current

The fuel gauge was lying to her. Paloma could feel it in the way the Beaver sat slightly wing-heavy to port, in the engine's note a half-tone flat, like a singer going hoarse mid-song. The gauge said forty minutes. Her gut said thirty, maybe less, and her gut had kept her alive through eleven winters of flying weather that killed better pilots.

Behind her, Elian gripped the back of her seat hard enough that she could hear the vinyl creak.

"How far?" he asked.

"Twenty minutes to the strip. Maybe twenty-five if I have to dodge them."

She didn't have to say who *them* was. Through the windshield, high and east against the pale morning sun, the government plane hung like a crow over a field mouse. It wasn't even trying to hide anymore. It had been shadowing them since the fog broke apart over the foothills—sleek, twin-engined, painted the gray of official indifference—and it flew the way Corrigan talked: patient, certain, already knowing how it would end.

Paloma pulled the yoke closer and dropped the Beaver lower into the canyon's throat.

The walls rose on either side, granite streaked with old water stains, close enough that she could see individual pines clinging to ledges where nothing should grow. Below, the river ran green-black and smooth as poured glass. The Current. Even from three hundred feet up she could feel it through the airframe—a hum in the stick, a tremor in her back teeth, like the whole canyon was a struck bell still ringing from some blow delivered long ago.

Elian felt it too. She'd watched him go rigid when they'd crossed the rim.

"It knows we're here," he said quietly.

"Rivers don't know things."

"This one does."

She didn't argue. Three days ago she would have. But she'd seen the tracks end at the bank. She'd heard the recorder—the humming, the voices of ten people walking calmly into water that should have killed them, sounding not afraid but *homesick*. Whatever lived down there, it had already met everyone aboard this plane except her.

The twin-engine plane slid left, then right, cutting its speed, dropping. Corrigan's voice crackled through on the radio frequency they'd been monitoring since dawn, smooth as ever, unhurried, a man reading terms aloud in a room with no windows.

*Paloma. It doesn't have to go this way.*

She keyed the mic and said nothing. Let him talk. Every second he spent talking was a second the Beaver crawled north.

*You're low on fuel. We both know it. Land at the mouth strip, hand over the recorder, and you walk away. Both of you. You have my word.*

"You gave your word about the debt," she said, mostly to herself. "That came true. Funny how the true promises are always the cheap ones."

Elian leaned forward, his breath warm near her ear. "He'll take me either way. You know that. If we land, they separate us before the wheels stop turning."

"I know."

"So don't land."

Her hands tightened on the yoke. The engine coughed once—a dry, ugly sound—and caught again. The gauge needle shivered below the quarter mark. Thirty minutes had become a rumor.

Ahead, the canyon bent west toward the lake country, toward strips and towns and witnesses. Behind, the survey camp, the riverbank, the place where everything had started coming apart. Between them: twenty miles of stone and one very tired airplane and two people who had run out of clever options somewhere around first light.

Corrigan again, softer now, almost kind. *I've read your file, Paloma. Your father's file too. I know what debt does to a family. I'm offering you the only clean exit you will ever be offered. Don't make me watch you drown in it.*

"There it is," she murmured. "There's the real voice."

The plane above them descended another hundred feet and matched their heading, close enough now that she could see the camera housing under its belly, the lens aimed down at them like an eye deciding whether to blink.

And Paloma understood, all at once, with the clarity that only comes when every other door has closed, what the recorder actually was. Not evidence. Not leverage. Leverage only worked if the other side needed something from you alive. What she held was a *reason*—the only reason Corrigan's people hadn't simply put them into the canyon an hour ago. As long as the recording existed, destroying the plane meant destroying it too. The moment it stopped existing, so did theirs.

Or—

The thought arrived sideways, the way important thoughts do, dressed as something small:

*Or give it to the river.*

She looked down through the side window. The Current slid beneath them, deep and patient, holding whatever it held. Ten people had walked into it because it showed them something they'd lost. Elian had come out of it carrying her face in his memory like a coin he refused to spend. And the government wanted the water's voice sealed in a vault because a secret that big is just power nobody else has been given yet.

If she dropped the recorder, Corrigan's mission died. No recovery, no containment, no loose ends worth chasing at altitude over open wilderness. The findings would belong to the only keeper who'd never sign an oath.

If she traded it, they lived—but lived owned. Lived looking over shoulders for the rest of their short, grateful lives.

The engine coughed again. Longer this time. The propeller's rhythm stuttered like a heartbeat skipping.

"Elian." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "When I tell you to brace, I need you to brace. Can you do that?"

"What are you doing?"

"Something my father never managed." She reached across and unlatched the pilot's side window, and the canyon roared into the cockpit—wind and river-hum and the thin scream of cold air through a gap built for ventilation, not confession. "Something clean."

The recorder sat in the map case between the seats, in its canvas wrap, heavy as a sleeping animal. Forty hours of humming water. Forty hours of voices calling it home. The last thing on it, she knew, was her own breathing, recorded by accident during the flight out, because the machine never stopped running until someone made it.

*Paloma.* Corrigan's voice had lost its patience; she could hear the men behind the voice now, the urgency underneath. *Whatever you're considering—*

She pulled the recorder free and held it out the window, into the slipstream, feeling it tug at her arm like something eager.

For one half-second—one full rotation of the propeller—she hesitated. Not because she doubted. Because she finally understood what she was doing: burning the ledger. Her father's whole life had been a column of numbers owed, and she'd inherited the arithmetic without ever signing for it. This was different. This was a debt she was choosing, freely, in open air, over the one witness in the world that didn't keep records because it *was* the record.

"Tell them it sank," she said—to Corrigan, to the canyon, to whoever was listening below the surface. "Tell them the Current keeps what it keeps."

She let go.

The recorder tumbled, caught the light once—just a flash of canvas and metal, absurdly small against all that stone—and then the river took it without a splash she could see or a ripple she could measure. The glass-green surface didn't even break.

The hum in the airframe changed. She'd swear to that later, under inquiry, stripped of her license, saying words officials wrote down without believing: the hum *lifted*, like a held breath released, like a bell finishing its ring.

Above them, the twin-engine plane wavered. Its nose swung, held, swung again—she could picture the cockpit, the radio call going back down the line, the mission evaporating mid-sentence. *Object jettisoned. Target asset void. Recovering is not—* static ate the rest. Then the plane peeled away in a wide, obedient arc, climbing east toward the sun, and within a minute it was just a glint, then a rumor, then gone.

Paloma latched the window. The roar cut off. The cockpit went suddenly, enormously quiet.

"That's it?" Elian asked. "They just leave?"

"They got what they were sent for. Nothing." She watched the fuel gauge, which had stopped mattering and was now merely honest. "A man like Corrigan doesn't chase wreckage. He files a report about it."

The engine coughed a third time and this time stumbled, caught, stumbled. The propeller wound down half a revolution, then grabbed again, grudgingly, like an old man agreeing to one more errand.

"How much do we have?" Elian asked.

"Enough to be a question, not an answer." She scanned ahead. The canyon was opening now, spilling out toward the broad gray shoulder of Kettle Lake, and on its southern shore—three miles, maybe four—there was a gravel bar the trappers used, wide enough for a Beaver driven by faith and skill in roughly equal parts. "I can put her down on the bar. Or I can stretch for the settlement strip and probably swim the last mile."

"The bar," Elian said without hesitation.

"The bar," she agreed.

The engine died completely with a mile to go. No drama—just a winding-down, a softness in the controls, the world going quiet except for wind and the river falling away beneath them. Paloma raised the nose and traded altitude for distance the way her father had taught her before he taught her anything about owing money: *never waste a good glide, kiddo. Gravity's the only thing in this world that gives you exactly what it promised.*

The gravel bar came up fast and true. Trees at both ends, water glittering beyond, stones the color of old bone. She flared late and firm, and the tires kissed, skipped, kissed again, and then the Beaver was rolling, rolling, rolling down that narrow ribbon of land, slower and slower, while Elian braced against the panel and Paloma held the yoke against her chest like something she meant to keep.

They stopped with fifty feet of gravel left and a silence so complete she could hear the lake lapping at its edges.

Neither of them moved for a moment. Then Elian laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and put his hand over hers on the yoke.

"You dropped it," he said. "You actually dropped it."

"Somebody had to return it." She looked out at the water, calm and green-dark and keeping its secrets, and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since the day she'd pinned her father's ledger above the instrument panel. "It was never ours to sell."

They climbed out onto the gravel. The morning was cold and enormous. Somewhere south, a floatplane droned faintly—civilian, local, headed for fish camps—and Paloma found herself waving at it, absurdly, before remembering it couldn't see her.

Then she saw the boat.

It was rounding the point at the north end of the bar, a battered aluminum skiff with an outboard, and standing in the bow was Marta Reyes from the settlement co-op, binoculars around her neck, and behind her two more figures she recognized from the fuel dock, from the clinic, from a hundred medicine runs and mail drops and nights she'd eaten alone at the co-op counter thinking nobody noticed whether she came or went.

They had seen the plane come down. They had come anyway—no questions visible even at this distance, just people moving toward her across the water.

Elian touched her elbow. "Friends of yours?"

Paloma watched the skiff approach and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging.

"No," she said. "Not yet."

But the Current had carried her here, and the Current, she was beginning to understand, did not deliver things to the wrong place.
