## 10. A Place on the Map

The letter came on a Tuesday, in an envelope with a government crest and no return address, and Paloma read it twice before she understood it was not the end of her.

*Your pilot's license is suspended pending inquiry. You are advised that this suspension does not constitute a finding of wrongdoing. You are further advised—*

She stopped reading there. The rest was boilerplate, the same careful language Corrigan had used on the strip at Fort Meridian, all those weeks ago, when he'd offered to erase a debt and buy a silence. She'd burned the survey's findings over the Current instead. She'd turned herself in. And now the machinery she'd defied was grinding through its slow, procedural digestion of her, one stamped form at a time.

Six weeks. That's how long the inquiry had been running. Six weeks of interviews conducted by men who flew in on government charters, asked their questions in the community hall, and flew out again without eating anything anyone offered them. Six weeks of Paloma sitting in the hangar with nothing to do but wipe down the Beaver's windows and listen to the radio traffic she was no longer allowed to join.

The Beaver sat on its mooring blocks like a dog tied out too long. Every few days she'd climb inside anyway, just to sit in the left seat, hands resting on the yoke, and look out through the windshield at the strip she couldn't use. The ledger of her father's debt was gone—Corrigan had made good on that much, though it felt less like payment now than like bait she'd refused to swallow whole. In its place, pinned above the panel with a single thumbtack, was a photograph: Elian, standing on the shoreline of the Current, squinting into the sun, taken by Marta Okonkwo on the day the inquiry team finally let them both go.

It wasn't much. But it was hers, and nobody had given it to her as part of any deal.

* * *

Elian heard about the suspension before she told him, which was how he learned most things in Kettle Falls now—the town talked, and he'd stopped pretending he wasn't part of what they talked about.

He was teaching again. Not geology, exactly, though that was the word on the notice board: *SURVEY SCIENCE, THURSDAYS, 7 PM, ALL WELCOME.* What he actually taught was stranger and harder to name. He taught the eleven days he didn't have. He taught the shape of a memory that comes back wrong. He taught the kids who showed up—which was most of them—that it was possible to lose something enormous and keep walking anyway.

"Tell us about the lights," said Dee Sandoval's youngest, every single week, because the answer never changed and she loved hearing it anyway.

"I don't remember the lights," Elian would say. "I remember wanting to see them. I remember my team walking into the water like they were going home. And I remember—" and here he always paused, because the next part still caught in his throat like a fishbone, "—I remember a woman in a floatplane, and I knew her face before I ever saw it."

The other ten members of the survey team had signed their oaths and scattered to cities where nobody asked questions. He'd gotten one postcard from the team lead, unsigned, postmarked Vancouver, with nothing written on it but a drawing of water. He kept it in his desk drawer and took it out sometimes and looked at it and put it back.

But Elian had torn up his oath in front of Corrigan himself, and the tearing-up had cost him his career, his clearance, and any future in the work he'd trained ten years for. The ministry had blacklisted him quietly, the way they did everything. He'd come north because there was nowhere else that felt true, and because Paloma was here, and because some animal part of him believed the Current had shown him her for a reason and he intended to find out what the reason was.

He hadn't found it yet. But most evenings, walking home past the river with the light going amber on the water, he thought he was getting closer.

* * *

The community meeting happened on a Thursday, which meant half the town arrived late because of Elian's class, and Paloma stood at the back of the hall expecting to be discussed and instead found herself defended.

Marta Okonkwo went first. Marta, whose insulin Paloma had flown through weather that grounded everyone else, twice, once in November with ice on the struts. Marta stood up in front of the two inquiry officers with their identical gray jackets and said, "You want to know if Paloma Kavanagh can be trusted. I'll tell you what I know. I know my daughter is alive because of her, and I know she's never once charged me for an emergency run, and I know she could have sold whatever she found on that river to your people or anyone else and she didn't. She gave it back to the water. Ask yourselves why."

Old Tom Bearpaw spoke after her, slower, about the years Paloma's father had flown this country and the debts he'd owed and the way his daughter had spent six years paying them without complaint. "The debt was inherited," he said. "So was the flying. One of those she chose."

Then Dee Sandoval stood up and said something that made the room go quiet:

"You suspended the only pilot rated for the Kettle Falls strip. The only one. So I want to know—who's going to fly our medicine in this winter? Because we've done the math, and your charter service costs four times what she charges, and half of us can't pay it, and some of us will simply do without. Is that the outcome you intended?"

The officers looked at each other. Nobody answered. It was, Paloma thought later, the first honest question anyone from the ministry had failed to answer in her presence, and it had come from a woman selling flour and shotgun shells out of a general store.

* * *

The license came back in October, by bush plane, hand-delivered by a young officer who wouldn't meet her eyes and left so fast he nearly forgot his hat.

Attached was a memo, unsigned: *Inquiry concluded. No findings. License reinstated subject to standard review in twelve months.* And beneath that, in different ink, added by someone with a steadier hand: *We know what you destroyed. We also know you could have kept it. Consider what that says about you, and consider that we considered it too.*

Paloma read it three times. Then she folded it into quarters and used it to scrape carbon off the Beaver's exhaust stub, which felt about right.

* * *

The charter was Marta's idea, formally, and the town's idea in truth. *KETTLE FALLS AIR — MEDICAL & SUPPLY — BY ARRANGEMENT,* painted on a new sign over the hangar door, the letters crooked because Tom Bearpaw had insisted on painting them himself and nobody had the heart to straighten them. The paperwork was thin and local and entirely legal, in the way small things in the north are legal: a cooperative, a fee schedule posted on corkboard, a fuel account at Fort Meridian paid in installments by fourteen families who had decided, collectively, that their pilot belonged to them.

Her first flight under the new arrangement was a medicine run to Three Loon Landing, forty minutes east, routine as breathing. But she walked around the Beaver beforehand the way she always did, and checked the fuel the way she always did, and when she climbed into the left seat she paused, as she always did now, beneath the photograph pinned above the panel.

No ledger. No debt. Just Elian on the shoreline, squinting, and behind him the faint silver line of the Current, looking on that day—for the first time she could remember—like just a river.

She fired up the engine and taxied out, and the whole town seemed to be watching from the shore, which they were, because in Kettle Falls a first flight was an event and always had been. Dee Sandoval raised a hand from the store porch. Tom Bearpaw touched his hat brim. Marta held up her daughter, waving, high against the grey sky.

Paloma lifted off into air that smelled like snow coming, banked once over the settlement—not showing off, just saying what needed saying—and pointed the nose east into the wide morning.

Below her, the country unrolled: lakes like dropped coins, creeks writing themselves across muskeg, and far to the west, half-hidden in haze, the canyon where the Current ran. She didn't avoid looking at it anymore. She didn't seek it out either. It was there, the way grief is there, and wonder, and everything else you carry that doesn't fit in a case.

On the radio, a voice crackled—a hunter reporting a downed canoe party near the old trading post, coordinates rough, weather building. She wrote it down, thumbed the transmit switch.

"Kettle Falls Air, copies. En route."

And she was—wheels up, tanks full, a photo above the panel and a town behind her that had stood up in a cold hall and said her name out loud until the government flinched. Whatever the Current had shown Elian in those three days under, whatever reason it had for putting her face in front of him, she was beginning to think it hadn't been a warning at all.

It had been an introduction.

She keyed the mic once more, more to herself than anyone listening, and smiled.

"Kettle Falls Air, airborne."
