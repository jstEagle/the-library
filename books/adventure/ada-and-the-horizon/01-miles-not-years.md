## 1. Miles, Not Years

The storm came up out of the west like a debt collector, and Bram Blackwood drove toward it the way he did everything these days: because stopping was worse.

He had the rig's nose into the wind on a gravel section of the Kestrel Plains road, dust boiling off the shoulders in long brown banners, and the sky ahead had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong—green-black at the base, sickly yellow at the top, the whole thing rotating with the slow patience of something that knew it was bigger than you. On his dash-mounted anemometer, the numbers climbed. Forty knots. Forty-five. The mast antennas hummed at frequencies only he could hear, or pretended to.

"Come on, then," he told it, and meant it in the way of a man who had stopped being afraid of weather and started being afraid of quiet.

Seven years ago, Bram Blackwood had been a name. It had been on research grants and conference programs and one very unfortunate subpoena. Now it was on nothing. He'd traded it away piece by piece—the publications first, then the bank accounts, then the phone number his mother still called until she couldn't—and what remained was a man in a reinforced pickup with three hundred thousand in Syndicate scrip hanging around his neck like an anchor chained to a smaller anchor.

The Vane Syndicate didn't call it a debt. They called it *consideration*. Consideration for the lawyers who'd made his problems disappear. Consideration for the new identity, the clean plates, the quiet little life they'd arranged for him the way you arrange furniture in a room you own. Every storm he chased for them bought back a few thousand against the principal, and every year the interest ate more than the storms earned, so that after seven years of professional driving through apocalyptic weather, Bram owed slightly more than when he started. He'd done the math once, drunk, on a napkin, and then he'd eaten the napkin, which felt symbolically appropriate.

The hail started at 4:17 in the afternoon, white and furious, hammering the roof like applause from an unfriendly crowd. Bram threaded the rig down a farm track, set the stabilizers, and let the instruments drink. This was the part he was still good at, the part nobody could repossess: reading a storm's mood the way other men read wives or horses. This one was all bluster and rotation but no spine—it would shear apart by dark and drop maybe two inches of rain on wheat that needed six.

Which meant the Syndicate would pay him for data, not for drama. Fine. Data paid the same.

* * *

The message came through at dusk, while he was packing the probes, wet to the elbows and oddly cheerful about it. The rig's encrypted channel crackled and resolved into the voice of Mr. Quill, the Syndicate's dispatcher—a dry, precise voice, like a man reading inventory aloud in an empty warehouse.

"Blackwood. Good numbers today."

"They'll do," Bram said, wringing out his sleeve.

"A formality follows. Are you stationary?"

"I'm always stationary. I'm stationary at eighty miles an hour half the time, but I'm stationary."

A pause, in which Quill declined to acknowledge humor as a category. "We have a contract matter upriver. The Halvorsen survey. Environmental assessment, delta reach, north fork. Nine days past their last scheduled transmission."

Bram frowned at the dash. He knew the name the way you know a name from an old life—Dr. Elin Halvorsen, sediment hydrology, a paper fifteen years back that Bram had cited twice before he'd become a man without citations. Careful work. Honest work. The kind of work the Syndicate usually hired precisely so it could be quietly buried.

"Nine days is long," he said carefully.

"It is within tolerance. Radio failure is common in that reach. However." Quill's voice did something almost human, which for Quill was like a glacier doing a cartwheel. "The survey is contracted under our flood-control portfolio. There are... sensitivities. We prefer a discreet party. Someone who understands discretion."

Meaning someone owned. Meaning me.

"What's the job exactly?" Bram asked, though he already felt the shape of it, cold in his stomach like swallowed river water.

"Resupply and status check. You will carry provisions, spare radio components, and medical stores to the Halvorsen camp at Marker Nineteen on the north fork. You will confirm the team's condition and reestablish communications. You will report findings to this channel and no other." A beat. "Compensation is substantial, Blackwood. Substantial enough that we can discuss restructuring your outstanding consideration."

Bram sat very still in the cab. Outside, the broken storm was limping off east, trailing veils of rain across the last light, and somewhere behind him a prairie dog was making a sound like a squeaky toy being murdered, and all of it was ordinary, and none of it was.

Restructuring. In seven years, the Syndicate had never once offered restructuring. Debts were how they held people; you didn't dangle a man's chain in front of him unless you wanted him to run toward something. Or away from something. Or into something.

"And if I find trouble up there?" he said. "If the team's had an accident, or—"

"Then you will report it accurately," Quill said, "and return. Discretion, Blackwood. That is the entire contract. Provisions up, information down, no stops, no questions, no local entanglements."

*No local entanglements.* As if the people upriver were weather. As if a whole survey crew were a thing you could drive past at eighty miles an hour.

"When do I leave?"

"First light. The boat is fueled at your berth. Manifest is transmitted." And then, because Quill was capable of exactly one courtesy per decade: "Blackwood. This is a final assignment. Complete it well, and the consideration conversation becomes a genuine conversation. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," Bram said, which was true, and which was also the most frightening sentence he'd said in years.

The channel clicked dead.

* * *

He didn't sleep much, which was normal, but the quality of the not-sleeping was new. He lay in the rig's sleeper berth listening to the wind finish dismantling the storm behind him and took inventory, the way he did on bad nights, counting what he owed and what he'd lost like a man sorting coins in the dark.

What he owed: three hundred twelve thousand, four hundred and change, plus interest, plus whatever a soul went for these days—he assumed market rate, he assumed the Syndicate set it.

What he'd lost: a name, a career, a mother whose letters he read and never answered because answering meant a trail, a brother who'd stopped writing after year three, a woman named Marta whom he thought about mostly at night and on bridges and during hailstorms, which was to say constantly. Seven years of invisibility, and here was the joke he never told anyone: invisibility hadn't protected him. It had just made sure that when the Syndicate finally decided to spend him, nobody would come looking.

And now they were offering it all back. Name, slate, future—one boat trip up a river, one look at a silent camp, one report filed the way they wanted it filed.

That was the part his gut kept snagging on. *The way they wanted it filed.* Nine days of silence wasn't a radio problem. Nine days of silence, from a Syndicate-contracted survey on Syndicate flood-control contracts, in the season before the monsoon, was a story with the ending already written. Somewhere upriver at Marker Nineteen, something had happened that the Vane Syndicate wanted confirmed, packaged, and buried, and they wanted it done by a man too deep in their pocket to ask what he was burying.

Bram stared at the ceiling of the berth and had a conversation with himself that lasted most of the night.

*Take the job,* said the part of him that was tired. *Take the job, say the words, be free. Free men can answer letters. Free men can find Marta. Free men can sleep.*

*And if there are people up there who need help?* said the part of him that had spent thirty years running toward storms instead of away from them. *And if the report they want isn't the truth?*

*You don't know that,* said the tired part.

*You know exactly that,* said the other part. *You've known since Quill said "discreet."*

Around three in the morning, rain began again, soft and steady, the good kind, the kind the wheat needed, and Bram got up and made coffee in the rig's little galley and watched it fall through the dome light. He thought about Dr. Elin Halvorsen, whose careful honest work he'd admired from a distance in another life. He thought about her team, nine days quiet. He thought about the word *entanglement*, and how the Syndicate used it to mean *people*, and how a man could tell everything he needed to know about an organization from its vocabulary.

He told himself he was taking the job for the money. He told himself he'd go up, make his observations, file an accurate report—accurate, he'd insist on accurate, that was his line in the sand—and come home to a renegotiated life. He told himself the dread sitting in his chest was just pre-trip nerves, the same flutter he got before every chase, and that professionalism was simply dread wearing a clean shirt.

He was lying, and some part of him knew it, the way you know a storm has a spine in it even when the sky hasn't decided yet.

At first light, the river was gray silk and the boat waited at his berth with the manifest already loaded: crates of provisions, a case of radio spares, medical stores, and—oddly, noted in Quill's precise hand—two empty document cases, waterproof, lockable. For samples, presumably. For paperwork.

For whatever came down.

Bram cast off the lines, eased the throttle forward, and pointed the bow up the north fork, into country he hadn't seen in seven years, where a silence nine days old was waiting for him like a hole in the water.

Halfway to the first bend, he understood that he was already lying to himself about coming back.
