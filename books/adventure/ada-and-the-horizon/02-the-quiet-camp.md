## 2. The Quiet Camp

The river told you things before your eyes did. That was the first lesson of the delta, and Bram Blackwood had learned it the hard way, back when learning things the hard way was still a hobby rather than a lifestyle.

For three miles now, the river had been telling him *wrong*. No smoke from the cook fire. No laundry strung between the survey poles. No sound at all except the water worrying at its banks and a fish hawk somewhere upstream, screaming about its personal problems.

He cut the engine a quarter mile out and let the boat drift, listening.

Nothing.

Nine days of silence from the Halvorsen expedition, and the Syndicate had finally gotten around to caring about it—enough to pay Bram, of all people, to run supplies upriver and "assess the situation." Assess. In Vane Syndicate vocabulary, that word had a way of meaning whatever the person holding the ledger needed it to mean.

"Probably they're fine," he said to the boat, because talking to boats was cheaper than talking to therapists, and he'd tried both. "Probably Halvorsen's got his whole team down with river fever and they've been too busy vomiting to radio anybody."

The boat did not answer, which was more honesty than most people offered.

Bram eased the throttle forward and rounded the last bend.

* * *

The camp sat on a shelf of high ground above an oxbow, six tents and a mess shelter and a crane for pulling sediment cores off the barge. It was the kind of camp that should have been loud. Generator hum, radio chatter, somebody always arguing about somebody else's coffee. Instead it was quiet in the specific, terrible way that meant *emptied*, not *sleeping*. Quiet like a house after a funeral.

Bram tied off at the rickety dock and stood there a moment with his hand on the cleat, doing the arithmetic he'd promised himself he wouldn't do.

Six tents. Nine days silent. One paid job from people who killed problems the way other organizations filed them.

"Could still be fever," he said.

He walked up the bank anyway. Cowardice and curiosity wore the same boots; you just pointed them different directions.

The mess tent came first. Inside, everything was in order—which was itself wrong. Plates washed and stacked. Benches pushed in. A chalkboard on the tent wall still bore somebody's handwriting: a supply list, half crossed out, ending mid-word in *keros—*. Nobody leaves a camp tidy unless they're leaving in a hurry or being made to. And nobody leaves a camp this tidy by choice.

The radio shack was next, and the radio shack settled it.

Somebody had taken a fire axe—or something with a fire axe's opinions—to both transmitter units. The main set was a ruin of glass and copper, its vacuum tubes scattered across the desk like the world's saddest marbles. The backup, a rugged little field unit bolted to a shelf, hadn't just been smashed. It had been *unbolted* first, carried outside, and destroyed against a rock, methodically, piece by piece. Bram crouched over the wreckage and felt his neck prickle.

Fever doesn't smash radios. Panic smashes radios in one place, fast, so you can run. This was patience. This was making sure nobody downstream ever heard a distress call.

"Okay," he murmured. "Okay, Halvorsen. What did you step in?"

He found the doctor forty minutes later, after searching every tent twice and calling out until his voice went hoarse—not because he expected an answer, but because the alternative to calling out was admitting what kind of silence he was walking through.

They'd buried him on the rise above camp, where the ground stayed dry even in flood season. A cairn of river stones, each one placed by hand, rounded and gray and fitted together with the fussy care of someone who'd loved the man and wanted the work done right. Dr. E. Halvorsen, the headstone said—a flat slab of slate, letters scratched deep with a knife or a chisel. *1951–1977. He measured honestly.*

Bram took off his hat.

He'd met Halvorsen exactly once, two years ago, at a Syndicate function Bram had attended under protest and a borrowed suit. The scientist had spent the entire evening cornering anyone who'd stand still about sediment load and levee compaction, eyes bright as a boy's, while the Syndicate men smiled their boardroom smiles and waited for him to finish so they could go back to discussing freight rates. Bram had liked him instantly, the way you like a dog that barks at cars.

*He measured honestly.* Whoever had carved that had wanted it understood. Wanted whoever came looking—and somebody would come looking—to know the man had died clean.

Bram put his hat back on and looked at the cairn a long moment.

"I'm sorry," he said. It wasn't adequate. Nothing was. But he'd learned that saying the inadequate thing out loud was better than carrying the silence, which got heavier.

Then he heard the click.

Small sound. Metallic. Behind him and to the left, maybe ten feet, at the treeline where the shadows pooled thick under the fig trees.

Bram raised his hands very slowly and turned.

A girl stood between two tree trunks, half in shadow. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Thin as a heron, sunburned, hair hacked short with what had clearly been a knife and clearly not a mirror anywhere nearby. She wore a man's canvas jacket two sizes too big, and in her hands, held in a two-fisted grip that said she'd practiced, was a flare gun.

"Easy," Bram said.

"You're Syndicate." Her voice cracked on it, but she didn't lower the flare gun. "The boat's a Syndicate charter. I know the paint scheme."

"The boat's mine. They're paying for the fuel." He kept his hands up, kept his voice level, the voice he used on spooked animals and angry lockkeepers. "Name's Blackwood. Bram Blackwood. I was hired to bring supplies upriver and find out why you'd gone quiet."

"And then report back." Her chin lifted. "So they can send the ones with guns."

"The ones with guns already came, didn't they." He nodded past her, toward the wrecked radio shack, toward the cairn. "That wasn't fever."

Something flickered across her face—grief and fury fighting for the same window—and for a second he thought she'd fire the flare gun right into his chest, which would hurt considerably and accomplish nothing except setting them both on fire. Then her jaw set, and the grief lost the fight, or at least agreed to wait its turn.

"They came nine days ago," she said. "Four men. My father sent me into the swamp with the journals before they reached the camp. Told me to count to five hundred and come back." Her grip tightened on the flare gun. "I counted to five hundred. Then I counted to five hundred again. When I came back, they were gone, and my father—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I buried him. It took all day. I'm not strong enough for it, so it took all day."

Bram lowered his hands slowly. "You did good work. The cairn. The stone."

"He measured honestly," she said, as if that explained the epitaph, and maybe it did.

"I'm not Syndicate," Bram said. "Not really. Not anymore than a ferry is owned by its passengers. They pay me to move things. That's the whole relationship. I move things and we pretend we don't know each other."

"That's a lot of pretending."

"It's the family business." He gestured carefully at the flare gun. "Can I put my arms down? They're getting heavy, and I want to seem competent."

She considered him with a long, unblinking stare that reminded him uncomfortably of the Syndicate's accountants, if the accountants had been armed and grieving. Then, slowly, the flare gun dipped—not lowered, never quite lowered, but angled away from his sternum toward a compromise position involving his knee.

"Ada," she said. "Ada Halvorsen."

"Pleasure. Under the circumstances, deeply not the word, but you take my meaning."

* * *

She wouldn't go back inside the survey tent until he went in first, and she watched him the whole time from the flap, flare gun ready, as though he might make off with a filing cabinet. The tent was the largest in camp and the most violated. Drawers pulled out and dumped. Trunk lids hanging open. Somebody had searched this place thoroughly and left in a temper.

But they'd missed something. Or missed the point of something.

On the central table, under a canvas groundsheet Ada swept aside with her free hand, lay a stack of field journals—eleven of them, oilcloth-wrapped, bound with cord. Beside them, a wooden case stenciled with sample numbers, packed tight with sealed brass tubes. Sediment cores. Bram knew cores; he'd hauled crates of them for the Syndicate without once asking what was inside, because asking questions was how you stopped being invisible.

"These are what they wanted," Ada said. It wasn't a question either.

"Looks like it."

"They tore the tents apart and didn't find them. Because Papa hid them in the *latrine trench* for two days before moving them here." For the first time, something almost like a smile touched her mouth—brittle, exhausted, but real. "He said no Syndicate man has ever voluntarily dug in a latrine trench. He said it was the safest place in the delta."

Bram laughed before he could stop himself—one short, surprised bark—and immediately regretted it when her face closed again. But she didn't raise the flare gun, and after a moment she sat down heavily on a crate, as if the standing part had become optional.

"My father found something," she said. "In the cores. Something about the levees. He wouldn't tell me exactly—he said knowing was dangerous, and look how safe ignorance kept him." Her voice wobbled and steadied. "But he wrote it all down. He wrote everything down. He was that kind of man."

*He measured honestly.*

Bram looked at the journals. Eleven volumes of a dead man's handwriting, wrapped in oilcloth, smelling faintly of latrine trench and survival. He thought about the smashed radios, built to keep this exact stack from reaching anyone. He thought about the four men who'd come and gone, and about the fact that the Syndicate had hired *him*—cheap, deniable, expendable Bram Blackwood—to come upriver and assess, which meant they weren't entirely sure the job was finished.

Which meant somebody might check whether it was finished.

And he thought about the girl watching him with a flare gun and her father's stubbornness written all over her face, waiting to find out what kind of man he was, and having every reason on earth to expect the worst.

"How much fuel you got?" she asked suddenly.

"Half a tank. Why?"

"Because they'll send more," Ada said. "The ones who came were scouts. My father said so—the Syndicate always sends scouts first, cheap ones, to see who's watching. The expensive ones come after." She stood, and tucked the journals against her chest like a child holding a cat, and looked at him with red-rimmed eyes that refused, absolutely refused, to beg. "So. Are you going to report back, Mr. Blackwood? Or are you going to help me carry my father's work home?"

Outside, far downstream, thin and wrong against the evening birdsong, Bram heard it—the low diesel knock of a boat running hard against the current, coming upriver.

Not his. He knew his own engine like his own heartbeat.

Ada heard it too. Her knuckles went white around the journals.

"Well," Bram said quietly, reaching for the canvas wrap. "Let's call that a deadline."
