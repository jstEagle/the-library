## 3. What Her Father Found

Ada Halvorsen had counted the seconds since the boat first appeared on the bend, and she had not liked the arithmetic. One man. Alone. No escort, no gun visible, a supply run's worth of crates stacked under a tarp like he expected to be paid for them.

She kept the flare gun level anyway, because her father had taught her that hope was a fine thing to carry and a terrible thing to aim with.

"Put it down," Bram said, hands out, voice pitched somewhere between apology and exhaustion. "I'm just the delivery."

"You're late," Ada said. "Nine days late. Everyone who was supposed to come is dead or gone, so forgive me if I don't hand you my father's life's work because you have a nice tarp."

Bram looked at her for a long moment—the flare gun steady in her grip, the mud on her knees, the cairn of river stones visible over her shoulder where Dr. Elias Halvorsen now resided under about four hundred pounds of geology—and something in his face shifted from wariness to a kind of grim respect.

"Fair," he said. "That's fair."

"I'm glad we agree." She lowered the flare gun an inch. Only an inch. "You can come into the survey tent. Slowly. If you reach for anything that looks like data, I will put a flare through your chest, and while it won't kill you, you'll spend the rest of your life explaining the scar."

"That's the second most reasonable threat anyone's made at me this year," Bram said, ducking through the tent flap. "What was the first?"

"My father's. He told the Syndicate men the same thing nine days ago." Her voice caught on the number, and she let it. "They didn't listen either."

* * *

Inside the tent, the lamps were low and the air smelled of canvas, kerosene, and river damp. Cores lay racked along one wall like specimens in a coffin display—aluminum tubes, each labeled in her father's small, precise handwriting: *Site 4, 6.2 m. Site 7, 8.9 m.* Journals everywhere. Notebooks, ledgers, loose pages weighted down with stones against the wind that came up the valley every evening like a debt collector.

Bram set down the crate of supplies—salt, fuel, antibiotics, the radio parts nobody would ever need now—and stood there awkwardly, a large man in a small room full of another man's ghost.

"Your father," he said. "I'm sorry. I didn't know him, but—" He stopped, started again. "The people who sent me here are going to want to know what happened. What do I tell them?"

"The truth," Ada said. "If you're capable of it."

"Occupational hazard," Bram said. "Go on, then. Show me why a sixteen-year-old is holding a survey camp alone with a flare gun."

So she did.

She pulled the first journal toward her across the folding table, and her hands were steadier than she felt. "My father spent eleven years coring this delta. Eleven years. The Syndicate hired him three years ago to do the sediment surveys for their flood-control contracts—the levees, the retention basins, all of it. The work that lets them sell protection to forty towns downstream." She opened the journal to a page dense with figures. "And for two years, the numbers came back wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong as in *impossible*. Wrong as in the clay layers don't match anything the Syndicate's published surveys say should be there." She turned the page so he could see the stratigraphy columns, neat as sheet music. "Sediment doesn't lie, Mr. Blackwood. It's the only honest witness in the world—it just sits there accumulating testimony, layer by layer, century by century. My father used to say you could interrogate mud all day and it would never once perjure itself."

Bram huffed something that was almost a laugh. "He sounds like he was fun at parties."

"He was *terrible* at parties. He'd corner people and explain braided channel migration until they fled into the garden." She smiled, and it hurt. "But he was right. Look."

She laid out three core logs side by side. "Here's what the Syndicate's own contracted surveyors filed: competent silt over stable gravel, load-bearing capacity well within design tolerances. Here's what my father actually pulled out of the ground at the same coordinates: sixty centimeters of uncompacted flood silt sitting on top of old channel sand. Do you know what happens when you build a levee on soup?"

"It lies down," Bram said quietly.

"It lies down. In a hundred-year flood event—and the monsoon coming up the coast is tracking to be exactly that—those levees don't hold. They don't even fail dramatically. They slump. They saturate, they soften, and they fold, town by town, starting with the lowest-lying ones. Marrow Bend goes first. Then Ferrow, then the whole string of them down to the sea." She tapped the final page, where her father had written a single line in red ink, underlined twice:

*The protections are the hazard.*

"They knew," Bram said. Not a question.

"They've known since before they poured the first footing. The falsified surveys weren't an accident—they were the product. Protection is what they sell, Mr. Blackwood. Whether it works is a rounding error." Ada heard her own voice go flat and cold, which surprised her; she hadn't known she could sound like that. "Forty towns. Tens of thousands of people. And my father tried to file a dissent report, and nine days ago the Syndicate's men came upriver to discuss it with him."

* * *

Bram was quiet for a long time. He sat with the journals open in front of him, big weathered hands resting flat on either side of them like a man being careful not to disturb evidence at a scene.

Then he asked the question Ada had been waiting for him to ask.

"Why is the radio smashed?"

"Because they got here first," she said. "Before you. Before anyone. Nine days ago. Four men came off a grey patrol boat with Syndicate markings painted over. They took the crew—marched them down to the water at gunpoint. My father refused to go, so they—" She stopped. Started again, because her father had raised her to finish her sentences no matter what. "They buried him themselves. Under the stones. I think they thought it would look respectful. I think they thought I wasn't watching from the tree line."

"Were you?"

"Every second." She met his eyes. "I was fourteen feet away with a flare gun and a knife, doing the math on four men, and the math said wait. So I waited. And then they took the radios apart with rifle butts, and they burned the duplicate field copies, and they left. But they didn't find these." She put her palm flat on the journals. "My father hid them in the septic trench. He hid them in the *septic trench*, Mr. Blackwood, because he knew exactly how much dignity the Syndicate was willing to spend digging for data. They searched the tents, the lab, the core shed. Nobody checks the latrine."

Bram laughed—a short, startled bark of genuine amusement—and then pressed a hand over his mouth like laughing in front of her was a crime. "Sorry. That's—God rest him, that's the most scientist thing I've ever heard."

"He would have been pleased you appreciated it. He'd have explained anaerobic decomposition to you next."

"And the cores?" Bram nodded at the rack of aluminum tubes. "Those too?"

"Every original. Sealed, labeled, chain-of-custody logged in his own hand. He was very thorough about custody." She watched Bram's face as understanding arrived there, slow and heavy, like weather. "You understand now. Why you're really here. Why the job came up so suddenly, why they needed someone invisible, someone with no questions and no ties. They didn't send you to deliver salt and antibiotics, Mr. Blackwood. They sent you to confirm the site was clean. And if it wasn't clean—"

"—to make it clean," Bram finished. His voice had gone rough. "Yeah. I did the math on the way upriver. I just kept hoping I was wrong."

"Were you ever right about anything else?"

"No," he said honestly. "Mostly boats and weather. Which, incidentally—" He stood, went to the tent flap, and looked out at the sky over the ridge line. Ada watched him read it the way her father read cores: patiently, professionally, with growing alarm. "That cloud build is early. Three weeks early, minimum. Barometric pressure's falling like my credit rating. Your father's hundred-year event isn't coming someday, kid. It's coming *now*."

"Then we take the data downstream now," Ada said. "To the capital. To the courts, the press, the—"

"To the courts the Syndicate owns," Bram said, "and the press they lease, and the magistrates they dine with." He turned back to her, and for the first time since landing, he looked tired all the way through. "Kid—Ada. There may be a door somewhere that isn't bought. I genuinely hope so. But I've spent seven years learning how far Vane money reaches, and it reaches further than a monsoon."

"Then we find the door anyway." She stood too, and pulled the pack she'd already packed—of course she'd already packed; she'd packed it on day two—onto her shoulders. "Because those towns have maybe ten days, and I am not letting my father's last eleven years rot in a septic trench while the Syndicate drowns its customers. You can leave. You can go back and tell them the site was clean. I'll even write it down for you so you don't have to lie face to face."

Bram looked at her. At the pack. At the flare gun still riding her hip like a sidearm.

"You know what they'll send after us," he said. "Not four men this time. A boat, fixers, Sable himself if the data matters enough—and it does."

"Then we'd better be fast."

"You know the river?"

"I know my father's charts better than the Syndicate does. He drew half of them himself, including three channels they don't have on any official map." She held up the oilskin-wrapped bundle. "Can you drive a boat fast at night?"

"Kid, driving boats fast at night is the only thing I've ever been praised for."

"Then we're leaving at dark," Ada said, "and you're going to wish you'd stayed invisible."

Something crossed Bram's face then—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if a man hadn't forgotten the route. He picked up the crate he'd carried nine days upriver, dug out the antibiotics and the chocolate—there was always chocolate in Syndicate supply crates, morale rations, as though confectionery could sweeten complicity—and set both in front of her without a word.

"Eat," he said. "Both of us. It's a long way to Marrow Bend, and the storm's coming down to meet us."

Outside, the first fat drops hit the canvas, and somewhere upriver, faint as a lie, came the low diesel thrum of a boat that wasn't theirs.
