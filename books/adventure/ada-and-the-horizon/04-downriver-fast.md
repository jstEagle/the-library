## 4. Downriver, Fast

The *Maggie B* had a top speed of eleven knots on her best day, and this was not her best day. This was the day she was carrying two hundred pounds of stolen survey data, one grieving teenager, and a man whose entire strategy for staying alive for seven years had been *don't be interesting* — all of it running downstream ahead of a Syndicate patrol boat that could do fourteen knots without breaking a sweat.

"Left," Ada said.

I eased the wheel left. The channel bent around a gravel bar that hadn't been on any chart I'd ever bought, because charts cost money and the Syndicate owned the companies that made them.

"Harder left."

"I heard you the first time, kid."

"You didn't *steer* like you heard me." She was crouched over her father's field journal in the wheelhouse's one good patch of lamplight, one hand flat on the page to keep it from flapping, the other pointing at nothing in particular with the confidence of someone who had never once been wrong in front of me. "There's a shoal. Papa drew it in red. He said red meant 'things that will ruin your week.'"

"Your father sounds like he'd have been fun at parties."

"He hated parties. He said they were just weather indoors." She turned a page. "Hard right after the shoal. Then we're in the narrows."

The narrows. I'd run the Vire River for nine years and I had opinions about the narrows, most of which involved the word *no*. Forty meters wide in places, current like a freight train with a grudge, and rock walls that ate propellers the way I ate instant noodles: constantly, and without joy.

But behind us, maybe six kilometers back, was a patrol boat with a searchlight and men paid to be thorough, so my opinions about the narrows had been overruled by arithmetic.

"Kill the running lights," Ada said.

"They're already killed."

"Then kill them deader."

I reached up and pulled the breaker anyway, mostly to give her the satisfaction. The wheelhouse went dark except for the hooded lamp over her journal. Outside, the moon was doing its best work behind a shelf of cloud, which was either luck or the river taking our side, and I wasn't ready to believe the river had opinions yet.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about running black at night: your ears become your eyes. Every sound gets a job. The hiss off the bow tells you your speed. The slap against the hull tells you if you're drifting sideways. And somewhere under all of it, if the water is shallow enough and mean enough, there's a sound like gravel being chewed slowly, which is the sound of your bank account dying.

We came into the narrows an hour past midnight. Ada stood beside me at the wheel, journal open, hair stuck to her forehead in the humidity, and she called the river like she'd grown up inside it.

"Bend left around the bluff. There's an eddy behind the point — don't fight it, ride it, it'll swing our nose."

I rode it. The *Maggie B* swung, wallowed, caught herself.

"Good," she said, and then, quieter, like she was embarrassed by it: "Papa always said good."

"How long did he run this stretch?"

"Every summer since I was eight. He said the river keeps honest books." Her voice did something small and complicated on the word *honest*, and I pretended not to notice, because that's a skill too. "He'd have hated this. Not the danger. The *faking*. He could forgive a storm anything. Storms don't lie about what they are."

"And the Syndicate does."

"The Syndicate would falsify a sunrise if there was money in it."

Ahead, the channel pinched between two bluffs, and the current picked up its pace like it had somewhere better to be. I throttled down to idle — engines muffled, exhaust baffled with a blanket and a prayer, the whole arrangement held together with wire and optimism — and let the river do the driving.

"Shoal, starboard side," Ada said. "Papa marked it three years ago. It might have moved."

"Might have *moved*?"

"Rivers do that. It's their whole personality."

The chewing-gravel sound started up off our starboard quarter, soft as a whisper, and I eased the wheel left until it stopped, and then the channel opened out again and we were through the worst of it, and I realized I'd been holding my breath long enough to qualify for a medical study.

"Not bad," I said.

Ada looked at me like I'd handed her a medal made of cardboard. "*Not bad?*"

"It's the highest rating I give. Ask anyone. Nobody has ever gotten more than 'not bad.'"

"Has anyone ever gotten less?"

"Once. A man who tried to pay me in live chickens."

She laughed — short, startled out of her, gone almost before it arrived — but it happened, and in the dark of the wheelhouse it sounded like something being unpacked after seven years in storage. I decided right then that I'd hear it again before this was over, even if it killed me, which, given the circumstances, remained a live possibility.

* * *

The patrol boat found us anyway. Of course it did. Syndicate fixers are like mosquitoes that way; you can outrun any individual mosquito, but they know where you're going.

It was Ada who heard it first — a low diesel thrum upstream, wrong rhythm for a barge. She killed the lamp with her palm and we both went still, listening, while the *Maggie B* drifted with the current like a leaf pretending to be dead.

Searchlight. A white blade sweeping the water a kilometer back, cutting the mist into pieces.

"They'll catch us in the open stretch below the falls road," I murmured. "Eleven knots against fourteen. It's just math."

"I hate math," Ada said. "Papa loved it. One more thing I have to forgive him for." She flipped pages, fast, finger walking down hand-inked contour lines. "There. Sable Creek. It comes in from the west about two kilometers down. Papa says—" she squinted at her father's cramped handwriting— "'navigable at high water for perhaps four hundred meters, then debris, then a logjam I intend to sue personally.' If we turn up it, we're off the main channel. They can't follow without lights, and lights in the creek mean rocks."

"And if we meet those rocks in the dark?"

"Then we'll have bigger problems than a patrol boat."

"That's not the comfort you think it is."

"It's not supposed to comfort you. It's supposed to focus you." She looked up at me, and in the hooded lamplight her eyes were her father's eyes, the same stubborn gray, the same *I have read the data and the data says move*. "Bram. They're gaining."

So we turned up Sable Creek.

I cut the engine to a bare burble and we ghosted off the main channel into water the color of strong tea, willows closing over us like a curtain drawn by careful hands. Behind us the searchlight swept the main stem, found nothing but empty river, and kept sweeping. The diesel thrum grew, passed, faded — fourteen knots chasing eleven knots down a river that no longer contained us.

I let out a breath and allowed myself exactly four seconds of feeling clever.

That's when the logjam introduced itself.

* * *

To be fair to everyone involved, Ada warned me. "Debris," she said, "then the logjam," and I heard her, and I even slowed, but the current in the creek had other plans — high water from the early rains had shoved half a forest downstream, and the *Maggie B*'s bow slid into a tangle of silvered trunks with all the grace of a man walking into a glass door.

The crack came from below decks. Anyone who has owned a boat knows that particular crack. It is the sound of money leaving.

We drifted free of the jam, listing gently to port, and I went aft and lifted the inspection hatch and shone the lamp down, and there it was: the propeller shaft, bent near the coupling, wobbling like a guilty conscience. The prop itself was fine. The shaft was done. No amount of wire or optimism was fixing that.

"Well," I said. "On the bright side, the patrol boat's gone."

"On the bright side," Ada agreed, "we're also stranded in a flooded creek full of logs, and the monsoon is coming early, and we're carrying the only copy of evidence that powerful people have already killed for." She thought about it. "Also there's a spider the size of a saucer on your shoulder."

I handled that last part with tremendous dignity, which is to say I made a noise I'll deny to my grave and swatted it into the river, and Ada laughed again — longer this time, real, doubled over the journal — and I stood there in the dripping dark thinking that if I had to be shipwrecked next to anybody, I could do worse than a sixteen-year-old who navigates like a harbor pilot and laughs at spiders.

"We walk," I said finally. "There's a trading post maybe ten kilometers south. Old Corvane place. Abandoned since the Syndicate bought out the lease. We hole up till daylight, figure out our next move."

"With the journals?"

"With the journals." I patted the waterproof satchel slung across my chest, the one holding seven years of Dr. Halvorsen's honest books. "These don't leave my sight, and I'm guessing they don't leave yours either."

"No," Ada said simply. "They don't."

We gathered what we could carry — food, water, the flare gun, the journals, a tarp — and I paused at the rail to look back at the *Maggie B*, sitting low and wounded in the tea-colored water. Nine years she'd carried me invisible. Felt rude to leave her like this.

"She did her job," Ada said beside me, reading my face the way she read her father's charts.

"Yeah," I said. "Not bad, old girl."

We climbed down onto the mud, and the wetlands took us in — dark, humming, ankle-deep already with rain that had no business falling this early in the season. Somewhere south, thunder grumbled like a landlord checking on late rent.

Ada stopped at the tree line and looked back once, north, toward the delta towns sleeping behind their beautiful, doomed levees.

"Bram," she said. "How fast can a person walk through a swamp?"

"Slower than the water rises," I said. "Let's find out how much slower."
