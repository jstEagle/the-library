## 5. The Optimists' Ledger

The trading post had been dead longer than my father had been dead, which felt like a strange kind of company to keep.

It sat on a bend of the river like an old man who'd decided not to get up anymore—roof sagging in the middle, porch boards gone soft as bread, a faded sign that read VASQUEZ & SONS: PROVISIONS, FUEL, HONEST WEIGHTS. The *and Sons* had been painted over at some point, and underneath, if you tilted your head, you could still see it. Somebody's inheritance, erased and repainted and abandoned anyway.

Bram hauled the boat's remaining supplies up onto the porch while I pried open the shutters with a crowbar he kept handing me without being asked, which I was beginning to understand was his whole personality.

"Careful," he said, for the fourth time.

"I'm always careful."

"You ran the Kettle Rapids at night with a broken chart."

"The chart wasn't broken. It was *incomplete*. There's a difference." I got the last shutter loose and light spilled into the main room, catching dust in the air like it was reluctant to settle anywhere either. "Also, we're alive, so history has ruled in my favor."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh but had been strangled somewhere on its way out. I'd noticed he did that. Laughed halfway and then remembered something that stopped him. I wondered what the something was. I'd find out tonight, though I didn't know it yet.

Inside, the trading post was a museum of somebody's ordinary life. A counter with a brass bell still on it, green with age. Shelves with nothing left but tins too dented to steal and jars of something that had once been preserves and was now geology. A woodstove. A back room with a cot, a table, and a window looking out over the water.

"Defensible," Bram said, doing his slow circuit of the room, touching things the way a doctor touches a patient. "One road in, one road out, sightlines to the river. Nobody's been here in years." He set down the case holding my father's journals—the cores were wrapped separately, padded in his spare clothes, because apparently even fugitives pack sensibly—and looked at me. "We rest here. One night. Then we move at first light."

"One night," I agreed, because arguing took energy I wanted to save for what I'd actually come here to do.

Because I hadn't told him yet. That among my father's things, tucked inside the false bottom of his instrument case where he thought I'd never look—where he *hoped* I'd never look, maybe, or maybe where he hoped someday I would—there was a bundle of letters. And one of them had a Syndicate seal on it.

I waited until Bram went out to check the perimeter, which he did every forty minutes like clockwork, like a man who had trained himself so thoroughly into caution that caution had become a form of sleep.

Then I sat at the dead man's table, in the dead town's dead store, and opened my dead father's letters.

* * *

The first one was dated eleven years ago. My handwriting-reading skills were excellent by then; Papa wrote field notes in a script like bird tracks, but letters home to Mama, before she died, in careful round print, like he was afraid emotion would smudge.

*Dearest Ingrid — The offer is real. More money than the university ever dreamed, and they want the delta surveyed properly, top to bottom. You'll say I've sold out. Maybe I have. But listen: nobody surveys the levees honestly because nobody pays them to. If I'm inside, I can make the data un-fakeable. Cores from every grid square, sealed, duplicated, witnessed. They think they're buying a rubber stamp. Let them think it.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the words kept rearranging themselves into sentences I didn't want.

My father had taken their money. Knowingly. Not tricked, not coerced, not desperate the way Bram was desperate. He had walked into the Vane Syndicate's offices with his hand out and a plan behind his back, and he had let me believe, all these years, that he was just a scientist who'd gotten a good contract.

There were more letters. Eleven years of them, one or two a season, never sent—or sent and returned, I couldn't tell. A ledger of optimism, each entry a little thinner than the last.

*Year three: The duplication is working. They've begun asking why I need witnesses. I told them vanity. They believed it. Vanity is the one sin they respect.*

*Year six: They've asked me to adjust two figures. Small ones. I refused. They smiled. I have learned to be afraid of their smiling.*

*Year nine: I have enough now. Enough sealed cores to bury them. But the courts—Ada, if you are reading this someday, forgive me—I checked. Quietly. Every judge in this jurisdiction owes them something. Every newspaper takes their advertising. I built a bomb and discovered there is nowhere legal to detonate it. So I will wait. I will keep surveying. And when the monsoon comes early, as it will, as the data says it must, the failure itself will be my testimony.*

*Year ten: I am tired. I am frightened. I am also, strangely, hopeful. Hope is a stubborn crop. It grows in worse soil than this.*

The last letter was dated four days before the camp went silent.

*If anything happens to me, the girl knows where everything is. She doesn't know she knows. She thinks she's just helping her old father sort samples. Forgive me that too. I wanted her safe more than I wanted her ready, and I was wrong, because those turned out to be the same thing.*

I put the letters down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

Then I cried. Not prettily. Ugly, honking, sixteen-year-old crying, the kind with hiccups, into a jar of prehistoric preserves because it was the nearest thing and I wasn't going to snot on my father's journals.

That was how Bram found me. He came through the door mid-perimeter-check, saw me, and froze with the particular helplessness of a man confronted by a crying teenager, which is to say he looked like he'd rather face the patrol boat again.

"My dad," I said, when I could talk. "He *knew*. He took their money on purpose. He was going to expose them from the inside." I shoved the letters across the table at him. "Eleven years, Bram. He played the long game and the game killed him and I didn't even know he was playing."

Bram picked up the letters. Read them slowly, top to bottom, while I watched his face do several complicated things.

"He sounds," Bram said finally, "like a man who bet everything on one hand and knew the odds."

"He sounds like a *liar*."

"He sounds like a liar who lied to keep you alive." Bram set the letters down. "Those aren't the same crime."

"You don't know that. You don't know what—" I stopped, because he'd flinched. Actually flinched, small, at the word *crime*, like a dog that's heard a specific whistle. And sitting there in the ruined trading post with the river muttering outside, I realized I had spent three days running beside a man whose entire biography was a locked drawer. "What did they have on you?"

He was quiet long enough that the stove ticked twice.

"Everything," he said. "That's sort of the point of debt."

* * *

He told it badly, the way people tell things they've rehearsed only to themselves. He'd owned a storm-chasing outfit, a real one, six trucks and a transmitter array and a crew that followed him across three states chasing supercells. He'd financed an expansion against the Syndicate's lending arm because they were the only ones who'd fund weather work—"nobody else thinks the sky is collateral," he said, with a ghost of that strangled laugh—and then the monsoon two seasons back had flattened his fleet, drowned his best truck, and put him under.

"They don't do bankruptcy," he said. "They do *arrangements*. Seven years of jobs. Ferrying, hauling, carrying things I didn't ask about. And the price of the arrangement is that you disappear. No name on paper, no calls home, no—" His jaw worked. "No sister at your mother's funeral. Because if they know where your people are, then your people are leverage, and the only way to protect them is to stop existing near them."

"That's horrible," I said.

"It's efficient," he said, which was worse. "I owed forty thousand when my mother died. I owed thirty-one when my sister stopped writing. You do the math on what staying away costs versus what coming home costs, and you stay away. Every year. Until the math is just a habit." He looked at his hands. "I used to chase storms because I loved it. Now I chase them because the sky's the only thing left that doesn't bill me."

The stove ticked. Outside, the river kept saying whatever rivers say, patient and repetitive, like a lawyer reading terms.

"So what happens," I said, "when the debt's paid? Do they just... let you go?"

"Nobody's ever gotten there." He said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the weather. "The arrangement renews. There's always another job. That's not a flaw in the system, Ada. That's the product. The debt is the product. We're the crop."

I thought about my father's letters again. *Hope is a stubborn crop.* Two farmers, then, my father and this man, planting hope in soil owned by other people.

"Papa thought he could beat them from inside," I said slowly. "Honest data, sealed and witnessed, waiting for its moment. And it didn't work. The courts are bought, the papers are bought. He died waiting for a moment that—" My voice cracked and I hated it. "And you hid until hiding ate your whole life. And neither of you beat them. So tell me why I shouldn't take these cores and sink them in the river and just *live*, somewhere far away, where nobody smiles at anybody."

Bram was quiet again. But it was a different quiet now—not the locked-drawer quiet. Something opening.

"Because your father was right about the data," he said. "Sealed, duplicated, witnessed—that's real. That part worked. What failed was the delivery. He was trying to hand the truth to institutions, and the institutions are the Syndicate wearing different coats." He tapped the journal case. "Truth doesn't need a courthouse, kid. It needs ears. Thousands of them. All at once."

"Like a broadcast."

Something moved across his face—grief and want tangled together, the look of a man hearing an old song he'd sworn off.

"Like a broadcast," he said carefully. "But that's a conversation for a night when we're not both bleeding and exhausted. Sleep. I'll take first watch."

"There's no watch needed. We lost them at the rapids."

"We lost the *boat we saw* at the rapids." He banked the stove. "Sable doesn't lose people. He loses time. Sleep, Ada."

* * *

I didn't sleep, not really. I lay on the cot in the back room listening to the building settle and the river run, and around midnight I heard Bram moving in the main room, and found him at the table with my father's letters spread out, reading them again by lamplight, his lips moving slightly like a man memorizing scripture.

"She forgave him, you know," I said from the doorway. "In the letters. Mama. Before she died. There's one where he tells her everything and she writes back—well, he copied her reply. It says, *You lied to me for six years and I have decided to be furious about it forever, starting Tuesday. Come home.*"

Bram laughed—really laughed, all the way through, for the first time since I'd met him. It changed his whole face. It made him look like someone who had once been young.

"Tuesday," he said. "That's fair. Forgiveness should cost at least a little."

"Yours might cost more," I said. "When you finally go home. If your sister's anything like me, she'll be furious forever, starting Tuesday."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something. Watched the math happen behind his eyes—the old math, the stay-away math—and then, visibly, deliberately, watched him refuse it.

"When this is done," he said, "if we're alive, I'm going home. Whatever it costs."

"Good." I sat down across from him and pulled the journals toward us, both of us, together. "Because I've been thinking. Papa waited eleven years for a moment. And you said truth needs thousands of ears, all at once." I tapped the cover of the journal, on the page where he'd written the monsoon projections, the dates circled twice. "Well. According to this, the moment is about fourteen days out. The storm's coming whether we're ready or not."

Bram looked down at the circled date. Then up at me. And I saw it land on him—the same thing that had landed on me an hour ago, ugly-crying into a jar of ancient preserves.

The inheritance wasn't the data. It never had been. It was the deadline.

"Fourteen days," he said quietly. "And one transmitter, if the rig survived the rapids."

Outside, far upstream, thunder grumbled—a dry, testing sort of thunder, like a throat clearing before a speech.

Neither of us said anything else. We didn't need to. We just started sorting the journals by date, side by side, in a dead man's store, planning a resurrection.
