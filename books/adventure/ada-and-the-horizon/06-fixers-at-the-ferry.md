## 6. Fixers at the Ferry

The ferry crossing at Kettle Bend was the kind of place that existed only because two banks of a river had never learned to be neighbors. A rusted cable strung bank to bank, a flat barge big enough for one truck or three mules or, in our case, one battered river skiff lashed to a pallet of shame. The ferryman was a woman named Odette who charged by the pound and didn't ask questions, which made her my favorite kind of businessperson.

"Boat and all?" she said, eyeing the skiff. "That's extra."

"The boat's not staying," I said. "We're just crossing with it."

"On my ferry." She looked at me the way a priest looks at a man who wants to baptize his own baby in her font. "With your boat. On my ferry."

"It's been a strange week."

Ada stood beside me with her father's journals wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across her chest like a bandolier, which is exactly how you'd want a sixteen-year-old to look if you were trying to convince a ferrywoman everything was normal. It was not convincing anyone.

Odette shrugged, named a price that would have made a highwayman blush, and I paid it, because the alternative was running the cable crossing ourselves, and I'd seen what the current did to the pilings downstream. Some humiliations are cheaper than drowning.

We were halfway across—midriver, committed, the cable groaning overhead like an old man climbing stairs—when I saw the trucks.

Two of them, parked under the dead fig tree on the far bank. Gray, unmarked, clean enough that they'd washed off the road dust before arriving, which told me everything. Men who wash their trucks are men who plan to be seen. Four figures leaned against the near one, not moving, not waving, just *waiting*, with the patience of people being paid by the hour rather than by the result.

"That them?" Ada asked quietly.

"Could be fishermen."

"They're wearing suits."

"Very serious fishermen."

The lead figure pushed off the fender and walked down to the water's edge as we approached. He was tall and narrow as a fence post, dressed in a linen suit the color of oatmeal, spotless, which on a riverbank is less a fashion choice than a threat display. His hair was silvered at the temples. His hands were folded in front of him like a man waiting for a train he knew would be late but had already forgiven.

I knew him before he said a word. Everyone in certain circles knew Mr. Sable, or knew of him, the way you know of weather systems. You don't meet Sable. Sable *occurs*.

"Bram Blackwood," he called pleasantly, before I'd even said hello. "Well. Seven years, and here you are, riding a ferry with a stolen boat and a child carrying what looks like a very heavy book. The world does keep its little jokes."

My stomach performed a maneuver I last felt during a tornado intercept gone wrong. Not fear, exactly. More like the moment you realize the storm has turned toward you specifically.

"You have the wrong man," I said. "Name's Cobb. Boat pilot. Hauling supplies."

"Of course you are." Sable smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who had read your file, your dental records, and probably your childhood diary. "Mr. Cobb. May I call you Mr. Cobb? I find it helps the fiction if we both commit."

* * *

He came aboard when we docked—stepped over the gunwale without asking, his shoes somehow avoiding the fish guts and bilge water as though the boat had rearranged itself out of courtesy. Two of his men stayed on the bank. One wandered, casual as Sunday, to stand behind Ada. I catalogued that and filed it under *later problems*.

Sable looked around the skiff with genuine interest. Tapped the fuel tank with one polished shoe. Examined the muffled engine housing.

"Clever," he said. "The exhaust baffling. Very clever. You built this yourself?"

"I bought it from a man who owed me money."

"Everyone owes you money, Mr. Blackwood. That's rather your signature." He turned, and for the first time looked directly at me, and his eyes were pale and patient and absolutely empty of anything except attention. "You've done well, staying hidden. Genuinely. Most men who cross the Syndicate last eleven months. You lasted seven years. There are people who speak of you the way sailors speak of the sea—the thing that took everyone, eventually, but you can't help admiring it."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No. Of course." He produced a handkerchief, unfolded it slowly, refolded it. "Let me lay out the arithmetic, because I respect arithmetic and I suspect you do too. Dr. Halvorsen is dead. Tragic. A man who took our funding and then, I'm told, developed a conscience somewhere around core sample forty. Consciences are expensive, Mr. Blackwood. Someone always pays."

"He died of fever," Ada said.

Her voice was steady. Her hands, wrapped around the oilcloth bundle, were white-knuckled. Sable turned to her with the same pleasant attention he'd given the engine.

"And you must be the daughter. Ada, isn't it? Your father spoke of you. Well—he spoke of his work, mostly, but you were in there between the sediment layers." He inclined his head, almost courtly. "You have my condolences. Truly. He was a brilliant man. Brilliance plus conscience is how most martyrs get made."

"He wasn't a martyr," Ada said. "He was right."

"Yes," Sable said softly. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I want it on the record that I did the math faster than I've ever done anything in my life.

Sable wanted the cores. He wanted the journals. He wanted this whole week to have never happened, and he was prepared to be extremely reasonable about it, because Sable's reputation rested on reasonableness the way a spider's rests on silk.

And I had one asset left. Not the boat. Not the flare gun Ada kept in her waistband, which he almost certainly knew about. My asset was that I was nobody. Seven years of nobody. No name on any lease, no face in any database that mattered, no thread you could pull without unraveling into nothing. The Syndicate couldn't kill a rumor efficiently. They needed a man.

So I gave him one.

"The boat's yours," I said. "Engine, hull, the baffling you admire so much. And my silence—I'll go back to being no one, you'll never see me again, and the girl goes home to an aunt in Marlow who's expecting her. That's the trade."

Sable considered this with his head tilted, like a man listening to a symphony for the second time to catch the parts he missed.

"The boat," he repeated. "You're offering me a boat."

"And seven years of quiet."

"Mr. Blackwood." He sighed, and it was almost fond. "Do you know why I remember you? Not the debt—that's a ledger entry, ledgers forget. I remember because seven years ago you stood in a room very much like this conversation and you lied to Vane himself, to his face, magnificently, about a cargo manifest, and you did it to protect a family that wasn't yours. And then you vanished instead of doubling down, which showed *sense*, which is rarer than courage." He spread his hands. "I have admired you for years. It's why I came personally. You don't trade boats. You've never once in your life traded the thing you're actually holding."

The rain started then. First fat drops, then the sky simply opened, monsoon-style, the whole delta exhaling at once. On the far ridge, thunder rolled like furniture being moved upstairs.

"There is no aunt in Marlow," Sable continued, over the drumming on the canopy. "There is no version of this where you two walk away and the data stays quiet, because the data doesn't stay quiet—it *floods*. So here is my counteroffer, offered with real regret: give me the journals, and the girl lives, and you live, and I will even let you keep the boat, because I'll be honest, Mr. Blackwood, the boat was never the point."

Behind him, the man near Ada shifted his weight forward half an inch.

And Ada—who had spent nine days alone with a corpse and a flare gun, who had buried her father with river stones one handful at a time—looked at me. Not scared. *Asking.* Like a co-pilot checking in before a turn.

I'd spent seven years making sure no one depended on me, because depending gets people killed and being depended on gets them killed faster. But she was asking anyway, and some doors, once knocked on, won't stay shut.

"Now," I said.

She fired the flare gun into the fuel-soaked rag she'd spent the ferry crossing pretending was part of the bundle—because I'd taught her that trick four days ago and she'd improved on it, God help me—and the far end of the skiff went up orange and roaring, and every Syndicate man on that deck became briefly, beautifully stupid, because fire makes everyone stupid, even professionals.

I shoved Sable into the river. I want that noted too: I put both hands on the chest of the most dangerous man on the river and I pushed, and he went over the side with a sound of genuine surprise, which I intend to treasure for the rest of whatever life I have left.

Then Ada and I went off the other side, into the brown water, into the rain, and swam for the reeds while the skiff burned like a small angry sun behind us.

* * *

The wetlands took us the way wetlands take everything—slowly, thoroughly, and with mud involved. We waded and stumbled through cattail stands and black water up to our ribs, the rain hammering down, until the firelight and the shouting faded behind us and there was nothing but storm and swamp and the two of us breathing like bellows.

We flopped onto a hummock of higher ground under a leaning willow, and for a long time neither of us said anything. The journals were still strapped to Ada's chest, dry under the oilcloth. She checked them twice, then a third time, then let herself shake.

"They'll come after us now," she said finally. "Really come."

"Yeah."

"You pushed him in the river."

"I did."

A long pause. Then, muffled, half-laughing, half-crying: "Was that in the plan?"

"The plan was the fire. Pushing Sable was improvisation."

"Good improvisation."

"Thank you. I've been saving it for years."

She got the giggles then, the awful adrenaline kind, hunched over with her arms around the journals while the monsoon tried to drown the entire world, and I sat there getting soaked and found myself laughing too, low and helpless, because the alternative was thinking clearly, and clear thinking could wait until morning.

But here's the thing I wouldn't say out loud, the thing sitting in my chest like ballast: Sable knew my name. Knew my face, my history, my sins, and now he knew I'd chosen the girl and the truth over the deal. Seven years of invisibility, spent in one afternoon on a ferry deck. There was no going back to being Cobb, no more quiet jobs and borrowed names. Every door in the delta that the Syndicate owned—and that was most of them—was now closed to me, and worse, they'd be watching the ones still open.

Which meant no courts, no magistrates, no newspaper offices on the Syndicate's leash. Which meant if those survey findings were ever going to reach anyone, we'd need a way to shout past all of them at once.

I looked east, where the storm was thickest, and thought about the old transmitter rig in storage outside Harrow Junction, the one I'd sworn I'd never plug in again.

Lightning lit the ridgeline, and beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, came a sound I knew too well: engines. More than two. Coming along the levee road.

Ada heard it too. She stopped laughing.

"How far to Harrow Junction?" she asked.

"In this flood? Two days' walk."

"Then we'd better start."
