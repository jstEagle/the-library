## 7. Monsoon Season

The rain began at dawn, and it did not begin politely.

It came the way my father had always described it to me when I was small enough to be soothed by such things — not as weather but as a decision, a vast gray animal that had been circling the coast for weeks and finally chose to sit down on top of us. One moment the wetlands were breathing their usual damp breath; the next, the sky simply opened its hand.

Ada Halvorsen, I told myself, you wanted proof. Here is your proof, arriving at approximately four hundred millimeters per hour.

Bram stood under the lean-to of the fisherman's hut we'd borrowed for the night, watching the treeline dissolve into gray. He had his arms crossed and an expression I was beginning to catalog the way other people cataloged birds: *the skeptical heron*, *the resigned ox*, and now this one — *the man doing arithmetic he doesn't like*.

"Early," he said.

"Eleven days early." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "My father's models put landfall at the twenty-third. It's the twelfth."

"Your father's models also said the levees would fail."

I didn't answer. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't come out as either a sob or a scream, and I'd used up my ration of both somewhere around the trading post, reading his letters by candlelight. Grief, I was learning, didn't leave you. It just changed jobs. For three weeks it had been a librarian — quiet, archival, dusting the shelves of everything I remembered about him. Now it was being reassigned as a drill sergeant.

*Move, Halvorsen,* it said. *People are going to die if you don't move.*

* * *

We walked out of the hut at first light, or what passed for first light under a sky the color of wet slate. The wetlands had already begun to drink. What had been sedge meadow yesterday was shin-deep water today, and the water had opinions — it pulled sideways, it found every hole in the ground and filled it before you could test it with your boot.

Bram went first, because he was heavier and because he insisted that drowning was "a two-person job only if done inefficiently." He carried the cores in a waterproof duffel strapped across his chest like a bandolier, which gave him the look of a very tired revolutionary. I carried the journals in a dry bag inside my coat, against my ribs, where I could feel them with every step. My father weighed about four kilos now, paper and ink and handwriting that slanted downhill when he was tired.

"You're limping," Bram said over his shoulder.

"I'm *pacing myself.*"

"You're limping *with ambition*. Sit on the log."

There was a log. There were, in fact, forty logs, all of them apparently competing for the title of Most Deceptively Rotten. I sat on the one he chose because he had a talent for choosing things that held — boats, silences, grudges, logs. I peeled off my boot and looked at the blister with the weary respect it deserved.

He crouched beside me, dug a strip of tape out of some pocket I had stopped trying to map, and taped my heel without asking. His hands were steady. Everything else about him was a controlled demolition, but his hands were steady.

"My mother did that," I said. "Taped blisters. Before she left."

Something moved behind his eyes — I saw it go past, like a fish under ice. He didn't tell me about her. He hadn't told me much, only the shape of it: seven years invisible, a debt that grew interest like mold, everyone he loved either gone or safer for his absence. I knew better than to ask for the names. Names were load-bearing walls in Bram Blackwood; you didn't knock on them.

"Your mother had good instincts," he said instead, and stood up, and offered me his hand, and we kept walking.

* * *

By midday we lost the path entirely, which is to say the path lost itself, which is to say the river came to collect the lowlands early, ahead of schedule, like a creditor. We waded thigh-deep through what my father's charts — God bless him, God keep him, wherever stones and rivers keep him — marked as *seasonal marsh, passable in dry months.*

The dry month had ended eleven days ago.

"We should have taken the ridge line," Bram muttered.

"The ridge line is Syndicate patrol territory."

"The ridge line is *dry* Syndicate patrol territory."

"Bram." I stopped, and the water pushed gently at my legs as if nudging me along. "Do you want to argue with the charts, or do you want to argue with me? Because one of us drew them and one of us has been reading them upside down since Tuesday."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he laughed — a short, surprised bark, the kind of laugh a man lets out when he's forgotten he owns one. "Upside down once. Once."

"It was a very formative once."

We cut east toward higher ground, following a string of termite mounds like stepping-stones across a drowned world. And that was when I saw it.

* * *

The ridge was maybe six kilometers north. In clear weather you could see the whole spine of it, dark green against the sky, stitched with the pale seam of the Northbank Levee — Levee Seven, the pride of the Vane contracts, the one my father called *"the optimist"* in his notes, because it was built on hope and compacted lies.

The rain had thinned to a hard, needling drizzle, and the light had gone strange and yellowish, the way it does when the atmosphere is deciding something. I stopped on the termite mound and looked north, and Bram, who missed nothing even when pretending to miss everything, stopped too.

"What?" he said.

"Wait."

The thunder was coming from the wrong direction — from the north, low and continuous, not the sharp crack-and-echo of storm thunder but something longer, deeper, a sound with weight and intent. A sound like the earth clearing its throat.

Then the ridge seemed to shrug.

That's the only word I have. A section of the levee — half a kilometer of it, maybe more, a pale seam suddenly interrupted — sagged, and then slid, and then was simply *gone*, dissolved into the brown flood pouring through the gap like tea through a torn bag. Even at six kilometers I could hear it: not the thunder anymore but a roar, broad and flat, the sound of a river remembering it was a river.

The water would take perhaps an hour to reach the lowland villages. Less, if the channels funneled it. Harrow Bend was closest. Harrow Bend had maybe nine hundred people, a church, a school, and a levee sign that said PROTECTED BY VANE HYDROLOGICAL WORKS.

I had read my father's prediction of this exact failure so many times I knew the page number. *Levee Seven will fail at the oxbow cut during sustained inflow exceeding seasonal mean. Failure will be sudden and total. The core samples show uncompacted fill below the two-meter mark. They built it on sand and signed their names to it.*

Validation, I thought, and the word tasted like ash and iron. This was what he had died carrying. Not a theory. A *schedule*.

I sat down on the termite mound in the rain and, to my own surprise, did not cry. Something in me had passed through crying the way water passes through cloth, and on the far side of it there was just this: cold clarity, and my father's voice in my head, calm as ever, saying what he always said when a model confirmed —

*"Well, Ada. Now we know we're right. Now the work starts."*

Bram crouched next to me. He didn't touch my shoulder, which I appreciated; he understood that some griefs are load-bearing and you don't lean on them.

"That's Seven," he said quietly. "That's the one."

"That's the one." I wiped my face, though whether from rain or anything else was between me and the weather. "And there are fourteen more, Bram. Fourteen more levees, and the monsoon hasn't even finished landing yet. Harrow Bend. Millbrook. All the delta towns. Thousands of people who think they're protected."

The roar from the ridge went on and on, patient as arithmetic.

"So we don't stop," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, and thought: *there you are, Papa. There's your stubbornness, working after all.* "We get to the junction. We get to whatever passes for authority there, and if nothing does —"

"Something will," Bram said. But he said it strangely, looking south toward the town we were walking toward, and there was something in his face I couldn't read yet. Doubt, maybe. Or the beginning of a plan he hadn't told me about.

* * *

We made the last kilometers in failing light, wading now almost to the waist, the journals riding high against my chest like a second heartbeat. Twice Bram stumbled and twice I caught him, and the second time he let me, which I understood was its own kind of trust — the kind a man like him paid out in grams.

The rail junction town showed itself as a scatter of lights through the rain, low and orange, hunched under the weather like animals under a hedge. Smoke from chimneys. A train whistle, distant and mournful, hauling something north ahead of the water.

"Harrow Bend's evacuation horn should be sounding," I said. "If anyone down there believes the levee failed."

"They'll believe it," Bram said grimly, "when the water arrives. People always believe the water."

Ahead of us, the lights of the town flickered — once, twice — and steadied. Somewhere in that town was a magistrate, a telegraph office, a newspaper, a sheriff, a whole architecture of people whose job it was to hear exactly what I was carrying and do something about it.

Somewhere in that town, I suspected, was Mr. Sable, or men like him, doing the same arithmetic we were.

Bram paused at the last rise, water streaming off his jaw, and looked at the town the way he looked at storms — measuring, respectful, already calculating where the danger would come from.

"Ada," he said. "When we get down there — let me talk first."

"Why?"

"Because I know how doors close in towns like this." He started walking again, into the rain, into the lights. "And because I've spent seven years learning which ones lock from the outside."

I followed him down the slope, my father's work pressed against my heart, the flood roaring behind us like applause, and I wondered — with the first small, cold prickle of real fear — what kind of door Bram Blackwood was planning to open, and what he intended to do if every one of them was already bought.
