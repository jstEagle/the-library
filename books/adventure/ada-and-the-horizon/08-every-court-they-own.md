## 8. Every Court They Own

The town of Corrick's Landing announced itself the way a tired dog announces visitors: one bark, then a long sigh. A water tower leaning like a drunk against the sky. A rail junction where three lines met and two of them still ran. A main street of brick facades softened by damp, awnings faded to the color of weak tea.

Bram walked into it wearing three days of wetlands mud and carrying a waterproof satchel that weighed less than a loaf of bread and more than every debt he'd ever owed.

Ada walked beside him, hood up, her father's journals distributed between their jackets so that no single drowning or mugging could take them all. She'd insisted on that division back at the trading post, laying out the pages on a dry shelf like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"If they take half," she'd said, "you'll still have enough to hang them."

"They can't hang a man for possessing paper."

"No," she'd agreed. "But they can drown a delta."

He was learning not to argue with her when she was right. It saved time.

* * *

Marta Reyes had been the best investigative journalist on the river coast until the Vane Syndicate bought her newspaper's parent company, its printing plant, and — rumor said — her editor's mortgage. Bram had known her in another life, seven years and forty pounds ago, when he'd still had a name that appeared in print. She'd once called him "the only storm chaser who files copy worth reading." He'd once fixed her truck's alternator in a Walmart parking lot during a hailstorm. Friendship, river-style: transactional on the surface, load-bearing underneath.

Her office above the feed store smelled of cold coffee and old ink. When Bram knocked, she opened the door on the chain, saw him, and went very still.

"Dead men don't knock," she said.

"This one's been practicing."

She let him in, relocked the door, and listened — really listened, chin propped on her fist, while he laid it out. The Halvorsen survey. The sediment cores. The falsified levee data, the monsoon coming early, the delta towns sitting behind walls built to fail. Ada sat beside him with her hands folded, contributing corrections with the precision of someone who'd proofread the original.

When he finished, Marta didn't speak for a long moment. Then she stood, went to the window, and looked down at the street as if checking for something.

"You know what happened to Osei," she said, not turning around. "The reporter who broke the dredging story in March?"

"I heard he took a job overseas."

"He took a job overseas the way you take a job overseas when there's a man standing behind your chair while you write your resignation." She turned. Her face had aged seven years too, but differently than his — hers had gone careful. "Bram. I believe you. That's the worst part. I believe every word, and I can't print it. Legal review would sit on it for six weeks. Six weeks is the monsoon. And even if it ran—" she spread her hands— "who owns the presses? Who owns the courts? You'd be suing a man who owns the courtroom."

"There are other papers."

"There are other papers owned by the same three holding companies." She came back to the desk and gripped his shoulder, hard, an anchor's grip. "Get the data somewhere they can't buy the pipe. Not me. Not yet. I'm sorry. I'm sorrier than you know."

On the stairs going down, Ada said nothing for half a block. Then: "She cried after we left. I heard it through the floor."

"I know."

"That makes it worse, somehow."

"Yeah," Bram said. "It does."

* * *

Magistrate Odell Fenwick received petitioners in a chamber that had once been a bank lobby, which explained the marble and the vault door now repurposed as a records room. He was a small man with a large mustache and the wary eyes of someone who'd learned that every visitor came with a bill attached.

Bram laid it out again — cleaner this time, rehearsed on the walk over. Fraud. Endangerment. A request for an emergency injunction on Syndicate flood contracts pending independent review of the cores.

Fenwick listened with his fingers steepled. When Bram finished, the magistrate was quiet long enough that Ada shifted in her seat.

"Mr. Ashgrove," Fenwick said at last, and Bram's stomach dropped, because he hadn't given that name to anyone in this town. "Do you know how many petitions cross my bench in a season? Four hundred. Do you know how many concern the Vane Syndicate? Eleven last year alone. Do you know what became of them?"

"Dismissed."

"With prejudice, mostly. One petitioner relocated. Permanently." He unfolded his hands. "I will tell you something off the record, because you strike me as a man who appreciates the difference between law and weather. The Syndicate's counsel contributed to the restoration fund for this courthouse. My clerk's daughter attends a school their foundation built. My wife's brother works their docks. Now — none of that touches my judgment. But it touches everything else. My docket. My security detail. The speed at which my rulings reach appeal. Justice here isn't corrupt, Mr. Ashgrove. It's *furnished*. And furnished rooms belong to whoever holds the lease."

"So the answer's no."

"The answer is that if you file, I must rule against you, publicly, and then report your presence to the authorities, who report to people you're hiding from." His mustache twitched — the closest thing to compassion the room allowed. "Walk away. Some storms you don't chase. You let them pass over."

Ada stood up before Bram could stop her.

"My father tried walking away," she said. "He took their money and told himself he'd fix it from inside. He's under a cairn of stones nine days now. You want to know what passes over? Everything passes over. That's the problem."

Fenwick looked at her for a long time. Something moved behind the wariness — regret, maybe, or memory.

"Go home, child," he said softly. "Both of you. Before the rain."

Outside, Ada kicked a loose cobblestone halfway down the street. Bram let her. It seemed therapeutic.

* * *

The rival contractor was named Hesper Quill, and her office was the nicest room they'd entered in a week: actual carpet, actual windows, a scale model of a levee system on a table like a wedding cake made of concrete. She was silver-haired, sharp-eyed, and had spent twenty years losing bids to Vane Consolidated, which made her either the perfect ally or the perfect trap.

She heard them out over tea she poured herself, no staff present, which Bram noted and appreciated.

"Halvorsen," she said, turning her cup. "Good man. Terrible negotiator. He lowballed us both on the Kestrel Reach survey years ago and won it anyway." She set the cup down. "Show me the cores."

Ada produced the journal pages — copies; the originals stayed hidden — and Quill read them the way a jeweler reads a stone. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Her face did several small things.

"This is real," she said finally. "This is catastrophic. This is—" She stopped. Looked up. And Bram watched the arithmetic finish behind her eyes.

"This is leverage," Ada said flatly.

Quill had the grace to wince. "Miss Halvorsen. If I take this to the licensing board, I get a hearing in eleven months. If I take it to the press, see above. But if I take it to Vane and offer to bury it in exchange for the coastal contracts—" 

"—you win everything you've lost for twenty years," Bram said.

"And the delta?" Ada's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. It got quieter. "The towns. The four thousand people behind those levees. What do they get?"

Quill closed the journal gently, like closing a casket. "A better contractor, eventually. Me, after I'm rich enough to be untouchable. Sometimes the only way across a flooded river is stepping on things that float." She stood, extended her hand to Bram. "I'm sorry. Genuinely. Take the pages. Don't come back."

They didn't shake it. On the way out, Ada said, "Everyone keeps telling us to go home."

"Everyone keeps telling us to go home," Bram agreed, "and nobody can tell us where that is anymore."

* * *

Sable found them at dusk, because of course he did. He was seated on the porch rail of the boarding house where they'd taken a room under false names, hat brim dripping, hands folded, patient as a spider who'd already counted the flies.

"Mr. Ashgrove." The courtesy never varied. "You've had a busy day. The magistrate sends his regards — well, he sends his report. Miss Reyes sent nothing, which was itself informative. And Ms. Quill—" a thin smile— "will be hearing from us about her discretion. Or lack of it."

Bram put himself between Sable and the boarding house door. Behind him, upstairs, Ada would already have the window open and the escape route mapped. He'd taught her that much. Or she'd taught him.

"Here's my offer," Sable said. "It comes once. Your debt — all of it, seven years of it — voided. Your name restored. Your boat replaced with a better one. A clean slate, Mr. Ashgrove, in exchange for the cores and the girl's silence. She keeps her father's memories. She loses the paperwork. Everyone walks away dry."

"And the delta."

"The delta," Sable said, with the mild tone of a man discussing drainage, "is a matter of actuarial reality. Walls fail. Towns rebuild. Insurance adjusts. The Syndicate has survived worse PR than water." He tilted his head. "You were invisible for seven years, Mr. Ashgrove. Comfortable, wasn't it? No debts but money. No name but an alias. I'm offering you that life back. All you have to do is stay invisible a little longer — long enough for a few thousand people you'll never meet to learn what a failed levee sounds like."

Bram looked at him. Really looked. And what he felt wasn't fear, or temptation, or even anger. It was clarity — arriving cold and complete, like the first drop before a downpour.

Seven years ago he'd gone invisible to hide from what he owed. He'd told himself it was survival. It had cost him everyone: Marta, the old crew, the man he used to be. And today he'd watched every legal door in the town swing shut — not broken, not barred, just *owned*, polished and locked and furnished by the same landlord. The law wasn't failing them. The law was working exactly as purchased.

There was no court left to file in.

So you don't file. You broadcast.

"Sable," Bram said, "do you know what I did before I owed you money?"

"Storm chasing. A hobbyist's vanity, I understand."

"A transmitter rig," Bram said. "Forty-mile range, emergency band capable, mounted on a truck I still own, parked two streets from here." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, like a joint unused for years. "You should have asked what I owed *besides* money."

Sable's smile thinned by a degree. "That would be a criminal act, Mr. Ashgrove."

"So was the survey," Bram said. "Goodnight."

He climbed the stairs two at a time. Ada had the window open, the satchel packed, and the journals divided, and she was looking at him with an expression he was beginning to recognize as her version of hope — narrow, fierce, unsentimental.

"Well?" she said.

Bram pulled his old keys from his jacket — the ones he'd carried seven years without using, rust-freckled, warm from his body. The truck keys. The rig keys.

"It's going to rain tomorrow," he said. "Let's give them something to talk about on every band in the delta."

Below them, on the street, Sable touched his hat brim — courteous to the last — and began giving quiet orders to men who melted out of the dark like weather coming in.
