## 9. Broadcast

The water tower at Corrin Junction had been built by optimistic men in a wetter decade, and it groaned like an old ship now that the monsoon had come to collect on its patience. Bram stood at the base of it in the hammering rain, looking up eighty feet of riveted iron at the little catwalk where his whole life was about to stop being invisible.

"Tell me again why we're doing this here," Ada shouted over the wind. She had her father's field satchel strapped across her chest like armor, and her hair was plastered flat against her skull, and she looked—Bram thought with a pang that had no business showing up at a moment like this—exactly like Dr. Halvorsen did in the one photograph she'd shown him. Same stubborn chin. Same eyes that treated weather as a personal opponent.

"Because," Bram said, "the tower's the highest metal for forty miles, and my rig's transmitter needs height the way a rumor needs a gossipy town. Because every emergency band in the delta relays off the junction repeater when the storm knocks out the low stations—which it just did." He patted the battered aluminum case slung on his shoulder, the storm-chasing rig he'd spent seven years and too much money keeping alive. "And because Sable's men are ten minutes behind us, which means we're doing it *now*."

"Good summary," Ada said. "Very organized. My father would have liked you."

"He'd have hated me. I'm the kind of man who takes money first and thinks later."

"He took money too," Ada said simply. "He'd have understood you fine."

* * *

The ladder was wet iron and bad news. Bram went up first with the rig case strapped to his back, feeling every rung try to leave the tower and take him with it. The rain came sideways now, warm as bathwater and twice as insistent, and somewhere south of them the sky had gone the color of a bruise going green—the particular shade that meant the storm had stopped being weather and started being a decision.

At the catwalk he hauled himself over the rail and knelt, clipping the rig to the tower's lightning ground—a thing he'd have called insane any other day of his life, but tonight the tower's own grounding was the only friend his equipment had. He unfolded the whip antenna, wired it into the repeater junction box with hands that shook from cold or fear or possibly both, and keyed the mic.

Static. Then, faintly, impossibly, the low murmur of a dozen bands at once—the whole frightened delta talking to itself in the dark.

"Ada!" he called down. "How's our door?"

She'd posted herself at the ladder's foot with the flare gun in one hand and a boat hook in the other, standing in the flooded yard like the world's smallest, angriest lighthouse keeper. "Still a ladder," she shouted back. "Come down and see!"

"That's the spirit. If anyone climbs it who isn't me, set them on fire."

"I was planning to aim for the eyebrows."

He almost laughed. Then he pressed transmit, and there was nothing funny left in him anywhere.

"This is an emergency broadcast on all bands," he said. "My name is Bram Blackwood. Seven years ago I made myself nobody so the Vane Syndicate couldn't find me, and tonight I'm done with that. Listen, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to keep saying it until someone makes me stop."

* * *

He read the data the way Dr. Halvorsen would have wanted it read: plainly, without poetry, because the numbers were terrible enough on their own.

Sediment core after sediment core. Levee section after levee section. The Halvorsen survey had drilled thirty-one sites along the lower delta, and at twenty-six of them the soil beneath the flood-control berms was river silt and old sand—not the packed clay the Syndicate's certified surveys claimed. Falsified bore logs. Stamped approvals for tests that had never been run. Contracts signed against data invented in an office in Vane City by men who had never gotten mud on their shoes.

"The levees will not hold the monsoon," Bram said, and the words came out of him steady, which surprised him, because inside he was screaming. "Not some of them. Not eventually. Tonight, tomorrow, within days. If you live behind a Vane-built levee in the lower delta, get your family out now. Go east, go high, go to the rail line, go anywhere that isn't behind their concrete. Don't wait for an official order. There will be no official order. The people who could give it are the people who buried this report."

Somewhere below him, Ada fired the flare gun. Not at anyone—at the sky, a red star climbing through the rain, and Bram understood: she'd seen lights coming up the ferry road. Three vehicles, maybe four.

"How long?" he called down.

"Long enough if you hurry," she called up. "They're walking the last stretch. Sable doesn't run in the rain; he strolls."

"Charming man."

"Bram." Her voice came up through the water-noise, smaller suddenly. "Read my father's name. At the end. Please. He should be on it."

"I was always going to put him on it," Bram said. "Both of you. Now get ready."

* * *

The fixers came up the ladder the way Sable's men did everything: competently, unhurried, in good boots.

Bram heard the first flare go off with a sound like the sky tearing, heard shouting, heard the flat crack of a pistol answering—and then he stopped listening, because listening was a luxury for men whose work didn't matter yet. He bent over the transmitter and kept reading. Cores eleven through nineteen. The grain-size analyses. The photographs of the doctored logs, described aloud in detail because the radio couldn't carry pictures but a thousand listening ears could carry memory.

Lightning hit something close—close enough that the whole tower rang like a struck bell and every hair on Bram's arms stood up under his soaked sleeves. The rig flickered, held, held. His grounding wire hissed steam.

"—repeat, the levee at Millbrook Bend is founded on uncompacted silt, repeat, uncompacted silt—"

A bullet spanged off the catwalk rail six inches from his knee. He flinched, then made himself un-flinch, then made himself keep reading, because that was the whole job now. That was the entire remaining architecture of Bram Blackwood: a man kneeling in a storm reading dead men's numbers into the dark while people shot at him.

And it was working. He could hear it working. The emergency bands were waking up—Corrin Junction acknowledging, Harrow Ferry acknowledging, a fishing cooperative on band nine asking him to repeat the Millbrook figure, a rail dispatcher cutting in with *we can move cars north, how many people, how many people*—

"As many as you've got room for," Bram said. "Start now."

Then, through the rain, from below: "BRAM! LADDER!"

He looked down. Two of Sable's men were on the rungs, climbing through the strobing red afterimage of Ada's second flare. And Ada herself was backing along the yard fence with an empty flare gun and a boat hook, and Mr. Sable was standing in the flooded gate, umbrellaless, immaculate even drowned, watching the tower with the mild interest of a man appraising property.

Bram did the arithmetic fast. He could stop broadcasting and fight, and lose, and the data would die with him. Or he could keep broadcasting and trust a sixteen-year-old with an empty gun.

It wasn't really arithmetic. It was faith. It had been faith since a girl met him in a silent camp with a flare gun and didn't shoot.

He kept reading.

* * *

What happened next, he pieced together afterward from what he saw and what Ada told him, and the two accounts never quite matched, which he suspected was her intention.

One of the climbers reached the catwalk. Bram swung the rig case at him—the transmitter, his beautiful irreplaceable transmitter, used as a club, forgive me—and caught him across the shoulders, and the man grabbed Bram instead of the tower, and for one long turning moment they were both weightless above the flooded yard.

Bram got a fist around the rail. The other man did not.

The second climber, hearing his colleague depart into history, decided the ladder offered poor career prospects and went back down.

Below, Ada had done something with the boat hook and the yard's old trip-wire gate latch that put one fixer face-down in three feet of floodwater, and Mr. Sable—Mr. Sable had simply stopped. Stopped advancing, stopped directing, stopped strolling. Because his men were pulling radios from their coats, and the radios were saying the same thing every radio in the delta was saying: *Vane-built levees will fail. Evacuate now.* And because across the river, on the far bank of the junction, the first whistle of the night train was sounding—not fleeing, loading. People were loading.

"You understand what you've done," Sable called up, and even through the rain his voice arrived intact, courteous as ever. "There is no court that won't hand you to me."

"There's a bigger court than yours now," Bram called back, and jabbed a thumb at the sky. "About four million members. They're all listening."

Something happened in Sable's face then that Bram had never seen in seven years of running from the man's organization: recalculation. Not fear exactly. The look of a man discovering the ledger he'd been keeping all his life had been written in the wrong currency.

A levee light on the southern ridge chose that moment to go dark. Then another. Then, distantly, a sound like the earth clearing its throat, and the sirens of Harrow Ferry began to wail—not the Syndicate's sirens, the towns' own, the ones the contracts said would never be needed.

Sable looked at the ridge. Looked at his men. Looked up at Bram one last time.

"We'll continue this conversation," he said, "when the water goes down."

"Bring a life jacket," Bram said. "I hear the surveys are unreliable."

Sable's mouth did something that in a better man might have been a smile, and then he was gone into the rain, and his men with him, because even vultures know when the weather owns the sky.

* * *

Bram broadcast until dawn, or until the thing that came instead of dawn—a gray-green churning light with rain falling through it like the sky had sprung a leak in every room. He read the cores until his voice cracked, then let the rig loop the recording, then read them again anyway, hoarse, because repetition was safety and safety was the point.

Ada climbed up at some point—he never learned how she'd talked her way past the ladder—and sat beside him on the catwalk with her father's satchel in her lap, and together they watched the southern ridgeline come apart in slow motion, exactly as the journals said it would, exactly to the week.

"They're getting out," she said quietly. Downriver, headlights strung along the east road like beads on a wire. The trains whistling north, loaded. Boats on the flooded flats, running lights bobbing. "Look at them. They're actually getting out."

"Yeah," Bram said. His voice was wrecked. He found he was crying, or the rain was, and he'd stopped keeping track. "Your father did that. You did that. I just read it out loud."

"You did the loud part," Ada agreed solemnly. "Historians will note the loud part."

He laughed—a broken, soaked, ridiculous sound—and the transmitter crackled beneath them, still carrying his voice out over the drowning delta, over the failing levees, over four million people waking to the truth:

*My name is Bram Blackwood. Listen, because I'm only going to say this once.*

Except he'd said it a hundred times now. And tomorrow, when the waters peaked and the counting began, everyone in the delta would know exactly who he was, where he was, and what he'd cost the Vane Syndicate.

Visible at last. Hunted forever. Worth it.

Ada touched his arm and pointed east, where the gray was splitting open around a thin gold seam—the sun, arriving late and unbothered, like a man who knew the meeting couldn't start without him.

"So," she said. "What happens to us now?"

Bram looked at the flooded road, the empty fuel gauge, the whole washed-out uncertain world—and grinned.

"Now," he said, "we learn to chase storms properly."
