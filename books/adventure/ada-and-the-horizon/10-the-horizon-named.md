## 10. The Horizon, Named

The first letter came on a Tuesday, which Ada decided was a suspiciously ordinary day for the world to start changing.

She was sitting on the porch of Bram's repaired rig—parked now in the yard of Mrs. Okonkwo's boarding house in Marsh Junction, where it had become something of a local landmark, like a lighthouse that had wandered inland—when the postman handed her an envelope with an official seal and her name spelled *Halvorsen-Ashgrove*.

"That's not my name," she told him.

"Tell that to the newspapers," he said, and rode off before she could argue.

Inside was a summons: the Delta Commission wanted her testimony. Not a request. A summons. She read it three times, waiting for the fear to arrive, and when it didn't, she understood that something in her had been permanently rearranged. There had been a time—not long ago, though it felt geological—when official paper meant men like Mr. Sable, meant doors closing, meant her father's work disappearing into a drawer. Now the paper meant they wanted to hear what she knew. All of it. Under oath.

She carried the envelope inside, where Bram was attempting to repair Mrs. Okonkwo's kitchen radio with a soldering iron and what appeared to be pure stubbornness.

"We've been summoned," she said.

"Both of us?"

"Both of us." She set the letter on the table between them. "They spelled your name right."

Bram looked at it for a long moment. Seven years of being nobody, and here it was in government ink: *Bramwell Ashgrove*. He touched the letters like a man checking whether a scar still hurt.

"Huh," he said, which from Bram was practically a speech.

* * *

The investigations bloomed the way mold blooms in the delta—everywhere at once, in colors no one expected.

The Commission first, then a federal audit, then two rival contractors who fell upon the Vane Syndicate's records like gulls on a dropped fish. The sediment cores went to three independent labs. All three came back with the same verdict: the levee surveys were fiction. Beautifully typed, expensively stamped fiction, but fiction nonetheless—the kind of documents that look authoritative until you hold them up against actual mud.

And the mud, as it turned out, did not lie.

Mr. Sable was arrested at a ferry crossing, which Ada found poetically appropriate, though she'd have preferred somewhere less comfortable for him. The papers said he'd surrendered without incident, courteous to the last, and she supposed that figured. Men like Sable were courteous the way levees were reassuring—right up until the water came through.

The Syndicate fractured along its own fault lines. Contracts voided. Assets frozen. Half the board turned witness against the other half, each of them suddenly discovering a conscience that had apparently been in storage all these years, available upon request.

Ada read every article. She clipped some. Her father would have pretended not to be pleased and then re-read them all at midnight, and she allowed herself both halves of that inheritance: the pretending and the midnight.

* * *

The publishing took longer than the arresting, because publishing always does.

Dr. Ilsa Reyes at the university press wrote to say the journals needed "contextual framing," by which she meant footnotes, by which she meant months of work. Ada didn't mind. Footnotes were where her father had lived, really—in the small honest print beneath the big claims. She spent whole afternoons cross-referencing his field notes with his letters, learning him again through his margins, discovering that he'd doodled little storms in the corners of pages when he was thinking hard, and that she did exactly the same thing without ever having noticed.

The title page took the longest.

*Delta Sediment Studies, 2011–2024*, by Dr. Erik Halvorsen. That was the easy part. But Dr. Reyes kept sending the proofs back with a question mark beside the second line, and finally Ada wrote back:

*By Erik Halvorsen and Ada Halvorsen. He collected the data. I carried it downriver. That counts as authorship. Ask anyone who was there.*

There was a pause of eleven days, and then the proofs arrived with the second line printed and no question mark anywhere.

She showed Bram. He read it twice, nodded once, and said, "He'd have hated sharing the credit for about a week, and then he'd have been insufferably proud forever."

"That's exactly right," Ada said, and laughed until she had to sit down, because grief, she was learning, didn't leave. It just learned to share the room.

* * *

"You're holding the wheel wrong."

"I'm holding it the way you told me yesterday."

"And yesterday I was being polite." Bram reached over and adjusted her grip, his hands weathered and steady. "Chasing isn't driving. It's negotiating. The storm sets terms; you agree to most of them."

The plains east of Junction had dried into their late-season gold, and the sky above them was doing something ambitious with cumulus towers—nothing dangerous, nothing like the monsoon monster, just weather practicing its scales. Perfect teaching weather. Bram had declared it so with the gravity of a man scheduling a coronation.

They'd fallen into this routine over six weeks without either of them announcing it. Mornings, Ada worked on the book. Afternoons, Bram taught her the rig—the transmitter, the radar, the strange liturgy of dials and antennas—and then they drove out past the last paved mile and let the sky be the classroom.

"Why do you keep doing this?" she asked one day, eyes on the road, hands almost right. "You got your name back. You could go anywhere. Nobody's chasing you except, apparently, me, badly."

Bram was quiet long enough that she glanced over. He was watching the storm build with an expression she'd come to recognize as his version of unguarded.

"When I was invisible," he said, "I told myself it was safer. For everyone. And maybe it was. But mostly it was easier. You can't disappoint anyone if they can't find you." He tapped the dash. "Then a sixteen-year-old pointed a flare gun at me in a survey tent and ruined everything."

"You're welcome."

"Don't mention it." Almost a smile. "Turn left up here. We want to get ahead of it, not under it."

* * *

The levee rebuild crews worked within sight of the highway, and sometimes they stopped to wave at the rig. Ada had gotten used to that—the waving, the recognition, the occasional child dragged forward by a parent to shake the hand of "the girl from the broadcast." It still sat strangely on her, like a coat cut for someone bigger.

At the new Halvorsen Levee—named over her objections, which she'd lost, loudly—they were pouring concrete cores around steel driven down to real bedrock, surveyed by people whose instruments hadn't been paid to lie. Her father's sediment maps hung in the site office, framed. The foreman, a broad woman named Castellanos who had lost a cousin in the spring floods, had told Ada once: "Your daddy's numbers are holding up our whole season. Best thing a dead man ever did for us."

Ada had thanked her and then walked out onto the unfinished embankment and cried for ten minutes behind a stack of riprap, because that was also part of the inheritance, apparently: the truth arrived carrying both pride and cost, and you never got one without the other.

* * *

On the last afternoon of the season, they chased a cell that grew teeth halfway through and became, briefly, genuinely dangerous—a wall cloud rotating with slow menace over empty farmland. Bram talked her through it calm as a chess instructor while hail ticked off the windshield like impatient fingers.

"Drop south. Give it room. Watch the base—that notch there? That's where it's deciding what it wants to be."

"What if it decides tornado?"

"Then we decide farther away." He glanced at her. "You're not scared."

It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway. "I am. I'm just scared in the useful direction now."

He looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable, the way he did when she said something that landed somewhere he kept locked, and then he said, "Halvorsen would've liked hearing that," and turned back to the sky, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

The storm decided to be only beautiful. They watched it go gold-lit and enormous over the fields, rain curtains sweeping like theater curtains, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because some things you don't narrate. You just stand inside them.

* * *

That evening, back at the boarding house, Mrs. Okonkwo brought out a cake for no reason she'd admit to—"it was going to go stale, don't flatter yourselves"—and the mail came late, and among the bills and the university proofs was a thin envelope with no return address.

Ada opened it at the table. Inside was a single sheet, typewritten.

*Miss Halvorsen —*

*I am informed my testimony is no longer required, owing to certain arrangements. I will not trouble your courts further.*

*A word of professional courtesy, since we are colleagues of a sort now: your father's later notebooks—the ones from the winter before the expedition—were never recovered from the camp. I know because I sent men to recover them, and they reported the strongbox empty. If you have them, burn them. If you don't, pray whoever does lacks your talent for publication.*

*Some truths are load-bearing. Remove the wrong one and things fall on people.*

*Respectfully,*

*M.S.*

Ada read it twice. Then she looked across the table at Bram, who had gone very still in the way she'd learned meant his mind was already moving, fast, downriver.

"The winter notebooks," she said slowly. "The ones about the money. Who paid him. Who else knew." Her father had told her once, drunk on celebration after a good core sample, that the survey was only half the story—that the other half was written in ledgers, and ledgers had names in them. Names above Sable's.

"Do you have them?" Bram asked quietly.

Ada thought of the strongbox she'd buried under the cairn stones before she'd buried her father, because her father had taught her that data outlives people only if someone protects it first.

"No," she said. "But I know who does."

Outside, far off over the plains, thunder rolled—harmless, distant, the sky clearing its throat—and Ada Halvorsen-Ashgrove, author, survivor, storm chaser in training, reached for her coat.
