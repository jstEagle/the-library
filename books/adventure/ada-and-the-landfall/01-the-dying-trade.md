## 1. The Dying Trade

There are three ways to learn a man is dying, and the river teaches you all of them. The first is the cough — the wet, apologetic kind that a man tucks into his shoulder like a debt he's ashamed of. The second is the eyes, which go bright and fixed the way a drowning man's do when he's decided the far bank is worth the swim. And the third, the one that never lies, is the way he pays. A healthy man haggles. A dying man opens his purse and asks how much you need.

Elias Vane did all three at the Sedge Landing, in the mud, under a sky the color of an old bruise, and I should have known better than to say yes.

I want it on the record that I said yes anyway, and that I'd say it again, and that this is the sort of thing that sounds noble until you're the one running the flood with a gunboat behind you. But I'm getting ahead of myself. At Sedge Landing I was just a woman with a boat, a debt, and a crew that expected to eat in October.

The *Magpie* sat low in the water that morning, which is to say she sat the way she always sat — half cargo, half regret. I'd taken a contract hauling dyewood down from the middle reaches and the dyewood had come back light, the buyer had come back dishonest, and the harbormaster at Corrin's Ford had come back with a ledger that made me want to throw him in the river and bill his family for the drowning. I owed the yard four hundred crowns for a new sternpost and two seasons of interest that compounded like gossip. Every day the *Magpie* sat still, she cost me money. Every day she moved, she cost me something else. A riverboat captain's arithmetic is simple and cruel.

So when the man came down the landing ramp leaning on a stick that had once been a surveyor's rod, I looked at him the way you look at weather: assessing what he'd cost me.

He was maybe fifty, maybe sixty, maybe a hundred and nine. Fever does that — it burns off the years you can spare and leaves you looking like a candle near its end. He had a prospector's hands, which is to say hands that had been broken and set by amateurs, and a canvas pack he held against his chest like a child. His coat had been good once. His boots had been good twice. The mud at Sedge Landing is the kind that takes a boot as tribute, and his were losing.

"Captain Marchetti," he said. Not a question. That was my first warning. Men who know your name at a landing you've never told anyone about have either done their homework or been sent by someone, and both kinds cost you.

"Depends who's asking," I said, "and whether the answer gets me paid."

"Vane. Elias Vane." He said it like he was checking whether the name still fit. "You run the *Magpie*. You take cargo north past the Split, and you don't ask your clients what's in the boxes, and you've never lost a shipment."

"I've never lost a shipment I've admitted to," I said. "Who told you that?"

"A man at Corrin's Ford who owed you money and paid you anyway." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that on a healthier face would have been a smile. "Said you were the only captain on this stretch who keeps her word out of habit instead of necessity."

I didn't like that. Habit is a thing you build a life on, and I don't hand mine to strangers on muddy landings. But he was already fumbling at his coat, and I'll be honest with you — the part of me that was listening to his cough was also doing sums.

"I need passage to the headwaters," he said. "The mission at Candlemere, past the Split, past the drowned country. Before the rains."

I looked north, where the river bent out of sight behind the willows, and I looked south, where the sky over the delta had that greenish weight to it that old boatfolk call the monsoon's shadow, and I did the arithmetic every captain on this river does by August. The rains were due in five weeks. Maybe six. The headwaters were three weeks' hard run if the channels behaved, and the channels hadn't behaved since the flood year, and the flood year had been nine years ago and the channels were still sulking about it.

"That's a hard run in a good season," I said. "And the season's turning."

"That's why I'm asking you and not the barge lines. The barge lines will tell me no. I need a captain who'll tell me what it costs."

There it was. I told him what it cost. It was a number I'd have been embarrassed to say out loud to anyone who wasn't dying, because it was the number that would clear the yard debt and leave a margin, and margins were a thing I'd read about but never personally met.

He didn't blink. That was my second warning. He reached into his coat and put a purse on the rail of the *Magpie*, and it landed with the heavy, honest clink of coin, not the soft whisper of paper promises. Then he reached into the canvas pack he'd been holding like a child, and brought out a thing wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed cord, and sealed — actually sealed, with a drop of red wax pressed under a thumb — and set it down beside the purse.

"What is it?" I asked, which was a stupid question, because I already knew what it was. You spend enough years on the river, you learn the shape of a map through oilcloth the way you learn the shape of a lie through a smile.

"A map," he said. "My life's work. Nine years of it."

"Nine years," I said, and something ticked in the back of my head, because nine years is a number that comes up around here. Nine years since the flood took Merrow's Landing off every chart and left a lake full of rooftops where a town used to be. Nine years since the magistrate's men came through the drowned country with fresh ink and fresh seals and started handing out land grants for property that was under six fathoms of water. I didn't say any of this. I just filed it, the way you file a sound in the dark — not a threat yet, but not nothing.

"I'm offering you the purse," Vane said, "for the passage. The map rides with you, and you don't look at it. You don't open it, you don't let anyone open it, and when we reach Candlemere — or if I don't reach Candlemere, if it comes to that — you carry it the rest of the way yourself, and you give it to no one but the person I name. And you tell no one what it shows, because you won't know what it shows, and that's the point."

"And if someone makes me tell them?"

"Then you tell them you promised a dying man, and you hope they're the kind of person that means something to." He coughed then, the full wet length of it, and had to grip the rail, and I saw his knuckles go white and stay white. "Captain. I don't have much time. I know how that sounds — every man with a hard bargain says it. But I'm not bargaining. I'm asking. There's a difference, and I think you know it."

I looked at the purse. I looked at the sealed map, sitting there on my rail like a small, patient bomb. I looked at the sky, which had gone from bruise to something with intentions.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about debt: it doesn't just take your money. It takes your caution. It takes the little voice that says *too easy, too clean, walk away.* I had a crew to feed — old Tammas with his bad knee and his encyclopedic memory of every sandbar's mood, and young Wren who'd come aboard with nothing but a knife and a talent for engines, and Junie the cook who could make a meal out of river water and attitude. Four hundred crowns to the yard. Interest like rising water. And here was a fever-wracked stranger offering me the whole weight of it in one purse, for a run I'd have made for a third of that, plus a sealed box and a promise.

A bargain that feels too easy is either a gift or a hook. I've been on the river long enough to know that. What I told myself, standing there in the mud with my hand hovering over that purse, was that I'd know which it was before it reeled me in too deep.

I was wrong, of course. But you don't know you're wrong yet, at the beginning. At the beginning you just know you're poor.

"Passage to Candlemere," I said. "The map rides sealed. I don't look, nobody looks. You give me the name at Candlemere when we're close enough that telling me doesn't give me time to be stupid." I picked up the purse and weighed it, and it weighed like freedom, and I hated how good that felt. "But I'll tell you now, Vane — I keep my promises out of habit, like your friend said. So don't make me one you'd regret."

"Captain," he said, and the fever-brightness in his eyes settled into something steadier, something that looked almost like relief, "I've been making bargains with this river for nine years. This is the first one I don't regret making."

He came aboard slow, one bad step at a time, and Tammas took his pack without being asked and stowed it in the aft cabin without asking what was in it, because Tammas has been with me since before the debt and knows which questions cost money. I put the sealed map in the strongbox under my bunk, under the false floor, under the coil of spare line, because I am not a fool, whatever else I am.

We cast off at midday with the current behind us and the green shadow sitting on the northern sky like a guest who's come early. Vane slept in the aft cabin, coughing in his sleep. The *Magpie* ran light and true for the first time in a season, and I stood at the wheel with four hundred crowns of freedom in the deck box under my feet, and I let myself feel it — the wary, unfamiliar lift of hope, the feeling of a current finally going your way.

That lasted until the next landing. That lasted until a young woman came down the ramp with a bag over her shoulder and my father's name in her mouth — no. His name. *Vane.* And eyes the same fever-bright color as the man sleeping in my aft cabin, and a voice like a mooring line pulled tight, and a story about a father who'd abandoned her nine years ago, and a demand to be taken north.

The river, as I said, teaches you three ways to learn a man is dying.

It never taught me what to do about the daughter.
