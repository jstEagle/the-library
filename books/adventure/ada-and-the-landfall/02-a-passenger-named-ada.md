## 2. A Passenger Named Ada

The river at Corben's Landing was the color of old tea, and the town was doing its best to match. I'd tied the *Magpie* up at the fuel dock with a knot I didn't entirely trust, which felt about right for a port where every second building leaned on its neighbor for support and the third had given up and become a saloon.

I had two days of coal to buy, one crate of engine parts to haggle over, and one sealed map in my cabin that weighed approximately nothing and sat on my conscience like an anchor.

Elias Vane's coin had bought me out of the worst of my trouble — enough to clear the interest on what I owed Old Sarno, enough for coal, enough to keep my crew fed and paid through the season. It hadn't bought me peace. That night at the landing, when he'd pressed the oilskin packet into my hands with fingers shaking too hard to count the money properly, he'd made me swear three things. Carry it north. Tell no one what it shows. And don't look back.

I'd kept the first two so far. The third was proving difficult, because looking back is most of what a captain does. You read the water behind you to know what's coming ahead. You watch what you've stirred up to see if it settles.

Something about this cargo hadn't settled.

"Captain." Tam appeared at my elbow on the deck, holding his ledger like a shield. My engineer's boy — sixteen, all elbows and earnestness, with a genius for machinery and none whatsoever for lying. "There's a woman at the gangplank."

"There's usually a woman at the gangplank. Or a man. Or a customs officer pretending to be both."

"She says she has passage booked."

"We don't take passengers."

"She says her name's Vane."

The word went through me like cold water through a cracked hull. I set down the manifest I'd been pretending to read and walked to the rail.

She stood at the bottom of the gangplank with one canvas bag at her feet and her arms crossed like she owned the dock, the boat, and possibly the river itself. Young — twenty, maybe twenty-one. Dark hair cut short in a way that said she'd done it herself with a knife and hadn't regretted it. A traveling coat too fine for this port and too worn for anywhere better, patched at the elbows with thread that didn't match. She looked like someone who had been respectable recently enough to remember how, and angry often enough to have stopped caring.

"You're Marchetti," she called up. Not a question.

"I'm nobody you've booked passage with."

"My father wired ahead. Elias Vane. He said there'd be a berth on a boat called the *Magpie*, captain named Marchetti, leaving Corben's Landing northbound before the rains." She picked up her bag. "So either you're her, or my father sent nine years of letters to the wrong river."

Behind me, I heard Tam make a small sound, the kind a boy makes when the world rearranges itself without asking permission.

Elias Vane had not mentioned a daughter.

He had mentioned money, a map, a promise, and a fever eating him alive from the inside. He had looked at me with eyes gone yellow as river mud and said *tell no one* with such weight that I'd have sworn on my mother's grave he had no one left to tell. And now here was the proof of everything I'd chosen not to ask: a daughter who'd waited nine years for a man who couldn't be bothered to send for her until he needed a courier.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome — a daughter who'd been sent for and never told why.

"Come aboard," I heard myself say, because apparently my mouth had made the decision while the rest of me was still arguing.

* * *

She took the forward cabin, the small one under the wheelhouse, and unpacked with the ruthless efficiency of someone who'd learned to move fast and leave nothing behind. I watched her through the open door while I pretended to check the mooring lines. Three shirts, a book with a broken spine, a tin of pencils, a folded chart of the upper river that was older than she was and wronger than sin. No photographs. Whatever she'd carried of her father for nine years, it wasn't pictures.

"You knew him," she said, without turning around. "You've met him recently. I can tell."

"What makes you say that?"

"Because you flinched when I said his name on the dock, and you've been watching me since like I'm a hole in your hull." She turned then, and her eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and far too sharp for anyone's comfort. "Where is he?"

"Upstream," I said, and it wasn't even a lie. "Headwaters country. He asked me to carry some papers north for him. Business matters."

"Business." She said the word the way you'd spit out a fishbone. "Of course. Nine years of business." She sat down on the narrow bunk and looked at her hands. "Do you know what he did, Captain? Do you know why I haven't seen him since I was eleven?"

I did not know. I very much wanted not to know, because every version of the story I could imagine made my promise heavier.

"He left," she said, answering herself. "One morning. No note, no goodbye. My mother lasted four more years and then the fever took her, and I went to the parish home in Darrow's Bend, and do you know what came of him? One letter a year. One. Always unsigned, always care of some trading post or mission, always money enclosed — never enough to matter, never enough to explain." Her jaw tightened. "And now, after nine years, he wires a stranger's boat instead of sending for me himself. So either he's ashamed, or he's dying, and I've decided I'll hear whichever one it is from his own mouth."

The engine chose that moment to cough, and I seized the excuse like a drowning woman seizing driftwood. "That'll be Tam fighting the injector. Excuse me."

I fled to the engine room, which is a coward's destination but a warm one.

Tam looked up from the injector housing, grease to his wrists. "Captain, is that girl—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"But if she's his daughter, doesn't she—"

"Tam." I crouched next to him and handed him a wrench he didn't need, just to give my hands something honest to do. "Mr. Vane made me swear. Tell no one. That includes her. *Especially* her."

"That seems wrong."

"It is wrong. It's also a promise, and on this river, promises are the only currency that doesn't devalue." I wiped my hands on a rag that was already blacker than my mood. "Besides, we don't know anything. Maybe he wants to tell her himself. Maybe that's the whole point of the arrangement — she rides north, he gets to look his daughter in the eye one last time and say whatever needs saying."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a boy who still believed adults had plans. "Then why does it feel like we're kidnapping her toward something?"

Out of the mouths of apprentices. I gave him the wrench back and went to get us underway.

* * *

We cleared Corben's Landing at dusk, running the last of the light up the narrowing channel, and Ada Vane installed herself on my wheelhouse like she'd been born there. Which, I suppose, in a way she had — prospector's blood, river-country childhood. She stood at the glass and watched the banks slide by with an expression I recognized and hated: the look of someone reading a letter they're afraid to finish.

"Your pilot knows these waters?" she asked.

"I pilot these waters."

"Alone?"

"The *Magpie* doesn't need company. She needs someone who remembers where the sandbars sulk." I eased us around the tail of Gravel Point, feeling the current lean against the hull like a tired horse. "Nine years is a long time to hold a grudge."

"It's not a grudge. Grudges are for people who matter to you." She said it lightly, and missed by a mile. "It's just... arithmetic. Nine years, twelve letters, no explanations. At some point you stop waiting for the sum to come out and you close the book."

"And yet here you are. On my boat. Going to find him."

Her silence stretched long enough that I checked the channel twice just to have somewhere to look.

"He owes me a reason," she finally said. "That's all. I'm not going for a reunion. I want him to look at me and explain why I spent my childhood being nobody's daughter, and then—" her voice caught, just once, quickly mastered, "—and then I'll decide what he is to me. If anything."

Guilt is a strange cargo. It takes up no space in the hold, draws no water, costs nothing at loading. But it shifts. Every mile upstream it shifted a little more, and I could feel it settling against my ribs like ballast loaded wrong. Because she talked, that first night and the next day, standing beside my wheel while the river unspooled — and everything she said about Elias Vane was a knife with my name on the handle.

She told me about the one time he'd taught her to read water, before he left. About how she'd found one of his letters opened by the parish matron, who'd read it aloud to prove it contained nothing worth keeping, and how Ada had memorized it anyway. About how she'd taught herself surveying from a stolen textbook because she'd decided that if her father loved maps more than people, she would learn maps well enough to understand why.

"If he's really up there doing business," she said, on the second evening, watching the first heat-lightning flicker over the western hills, "then maybe the business is why. Maybe he found something. Something big enough that he thought it mattered more than—" she stopped, restarted. "More than coming home."

*I know what he found,* I didn't say. *It's sealed in oilskin under my bunk, and it's killing him, and I'm carrying it north like a good little courier while his daughter stands six feet away wondering what she did wrong.*

"Maybe he did," I said instead, and hated how gentle it came out.

She glanced at me. "You know something."

"I know the river. Ask me about that."

"Captain Marchetti." She turned to face me fully, and there was something in her stance that reminded me unpleasantly of her father at the landing — that same stubborn verticality, refusing to lean even while falling. "I've spent nine years learning to tell when people are lying to me kindly. Parish matrons, charity boards, loan officers. You're doing it right now. Whatever my father paid you to carry, it isn't just papers, and whatever's wrong with him, you know it, because you keep looking at me like I'm already grieving and trying not to let me notice."

The wheel was cool under my hands. The channel marker blinked green off the starboard bow, steady as a heartbeat. Somewhere below, Tam had the engine humming sweet, and Brannic was cooking something with onions, and the *Magpie* ran true through the darkening water, and none of it — not one plank of it — felt like mine anymore.

"Get some sleep, Miss Vane," I said. "We make the narrows by dawn, and I'll need quiet at the wheel."

She held my gaze a moment longer, and I watched her decide — deliberately, visibly — to let it go. For now. "Goodnight, Captain."

She climbed down from the wheelhouse. I heard her door close. I heard, faintly, the sound of a pencil scratching paper — she was writing, or drawing, or making lists of my lies, and I honestly couldn't have guessed which frightened me more.

I ran the boat another hour into full dark, then anchored in the lee of Widow's Island and stood alone at the rail watching the lightning walk the horizon, closer every flash. The monsoon was early this year. Everyone said so. The barometer had been falling for two days like it had somewhere to be.

Below my feet, in the cabin under the wheelhouse, Elias Vane's daughter slept a few feet from the secret that was going to break her heart. In my cabin aft, his map lay wrapped in oilskin, patient as a stone.

Two days to the headwaters, if the weather held. Three, if it didn't.

And somewhere behind us, I couldn't help noticing, a salvage barge with magistrate's colors had passed Corben's Landing the same afternoon we did, riding low and fast, and taken the northbound channel.

I didn't believe in coincidence. On this river, coincidence was just pursuit you hadn't identified yet.
