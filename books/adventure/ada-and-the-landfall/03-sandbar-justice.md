## 3. Sandbar Justice

I learned three things about the Magpie in my first two days aboard, and none of them were flattering to her captain.

One: the boat ran on tea the way other boats ran on coal, and the tea was terrible enough to constitute a navigational hazard in its own right.

Two: the crew — a laconic engineer called Brannigan who communicated mostly in grunts and a deckhand named Sefa who communicated mostly in laughter — had already decided I was cargo with opinions, which was at least more dignity than most cargo got.

Three: Kestrel Marchetti lied the way other people breathed. Effortlessly, constantly, and with a certain artistry that would have been admirable if it weren't aimed directly at me.

"Your father," she'd said on the second evening, when I asked — for the fourth time, because repetition is its own kind of weapon — where exactly we were going and why she wouldn't say. "He hired me for a delivery. That's all I know."

"That's all you know," I repeated.

"That's all I know." She said it while looking me dead in the eye, hands steady on the wheel, and if I hadn't spent nine years learning to read liars from a man who'd apparently invented the craft, I might have believed her.

The river didn't care about any of this. It carried us north through country that kept getting greener and more indifferent, past fisher stilt-villages and floating chapels and one memorable stretch where the banks narrowed to a green throat and the Magpie's paddle wheel beat against the current like a heart working too hard. I stood at the rail a lot. It gave me something to do besides interrogate the crew and rehearse the speech I'd give my father when I found him — nine years of speeches, compressed and edited down to something with actual dramatic structure.

I never got to deliver it. The river delivered Corben Hale instead.

* * *

It happened on the third morning, at a sandbar landing so temporary it barely qualified as geography — a tongue of grey silt that existed only because the river was feeling generous that week. We'd tied up to take on water when the other boat came around the bend.

She was called the *Iron Mercy*, which told you everything, because nothing genuinely merciful needs to advertise it. Flat-bottomed, gunwale-high with men, and flying the magistrate's colors off the stern like a threat written in bunting. Kestrel saw her before anyone else did. I knew because her whole body changed — a stillness coming over her that wasn't calm, exactly. More like the stillness of an animal deciding whether to run or fight.

"Ada." Her voice was low and very even. "Go below."

"I don't take orders on boats I'm paying passage on—"

"Go *below*." And there was something underneath the evenness then, something that made my feet move before my pride could object. "Sefa, get the tarp over the crates. Brannigan, look bored. Bored, not guilty — there's a difference."

"There is?" said Brannigan.

"You're doing it wrong right now."

They came alongside with a bump that rattled my teeth, and six men swung aboard before our lines were even fastened. They wore the mismatched armor of salvagers — river-plate, canvas, one man with a magistrate's badge pinned crooked over his heart like an afterthought — and they moved with the confidence of people who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered.

The man in front of them was big without being soft about it, broad through the shoulders, with a face like something a river had worn smooth out of spite rather than patience. His hair was cropped to grey stubble. He smiled at Kestrel the way a creditor smiles.

"Captain Marchetti." His voice was pleasant the way a garrote is pleasant — efficient, intimate, designed for one purpose. "Corben Hale. Salvage inspection, by authority of Magistrate Ondrell. You'll open your hold."

"Morning to you too, Hale." Kestrel leaned against her own wheelhouse like she owned the river and he was renting space on it. "Inspection of what? I'm carrying rice, rope, and a shipment of pickled eels that's gone so far past questionable it's achieved philosophy."

"Then you won't mind showing me."

"I mind on principle. Principle's free. Eels are extra."

Hale's smile didn't move but his eyes did, flicking past her, taking inventory: the deck, the crew, the tarped crates, me. On me they stopped a half-second longer than was comfortable, the way eyes do when they're filing something away.

"And who's this?"

"A passenger," Kestrel said. "Paying. Which makes her the only person aboard whose opinion of your manners matters, and she hasn't complained yet, so count yourself lucky."

"I hadn't gotten to complaining yet," I said. "I was pacing myself."

Something crossed Hale's face — not amusement, but the place where amusement would go if he'd ever installed it. Then he snapped his fingers and his men fanned out across the deck, and the performance began.

I want to be fair here. What happened next was, in its way, a masterpiece.

Brannigan chose that exact moment to erupt from the engine hatch waving a manifest and shouting about how *nobody*, *nobody*, respected the cargo schedule anymore, and if these gentlemen wanted to poke through the hold they could start with the eels personally because *he* wasn't touching them again, and also the manifest was wrong, it had been wrong since Marrowford, and someone — and here he turned and pointed a shaking finger at Kestrel — someone had signed for forty bolts of canvas that did not exist—

"They exist," Kestrel snapped back, with the precise pitch of wounded authority. "They're under the tarp."

"The *tarp*? The tarp's for the *rice*—"

"The rice moved."

"The rice can't just *move*, Captain, rice isn't *nomadic*—"

And Sefa started laughing — that helpless, infectious laugh of hers — and one of Hale's men laughed too before catching himself, and in the middle of the chaos Hale stood very still, watching Kestrel argue with her own engineer about the migration patterns of rice, and I watched him decide. I watched him weigh the cost of tearing apart a riverboat over pickled eels against the paperwork and the hour and the fact that Captain Marchetti was clearly just another broke woman hauling rotting cargo north, nothing worth the trouble.

"Fine," he said finally, and the word dropped into the argument like a stone into a well. "Keep your eels." He produced a paper from his coat. "New river levy, though. Inspection fee. Magistrate's orders."

Kestrel paid it without blinking, which told me she'd budgeted for it, which told me she'd expected him, which told me — as I stood there amid the fading theater of the rice dispute — that Kestrel Marchetti had known exactly what was coming up this bend of the river, and had prepared a play for it, and hadn't told me a single word.

Hale paused at the gangway on his way back to the *Iron Mercy*. He looked at Kestrel for a long moment.

"Heard Vane's boy died in Marrowford," he said, conversational as weather. "Fever. Shame. Old man Vane was worth knowing, once."

"Was he," said Kestrel.

"Mm." Hale's gaze slid to me, held for one deliberate heartbeat, and returned. "Safe travels, Captain. River's getting crowded these days. Full of things washing downstream."

Then he was gone, and the *Iron Mercy* churned away south, and the Magpie sat rocking in its wake like a lie settling back into place.

* * *

We didn't speak for an hour. Kestrel took us off the sandbar and upriver with a face carved out of oak, and I let her have the silence because I was busy being furious in a productive, organized fashion, cataloguing evidence.

Item: she'd known Hale was coming. Item: she'd prepared a forged manifest — I'd seen Brannigan wink at Sefa during the rice argument, a small conspiratorial twitch he probably thought was invisible. Item: Hale had said *Vane*. My name. My father's name, spoken like a card he was holding, and Kestrel hadn't flinched, which meant she'd already decided how to play it, which meant she knew what it meant.

Item: the map.

Because here is the thing about watching a staged argument from six feet away instead of participating in it: you see everything. When Brannigan flung the tarp back in theatrical outrage, it caught on the corner of a crate and dragged it half a foot across the deck, and beneath it — just for a moment, just for the length of a heartbeat — I saw oilcloth. Waxed, sealed, wrapped tight around something flat and rectangular, with a strip of paper glued along one edge where a broken seal had been mended with resin.

And on that strip, in a hand I would have known anywhere, because I'd watched it write my birthday letters every year until the letters stopped:

*E.V.*

Elias Vane. My father's map, sealed in oilcloth, hidden under rice on a stranger's boat, while the man himself was — where? Alive? Dead? Dying in some fever-town while his daughter hauled north on false pretenses?

I found Kestrel at the stern rail that evening, when the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing and the river ran gold underneath it. She heard me coming — she always heard me coming; I'd begun to suspect she counted my footsteps the way she counted knots — but she didn't turn around.

"Nice performance today," I said. "The rice bit was inspired."

"Brannigan improvises."

"You knew Hale was coming."

"I suspected."

"You paid an inspection fee you'd budgeted for. You had a forged manifest ready. You've been lying to me since Marrowford, Captain, and I'd like to know — precisely, and in order — what about."

She turned then. And I was braced for the smooth mask, the easy lie, the breath-and-done dishonesty I'd already catalogued twice over. Instead what I saw was tiredness. Real tiredness, deep-water tiredness, the kind that doesn't come from lack of sleep but from carrying something heavy in the dark for a long time.

"Ada." She said my name carefully, like testing ice. "There are things I can't tell you yet. Not won't. Can't. There's a difference, and I know how that sounds, and I know you don't believe me."

"You're right. I don't."

"But I'll tell you this much, and it's true, and you can weigh it however you like." She looked out at the water, jaw working, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped its captain somewhere below decks. "Everything I'm doing — the lies, the detours, the fees I pay to men like Hale — it's all pointed the same direction. North. Toward your father. I made him a promise, and I keep my promises even when keeping them costs more than breaking them. That's the whole of what I can say tonight."

I stood there with nine years of abandonment in my chest and a liar's word offered to me like a coin of uncertain mint, and I should have walked away. Every instinct I'd honed on my father's example said walk away, trust nothing, trust especially the people who ask for trust while withholding reasons.

But I'd watched her face down Corben Hale with nothing but nerve and a bad manifest. I'd watched her crew lie for her without hesitation, not because she ordered it but because they *wanted* to. And I'd watched her, just now, choose a partial truth over a comfortable lie, which was more honesty than my father had managed in thirty years of birthday letters.

"One condition," I said.

Kestrel raised an eyebrow.

"When we find him — whatever you're not telling me, whatever this promise of yours actually is — I hear it from you first. Not from Hale. Not from the river. From you."

A long moment. The paddle wheel thumped. Somewhere ahead, thunder muttered in the hills like a creditor making notes.

"Deal," Kestrel said.

We shook on it, her grip dry and strong, mine steady enough to pass. First deal I'd ever struck with a liar. First one I'd ever wanted to keep.

That night I lay in my narrow bunk listening to the river work against the hull, and I thought about the oilcloth, and the seal mended with resin, and the initials in my father's hand. Whatever Elias Vane had sent up this river, it wasn't cargo. It was a confession, or a weapon, or both — and Corben Hale knew the name Vane, and the *Iron Mercy* was behind us, and the clouds stacking up over the northern hills looked less like weather every day and more like a wall coming down.

Three days later, we passed the drowned town, and I understood everything.
