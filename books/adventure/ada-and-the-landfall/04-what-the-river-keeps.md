## 4. What the River Keeps

Fog on the Verrine is a living thing. It doesn't drift so much as settle, the way a cat settles on the one chair you wanted, and by midnight it had swallowed the Magpie whole. I ran dark, one lamp hooded at the wheel, reading the river by sound and memory and the occasional sour smell of a mudbank getting too friendly with my hull.

Behind me in the pilothouse, Ada Vane sat on the chart locker with her arms crossed, watching me steer water I couldn't see.

"You're going to hit something," she said.

"I'm going to miss everything," I said. "There's a difference, and it's mostly confidence."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be true." I eased the wheel a spoke to port, feeling the current lean against us like a drunk leaning on a lamppost. "Sandbar off the starboard bow in about two hundred yards. Old friend of mine. She shifts her mind every spring, but she's been lazy this year."

Ada watched the fog where the sandbar should be, and when nothing appeared, something in her shoulders loosened half an inch. I noted that. I was keeping a ledger of her, the way I keep ledgers of everything — debts, favors, the exact weight of what I owe and to whom. Hers had gotten heavy lately.

Because here's the thing about secrets: they don't weigh anything until you have to carry them past someone you've started to respect. Then they weigh everything.

I'd been carrying Elias Vane's secret for six days now, and Ada's questions for five, and the combination was starting to grind like sand in the stuffing box.

* * *

She didn't go below until nearly three. When she did, she paused at the pilothouse door.

"Kestrel."

"Mm."

"My father. You knew him, didn't you? Before."

The question hung there, patient as the fog. I kept my eyes on the black water. "I know a lot of people before."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Get some sleep, Ada. Fog runs are long, and I need your eyes sharp at dawn for the Narrows."

She went. Her footsteps down the companionway were even and unhurried, which somehow made it worse. An angry woman stomps. A woman building a case walks quietly.

* * *

The fog broke apart around four, ragged shreds of it streaming through the pilothouse like ghosts late for an appointment, and by then I'd made my decision, or rather the decision had made me, the way decisions do when you put them off long enough. They ripen into inevitability.

I woke her myself. Knocked on her cabin door and stood there in the grey pre-dawn feeling like a man walking to his own trial.

She opened the door already dressed. Of course she was. "Something's wrong."

"Sit down," I said.

"Kestrel—"

"There's coffee in the galley. Sit down anyway. This goes better sitting."

She sat on her bunk, spine straight, hands folded, and I thought: she looks exactly like him. Elias Vane had that same terrible stillness when he was waiting for news he already knew would be bad.

"Your father is alive," I said.

The lamp flame didn't move. Neither did she. "Alive," she repeated.

"He's dying. Fever, and something the mission doctor upriver called 'worn through,' which is a polite way of saying the work ate him alive. He's at a ward station past the headwaters. He hired me at Miller's Landing six days ago to carry something north for him before the rains close the delta."

Ada stood up. Not fast — that was the frightening part. She rose the way water rises, steady and total. "You've known. The whole time. Six days. You let me sit in your galley and talk about him. You let me tell you—" her voice caught, and she hated it, I could see her hating it, "—you let me tell you he abandoned me."

"He asked me not to tell you."

"He asked." She laughed, and there was nothing funny anywhere near it. "A dying stranger asks, and you obey. What did he pay you, Kestrel? What was my father worth?"

"Enough to save my boat," I said, because lying to her now would have been like kicking a drowning woman. "And no, that's not a good answer either. But it's mine."

For a moment I thought she was going to hit me. I want to be clear that I would have deserved it, and also that I would have ducked, because I'm honest but not a martyr.

Instead she said, very quietly, "Show me the map."

"No."

Her head came around like a gun turret. "Excuse me?"

"He sealed it. His instructions were specific — it goes to the headwaters unopened, and I tell no one what it shows. Not the crew, not the magistrate's men, and not you. Especially not you, he said, though I confess I don't fully understand why a man wouldn't want his own daughter—"

"Because he left." Her voice cracked clean through. "Because nine years ago he walked out of our house with his kit and his compass and he never sent word, never sent money, never sent so much as a letter to say whether he was breathing, and now he's dying upstream and he's sending maps instead of himself, and you're standing there telling me a promise to a stranger matters more than—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice was low and terrible. "Open it. Or take me back downstream and I'll swim if I have to."

"Can't do the first," I said. "Won't do the second."

"Why?" It came out raw. "Why is his promise worth more than mine? I'm his daughter. Whatever he's carrying, it's mine too. It's the only thing of his I'll ever get, and you're going to hand it over like freight while I stand there and watch."

"Ada—"

"Don't say my name like you're sorry. You don't get to be sorry. Sorry is what people say when they've run out of reasons."

I looked at her then, really looked, and what I saw scared me more than Hale's men ever had. Not rage. Grief wearing rage as a coat, and underneath both, a girl of maybe nineteen who had spent nine years constructing a father out of absence and had just watched me burn him down to a fevered old man in a mission ward.

So I told her the truth, or the part of it I could carry.

"I promised a dying man," I said. "That's all. You want it to be more complicated than that, and I understand why, but it isn't. He shook my hand on it, and his grip was strong enough that I believed him, and where I come from a handshake is a hull — you don't drill holes in it to see what's inside. If I open that map for you, then I'd open it for Hale, if he asked nicely enough. Then for the magistrate. Then for anyone with a sad enough story. A promise only holds if it holds against the people you love. That's the whole test. That's the only test."

"And if the test breaks me?"

"Then it breaks me first," I said. "I'm carrying it."

We stood there in her cabin, two women and a lamp, and neither of us blinked, and the river went on under us doing what rivers do, which is take everything personally and forgive nothing.

Finally Ada sat back down on the bunk. All the fight went out of her at once, like air out of a punctured line, and what was left sitting there was worse than the fury had been. She put her face in her hands.

"He could have written," she said, muffled. "Nine years. Paper costs almost nothing."

"No," I said. "It costs exactly this."

* * *

I made coffee neither of us drank. We sat in the galley while the sky went from slate to pearl, and somewhere in the middle of it she started talking — not to me, exactly, more to the middle distance where fathers live — about a man who used to carry her on his shoulders through the market at Corrin's Ford and name every boat on the water, and how she'd believed for years he'd come back for her birthday, every birthday, right up until the year she stopped setting a place for him at the table.

"I used to think he was dead," she finished. "It was easier. Dead men have excuses."

"Elias Vane doesn't strike me as a man with excuses," I said. "He strikes me as a man with debts. There's a difference, and it's mostly shame."

She looked at me then — the first real look since the cabin, red-eyed, wrecked, level. "You do love him a little. Your promise. Don't you."

"I love my word," I said. "He happens to be attached to it."

The corner of her mouth moved. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile would eventually build, given materials and time.

"Then take me to him fast," she said. "Captain."

"Was already doing that," I said. "But it sounds better when you say it."

* * *

I went back up to the pilothouse and gave the Magpie everything she had. The Narrows at dawn, white water braiding between rocks the charts marked with the cartographer's equivalent of a shrug, and I took the center channel on memory and nerve while Ada stood beside me calling the water ahead — she'd learned to read foam in one morning, which annoyed me deeply, because it took me two seasons.

We cleared the Narrows by midmorning and turned north into the long reach, and that was when I saw it, far upriver where the water bent: smoke. Not cookfire smoke. Black, greasy, deliberate smoke, the kind men make when they want other men to see it.

And under it, riding at anchor across the channel like a statement, a gunboat. Grey hull. Magistrate's pennant.

Hale had found us again, or never lost us, and between us and Elias Vane lay a mile of open water and a man who burned fisher families for practice.

Ada came to stand at my shoulder. She'd seen it too.

"How fast can this boat go?" she asked.

"Faster than it should," I said, and reached for the throttle.
