## 5. The Drowned Town

The storm came at us out of the west like a debt collector, all black shoulders and no patience, and Kestrel did the thing she does instead of panicking: she got quiet, and then she got specific.

"Merrow's cut," she said, tapping the chart with two fingers. "We'll tuck up into the old channel and let it spend itself."

"The old channel's not on the chart," said Brannic, our engineer, who had the face of a man perpetually mid-argument with a stove.

"No," Kestrel agreed. "It isn't."

That was the whole of the navigation briefing. I was learning that aboard the *Magpie*, authority was mostly a matter of knowing things other people didn't and being too stubborn to drown about it.

I went forward while they worked the wheel and the throttle, because there is a particular uselessness to standing in a pilothouse when you can't steer, and I'd had my fill of uselessness in nine years of waiting for a father who never came. The rain hit sideways. The river went the color of gunmetal and started throwing itself against the hull like it had a grudge.

And that was when I saw the town.

Or what the river had left of it.

* * *

You have to understand — I grew up on stories of Merrow's Landing. Not good ones. My father used to talk about it the way people talk about a war they survived. He was there, he said, the year the water came. He never said much more than that, and when I pushed, he'd go quiet in a way that felt like a door closing, and eventually I stopped pushing, and eventually I stopped asking about anything, and eventually he left, and I told myself the silence and the leaving were the same cowardice wearing different coats.

Now here was the town itself, rising out of the flood-swollen channel like a confession.

A church steeple first, tilted, its bell still hanging — I could see the dark shape of it through the rain, swaying slightly, though whether from wind or current I couldn't say. Then rooflines, just the ridges of them breaking the surface, a whole street's worth of houses drowned to their eaves. A chimney standing alone in open water with no house attached to it anymore, smoking nothing, keeping nobody warm. A fence line running off into the deep like it was fencing in the river now, doing its job for a new landlord.

Nobody lives here. Nobody has lived here for eleven years. And yet the place didn't feel empty. It felt *occupied*. It felt like a room you walk into where someone has just left, and you can feel them, and you know if you turn fast enough—

"Merrow's Landing," Kestrel said behind me. She'd come forward without my noticing, which was becoming a habit of hers I resented. Her hands were steady on the rail, her eyes doing that thing where they read the water like other people read letters. "Flood took it in the spring rise, eleven years back. Forty-one dead."

"I know," I said. "My father was here."

Something moved across her face — quick, gone before I could name it. Guilt has a look, I've learned. It looks like a person deciding how much truth they can afford.

"He doesn't talk about it," I said. "Didn't. I used to think that meant he was ashamed of something." I watched the steeple slide past, closer than I liked, close enough that I could see the bell rope still trailing down into the drowned doorway beneath it, green with weed, reaching toward us like it wanted something. "Now I'm starting to think maybe he was just carrying something heavy and didn't want to set it down where I could see it."

Kestrel said nothing. Kestrel, I was discovering, treated silence the way other captains treated cargo: she stored it, weighed it, and delivered it only when paid.

But I wasn't watching her anymore. I was watching the water.

Because I'd heard the coordinates.

* * *

Here is what I hadn't told anyone aboard: three nights ago, half-asleep in the hold, I'd heard Kestrel and Brannic talking low over the chart table, and I'd caught numbers. Rivermen speak coordinates like a litany — marks, cuts, confluences — and I'd grown up on a prospector's knee, so numbers are a language I dream in. Two marks. A bearing off the ruined bell tower. A depth reading that made no sense for anything except something sunk and meant to stay sunk.

I'd written them down. I'm not proud of it. I'm also not sorry.

And now, as the *Magpie* slid through the drowned street with the storm hammering us and the church tower swinging slowly abeam, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since I wrote those numbers down.

The bearing off the bell tower. The depth. The second mark — which wasn't a mark at all, I realized, my stomach going cold and then colder. It was a survey line. A grant line. The kind drawn by magistrates' clerks with clean pens and dirty hands, the kind that appear on land registries after disasters, when the people who owned the ground are dead or scattered and can't argue.

I had spent the last four years working for a land office myself. Junior clerk, lowest rung, copying grant documents in a fair hand. I knew exactly what those lines looked like. I had inked a hundred of them without ever once asking where they came from.

The map my father sealed in oilcloth and pressed into Kestrel Marchetti's hands — the map everyone aboard pretended wasn't the actual reason we were running north ahead of the rains — didn't mark gold. Prospectors mark gold with crosses and depths and assay notes. This marked *boundaries*. Boundaries and a bearing and a depth, and a drowned town sitting square in the middle of all of it like a signature on a confession.

The flood killed Merrow's Landing. And then the magistrate drew lines around its bones and called the ground his.

* * *

The storm passed over us by midafternoon, spending its temper on the hills, and the river went flat and gray and strangely gentle, the way water does after it's done something terrible. We tied up at the edge of the old channel to let the light settle, and I stood at the rail looking back at the steeple until the bend took it.

Kestrel found me there. She leaned on the rail beside me, not looking at me, offering me the courtesy of not being watched while I worked out how to say a hard thing.

"My father was here when it happened," I said. "Eleven years ago. He left me with an aunt the winter before — said it was one last season, one last claim. One last everything. That was always his phrase. *One last.*" I turned the words over and found, underneath nine years of hurt, something else. Something with edges. "He wasn't prospecting. Was he. He was *witnessing*. He saw what happened here — what really happened, whether the flood was just weather or whether somebody upstream moved water where it shouldn't go, whether the grants were signed before the bodies were cold. And he spent eleven years gathering proof, and he couldn't bring it anywhere, because the magistrate owns every court between here and the capital."

Kestrel was very still. River-still. The stillness of someone deciding whether the ice will hold.

"You've been reading my chart table," she said.

"I've been listening to your crew," I said. "There's a difference, and we both know which of us should be more embarrassed."

A sound escaped her that might have been a laugh in a better mood. Then she looked at me straight on, and I braced for the lie, the smooth deflection, the captain's answer.

What she said was: "I promised him no one would see that map but the tribunal."

"And I'm asking you to break a promise made to a man who broke every promise he ever made me?" I shook my head. "No. I'm not asking you to show it to me. I'm telling you what I already know, so you understand I'm not a passenger anymore. I'm cargo with opinions." I made myself breathe. Made my voice level, because fury and grief taste identical in the throat and I refused to hand her the wrong one. "The debt he paid you with — it was never money, Captain. It was testimony. He hired you to be a witness's legs, because his don't work anymore. And whatever's at the headwaters that he thinks can finish this—" 

I stopped. Because Kestrel's face had done the thing again. The guilt thing. But deeper this time, older, like a scar reopening rather than forming.

"What happens at the headwaters?" I asked slowly. "Kestrel. What aren't you telling me?"

She looked out at the drowned town, at the chimney standing its lonely watch in the middle of the river, and when she spoke her voice was quieter than the water.

"Hale's gunboat was seen at Graylock Lock yesterday," she said. "Two days ahead of us. And your father isn't at the headwaters, Ada. He's at the mission ward above it, and he's dying faster than this boat runs." She turned to me, and her eyes were steady and sorry and absolutely honest, which somehow was worse than any lie. "I was going to tell you tonight. I keep choosing the moment, and the river keeps moving it."

The bell in the drowned church chose that instant to shift on its rotted beam, far off across the water, and though no wind reached it, I swear I heard it toll — one low note, swallowed by the flood, like a town counting its dead and finding one name still owed.

I put my hand flat on the rail and held on.

"How long," I said, "to reach him?"

Kestrel looked upstream, at the darkening water, at the season turning against us.

"If the river lets us," she said, "three days."
