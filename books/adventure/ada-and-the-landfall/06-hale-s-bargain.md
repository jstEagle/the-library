## 6. Hale's Bargain

The lock at Stonepile Narrows was the kind of place where a riverboat went to die of boredom. Two stone walls, a gate that groaned like a penitent, and a lockkeeper who charged by the hour and moved at the speed of moss. I'd shot it a dozen times over the years, and every time I swore I'd find another way north, and every time I came back, because there wasn't one. Rivers are honest that way. They'll forgive you anything except forgetting where the choke points are.

Corben Hale had not forgotten.

We came around the last bend at mid-morning, the Magpie riding heavy with water and woodsmoke and the particular silence of a crew that knows trouble is waiting, and there he was. His gunboat sat moored just downstream of the lock gates, all fresh black paint and mounted iron, looking like a sermon on the value of obedience. Hale himself stood on the lockkeeper's platform with his hands folded behind his back, patient as a bill collector, which is precisely what he was.

"Captain Marchetti," he called up, pleasant as a Sunday. "Fancy meeting you here."

"Fancy," I agreed, and put the wheel over to tie up, because the river had already decided this conversation was happening. The current at Stonepile runs one direction, and it isn't mine.

Ada came up on deck as the lines went taut. She'd learned to read my silences in the weeks since Merrow's Landing, and she read this one correctly, which is to say she went below without being told and took the map's hiding place with her, or at least the knowledge of it. We'd never moved it. Hale would expect us to move it. Sometimes the boldest thing is to leave the thing exactly where a fool wouldn't leave it.

I went down the gangplank to meet him.

He looked well. That was the first insult of the day. Hale had the kind of health that comes from eating other people's labor — broad through the shoulders, sun-browned, teeth too white for a man whose business was arson and paperwork. He offered a hand. I looked at it until he put it away.

"You've been hard to find," he said.

"Rivers are long."

"Rivers are narrow, Captain. That's the trouble with them. Everything on a river is going where you're going, sooner or later, and all you have to do is wait." He gestured at the lock, the gates, the whole stone throat of the place. "I waited."

"Congratulations. Did you want a medal or a manifest?"

"I want to make you rich."

I'll give him this: he didn't waste time. He produced a paper from his coat, unfolded it on the lockkeeper's table like a man laying out cards he already knew would win, and turned it so I could read. My name was on it. So was the Magpie's. So was a number I knew better than my own heartbeat — the debt I owed to Verrone's lending house, the debt that had me running night cargo and questionable manifests and sleeping with a pistol under my pillow. The debt that had made Elias Vane's bargain feel like salvation.

"Paid in full," Hale said. "Today. One signature. You hand over whatever the old man gave you, and you walk away clean. Boat's yours outright. River's yours. No Verrone, no interest, no collectors. You could fish for a living if you wanted. Retire. Become a nun. Whatever it is honest women do."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then nothing happens today." He smiled, and it was the smile that told me everything. "Nothing ever happens today with me, Captain. That's what people don't understand. I'm very patient. But the monsoon's coming early this year. The magistrate's already moved his ledgers inland. And when the water comes up, a lot of things get lost. Boats. Papers. People. It's a dangerous season for a woman alone with a heavy boat and no protection."

"You're threatening me at a lock," I said. "That's a new low even for the magistrate's payroll. Most of you wait until you're off the clock."

"I'm offering you a gift." His voice didn't change, which was somehow worse. "I'm offering you the only exit you'll ever be offered. Most people in your position would weep with gratitude."

"Most people in my position haven't met you."

He laughed at that, genuinely, and I understood I was talking to a man who had never once in his life heard a no that stuck. That was the thing about Hale. It wasn't cruelty, exactly, or not only. It was the absolute, untroubled certainty that everything could be bought, and that anyone who said otherwise was simply quoting too high.

"Take the day," he said, folding his paper away. "Talk to your crew. Sleep on it. I'll be here when the gates open tomorrow." Then, almost gently: "The old man's dying, isn't he? Vane. Word travels. You're carrying a dead man's errand, Captain, and dead men don't pay debts. I do."

I walked back up the gangplank without answering, which took more discipline than anything I'd done that year.

* * *

The crew gathered in the galley that evening, and I told them all of it, because a captain who lies to her crew about a man like Hale deserves whatever mutiny she gets. Brannick, my engineer, a man built like a boiler and about as talkative, listened with his arms crossed and said, "Pay the debt," which was Brannick for *I have thought about this and I am afraid for you*. Old Sefa, who'd run cargo on this river since before the magistrate had a title, just shook her head and said, "You don't pay a wolf to leave the herd. You pay him to come back."

Ada said nothing for a long time. She sat with her hands around a tin cup of coffee she wasn't drinking, and when she finally spoke, her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone holding something heavy very still.

"He burned boats before," she said. "Down at Ferrow's Bend. Three years ago. A family wouldn't sell their landing rights, and one morning there was nothing left but nails. I read about it. Everyone read about it, and nothing happened, because the magistrate's tribunal ruled it an accident." She looked up at me. "He's going to make an example of someone. That's what the day is for. He wants you to watch."

She was right. I'd known she was right since the moment Hale smiled, but hearing it said out loud made it a fact instead of a fear, and facts are heavier.

"Then we watch," I said. "And we remember."

Dawn came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet. The monsoon was somewhere out over the sea, days out, maybe less — the birds had gone quiet along the banks, and the river had that held-breath quality it gets before weather. Hale was on his platform again, and this time he wasn't alone. He had six men with him, and they were standing around a fisher family's boats like men admiring livestock.

I knew the family. The Ondos. They'd fished the Narrows for two generations, a grandmother and her son's kids, three narrow skiffs and a drying rack and a stilt shack the color of driftwood. The grandmother was on her platform arguing with Hale's men, and her voice carried across the water the way old women's voices do when they have run out of patience for the world.

Hale let her talk. Then he nodded to one of his men, and the man walked along the line of skiffs with a lamp of burning oil and touched it to each hull, unhurried, almost courteous, the way you'd light candles in a church.

The grandmother stopped arguing. That was the worst of it. She just stopped, mid-word, and stood there while her family's livelihood went up in orange light, and her grandchildren came out of the shack and stood beside her, and nobody screamed, because everyone on that river understood exactly what this was. Not rage. Not punishment. Arithmetic. Hale was showing me the price of the map in a currency I couldn't refuse to count.

"Captain Marchetti," he called across the water, not even raising his voice, because he knew I was listening. "The gates open in an hour. I'll have my answer before they do."

I stood at the Magpie's rail with my hands on the wood until they ached, and I felt something in me go very cold and very quiet, the way the river does before a flood. Not fear. Fear would have been useful. This was clarity, the kind that comes once and stays. There was no number on Hale's paper that could buy my signature, because it was never about the debt. It had never been about the debt. The debt was just the hook, and hooks are for fish, and I was done being fished.

"Kestrel." Ada stood beside me. She'd watched it too, all of it, her jaw set like her father's — I'd seen Elias's face in hers often enough by then to know the family stubbornness when I saw it. "The Ondos will need a boat. After."

"After," I agreed. "We'll tow their people north with us. And when this is done, they'll fish their own water again, and the magistrate can write his grants on ash."

Below us, Brannick was already at the boiler, and I could hear him building steam — more steam than the gates required. Sefa was at the bow with a boat-hook, watching the lock gates, watching the gunboat, watching the water. The crew had made its decision without a word, the way good crews do.

I went to the wheelhouse and took down the chart of Stonepile Narrows, the official one, the one every captain used. Then I put it away and closed my eyes and called up the other chart, the one no paper held — every snag, every back-eddy, every mood of that water at every season, learned the hard way over fifteen years. There was a second channel through the Narrows. It ran behind the lock, through water the charts called impassable, and it was impassable, at most water levels, in most hands.

The rain's first outriders came down the gorge in cold gray sheets, and the river rose an inch while I watched.

Hale wanted his answer before the gates opened. I intended to give him one he hadn't priced.
