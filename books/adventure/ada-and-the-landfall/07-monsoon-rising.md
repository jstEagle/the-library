## 7. Monsoon Rising

The sky had been lying to us for three days.

It did it the way rivers do — patiently, with a flat white ceiling of cloud and a heat that pressed down like a hand on the back of your neck, so that every breath felt borrowed. The birds went quiet first. Then the water itself changed color, going from tea to something darker and more secretive, as if it knew what was coming and had decided not to tell us either.

I stood at the rail of the *Magpie* and watched the horizon do nothing, which is a skill I'd learned from my father, though I didn't know that yet. Nine years of not-knowing. You'd think a person could fill nine years with other things — and I had, with ledgers and lodgings and a talent for not writing letters — but there was always a room in me with his name on the door, kept dark, kept locked, kept anyway.

Kestrel came up beside me with two cups of chicory and handed me one without asking, which by then was its own kind of sentence between us.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic," she said.

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"This is the bad kind." She sipped hers. "The kind where the numbers are days."

She wasn't wrong. We were two days from the mission at Vesper's Bend, where Elias Vane lay dying in a leper ward that smelled of carbolic and river damp, according to the letter Kestrel had finally shown me three nights ago — after the fog, after the fury, after I'd cried myself hoarse against her shoulder like a girl half my age and twice as foolish. Two days, if the river behaved.

The river was not behaving.

"You feel that?" Kestrel said quietly. She wasn't looking at the horizon anymore. She was looking at the current, which had begun to move against itself, eddying upstream along the banks while the middle ran down. "That's the landfall wind. It pushes the water backward before it pushes anything else."

"How long?"

"Ask the sky. It's been lying, but it's about to stop."

* * *

We made Vesper's Bend at dusk, running the last miles with the engine opened up and the *Magpie* shuddering like a living thing that understood urgency better than any of us could say aloud. Kestrel took channels I would have sworn didn't exist, threading sandbars she named as we passed them — Old Sulk, the Widow's Tooth, the Graveyard Shift — each one an old acquaintance with a grudge. The crew worked without being told. Even Brannigan, who complained professionally and constantly, said nothing at all, which frightened me more than the wind did.

The mission sat on a bluff above the headwaters: whitewashed walls gone gray, a chapel with a tin roof, and rows of low wards where the sisters kept the people the world had decided to forget. Rain hadn't come yet. The air had the held-breath quality of a church before the organ starts.

A sister met us at the landing — Sister Ondine, small and iron-spined, hands like tree roots. She looked at me and knew me on sight, which should have been impossible.

"You have his chin," she said. "And his way of standing like the ground owes him money."

I followed her up the bluff path through frangipani trees that had stopped bothering to bloom. The wind was rising now in earnest, dragging its fingers through the palms, and somewhere south the light had gone a bruised green-black that I recognized from childhood storms and had never once been glad to see.

Elias Vane was in the third ward, in a cot by the window, and he was so diminished that I walked past him twice before Sister Ondine touched my elbow and turned me around.

Nine years will do that. Nine years had made me a woman; they had unmade him into a bundle of bones wrapped in yellowed skin, hands ruined past recognition, face collapsed inward around eyes that were still, impossibly, his — quick, wry, watching, as if he'd already thought of three jokes about his own condition and discarded two of them as too easy.

"Ada," he said. Not a question. He'd known, then. He'd known she'd come, or hoped it hard enough to make no difference.

"You absolute bastard," I said, because grief makes poets of nobody and I had rehearsed this moment for nine years and what came out was what was true. "You let me think—"

"I know what I let you think." His voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "Best lie I ever told. Only lie that ever cost me everything." He tried to lift his hand — the ruined one — and failed, and settled for turning his palm upward on the blanket, an offering, an apology, an old man's surrender all at once. "Sit down, daughter. The rain's coming and I've got maybe one good night in me, and I mean to spend it badly."

So I sat, and held the hand I could hold, and we spent it badly together, in fragments, because that's how the truth comes out when you've saved it too long — not whole, but in pieces, like a plate broken and glued and still bearing the cracks.

He told me about the flood at Merrow's Landing. Not the map version — the human one. How the magistrate's men had come a week before the water did, buying signatures for land grants most people couldn't read, telling everyone the levee was sound. How his brother — my uncle Tomas, whom I'd been told died of fever — had refused to sign, and how the levee had failed exactly where the survey said it wouldn't, and how forty-one people went into the water including Tomas and his wife and their boy.

"They called it an act of God," Elias whispered. "God gets blamed for a lot of paperwork."

He told me why he'd left. Not abandonment — exile. He'd seen the grant documents afterward, in a magistrate's office he'd broken into with a crowbar and a grudge, and he'd copied what mattered onto oilcloth and run, because staying meant dying uselessly and he wanted to die usefully instead, later, farther away. He'd sent money home through third parties and never signed his name, because a signature was a trail and a trail was a grave.

"And you—" his breath caught, and Sister Ondine appeared with water and vanished again, a woman who moved like weather herself, "—you were the one clean thing. I couldn't bring the war to your door. So I brought it nowhere, and it found me anyway, in the end. Fever from the ward work. Fitting. I caught it from the people everyone else threw away."

Outside, the first rain arrived — not drops but a wall, a sound like the whole river deciding to fall upward, the tin roof of the chapel ringing like a struck bell that wouldn't stop. The windows went white with water. The lamp guttered and steadied.

I told him things too. Small things, stupid things, the inventory of nine years: the landlady in Corvane who'd cheated me, the clerkship I'd hated, the year I taught myself surveying out of spite. He laughed at the spite part until it turned into coughing, and I held the cup for him, and when he could speak he said:

"Spite's a fine inheritance. Better than money. Money runs out. Spite compounds."

"Is that what the map is? Spite?"

"It's testimony," he said. "Forty-one names. Boundaries that don't match the grants. Dates that prove the papers were signed before the flood they claim caused them. It's a courtroom in oilcloth, Ada. I just need someone to read it aloud where the right people can hear." His eyes fixed on mine, and they were clear in a way the rest of him wasn't. "I asked Marchetti to carry it. I'm asking you to finish it. Not because you're my blood. Because you're the only one left who can, and because you're angry enough to do it properly."

I swore it. I won't dress it up. I took his ruined hand and I swore it over the roar of the monsoon, that I'd carry his testimony to the delta tribunal, that I'd see the grants voided and the names read aloud, all forty-one, starting with Tomas Vane. And something in his face let go then, some knot nine years tight, and he smiled with the whole wreck of himself.

"There's my girl," he said. "Now get some sleep. Dying takes all night and I refuse to have an audience."

* * *

He died at dawn, in the grey pause between the rain's worst and the river's rise, with Sister Ondine reading psalms in a language neither of us spoke and me holding a hand that had finally gone still as moored water. The storm kept on outside, indifferent and enormous, and inside the ward was very quiet, the quiet of a thing completed.

I did not cry. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only here: I sat with my father's body through the dawn watch and I did not cry, because crying was for the girl who'd waited nine years, and that girl had drowned at Merrow's Landing with the rest of them, and the woman who remained had work to do.

Sister Ondine helped me wrap him in sailcloth — "he'd have wanted canvas, he talked about boats the way other men talk about women" — and we buried him on the bluff above the headwaters, fast, in the rain, four of us with spades while the ground turned to soup around our boots. Kestrel stood at the head of the grave the whole time, hat off, hair plastered flat, saying nothing, and when it was done she put her hand on my shoulder and left it there, and that was the whole eulogy, and it was enough.

Then she turned to me, rain streaming off her jaw, and said the thing I already knew she'd say.

"The river's rising faster than it should. Hale's gunboat was sighted at the confluence yesterday, and the tribunal convenes at the delta in six days — five, if the flood eats the channel markers. We can't wait out the storm, and we can't go the main channel with him sitting on it."

"So we go where there's no chart."

"So we go where there's no chart," she agreed, and for the first time since I'd met her, Kestrel Marchetti smiled like the weather was good news. "Get aboard, Ada. Your father gave me a map. You're going to give me a reason to outrun a flood."

The *Magpie* was waiting at the landing, riding high and restless, straining at her lines like something that had heard the same promise I had. Behind us the mission bell rang through the rain, marking a hour, any hour, all hours the same now. Ahead the river had come up over its banks and swallowed the landing road whole, brown and roaring and wide as a sea.

Kestrel cast off the lines with her own hands.

"Hold on to something," she said. "This is the part I was born for."
