## 8. Running the Flood

The rain came down like the sky had a grudge and all day to settle it.

I stood at the wheel of the Magpie with water sheeting off my hat brim and my knuckles white on spokes that had gone slick as eels, and I will confess something I'd deny under oath: for one full minute, I was happy.

Not comfortable. Comfortable had drowned back at the lock with Hale's fire still smoking on the far bank. Happy is a different animal entirely — meaner, hungrier, born in bad weather. The river had come up four feet since dawn and was coming up more, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees like kindling and a chicken coop that had clearly seen better days, and every instinct I owned was screaming at me to tie off and wait it out.

But behind us, riding the main channel with her searchlight carving the rain into ribbons, came the *Harrier*. Hale's gunboat. And waiting it out meant letting a man who burned fisher families for punctuation catch us moored like ducks at a fair.

So we ran.

"There!" Ada shouted from the bow, one hand locked on the jackstay, the other pointing left of a drowned fence line. "The channel marker — it's under!"

"I see it," I said, though what I actually saw was memory. That marker had been planted by a dredging crew eleven years ago to mark the deep water east of Corley's Bend, and I'd run past it maybe two hundred times in low water, cursing it, because in low water it sat proud on a sandbar that ate hulls for breakfast. Which meant right now, with the bar drowned under six feet of flood, the safe water wasn't where the marker said. It was where the *old* channel said — the one no chart bothered with anymore, the one that curved west through what was currently a cornfield.

"Trust me," I called back, and swung the wheel hard to port, straight at the treetops of a submerged orchard.

The Magpie is forty-one feet of stubborn cypress and iron, and she answered the way she always does: complaining first, then committing. We slid over the orchard so close our wash combed through the top branches, apples whipping loose and drumming off the deck like hail, and then the old channel took us and the current grabbed the stern and suddenly we weren't fighting the river anymore. We were riding it.

* * *

You want to know what a flooded back-channel sounds like? It doesn't sound like anything. That's the trick of it. The main channel roars; the flood plains whisper. Water spreading out over country it used to own moves quiet and flat and fast as a rumor, and if you don't know where the fences are — the drowned fences, the sunken culverts, the farm roads with their gravel beds sitting two feet under silk-smooth water — you'll find them all at once, at speed, with your keel.

I knew them. God help me, I knew them. Not because any chart recorded them — charts are written by surveyors in calm weather with government pay, and they write down what a river *is*, not what it *remembers* — but because I'd spent thirty years learning this delta the way you learn a lover's moods. Every sandbar has one. The bar below Teller's Point sulks after a dry summer and gives up its sand slow. The cut behind Miller's Island runs shallow on its north side always, always, no matter what the gauge says. The levee breach at Hollis's field floods early and drains late, which means in the first week of a rise it's the fastest water on the river and in the second week it's a trap.

This was week one. Day three, if we were precise, and we were being precise, because precision was the only thing standing between us and becoming a story other boatfolk told each other in the winter.

"Depth under the port quarter's dropped," Finch hollered from the sounder, rain hammering his oilskin. "Two fathoms. One and a half."

"Hold on." I eased the throttle back a hair and read the water ahead — not looked, *read*, the way you read a face. There was a seam running diagonal across the channel about fifty yards on, a line where the brown water went suddenly smooth and oily. Smooth water over fast water means something underneath is holding the current up. A submerged levee crown, most likely, or the roof of somebody's barn. "Ada! Starboard side — see that line where the ripples stop?"

She squinted through the rain. "Yes!"

"That's a wall under there. We go around it, left, wide."

"You can't possibly know that's a wall."

"No," I agreed, hauling the wheel over. "But I know what the river looks like when it's lying, and that's it."

We cleared it by less than a boat-length. Behind us, the *Harrier's* searchlight swept the cornfield we'd just crossed and kept going, hunting the main channel, hunting a ghost. Hale had charts. Hale had engines with twice our horsepower and a crew with twice our numbers and a gun mounted on her foredeck that could take us apart at four hundred yards.

What Hale didn't have was thirty years of grudges against specific sandbars.

* * *

We lost him proper at dusk, or what passes for dusk under a monsoon — the light just thickening from grey to bruise. The rain slackened to a hard steady pour and the wind backed around, and I made the call to push on through the dark rather than anchor, because anchoring in rising flood water is how boats end up in treetops.

That was when the river decided to test whether I believed my own reputation.

It came at the old S-turn below Ratchett's Landing, a place I've run a hundred times in every condition short of fire. In normal water it's a gentle bend around a long spoon-shaped bar. But the flood had done something to it. The bar had shifted — I felt it before I saw it, a wrongness in the way the current set against the rudder, the wheel going light in my hands like a held breath — and where the deep channel should have been, there was instead a boiling seam of crosscurrent where the new flow collided with the old.

"Hard astern!" I shouted, and Finch slammed the engine into reverse, and for three seconds the Magpie stood still in moving water, trembling like a horse at a jump.

Then the current took her stern and swung us broadside.

Broadside to a flood current is death. Every boatman knows it. The water hits your flank and rolls you over like a child flipping a toy, and once you're over in water this cold and this fast, nobody surfaces twice. I felt the deck tilt, ten degrees, fifteen, cargo shifting below with a groan of breaking lashings, and Ada came sliding across the wet planks toward the lee rail with her eyes enormous, and I thought — with a clarity that still shames me — *not the map, please, anything but the map.*

No. That's a lie. What I thought was: *not her.* The map didn't enter into it. Somewhere between the lock and the storm, the cargo had rearranged itself in my ledger, and the girl was worth more than the gold now, and I hadn't even noticed when it happened.

"Weight to starboard!" I bellowed. "Everyone starboard — *now!*"

They came. Finch, soaked and swearing. Old Marta from the galley doorway, throwing her whole small body against the rail. Ada, scrambling hand over hand along the jackstay. And the Magpie, bless her crooked heart, felt them — felt the weight come home to the high side — and paused at twenty degrees of heel with green water walking calmly across her deck, and then, degree by degree, sullen as a mule, she came back.

I threw the wheel hard over, kicked the engine forward, and let the same current that had tried to kill us carry our bow around through the turn. For a moment we were surfing — genuinely surfing, stern lifted, the whole boat planing down the face of the flood like something alive and delighted — and then the channel opened out below Ratchett's Landing and we were through, wallowing, shipping water, alive.

We floated past a farmhouse roof with a family on it, waving lanterns, and I hated myself for not stopping, and I knew stopping would kill us all, and I did not stop. The river keeps a tally of such things. So do I.

* * *

We grounded at midnight on the high bank above an abandoned ferry crossing, and took stock.

The stock was ugly. The near-capsize had burst the cargo hold's lashings, and the flood had claimed our freight — crates of trade goods, salt, tools, the honest cargo that paid for the dishonest voyage. Gone downstream, scattered across three counties of drowned farmland. Marta found the galley stove had shifted and put out a small fire nobody had noticed starting, which tells you the quality of the evening we were having.

"The manifest," Finch said grimly, wringing out his hat. "There goes the season. There goes the debt money, probably."

"The debt money was gone the day I signed for this trip," I said. "Everything since is gravy."

Ada came aft dripping, arms crossed against the cold, and stopped in front of me. Rain still fell, gentler now, a fine persistent drizzle like the sky couldn't quite finish the argument.

"You knew that turn," she said. "Even changed like that. You felt it before it happened."

"Felt the wheel go light."

"That's not knowing. That's — " She searched for the word, gave up, shook her head. "My father talked about pilots like you. Said the good ones didn't navigate the river, they negotiated with it. I thought he was being poetic."

"He was being generous. Mostly I bribe it with respect." I nodded at the black water sliding past, heavy and patient. "It'll drop in nine days. Ten. Then every channel we ran today will be a different river again, and half of them won't forgive a second visit."

"And Hale?"

"Gone to ground somewhere, or gone ahead. He knows where we're bound. The tribunal convenes at the delta in five days, and he can't let Elias's testimony reach it." I looked south, downriver, where the dark was total. "He doesn't need to catch us anymore, Ada. He just needs to be there first, with enough men, when we arrive."

Ada was quiet a moment. Then she reached inside her coat and brought out the map case, which she'd carried lashed to her body through the capsize without my ever asking her to, and pressed it flat against her chest like something with a heartbeat.

"Then we'd better not take the river's time," she said. "Take yours instead. The channels only you know."

I looked at this woman I'd lied to, ferried, fought with, and would now follow anywhere, and I felt the last of my old dread — the debt, the promise, the whole anchored weight of Kestrel Marchetti's careful life — lift off me like fog burning off morning water.

"Marta," I called. "Get the stove hot. We leave on the pre-dawn."

Below us, the flood went on talking to itself in the dark, and for the first time in thirty years, I understood everything it was saying.
