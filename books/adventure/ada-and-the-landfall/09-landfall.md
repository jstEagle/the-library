## 9. Landfall

The delta announced itself the way grief does — not all at once, but in stages you can't mistake for anything else.

First the water went wide and brown and patient, the river forgetting it had ever been a river at all. Then the banks dissolved into a hundred braided channels, each one threaded with boats: fisher skiffs, cargo punts, houseboats with laundry strung like signal flags. And then the smell — mud and woodsmoke and drying nets and, underneath it all, something older, something that said *people have argued here for four hundred years and will argue here for four hundred more.*

"They call it the Shambles," Kestrel said from the wheel, one hand on the brass, the other holding a mug of tea she'd been not-drinking since dawn. "The tribunal grounds. Don't let the name fool you. It's the most orderly place on this whole river."

"It looks like a shipwreck that decided to become a town."

"That's essentially what it is."

She wasn't wrong. The tribunal convened on beached hulls — old vessels run aground deliberately generations ago, their spines half-sunk in the delta mud, decks canted at angles that would have made me seasick if I'd been standing still. Ramps of planking connected them like the ribs of some enormous dead leviathan that the boatfolk had colonized instead of mourning. Lanterns hung from every yardarm despite the gray afternoon light, because, as I was learning, the river people did nothing by halves when law was on the table.

The Magpie slid into her berth among dozens of other boats, and I felt the deck shift under my feet as lines went taut — that particular settling, like an animal lying down.

I hadn't slept. Nobody had. We'd run the flood for two days straight, down back-channels Kestrel navigated from memory and what she called *the sandbar's mood*, which is not a sentence I ever expected to hear spoken with a straight face. We'd lost our cargo to the river — crates of salted fish and bolts of cloth, gone over the side when we nearly rolled in a flooded cut — but we still had the map, still had Elias's testimony in its oilskin wrapping against my chest, and still had every member of the crew alive, which Kestrel informed me was the only cargo ledger that mattered.

My father's map. My father's words. Nine years of believing he'd chosen the river over me, and it turned out he'd been carrying a debt the whole time — not money. *Testimony.* Proof that Magistrate Oren Vasse had forged land grants after the flood drowned Merrow's Landing, proof that the man who now owned half the valley's paper had built his fortune on the bones of a town.

And Corben Hale wanted it back.

* * *

The tribunal gathered at dusk, because the boatfolk held that law should be spoken when the lamps are lit, so no one can hide their face while they lie. I liked that immediately. It was the first legal custom I'd ever encountered that seemed designed for honesty rather than theater.

There were eleven elders, they called themselves Speakers, seated in a crescent on the deck of the largest hulk — a beached paddle-steamer named the *Charity*, though her wheelhouse was long gone and her nameplate was now home to a family of barnacles with strong squatter's rights. Around the crescent, packed onto every hull and rail and rope ladder within earshot, stood several hundred river people. Fisher families. Cargo runners. A woman who introduced herself only as the Eel-Wife of the Third Channel and looked at me like she could count my debts by sight.

Kestrel stood beside me at the foot of the Charity's ramp, and I noticed her hands were shaking.

"You're afraid," I said.

"I'm furious," she corrected. "Fear is for before. This is after." She nodded at the far bank, where three lanterns burned on Hale's gunboat, anchored just outside the delta proper — inside the boundary, technically, which everyone understood to be a message. "He'll make his move during the hearing. He always does. Men like Hale can't stand a room where they're not the loudest thing in it."

"Then we say our piece fast."

"We say it *true*," Kestrel said. "Fast is his language. True is ours."

The eldest Speaker — a small woman with a voice like a foghorn and hands like knotted rope — struck a boat-bell three times, and the delta went quiet in a way I have never heard any crowd go quiet. Not hushed. *Stopped.* Even the water seemed to hold still.

"Who stands for the drowned?" she called.

"I do." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Ada Vane, daughter of Elias Vane, prospector, late of Merrow's Landing by way of everywhere else."

"And what do you carry?"

I unwrapped the oilskin. The map went up onto the Charity's deck, weighted at the corners with river stones the Speakers provided, and I watched it catch the lantern light — my father's careful ink, the coordinates he'd whispered into fever, the drowned streets of Merrow's Landing drawn in a hand that shook and never stopped drawing anyway.

Beside it, I laid the sworn testimony. Elias had dictated it to the mission sister in his last week, in fragments, between coughs that sounded like the river eating a bank. His mark was at the bottom — he'd been too weak to write his name — pressed there with the last strength he had, and the sister had witnessed it, and two boatfolk who'd sheltered us on the headwaters had witnessed her witnessing. Under river law, apparently, that was ironclad. Under river law, apparently, a dying man's word carried more weight than a magistrate's seal, because the magistrate could buy seals and the dying man had nothing left to sell but truth.

I told them everything. The grants. The flood. How Vasse had redrawn the maps while the town's survivors were still pulling bodies out of the water, how he'd claimed the drowned land as *reclaimed* and sold it three times over, how anyone who objected had found their boats burned or their moorings cut or their names on a tax roll that grew teeth every year. I told them about Hale — the lock, the bargain he'd offered Kestrel, the fisher family's boats he'd put to the torch as an object lesson. In the crowd, I saw a woman go rigid at that, and someone put a hand on her shoulder, and I understood that the object lesson had been aimed at *her* family, and that she was standing here anyway.

The Speakers listened. That was the thing about them — they actually listened, the way my father used to listen to the river, like the answer was already there and they were just waiting for it to finish speaking.

* * *

Hale made his move, as Kestrel predicted, mid-hearing.

We heard the gunboat's engine before we saw it — a low, arrogant churn cutting across the delta's etiquette of oars and poles. He came in fast with two longboats behind him and a dozen men with cudgels and at least two rifles, and he didn't slow at the boundary markers, and he didn't slow at the moored skiffs, and he ran one of them down — a little green fishing punt that scattered its occupants into the shallows like startled birds.

"There it is," Kestrel said quietly, beside me. "His opening argument."

What happened next happened without any order being given, which is the part I keep turning over in my mind. The river people simply *moved*. Fishers poled out from the reeds in numbers I hadn't guessed at — thirty skiffs, fifty, more, materializing out of the channel grass like the delta itself growing teeth. Cargo boats swung their beams athwart the fairway. The Eel-Wife of the Third Channel stood up in her punt, planted a barbed eel-spear in the mud before her like a standard, and shouted something in boat-cant that needed no translation.

Hale's longboats hit that wall of hulls and stopped being an advance and started being a traffic jam. His men shoved; the river people shoved back, unhurried, immovable, the way water is immovable when it decides to be. One of Hale's riflemen raised his piece and found himself looking down the barrels of six fowling guns held by grandmothers who had clearly done this before.

And through all of it, the eldest Speaker rang her bell once, and said, in a voice that reached every corner of the Shambles:

"The court is in session. Violence interrupts at your peril, Corben Hale, son of nobody worth naming."

I have never heard an insult land so cleanly. Half the delta laughed. Hale's face did something complicated and ugly.

They pulled his men back eventually — dragged them off, really, by their own officers, because even enforcers can count, and the arithmetic on those sandbars was running badly against him. The gunboat withdrew to the boundary line and sat there sulking with its lanterns, and the hearing resumed as though a small war had not briefly broken out, because to the boatfolk, apparently, it hadn't. It had been a *procedural objection*, raised and overruled.

* * *

The verdict came at midnight.

The eldest Speaker stood, and the bell rang once, and she read it out in the flat cadence of something that has been true for a long time and has finally been said aloud. The grants of Magistrate Oren Vasse, derived from the drowning of Merrow's Landing, were void — every parcel, every deed, every scrap of paper bought with drowned men's fields. The land reverted to the descendants of the town, to be held under river law, which meant held in common, which meant held *forever*, because the river does not sell.

And Corben Hale — the Speaker's voice dropped into a register I felt in my sternum — was branded outlaw on the waters. No mooring, no market, no pilot, no passage. Any boat that fed him fed him at the cost of its own name. On the river, where a boat's name is its soul, that sentence was death with extra steps.

Hale heard it from his gunboat, close enough that the words crossed the water to him intact. I watched him stand at the rail, and I watched him understand, and for one unguarded moment his face held something I recognized — the look of a man realizing the current has turned and he never learned to read water.

Then the gunboat's engine started, and it backed away into the dark, and the delta lit every lamp it owned.

* * *

Later — much later, past the point where the celebration had worn itself down to embers and quiet singing on the hulls — I found Kestrel at the Magpie's rail, watching the lantern-light shiver on the brown water.

"You knew they'd rule for us," I said.

"I hoped." She turned the compass over in her hand — her father's compass, the one thing she kept from a life she rarely talked about. "Knowing and hoping are different cargoes. You carry both or you carry neither."

I stood next to her. Somewhere across the Shambles, the fisher family whose boats Hale had burned were being handed the keys to a new hull, funded by a tribunal tithe that had apparently existed for exactly this purpose for two centuries. Justice, rendered outside any courtroom, paid for in scars and lantern oil.

"My father would have liked this," I said. "All of it. The arguing especially."

"He'd have hated the attention and loved the outcome," Kestrel said. "Which makes him half boatfolk already."

I laughed, and it came out wet, and neither of us pretended otherwise.

But downriver, past the last of the lamps, the dark water lay open and waiting — and somewhere out in it, a branded man with nothing left to lose was learning exactly how much damage a drowning animal can do.

Kestrel saw it too. I felt her go still beside me, the way she went still before every hard reach of the river.

"He won't accept it," she said. "Men like Hale don't drown quietly. They grab for whatever's nearest."

The nearest thing was us.
