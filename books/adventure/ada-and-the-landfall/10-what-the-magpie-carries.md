## 10. What the Magpie Carries

The river forgave faster than I did.

Six weeks after the flood let go of the delta, the water had settled back into its old manners — polite, navigable, dotted with the usual liars of current and sandbar. The Magpie ran light cargo: rice, rope, a crate of chickens that hated all of us equally. No gold, no gunboats, no sealed maps sewn into oilcloth. Just work. Honest, tedious, glorious work.

Ada stood at the bow with her chart case open against the rail, pencil behind her ear, arguing with the river the way she argued with everything — patiently, and with the absolute certainty that she'd win eventually.

"Your old charts are wrong," she called back to me.

"Everyone's charts are wrong. That's the first thing I ever learned."

"Yours are *artistically* wrong. You've got a sandbar here that hasn't existed since before I was born."

"He had moods," I said. "I liked to remember him as he was."

She rolled her eyes and marked the correction anyway, in her own careful hand, with the depth soundings taken that morning and not inherited from some dead pilot's guesswork. That was the whole difference between her maps and every chart that had come before: she went and *looked*. Nine years of her father's silence had taught her that much, at least — that the truth of a thing is not what you're told, it's what you measure yourself, twice, and write down even when it costs you.

The tribunal's verdict had gone upriver ahead of us, passed boat to boat like a song everyone already half knew. The magistrate's grants: void. Merrow's Landing: restored to the descendants of the drowned, which mostly meant the boatfolk who'd never left. Corben Hale: branded outlaw, which on the river is not a legal term so much as a practical one. It means no lock opens for you, no landing sells you fuel, no pilot will take your coin, and every boy with a slingshot knows your face. He'd been seen once, far down the coast, trying to buy passage on a salt freighter. The freighter's captain had sold him a ticket to nowhere in particular and put him off at a mudflat. The river people are not cruel. We are just very, very thorough.

The magistrate himself had resigned in the night and been last seen boarding a steamer with four trunks and no dignity. The tribunal didn't chase him. The tribunal's opinion was that a man who steals land has already sentenced himself to live in a world where everyone knows what he is. I've heard harsher punishments. I've rarely heard better ones.

* * *

We came into Marchetti Landing — no relation, the name was there first, and I've decided to take it as a compliment — on a Tuesday, with the light going gold and the market smells doing their usual work on my appetite. Old Sefa waved us into the mooring she'd been holding for us since noon, which on this river is as close as anyone comes to saying *I missed you*.

"You look thinner," she said, taking our lines.

"I look *richer*. There's a difference. It's in the eyes."

"It's in the eyes," she agreed, and then, quieter, "They're saying what you did. Down at the delta. They're saying it all up and down the water now."

"They're exaggerating."

"They're saying you ran the flood through the back-channels with no chart and a gunboat on your stern."

"Also exaggerated. Ada had a chart. She'd just made it that morning."

Sefa laughed, and the sound of it did something in my chest I didn't examine too closely, because examining things too closely had been my whole problem for about nine years.

That's what the debt had been, if I'm honest — and the whole point of this chapter, if chapters can have points, is that I'm finally trying to be. When I was twenty-three I'd borrowed against the Magpie to buy her free of a salvage yard, and the terms had been the kind that grow teeth. Every season the ledger got heavier. Every season I told myself *next run*, *next cargo*, *one more job and I'm clear*. It was a fine arrangement, as far as my creditors were concerned, because a woman rowing to pay off yesterday never looks up to see where she's going.

Elias Vane had looked at that ledger in my cabin — I never learned how he knew it was there, dying men have a nose for other people's debts — and said, "You know it isn't money, don't you."

I'd told him he was feverish.

He'd smiled like a man who'd been feverish for years and had made his peace with the view. "It's the *owing*. You've been owing so long you built a life on the foundation of it. Take the debt away and who's Kestrel Marchetti?"

I'd hated him for it. I'd hated him for roughly a month, which was about how long he had left, and then I'd hated him for another month after that because grief is generous that way.

* * *

Ada found me at the stern that evening with the ledger on my knees.

She didn't say anything at first. She'd gotten good at that — at reading the weather of my silences and knowing which ones wanted company and which ones wanted to be left to rain themselves out. She sat down on the coil of line beside me, opened her chart case, and went on inking soundings by the last of the light, which was her way of sitting with me without making a ceremony of it.

"You're going to burn it," she said eventually.

"I'm considering it."

"You've been considering it for three days. I can smell the lamp oil."

"It's a big decision. There's a *process*."

"There's a tin," she said, "and a ledger, and a match, and a river that's been waiting to hear the end of this story as long as I have."

I looked at her. Nineteen years old, ink on her fingers, her father's stubbornness worn like a coat that finally fit. Somewhere in the last few months she'd stopped being cargo and become crew, and somewhere in the last few weeks crew had quietly become something the old charts had no symbol for. Partner, I supposed, if I was going to be honest, and honesty was apparently the theme of the season.

"Equal shares," I said. "That's the offer. Not because you earned it — though you did — but because a boat run by one stubborn person is a liability, and I'm tired of being a liability."

Ada's pencil stopped. For a moment she was nine years younger, standing on some dock waiting for a father who didn't come, and I watched her decide, right there in front of me, not to be that girl anymore. She held out her hand. Her grip was strong and ink-stained and certain.

"Equal shares," she said. "And I get the chart case."

"You'd get the chart case anyway. You already took it."

"I wanted it on the record."

* * *

The ledger burned well.

That's the thing nobody tells you about paper debts — they burn exactly as easily as they were written, all those years of interest and penalties and the small humiliations of the payment book, and the fire doesn't distinguish between them. It took eleven minutes. I fed it page by page, and Ada sat with me, and the river carried the ash off downstream the way it carries everything, without judgment and without memory, which is more than I can say for myself.

I kept one thing. Not the ledger, not the deed, not even the last of Elias's savings, which went to the mission at the headwaters where he'd died, with a note that said only *he paid his passage in full*. What I kept was the compass. His compass — brass, salt-scarred, the needle drunk on every storm it had ever survived. He'd pressed it into my hand at that muddy landing the day we met, before I'd even said yes, as if he'd known I would.

"A boat's got two ways to know where she is," he'd told me. "The chart and the needle. The chart tells you where you've been. The needle tells you what you're pointed at." He'd coughed, and grinned, and ruined the moment beautifully. "Most people spend their whole lives confusing the two."

I hung the compass over the Magpie's wheel, where it catches the lamplight at night and swings a little when the current talks to the hull. Some nights I stand there and watch it and think about the man who gave it to me, who'd spent his last season of life buying back, inch by inch, a truth he'd helped bury — because the flood that drowned Merrow's Landing hadn't been only weather. He'd surveyed those grants for the magistrate. He'd drawn the lines on paper that erased a town. And then he'd spent nine years away from his daughter, and a fortune, and finally his life, making the map that un-drew them.

Testimony. That's all it ever was. The most dangerous cargo on the river, and I'd carried it in a false bottom under a false manifest, and it had turned out to weigh nothing at all, and everything.

* * *

The Magpie left Marchetti Landing at dawn with the mist still on the water, and I'll tell you what she carried, since you've been kind enough to read this far.

Rice and rope. A crate of chickens, still furious. Ada's charts, forty sheets and growing, every sounding honest, every sandbar drawn as it is and not as legend prefers. A compass that belonged to a man who paid his debts the hard way. A crew of five who'd run a flood together and come out the other side with the particular affection of people who've seen each other terrified and kept it to themselves.

And no debt. No ledger. No anchor.

We came around the bend below the landing and the river opened up ahead of us — wide, brown, glittering, going where it goes. Ada stood at the bow with the wind pulling her hair loose, and she didn't look back at the town, and neither did I. I'd spent so many years thinking home was a place I'd get back to when the books were balanced. It turns out home had been standing next to me the whole time, arguing about sandbars, holding out an ink-stained hand.

"Hard to starboard," Ada called. "There's a bar forming off the point. Your old charts missed it."

"Of course they did." I turned the wheel. "New water, new maps."

The Magpie leaned into the current, the compass swung true, and we ran on — light, paid-up, and pointed at whatever came next.
