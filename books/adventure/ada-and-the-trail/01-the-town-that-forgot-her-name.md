## 1. The Town That Forgot Her Name

The trouble with inventing a new life, Beatrix had learned, was that you eventually had to live in it. And living in it meant mornings like this one: kneeling in the bilge of the *Merry Adeline*, the Kessel's Landing ferry, elbow-deep in water that smelled of fish and diesel, coaxing a fuel line back into submission while a spider watched her from the bulkhead with what she chose to interpret as professional respect.

"You're not helping," she told the spider.

The spider declined to comment.

She tightened the coupling, wiped her hands on a rag that had once been white and was now the color of every job she'd ever done, and sat back on her heels. Through the engine hatch above her, morning light came down in a slanted gold column, and dust motes drifted through it like slow snow. Somewhere overhead, boots thumped across the deck as the first commuters of the day took their places — the flour merchant's boy, two sisters who sold eels at the market and argued about it constantly, old Pem with his cane and his opinions about everything.

This was her life now. Grease under the fingernails. A kettle in the corner of the workshop that whistled slightly flat. A landlady named Mrs. Hobb who left turnips on her doorstep without explanation and expected no thanks, only that the turnips be eaten.

Seven years since the Veyl headwaters. Seven years since the name on her papers stopped being hers. Seven years of being nobody, which — she would admit this to no one, least of all herself — had turned out to be a kind of rest.

"Bea!" The voice came down the hatch, accompanied by a shower of rust flakes. "Bea, the portside pump's doing the thing again."

"The thing," Beatrix called back, "is called cavitation, Doss, and it's called that because you keep running the ferry aground on sandbars you could see from the moon."

"I can't see the moon from the wheelhouse, it's daytime."

Beatrix closed her eyes briefly, the way she did six or seven times a day, and climbed up into the light.

Doss Ferrow was twenty-three, all elbows and enthusiasm, with hair that stuck up as though permanently alarmed. She'd taken him on two winters ago when he'd shown up asking for work and demonstrated, unprompted, that he could lift an engine block. He could not fix an engine block, but he could lift one, and in Kessel's Landing that was considered half the trade.

"Show me," she said.

He showed her. It was, in fact, cavitation, caused by a fouled intake screen, which he could have cleared himself if he'd ever once looked at the intake screen. She cleared it. He watched with the rapt attention of a man witnessing surgery.

"How do you know all this stuff?" he asked.

"Books," said Beatrix.

"Which books?"

"All of them."

He grinned, satisfied, and went back to the wheelhouse. That was the other thing about being nobody: people believed simple answers. Nobody looked too hard at a ferry mechanic who read. Kessel's Landing was a town that had decided, collectively and without malice, not to be curious about her. They knew she'd come from somewhere upriver, because everyone came from somewhere, and they knew she didn't talk about it, because nobody did. The town had filed her away under *competent, keeps to herself, good with engines* and shut the drawer.

She had built that drawer herself, plank by plank, and she slept well inside it.

Mostly.

* * *

The supply expedition had been assembling at the north docks for three days, and Beatrix had made a point of not looking at it.

This required discipline. Kessel's Landing lived by the river, and anything happening on the river was automatically the town's business — its entertainment, its gossip, its communal novel read aloud in installments at the Anchor & Eel. A proper expedition, with crates and canvas and hired muscle, was better than a festival. By the second day the whole town had opinions about the tents.

Beatrix had opinions too. She kept them below decks, where she kept everything.

But on the morning they were due to cast off, the ferry schedule betrayed her. The *Merry Adeline*'s first crossing put her at the north dock at half past seven, exactly when the expedition was loading its last crates, and there was nowhere to look that wasn't them.

There were four boats. Not rafts, not barges — proper expedition hulls, shallow-drafted, reinforced at the bow for rock work. She counted the crew from across the water: eleven visible, probably fourteen total. Good number. Sensible. Crates marked with the stenciled crest of the Corvane Supply Consortium, which meant money behind them, which meant insurance, which meant someone upstream had signed off on the route.

The route.

She felt it before she let herself confirm it — a coldness starting low in her stomach, the old animal instinct that had kept her alive in places where maps gave up. The lead boat's pilot stood at the helm checking his soundings against a chart case, and even at this distance she recognized the posture: a man about to take the cut on the river's east side past Gannet Rock rather than the west channel.

Everyone took the west channel. The east cut was faster. The east cut was also where the river lied.

She told herself it wasn't her business. She told herself this while tying off the ferry's stern line, while collecting fares from the eel sisters mid-argument, while waving to Pem, who waved back and immediately resumed telling Doss about the flood of '09. She told herself it all the way through the crossing, and then she made the mistake of looking back.

The lead boat was moving. Behind it, the second and third, then the fourth, a tidy procession heading upriver into the morning haze, toward the narrows, toward the falls country, toward the gorge.

Toward the source of the Veyl.

"Well," said Mrs. Hobb's voice in her memory, in the tone she used for turnip-related announcements, "there goes that lot. Off to the headwaters, they say. Supply run for the mining survey." A pause, in memory, delicate as a knife laid on a table. "Dangerous water up there, I hear. River's eaten better expeditions than that one."

Mrs. Hobb had never been within two hundred miles of the headwaters. Mrs. Hobb got most of her information from the fishmonger, who got his from a man who claimed to have once seen the Veyl's source and described it, variously, as a hole, a wall, and "a great deal of ice." But the town repeated these things comfortably, the way people repeat weather, and Beatrix had spent seven years letting them.

The river's eaten better expeditions than that one.

She stood at the ferry rail long after the boats had shrunk to specks, long after Doss had asked her twice if she wanted tea and once whether she was ill. The monsoon season was coming — it always came, but the clouds this year had been building wrong, stacking themselves high and green-black over the western ranges a full month early. Any guide worth her salt could read it. Any guide worth her salt would have told those men to wait.

I'm not a guide, she reminded herself. I'm a mechanic. My salt is for de-icing the ferry deck in winter.

The coldness in her stomach did not agree.

* * *

That evening she walked home along the levee, as she always did, past the drying nets and the boatyards and the shrine some fisherman's widow maintained to the River's Mercy, which consisted of a chipped statue, a bowl of coins, and a permanent smell of incense and carp. The town was settling into its evening noises — pots, dogs, the Anchor & Eel warming up its fiddle player. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds.

At the top of the levee she allowed herself one look upriver.

The Veyl ran wide and brown-green here, lazy as a sleeping dog, so unlike what it became ninety miles north that a stranger would never believe they were the same water. Up there it narrowed, quickened, turned white-toothed and loud. Up there it ran through the Serrated Narrows, where the cliffs leaned in close enough that the sky became a ribbon; past the falls that no portage had ever made easy; into the gorge country where the company charts simply stopped, the cartographers having been instructed, politely and permanently, that there was nothing further worth drawing.

Nothing worth drawing. She almost laughed. There had been plenty worth drawing. That had been rather the point.

She had drawn it herself, once. Every mile of it. Her own hand on her own charts, the best survey of the upper Veyl that had ever existed, and every sheet of it confiscated or burned or locked in a company archive under seal, along with the report, the testimony, and the names of the dead.

Four names. She still kept them the way other people kept prayer beads, turning them over at night: Aldous. Wren. The twins, Hallam and Cade. And Merrick, the radio operator, whose last transmission had come apart in the middle of a word and never finished.

The inquiry had needed someone to blame. The company had needed the blame to wear a face small enough to bury. Hers had fit.

So she had come downstream, changed her name the way you change a shirt, and let the river take the rest. Kessel's Landing had obliged by forgetting her — or rather, by never bothering to learn her, which amounted to the same mercy.

She was halfway down the levee steps when she saw the girl.

Not unusual, girls on the dock. But this one wasn't fishing or idling. She stood at the end of the north pier with a travel bag at her feet and a folded paper in her hand, and she was asking questions of anyone who passed — Beatrix could see the shape of it in people's body language, the way they shook their heads and moved along. The girl asked anyway. Asked the next one. And the next.

Something about her made Beatrix stop on the stairs. The set of the shoulders, maybe. Stubbornness has a silhouette; she'd spent enough years reading it in young guides to know it on sight. This girl had been refused a dozen times tonight and was visibly planning to be refused a dozen more.

Then the girl turned, and the lamplight caught her face, and Beatrix felt the coldness in her stomach move up into her chest — because there was something familiar in it. Something in the jaw, the brow. A resemblance she couldn't place and very much did not want to place.

The girl consulted her paper again, squinting at it, then at the darkened workshops along the quay. Then, inevitably, at the *Merry Adeline*, moored and dark except for the single lamp Beatrix always left burning in the wheelhouse window.

The girl started toward the ferry.

Beatrix stood very still on the levee stairs, a woman with grease on her hands and a false name on her door, watching seventeen years' worth of bad luck walk toward her across the planks.

She could go inside. Lock the workshop. In the morning the girl would be gone, or would have found some other fool, and the drawer would slide shut again, and the calm would hold.

The girl stopped at the foot of the gangplank, looked up at the lamp in the wheelhouse window, and squared those stubborn shoulders.

Beatrix exhaled slowly, and did not go inside.
