## 2. Ada at the Dock

The ferryman at Kessel's Landing had a laugh like a rusted hinge and no interest whatsoever in helping her.

"Guides?" he said, spitting a stream of tobacco into the brown water. "Girl, you want a guide upriver in monsoon season, you want a priest too. One to say the words over your body."

"I'll take my chances with the river," Ada said.

"You'll take them anyway." He shrugged and went back to coiling rope, which he did with the leisurely air of a man who had never once been in a hurry in his life.

Ada stood on the dock of Kessel's Landing and let the town introduce itself. It was smaller than she'd imagined from her father's letters — a crooked spine of buildings along the water's edge, all weather-gray shingles and sagging porches, the kind of place where the river was not scenery but employer, landlord, and occasional executioner. Fishing nets hung drying like enormous tattered flags. Somewhere a dog was barking at something with tremendous conviction. The air smelled of fish, tar, and woodsmoke, and beneath it all, faintly, the green mineral breath of the Veyl itself, coming down from mountains she could not yet see.

Her father was somewhere up that river. Or he wasn't, and she refused to finish the thought.

Seventeen days since the last radio relay from the Vance Supply Expedition. Seventeen days of static shaped like her father's voice saying *all's well* and then nothing shaped like anything at all. The company office in Corvane had sent two polite telegrams and then, when she'd shown up in person, one very impolite clerk who explained that expeditions went quiet sometimes, it was the nature of the trade, and surely a young lady would want to be home with her mother.

Her mother was dead. Her father was missing. *Home* was a word with nothing inside it, like an empty crate.

So: Kessel's Landing. Last town before the gorges. Every guide on the Veyl passed through here eventually, like water through a funnel.

She'd been in town four hours and had already collected six refusals.

The first guide had been polite about it. The second had been honest — "monsoon's coming, girl, nobody runs the upper Veyl this time of year for love or money." The third had laughed. The fourth through sixth had achieved a kind of collective rudeness that suggested they'd discussed her between appointments. The seventh was the ferryman, who at least had the decency to be entertaining about it.

"You could point me toward someone who *hasn't* said no yet," she tried.

He considered this, chewing. "Brannick's drunk till noon. After noon he's drunk and lying."

"That's everyone?"

"That's everyone worth the name. And half the ones who aren't worth it left on the supply run three weeks back, if you follow me." He gave her a look that was almost kind. "Your folk on that expedition?"

"My father leads it."

The rope-coiling stopped. Just for a moment. Then resumed, slower. "Ah," he said. "Well." And Ada understood that she had just become a tragedy, a small local story, something to be told over beers by Thursday. *Girl came looking for her da. Nobody'd take her upriver. Shame.*

She was not going to be a story. She was going to be a rescue.

* * *

By afternoon she had worn a groove in the dock boards between the chandlery and the tavern called the Drowned Rat, which she felt was an unnecessarily honest name. She'd posted a notice on the chandlery board — REWARD. GUIDE WANTED. URGENT AND SERIOUS INQUIRIES ONLY — and watched a fisherman read it, snort, and walk away with the particular gait of a man fleeing before he could be recruited.

It was while she was re-inking her pen against the chandlery counter, haggling with the owner over the price of a second sheet of paper (extortionate; everything here was extortionate; she suspected the entire town ran on extorting passing expeditions), that she saw the tattoo.

The woman came out of the alley behind the chandlery carrying a crate of engine parts like it weighed nothing, which told Ada two things: she was strong, and she worked with boats rather than on them. She was perhaps forty, dark-haired with gray coming in at the temples, sun-browned, with the rolled sleeves and forearms of someone who spent her life elbow-deep in machinery. She set the crate down on the dock beside a disassembled outboard motor, wiped her hands on a rag, and turned —

— and there, on the inside of her left forearm, below a faded sunburn line, was the mark.

Ada knew it the way you know your own front door. She'd grown up under it. It was inked into the spine of every atlas her father owned, stamped on every crate in their warehouse, embroidered on the flag that hung in the entryway at home: a mountain peak above three wavy lines, the crest of the Corvane Survey & Supply Company. Except this one was wrong — older, cruder, hand-pricked rather than professionally done, the way sailors and soldiers used to do it, with a needle and soot and more stubbornness than sense.

And beneath the peak, where the company crest read *CORVANE*, this one read something else. A word she couldn't see from the dock. A name.

Ada's pen stopped moving.

Because here was the thing — the thing she'd learned in seventeen days of asking questions in offices and taverns and one memorable incident with a company archivist who'd threatened to have her removed: seven years ago, an expedition had gone up the Veyl to its source and come back without most of its people. Four dead. The leader blamed. Struck off every roll, blacklisted in every port, name scrubbed from the company books so thoroughly that even the archivist had claimed — with a sweat on his brow that suggested otherwise — never to have heard it.

Ada had spent three weeks digging for that name. She'd found everything around it, a silhouette of absence, a hole shaped exactly like a person. The disaster had a date, a boat manifest, a list of the dead. The leader had a rank, a reputation, a ruin. But the name itself had been cut out of the record like a tumor.

And now here it was, pricked into a stranger's arm in a river town at the end of the world, walking around in broad daylight fixing boat engines.

The woman caught her staring. Most people would have looked away. This one didn't; she looked back, gray-eyed and level, with the patience of someone waiting out weather.

"You're dripping," the woman said.

Ada looked down. Ink had pooled off her pen nib onto the chandlery counter and was running toward the owner's ledger. She blotted it with her sleeve, which was already ruined, and decided the sleeve was a fair price.

"That mark," Ada said. "On your arm. That's the old Corvane survey crest. Pre-standardization. They changed the design after the—" she stopped herself, "—after some years ago. Where did you get it?"

Something moved behind the woman's eyes. Not much. A shutter adjusting.

"Got it young and stupid," she said. "Like most tattoos."

"The word underneath. What does it say?"

"It says *none of your business*." But she said it without heat, almost wearily, the way you repeat something you've said many times, and Ada — who had spent seventeen days being lied to by professionals and had developed a nose for it — noticed that this wasn't quite a lie. It was a deflection. Lies were built; deflections were bought off the shelf.

"You worked for Corvane," Ada pressed. "Survey side, not supply. You knew the upper Veyl."

"Everyone here worked for somebody once." The woman picked up the rag again, folded it, unfolded it. "You're the girl looking for a guide. Word travels faster than the current in this town."

"Then you know why I'm asking."

"I know you're asking the wrong person. I fix engines. Engines don't have opinions about rivers." She hoisted the crate again, and Ada saw the conversation closing like a door.

"Fourteen days," Ada said quickly. "That's how long since anyone's heard from my father's expedition. The company won't act for another month, minimum — I asked, I made them show me the protocol, they file a report, the report waits for review, the review waits for—" Her voice cracked, traitorously, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Every day I stand here arguing with people is a day further upriver he might be needing help. So I'm not going to stop asking. I'm going to ask everyone in this town, including you, and then I'm going to start asking the boats, and if no one will take me I will buy a canoe and paddle it myself, badly, and probably drown, and then it will be on your conscience specifically, because you knew the river and said nothing."

Silence. Down the dock, the rusted-hinge ferryman had stopped pretending to work and was watching with open enjoyment, the absolute vulture.

The woman set the crate down again.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Ada. Ada Vance."

Something flickered — recognition? No. Not of her. Of the surname, maybe, of the expedition notices that must have come through here weeks ago. Or of something else entirely, some private arithmetic.

"And your father's expedition went silent at—"

"The last relay station above the Narrows. Seventeen days ago now. Eighteen tomorrow."

The woman looked upriver, though from the dock you couldn't see much of the Veyl — just the bend past the fish racks, the water sliding north between hills that got steadily less friendly the farther they went. When she spoke again her voice had changed. Flatter. Careful. A voice walking on ice and testing each step.

"Go home, Ada Vance."

"I don't have one."

"Then go anywhere else."

"No."

"It's not a negotiation."

"Neither is mine." Ada planted her feet on the dock boards, which were slimy and uneven and nearly took her down, undermining her position considerably, but she recovered. "You have that mark because you were survey corps. You know what eighteen days of silence means. You know better than any of these guides who've never been past the second gorge. And you know — you *know* — that whoever goes up there needs to go soon, before the monsoon, or not at all until spring. Spring is too late. You know that too."

The woman's jaw tightened. For a moment Ada thought she'd won — thought she'd seen the crack of conscience open behind those gray eyes.

Then the woman picked up the crate, walked past her, and set it down in the bed of a waiting skiff without another word.

"Hey!" Ada followed. "I'm talking to you!"

"No, you're reciting," the woman said, finally turning. Up close, in the flat afternoon light, Ada could see the fine web of scars on her hands, the burn scar disappearing under her collar, the deep tiredness carved around her mouth. "You've got a speech. It's a good speech. You've clearly given it six times today and it's gotten better with practice. But I'm not a guide. I'm not anything. And whatever you think that tattoo makes me, it doesn't make me yours."

"It makes you someone who knows the route."

"It makes me someone who made a mistake once and has been paying for it ever since." She said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Take the ferryman's advice. Take anyone's advice. Go home, wait for the company, let men with insurance and regulations handle it."

"The company buried the last expedition's leader alive," Ada shot back. "I've read the files. Whatever happened up there seven years ago, they didn't even hold a real inquiry. They just — erased her. So forgive me if 'let the company handle it' doesn't sound like a plan."

The effect was immediate and strange. The woman went very still — not angry-still, but struck-still, like a bell that had been hit. The ferryman, shamelessly eavesdropping ten feet away, suddenly found urgent business with his rope.

"You've read the files," the woman repeated quietly.

"All of them. Everything Corvane kept, which wasn't much, because they burned the rest." Ada stepped closer, lowering her voice, watching the woman's face the way her father had taught her to watch water — reading the surface for what moved underneath. "They erased her name so completely that even I couldn't find it. Three weeks of digging. Do you know how that feels? To know someone exists only as a hole in a record?"

"Yes," the woman said, barely audible.

"So whoever she is — whatever she did — she's the only person alive who knows that stretch of river well enough to get there fast and come back out. And she's apparently standing right in front of me, fixing engines, pretending to be nobody." Ada swallowed. Her heart was going like a paddle wheel. "I don't care what you did. I don't care what they say you did. My father is up that river, and I am going after him, and I would very much rather not drown doing it. Help me."

For a long moment the only sounds were the water slapping the pilings and the dog, still barking at whatever it had been barking at for the last hour, committed to the bit.

Then the woman laughed — short, humorless, aimed mostly at herself.

"You really won't stop, will you."

"No."

"They warned me about people like you. In another life." She rubbed the tattoo absently with her thumb, an old habit, a wound worried by its owner. "Listen to me carefully, Ada Vance, because I'm only saying this once. I am not that person anymore. That person is dead, and I intend to keep her that way. I will not guide you upriver. I will not tell you about the river. I will not so much as draw you a map. If you find some other poor fool to take you, that's your funeral, arranged and paid for. Do you understand?"

Ada understood perfectly. She also understood — with the same certainty she'd felt the moment she'd seen the crest inked into that forearm — that she had just found the only person in a hundred miles who could save her father, and that the woman knew it too, and that neither of those facts had yet finished winning the argument.

"Understood," Ada said sweetly. "What's your name, at least? For the record. Since you're not anything."

A pause. The gray eyes narrowed, seeing the trap a half-second too late, or pretending to.

"Sable," the woman said at last. "Sable Thackeray."

Ada wrote it down in her notebook, right beneath the sketch of the tattoo she'd already drawn from memory, and underlined it twice.

It was, she would reflect later, the first lie she'd ever caught Sable Thackeray telling — though it would take her a long time, and cost her a great deal, to learn what the truth had been.

* * *

That evening, Ada sat on the end of the dock with her boots off and her notebook open, working through the day's intelligence by lamplight. Six refusals, one non-refusal-that-was-a-refusal, one name that didn't exist in any record she could access, and one tattoo that said otherwise.

*Sable Thackeray.* She'd checked the chandlery's ledger of licensed boat workers — the owner had finally relented, mostly to make her leave — and there it was, entered fourteen months ago, unremarkable, one line among dozens. But the handwriting of the registrar had a small asterisk beside it, the kind clerks use for *verified elsewhere*, and when she'd asked, the chandlery owner had gotten vague and sweaty and mentioned something about papers from downriver, references, all in order, no need to pry.

Nobody arrives in Kessel's Landing with *references*. People arrive here escaping things.

Ada clicked her pen shut and looked upriver, where the first stars were coming out over the blackening hills, and somewhere beyond them, past the Narrows, past the gorges, past the last relay station with its seventeen days of silence, her father was either waiting or—

*Waiting,* she told herself firmly. *He's waiting.*

Behind her, across the cove, a single lamp still burned in the boathouse where the engine-woman worked. Ada could see her silhouette moving among the tools, steady and solitary, a person who had built herself a life out of small mechanical certainties because the big ones had failed her.

"Whatever your name is," Ada murmured to the silhouette, "you're coming with me."

The silhouette, mercifully, could not hear her. Yet.

Ada put her boots back on, closed the notebook, and went to find out everything the town of Kessel's Landing knew about Sable Thackeray — starting with why the customs officer at the river gate had gone pale when she'd mentioned the name.
