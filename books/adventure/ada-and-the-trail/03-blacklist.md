## 3. Blacklist

Beatrix slept badly, which was nothing new, but she slept badly in a new key.

For seven years her nightmares had been the standard inventory: white water, a rope going slack, a voice calling her name that wasn't her name anymore. Last night's had been different. Last night's had been arithmetic. She'd dreamed of tonnage and fuel ratios and the specific angle at which the Veyl bends around Widow's Tooth, and she'd woken at dawn with the unwelcome understanding that some part of her mind had already said yes to Ada Vance and was simply waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

She dealt with this the way she dealt with most problems: by hitting it with a wrench.

The *Maren K* needed her lower bearing repacked, and Beatrix was elbow-deep in grease up to the shoulder when Ada Vance appeared on the dock for the second morning running, carrying two paper bags of buns and an expression like a woman arriving to collect a debt.

"You didn't answer my question yesterday," Ada said.

"I answered it fine. The answer was no."

"The answer was a list of reasons why no. That's not the same thing."

Beatrix sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on a rag that was, if anything, dirtier than her hands. "Girl, I've been turning down work since before you could swim. I'm good at it. Ask anyone."

"Nobody here remembers you doing anything but fixing boats."

"Exactly." Beatrix stood, joints cracking in a way that felt editorial. "That's the whole point of me."

Ada set one of the paper bags down on the gunwale of the *Maren K*, which was either a bribe or a hostage situation involving pastry. "I brought you breakfast."

"I noticed."

"It's got honey butter."

"I can see that from here." Beatrix looked at the bag. The bag looked back. This was, she reflected grimly, exactly how they got you. Not with arguments—with honey butter. "You're wasting your money. And your time. There are four licensed guides in this town and every one of them has run the upper Veyl more recently than—"

"Every one of them refused me," Ada said flatly. "Old Sef crossed himself. The Ferrow brothers suddenly remembered appointments. One of them started sweeping his porch at six in the morning just so he'd have something to be doing when I knocked. So don't tell me about the guides, Ms. Thackeray. The guides have all decided the river ate my father, and the river doesn't give refunds."

Beatrix said nothing. Downstream, a barge horn moaned its slow complaint against the bridge pilings, and somewhere a gull made the sound gulls make when they've found something dead and want everyone to know about it.

"Your father knew what he was running into," Beatrix said finally. It came out harder than she meant it to, and she watched it land, and she didn't take it back, because taking things back was how you ended up on rivers you'd sworn off.

"Then he knew, and he went anyway, and he hasn't radioed in eleven days." Ada's voice didn't crack. That was somehow worse. "You looked at that boat leaving yesterday like you were watching a funeral procession. You knew the route. You knew something. And now you're going to stand there with your wrench and your honey butter and pretend you're nobody."

"I am nobody," Beatrix said. "It took me years. It's my best work."

They stared at each other across the greasy deck of the *Maren K*. Ada picked up her bag of buns, apparently deciding against feeding the enemy, and Beatrix felt something twist in her chest that she chose to identify as relief.

"Ms. Thackeray." A voice from the dock steps, smooth as river mud. "Got a minute?"

Customs Officer Brannic came down the planks with his ledger under his arm and his hat tilted at the precise angle of a man who enjoys his own authority more than his salary. He was broad, pink, and meticulously clean in a town where clean lasted about nine minutes. Beatrix had dealt with him twice in seven years—once over a landing fee, once over a misunderstanding involving Juno's "spices"—and both times he had looked at her the way a man looks at a locked box he suspects contains money.

"Officer," she said.

"Didn't mean to interrupt the—" He glanced at Ada, then back. "Family discussion."

"We're not family."

"Shame. Family's important." He opened the ledger with the ceremony of a priest unveiling relics. "Funny thing. Had a circular come through from the provincial office last week. Routine stuff, mostly. Stolen outboard motors, a counterfeit stamp ring operating out of Harlow Bend." He turned a page. "And then there's this."

He held the ledger out. Beatrix didn't take it. He held it a moment longer, then read aloud anyway, savoring it:

"'Advisory to all river jurisdictions: subject formerly registered as B. Dravenmoor, guide license 4-117, revoked following the Veyl Source expedition of—' well. You remember the year better than I do, I expect. 'Subject believed relocated. Any vessel, cargo, or passenger contract entered into under subject's current alias may be deemed void, and subject liable for—' et cetera, et cetera. Fraud statutes. Impersonation. They get quite creative when they're embarrassed, the provincial office. Somebody upstream cost them a lot of money once, and they never forget a face."

The dock went very quiet. Even the gull shut up.

"Brannic," Beatrix said pleasantly, "if you've got something to say, say it. If you've got paperwork, serve it. If you've got neither, the *Maren K*'s owner is expecting her back by noon and I don't work for free."

"Oh, I'm not serving anything." He closed the ledger and patted it fondly. "I'm offering a service. See, this advisory comes with a response form. Jurisdictions confirm whether the subject is present. Most officers file theirs late. Some lose them entirely." He smiled. "I'm a very thorough officer, Ms. Thackeray. But thoroughness takes time, and time is expensive, and a man in my position has to think about retirement."

Ada had gone pale. "You're shaking him down."

"Miss Vance." Brannic turned to her with genuine warmth. "Your father's expedition posted bond with my office. Eleven days of silence means that bond converts to a recovery claim, and recovery claims need a licensed lead guide. Which is why you've been knocking on doors." He looked back at Beatrix. "And why you'll keep getting knocked on, unless our friend here leaves town. Quietly. Before the monsoon season makes everything official and complicated."

"There it is," Beatrix said.

"There it is," Brannic agreed comfortably.

She looked past him, downriver, toward the gray-green line where the Veyl bent away north and the country stopped being anybody's and started being the river's. Seven years ago she had come down that water with three survivors and a dead man's boots, and she had sworn—out loud, on the bank, half-drowned—that she would never go up it again. The vow had held beautifully. Vows hold best when nothing tests them.

"Give us a minute, Officer," she said.

"Take two." Brannic tipped his hat and climbed the steps, unhurried, a man who owned the stairs and knew it.

Ada waited until his footsteps faded. "You're not actually considering—"

"Don't tell me what I'm considering." Beatrix sat down hard on the *Maren K*'s rail, which groaned in solidarity. "Sit down, girl. No—stand. Standing's better for what I'm about to say, because if you sit you'll argue, and if you stand you might listen."

Ada stood.

"Seven years ago I led an expedition to the source of the Veyl. Nine people went up. Four came down. The company that hired me put every failure on my name, and the guild struck me off, and the province blacklisted me, and I let them, because the alternative was telling a story I couldn't make anyone believe." Her voice stayed level. She'd practiced level for seven years; it was the one skill the disaster had left her. "That's the shape of it. The details stay mine. But you deserve to know who you've been begging."

Ada's jaw worked. "My uncle Rennick was on a source expedition. Seven years ago."

The word landed like a dropped anchor. Beatrix closed her eyes. Of course. Of course the river would fold the map like that. "Rennick Vance," she said quietly. "Rope lead. He tied off the line at Gray Shallows that saved two of the four. I never got to tell his family that."

"He never came home."

"No. He didn't." Beatrix opened her eyes. "So understand what I'm saying when I tell you the honest answer to your question isn't no. The honest answer is that I'm the worst possible person to help you and the only possible person who will. And both of those things are true because of the same stretch of water."

"That's not an answer either."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "But it's the last free one you're getting." She stood, and the old ache in her knee—the one that always flared when she thought about the gorge—flared right on schedule, punctual as rent. "Here's my terms, and they're not negotiable, and if you agree to them and then renegotiate I will turn the boat around mid-current and you can swim home to your principles. I take you upriver as far as Relay Nine. The last working station before the falls. If there's any sign of your father's boat below Nine—if we find wreckage, a cache, a note in a tin—I'll follow it as far as the evidence goes. But I do not go above Nine. Not to the falls, not to the portage, not to the source valley. That country is closed to me in every way a place can be closed. You get my hands, my boat sense, and my eyes. You do not get my ghosts. Are those terms?"

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Then: "If we find proof he went higher?"

"Then we'll discuss it like adults, on a riverbank, with the weather closing in, and we'll both hate it." Beatrix stuck out her grease-blackened hand. "Well?"

Ada took it. Her grip was stronger than it looked, and dry, and steady. "Terms accepted, Ms. Thackeray."

"Beatrix. If we're going to die together on the same boat, you may as well use my name."

"Which one?"

Beatrix snorted despite herself. "Careful. You're funnier than your father ever was, and he owed me money too."

The rest of the day went the way days go when a decision has been made and the body resents it: fast, loud, and full of lists.

Beatrix wrote hers on the back of a repair invoice. Boat—she'd need something shallower than the *Maren K*, something that could take rock strikes and be flipped by two people. The *Corbie*, then, old Halloran's derelict skiff, if she could talk him down from his insulting opinion of its worth. Provisions for ten days, plus thirty percent, because rivers collected interest. Rope—two hundred feet of new line, none of the rotting salvage the chandlery tried to pass off on anyone desperate enough to buy it. Fuel. Charts, such as they were, though the charts above Widow's Tooth were less maps than rumors with contour lines.

And a crew. Because she couldn't row the *Corbie* up the Narrows alone, and Ada Vance, whatever her other qualities, had hands that had clearly never met a rope professionally.

By evening she'd walked the whole waterfront twice, and the waterfront had looked back at her with the particular expression of a small town watching a scandal assemble itself. Word traveled faster than water here. By the time she reached the Salt Larder, Juno was already standing in the doorway with her arms crossed, wearing the look of a woman who has been waiting to be asked and intends to make the asking expensive.

"I heard," Juno said.

"You heard wrong, probably."

"I heard you're going upriver." Juno's eyes glittered. "I heard the Vance girl finally found somebody crazy enough. And I heard Brannic spent an hour on your dock this morning smiling like a cat that's found the cream *and* the can opener."

"Juno."

"I'm just saying, a woman in your position wants to watch her—"

"Juno. I need your maps."

That stopped her. Juno's smuggler's charts were the worst-kept secret on the waterfront, drawn from runs she'd never admitted to, marking channels and cutoffs and sandbars that shifted yearly and that no licensed surveyor would dignify with ink. Officially they didn't exist. Unofficially, half the town had seen the corner of one poking out of her recipe book.

"My maps," Juno repeated slowly.

"Above the Narrows. Everything you've got between Widow's Tooth and Relay Nine."

"And why would I give those to you? Those maps took me ten years and two arrests."

"Because whoever burned Relay Seven yesterday didn't want anyone following the supply boat," Beatrix said, "and I'd rather find out who with good charts than without."

Juno's grin spread slow and wide and entirely untrustworthy. "Relay Seven's burned?"

"News travels fast."

"News travels *upstream* fast, girl. Think about that." She stepped aside and held the door. "Come in. Have some stew. We'll talk price."

*

Night found Beatrix on her own dock with her boots off and her feet in the cold green water, doing the thing she did instead of praying: reading the river.

The Veyl moved past in the dark, patient and enormous, carrying everything downstream and returning nothing. Somewhere up there—past the Narrows, past the burned relay, past the falls she would not name even in her head—a boat full of men hadn't called home in eleven days. Somewhere up there was a grave she'd helped dig with her own orders, and a company that had buried the truth along with her reputation, and now a seventeen-year-old girl with her uncle's stubborn chin was pulling all of it back into the light one question at a time.

Brannic thought he'd cornered her. Ada thought she'd won her. Both of them were wrong, and both of them were right, and Beatrix sat with her feet in the current and let the resentment curdle slowly, thoroughly, into something with more structure. Anger was fuel. She'd learned that much. Anger had gotten her down the river alive seven years ago, and anger would get her back up it now—not forgiveness, nothing so soft, just the cold mechanical certainty that she would deliver this girl to her answers if it killed them both, and that Brannic's smug little ledger could go hang.

Behind her, footsteps on the planks. Light ones. Ada, wrapped in a blanket against the river damp, holding two cups of something hot.

"You missed dinner," Ada said.

"I ate."

"You didn't. Juno told me. She also told me she's coming with us, and that Odell the mapmaker owes you a favor, and that Tam will crew for you specifically and won't say why." Ada sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and handed over a cup. "She talks a lot, that woman."

"When she stops talking, worry." Beatrix took the cup. Tea, heavily sweetened, the way river people made it when they wanted to say something kind without saying it. "Get some sleep, girl. We provision at dawn. And Ada—"

"Yes?"

"When we're on the water, my word is the whole law. Not most of it. All of it. People who argue with their guide on the upper Veyl end up as cautionary tales." She drank. The tea was terrible. It was also, she noticed, exactly right.

Ada nodded solemnly. Then, after a moment: "What happened at Relay Seven, do you think? Really?"

Beatrix looked north, where the river vanished into dark hills and older darks beyond them. Fires didn't start themselves at relay stations. Relays were stone, iron, and one bored operator; they didn't burn by accident, and they especially didn't burn the week after an expedition went silent on the same stretch of water.

"I think," she said slowly, "that somebody upriver doesn't want company. And I think we're about to be very rude."

Far off in the hills, thunder muttered—the first word of the monsoon, weeks early, clearing its throat.

Beatrix finished her tea and went to pack.
