## 4. Crew of Strays

Ada had made a list. It was not a good list, but it was a list, and her father always said that a plan you could hold in your hand was worth ten plans floating in your head.

*One: someone who knows the river above the Narrows. Two: someone who can drive a boat. Three: someone who can feed four people for three weeks without poisoning them.*

She'd written it on the back of a laundry receipt in the boarding house where she was running out of money at an alarming rate, and she read it over breakfast while the landlady's cat judged her from the windowsill.

"Guides won't go," the landlady had told her that first night, not unkindly, sliding an extra roll onto her plate like pity could be baked. "Monsoon season coming, and the Veyl's already up. You'll need to look lower down the ladder, girl. Look for people with nothing left to lose."

Ada had taken this as advice. Later she would understand it had also been a description of everyone she was about to hire.

* * *

Odell Quill worked out of a shed behind the chandlery that smelled of ink and mildew and, faintly, of ambition that had died some years ago but hadn't been buried properly. His sign — hand-lettered, slightly crooked — read **MAPS & SURVEYING**, and beneath it, smaller, as if added under duress: *and other paper work.*

She found him hunched over a drafting table, drawing a coastline she was fairly sure didn't exist.

"Mr. Quill? I need a mapmaker."

He didn't look up. "Guild cartographer," he said. "Certified. Or I was." His pen kept moving. "You want charts of the Lower Veyl, they're in the chandlery, two bits each. You want anything above the Serrated Narrows—" the pen stopped, "—you want a different man."

"That's exactly what I want."

Now he looked up. He was perhaps fifty, with ink-stained fingers and the particular squint of a man who had spent decades arguing with rivers about where they ought to have gone. "Above the Narrows is closed water. Guild decree. Company decree, really, but the guild does their stamping for them." He said it lightly, the way people say things they've said so many times the words have worn smooth as river stones. "No maps exist. Officially."

"But unofficially?"

The pen hovered. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed its opinion.

"You're the Vance girl," he said. "The one asking after the supply run."

"My father's up there. Or he was. Eleven days of silence."

Something moved behind Odell's squint — not softness exactly, more like recognition, the way one locked door knows another. "Eleven days," he repeated. "And the guides here won't take you."

"They took one look at my money and invented reasons. One of them told me the monsoon was coming early. Another told me the river gods were angry. A third just laughed, which I feel was the most honest of the three."

Despite himself — she watched him try not to — Odell smiled. "And your guide now?"

"She agreed. As far as the last relay station." Ada hesitated. "She's... not from around here. She wouldn't give me much of a name."

"Thackeray," Odell said quietly. Not a question.

Ada went still. "You know her?"

"I know the name." He set down his pen with great care, aligning it parallel to the table edge, buying himself a moment. "Everyone in this trade knew the name, once. Then nobody did, which is worse." He stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and began pulling drawers. "If Thackeray's going upriver, then the river's worse than the gossip says, or she is. Possibly both." He pulled out a rolled chart tied with string, blew dust off it, and held it against his chest without offering it. "I'll come. On conditions."

"I don't have much money."

"I don't want money." His eyes had gone somewhere else, seven years and several hundred miles upstream. "I want to see it again. The source valley. They struck me off the rolls for surveying it once. Erased every line I drew." He almost laughed. "Do you know what that's like, girl? To spend your whole life learning to draw the world, and then be told one piece of it doesn't exist?"

"No," Ada said honestly. "But I know what it's like to be told my father doesn't exist anymore. Same shape of thing, maybe."

Odell looked at her for a long moment. Then he tucked the chart inside his coat, where it made a suspicious rectangular bulge, and reached for his hat. "Same shape exactly," he said. "That's why I'm coming."

* * *

Finding a boatman proved harder, because there was only one candidate and he didn't talk.

Tam worked the ferry landing — coiling rope, bailing bilge, patching hulls with a competence that made the other boatmen look like children playing at it. He was broad and quiet and maybe thirty, with a scar along his jaw that looked old enough to have stories attached, none of which he'd ever told. According to the chandlery's boy, Tam hadn't spoken in four years. Not since the wreck of the *Cormorant*. Nobody knew why. Nobody asked twice, because the one time somebody had asked twice, Tam had picked the man up by his collar and set him down in the river, gently, the way you'd return a fish.

Ada approached him at the end of a shift, when the light was going gold on the water and even the gulls had run out of complaints.

"Mr. Tam? I'm hiring a crew. Up the Veyl. Past the Narrows."

He kept coiling rope. She waited. She was learning that waiting was most of talking to people.

"We'd pay well," she tried.

Coil.

"It's dangerous," she offered next, because honesty seemed like the right currency with this one. "My father's expedition went silent eleven days ago. We think something happened at the relay stations. Maybe burned. Maybe worse."

His hands paused — just a hitch, half a second — and resumed.

"And the monsoon's coming," she finished. "So we'd be racing it. Up and back. Probably in weather no sane person would boat in."

Tam set down the coil. He looked at her — really looked, the way you'd read a chart — and then he walked away.

Ada stood on the dock feeling like a fool, and might have given up entirely except that he came back. He carried a wooden slate on a leather cord, the kind children used in school, and a stub of chalk worn round. He wrote four words in a quick, sure hand:

**WHOSE BOAT. WHOSE GUIDE.**

"Oh!" Ada blinked at it. "The boat's mine — well, hired, mine for the season. And the guide is..." She hesitated, and decided, on the spot, that lying to a man who communicated via schoolroom slate felt uniquely shameful. "Her name's Beatrix Thackeray. Though she goes by something else here."

Tam read the name twice. Something crossed his face — not fear, she thought. Recognition's quieter cousin. He erased the slate with his thumb and wrote again:

**SHE'LL GET YOU THERE.**

Then, after a pause long enough that Ada thought he was done:

**I'LL GET THE BOAT THERE.**

"That's it? That's yes?"

He shrugged, which she took to mean *that's it*, and turned back to his ropes. But as she walked away, elated and bewildered in equal measure, he called out — actually called out, voice rusty as a hinge, startling a nearby fisherman nearly into the water:

"Girl."

She spun. "Yes?"

He held up the slate one last time. **BRING YOUR OWN CHALK.**

* * *

Juno they found by accident, which was fitting, because everything about Juno suggested she was a woman who preferred to be found that way.

They'd gone to hire a cook — any cook — and the boarding house kitchen had produced a wiry, sun-browned woman stirring a pot with one hand and dealing cards to herself with the other. She took one look at Odell's bulging coat pocket and said, "You're carrying contraband charts, and you're taking them upriver, and you need a cook who can keep her mouth shut. Sit down. Both of you. Eat something first; negotiations go better fed."

"How did you possibly—" Ada started.

"The coat bulges to the left. Honest men carry documents flat." Juno ladled out stew without waiting to be asked. It was, Ada had to admit, extraordinary stew. "Also, the whole town's been talking about the mad Vance girl hiring a blacklist guide and a mute boatman. Word travels faster than water here. Now. Terms."

"You haven't heard our terms yet."

"Don't need to. My terms are these: I cook, I don't clean, and when we pass through the middle country, I get to stop at two places. You don't ask what for." Her eyes glittered with the specific joy of a person about to break several small laws. "In exchange, you get the best food on the Veyl, and—" she leaned in, dropping her voice to a stage whisper, "—I happen to own certain maps. Smuggler's maps. Of channels the company doesn't chart because the company doesn't *know* about them. Shortcuts. Backwaters. Places to hide a boat when you'd very much like the boat not to be found."

"Why would we need to hide a boat?" Ada asked.

Juno pointed her ladle at her. "Sweetheart, you're going up a forbidden river with a disgraced guide, a disgraced mapmaker, a boatman who communicates exclusively in chalk, and a cook who's wanted in three towns — only two of which deserved it. If nobody wants to stop that boat, I'll eat my apron."

There was a silence while Ada considered the apron, which was stained and enormous, and decided Juno probably would.

"One condition back," Ada said. "You tell us what's in those two stops. Afterward. Not before — afterward. I'm not running cargo, whatever it is. But I don't like secrets sitting next to me in a canoe for three weeks. They leak."

Juno's grin widened until it threatened the structural integrity of her face. "Oh, I *like* you. Your father raised you right, whoever he is." She stuck out a flour-dusted hand. "Done. Chalk it up."

* * *

They met at dawn two days later at the north dock, where the hired boat — a shallow-drafted river scow named the *Patient Lark*, which Ada chose partly for the name and partly because it was the only vessel whose owner didn't ask questions — sat low in the gray water, loaded and listing slightly to starboard.

It was not, objectively, a promising sight. Odell had brought a trunk of instruments and was already arguing with the boat about where to stow them. Tam stood at the stern saying nothing with tremendous authority, testing the tiller lines. Juno had somehow acquired a crate of provisions larger than the crate of provisions Ada had paid for, contents unknown. And Beatrix arrived last, as if arriving last were a job requirement, a canvas pack over one shoulder and an expression that suggested she'd spent the night reconsidering and lost the argument.

She looked at the crew. The crew looked at her.

"This," Beatrix said finally, "is either a rescue expedition or a jailbreak, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," said Odell.

"Neither," said Tam's slate, which he'd apparently decided to use for group communication now, holding it up like a small verdict.

"Definitely both," said Juno cheerfully. "Who wants breakfast? I made enough for eight, because I assumed we'd lose some of you en route."

Ada laughed — a real laugh, the first in eleven days, surprising her with its sound — and climbed aboard. For one bright moment, standing on the deck of the *Patient Lark* with her strange, damaged, secretly magnificent crew, she let herself believe the worst part was behind them.

Beatrix cast off the lines herself, hands moving with an economy that needed no words, and pushed them out into the current. The town fell away behind them. Ahead, the river bent north toward the hills, and the hills wore clouds like a warning.

"First rapids are two days up," Beatrix said, eyes fixed ahead. "The Serrated Narrows. Sleep well tonight, all of you."

Nobody asked why she'd said it the way she had — softly, almost to herself, like a prayer said backward.

They would find out soon enough.
