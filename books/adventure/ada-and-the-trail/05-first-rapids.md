## 5. First Rapids

The Serrated Narrows announced themselves the way bad news does—not all at once, but in pieces, each one worse than the last.

First came the sound. Beatrix heard it from half a mile out, a low grinding under the river's voice, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the water. She stood at the bow of the *Pennywhistle* with one hand on the rail and let the sound settle into her bones, cataloguing it the way she used to catalogue weather: *that's granite on granite, that's volume, that's a drop hiding behind that bend.*

Then came the smell—cold stone and churned spray, sharp as struck flint.

Then, finally, the sight of it: the river pinching itself between two walls of black rock, jagged as a broken zipper, white water stacked up in the gaps like someone had crumpled a mountain of lace and shoved it into a bottle.

"Ah," said Odell, appearing at her elbow with his notebook already open. "The Narrows."

"You've run them?"

"I've *mapped* them." He adjusted his spectacles. "There is an important distinction, and I suspect we're about to learn it experientially."

Behind them, Tam stood at the tiller without expression, which was how Tam stood everywhere, but Beatrix noticed his weight shift forward onto the balls of his feet. Ready. Juno was below decks lashing down the cookware, and Ada—

Ada was at the rail, eyes shining, drinking it all in with the naked hunger of someone who had never yet watched a river try to kill anybody she loved.

"Miss Thackeray," Ada called. "Is it true they call it the Serrated Narrows because of the rocks?"

"It's true they call it that," Beatrix said. "The reason is less poetic than you'd hope."

"What's the plan?"

Beatrix looked at the water for a long moment. Seven years since she'd read a stretch of river this hard, and the reading came back to her hands before it came back to her head—the way a language comes back when you hear it spoken again. Left channel, but not the obvious left; hug the seam where the eddy line folded back on itself, then punch through the gap behind the third fang, the one that looked too narrow until you realized the current had already dug the path for you.

"The plan," she said, "is that I tell Tam what to do, Tam does it perfectly, and everyone else holds onto something and doesn't scream until after we're through. Screaming during costs us concentration."

"And after?"

"After, you can scream all you like. It's traditional."

Ada grinned. Odell made a small note in his book that Beatrix strongly suspected was titled *Traditional Screaming*.

She moved aft to stand beside Tam. The mute boatman glanced at her, and she held up four fingers, then pointed left. He nodded once. She pointed at the gap behind the third fang, traced the line with her finger through the air, and he studied it with the flat patience of a man memorizing scripture.

Juno came up from below, wiped her hands on her apron, and took position amidships without being asked. "I've run cargo through worse," she offered, to no one in particular.

"No, you haven't," Beatrix said.

"No," Juno agreed cheerfully, "but it's good luck to say it."

They entered the Narrows at mid-morning, and the world became noise.

It was not like the gentle rapids below Kessel's Landing, the ones tourists paid ferry fares to bounce through while holding their hats. This was the river showing its teeth. The *Pennywhistle* dropped into the first chute and the walls closed in—black rock on both sides, close enough that Beatrix could have touched them with an oar, streaked white with old flood-marks ten feet above their heads. The water didn't flow so much as *argue*, shoving the boat left, then right, then dropping out from under her entirely for one stomach-lifting instant before slamming her back against the rail.

"Left!" she shouted, and Tam was already there, the tiller moving with a smoothness that looked lazy and wasn't. "Hold—*hold*—now right! Right, right—"

They threaded the first gap. Then the second. Spray came over the bow in cold sheets and Ada whooped—a bright, involuntary sound that cut through the roar—and Beatrix felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched.

This was the part she'd missed. That was the ugly truth of it, the thing she'd never say aloud: seven years of fixing ferry engines in a town that had forgotten her name, and some traitorous corner of her had been *bored*. The river was trying to kill them, and some traitorous corner of her was *glad to be here*.

Then they hit the third fang, and the river stopped arguing and started attacking.

The gap was there, exactly where her memory said it would be—but the water wasn't. A lateral wave, a pour-over from some submerged shelf she couldn't see, was stacking across the entrance like a door closing. Beatrix saw it a half-second before they reached it, did the arithmetic—too fast to stop, too narrow to dodge—and made the only call there was.

"Punch it! Tam, *through*, don't fight it—"

The wave took the bow and lifted it. For one endless moment the *Pennywhistle* was more air than boat, hanging on the crest with her propeller churning nothing, and Beatrix saw the rock wall coming around to meet them in a slow, terrible arc—

—and then Tam's hand was on hers, not taking the wheel, just pressing it, *this way, two degrees, trust me*, and she gave it to him because there was no time to do anything else, and the bow swung three feet, five feet, just enough, and the hull came down on the back side of the wave with a crack like a cannon and a sheet of water that drenched them all to the skin.

They were through. The Narrows fell away behind them, still roaring, cheated.

For a moment nobody moved. Water dripped off everything. Juno's hat was gone. Odell's notebook was clutched above his head like a rescued infant, sodden but alive.

"Well," Odell said finally. "Experientially."

Ada turned around. She was soaked through, hair plastered to her face, and she was laughing—until she looked at Beatrix, and the laugh died in her throat.

Because Beatrix's hands were shaking.

Not much. Just enough that anyone watching could see it, and Ada was watching. The guide stood at the wheel with perfect posture and a face carved from calm, and her hands trembled against the wood like a bird's heartbeat, and her breath came shallow and fast, and her eyes were fixed on the middle distance with an expression Ada had no name for yet but would learn to recognize: the look of someone who has been here before, and remembers what it cost.

"You were scared," Ada said quietly. Not accusing. Wondering.

"I'm always scared," Beatrix said. "That's the job. Fear's the instrument. You read the river with it." She flexed her fingers once, deliberately, until the trembling stopped. "The day I stop being scared is the day you should all get off my boat."

It was a good answer. It was even mostly true. But Ada kept looking at her a moment longer, filing something away, and Beatrix turned to check the engine and let her.

* * *

They made camp that evening in a gravel bend below the ruins of Relay Station One.

Beatrix knew something was wrong before she saw it. The relay stations were supposed to answer the evening check-in—a coded whistle-tone from the horn antenna, three rising notes, like a kettle singing. She'd been listening for it since noon, telling herself the range was bad, telling herself the storm clouds building in the west were eating the signal, telling herself every lie a person tells when they don't want to arrive somewhere and find out the truth.

The truth had a smell. Woodsmoke, old and sour, soaked into everything.

Relay Station One had been a tidy little structure in its day—timber frame, iron roof, the tall lattice mast with its copper wire running down into the riverbank like a root. Now it was a blackened skeleton. The roof had collapsed inward. The mast lay snapped across the yard, and the copper wire had been stripped away entirely, coiled off and carried off, or burned, or both.

Ada walked the site in silence, touching things. The melted glass of a signal lamp. A tin cup fused to a shelf. "This wasn't lightning," she said at last. Her voice had gone very level, the way voices do when they're working hard to stay that way. "Was it?"

"No."

"There's no storm damage. No scorch pattern from the sky. It burned from—" She crouched by the base of the wall, where the char was deepest. "From inside. From the floor, near the fuel store."

"Yes."

"Someone set it." Ada stood up. She was seventeen years old and trying very hard to be a professional, and Beatrix watched her almost succeed. "Someone burned the relay station that my father's expedition would have needed to call home. When? Before they passed, or after?"

"That," Beatrix said, "is the question."

Odell crouched at the edge of the yard, examining the ground with his spectacles nearly touching the dirt. "Footprints," he said. "Several sets, days old at least—rained on twice. And look here." He pointed to a patch of ash near the fire pit, where something pale showed through the grey. He brushed it free with a pencil stub: a scrap of oilcloth, military grade, the kind used to wrap sensitive equipment. Stamped along one edge, faint but legible, was a maker's mark and a batch number.

"Government issue," Juno said, appearing beside him. She'd gone quiet in the way she went quiet around anything official, which was very quiet indeed. "That's not salvage. That's not driftwood. Somebody outfitted properly came up here and did this."

Tam stood apart from all of them, looking not at the station but at the ridge above it—at the treeline, dark against the bruised evening sky. He stared at it for a long time. Then he raised one hand, slowly, and pointed.

Beatrix followed his finger. High up, where the pines broke against the sky, one gap in the canopy looked wrong. Too straight. Too deliberate. Like a sightline.

Like a place where a man might stand and watch a relay station burn, and watch a boat come upriver afterward, and know exactly who was aboard.

"We camp downstream tonight," Beatrix said. "Away from the light. Nobody wanders. Watches in pairs." She looked at each of them in turn—Ada with her father's stubborn jaw, Odell clutching his ruined notebook, Juno with her smuggler's maps and her smuggler's silences, Tam with his pointing hand still half-raised toward the ridge. "Tomorrow we make better time. I want to reach Relay Two by nightfall."

"And if Relay Two is burned too?" Ada asked.

Beatrix looked west, where the monsoon clouds had begun to stack themselves against the mountains like furniture being moved into an upstairs room.

"Then we'll know this isn't an accident," she said. "And we'll know whoever's doing it is still ahead of us—or still *with* us—and either way, Miss Vance, we'd best find your father before they do."

Nobody slept well. And sometime past midnight, from high on the dark ridge above their camp, a single stone clicked loose and rolled down through the scree, and stopped, and the silence that followed was the kind that listens back.
