## 6. What the River Keeps

The river gave things back. That was the first thing Ada learned about the Veyl, and she learned it the way you learn most things on water: unwillingly, and covered in mud.

It had been three days since the burned relay station, and the mood aboard the *Pennywhistle* had settled into something Ada could only describe as polite dread. Everyone was friendly. Everyone was helpful. And everyone kept glancing at the banks like the river might be keeping score.

"Look at that," Juno said from the stern, where she'd established a kind of outdoor kingdom of pots and drying herbs lashed to the rail. "Driftwood. Good-sized piece, too." She said it the way other people said *look at that, a rainbow*.

"It's just wood," Ada said.

"It's *free* wood," Juno corrected, with the fervor of a woman who had once smuggled an entire stove downriver in pieces disguised as cheese. "Wood is money, girl. Everything on this river is money if you're not too proud to pick it up."

So when Tam — who communicated primarily through pointing, grunting, and an elaborate system of eyebrow semaphore that Odell claimed to be fluent in but which Ada suspected he invented on the spot — tapped the gunwale twice and pointed upstream-left, everyone looked. And there, snagged in the roots of a fallen alder at the water's edge, half-sunk in silt, was something that was very much not driftwood.

A crate.

Or what had once been a crate. The river had been chewing on it for what looked like years — the slats were gray and furred with algae, one corner crushed inward as though something enormous had stepped on it, and the whole thing wore a coat of river mud so thick it seemed less like an object than a small island with ambitions. But it was unmistakably a shipping crate, iron-banded, built to survive exactly this kind of abuse.

"Salvage rights," Juno announced immediately, already untying the boat hook. "I saw it first."

"You're on a boat," Odell said. "We all saw it first. We are a collective of first-seers."

"Then we'll split it six ways."

"There's four of us," Ada said.

"And Beatrix makes five, and I'm counting the boat, because that woman loves that boat more than any of us and we all know it."

Beatrix, at the tiller, did not rise to this. She brought them in against the current with that maddening, frictionless competence of hers — three small corrections, no wasted motion, the hull kissing the bank soft as a moth landing — and then stood looking at the crate for a long moment before anyone touched it.

Something crossed her face. Ada caught it because she'd been practicing catching it. It was there and gone, quick as a fish turning under the surface: not surprise, exactly. More like recognition. Like meeting someone at a market you'd hoped never to see again.

"Careful," Beatrix said. "River-soaked cargo shifts. If there's anything heavy in there, let it settle before you tip it."

That was reasonable. That was sensible guide advice, the kind of thing she said a dozen times a day. So why did Ada's stomach do a slow, cold roll at the sound of it?

They hauled it aboard with rope and grunting and one undignified moment where Juno lost her footing and sat down hard in the bilge, declaring loudly that she had meant to do that, it was a technique, very advanced. They laid the crate amidships on the deck planks, and Odell — who turned out to own a knife for every conceivable purpose plus several inconceivable ones — went to work on the bands while the rest of them crowded around pretending they weren't crowding.

The mud came away in sheets. Underneath, the wood was darker, older, but intact. And stamped into the headboard, in faded black ink that the river hadn't managed to fully drown:

**VANCE & HALLORAN SUPPLY CO.**
*Upriver Outfitters — Est. 41*

Ada stopped breathing.

She knew that mark. Of course she knew it. It was burned into the sign over her father's warehouse door. It was branded into the handles of every good tool she'd ever owned. Her father had it tattooed on his forearm, badly, by a man in Kessel's Landing who charged him two coins and a bottle and delivered something that looked more like a squid than a compass rose.

"That's—" Juno started.

"I know what it is," Ada said. Her voice came out strange and flat, like someone else's. "That's my father's company mark."

The deck went quiet except for the river talking to itself along the hull. Tam had gone very still, hands flat on his knees. Odell had stopped cutting and was looking at the stamp, then at Ada, then — briefly, carefully — at Beatrix.

"It's from the expedition," Ada said. "It has to be. This is the route they took. This is—" She crouched, touching the crushed corner, the algae, doing the arithmetic she didn't want to do. "This has been in the water a long time. Years. But it can't have floated far; crates sink. Which means it sank near here, or upstream of here, and the river held onto it until now."

"The Veyl keeps things," Tam said.

Everyone stared at him. It was, by Ada's count, only the fourth or fifth time any of them had heard his voice, and each occasion had the quality of a minor religious event. He shrugged, as if embarrassed by the sentence, and looked back at the water.

"He's right, though," Odell said quietly. "The Veyl gives back on its own schedule. Fishermen pull up lamps and boots and once, famously, a piano. Things go down and come up years later, miles later. There are eddies below the second gorge that hold debris for decades." He wiped his knife and folded it away. "If your father's expedition lost cargo overboard, this could be it. Could have been circling in a backwater ever since."

"Then where's the rest of it?" Ada asked. "A supply expedition doesn't lose *one crate*. Where are the others? Where are the boats? Where are the *people*?"

Nobody answered. The river kept talking.

* * *

They opened the crate on the bank, because Beatrix refused to have unknown cargo pried open aboard her boat — "You don't open mystery boxes on a vessel, that's how vessels become mysteries" — and because, she added, whatever was inside had been marinating in river filth for years and she wasn't having it anywhere near Juno's galley. Juno took this as a compliment to her standards, which said something about her standards.

Inside, packed in sodden straw that had gone the consistency of porridge, they found:

Six tins of lamp oil, rusted shut. A coil of signal wire, green with corrosion. A canvas tool roll that disintegrated at the touch, shedding wrenches into the mud like a magician's trick gone wrong. And at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth that had actually done its job, a ledger.

Not a journal. A ledger. Supply tallies, in a neat clerk's hand. Dates, quantities, weights. Ada recognized the handwriting the way you recognize a voice through a wall — her father wrote numbers the same way, with the sevens crossed and the fours open at the top.

Her throat closed up. She had to hand the ledger to Odell for a minute and pretend to be very interested in the shoreline.

"Last entry's dated," Odell said gently, "seven years back. Sixth of Thawmonth." He looked up. "That fits. That's the season the Vance expedition went silent."

"So it *is* their cargo." Ada knelt and took the ledger back, holding it like something warm-blooded. "One crate out of — there were dozens. My father told me the manifest once. Lamp oil, wire, tools, dried goods, spare parts for the drills at the survey camp..." She flipped pages with fingers that shook slightly. "This is real. This is proof they made it at least this far upriver. Nobody's found so much as a boot in seven years, and now the river just — hands us this?"

"Lucky," Juno said.

"Very lucky," Odell said.

Neither of them sounded like they believed it, and Ada noticed that too, and hated that she noticed.

Because here was the thing she kept circling back to, the splinter working its way deeper: Beatrix had known. Before the bands were cut, before the mud came off, Beatrix had looked at that crate and her face had done the fish-turn. And when Ada thought about it harder — and she was trying very hard not to think about it harder — there had been something else. When they'd hauled it aboard, Beatrix had positioned herself between Ada and the crate, casually, the way you'd stand between someone and a drop. And she had been the one to say *careful, let it settle*, and her voice had been perfectly level, and perfect levelness, Ada was learning, was Beatrix's most expensive emotion.

"Beatrix," Ada said. "When you saw the stamp. Before we opened it. You knew what company it was."

Beatrix was coiling the tow rope, and her hands did not pause, which was itself an answer of a kind. "Every outfitter on this stretch used that route. Half the marks on the river look alike."

"It's my father's mark."

"Then it's your father's crate." She set the coil down. Met Ada's eyes, finally, and gave her nothing — a wall with a face painted on it. "I'm sorry, Ada. Truly. I know what finding this costs you. But a stamp tells us where cargo came from, not where people went. Don't build a church on a crate."

*Don't build a church on a crate.* It was good advice. It was even kind advice, in its flinty way. And it slid off Ada entirely, because underneath it she could hear the other thing, the thing Beatrix wasn't saying, which was: *stop asking.*

That night they camped on a gravel bar beneath a stand of birches gone gold at the edges, early for the season, or late — Odell argued both positions with himself over dinner. Juno produced a stew that tasted improbably of home, given that home, for most of them, was a place they'd left under clouds of one kind or another. Tam fixed the boat's rubbing strake by firelight without being asked. And Ada sat apart with the ledger, reading entries she mostly couldn't decipher, feeling the splinter turn.

Seven years ago, her uncle Halloran — her father's partner, the Halloran of the stamp, the one who'd insisted on leading the source run himself because he never trusted a hired pilot past the Narrows — had come to Kessel's Landing looking for a guide. Ada had been ten. She remembered him at the kitchen table, maps spread out, arguing with her father about pilots. She remembered a name coming up. She remembered her father saying *no, absolutely not, not her, she's trouble*, and her uncle laughing and saying *trouble is exactly what the upper Veyl needs.*

She hadn't known whose name it was. Ten-year-olds don't memorize the names of pilots their fathers veto.

But somebody had led that expedition. Somebody had taken her uncle's money and his trust and his crew up a river that ate them. And the guides in Kessel's Landing wouldn't so much as say the source's name out loud, and the guild rolls had a blacklist nobody would show her, and the one person willing to take her upstream was a ferry mechanic with a guide's hands and a face that flinched — just barely, just once — at a seven-year-old crate.

Ada looked across the fire at Beatrix, who was cleaning her boots with the focused intensity of a woman scrubbing something other than boots.

"What happened to the pilots who worked the upper Veyl?" Ada asked the fire, lightly. Too lightly. "Back when runs went that far. Before everything got careful."

Odell glanced up. Juno's ladle paused mid-stir.

"Gone," Beatrix said, to her boots. "Like everything else on that river."

"Gone where?"

"Ada." She set the boot down. In the firelight her eyes were unreadable, which Ada now understood was a skill, practiced, maintained, costing her something daily. "Get some sleep. We make the third bend tomorrow, and after that the water gets honest. You'll want your strength."

*The water gets honest.* As if the water were the dishonest party here. As if anything out here were more dishonest than a woman who looked at her employer's family crest floating out of the mud and called it a coincidence.

Ada rolled into her blanket and lay listening to the river, which went on keeping its things, patient as a pawnbroker. Somewhere upstream, in the dark, was whatever remained of her uncle's expedition. Somewhere upstream was the truth. And somewhere between here and there, she thought, watching the firelight play on Beatrix's sleeping face and finding it, even asleep, guarded — somewhere between here and there was the answer to a question Ada was suddenly afraid to ask directly.

*Who did my uncle hire?*

She fell asleep composing the question anyway, polishing it, weighing it, deciding how and when to ask it — and whether she wanted the answer enough to survive it.

Behind her, unnoticed by anyone, the ledger lay open in the grass to its final page, where the last line of her father's neat clerk-hand read: *Pilot engaged per H.'s insistence. God help us all.*
