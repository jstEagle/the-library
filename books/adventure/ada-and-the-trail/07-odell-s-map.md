## 7. Odell's Map

Odell Fenwick had been struck off the Cartographers' Guild rolls for eleven years, and in that time he had learned exactly three things about disgrace.

First: it traveled lighter than you expected. He had once owned a house with a map room and a wife who loved him on Tuesdays; now everything he owned fit in a brass tube and a canvas satchel, and he slept better than he had in years.

Second: people could smell it on you. Not the guild seal burned out of his ledger—any fool could forge a seal—but the particular way he held a chart, flat and reverent, like a priest handling scripture. Beatrix held charts the same way. So did Tam, come to think of it, though Tam mostly used them to line the cookbox.

Third—and this was the one that had taken him the longest to learn—a map was never just a map. It was an argument. Every line drawn was a claim made, and every line *omitted* was a claim made too, only quieter.

He was thinking about quiet claims the morning after the burned relay station, sitting in the stern of the *Pennywhistle* with his knees folded up like a folding chair, watching Beatrix Thackeray—or whatever her name was this decade—read the river the way other people read threatening letters.

They had made camp on a gravel bar below the relay's black ribs. The fire was small. Juno was making something with lentils that smelled, against all odds, like forgiveness. Ada sat close to Beatrix without appearing to sit close to Beatrix, which Odell recognized as a skill usually acquired by spies and daughters.

And Beatrix was wrong about something.

Odell had noticed it at supper and had spent two hours turning it over like a stone with something underneath. She'd told them tomorrow's route would take them through the Weir Narrows by midday, then a half-day's pull to the confluence where they'd pick up the old survey trail toward the source country. She'd said it lightly, the way you say things you've said so often they've gone smooth as river glass.

But the confluence she'd described didn't exist. Not anymore. Not since the Decree.

* * *

He waited until the others turned in. This was not cowardice, whatever Juno said later; it was courtesy. Some truths were like porridge—better served warm, alone, with no audience to watch you burn your mouth.

Beatrix took first watch, naturally. She always did. She sat on an overturned crate with her boots off and her knife out, sharpening it in long slow strokes, and when Odell lowered himself onto the gravel beside her she didn't startle, which he took as either trust or exhaustion.

"Your river's wrong," he said.

She didn't look up from the knife. "It's not my river."

"The confluence. You told Ada we'd strike the survey trail at the meeting of the Veyl and the Corren." He let that sit. "There is no Corren. Not on any chart printed in the last nine years."

The knife stopped.

In the firelight Odell watched her face do something complicated—the small muscular work of a person deciding how much of themselves to spend tonight. He knew that face. He wore it most mornings.

"Go on," she said finally.

So he did. He unstrapped the brass tube from his back—he carried it everywhere, even to the privy, a habit Juno called "romantic" and "insane" in the same breath—and drew out the thing he had not shown another living soul in eleven years.

It was a map. Of course it was a map. But not one of his.

It was older than his disgrace, older than hers, drawn on linen-backed paper gone soft as cloth at the folds. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea, but the hand that made it had been magnificent—confident, economical, every contour line placed like a word chosen after ten worse words were considered.

"This is a survey of the upper Veyl," Odell said. "Complete. Source to gorge. Dated forty-one years ago." He tapped the corner, where a company mark had been stamped and then, deliberately, half-scratched away. "Before the Decree."

"I know what it is," Beatrix said quietly.

"Do you?" He looked at her properly then. "Because I found this in a guild archive sealed under a false catalog number, and I spent four years of my life trying to understand why the finest piece of field cartography I have ever seen—one that maps a valley that officially does not exist—was buried like a crime. And then I tried to publish my findings, and now I sleep on boats." He smiled, because the alternative at that moment was not smiling. "So yes. I know what it is. I'm curious what *you* know."

The fire popped. Downriver, water worried at the gravel bar with its endless patient fingers.

"You want to know why there's no Corren River on the charts," Beatrix said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I want to know why there's no *valley* on the charts. The river I could forgive—an oversight, a flood, rivers move. But valleys don't move, Mrs.—"

"Don't."

"—Ms. Ferry Mechanic," Odell amended smoothly. "Valleys don't move. Someone erased forty miles of terrain from the official record, and they did it with a decree, which means they did it with money, which means someone upstream is still paying for the erasing." He unrolled the old map between them, weighting the corners with stones. "Look here. The Veyl doesn't rise from a single glacier headwall the way the schoolbooks say. It rises from a basin. A hanging valley behind the source ridge, fed by meltwater through a rock throat. And across that throat—" he traced a line with one blunt finger, "—someone built a dam. Forty years ago. Before the company ever ran supply expeditions up here. Before your time. Before mine."

Beatrix had gone very still. Not guide-still, which was a living stillness, tuned to water and weather. This was the other kind. The kind Odell had seen once in a mirror, the night before the guild tribunal.

"How did you get this?" she asked.

"A dead archivist who owed me money. Long story, involves a horse, I'll tell it never." He kept his eyes on the map. "The point is, I cross-referenced it. Spent two years on it. The dam appears in no company record. No engineering registry. Nothing. A structure big enough to hold back a glacial lake, and it simply—" he snapped his fingers, "—wasn't. Except that it *is*, because the surveyor mapped it, and then somebody realized what mapping it meant, and the decree came down, and the valley came off the charts, and everyone who might have asked questions was paid off or promoted or ruined."

"And you were ruined."

"I was *inconvenient*, which is the same thing with better stationery." He said it lightly, and meant it lightly, mostly. "I published a paper on anomalous hydrology in the upper Veyl. Cited the survey. Suggested, delicately, that the guild's master charts contained a deliberate omission. They struck me off for 'unsound methodology.' Which is guild language for *you saw the thing we agreed not to see.*"

For a long moment Beatrix said nothing. Then she leaned forward, and Odell watched seven years fall off her posture all at once—not youth, exactly, but the weight of pretending to be someone lighter.

"The expedition I lost," she said. "Seven years ago. The one that ended me. We weren't running supplies." Her voice was low and even, the voice of someone reading coordinates off a chart she hated. "We were running a route check for the company. Verifying the corridor for a new relay chain all the way to the source. And the route we were given—" she stopped. Started again. "The route we were given avoided the basin. Deliberately. There was a clause in our contract, a survey exclusion zone, and I thought it was mineral rights. Everyone thinks it's mineral rights. It's always mineral rights."

"But it wasn't."

"It wasn't." She looked at the old map, at the dam line, and Odell saw her do the arithmetic he had done alone in a rented room four years ago, and he wished—as he always wished—that arithmetic didn't have to be done alone. "Something happened at that dam," she said slowly. "A long time ago. Something bad enough that the company erased the valley rather than explain it. And when my expedition got too close to asking why—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Odell had read the tribunal summaries; he'd read them the way you read anything about a wreck you nearly had yourself, hungrily and with your heart in your teeth.

"They needed a reason the expedition failed that wasn't the valley," he said gently. "So they gave them a guide."

"So they gave them a guide." She almost laughed. It wasn't a pretty sound. "Eleven years you've been ruined for a map. Seven for a crossing. And neither of us has ever once said any of it out loud to another living soul."

"No," Odell agreed. "We've been very thorough about that."

* * *

He showed her the rest because it was time, and because—though he would have denied this under oath—it felt, for the first time in eleven years, safe to show someone.

From the bottom of the brass tube he drew a second sheet: his own work, drafted and redrafted until the paper wore thin. An overlay. His reconstruction of the erased valley, built from the old survey, from satellite scraps, from a smuggler's chart Juno had sold him over a disputed game of cards, and from one thing more.

"One more thing," he said, and heard Juno's voice in his head saying *you and your one more things,* and pressed on anyway. "The old surveyor left a verification method. A hidden one. If the company ever challenged his map, he wanted proof it was true—proof they couldn't buy or burn. So he anchored it to something only a specific kind of person could confirm."

"What kind of person?"

"The kind who's stood on the source ridge." Odell laid his overlay across the antique map and weighted it. "Here. The survey line runs through the basin at a bearing of 312 degrees. On the far wall of the valley, the surveyor marked a cairn—three stones, the top one split by frost, with a brass pin driven into the split. The pin holds a stamped plate. Company issue, pre-Decree serial range. If that cairn is where he says it is, the whole map is true, and the erasure is provable, and eleven years of my life stops being a madman's hobby."

"And if it isn't there?"

"Then I'm a madman with a hobby, and we're all going upstream for nothing, which frankly describes most expeditions I've been on."

Beatrix studied the overlay for a long time. The fire had burned down to a red philosophy of coals. Somewhere across the camp, Tam turned over in his bedroll with a sound like a ship settling.

"Bearing 312," she murmured. "Through the basin." Her finger moved across the lines, and Odell watched her navigate from memory, eyes half-shut, lips shaping distances. He knew that trance. He fell into it over charts the way other men fell into drink.

"There's a notch in the east ridge," she said at last, reluctantly, as though each word cost her something. "You can't see it from the river. You'd never know it was there unless you'd climbed out of the gorge on the north side and looked back." Her finger stopped. "The bearing through the notch would put a cairn right about... there." She touched the map. "On the shelf below the glacier lip."

Odell felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for eleven years. "You've seen the notch."

"I've stood on that ridge." She said it flatly, but her hand wasn't quite steady. "Seven years ago. With six other people. Five of whom came down." She looked at him, and in the coal-light her face was stripped of everything she'd built over it—the mechanic's calm, the ferry-town blankness—and underneath was just a woman who had carried something too heavy for too long. "Your dead surveyor's line passes within sight of where my team made camp. Your ruin and mine are on the same hillside, Odell."

"Well," said Odell, whose voice chose that moment to go rough, "the guild always did say good work clusters."

She laughed—a real one, short and startled out of her, the first he'd heard from her—and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes for a moment, and he pretended very hard to be interested in weighting the map corners.

When she looked up again, she was Beatrix the guide, but a different edition of her. Revised. "Why show me this? Why now?"

"Because tomorrow we pass the last point where turning back is cheap," Odell said. "After the narrows, retreat costs more than advance, and everyone on this boat deserves to make that choice knowing what we're actually sailing toward. Not a rescue. Not a supply run." He rolled the maps together with hands that were, he noted with irritation, not entirely steady. "An exhumation. We're going up there to dig up a valley the company buried, and your name is going to be on the shovel whether you like it or not. Ada's father may already be dead because of what's up there. Merrick knows—I mean, whoever burned that relay knows. And I am tired, Ms. Ferry Mechanic, of being the only man awake in a room full of sleeping people."

Beatrix was quiet for a while. When she spoke, she looked at the dark shape of the river rather than at him.

"The girl," she said. "Ada. She traced my tattoo to a name that shouldn't exist. She's carrying letters that prove who I am. She knows, Odell. Or she will." A breath. "And I haven't told her the worst part yet. That the crossing was mine. That I ordered it."

"I know," Odell said softly.

"You *know*?"

"I read the tribunal record. The guide's order log was entered as evidence." He met her eyes. "I've known since the dock. I came aboard anyway. So did Tam, probably. So did Juno, definitely—Juno knows everything, it's her single redeeming feature."

"Why?"

"Because a crew of liars who've decided to stop lying to each other is worth ten crews of honest strangers." He began packing the tube, businesslike, before his face could do anything embarrassing. "Also because I need someone to confirm a cairn, and you are, infuriatingly, the only person alive who can."

Beatrix snorted. It was, Odell felt, progress.

They sat together as the fire died, two disgraced professionals watching a river run toward a place that officially wasn't there. It was, Odell reflected, the best conversation he'd had in a decade, which said less about the conversation than about the decade.

He was just beginning to think about sleep when Beatrix went rigid beside him.

"Odell." Her voice had changed entirely—all the warmth banked, the guide lit like a lamp behind her eyes. "The fuel drums. Juno checked them at dusk, yes?"

"She topped the reserve tank herself. Why?"

"Because I checked them an hour ago." Beatrix was already on her feet, knife in hand, staring downriver into the dark. "And the reserve tank was full of water."

* * *

Dawn found the whole crew kneeling around the contaminated cache in grim silence, while Beatrix held up a stoppered vial of cloudy fuel and asked, in a voice of terrible mildness, who had handled the drums since Kessel's Landing.

Everyone had. Everyone always had. Which meant, Odell thought, watching five faces compute the same terrible arithmetic, that the answer was either all of them or none of them—

Or someone who hadn't set foot on the boat at all.
