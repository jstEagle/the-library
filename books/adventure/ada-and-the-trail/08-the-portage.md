## 8. The Portage

Tam had not spoken in four years, and the falls did not care.

That was the thing about the Veyl above the Serrated Narrows: it didn't ask questions, didn't wait for answers, didn't notice whether a man had words or not. It just went over the edge of the world with a sound like the sky tearing, and it expected you to deal with it.

He heard it before he saw it. Everyone did. For the last half mile of river the water had been talking to itself, low and urgent, the way a crowd talks before something happens. Then the trees opened up and there it was — the whole Veyl pouring off a shelf of black rock into a gorge of white mist, and the mist rising back up like the river's ghost, refusing to stay dead.

"Saints' teeth," Juno said beside him. She said it quietly, which for Juno was practically a prayer.

Ada stood at the rail with her mouth open. Odell had his notebook out already, sketching the falls from memory of other people's descriptions, because no guild map showed them. The decree had erased this stretch of the Veyl so thoroughly that official charts showed a smooth blue line and a note reading *impassable — do not attempt*, as if the river were a locked door and not a thousand tons of water falling two hundred feet.

Beatrix stood at the bow with her hands on her hips, looking at the falls the way Tam imagined a woman looks at a bill she'd been hoping would get lost in the post.

"All right," she called back. "Nobody's running that. We portage."

* * *

The word *portage* sounds tidy. It is not tidy. It means taking everything your life currently consists of — boat, engine, fuel drums, crates, rope, food, the cook's suspiciously heavy trunk — and carrying it around a waterfall on a trail that the mountain has been actively trying to erase since the last ice age.

They found the start of the old portage path an hour later, half-swallowed by alder scrub. Beatrix walked it first, alone, for a long time. When she came back she had mud to the knee and an expression Tam recognized from mirrors: the look of someone who has remembered something they were happier forgetting.

"There's a route," she said. "Barely. Switchbacks on the north wall, then a shelf above the gorge, then down through the scree to the pool below the falls. Two miles if the trail were honest. It isn't, so call it three." She looked at each of them in turn. "We'll need four trips. Maybe five. The boat comes last, on rollers we cut ourselves."

Odell raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Question. When you say *scree*—"

"I mean rocks that want to kill you."

"And when you say *shelf*—"

"A ledge. With air on one side."

"Right. Yes. Wonderful." Odell closed his notebook with the air of a man closing a door on a bad memory. "I once mapped a cliff face for the Guild. They gave me a medal and then struck me off the rolls for drawing what was actually there. I feel this trail and I have an understanding."

Tam picked up the heaviest crate without being asked. It was easier than talking about it.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about silence: it makes you useful.

A man who can't speak learns to listen with his whole body. He learns that most of what people say is furniture — it fills the room but you could move around it. What's left underneath, the load-bearing part, is small and heavy and true. Beatrix's orders were like that. Short, weighted, placed exactly where they needed to go. *"Rope high. Feet wide. Don't look down, look at me."* Tam had carried those words up the switchbacks all morning like stones in his pockets, and they held.

The trail was worse than she'd promised, which took effort. In places it was a groove worn into the rock by decades of feet and then abandoned; in places it simply stopped being a trail at all and became a suggestion. The spray from the falls reached them even up here, drifting across the path in cold grey veils, slicking every stone. Twice Tam went down to one knee and caught himself on the rock with a crack that went up his arm like a struck bell.

The second time, Ada was behind him with the medicine chest strapped to her back like a turtle.

"You're bleeding," she said.

He shook his head. He wasn't, quite. Scraped. He held up his palm to show her — skin, mostly intact.

"That's not nothing," Ada said, with the particular stubbornness she applied to everything, including whether grown men should bandage their hands. She dug out a wrap and knelt right there on the ledge, thirty feet of air beyond the lip of the trail, and bound his palm while the falls roared below them like a congregation that had lost its mind.

Tam let her. It seemed rude not to.

"You don't have to carry the heaviest things," she said, tying off the knot. "You know that, right? Nobody's keeping score."

He looked at her. He almost answered. The words came up his throat the way they always did now — slow, rusted, stuck somewhere behind his collarbone — and he swallowed them back down and shrugged instead.

Ada studied him for a moment. Then she nodded, once, like they'd agreed on something, and stood, and shouldered her pack, and kept climbing.

Tam followed, and found that his chest felt strange. Lighter. As if the shrug had been a kind of sentence after all, and she had heard it.

* * *

By midday they had the camp moved to the shelf above the gorge, a wind-scoured terrace where the spray fell as constant rain and nothing stayed dry, including hope. Juno cooked anyway. She always cooked anyway. She produced a stew out of supplies that Tam had personally inventoried and knew did not contain a stew, and when he gave her a look she said, "Smuggler's arithmetic," and refused to elaborate.

They ate under a tarp, watching the falls. From up here the water looked less like water than like weather given a deadline — the whole river arriving at the edge and being thrown off it, over and over, forever, without complaint.

"It's louder than I imagined," Ada said. "My father's letters never mentioned—" She stopped herself. Her father's letters hadn't mentioned much, lately. That was the whole reason any of them were here.

"The letters wouldn't," Beatrix said. "Sound doesn't travel in ink." She was coiling rope while she talked, her hands moving on their own, and Tam watched them because watching Beatrix Dravenmoor's hands was a free education. "We'll run the boat on rollers tomorrow. Today we move the rest. Odell, you're on the lead line past the shelf. Juno, rear guard. Ada—"

"I'm coming."

"I was going to say that." Something crossed Beatrix's face, quick as a fish under a boat — surprise, maybe, at her own voice. "Stay inside my sightline. Not ahead of me. Never ahead of me on this trail."

"Why?"

"Because the person in front sets the pace, and the person in front finds out about the bad ground first." Beatrix's coil of rope tightened, neat as a knot in a story. "I've been the person who found out first. Once. It cost more than I'm ready to price today."

Nobody asked. That was the rule none of them had spoken aloud yet, though all of them lived by it: everyone aboard was carrying something, and you didn't jostle another person's load. Tam appreciated the rule more than he could say, which was, admittedly, not saying much.

* * *

The trouble started at the scree.

The scree was a slope of broken stone below the shelf, forty yards of it, angling down toward the pool at the base of the falls where the river gathered itself back together and pretended to be calm. It was the last leg before the landing site, and it was exactly as trustworthy as a stranger's smile. Every stone sat on top of every other stone, waiting for an excuse.

Beatrix rigged the safety line herself. Tam watched her work and understood, in the way he understood weather in his bad knee, that she had done this exact thing before, many times, and that one of those times had gone wrong in a way that still lived in her wrists. Her knots were perfect. Her hands shook slightly between them.

"Line's good," she said. "One at a time. Weight low. If you slip, don't grab the rope — sit down. Sitting down is free. Grabbing is how people die."

"Cheerful," said Juno, and went first, because Juno claimed fear slowed her metabolism and she couldn't afford the weight loss.

Odell went second, narrating his own footsteps in a low mutter — *left foot, good stone, right foot, liar* — which Tam suspected was how the man prayed.

Then it was Tam's turn, with the fuel drum lashed to a frame on his back, forty pounds of the reason they could still run the engine, and therefore the reason they could still go home.

He was halfway across when the rock moved.

Not his rock. A stone upslope, head-sized, dislodged by some earlier footstep finally giving up its argument with gravity. It came down the scree with a dry rattling whisper, then a clatter, then speed, and Tam's body understood it before his mind did — the way the sound changed, the way the air changed — and he threw himself sideways against the slope, drum and all, and the stone passed where his head had been and bounded away down into the white noise of the falls.

He lay there, cheek against wet stone, heart going like a paddle wheel.

Above him, Beatrix's voice came down the line, level as a spirit gauge. "Tam. Report."

He lifted one hand. Made a fist. Opened it. *Fine.*

"Good. Sit up slowly. Check the drum straps."

He checked. One strap had parted. The drum was hanging by the second, swinging gently over the drop, forty pounds of fuel and consequence ticking like a clock.

And here was the problem, the plain stupid mechanical problem that four years of silence had been building toward: he needed to tell her. The strap was cut clean — not frayed, *cut*, though he didn't know that yet, wouldn't find that out until tonight — and the rigging was wrong for a solo fix, and Beatrix was ten feet up the slope with the spare line, and she was asking him something with her eyes because she'd learned to read him these past days, but what she read was *fine*, and he wasn't fine, and the word he needed —

— the word was *down*. The drum wanted to go down. Everything about the geometry screamed it. And he could not say it.

His throat closed the way it always closed. Four years ago there had been water too, and shouting, and a moment when his voice had mattered exactly as much as it ever would, and it hadn't been enough, and after that the words had simply — left. Packed their things in the night. He'd gotten used to the empty house of himself. He'd made it a skill. But skills fail at the worst moment by definition, that's what makes them skills and not miracles, and now Beatrix was leaning over the slope with her hand out and her eyes asking *what do you need*, and Tam opened his mouth and produced nothing at all.

The drum swung. The strap creaked.

And then — a voice. Low, rough, unused, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"*Down.*"

It was his own voice. That was the astonishing part. It came out of him cracked and half-strangled and absolutely clear, one syllable, and it hit Beatrix like a thrown rope.

She didn't waste a second on surprise. Later she would allow herself some; he saw it banked in her face and set aside. "Down — yes — hold still—" She came down the scree fast and low, weight on her heels, and got both hands on the drum, and together — his shoulders, her grip, the surviving strap — they walked it down the last twenty feet to flat stone and set it down as gently as an egg.

Then they both just breathed for a while.

The others arrived in a clatter of relief and questions. Ada reached him first and gripped his sleeve hard enough to leave a bruise he'd be glad of. "You *spoke*," she said. "Tam. You spoke!"

He nodded. His ears were hot. Somewhere in his chest, four years of carefully maintained emptiness had developed a draft.

"One word," Juno observed, handing him tea from a flask that had no business existing. "But it was the correct word. In my experience that puts him ahead of most talkers."

Odell was examining the parted strap, and Tam watched the cartographer's expression change as he turned the cut end in the light. Watched him go quiet. Watched him glance up the slope, at the rock that had fallen — at the place where it had come from, where no footstep of theirs had been.

Odell folded the strap away into his coat without showing anyone, and caught Tam's eye, and gave the smallest shake of his head. *Later.*

Tam, who had spent four years learning the grammar of things unsaid, understood him perfectly.

* * *

They made camp that night below the falls, in the pool's shadow, where the river ran strangely quiet after all that thunder, gathering its breath. The boat came down on rollers at dusk, all of them hauling together, Beatrix calling the count, and when the hull finally touched flat water again Ada threw her arms around the gunwale like it was a returning relative.

Dinner was Juno's mystery stew again, and someone had found cinnamon, and for an hour the crew was loud and warm and ordinary, and Tam sat among them feeling the strangeness of belonging to a conversation he couldn't enter. Except — and this was new — he didn't feel outside it. He felt like a keel. Unseen, mostly. Holding things true.

Ada sat next to him afterward, while the others argued amiably about whether Odell's sketch of the falls flattered the rock.

"You know you don't owe us an explanation," she said. "For the quiet. Whenever you're ready. Or never."

Tam turned his cup in his hands.

"But I'm glad the word came back," she added. "Even just one. It's like — finding a seed in a drawer. You don't have to plant it. It's just nice to know it's still alive."

He looked at her — seventeen, stubborn, carrying her missing father up a river that ate expeditions — and he thought about seeds, and drawers, and the word *down* sitting in his chest now like a coal that refused to cool. There were more words behind it. He could feel them. A whole winter's worth, stacked and silent. He wasn't ready. But for the first time in four years, *not ready* felt different from *never*.

Across the fire, Odell stood, stretched, and said casually — too casually — "Beatrix. Might I borrow you a moment? About the rigging."

Something in his tone made the fire seem quieter.

Beatrix looked up. Tam watched her read Odell's face the way she read water — the surface, and then the dangerous things moving under it.

"Of course," she said, and set down her cup, and the two of them walked out of the firelight together, toward the dark shape of the boat, where Odell drew the cut strap from his coat and held it up between them where the flames could reach it.

From where Tam sat, he couldn't hear what Beatrix said next.

He only saw her go very still, and then very pale, and then turn — slowly, deliberately — and look at each member of her crew in turn, counting them, the way a woman counts fingers on a hand she no longer trusts.

Five of them by the fire. Five shadows on the rock.

And somewhere out beyond the light, along the ridge above the falls, a sixth shadow detached itself from the dark and was gone.
