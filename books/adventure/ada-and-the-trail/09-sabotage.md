## 9. Sabotage

The fuel cache was Beatrix's idea, which made the discovery of what had been done to it feel like a personal insult.

She'd learned the trick on her third season guiding: never trust a single fuel store. Split it. Three drums, three locations along the bank, each one hidden differently—one sunk in the shallows under a net of river stones, one wedged in a hollow beneath the roots of a fallen cedar, one buried in a sandbank with a decoy marker planted twenty feet away for anyone who thought they were clever. Paranoia, Odell had called it, when she'd explained the system on the second morning out. She'd corrected him. *Experience.*

Now, on the sixth morning, she crouched beside the cedar-root drum with a rag in her hand and a smell in her nose that did not belong.

Petrol had a voice. Anyone who'd spent enough years around engines learned to hear it—the bright, clean bite of it, the way it stung the sinuses and promised fire. This drum's voice was wrong. Underneath the petrol there was something sweeter and heavier, like syrup gone to rot, like the inside of an old cask. She dipped the rag, sniffed, touched a drop to her tongue and spat immediately, which was a disgusting habit that had saved her life twice.

Sugar. Someone had poured a syrup—molasses, maybe, or honey cut with water—into the drum. Enough of it to gum every fuel line and carburetor they had. Run it through the engine and they'd be dead in the water at the worst possible bend of the river, which was every bend of the river.

Beatrix sat back on her heels and looked at the drum for a long moment, the way you look at a snake that has somehow gotten into your boot.

"Well," she said, to no one. "Aren't you a charmer."

* * *

She didn't tell the crew. Not yet.

That was instinct, and her instincts had been wrong before—catastrophically, expensively wrong—but they were all she had, and they said: *wait, watch, learn*. If the saboteur was among them, an announcement would only teach them to be careful. If the saboteur was following them—and the burned relay station at the first night's camp suggested someone had come this way ahead of them—then an announcement would teach them nothing at all, but it would cost her the element of surprise she didn't yet know she had.

So she sealed the drum as best she could, marked the spot in her head, and walked back to camp wearing the same expression she wore for everything these days: mild, competent, faintly bored. It was a good expression. She'd practiced it in ferry-house windows for three years.

Camp was in its usual state of cheerful chaos. Juno was arguing with a fish. This was not a metaphor; there was an actual fish, a fat gray thing the length of her forearm, and she was holding it up by the gills and informing it that it had died in vain if it didn't take her spice rub properly. Ada was mending a torn spray skirt with a bone needle, tongue between her teeth, and Odell was doing what Odell did every morning, which was redraw the same section of riverbank chart with increasingly aggressive annotations about the guild's cartographic incompetence.

Tam sat at the water's edge, as always, watching the current go by. He'd been there when she woke and he'd be there when she slept. The river seemed to be the only thing he trusted enough to look at for that long.

Beatrix counted them the way she'd once counted her team on a glacier: one, two, three, four, plus herself. Five souls. One of them—or someone behind them—had just tried to strand them all in the gorge country with the monsoon stacking up over the coast like a debt coming due.

"Fuel check," she announced. "Routine. I'll want two hours with the engine this morning, so don't plan anything heroic."

"Define heroic," said Juno, still addressing the fish.

"Anything involving you and a rope, Juno."

"That's most of my personality."

"Then plan something else."

* * *

The engine gave her a legitimate excuse for solitude. She spent the morning with her head under the cowling, tools scattered around her, and let the work settle her thoughts the way it always had. Engines were honest. Engines broke for reasons, and the reasons were always findable if you were patient. People were not engines. People broke for reasons that were sometimes invisible even to themselves, and Beatrix knew that better than most, because she'd been one of them for seven years.

*Who?* She turned the question over like a stone.

Ada. Seventeen, stubborn, desperate to find her father. Motive: none. If anything, Ada wanted this journey to succeed more than anyone alive. Unless—Beatrix's mind, being a treacherous instrument, offered this unprompted—unless Ada's desperation was an act, and her real purpose was to lure a disgraced guide with a guilty past back up the Veyl and into something. A trap. A reckoning. The letters in her bag, the uncle who'd died at the source—

Beatrix made herself stop. That way lay the kind of thinking that had gotten good people abandoned on a glacier, once, because their leader saw treachery in every silence. She'd been that leader. She remembered what it had cost.

Still. She watched Ada all through lunch. The girl laughed at Juno's impression of Odell's chart-annotating face, and the laugh was too big and too real to be manufactured. Beatrix noted it down anyway, because paranoia was a discipline, and disciplines didn't care about your feelings.

Odell. A cartographer struck off the guild rolls for mapping what he'd been ordered not to map. He had every reason to hate the companies that ran the river trade—and Beatrix's old company, Vance & Corliss Supply, had been one of them. Motive: revenge, or ideology, or the particular spite of a man who'd lost his name to a decree. But Odell had also, she remembered, been the one who insisted they carry extra water and had spent an entire evening teaching Ada to read contour lines, with the patient delight of a man handing someone a gift.

Tam. A boatman who hadn't spoken since a wreck he wouldn't explain. Tam knew boats. Tam knew fuel. Tam had been alone at the camp for an hour that morning while the rest of them broke down the tents—or had he? She tried to reconstruct it. Yes. He'd gone to check the bow line and come back ten minutes later, and she'd thought nothing of it, because Tam checking a bow line was as natural as the sun checking in on the morning.

And Juno. Juno with her smuggler's maps and her smuggler's grin and her smuggler's suspiciously comprehensive knowledge of which riverbank caches were worth robbing. Juno who had, on the second night, asked—casually, over the cookfire—exactly how much fuel they were carrying and where.

Beatrix's stomach tightened. She'd answered, because Juno was their cook and it had seemed like small talk. *Three drums. Split. Don't worry your pretty head about where.*

Juno had smiled and said, "I never worry. I season."

* * *

She started with the easy ones, which was to say she started by lying to them.

That afternoon, she gathered the crew and announced, with a straight face, that the cedar-root drum was running low—faster than expected, probably a bad seal—and that they'd need to ration. She watched faces as she said it.

Ada's brow creased with worry, quick and genuine. Odell did arithmetic with his lips and asked whether rationing would slow them below the pace needed to outrun the monsoon, which was the question of a man thinking about the schedule, not the sabotage. Tam looked at the river. Juno said, "Rationing. Fine. I'll make the stew stretch. Again. I'd like the record to show I am a miracle worker operating under conditions of sustained adversity."

"Recorded," said Beatrix.

Nobody flinched. Nobody looked away too quickly or too slowly. Which meant nothing, of course. Guilty people were often the best actors, and innocent people often looked guilty for no reason at all. Beatrix had once watched a grown guide—a grown, bearded, glacier-hardened guide—confess to eating the last of the chocolate purely because everyone was looking at him.

She checked the other two drums that evening while the crew ate. The sandbank cache was untouched. The sunken one, raised dripping from the shallows, was clean—pure petrol, bright-voiced, honest. Only the cedar drum had been hit.

Which told her something. The saboteur had found one cache and not the others. Either they didn't know her system—which pointed to an outsider, someone following the expedition's obvious stops—or they'd had only one opportunity. And the cedar drum was the one nearest camp. The one Tam had walked past that morning. The one Juno had asked about on the second night.

Or the one anyone might have stumbled across in the dark, if they'd been awake when everyone else was sleeping.

Beatrix lay in her bedroll that night and did not sleep. She listened to the camp breathe—Ada's even young breathing, Odell's occasional scholarly snore, Juno talking in her sleep about cardamom, Tam's silence, which had its own texture, a held-note silence like the pause before a downbeat.

Around two in the morning, she heard it. A footstep. Soft, careful, at the edge of the camp, moving toward the boats.

She was up with her knife before the second step landed, moving low and silent along the tent line, heart going like a drum in a parade. She rounded the stern of the boat and—

Ada. Standing in the shallows in her nightclothes, holding a lantern with the shutter half closed, staring at something in the water.

"Ada." Beatrix kept her voice flat. Her knife hand, she noticed, did not entirely agree to stay flat.

The girl jumped a foot. "Beatrix! I—there was a light. Out on the water. I saw a light, way upriver, and it went out, and I thought—" She stopped, and in the sliver of lantern light her face was pale and young and frightened. "I thought maybe it was my father's boat. I know it's stupid. I know it's been weeks. But I had to look."

Beatrix looked upriver. Black water, black hills, no light. If there had been one, it was gone—and a light that showed itself and then vanished could be a signal, or a mistake, or bait.

"Next time you see a light," Beatrix said, "you wake me first. Not because I don't trust you to look. Because if there's someone out there, I'd rather they meet both of us than just the one of you."

Ada's shoulders came down an inch. "You think someone's out there too."

"I think," Beatrix said carefully, "that the river's been too quiet to be empty. Go back to bed, Ada."

She watched the girl go, and then she stood in the cold shallows for a long while, letting the water pull at her ankles, and admitted the thing she'd been not-admitting since morning: the light changed everything. A light upriver meant eyes. A watcher. And if there was a watcher, then the sugar in the fuel drum might not have come from inside the camp at all.

It might have come from someone who'd visited in the night, the way they'd visited the relay station with a torch.

Or it might not. That was the winch, she thought. That was the exact feeling of a winch tightening: every fact pulling two directions at once, and her standing in the middle holding a rope that could cut her hands if she let it run.

* * *

In the morning she tested the last of her theories, and it was almost pleasant, in the way that pulling a tooth is almost pleasant once it's over.

She waited until Juno went down to the water with her washing, then said, casually, to the camp at large: "Good news. The low drum was a bad seal after all. Barely lost anything. We're fine on fuel."

Juno, thirty feet away, mid-scrub of a shirt, did not react at all. No flinch, no relief, no guilty stillness. She just kept scrubbing and shouted back, "Then I'll take cream in my coffee after all, since we're rich."

Ada whooped. Odell muttered something about fuel reserves and the folly of optimism.

And Tam—Tam set down the rope he was coiling, walked over to Beatrix, and held out one open hand. In his palm: a small tin, the kind that once held pipe tobacco, and inside it, a smear of dark syrup on a scrap of cloth.

He looked at her. He looked at the tin. He tapped the cloth once with one blunt finger, then pointed—upriver, toward the hills, toward the burned relay station—and then made a shape with his hand, slow and deliberate, like a man pouring something out of a bottle.

Beatrix stared at him. "You found this?"

Tam nodded.

"Where?"

He pointed again: upriver. Then he held up three fingers, then folded one down. Two. Two days back. Near the first camp. Near the burned station.

Someone had spilled the syrup there—decanted it from its container, maybe, or tested the mix—two days before the drum was hit. Before they'd even reached the cedar cache. Which meant the saboteur had prepared the contamination in advance, carried it, and used it on the one drum they'd found.

Tam put the tin in her hand, closed her fingers over it, and went back to his rope. On the way he paused, and without turning around, he spoke—the second word Beatrix had ever heard him say, low and rough as river gravel:

"Watch."

Then he was coiling rope again as if he'd said nothing at all, because as far as Tam was concerned, he had.

* * *

Beatrix spent the rest of that day in a state of focused, humming alertness, and the strange thing—she noticed it around midday, poling the boat through a slow reach while Ada called out rocks from the bow—was that the fear had changed shape.

Yesterday it had been a noose: *one of them, one of them, which one.* Today it was a map. Someone was ahead of them. Someone knew the route, knew the caches, knew the relay stations, and was dismantling this journey piece by piece with a patience that frightened her more than any rage could have. Rage made mistakes. Patience didn't.

And there was one more thing, small and cold, that she turned over as the gorge walls began to rise around them. The syrup had been carried from two days back. Which meant the saboteur had been moving with them, or ahead of them, the whole way—and had chosen the cedar drum, the one nearest camp, the one they were guaranteed to find.

Guaranteed to find.

Beatrix's hands went still on the pole.

A saboteur who wanted them stopped would hide the contamination. Let them run the engine dry, or seize it, or die of it. But a saboteur who wanted them *scared*—who wanted them to find it, to know they were being hunted, to feel the winch tighten—would leave the evidence exactly where a careful guide would look.

*This is a message,* the sugar had said. *Turn back.*

She thought of the burned relay station. Of the light on the water. Of a figure, somewhere in these rising hills, who had been to the source before—or who had never left it.

"Beatrix?" Ada's voice, from the bow. "You've gone quiet. Quieter than Tam, and that's saying something."

"Just navigating," Beatrix said.

But that night, when they made camp in the shadow of the first true gorge walls, she took Odell aside, showed him the tin, and told him everything—the drum, the syrup, the light, Tam's two-day-old evidence. Odell listened with his chart-inked fingers pressed together, and when she finished, he was quiet for a long moment.

"You know what's above the gorge country," he said at last. It wasn't a question. "You know what's between here and the source."

"I know what the charts say."

"The charts say nothing. That's rather the point." He looked up at the walls, black against the stars. "Whoever's out there knows this river better than the guild does. Better than the companies. Which means, Beatrix—" and his voice dropped, gentle and terrible, "—they learned it the same way we're learning it now. The hard way. The way you did."

Beatrix looked upriver, into the dark throat of the gorge, and somewhere in the black above them—she was almost sure of it now, almost—something moved along the ridgeline. There and gone. Like a lantern shutter closing.

Like someone watching to see whether they'd take the hint.

"Sleep in shifts," she said. "Starting now."
