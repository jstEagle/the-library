## 10. Ada's Evidence

The letters had been riding against Ada's ribs for eleven days.

She'd sewn them into the lining of her jacket back in Kessel's Landing, the night before departure, in her room above the chandlery with a borrowed needle and a candle burning down to a stub. She'd told herself she was being practical. Documents were documents; rivers ate luggage. Any sensible person would protect their papers.

Any sensible person did not sleep with their hand pressed flat over their own heartbeat to feel the paper crinkle, but that was what Ada had done every night since, and she was tired of pretending otherwise.

Tonight the crew camped on a gravel bar below the second gorge, where the Veyl widened and went suddenly, suspiciously gentle, like a dog that had decided not to bite yet. The fire was small — Beatrix's rules, always small fires, always downwind of the tents — and Juno was doing something criminal to a pot of lentils and calling it dinner. Odell was re-inking a chart by firelight, tongue between his teeth. Tam sat at the edge of the light sharpening a knife that was already sharp, which was how Tam prayed, as far as anyone could tell.

And Beatrix was apart, as always. Sitting on an overturned crate at the waterline, mending a frayed painter with quick, economical stitches, her face turned toward the current as if it might say something worth hearing.

Ada stood up. Her legs felt like they belonged to someone taller.

"You've got that look," Juno said without looking up from the pot. "The look you get before you do something."

"I'm going to talk to her."

"Mm." Juno stirred. "Want me to make more lentils for after? People are always hungry after."

"That's not comforting, Juno."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be about lentils." She waved a wooden spoon toward the waterline. "Go on. I'll keep the fire honest."

* * *

Ada walked down the shingle, each step loud in her own ears. Gravel is not a discreet surface. By the fourth step Beatrix had stopped stitching, though she didn't turn around.

"Whatever it is," Beatrix said, "the answer about the fuel line is no, we're not rerouting through the north channel, and before you argue, Odell already tried and he brought charts."

"It's not about the channel."

"Then it's about the pace, and the answer is also no—"

"Vance Expedition Supply," Ada said. "Seventh year of the Veyl charting run. Four souls upriver, one soul came back, and the company burned the report and blacklisted the guide."

The stitches stopped entirely now. The river filled the silence, patient and enormous.

"Where did you hear that name?" Beatrix asked quietly. Not *what*. Not *who told you*. She asked where, the way a person asks where the leak is in a hull.

"My father talked about it my whole childhood. The Vance run. He wasn't on it — he'd broken his ankle in spring and stayed home, and he never forgave himself for it, because his brother went instead." Ada's voice held, barely. She'd rehearsed this part on the water for days and it still came out wrong, too fast, too flat. "My uncle Tomas. He was the surveyor. They found his kit bag forty miles downstream. Just the bag."

Beatrix set down the needle. Set down the painter. Turned around slowly, and her face in the firelight was doing something complicated — not guilt exactly, or not only guilt. Something older and wearier, like a door that had been locked so long the lock had rusted into the frame.

"Say what you came to say," Beatrix said.

So Ada reached inside her jacket, tore the stitching she herself had sewn eleven days ago, and pulled out the letters.

Three of them. She laid them out on the flat rock between them, one by one, like tarot cards, like evidence, like an accusation.

"This one's a contract of passage. Signed by the guide of record for the seventh-year Veyl run." She tapped the first page. "This one's a letter my father kept, from the company, confirming the guide of record refused the turnback vote at Marker Nine and ordered the crossing into the upper gorge. And this one—" her hand shook, and she hated it, hated that her hand shook when she wanted so badly to be granite — "this one is the blacklist notice. The guide's name is on all three. In ink. In your handwriting."

She looked up.

"Beatrix Thackeray isn't your real name. It's Beatrix Vane. You're the guide who killed my uncle."

* * *

The words landed the way she'd imagined they would for eleven days: like dropping an anchor through thin ice. She waited for the crack, the plunge, the drowning.

What happened instead was that Beatrix Vane looked at the letters for a long time, and then said, "May I?"

Ada nodded, not trusting her voice.

Beatrix picked up the first letter and read it as if it were scripture she'd once known by heart and had deliberately forgotten. Her thumb moved along the signature line and stopped there.

"Yes," she said. "That's mine."

Something in Ada's chest, wound tight for eleven days, snapped loose and swung wild. "That's it? That's all? *Yes, that's mine?*"

"What would you like me to say instead?" Beatrix asked, and there was no coldness in it, which somehow made it worse. "I can tell you the crossing was justified by the weather. I can tell you the company altered the river gauge readings. I can tell you I was twenty-six years old and the most decorated guide on the Veyl and absolutely certain I was right." She folded the letter along its original crease, precisely, reverently. "All true. None of it brings him back. I've had seven years to find the sentence that does that, Ada. It doesn't exist."

"You could have told me." Ada heard her own voice crack and let it. "At the dock. When I begged you. You looked me in the eye and you said you'd take me as far as the relay station, and the whole time — the whole *time*, Beatrix, you knew. You knew who my family was. You knew what I was carrying."

"I knew who you were the moment you said your surname," Beatrix said. "You have his chin. Did you know that? Tomas Vance had a chin like the prow of a boat. Your father wrote to me, after. One letter. I still have it. He didn't blame me. That was the worst part. He wrote, *I know you did everything a person could do, and I need you to live long enough for that to matter.*"

"And did it?" Ada's voice came out ragged. "Did it matter? Because from where I stand, you ran to a river town, changed your name, and fixed ferry engines for seven years while my grandmother died not knowing where her son actually—"

"Don't." For the first time, something moved behind Beatrix's eyes like ice shifting on a spring river. "Don't tell me what those seven years were. You think I hid? I did hide, yes. But I also read the river reports every season. Every flood, every body recovered, every crate of salvage washed up between here and the delta, I checked. Seven years, Ada. I checked whether any of it was his. So don't stand there and tell me I forgot him. Nobody on this earth has thought about Tomas Vance more than I have, except possibly you."

The gravel bar seemed very far away suddenly. The fire, the lentils, Odell's scratching pen — all of it receded, and there was just the two of them and the dark water going past, going down, going home to a sea neither of them was facing.

Ada wrapped her arms around herself. "Why did you come, then? When I begged you at the dock. If you knew who I was. Why not just refuse and let the town's cowards keep their consciences clean?"

"Because a customs officer was about to hang my old name around my neck like a millstone, and running upstream was the only direction left." Beatrix said it flatly, without decoration, and Ada realized with a strange lurch that this — this refusal to dress it up — was its own kind of respect. "But that's the reason I gave myself. The real reason is that a seventeen-year-old girl stood on a dock with her whole heart in her hands and nobody in that town would take it, and I remembered being twenty-six and certain, and I thought: someone should go with her who knows exactly how much this river can take from a person. Someone should be there who owes it."

"Owes it," Ada repeated. "To whom?"

"To him. To you. To the version of me who made the call at Marker Nine." Beatrix looked out at the water. "Take your pick. The debt doesn't care whose name is on it."

* * *

They stood there a while. The river did its work of filling silences; it was very good at that. Eventually Ada became aware of a presence at the top of the shingle — Juno, holding two tin cups, radiating the studied nonchalance of someone who had absolutely been listening.

"Lentils are done," Juno announced to the middle distance. "Also the fire's fine, Odell's fine, Tam sharpened the same knife four times, which I believe is a record. There's tea. It's terrible. It's the only kind we have."

She set the cups down on the rock next to the letters, with the delicacy of a diplomat laying down terms, and withdrew.

Ada looked at the tea. Looked at the letters. Looked at the woman who had ruined her family and then rowed her through the Serrated Narrows with hands steady as a surgeon's.

"I should turn back," Ada said. It came out steadier than anything else she'd said all night. "That's what a smart person does. Finds out her guide is the person responsible for the worst day of her family's life, and turns the boat around."

"Probably," Beatrix agreed.

"But my father is upriver. Or was. Eleven days of silence, Beatrix. The relay station was burned. Someone crushed a crate with your company mark and left it in the shallows like a message. And in nine days—" she stopped. Recounted. Felt her stomach drop. "In eight days the monsoon makes landfall, and retreat takes longer than advance, and every hour I spend hating you on this gravel bar is an hour off my father's life if he's alive to lose it."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. "You've been doing arithmetic."

"I've been doing nothing *but* arithmetic. It's very lonely up here in my head. There's just numbers and my dead uncle."

A sound escaped Beatrix then — short, startled, gone almost before it arrived. It took Ada a full second to identify it as a laugh. A terrible, exhausted, genuine laugh.

"He argued with the cook the entire sixth-year run," Beatrix said. "Tomas. About salt. Said too much salt was cowardice. Six weeks on a river eating dried fish and he fought about *salt*." She shook her head slowly. "You argue like him. Same jaw-set. Same refusal to lose an argument even when losing is clearly the correct strategic choice."

"Are you saying I should turn back?"

"No." Beatrix met her eyes, and whatever Ada had expected to find there — pity, evasion, the smooth mask of the ferry mechanic — none of it was present. What was there was raw and unarmored and looked, frankly, exhausted. "I'm saying that if you turn back, I'll row you down myself and I won't resent you for it. And if you go on, I'll row you up, and I won't resent you for that either. But I'm done lying to you, Ada. From tonight forward, everything I know, you know. Even the parts that make me look worse than you already think I am. Especially those."

Ada looked down at the letters on the rock. Three pages of proof. She'd carried them against her heart for eleven days waiting for this moment, imagining it would feel like victory.

It felt like setting down something heavy and discovering her hands didn't know what to do without the weight.

"We're not finished," she said. "This isn't forgiveness. I want to be extremely clear about that. My father may be alive upriver and I need you, and that is a different thing from forgiving you, and I will be angry about it probably for the rest of my natural life, and you're going to have to put up with that."

"Noted," Beatrix said.

"And when we find him — *if* we find him — you're going to tell him yourself. Face to face. What you did. What happened to Tomas. All of it. I'm not carrying that letter for you."

"All right," Beatrix said softly. "Yes."

Ada picked up the letters, folded them away into her jacket — she'd need new stitching, better stitching — and picked up the terrible tea. It was, as promised, terrible. She drank it anyway.

They walked back up the shingle together, not quite side by side, a careful arm's length of grief between them. At the fire, Odell glanced up, assessed the situation with the speed of a man who had spent his life reading terrain, and wisely said nothing except, "There's lentils."

"There are always lentils," Ada said, and sat down.

It was Tam, later, as the fire burned low, who broke the circle of quiet. He didn't speak — Tam didn't speak — but he touched Beatrix's sleeve and pointed upstream, toward the black shoulder of the gorge, and then held up his fingers.

One. Two. Three.

A light. Far up on the ridge, small and steady, where no light had any business being. It hung there a moment — watching, the way a watcher watches — and then it winked out, deliberate as a closing eye.

Beatrix rose slowly to her feet, staring at the empty darkness where it had been.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly, "we make good distance. Whatever's up there, I want it behind us by noon."

Nobody asked what she'd seen. Nobody needed to. But Ada lay awake a long time in her bedroll, one hand over the letters against her ribs, listening to the river run north toward the source, and thinking: *someone up there knows we're coming. Someone up there has been waiting.*

Above her, the clouds coming down from the mountains had begun, very faintly, to smell like rain.
