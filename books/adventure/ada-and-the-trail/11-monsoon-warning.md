## 11. Monsoon Warning

The signal came in at dawn, crackling out of the little receiver Odell had rigged on the boat's cabin roof like a dying wasp trapped in a tin cup.

Beatrix heard it from the galley, where she was pretending that the coffee she'd made was drinkable. It wasn't. River water boiled three times and strained through a sock had a way of asserting itself. She was on her third sip when Ada's voice cut through the morning fog, sharp as a fish hook.

"Odell. What does it say? What does it *say*?"

"Patience," Odell murmured, the way he always did when he wanted everyone to know he was enjoying himself. He hunched over the receiver, pencil scratching. "Station at Greywater Bend. Weather dispatch, relayed from the coast." A pause. His pencil stopped.

"Odell."

"Monsoon makes landfall in nine days."

The word landed on the boat like a dropped anchor. Beatrix set down her cup. Around her, the river kept doing what rivers do—murmuring, pushing, indifferent—but everything human aboard went very still.

"Nine days," Ada repeated. She said it the way you'd say a diagnosis.

"And," Odell went on, because he was constitutionally incapable of delivering bad news without an accompanying footnote, "the dispatch estimates flood stage on the lower Veyl within twelve. Which means—" he did the arithmetic aloud, lips moving, "—retreating now would take us eleven days back to Kessel's Landing. Pushing forward to the source is six, maybe seven if the gorges behave."

"They won't behave," Tam said.

Everyone turned. Tam spoke perhaps four times a season, and each utterance arrived with the weight of scripture. He stood at the stern with a rope coiled over one shoulder, looking not at any of them but at the water, which had gone a color Beatrix didn't love—a bruised green, thick with silt from rain falling somewhere upstream where they couldn't see it.

"He's right," Beatrix said. "They never do."

* * *

They held the vote on the gravel bar at midmorning, because Beatrix refused to make this decision inside the cabin, where tempers could fester in the dark like things growing under a tarp. She'd learned that much from her last expedition, among other lessons—the kind that came stamped and notarized in the form of a blacklist.

She laid it out plainly, squatting by the fire with a stick and a stretch of flat stone. Two lines drawn in the ash. One going back. One going up.

"Back is eleven days of retreating through rising water, ending in a town where I'm one customs officer's memo away from ruin, and Ada ends up with nothing but a father still missing and a story nobody will print. Forward is seven days into gorge country with a monsoon on our heels, ending at a place I swore I'd never see again." She tapped the upper line. "I'm not going to pretend those are equal choices. They're both bad. One of them is bad with a chance of answers at the end of it."

"That's not comforting," Juno said.

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be honest, which is rarer and less comfortable." She looked around the circle. "We vote. Everyone. Tam, you too—you can nod or shake your head, or write it in the sand if you'd rather. Nobody rides along on somebody else's gamble. Not after last time."

That last part cost her something to say. *Not after last time.* Seven years ago she hadn't asked. Seven years ago she'd decided, because she was the guide and the guide decides, and four people had followed her order into a valley that then decided differently. The habit of command had died hard in her; the guilt had died harder, which is to say not at all, just changed shape, the way ice changes shape but stays cold.

Ada voted first. Of course she did. "Forward," she said, and her voice didn't shake, which told Beatrix more about how much it had cost her than shaking would have.

Juno shrugged with her whole body, which was how she did most things. "Forward. My maps end at the source anyway. Might as well see what the cartographers were paid to forget."

Tam wrote his vote in the sand with one finger. An arrow, pointing upstream. Then he studied it a moment, as if checking his own work, and nodded once.

All eyes went to Odell, who had produced, god help them all, an actual list of pros and cons, written in surveyor's shorthand on the back of a chart fragment.

"You're not seriously reading that," Juno said.

"I am seriously reading that." He cleared his throat. "Con: drowning, rockfall, hypothermia, monsoon, Merrick—assuming our ridge-line friend is who we're starting to think he is. Pros: the truth, the possibility of survivors, the fact that retreat through eleven days of floodwater is statistically worse than advance through seven days of it, and—" he paused, and for once the dryness fell away from his voice, "—and because I spent nine years being punished for drawing a line on a map that someone powerful didn't want drawn. I would like to walk to the end of that line. Professionally speaking. Personally speaking, I'd like to spit on it, but the guild revoked my license, not my dignity."

He looked at Beatrix. "Forward."

Which left her.

They all knew it left her. She was the guide; her vote was the keel under the boat whether she cast it or not. She could have hidden behind procedure—guide's discretion, final call, all the old language of authority. Instead she picked up the stick and drew a third mark on the stone, next to the two lines: a small, lopsided X, upstream of both.

"Forward," she said. And then, because the morning deserved one clean breath of something other than dread: "But I want it noted for the record that every person on this boat voted to follow a blacklisted guide into a forbidden valley during monsoon season, and when the inquiry board asks, I intend to tell them you were all mad long before you met me."

Ada laughed—a short, startled sound, surprised out of her—and Juno cackled, and even Tam's mouth did something at one corner that in a more expressive man might have been called a smile.

It lasted four seconds. Then Beatrix stood, brushed ash off her knees, and became again the woman they needed her to be.

"Rig for fast water. Everything lashed double. Juno, break out the heavy-weather rations—we eat well tonight, because tomorrow we won't. Odell, I want your gorge charts spread on the deck before we cast off. And Ada—" she hesitated, then made herself finish it, "—Ada, come walk the bow line with me. You should learn what slack looks like before it matters."

* * *

They cast off at noon into a river that had begun, quietly, to change its mind about them.

Beatrix felt it through her boots before she saw it anywhere: a new vibration in the hull, a deeper pull. Upstream, somewhere past the bend, the Veyl was gathering what the mountains were giving it. By afternoon the current had thickened until the boat ran a full knot faster without extra fuel, and the eddies along the banks spun with a lazy, muscular menace, like dogs deciding whether to bite.

She stood at the helm and read the water the way other people read letters from home—with hunger and fear in equal measure. There was craft in it still. That was the thing nobody warned you about disgrace: the skill didn't leave. It sat in your hands like a knife you weren't allowed to use, sharpening itself on nothing, waiting.

"Gorge country starts at the Narrows Above Stonebridge," she said, when Odell brought the charts aft. She weighted the corners with fuel cans. "Fifty miles of canyon with walls close enough to spit across. No banks to land on worth the name. If the rain catches us in there, we ride it out anchored to the rock, and we pray the anchors hold, because there is no portage. None. Whatever goes in that canyon comes out the far end or doesn't come out."

"Cheerful," said Juno.

"Honest," said Beatrix. "You voted for it."

Odell traced the survey line with his fingertip, the one only she could confirm, the one that had ended his career. "And the old campsite?"

Her hands went still on the wheel.

"Day five," she said finally. "If the water lets us keep schedule. Day five, we'll pass the place where my expedition stopped being an expedition and started being a search party." She kept her eyes on the channel ahead. "I'll point it out. That's all I can promise. Pointing is more than I've managed in seven years."

Nobody said anything clever to that, which Beatrix appreciated. There was a species of kindness that consisted entirely of not filling a silence, and her crew—her strays, her misfits, her band of the professionally ruined—were fluent in it.

* * *

That night they camped on a shelf of rock above a long riffle, and Beatrix lay awake listening to the river get louder.

It wasn't louder, really. Rivers don't raise their voices. But the pitch of it had changed, the way a crowd changes before bad news, and she'd heard this exact change once before, seven years ago, on the night before the crossing she'd ordered. She remembered lying in her tent thinking: *the water sounds nervous.* She remembered dismissing it. She remembered, with the terrible fidelity that memory reserves for its cruelest work, the exact temperature of the coffee she drank the next morning while she told her team the crossing would be routine.

Routine. She had used that word. She had used it about the source of the Veyl.

She gave up on sleep and climbed out onto the rocks, and found she wasn't the only one awake. Ada sat wrapped in a blanket near the cold fire ring, knees up, chin down, staring at the water with her father's stubborn jaw set against the dark.

"You should be sleeping," Beatrix said. "Tomorrow's a hard day."

"So is the day after. So is every day until we find him." Ada didn't look over. "Do you ever actually sleep, or do you just lie there auditing the river?"

"Auditing the river," Beatrix admitted. "It keeps sloppy books."

That got half a smile out of the girl. Then the smile faded, and Ada asked the question Beatrix had been waiting for since the vote, the one that had been building pressure behind her eyes all day like weather of its own.

"If we find out what happened—if we find him, or find out why we won't—will it fix anything? The blacklist. Your name. Any of it?"

Beatrix considered lying. She was, historically, quite good at it; she'd built a whole second life out of the material. But the girl had voted to follow her into a canyon, and some debts you don't pay in counterfeit.

"No," she said. "Some things don't get fixed. They get *told*, and telling isn't fixing—it's just making sure the lie stops winning." She sat down on the rock beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to keep the distance that was fair. "Seven years ago, men with good handwriting decided what happened at the source, and their version has been the official one ever since. Whatever we find, whatever the truth costs me—the one thing I can promise is that their version stops being the only one."

"And if the truth costs more than that? If it costs—" Ada's voice caught, steadied. "If it turns out you did what they said you did?"

"Then I'll stand in front of whoever's asking and say so." The words came out of her flat and certain, and Beatrix realized with a small internal shock that she meant them—that somewhere between the burned relay station and the crushed crate and the letters Ada carried, the old terror of exposure had quietly drowned, and what floated in its place was something harder and lighter at once. "I've been paying interest on a debt for seven years, Ada. At some point you just want the principal called in."

Ada was quiet a long moment. Then, in the tone of a seventeen-year-old delivering a verdict she'd been assembling for days: "You're not what I expected. When I found you at the dock, I thought you'd be a coward who used to be brave."

"And now?"

"Now I think you're brave who used to be a coward. It's better. But it took you longer to get here, so don't be smug about it."

Beatrix laughed—actually laughed, out loud, into the teeth of the coming storm—and beside her Ada grinned like she'd just landed a fish bigger than the boat.

* * *

Dawn came gray and low, with the smell of wet stone riding down-canyon on a wind that hadn't been there yesterday. The first drops hit the deck while they were striking camp—fat, scattered, unhurried, scouts sent ahead of an army.

By the time they cast off, the walls of the first gorge had risen on either side of them, sheer and dripping, close enough that voices came back off the stone. The river narrowed, quickened, turned the color of gunmetal. Somewhere ahead, thunder rolled—not the sky's thunder, Beatrix noted, with a guide's cold professional attention, but the deeper, longer roll of water working through rock.

She stood at the wheel with the rain beginning to speckle her shoulders and led them in.

Behind her, the crew moved without being told—Juno securing the cargo, Odell calling depths off his charts, Tam at the bow line like a figurehead that occasionally pointed at rocks, Ada watching everything with her father's eyes. Her strange, disgraced, indispensable family, sailing into the throat of the country that had eaten her past.

Halfway through the first mile of canyon, Odell looked up from his charts and frowned at the high southern rim, where the mist was shredding in the new wind.

"Beatrix," he said quietly. "On the ridge. Third notch from the spire. Tell me that's a dead tree."

Beatrix looked. The figure standing motionless against the gray sky did not look like a tree. It looked like a man in gear ten years out of date, watching them go by, and as the boat carried them beneath him, he raised one arm—not waving, she thought. Counting.

Counting heads.

"Get below," Beatrix said, and put the wheel hard over into the rapid.
