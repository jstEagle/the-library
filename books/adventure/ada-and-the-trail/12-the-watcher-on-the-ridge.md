## 12. The Watcher on the Ridge

Juno had three rules for cooking in the gorge country, and she was breaking all of them at once.

Rule one: never cook with your back to the water. Rule two: never trust a pot that boils faster than it should, because that meant altitude and altitude meant bad decisions. Rule three—and this one she'd learned from a smuggler named Ferro who'd once lost two fingers to a river patrol—*always know where everyone is.*

She broke rule three because the crew had scattered into their evening rituals: Odell hunched over his charts like a man praying to a jealous god, Tam checking the boat's seams for the fourth time since lunch, Ada hauling water up from the river with the stubborn rhythm of someone proving something to nobody. And Beatrix—

Beatrix was standing at the treeline, very still, looking up.

Juno followed her gaze to the ridge.

The ridge ran along the eastern wall of the gorge, a jagged spine of granite and pine maybe four hundred feet above them. In the last hour of light, its western face went gold and then amber and then a deep bruised purple, and everything on it stood out in silhouette—the trees, the boulders, the scree slopes, and the figure.

The figure was not a tree. The figure was not a boulder. The figure was a person, standing at the crest with the particular stillness of someone who had been watching for a long time and did not intend to stop.

"Huh," said Juno, and set down her ladle.

She'd spent eleven years running cargo that didn't want to be counted, and there was a taxonomy to being watched. There were patrols, who wanted you to see them so you'd behave. There were rivals, who didn't care if you saw them because they had more guns. And then there was the third kind, the rarest and worst: the kind who watched because they were deciding something about you, and hadn't finished deciding.

This was the third kind. She could feel it in her back teeth.

"Beatrix," she said quietly, not turning her head. "How long has our friend been up there?"

"Since before the bend," Beatrix said. Her voice was flat as slate. "Maybe an hour. Maybe longer."

"You didn't say anything?"

"I wasn't sure." A pause. "I'm sure now."

Juno finally looked away from the ridge, casual as anything, and raised her voice to kitchen-volume. "Soup's on! Lentil and smoked fish, and if anyone complains about the smoked fish again they can cook tomorrow."

Ada came up the bank with the water yoke across her shoulders, cheeks pink. "I don't complain about the smoked fish."

"You complained Tuesday."

"I *observed* that it was mostly bones."

"That's called flavor concentration, girl." But Juno's heart wasn't in the banter, because Beatrix was moving through camp with a new geometry, positioning herself so her body blocked Ada's sightline to the ridge, and Juno understood: the guide didn't want the kid spooked. Not yet.

Tam appeared at her elbow without sound, as he always did, and touched her shoulder once—a question.

"Ridge," she murmured. "East side. Don't point."

He didn't point. He just went still in that way he had, all the noise of him draining out until he was practically furniture, and then he drifted toward the boat with a coil of line over his arm, doing nothing, being no one, seeing everything. Juno had worked with smugglers, mercenaries, and one memorable nun-turned-fence, and Tam was the best natural liar she'd ever met precisely because he never spoke. Words were where lies got caught. He was all clean surface.

They ate. The soup was good—Juno made sure of it, because scared people eat better when the food is good, and she suspected everyone at this fire was somewhere between uneasy and afraid, even if only she and Beatrix knew why. Odell talked about contour lines. Ada asked questions about contour lines. Somewhere above them, the light died off the ridge entirely, and whatever stood there became indistinguishable from stone.

"Early night," Beatrix said, when the plates were scraped. "Long portage tomorrow. Double watch."

Nobody argued. That was the thing about this crew, Juno thought as she banked the fire—they'd each been somewhere bad enough that orders like *double watch* landed without explanation. They just nodded, and checked their knives, and lay down facing outward.

Juno took first watch. She sat with her back against a boulder, a blanket over her knees and a cup of chicory going cold in her hands, and she watched the ridge the way she used to watch customs cutters: patiently, hungrily, letting her eyes do laps until the landscape memorized itself and any change would ring out like a dropped plate.

Around midnight, the moon cleared the eastern wall and poured silver down the scree, and she saw it again—or thought she did. Movement near the crest. Not walking. *Crouching.* Something low and deliberate, there and gone behind a granite tooth.

She woke Beatrix with two fingers on her shoulder, the way you wake soldiers.

"Still there?" Beatrix whispered.

"Crouching now. Moving along the crest, north to south. Following us."

Beatrix was silent a moment. Then: "It knows where we're sleeping. It could come down anytime it liked. It doesn't." She said it not with fear but with the tone of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "That's not a raider. Raiders come down."

"What is it, then?"

"I don't know yet." Beatrix's jaw worked. "But it's been keeping pace with us since the burned relay station, and I'm done pretending otherwise. Tomorrow we talk to the crew. All of them."

* * *

Morning came gray and cold, with the smell of rain that hadn't committed yet. They ate hardtack and dried apples standing up, and Beatrix laid it out plainly, which Juno respected: someone or something had been tracking them from the high ground since the Narrows. It had not attacked. It had not shown itself. Until they knew what it wanted, they traveled armed, traveled tight, and nobody wandered.

Ada's face went through three expressions in quick succession—fear, then a flash of anger, then a fierce bright resolve that reminded Juno uncomfortably of her father, whom Juno had met exactly once and remembered as a man who shook hands like he was signing a contract. "Then we go faster," Ada said.

"We go *careful* faster," Beatrix corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is staying alive."

Odell spread his charts on a flat rock and traced their route with a cracked thumbnail. "If it's following the ridge, it's limited too. The crest breaks down past those towers—" he tapped a cluster of jagged marks "—and doesn't pick up again for six miles. If we make good time today, we leave it behind."

"Or it comes down ahead of us," Tam wrote on his slate, and held it up. He'd started writing more since the portage. Nobody commented on it; commenting would have been like applauding a bird for flying.

"Or that," Beatrix agreed grimly.

They ran the morning water fast and clean, Juno wedged amidships between the cargo with a boathook and a view of both banks, because Beatrix had assigned her the watching and Juno intended to watch like it was paid work. The gorge narrowed. The walls climbed. The river went from green to a churning white-brown, and the towers Odell had marked rose ahead like broken teeth.

And there—third tower, left side, maybe two hundred feet up—a flicker.

Not imagination. Not a bird. A shape, angular and wrong, moving parallel to the boat with an ease that made her stomach drop. She got the boathook and rapped it twice against the hull, their signal, and Beatrix's head snapped around, and Juno pointed with her chin.

For ten seconds they ran in silence, the whole crew's eyes crawling up the rock face.

"It's wearing a pack," Ada breathed. "I can see straps. And—" she squinted against the spray "—it's orange. Part of it. The jacket."

"Orange," Odell repeated, and his voice had gone strange.

"You recognize it?"

"Veyl Survey Company issue," Odell said slowly. "High-visibility field shell. They phased that color out—" he swallowed "—ten years ago. Eleven. I mapped for them, before. Before my disgrace." His thumb worried the edge of his chart. "Nobody wears that jacket anymore. Nobody *has* that jacket anymore."

The shape paused on a ledge, and for one impossible moment it seemed to look directly down at them—not a silhouette now but a person, small with distance, deliberate as a chess piece. Then it stepped back behind the rock and was gone.

Nobody spoke until the towers fell astern.

* * *

They made camp early in a hollow beneath an overhang, a defensible spot Beatrix picked with a surveyor's economy, and built the smallest fire Juno had ever cooked on—coals barely bigger than knuckles, pot balanced on stones, smoke so thin it vanished before it cleared the treetops. Juno made stew anyway, because rules were rules, and if the world was ending you might as well end it fed.

The mood at supper was different tonight. Not panicked—this crew didn't panic, or rather they'd each done their panicking privately years ago—but tight, like rope drawn to working load. Ada kept glancing at the dark mouth of the hollow. Odell kept glancing at nothing, which for him meant thinking. Tam sat closest to the entrance, whetstone whispering over his knife in slow, even strokes, and even that sound felt like conversation.

"All right," Beatrix said at last, setting down her bowl. "Let's say it out loud. Someone is following us. They're dressed in gear from the old Veyl Survey expeditions. They've had every chance to hurt us and haven't taken it." She looked around the fire. "Which means they want something other than our deaths. For now."

"Could be a hermit," Juno offered. "Some old prospector living up there in a dead man's jacket."

"In expedition-grade kit, tracking five boats' worth of strangers along a ridge line for days?" Odell shook his head. "Hermits avoid people. This one *studies* us."

"Then what does it want?" Ada asked.

Silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered—the monsoon clearing its throat, weeks early or right on time depending on how much luck they had left, which Juno estimated as none.

"I think," Beatrix said carefully, "that it wants us to turn back. And that it's been trying to tell us so in the only language it trusts." Her eyes moved around the circle. "Burned relay station. Salvage where salvage shouldn't be. Now this. Every message so far has said the same thing: *go home.*"

"And if we don't?" Ada asked.

"Then eventually it stops messaging," Beatrix said, "and starts doing."

Juno looked at the faces around the fire—the cartographer with his erased maps, the mute boatman with his knife, the girl with her father's stubbornness, the guide with seven years of secrets stacked behind her eyes—and thought, not for the first time, that she'd signed on to cook for a rescue and somehow joined a ghost story.

"Well," she said, standing and gathering bowls, "whatever it wants, it can want it after dessert. I found wild plums."

* * *

Second watch was Tam's, then Ada's, then Beatrix's till dawn. Juno slept in her bedroll under the overhang with her boots on and her knife inside her coat, which she'd done for eleven years and expected to do for eleven more.

She woke once, deep in the dead hours, to a sound she couldn't name—a soft, fibrous *tick*, like a knuckle cracking in another room. She lay listening. Wind in the pines. Water on stone. Nothing else. She told herself it was the cold shrinking the rocks, and slept.

Dawn came pale and smelling of wet stone, and it was Ada who found it, because Ada was the one who went down to the boats first to pack.

Her shout brought them all running.

The spare rope—the good rope, the hundred-foot coil of braided core they'd need for the weir portage, the rope Beatrix had inspected personally two nights ago—lay in the bottom of the boat where it always lay. Except it wasn't a coil anymore. It was two lengths, cut clean through the middle, the severed ends lying side by side with an almost courteous neatness.

And it hadn't been cut with a knife. Juno crouched and touched the ends, and her stomach turned over. The fibers were fused, melted at the edges, singed-smelling. Cut with heat. With something fast and hot, while they all slept twenty feet away.

"Everyone accounted for?" Beatrix's voice was quiet. Very quiet. "Nobody left camp in the night?"

Five heads shook. Five.

"Then either it can move through camp without waking any of us," Beatrix said, "or—"

She stopped. She didn't finish, and she didn't have to, because everyone had arrived at the same place at the same moment, and the silence where the sentence should have been was worse than any sentence.

Juno straightened up slowly and looked around the hollow—at the overhang above them, at the treeline, at the ordinary gray morning—and felt the wilderness tilt underneath her. She'd spent her whole adult life believing the river was the enemy, the rapids and the floods and the cold, honest water that killed you openly if it killed you at all. She'd catalogued every danger the gorge could offer and priced them all.

But the gorge hadn't crept into their camp in the night. The gorge hadn't taken the rope they needed to survive next week and snipped it like a tailor.

Something had.

Ada picked up the two cut ends and held them together, measuring the loss, and when she looked up her fear had burned off into something harder. "We're nine days from the source," she said. "The monsoon's coming. And now we've got half a rope."

"Then we make half a rope do the work of a whole one," Beatrix said. But her eyes weren't on Ada. They were lifted, fixed on the eastern ridge, where the morning mist was peeling off the granite in long ribbons—and where, for just a heartbeat before the fog closed, Juno saw it too: a single figure, standing motionless at the crest, watching them find what it had left for them.

Then the mist swallowed it, and the ridge was empty, and Beatrix said, in a voice Juno had never heard her use—low, and certain, and almost tender, like a woman greeting an old wound:

"I know that stance. I've seen it before." She turned to the crew, and her face had gone the color of the fog. "Seven years ago. On this same river. That's Merrick."
