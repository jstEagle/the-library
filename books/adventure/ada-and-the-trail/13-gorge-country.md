## 13. Gorge Country

The gorge announced itself before they saw it.

Ada felt it first in her teeth—a low, grinding hum that came up through the hull of the *Peregrine* and settled somewhere behind her jaw, like the river was chewing on something and had been chewing for a very long time. Then came the sound proper: not the bright chatter of rapids but a deep, continuous roar, the sound of water that had stopped being polite about where it wanted to go.

"Gorge country," Beatrix said from the tiller. She said it the way other people said *storm coming* or *bad year for apples*—flatly, with a kind of resigned respect.

Ada climbed up onto the cargo crates to look. The river narrowed ahead into a corridor of stone so sheer it looked cut rather than carved, walls rising two hundred feet on either side, streaked black and rust-red with mineral stain. The water between them moved fast and green and deep, folding over itself in long slow coils. There were no eddies here. No slack water. Nowhere, Ada realized, to stop.

"Rules change in the gorge," Beatrix went on, raising her voice over the roar. "No swimming unless you're drowning. No showing off." She glanced at Odell, who clutched his map case with both hands like a man holding a hymnal at a funeral. "No mapping while underway."

"I wasn't going to—" Odell began.

"You were absolutely going to."

Juno laughed from the galley box amidships, where she was somehow managing to fry onions in a swell. "Last time we ran a gorge, Odell tried to sketch the stratigraphy and dropped his pencil in the drink. Cried about it for two days."

"It was a *good* pencil," Odell said with dignity.

Tam said nothing, because Tam never said anything, but he shifted forward to the bow and began checking the tie-downs with the methodical care of a man who expected the world to try to shake everything loose. Ada watched him work. Seven days on the river and she still couldn't read him—the broad silent back, the hands that seemed to know what every rope wanted before it knew it itself. Juno had started leaving an extra biscuit by his bedroll every morning. Tam always ate it. That was, as far as Ada could tell, the entire extent of their relationship, and both of them seemed satisfied with the arrangement.

"Everyone clip in," Beatrix ordered. "Ada, you're on the bail line. If we ship water, you pull the plug cord and don't stop pulling until I say."

"And if you say nothing?"

"Then keep pulling."

* * *

The first hour of the gorge was terrible and wonderful in equal measure.

The walls closed around them until Ada could have touched both sides with outstretched arms if she'd dared lean out—which Beatrix forbade twice, then gave up forbidding because Ada kept doing it anyway. The light turned the color of old honey. Water fell from springs halfway up the cliffs in silver threads, some of them shattering to mist before they reached the river. Once, a herd of sure-footed gray goats watched them pass from a ledge no wider than a dinner table, chewing with the profound indifference of creatures who had never once needed to worry about anything.

"They're judging us," Juno said.

"Everything up here judges us," Beatrix said. "That's how you know you're where you shouldn't be."

They shot three rapids that afternoon, each one bigger than anything in the Narrows, and the *Peregrine* took them the way a good horse takes bad ground—jarring, splashing, complaining, but honest. By the third, Ada found herself whooping, actually whooping, spray freezing down the back of her collar, and she caught Beatrix watching her with something that on a less guarded face might have been called a smile.

It was almost enough to make her forget. The burned relay station. The crate with the crushed company mark. The letters in her satchel that she still hadn't shown anyone, that sat against her hip like a stone she'd swallowed. The figure on the ridge—Merrick—who had vanished into the fog two nights ago and left them all sleeping in shifts with knives under their pillows.

Almost.

* * *

The rockslide came without warning, which Ada would later understand was the only way rockslides ever came.

They rounded a bend where the cliff wall leaned out over the channel, and Beatrix's head came up sharply. "Backwater," she snapped. "Hard to starboard—" and then the sound hit them, a crack like the mountain clearing its throat, and the far side of the gorge shrugged.

It wasn't an avalanche. It was worse than that, in its way—a section of the talus slope above the opposite bank simply let go, a few tons of it, sliding down in a slow grinding rush and pouring into the river like sugar into tea. The wave it threw across the channel caught the *Peregrine* broadside and shoved her sideways into the near wall.

Ada remembered it afterward in pieces. The world tilting. Juno's galley box breaking loose and Juno lunging after it and Odell catching Juno instead, which everyone agreed later was the correct prioritization. The shriek of wood on stone. And then a horrible grinding stillness, the boat wedged bow-first between two submerged boulders, the full weight of the gorge pressing against her stern, water piling over the gunwales in cold green sheets.

"Bail!" Beatrix roared. "Bail and hold!"

Ada pulled the plug cord and bailed with the tin pan and held, and the boat groaned beneath her like a living thing pinned down. They weren't sinking—not yet—but they weren't floating either. The current stood in the stern like a crowd pushing at a door.

Beatrix worked her way forward along the gunwale, clipped double, reading the water with her whole body. After a long moment she spat over the side.

"We're hung up on a strainer bar. Boulder's got our keel, second rock's got the bow chock." Her voice was calm, which frightened Ada more than shouting would have. "We need to shift her weight or she'll fold."

"Shift it how?" Odell called, white-knuckled on the thwart.

"Somebody goes down and clears the chock. Wedge stone's maybe this big—" Beatrix held her hands apart, the size of a bread loaf. "Knock it loose, she'll swing free."

A silence. Everyone looked at the water. The water looked back—green, fast, and full of the kind of cold that stopped hearts.

"I'll do it," Ada said.

"No," said four voices at once, including, to her astonishment, Tam's—not a word, exactly, but a sound, a flat negative grunt that carried absolute authority.

And then Tam was already moving. He unclipped his knife, tied the safety line around his own waist with two quick turns, tested Beatrix's knot, frowned at it, retied it properly, and stood at the rail looking down into the green like a man considering a doorway he'd been through before.

"Tam," Beatrix said quietly. "You don't have to."

He looked at her. Something passed between them—something old, something about wrecks and rivers and the things people carry—and then he took one breath, huge and deliberate, like a diver in a story, and stepped off the edge.

* * *

The water took him the way the gorge took everything: completely.

Ada saw him go under, saw the line pay out fast, too fast, and then the line went taut and *hummed*, and Tam surfaced ten feet downstream with the current already worrying at him like a dog with a rag. He got one hand on the boulder shelf. Ada could see him working—she could see the shape of him through the churned water, dark against the green, braced against the hull, arm buried to the shoulder in the gap beneath the chock stone.

"Hold him!" Beatrix shouted, and Ada wrapped the line around her forearms and leaned back until her shoulders screamed, and Odell threw himself onto the line beside her, and even Juno abandoned the galley to add her weight. Three of them hauling against the river, and the river didn't care. It paid out inches anyway, patient as erosion.

"He's got it—" Odell gasped. "I can see him moving it—"

The chock stone came loose. Ada felt it rather than saw it—a shudder through the hull, a sudden lurch—and then the *Peregrine* swung free with a groan and the current slammed her stern around, and the line went slack.

Slack.

Ada's stomach dropped through the bottom of the world. Slack meant the line wasn't holding anything. Slack meant—

"TAM!" She was at the rail before she'd decided to move. The green water rolled past, featureless, endless. One second. Two. Somewhere behind her Beatrix was shouting something about the rope, about coiling, about getting ready to run the boat down after him, and none of it mattered, none of it was fast enough, because Tam was gone, the gorge had simply taken him the way it took everything, quietly and permanently—

There. A hand. Just a hand, knuckles breaking the surface thirty feet downstream, and beyond it the pale blur of his face, and Ada did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat: the next rapid was forty feet below that, and nobody came back from the gorge rapids, nobody, Beatrix had said so, the water folded you under and kept you—

She clipped the spare throw line to her belt without thinking about it. She heard Beatrix scream her name. She stepped up onto the rail.

The water hit her like a building falling on her.

Cold wasn't the word. Cold was a thing you felt; this was a thing that happened *to* you, that reached in through skin and muscle and squeezed. The river tumbled her end over end, stole her breath, filled her ears with a roar like the inside of a bell. She clawed for the surface, found it for half a second—saw the boat, saw Beatrix leaning impossibly far over the rail with the line in her fists, paying it out—and then the current dragged her under again and she let it, stopped fighting, angled her body downstream the way Beatrix had drilled into all of them on day two, *feet up, feet up, never lead with your head*—

Her flailing hand struck fabric. An arm. She grabbed it—forearm, thick and slick, and hauled, and Tam's face came up out of the green beside hers, eyes open, terrifyingly calm, and his mouth formed one word:

*Up.*

She kicked. He kicked. The line went taut above them and began, blessedly, to shorten, hand over hand somewhere above, and together—they were together now, his arm locked around her chest, and later she would realize he had been steering *her* the whole time, protecting her head from the rocks even while he drowned—they broke the surface into air and noise and Beatrix's hands closing on her collar like iron.

They got Tam over the gunwale first. He came up streaming, coughed once, hugely, and lay on the deck plates while Juno stripped his coat and Odell wrapped him in the wool blanket and Ada knelt there shaking so hard her teeth chattered against each other.

Tam opened his eyes. He looked at her for a long moment.

Then he raised one hand, weakly, and tapped his own chest twice, and pointed at her.

"What does that mean?" Ada whispered.

Juno's voice cracked in the middle. "It means *you saved my life*, honey. In whatever language he's using these days."

Tam's mouth twitched. It was, possibly, the beginning of a smile. Then his eyes rolled back and he was asleep, or unconscious, and nobody could tell the difference and nobody much cared, because he was breathing, great slow bellows-breaths, alive.

* * *

They made camp an hour later on a gravel bench below the slide, the first flat ground in miles, and nobody suggested pushing on. Some unwritten law had come into force: the day ended when someone almost died, and not before.

Beatrix built the fire herself, which she never did. Juno cooked, which she always did, but tonight she made the good stew, the one with the dried plums she'd been hoarding since Kessel's Landing, and she made enough for six and set out six bowls even though they were five, and when Odell asked about the sixth she said, "Habit," in a voice that meant *Merrick*, and everyone understood and nobody said anything else.

Tam woke at dusk, drank a bowl of broth, and slept again by the fire like a large, weathered child. Ada sat beside him and mended the tear in his sleeve where the rocks had taken their toll, badly, because she was a terrible seamstress, and he let her, which she understood was its own kind of thanks.

"You know," Odell said, around a mouthful of stew, "in eleven years on guild survey crews, I never once saw a man go into flood water on purpose. We had a rule. Rule one, in fact. *Never enter the water.*"

"He broke rule one," Juno said.

"He broke rule one," Odell agreed solemnly, "and thereby demonstrated why rules are written by cowards."

Beatrix snorted—actually snorted, into her bowl, and Ada stared at her, because in nine days she had seen Beatrix Dravenmoor be hard and competent and grim and occasionally, briefly, almost kind, but she had never seen her be *funny*. The firelight did something to her face. It made her look younger. It made her look like someone who, seven years ago, had perhaps been easy to follow into a gorge.

"The wreck," Ada said quietly, before she could stop herself. All eyes turned to her. She kept her gaze on the mending in her lap. "The one that took Tam's voice. Was it here? On the Veyl?"

The silence stretched. Tam, propped on one elbow, watched her with those unreadable dark eyes.

"He can't answer," Juno said gently.

"He can nod."

Another silence. Then, slowly, Tam shook his head. Not here. Somewhere else. Another river, another life.

"He was a ferryman on the Sorrel, before," Beatrix said at last, staring into the fire. "Passenger packet went down in spring melt. Fourteen aboard." She paused. "Twelve came up."

Ada looked at the big quiet man by the fire, at the hands that had retied Beatrix's knot without being asked, that had shielded a stranger's head in the water while his own lungs filled, and she thought: *he has been carrying twelve names for years, and today he almost joined them, and the first thing he did when he woke was check that I was all right.*

Something in her chest went loose and warm and painful all at once. She looked around the fire—at Odell with his disgraced maps and his good-pencil grief, at Juno feeding the flames biscuit crumbs like the smuggler-priestess of some small domestic order, at Beatrix with her secrets stacked like ballast, at Tam breathing—and thought, with a clarity that startled her: *these are my people now.* Not the town she'd grown up in. Not the father waiting somewhere upstream, silent in the silence. These five, this fire, this ridiculous battered boat.

Family, she thought, wasn't the people you were given. It was the people who jumped in after you.

"Get some sleep," Beatrix said, standing, brushing off her trousers. "We run the middle gorge tomorrow. Long day." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and her voice softened by a degree. "Ada. What you did today was stupid and magnificent and exactly right. Don't ever tell me which order those go in."

Ada grinned into the dark. "Yes, Captain."

* * *

She woke once in the night, cold seeping through her bedroll, and lay listening to the gorge breathe.

That was when she noticed the smell. Faint, wrong, threaded through the clean mineral scent of wet stone: smoke. Old smoke, cold smoke, the ghost of a fire that had burned hours ago somewhere close.

She sat up. Across the dead embers, Beatrix was awake too, sitting motionless, and Ada followed her gaze up the slope behind camp to where the talus field met the cliff shadow.

Halfway up, catching the starlight, something glinted. Glass, maybe. Or a lens.

And below it, pressed into the soft ash of an old fire ring that none of *them* had built—a boot print. Fresh. Large. With a cracked sole that would leave a distinctive mark, the kind of mark a man might leave for a decade without replacing his boots, because out here, alone, who was there to impress?

Beatrix rose without a sound, crossed to Ada's side, and crouched low.

"Sleep," she murmured. "Tomorrow we'll want our strength. Because I think I know where he's leading us—and it's a place I swore I'd never see again."

Above the camp, in the dark, the mountains went on rising toward the source, and somewhere among them, Ada knew, waited the frozen tents of the lost expedition—and whatever truth Merrick had spent seven years killing to protect.
