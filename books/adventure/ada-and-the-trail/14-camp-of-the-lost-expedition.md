## 14. Camp of the Lost Expedition

The gorge narrowed all morning until the walls pressed so close that Juno claimed she could touch both sides of the river at once if she stretched her arms out over the gunwales.

"Go on then," Odell said, not looking up from his charts. "I'll write it in the margin. *Cook achieves cartographic immortality, dies wet.*"

"I'd want a better epitaph."

"*Died as she lived: reaching for things.*"

Beatrix let them talk. It was easier than listening to the sound of her own pulse, which had been climbing steadily since dawn, since the moment the river bent east and she recognized—not remembered, *recognized*, the way you recognize your own handwriting—the shape of the water ahead.

Two more bends. A shelf of gray stone like a kneeling giant. Then the gravel fan where the Veyl spilled shallow and braided across a wide bench of land, and above it, half-swallowed by seven years of alder and lichen, the camp.

"There," she said.

Her voice came out flat, and everyone heard it, and mercifully nobody said anything about it.

* * *

They beached the boat on the gravel fan and stood for a moment in the strange hush that lives in places where nothing has moved for a long time. The light came down green through the alders. Somewhere above, meltwater ticked off the rocks like a clock counting something other than seconds.

"It's like walking into a photograph," Ada said softly.

It was worse than that. It was like walking into a room you'd left in a hurry, expecting to come back, and never coming back—and now here you were, seven years late, and everything had been waiting.

Three tents. Two of them collapsed under snow-load they must have taken some winter after the men left; the third still stood, sagging but stubborn, its guy-lines furred with moss. A fire ring with charred wood that rain had never quite managed to dissolve. Crates stacked against a boulder, lids weighted down with stones, exactly the way Beatrix had taught her crews to stack them against bears. A kettle on a flat rock, waiting for a fire that was never lit.

And boots. One pair, by the nearest tent, set neatly side by side, toes pointing toward the trail.

"Nobody panicked," Tam said.

Everyone turned to look at him. He hadn't spoken more than a dozen words since the portage, and each one landed like a coin dropped in a quiet church. He nodded at the boots, at the stacked crates, at the whole patient arrangement of the place.

"They left orderly." His voice was low and rough, an engine turning over after years in a shed. "That's worse."

Beatrix knew what he meant. An orderly camp meant an orderly departure, and an orderly departure meant they'd believed there was somewhere to go. She looked at the boots again and made herself think it plainly: *they took their packs, they left the heavy gear, they walked up-valley in good order, and none of them came back down.*

"Juno, Tam—inventory, but don't open anything with company seals. Odell, with me. Ada—" She hesitated. "Ada, stay within sight of me."

"I'm not made of glass."

"No," Beatrix said. "You're made of your father's stubbornness, which is worse. Stay within sight anyway."

* * *

The third tent was hers.

She knew it before she saw the mark—a faded stencil of the Veyl & Meridian Supply Company's compass rose on the flap—but her hands knew it first, knew the particular way the buckle stuck, knew that you had to lift the toggle and slide it sideways or it would bite your thumb. Seven years and her hands still remembered. That was the thing about the body. It kept faith with the past long after the mind had signed the divorce papers.

Inside: a bedroll rolled tight. A lantern with its glass still clean. And on the small folding table, weighted under a stone, a leather journal with a broken clasp.

Her name was inside the cover. Not *Sable*, not any of the names she'd worn since. Her old name, in her own handwriting, with the date, and beneath it the expedition designation she'd spent seven years trying to scrub out of the world's memory and out of her own.

*V&M Survey, Source Approach, Third Season. Field Leader: B. Dravenmoor.*

Odell stood in the tent mouth and didn't come in. "You don't have to," he said.

"Yes, I do." She sat down on the ground because her legs had opinions of their own. "This is the whole reason I came back, even if I lied to myself about it being about Ada's father. Read the rest of the camp if you like. Leave me this one."

He went. He was good like that—one of the few people alive who understood that sometimes the kindest thing a friend can do is walk away loudly enough that you know he's not eavesdropping.

She opened the journal.

* * *

The early entries were almost funny, and that undid her more than grief would have. There she was, twenty-nine years old and certain of everything, complaining about the coffee, teasing her radio operator Merrick about his courtship of a woman in the last supply town ("he has written her forty-one letters and received two, and describes this as *promising*"), sketching little maps in the margins with arrows and marginalia in a hand so confident it practically swaggered.

*Day 12. Rations holding. Crew morale excellent. Hollis caught a fish the size of my forearm and has not stopped talking about it. I have decided to let him believe he is the first man ever to catch a fish.*

Hollis. Ada's uncle. She'd known his name was in these pages somewhere, and knowing didn't prepare her. She read his name again and again, letting it hurt, because she had decided somewhere in the last three days—if she was honest, somewhere back in Kessel's Landing, at the dock, when Ada traced the tattoo on her wrist—that she was done flinching from this.

*Day 15. Scouted the upper gorge. The river behaves strangely above the narrows—flow drops overnight without weather to account for it. Merrick says the old trappers' stories mention a "door" upstream. Superstition, probably. But I've asked Odell's guild contact to pull the original survey plats for this reach. Something upstream is holding water.*

She stopped.

Something upstream is holding water.

Seven years ago she had noticed it. Written it down. Asked the right question. And then—

Then the entry for Day 16, and the one after, and she read them with her heart going like a fist on a door:

*Day 16. Reply from guild: no plats exist for the upper reach. Officially the valley "has no navigable headwater." Which is absurd—I am looking at it. Filed formal objection. Also: flow dropped another four inches overnight. Marking this clearly: THE RIVER IS BEING REGULATED BY SOMETHING ABOVE US.*

*Day 17. Company wireless instructs us to discontinue hydrological observations and proceed directly to source survey for mineral claims. Ignoring the water question. I have put my objection in writing and sent a duplicate by Merrick's relay. If anything happens here, I want it on record that we were told to stop asking.*

The page blurred. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, annoyed at herself, and kept reading, because the journal was building toward something with the terrible momentum of a landslide—you could see where it was going and you could not look away.

*Day 19. Found it. Upstream of the high weir there is a dam. Old, enormous, built into the gorge itself—stone and timber, decades abandoned, NOT on any chart. The company's claim maps show a lake here. There is no lake. There is a wall holding back whatever used to be one, and it is failing. Seepage streams along the whole base. We heard it groan twice last night. Twice.*

*I have ordered the camp moved down-valley to the gravel fan. Effective immediately.*

The gravel fan. Where she was sitting. Where the tents still stood around her like mourners.

Because they had moved. They had done everything right. She turned the page with fingers gone clumsy.

*Day 20. Camp relocated. Sent Hollis and two others downriver with full reports and instructions to raise the alarm at the relay station. The rest of us hold here to monitor the dam and wait for the company survey team that wireless says is coming to "assess." I do not trust the word assess. I trust Hollis. He'll get the truth downstream if it kills him.*

*Day 21. The groaning is continuous now. Birds have left the valley entirely. I have never heard a silence so loud.*

*Day 22 —*

Day 22 was half a line. *At dawn the sound changed from groaning to—*

Nothing. The entry simply stopped, mid-sentence, mid-word, the ink dragged into a streak where the pen had skidded, and Beatrix sat in her own seven-year-old tent and remembered, all at once and completely, the sound the mountain had made when the dam let go. Not a roar. A *click*. An enormous, patient click, like the world's largest lock opening, followed by a silence so total she could hear her own blood, and then the river arrived.

She had survived it because she'd been upslope checking a seepage gauge. Three of them hadn't. And the official story—the story the company told the inquiry, the story that blacklisted her—was that Field Leader Dravenmoor had ignored protocol, camped her team in a flood zone, and led them across the gorge on the morning of the collapse against the advice of her own crew.

Against the advice of her own crew.

She flipped to the back of the journal, to the final entries, written in the days after, in a hand that shook so badly it barely resembled the confident swagger of Day 12.

*Survivors: 4 of 9. The dam was company-built, thirty years ago, and abandoned when the claims played out. They knew it was failing. My hydrology objections were flagged and buried—Merrick found the wireless log copies. The inquiry will hear none of this unless someone carries it out. I am sending the journals with the relief party. All of it. Let them hang me for the crossing if they must, but the record will show what that wall was and who built it and who let it rot.*

*If anyone reads this: we did not die of the river. We died of paperwork.*

Beatrix closed the journal and held it against her chest and breathed.

Here was the thing she had never managed to explain to anyone, not the inquiry, not the tavern gossips, not herself on the bad nights: she had sent the journals out with the relief party. Every page. And the relief party had come back reporting the pack lost in the rapids, and the inquiry had proceeded on the company's evidence alone, and her name had gone onto the blacklist, and she had run—not because she was guilty of what they said, but because she couldn't prove otherwise, and a person can only stand in a courtroom being called a liar for so long before she starts to wonder.

But the journals were supposed to be *gone*. Lost in the white water below the falls, swept to the sea.

So why was hers sitting on a folding table in a camp that time had forgotten?

* * *

She came out of the tent into late afternoon light and found the others gathered around the fire ring, and their faces told her before anyone spoke.

"Beatrix," Odell said carefully. "You need to see what we found."

"Where?"

"The big crate. The one with the stones on the lid." Juno's usual cheer was absent; she looked like a woman who'd found a snake in the flour bin. "Company seal was already broken. Somebody opened it before us. Recently—the cut edges are bright."

Inside the crate, arranged with terrible neatness, were documents. Wireless logs. Claim maps showing the upper valley—with the dam drawn in and annotated in a surveyor's hand. Copies of internal memos, thirty years old, discussing maintenance costs for the "upper impoundment structure" and concluding, in the bloodless arithmetic of corporations, that abandonment was the economical option.

And on top of the whole stack, centered like a paperweight, a single sheet in fresh ink:

*Some records survive. Some witnesses too. Turn back, and everyone keeps breathing.*

Ada read it twice. Then she looked up at Beatrix, and Beatrix watched the girl do the arithmetic—the crate stamped with a company mark, the camp preserved instead of looted, the note addressed to whoever came next, which could only mean someone had known, all along, exactly who would eventually follow this river home.

"That's why the relays are burned," Ada said quietly. "That's why the fuel was fouled. Someone doesn't want this valley read." She looked around at the tents, at the neat boots, at the whole patient museum of the dead. "But they didn't burn *this*. They've had seven years to burn this."

"Because it's not evidence to them," Beatrix said. Her voice sounded far away, like it belonged to the woman in the journal. "It's bait. Or a warning. Or—" She stopped, because a thought had just walked coldly up her spine. "Or they're waiting for a specific reader."

Tam, who had been standing apart, turned his head slowly toward the tree line above the camp, where the shadows had begun to gather ahead of the sun.

"We're being watched," he said. "Right now. Have been since the fan."

Nobody moved. High on the ridge, at the edge of sight, something that might have been a man shifted against the sky—and was gone.

Beatrix picked up her journal, her own young handwriting heavy in her hands, and felt the ghost she'd been running from finally fall into step beside her.

"Then we give them something to watch," she said.
