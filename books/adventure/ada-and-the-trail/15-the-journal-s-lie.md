## 15. The Journal's Lie

Odell Fenwick had spent thirty years making maps, and he had learned one immutable truth about them: a map was never a record of the land. It was a record of what someone had decided you should see.

He was thinking about this at dawn, crouched over the cold fire pit with his tin cup of chicory substitute, when Ada came out of Beatrix's tent holding the journal like it had bitten her.

"Odell." Her voice was very level, which in a seventeen-year-old was more alarming than screaming. "Where are pages forty-one through forty-six?"

* * *

The night before had been, by the standards of the last two weeks, almost pleasant. They'd found the old camp on a gravel shelf above the river — three collapsed tents gone green with moss, a cook pot fused to its stand by rust, and, wedged beneath a waterproof sledge in a cache barrel that Beatrix had opened with hands that shook so badly Tam had quietly taken the pry bar from her and done it himself — the expedition journals. Seven years old. Sealed in oilcloth. Waiting.

They had made camp there because there was no honest alternative; the light was failing and the gorge offered nothing but vertical rock for another six miles. But nobody had pretended it was comfortable. The place had a weight to it, like sleeping in a room where someone had once wept for a long time and then stopped abruptly.

Beatrix had read her own handwriting by firelight until well past midnight, saying nothing, her face doing things Odell had no chart symbols for. Eventually she'd wrapped the journal up again and put it under her bedroll, and the camp had settled into the uneasy sleep of people sharing space with a ghost.

Which meant the journal had been within arm's reach of exactly four people all night: Beatrix, who owned it; Tam, who slept like a stone and moved like one; Ada, who had been awake — Odell had heard her turning over for hours; and himself.

And now Ada was standing in front of him with the journal open to a stubbed spine, six pages gone, cut cleanly at the binding with a blade, and her eyes were doing arithmetic.

"You were up early," she said. "I heard you."

"I'm always up early. Old cartographers rust if we lie still."

"Where are they, Odell?"

He looked at her — this girl who had dragged five broken people up a forbidden river on stubbornness and grief, who had pulled Tam out of floodwater two days ago without hesitation, who deserved, more than anyone alive, the truth — and he found that thirty years of professional discretion sat in his throat like a swallowed fishhook.

Because here was the thing Odell Fenwick could not say: he knew what was on those pages. He had known for seven years. He had been carrying them against his own ribs since the week after the disaster, through the guild inquiry, through the stripping of his name from the rolls, through every cheap room and cheaper meal since, and he had carried them not because they would clear Beatrix Dravenmoor, but because he had believed — still believed, God help him — that they would destroy her.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'd rather not."

"Sit down anyway. This is a long story and I tell it better to a seated audience."

Ada did not sit. She stood there with her arms crossed and the wind coming off the glacier-fed river flattening her hair sideways, and Odell sighed, and began anyway.

* * *

"I wasn't struck off the guild rolls for mapping the source valley," he said. "That's the version I let people believe, because it's a noble story. Martyrdom plays better than cowardice."

The fire needed feeding. He fed it rather than look at her.

"The truth is I was struck off for falsifying a survey. Or — no. Let me be precise, precision is all I have left. I was struck off for *destroying* a survey. My own."

Ada's arms uncrossed slightly. "What survey?"

"Seven years ago I was contracted as secondary cartographer on the Veyl Source Expedition. You know the primary guide. The company was Corvane Supply and Survey — their mark was on that crushed crate we pulled out of the water, the one that sent your eyes straight to Beatrix like compass needles finding north." He turned the cup in his hands. "I was young-ish and ambitious and I kept a private field book, separate from the official log. Cartographers do. It's where we put the observations that don't flatter anybody. Water levels. Rock strata. Notes on things the client paid us not to notice."

"Such as?"

"Such as the fact that the headwaters valley above the last weir was dammed. Not naturally. There was a timber-and-earth barrage across the upper gorge, decades old, built by Corvane's predecessor company to hold a millpond for logging operations that had failed and been abandoned. Nobody maintained it. Nobody had looked at it in twenty years. And in my private book, in my own hand, dated nine days before the collapse, I wrote: *Retaining structure compromised. Seepage visible along eastern abutment. Recommend immediate inspection and mandatory downstream clearance.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere up-valley, ice calved off the glacier with a sound like a door closing.

"I reported it verbally," Odell said. "To the expedition sponsor's representative. A man named Halloway. He thanked me, said it would be looked into, and asked me to leave the formal notation out of the official survey pending 'further assessment.' And I —" His voice caught on something. "I agreed. Because Halloway mentioned that Corvane held options on my next three contracts, and because I told myself the structure had stood for twenty years and would stand nine more days until we were out of the valley. Nine days. It stood six."

Ada had gone very pale. "The collapse. The flood that killed—"

"That killed four people, including your uncle, and nearly killed Beatrix, and destroyed a village eight miles downstream that the official record says was hit by a 'glacial outburst event.' An act of God." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "It was an act of Corvane. And I helped bury it, because I was a coward with a mortgage."

"But Beatrix took the blame."

"Beatrix took the blame because the official story needed a villain with a face, and hers was available. She ordered the crossing that put the team in the gorge when the dam went. That part's true — she'll tell you herself, she's been carrying it like ballast for seven years. But the crossing wouldn't have mattered if the valley hadn't been a loaded gun. And the only proof the gun existed was in my private field book." He set the cup down. "Halloway approached me the week after the funerals. He didn't threaten me, exactly. He bought me dinner and talked about my career and how tragic it would be if my negligence in failing to file a hazard report became part of the inquiry. And somewhere between the soup and the cheese I understood that if those pages surfaced, Corvane would make sure they surfaced *twisted* — that I'd be painted as the man whose sloppy notes proved the guide had been warned and crossed anyway. That Beatrix would go from disgraced to damned. Deliberate. Knowing."

"So you tore them out."

"I tore them out of the copy of the field book that entered evidence, yes. Before submission. Six pages. And then—" He reached inside his coat, into the inner pocket where he kept his folding scale and his pencil stubs and the small tin of pencils he'd carried so long the paint had worn to bare wood, and drew out an oilskin packet soft with age and handling. "Then I kept them. Because destroying them entirely felt like murder, and keeping them felt like — I don't know. A hostage. Insurance. A wound I could press whenever I needed to remember what I was."

Ada stared at the packet. She didn't reach for it.

"Why are you telling me now?" she asked. "Why not seven years ago? Why not give them to her at any point in the last decade and end her exile?"

"Because they don't end it." Odell's voice dropped. "That's the thing you have to understand, girl. Those pages cut both ways. Yes, they prove the dam was unsound and Corvane knew. They also contain my original route annotations — the ones showing that *I* recommended the low-gorge crossing as the safe option, and that Beatrix overrode me and chose the high line. She was wrong about the danger. I was right. If those pages come out whole, they clear her of the cover-up and hang her with the decision. Corvane walks, she burns twice, and your uncle is still dead." He finally met her eyes. "I have spent seven years trying to find a way to cut those pages apart — to free the truth about the dam without handing Corvane the knife for her throat. And I couldn't do it. So I carried them. All this way. Up this river. Like a fool."

For a long moment Ada said nothing. Then, quietly:

"She doesn't know you have them?"

"No."

"And she thinks *she* lost them? That she tore them out and forgot?"

"I think," Odell said carefully, "that Beatrix has spent seven years believing the worst possible version of herself, because the worst possible version at least has clean edges. I let her keep it. That's the cost, Ada. That's what protecting someone from the truth costs — you don't protect them. You just choose which lie they live inside. And you live in it with them."

Ada looked down at the journal in her hands, at the stubs of the missing pages, and Odell watched her arrive at the question he'd been dreading.

"Does she think *I* took them?" Ada said. "She woke up and they were gone. She woke up and the girl whose uncle she killed was holding her journal with pages cut out—"

The color drained out of his face. In all his seven years of careful, cowardly stewardship of those six pages, he had somehow never once imagined this specific morning: Beatrix opening the journal and finding the gap, and doing the arithmetic of who had been closest, and arriving — as Ada had arrived at his fire ten minutes ago — at the wrong answer.

"Oh no," Odell said.

* * *

They found her at the riverbank.

She was standing at the edge of the gravel shelf with her back to the camp, and she had the journal's oilcloth wrap folded in her fist, and she was looking upstream toward the source with the expression of a woman reading her own sentence. Tam stood a few paces off, wringing his hat in his hands, radiating the particular helplessness of a mute man watching a disaster he can't talk anyone out of.

"Beatrix," Odell called.

"Don't." Her voice was flat and terribly calm. "I've done the sums, Odell. There were four of us in this camp. Tam sleeps like the dead and hasn't lied to me yet. Ada was awake half the night — I heard her — and she had every reason in the world. Her uncle. Her grief. And me, asleep, with the one document that could hang or free me sitting a foot from her hand." She turned around, and her eyes were dry and awful. "So either the daughter of Marcus Vance just stole my confession out from under me, or—"

"Or a fat old mapmaker finally grew a spine seven years too late," Odell said, and held out the oilskin packet.

Beatrix went still. Completely still, the way water goes still over deep places. Her gaze moved from the packet to his face and back, and Odell watched her rebuild the world behind her eyes — watched her take in the worn oilskin, the pocket-worn shape of it, the way his thumb was pressed flat against the fold as though it had rested there ten thousand times.

"You've had them," she said. "All this time."

"Since the inquiry."

"You let me believe—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "You let me believe I'd burned them. On the mountain. I *remembered* burning them, Odell. I built a whole person out of remembering burning them."

"I know." His voice cracked. "I'm sorry. I told myself it was mercy. It was cowardice wearing mercy's coat, and I've known that for years, and I carried them anyway because I couldn't decide whether they'd save you or kill you, and I thought — I thought if I just kept walking, kept them against my ribs, I'd never have to find out." He held the packet out further. His arm was shaking. "Well. Here's the finding out."

Beatrix didn't take it. She looked at it the way you look at a snake you've stepped near.

"Open it," Ada said softly, beside him. "Please. Both of you. Whatever's in there — we're past the point where it can be worse than not knowing."

Tam made a sound. Everyone looked at him. He pointed at the packet, then at Beatrix, then pressed his fist to his chest — *yours, yours* — and nodded once, hard.

Beatrix crossed the gravel. She took the packet from Odell's hands, and her fingers brushed his, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing left that words were shaped for.

She unwrapped the oilskin. Six pages, soft-edged, covered in Odell's cramped surveyor's hand. She read the first one. Then the second. Her lips moved slightly, the way they did when she was reading water.

And it was Juno — up on the ridge line with the spyglass, checking the high ground as she did every morning now, ever since the figure in outdated expedition gear had appeared there — who broke the silence, her voice dropping down the slope thin and sharp as wire:

"Boss! Someone's on the trail above the weir. Coming down. Coming down *fast*, and he's carrying a rifle, and—" a pause, the spyglass trembling, "—and I think he's wearing our colors. Old expedition issue. Corvane gray."

Beatrix looked up from the pages of her own buried past, toward the ridge, toward the dead man walking down out of seven silent years.

"Merrick," she said.
