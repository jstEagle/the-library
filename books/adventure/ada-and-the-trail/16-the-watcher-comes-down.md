## 16. The Watcher Comes Down

Merrick Voss had been dead for seven years, and he had gotten very good at it.

Dead men, he had learned, enjoyed certain advantages. Dead men did not pay customs duties. Dead men did not answer letters. Dead men could stand on a ridge in the rain for six hours without anyone wondering why, because nobody was looking for them at all. The living were so busy with their own noise—their engines and their arguments and their terrible singing around cook fires—that they never once looked up at the high ground where the quiet things waited.

He had watched them for eleven days now.

The girl first. Ada Vance. She moved like her father, which was a small cruelty the universe had arranged just for him: same stubborn set of the shoulders, same way of planting her feet when she'd decided something. Merrick had held the radio handset while that stubbornness talked its way through three avalanches' worth of bad decisions, and he had loved Hollis Vance like a brother, and it had not been enough to save him.

Then the guide. Beatrix Thackeray, wearing a mechanic's name like a coat two sizes too small. He had recognized her walk before he ever saw her face—guides walked like they were listening to the ground—and when she'd turned toward the ridge on the fourth day and stared directly at his hiding place for a full ten seconds, he had felt seven years of careful death tremble at its foundations.

She hadn't seen him. Of course she hadn't. But she had *looked*, and that was worse.

* * *

Tonight they camped in the lee of a granite shelf above the river, and Merrick lay flat among the junipers on the slope above them, close enough to hear the murmur of voices through the drizzle. He had cut their spare rope two nights ago—not to hurt anyone, never to hurt anyone, just to slow them down, to give the river time to change its mind about carrying them forward. Before that, the fuel. Before that, the burned relay station, which had pained him more than he expected; old Wenna had run that relay for thirty years and deserved better than arson, but the alternative was letting word travel downriver that an expedition was moving toward the source, and Merrick had learned the hard way what happened when word traveled.

People came. People always came. And then people died, and then the company wrote its tidy report, and then one man was left holding the truth alone in the dark with no name and no grave and no one to tell.

He was tired. That was the thing nobody warned you about being dead. It was exhausting work, staying unalive.

Below, the cook—Juno, the smuggler's maps, he'd catalogued them all by now—was saying something that made the cartographer laugh. Odell Frayne. Merrick knew that name too, from the guild expulsion notices he'd made a hobby of reading, back when he still pretended to be a person who read notices. A man struck off for mapping the unmappable. The company's left hand cutting off its right.

And Tam. The mute boatman. Merrick had watched him work the lines during the portage with something like awe. There was a man who understood silence, who had made a home in it the way Merrick had only ever squatted in it.

"You're doing it again," said a voice behind him, and Merrick's whole body went to stone.

Because that voice belonged to a dead man too.

* * *

Hollis Vance stood six feet away in the junipers, dripping, holding a lantern with the shutter half closed, and he looked older than seven years should have made him, grayer, thinner, but unmistakably, impossibly alive.

"No," Merrick said. His voice came out rusted. He hadn't used it above a whisper in weeks. "No, you're—I carried your pack out. I identified—" He stopped. He had not identified anything. He had told the searchers what they needed to hear so they would stop searching, so the mountain would keep what it kept, so he could go down the river and die quietly in the records.

"Sit up slow," Hollis said, not unkindly. "You've got mud all down your back. You look like a bog that learned to frown."

"I look like a dead man."

"You look like a man who needs soup." Hollis crouched, keeping the lantern low. "I watched you cut our rope, by the way. Sloppy work. You used to splice cable for a living."

"That was different cable."

"It was indeed." Something moved across Hollis's face—not anger, exactly. Grief wearing anger's clothes. "Seven years, Merrick. Seven years I've been dead to everyone who mattered, and I come up this river to find my daughter dragging a crew of exiles toward the thing that killed my brother, and every mile of it, someone has been picking at our rigging like a gull after fish guts. And every mile, I think: I know that pattern. I know whoever this is, because only one other man survived that valley, and I buried him myself. Figuratively. You were very thorough about the literally."

Merrick sat up. There didn't seem to be any point lying down anymore. "You let me believe you died in the collapse."

"You let *everyone* believe I died in the collapse. We appear to have a symmetry problem." Hollis settled onto a rock, joints cracking. "I went under. Truly. The water took me down the east channel and spat me out two miles below against a gravel bar, half drowned, and by the time I crawled back toward the camp there was nothing there but wreckage and the company's advance team already writing their report. I heard them, Merrick. I heard Corvane's man say the words 'structural failure of the crossing' and 'guide error' and 'unfortunate but contained,' and I understood that if I climbed out of that valley alive, I would be either blamed or silenced, and possibly both. So I stayed dead. It seemed the polite thing."

"The dam," Merrick said. The word came out of him like a splinter that had been working toward the surface for seven years. "You saw the dam."

"I saw what was left of it." Hollis's voice went flat and careful. "The upper weir. Company-built, company-hidden, failed in the spring melt, and the flood came down the valley forty minutes before anyone knew to climb. Forty minutes, Merrick. My brother died of a schedule."

Above them, faintly, the campfire crackled. Someone—Juno, by the laugh—was losing an argument about star navigation. The ordinary sounds of the living.

"Why sabotage us?" Hollis asked. "Why burn Wenna's relay? Why the fuel? If you wanted the truth out, you could have walked into Kessel's Landing and shouted it."

"Because shouting doesn't work." Merrick heard his own voice crack and hated it. "I tried. Year two. I sent the survey notes to the provincial examiner. Do you know what happened? Nothing happened. The notes vanished. Then a man found me in a dockside tavern in Lowmarsh and bought me dinner and explained, very reasonably, over very good wine, that dead men who stayed dead lived comfortable lives, and dead men who came back to life had accidents. So I stayed dead. And then I started making sure nobody else walked into that valley, because every expedition the company lets go up there is another chance for them to tidy the story again, and every rescue party is another set of families to bury." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin as ice cracking. "I've stopped four expeditions. Fires, flooded caches, a forged quarantine notice once—I'm rather proud of the quarantine notice. Nobody dies. That's the rule. Nobody dies, everybody turns around, and the secret stays safe in the ground where it can't hurt anyone."

"And instead it hurts you."

The rain ticked against the juniper branches. Down the slope, the fire popped.

"Yes," Merrick said finally. "It hurts me. Every day. But it's *my* hurt, and I know its shape, and I will not hand it to some seventeen-year-old girl who thinks her father is waiting at the headwaters with open arms." He stopped. Looked at Hollis. Looked at the gray in his beard, the way he favored his left knee getting up. "Does she know?"

"Not yet."

"She thinks you're alive?"

"She thinks I'm a supply agent named Garrick who joined the crew at the last relay because I owed her mother a favor." Hollis rubbed his face. "It was going to be a reveal at the source. A reunion. I had it planned. There was going to be a speech."

"There's always a speech."

"There is *always* a speech," Hollis agreed solemnly, and for one absurd moment they were two dead men laughing in the rain above a fire full of living people, and it was almost like the old expeditions, almost like the mess tent, almost like before.

Then it wasn't.

"They'll reach the source in three days," Merrick said. "Four, if the gorge floods early. Thackeray knows the route. She'll take them straight into the collapse zone, because that's where the trail goes, because she doesn't know—" He caught himself.

"Doesn't know what?" Hollis asked quietly. "That she ordered the crossing? She knows. I've watched her carry it the whole way up this river like a stone in her pack. What don't I know, Merrick?"

Merrick looked down at the fire, at the small figures around it. The girl. The guide. The mapmaker, the boatman, the cook. Misfits, strays, people with something to hide—he'd thought that made them easy to scatter. He was beginning to suspect it made them dangerous.

"The pages," he said. "Her journal. The official record says her logbook was lost in the flood. It wasn't lost. I had it. I've had it for seven years, and there are pages in it that would hang her and pages that would clear her, and I tore out the ones that would clear her, because as long as the world believed she was a murderer, nobody would ever follow her route again." His hands were shaking. "I made her the scapegoat on purpose, Hollis. I built my safety out of her ruin. And now she's climbing toward the source with her ghost's daughter beside her, and I don't know how to stop it anymore without becoming the thing I've spent seven years pretending I wasn't."

Hollis was silent a long moment.

"Well," he said at last. "The good news is, neither of us is any good at being dead. The bad news is, we're about to get much worse at it." He stood, wincing at the knee, and shuttered the lantern. "They vote tomorrow whether to push past the last weir. If we're going to tell them the truth, we do it together, or not at all."

"Together," Merrick repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth, like a knot tied with the wrong hand.

"One condition." Hollis's eyes glinted in the dark. "When we go down there, you let me introduce you. I've had seven years to plan this speech, and I'll be damned if you ruin it by walking in first."

* * *

But the river, as usual, had its own schedule.

They came down the slope at first light, two dead men side by side, and found the camp already in uproar—because Juno, checking the cache at dawn, had found the spare flares gone, and because Ada, following a line of boot prints along the bank, had followed them not up the slope where Merrick had slept, but downriver, to the final weir, where a figure stood on the sluice gate with a signal flare in each hand and the whole grey morning behind him.

Beatrix Thackeray stepped to the front of her crew the way she must have stepped to the front of everything her whole ruined life, and called up: "Merrick Voss. Come down."

"I've been down," the figure called back, and even at fifty yards Merrick could hear the break in his own voice echoing off the water. "I've been down for seven years. The question is what the rest of you are willing to lose to come down with me."

Ada's hand went to her belt knife. Odell grabbed the girl's shoulder. And Hollis, beside Merrick in the shadow of the bank, murmured, "There goes my speech," as the flare in Merrick's hand hissed alight, red as an alarm, red as the record burning, red as everything that was about to happen at the water's edge.
