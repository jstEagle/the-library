## 17. Standoff at the Weir

The weir announced itself before they saw it—a long white seam of foam stitched across the river, and above it, the concrete bones of the old company dam jutting from the gorge wall like a broken jaw. Beatrix had been dreading this stretch since Kessel's Landing. Weirs were liars. They looked like stairs and behaved like teeth.

She was studying the drop-off, calculating their line through the sluice channel on the left, when Merrick stepped out of the alder thicket with a rifle in his hands and ten years of grief on his face.

"Nobody move," he said, which was unnecessary. Nobody had moved since the sound of the bolt sliding home.

* * *

Later—much later, when she could afford to be wry about it—Beatrix would reflect that of all the ways she'd imagined dying on this river, being shot by her own radio operator while holding a bag of torn journal pages over her head like a market woman haggling over fish had not made the list.

But that was later. First there was the moment itself.

Merrick stood on the flat rock shelf beside the old fish ladder, maybe thirty feet away, and he looked terrible. Not menacing-terrible. *Tired*-terrible. The decade out-of-date expedition jacket hung off him like a costume from a play nobody remembered writing, and his beard had gone grey in patches, as if time had simply given up on parts of him. The rifle was steady, though. That was the thing about Merrick that hadn't changed in seven years: his hands never shook.

"Put the boat in," he said. "Turn around. Go back downriver."

"Merrick." Beatrix kept her voice level, the way you talked to a spooked horse, or a client who'd just realized the river was bigger than the brochure promised. "It's good to see you too."

"Don't." His voice cracked on the word. "Don't do that. Don't be funny. You don't get to be funny."

Behind her, she heard Odell's breath catch—the particular inhale of a man doing rapid cartographic arithmetic and arriving at a bad sum. Tam had gone very still at the sweep oar, which for Tam constituted screaming. And Ada—

Ada was three feet to Beatrix's left, out in the open, exactly where a seventeen-year-old with more courage than sense should not have been.

"Merrick," Beatrix said slowly, "why don't you let the girl step back behind the gear."

"Why?" A bitter laugh. "So you can all decide I'm crazy and rush me? I know how this works, Bea. I sat through the inquiry too. I watched them decide what happened before anyone asked me a single question."

"You didn't answer any questions," Beatrix said. "You were dead."

"I was *reported* dead." He said it with a strange pride, the way a man might say *I escaped*. "There's a difference. There's a whole world of difference, and nobody on that river seven years ago understood it, least of all the company men who wrote the report."

The water boomed over the weir behind him, throwing spray that caught the morning light and turned it briefly into something almost beautiful. Beatrix thought, absurdly, of the ferry dock at Kessel's Landing, of coffee that tasted like rust, of the small gray life she'd built one careful brick at a time. She had traded that life once already. Apparently the river intended to invoice her again.

"Ada," she said quietly, without turning her head. "Step back toward Tam. Slow as syrup."

"I'm not leaving you."

"That wasn't a request, sweetheart. That was an order from your guide, and unlike everyone else in your life, I actually know what I'm doing."

A pause. Then the scuff of boots on stone, retreating. Good girl. Beatrix let out a breath she'd been rationing.

"There," she said to Merrick. "Happy?"

"No." But the muzzle dipped two inches, which in Merrick's language was practically a sonnet.

* * *

They had known he was close. Juno had spotted him on the ridge two days back, a figure in ancient company gear moving with the patience of a heron, and since then the whole crew had traveled in a state of low simmer. They'd found the cut rope. They'd found the contaminated fuel. Every misfortune since the burned relay station now wore the same face, and the face belonged to a man they had all, in some form or another, mourned.

Beatrix had spent those two days rehearsing conversations. In none of them did Merrick take a hostage. In most of them, he wept. In several, she did.

What she had not rehearsed was how quickly it went wrong.

He moved when Odell shifted his weight—just a shift, just a man with bad knees redistributing himself on a wet rock—and the rifle came up and swung, and Merrick crossed the space between the fish ladder and the boat in four strides that no one, afterward, could quite reconstruct, and then his hand was fisted in Ada's collar and the barrel was against her shoulder and the whole world narrowed to a point.

"Nobody's rushing anybody," Merrick said, breathless. "New terms. The girl comes with me. The rest of you turn the boat around. You have until the count of ten, and I've been alone up here a long time, so my counting is very reliable."

"Merrick—" Beatrix started.

"*One.*"

"Odell," Beatrix said, calm as glass, "get the satchel."

"The—Bea, are you sure—"

"*Two,*" said Merrick.

"The green satchel. Under the thwart. Now, please."

Odell hesitated for one heartbeat—one long, expensive heartbeat—and then he did as he was told, because whatever else Odell Fenn was, he was a man who had learned, expensively, when the map ran out and someone else needed to navigate. He pulled the satchel out and held it, and Beatrix saw him understand what was inside, and saw him choose, in real time, to trust her with it.

Seven years ago, Odell had torn four pages out of her expedition journal and lied about it to an inquiry, and had carried those pages folded against his heart through every disgrace and every border crossing since. He had done it, he'd confessed at the ruined campsite, because the pages told a story that would either save her or hang her, and he hadn't been able to bear finding out which. He had protected her from the truth the way you protect someone from weather—with a roof that eventually becomes a prison.

Now she held out her hand.

"Odell. Give it here."

* * *

"Three," Merrick was saying. "Four."

"Merrick." Beatrix raised the satchel above her head, and something in the gesture stopped him mid-count, the way a strange sound stops any animal. "Before you finish counting, I want to make you a trade."

"There's no trade. There's turning around or there's—"

"There's always a trade. You taught me that, actually. Up at the high camp, remember? When the radio died and I wanted to push on and you wanted to wait, and you said, *everything on a mountain is a negotiation, Bea, even the weather.*" She kept her voice soft, conversational, a rope thrown gently across fast water. "You were right. You were right about so much up there, and nobody ever said so, and I think that's part of why you're standing here with a gun instead of a pension."

The barrel wavered. Ada, bless her stubborn heart, had gone perfectly still—not limp, not struggling, just still, the way Tam had taught her during the portage: *don't fight the water, become heavy in it.*

"What's in the bag?" Merrick asked.

"Pages," Beatrix said. "Four pages, torn from my journal, seven years ago, by a cowardly cartographer with excellent instincts. Pages that describe what I ordered, and when, and why. Pages that describe the crossing I called for against every argument you made—including yours, Merrick. Yours is in there too. Word for word. *The ice upstream sounds wrong. Request we hold position until it settles.*"

Merrick's breathing changed.

"I signed off on the crossing anyway," Beatrix went on, each word laid down like a plank over a chasm. "Because the client was screaming about schedule, and because I was thirty-one years old and certain the mountain would forgive me. It didn't. It took six people instead. And when the report came out, the company wrote a story where the collapse was an act of God and the guide was incompetent, and they blacklisted me to keep anyone from asking why a licensed guide would cross a valley she'd flagged as unstable—which I *had* flagged, in writing, in that journal, until someone senior to both of us decided my caution was bad for the liability picture and my signature was better used elsewhere."

"That's not—" Merrick started, and stopped, and started again. "That's not what happened."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "It isn't. What happened is worse than what they printed, and it's worse than what you think too. And these pages prove it. Every word of it. My handwriting, dated, witnessed." She lowered the satchel slowly, held it out at arm's length like an offering, like bait, like a confession. "You've spent ten years keeping people away from that valley because you couldn't survive the story getting told wrong twice. I understand that better than anyone alive. So here's the trade: the pages, the truth, everything—for the girl. Unharmed. Right now."

Silence, except the weir, roaring its one note.

"And what do you get?" Merrick said finally, and his voice had gone thin and young, the voice of the man who'd fixed her radio in the rain and hummed while he did it.

"The truth gets told *right*," Beatrix said. "By me. At the source, if we make it, or in a courtroom, if we don't. Either way, with my name attached, which is the only currency I've got left. You wanted someone to pay. Here I am, Merrick. I've been paying for seven years. Let me pay in a currency that actually buys something."

* * *

Here is what Beatrix did not say, what she would carry privately like a stone in her boot for the rest of her life: she had not planned to offer the pages. She had planned, in her rehearsals, to talk him down with sympathy and shared memory and the gentle arithmetic of survival. But watching him hold Ada—that furious, frightened, magnificent girl who had dragged Beatrix out of her gray life by the collar the way Merrick now held her—Beatrix had understood something simple and terrible.

You cannot bargain with a man using things he wants. You can only bargain with him using things he *needs*. And Merrick did not need revenge. Revenge was just the shape his need had frozen into, the way floodwater freezes into beautiful, lethal sculpture. What he needed was for the record to stop lying. What he needed was for someone to stand in the light and say *I did this, and here is how, and here is who told the truth when it mattered.*

And so she offered him the only thing she had left: herself, on the record, permanently.

It was, she reflected, the second time she'd traded her anonymity for someone else's survival. The first time had bought her a new name and a quiet town. This one, she suspected, would buy her nothing but a courtroom and a headline. And yet her hands were steadier offering the satchel than they had been in years. There was a lightness in it, like setting down a pack after a portage. Grief, it turned out, weighed less when you stopped hiding it.

* * *

Merrick looked at the satchel for a long time.

Then he looked at Ada, and something in his face broke—not dramatically, not like in the ballads, but the way river ice breaks: quietly, structurally, all at once beneath a surface that had seemed solid for years.

"She looks like him," he said. "Your brother. Tomas. He used to stand exactly like that when he was scared—chin up, feet planted, daring the fear to say something about it."

"You knew my uncle?" Ada's voice came out small and fierce.

"Everybody knew Tomas Vance. He gave me half his rations on the approach and told me it was because I cooked better than him, which was a lie, but a kind one." Merrick's hand loosened on her collar. Did not release. Loosened. "Is he—"

"He's dead," Ada said. "Seven years. They told us it was the guide's fault. They told us—" Her voice caught, and Beatrix watched the girl make a choice, watched her plant her feet harder. "They told us a story. And I came up this river because I didn't believe it."

The rifle lowered. All the way. Merrick stood there holding it pointed at the ground like a man who'd forgotten what it was for, and then he laughed—one short, cracked, disbelieving syllable—and pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"Ten years," he said. "I burned a relay station. I poisoned your fuel—I watered it, I wasn't going to kill anyone, I just—I cut your rope, I followed you, I—" He shook his head. "I became the company's story. Did you notice that? They said the guide was reckless and the river was cruel and no one survived who shouldn't have. And I've been out here being reckless and cruel, keeping survivors away, telling myself it was for the dead." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terribly clear. "I'm tired, Bea. I am so tired of being the lie."

"I know," Beatrix said. "Come down off the ridge. Come with us to the source. See it with your own eyes, and then help me tell it straight—for Tomas, and for the other five, and for you, who they killed on paper because a dead witness asks no questions."

Merrick looked at the satchel. At Ada. At the boat full of strays—Odell clutching his knees with relief he was failing to disguise, Juno already, somehow, heating water on the camp stove as though armed standoffs were a normal pre-breakfast activity, Tam watching Merrick with an expression that Beatrix slowly recognized as recognition. Tam, who hadn't spoken since a wreck he wouldn't explain. Tam, who had been pulling bodies from rivers in his silence.

*Later*, Beatrix thought. *Add it to the pile.*

"Give me the pages first," Merrick said hoarsely.

"After the girl walks to me."

"Afraid I'll renege?"

"Afraid you'll read them standing on wet rock next to a weir," Beatrix said, "and fall in. Sit down first. Then read. Those are my terms, and unlike some people, I count very reliably."

Something that was almost a smile moved under Merrick's beard. He released Ada's collar, and she crossed the rocks to Beatrix in four strides of her own, and Beatrix put a hand on the back of her head—briefly, gruffly, the way you touch something you're terrified of breaking—and then handed Merrick the satchel.

He sat on the rock shelf with the water thundering behind him and unfolded seven-year-old pages with hands that, for the first time in Beatrix's memory, were shaking.

He read for a long time. The crew waited. Juno passed around tin cups of something hot and bitter. Even the weir seemed to quiet, as if the river itself were leaning in.

When Merrick finally looked up, his face had changed—emptied of the siege, filled with something rawer and more useful.

"This line," he said, tapping the page with one finger. "*Ice upstream sounds wrong. Request hold.* That's my entry. That's my handwriting." He swallowed. "Bea. If this goes to the capital—if this clears you—it means the company knew the valley was unstable and sent the crossing order anyway. It means the schedule pressure came from *above* you. It means—"

"It means the blacklist wasn't punishment," Beatrix said quietly. "It was burial. Mine, and the truth's along with it."

Merrick stared at her. Then at the source gorge, invisible beyond the last bend, where the river began and the lies ended.

"Then we'd better hurry," he said, and stood, and slung the rifle across his back like a tool being put away for the season. "The melt's early. And Bea—" He paused, and for a moment he was just a radio operator in the rain again, humming over his wires. "Whatever's left of the old dam up there, it's not stable. I've heard it settling at night. Like something breathing."

Above the gorge, far to the south, the first monsoon clouds were stacking up like unread letters.

Beatrix looked at her crew—her impossible, disgraced, magnificent crew—and picked up her paddle.

"Then let's go meet it," she said, "before it comes down to meet us."
