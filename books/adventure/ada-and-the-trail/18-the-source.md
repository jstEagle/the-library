## 18. The Source

Ada had imagined the headwaters a hundred times on the long pull upriver—usually at night, when sleep wouldn't come and the gorge walls pressed close enough to touch from her bunk. In her imaginings it was always small. A trickle squeezing out of rock, something you could cup in two hands and carry home to show her father: *See? This is what he was after. This is why.*

She was wrong by an order of magnitude that made her laugh out loud, once, helplessly, before she remembered she was furious.

The river simply *ended*.

One moment they were poling through a channel of braided silver water, and the next the channel opened like a door and there it was: a valley walled in ice, rising so high that the morning sun hadn't yet found its floor. Glaciers came down three sides like frozen waterfalls caught mid-plunge, blue-veined and groaning, and between them stood a horseshoe of stone peaks with snow streaming off their summits in long white banners. The air itself seemed lit from below, bounced up off the ice until everything glowed—the boat, Tam's hands on the pole, Beatrix's face.

And at the center of it all, where the glaciers converged, the Veyl poured out of the mountain in a single colossal spring—a wall of water bursting from a cavern mouth wide as a cathedral door, thundering down into a pool so violently turquoise it looked dyed.

"Saints above," Odell whispered beside her. He had his sketchbook out and hadn't drawn anything. He just held it against his chest like a shield. "It's not on any chart. Not one. And it's *this*."

"It's not on any chart," Beatrix said quietly, "because someone paid to have it removed."

Ada turned. Beatrix was standing at the bow with one hand on the painter line, and she wasn't looking at the glacier. She was looking lower—at the valley floor, at the shoreline downstream of the great spring, where the turquoise shallowed into pale gravel bars.

Where a shape sat across the river mouth like a broken jaw.

At first Ada took it for a moraine, a ridge of rock dumped by some ancient glacier. But moraines didn't have straight edges. Moraines didn't have rebar sprouting from them like whiskers, rusted orange against the gray. Moraines didn't have a spillway gate half-buried in scree, forty feet tall, its steel face peeled back like the lid of a tin.

"That's a dam," Ada said. Her voice came out flat and strange, as if it belonged to someone else.

"That's a dam," Beatrix agreed.

* * *

They beached on the eastern gravel bar, in the lee of a ridge, and Ada was the first one over the side because her legs simply decided without consulting her. The gravel crunched under her boots, absurdly loud after days of river-sound, and the cold coming off the ice hit her lungs like a swallowed winter.

Up close, the ruin was worse. Better. Worse and better.

The dam had been enormous—she could trace its curve along the valley floor, a quarter mile of concrete and masonry, most of it still standing, holding back nothing. It had been built to raise the level of a lake that no longer existed; behind it lay only a shallow tarn and a field of boulders dropped by the retreating ice. Which meant the dam had been old, or badly sited, or both—and it had failed, catastrophically, sometime before any of them were born, and the flood it released had carved the lower gorge into the Serrated Narrows they'd nearly died running a week ago.

Except that couldn't be right either, because Odell was crouched at the base of the broken section with his fingers on the fracture face, and he said, in the careful voice of a man reading a will aloud:

"This break is fresh."

Ada looked. The concrete inside the fracture was pale. Unweathered. She'd spent enough of her childhood around her father's mining camps to know what ten-year-old concrete looked like versus fifty-year-old, and this was—

"How fresh?" Beatrix asked. She had come up silent as a gull behind them, hands jammed in her coat pockets.

"Eight years," Odell said. "Maybe nine. Give or take a hard winter." He looked up at her, and there was no accusation in it, only arithmetic. "Your expedition came through seven years ago."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the glacier seemed to stop creaking to listen.

Beatrix walked past them both. She climbed the rubble slope to the torn-open spillway gate, laid her palm flat against the peeled steel, and stood there a long time with her head bowed, like a woman at a graveside who has only just learned whose grave it is.

"The company built it," she said at last, not turning around. "Vance Consolidated. My company. Your father's company, Ada." Now she turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed but steady. "They built a dam at the source of the Veyl to feed a smelter scheme nobody ever approved. It was illegal—it was in the protected watershed, which is why the valley got erased from the charts. And then it broke, and the flood came down the gorge, and seven years ago my team was in that gorge when the second flood came."

"The second flood," Ada repeated.

"Dams fail in stages." Beatrix's voice was very even, the way it went even when she was holding something down. "The first collapse took the center section. The company sent a crew to assess—quietly, off the books. That crew reported the rest was stable. Sound. They put that report in writing, sealed it, and started building again. My expedition was contracted to survey the upper route for their supply line. We were camped in the gorge when the remaining sections let go."

Ada's whole body had gone cold in a way that had nothing to do with the glacier. "My father signed those assessment reports. I've seen his signature on—" She stopped. Started again. "He told me the disaster was your fault. He told me you pushed on past safe margins, that you ignored warnings, that—"

"I know what he told the inquiry." Beatrix's jaw tightened. "Because the alternative was telling the inquiry that his own engineers certified a dying dam, and that the company knew, and that they buried it. One guide's reputation against a hundred jobs and a shareholder class. It wasn't a hard sum for them."

"And you *let* them say it."

For the first time since Ada had known her, Beatrix flinched. Actually flinched, like something thrown.

"I didn't *let*—" She stopped. Breathed. "I tried, Ada. I filed a counter-report. Do you know what happened to it? It never reached the registry. My testimony was struck as 'that of a party under review.' Then the review concluded, and I was blacklisted, and every guide license I ever held was voided, and the valley was erased twice over—once from the charts, once from the record. You cannot fight a company that owns the paper the truth is printed on." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Ada could see her hating it. "So I stopped fighting. I changed my name and I fixed ferry engines. That's my confession, if you want it now, here, on the gravel. I gave up. Seven years of giving up."

Ada wanted to scream at her. She wanted to throw her arms around her. She did neither; she stood in the freezing wind with her fists clenched and understood, with terrible clarity, that both impulses were the same impulse.

* * *

It was Juno who broke the spell, because Juno always broke the spell, usually with food.

"If we're going to have a reckoning," she called from the boat, "we're going to have it fed. There's coffee. There are also the last of the honey biscuits, and I will eat all of them myself if people keep shouting at glaciers."

Nobody was shouting. But they gathered anyway, on a tarp spread over the gravel, cups steaming in the bitter air, and the ruin of the dam loomed over them like a chaperone at a funeral breakfast.

Tam sat closest to the water. He'd been quiet even by Tam standards all morning, and now he set down his cup, picked up a stick, and began to draw in the gravel. Ada watched over his shoulder: a long boxy shape. A gate. An arrow pointing downstream. Then—he paused, glanced at Beatrix, got a nod—numbers. Dates. He wrote *first break* beside one date and drew a second arrow, bigger, and beside it wrote nothing at all, just a jagged line like a heartbeat ending.

"You saw it," Beatrix said softly.

Tam nodded. He tapped the first date. Tapped himself. Then pointed downstream, toward the narrows, toward home, and made a shape with his hands that Ada slowly understood was a boat. A small boat. A boy in a small boat, fishing below a dam that the maps said wasn't there.

"Oh," Ada breathed. "Oh, Tam. The wreck. The one you won't talk about—that was—"

He was already nodding, jaw tight, eyes bright and fixed on the middle distance. He tapped the jagged line. Held up fingers: family. Two fingers, then folded one down. His sister. He'd lost his sister to a flood that officially, on every chart and in every ledger, had never happened. No cause. No compensation. No name for the thing that killed her except *act of the river*, as though the river had done it alone, unassisted, out of spite.

Juno put her hand on his shoulder and left it there.

Odell had gone very still. "That's why the guild struck me off," he said slowly. "I didn't map the valley. I mapped the *flood*. I traced the debris line downriver, hydrology, sediment layers—I published a paper saying the Narrows flood of nine years ago came from a point upstream of the last surveyed tributary. From a structure that shouldn't exist." He laughed, short and bitter. "They called it fraudulent data. Vance Consolidated holds the guild's endowment."

"They hold everything," Beatrix said. "That's rather the point."

Ada looked around the circle—Tam with his ruined numbers in the gravel, Odell with his retracted paper, Juno with her smuggler's charts that had shown a blank valley and a dotted line marked *ask no questions*, Beatrix with seven years of exile sitting on her shoulders like snow—and felt fury rise in her chest so clean and so total that it burned away everything else, grief included, at least for the moment.

Not at them. At the *tidiness* of it. A company had built an illegal dam, certified it with a lie, killed a village's worth of people downstream and a good expedition in the gorge, erased the valley, blamed a guide, silenced a cartographer, broke a boatman, and gone on doing business under her own father's name—and the machinery of the world had *helped*. Every gear had meshed. Every page had stayed torn.

Well. Ada pulled her father's letters from her inner pocket—the ones she'd confronted Beatrix with in the gorge, the ones proving the connection between them—and looked at the topmost signature, the neat confident *Elias Vance* on the assessment certification, dated eleven days before the second collapse.

Her father had signed a lie. Maybe he'd believed it. Maybe he'd been paid to believe it. Either way, the paper existed, and paper could be carried, and she was carrying it, and so was everyone else in this ridiculous, damaged, loyal circle of strays.

"We need evidence," she said. "Physical. Dated. Company-marked. Enough that no endowment can bury it." She looked up at the dam, at the spillway gate with its peeled steel, and her eye caught on something she'd taken for graffiti. Stenciled letters, faded but legible, on the gate housing: **V.C. HYDRO — SITE 1 — PROPERTY OF VANCE CONSOLIDATED**. Below it, a serial number. "Starting with that."

"There'll be more in the works office," Beatrix said. She was staring at the stenciling too, and something in her expression had shifted—not softer, exactly. Sharper. Aimed. "If they ran a site, they ran a site office. Filing cabinets don't drown. I know where it would have been built—high bench, above flood line, standard practice." She stood, brushing gravel from her knees, and for a moment she looked less like a disgraced ferry mechanic and more like the woman who had once led six people into a gorge and gotten five of them out alive. "We make camp here. We search the site tomorrow at first light. And then—"

She stopped.

Ada followed her gaze and felt the hair lift on her arms.

On the far shore, at the mouth of the great spring where the turquoise water boiled out of the mountain, a figure stood on the rocks. Small with distance, motionless, dressed in expedition gear gone gray with years. Watching them. It had been watching them, Ada realized, for some time—long enough that the light had changed around it.

It raised one arm. Not a threat. Almost a greeting. Or a summons.

Then it turned and walked into the shadow of the cavern mouth, and was gone, and the roar of the spring swallowed everything after it like a held breath finally released.

"Tomorrow," Beatrix said quietly, "just got complicated."
