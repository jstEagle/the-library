## 19. Confession at the Headwaters

The river ended the way a life sometimes ends—not with a crash but with a hush.

They came around the last bend at first light and the Veyl simply stopped being a river and became a lake, flat and green-gray as old bottle glass, held in the cup of a valley so steep the sun wouldn't touch the water until midmorning. And above it all, filling the whole southern sky, stood the glacier: a wall of blue-white ice a thousand feet tall, veined with silt and shadow, calving with a sound like distant artillery that rolled across the water and made the hull of their battered boat tremble under Beatrix's boots.

Nobody spoke. Even Juno, who had a running commentary for everything from the weather to the quality of Tam's rowing, went quiet. Odell stood at the bow with his ruined hands folded on his chart case like a man in church. Ada's breath came out in little clouds that hung in the cold air and didn't want to leave.

"There," Beatrix said, because someone had to say something, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. She pointed to the eastern shore, where a shelf of gravel ran out into the lake beneath a scarp of gray stone. "That's where we camped. Seven years ago. That shelf."

It looked smaller than she remembered. Everything did, except the ice.

They beached the boat on the gravel and began, without anyone ordering it, to make camp—because hands needed work when hearts needed time, and all four of them knew it. Juno got the stove going. Odell spread his charts on a flat rock and weighted the corners with stones. Tam took the rope and walked the perimeter the way he always did, silent as a heron, checking their lines against a flood that hadn't come yet but would.

Ada didn't make camp. She stood at the waterline, looking up at the glacier, and then she turned and looked at Beatrix, and Beatrix understood that the hour she had been walking toward for seven years had finally arrived, and it was not going to wait for her courage to catch up.

"Merrick will come down today," Ada said. "He'll have watched us beach. He'll know this is the end of the line."

"I know."

"And you're still going to do what you said? At the weir?"

Beatrix looked out at the water. Somewhere under that green glass lay the bones of a dam—the company's dam, the one that had never appeared on any survey, the one whose collapse had sent a forty-foot wall of water down this valley and killed six people and been blamed, in every official record, on the poor judgment of one guide named Beatrix Dravenmoor. She had spent seven years letting that record stand, because standing under it had felt safer than digging underneath it. Safer, and cheaper, and lonelier than any exile she'd ever known.

"Yes," she said. "I'm going to do what I said."

* * *

Merrick came down at noon, as if he'd kept an appointment.

He descended the scarp on the old fixed lines—his own lines, Beatrix realized, rust-eaten anchors he must have placed years ago and maintained with the devotion other men gave to religion—and he stepped onto the gravel shelf carrying nothing but a hatchet and a canvas satchel, and he looked thinner than he had on the ridge, hollowed, like a man who had been holding a door shut for a decade and could no longer remember what was on the other side.

"Dravenmoor." His voice was rusty too. "You actually came."

"You actually let us." She stayed by the fire, hands open on her knees, the way you sat with a spooked animal—or an armed one. The hatchet hung loose in his grip, which was worse than if he'd brandished it. "You could have sunk us in the Narrows. You could have cut our main line instead of the spare. You didn't."

"I wanted you to turn back." He said it simply, almost wearily. "Every sabotage was a message: *go home.* Nobody ever read it. Your customs men burned my relay stations before I could even—" He stopped. Started again. "Six people died here, Dravenmoor. Six. And the world thinks they died because of *you*."

"I know."

"Do you?" He took a step closer, and Juno shifted by the stove, and Tam materialized at the edge of the camp with a coil of rope over his shoulder, and Merrick laughed—a dry, cracked sound. "Look at them. Ready to die for you. Do they know? Do they know what you did?"

"They know pieces," Beatrix said. "Not the whole. That's mine to give, and I'm going to give it now, if you'll stand still long enough to hear it. All of it. In front of witnesses. On the record." She nodded at Odell. "Odell has paper. Odell has ink. Odell was struck off the guild rolls for caring about records, which makes him the only honest clerk I've ever met."

Odell was already unrolling a fresh sheet on the chart rock, pen ready, his hands steadier than Beatrix had ever seen them.

Merrick's eyes flicked from face to face—the mute boatman, the cook with smuggler's maps, the girl who should never have come this far upstream—and something in him wavered. "A confession doesn't raise the dead."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "But it can stop burying them twice."

She stood up. Her knees cracked. She was fifty-one years old and tired down to the marrow, and she thought: *this is the last thing I will ever be afraid of, so let me do it properly.*

* * *

"Seven years ago," she began, "I led an expedition up this river. Six clients and a crew of five. We were contracted by the Corvane Hydraulic Company to survey the upper Veyl for a proposed power scheme. What we were not told—what the contract deliberately omitted—was that Corvane had already built a diversion dam in this valley two years earlier. Unlicensed. Unreported. It wasn't on any chart because its existence was a crime, and Odell, you were right, the decree erasing the source valley from the maps wasn't about superstition or sovereignty. It was about liability."

Merrick had gone very still.

"We camped on this shelf," Beatrix continued. "On the third night, the river dropped. Dropped fast—two feet in an hour. Any sensible guide reads a falling river as a warning. I read it as luck. A lower crossing meant an easier crossing, and we had a schedule, and schedules were how I'd built my reputation." Her voice caught; she let it catch. There was no point in a confession delivered smoothly. Smoothness was the disease. "So I ordered the crossing moved up. Moved it to dawn. Put eleven people in boats on a river that was falling because something upstream was holding back everything the glacier was sending down."

"The dam was already failing," Merrick said quietly. "It groaned for three days before it went. I heard it from the relay station. I radioed Corvane. They told me to log it as seismic activity."

"And at dawn it finished failing," Beatrix said. "Forty feet of water, maybe more, coming down a valley with nowhere to run sideways. I lost three crew and three clients. You were at the relay station downstream, Merrick, and the world decided you'd drowned with the rest, because Corvane found it convenient to close the expedition's books entirely. And me—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—me they left alive. Alive and guilty. Because a disgraced guide was a much better story than a criminal corporation. I signed the incident report they wrote for me. Did you know that? I was so broken I signed whatever they put in front of me. I confessed to bad judgment because bad judgment was survivable and the truth was not. I told myself the truth would get buried anyway. I told myself I was protecting the families from a fight they couldn't win."

"That's the part I came to hate you for," Merrick said. His knuckles were white on the hatchet haft. "Not the crossing. The signing. You let them write *her fault* on six graves, and then you disappeared and changed your name and fixed ferry engines while I—" He stopped. Looked at the glacier. "While I stayed. Seven years. Keeping watch over a graveyard nobody was allowed to visit."

"I know," Beatrix said. "And I'm not asking you to forgive the signing. I'm telling Odell to write it down exactly as I said it. Every word. My name, my signature, my order, my cowardice after. And then the rest of it—the dam, the dates, your radio logs if you still have them, the decree, all of it. The record gets corrected or I don't leave this valley. That's not a bargain, Merrick. That's just what's owed."

The silence stretched. The glacier calved somewhere high up, a long white avalanche of powder, and the boom of it rolled across the lake and broke over them like weather.

Ada stepped forward. She had her father's letters in her coat, Beatrix knew—the ones proving the connection between them, the uncle Beatrix had gotten killed. Beatrix braced for it. But Ada didn't take the letters out. She just stood beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, and faced Merrick.

"My uncle was Elias Vance," Ada said. "He was one of the crew you lost. I grew up on the story they told—that a vain guide rushed a crossing to protect her reputation. I hated you before I ever met you, Ms. Thackeray. Or Dravenmoor. Whoever you are." Her voice shook and she let it. "But I've watched you for three weeks now. I've seen you read water nobody else can read, and I've seen you flinch every time someone says the word *source*, and yesterday I saw you offer this man anything he wanted to keep my father's crew safe. So here's what I think. I think the woman who signed that report and the woman standing here are both real, and I'm done pretending only one of them counts. Write it all down, Mr. Odell. All of it. Then let's take it home."

Merrick looked at her for a long moment. Then at Beatrix. Then, unexpectedly, at Tam—who had drifted closer and now stood holding out something in his open palm.

It was a rusted relay-station key. Morse telegraph key, brass gone green, cord frayed. Merrick's breath went out of him like a punctured lung.

"You went back for it," he whispered. "At the burned station. You went back into the fire for—"

Tam shrugged, eloquent as a speech. He tapped the key once with one finger, a soft metallic *tick*, and pointed at Merrick, then at his own ear, then at the sky, and though he said nothing—had said nothing since a wreck he wouldn't explain, on a river none of them had asked about—it was as clear as language: *someone ought to be listening.*

Merrick sat down on the gravel. Just sat, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and the hatchet fell beside him and neither he nor anyone reached for it.

"Corvane's regional director was a man named Halloway," he said, staring at the water. "I have his letters. Ordering me to falsify the seismic logs. I kept them because I didn't know what else to do with them. I kept them for seven years like they were burning a hole through the floor of my chest." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious and, underneath it all, so young. "What happens to me now? I burned two relay stations. I cut your ropes. I poisoned your fuel—I diluted it, I wasn't trying to kill anyone, I was trying to *stop* you—"

"You nearly killed Tam in the gorge country," Juno said, not unkindly, stirring the pot as if discussing soup. "Nearly drowned the lot of us twice. But you also kept the old portage lines rigged, and I'd bet my best pan those channels you know are about to matter, because—" she jerked her chin at the southern sky, where the clouds had been stacking all morning, bruise-dark and low, "—that monsoon's running ahead of schedule, and I'd say it makes landfall tomorrow, not in five days."

Everyone looked at the sky. Everyone looked at Beatrix.

"So here's the trade," Beatrix said. "You come downriver with us. You testify. Halloway's letters, your logs, my confession—all of it goes to the capital together, one story, no gaps for lawyers to hide in. And when the record's corrected, you turn yourself in for the stations and the sabotage, and I'll stand in that courtroom and say you warned us every single time. That you never once tried to kill us. That the worst thing you wanted was to be heard." She extended her hand. "That's the price. Survival, paid in full, by everyone. No discounts."

Merrick stared at her hand. Then he took off his own glove, and his grip was freezing and desperate and strong.

"For Elias Vance," he said.

"For all of them," Beatrix said.

Behind them, Odell's pen scratched steadily across the page, writing down the truth at last, and the first fat drops of rain began to spot the gravel around them like dark stars.

* * *

They struck camp in twenty minutes, because there is nothing like rising water to settle an argument, and by the time the rain had thickened into a curtain the boat was loaded and Merrick was at the bow pointing out channel markers only he remembered—a submerged fence post here, a drowned boathouse roof there, the skeleton of the old dam itself lurking a fathom under the green water, its broken gates yawning like a shark's jaw.

"Stay left of the gates," he called back over the rain. "Left, and pray!"

Beatrix took the tiller. Ada and Tam hauled the sweeps. Juno bailed and swore cheerfully. The lake was already breathing—rising, shivering, the far shore dissolving into gray.

Ada leaned close to shout over the storm. "Where does the current go fastest?"

Beatrix grinned at her, wild and soaked and lighter than she had been in seven years. "Downhill, girl! Everything else is detail!"

The rain came down like the sky had given up holding it, and the Veyl, full of glacier and monsoon and seven years of buried truth, carried them toward home.
