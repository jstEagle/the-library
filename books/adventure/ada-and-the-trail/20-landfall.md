## 20. Landfall

Merrick had spent seven years learning to read the sky, and the sky was lying to him.

He stood at the stern of the boat with his hands cupped around a tin mug of Juno's tea—too sweet, always too sweet, as if sweetness could be rationed out like an apology—and watched the clouds come down the valley in a way clouds were not supposed to come. They should have been another day out. Maybe two. The weather signals from the last relay had been clear: landfall in nine days, and they had planned around nine days the way you plan around anything, which is to say hopefully.

But the wind had shifted in the night, swung around to the northeast, and it carried that smell he knew from his years running radio for supply expeditions—the smell of wet stone and green rot, of a country about to be wrung out like a rag. The monsoon wasn't coming down the river toward them. It was coming *across* the divide, early and angry, cutting off every retreat they'd mapped.

"Your face is doing something," Juno said beside him, ladling porridge into bowls with the serenity of a woman who had decided long ago that panic was a waste of good seasoning. "I want to know if it's a bad something or a *bad* something."

"The second one."

"Hm." She handed him a bowl. "Eat first. Bad somethings go down easier on a full stomach. That's not wisdom, it's just true."

He ate. It was strange, being included in things now. Strange and uncomfortable, like wearing boots borrowed from someone with different feet. For seven years he had been a man outside fires, watching light through trees, counting other people's laughter from a ridge. Now Odell nodded to him in the mornings. Now Tam—Tam!—had taken to sitting next to him at meals, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing, which from Tam was practically conversation.

Now Ada looked at him without flinching, most of the time.

"You're sure?" Beatrix came up the deck, rolling her sleeves, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of survey twine. She didn't look at him when she asked. She hadn't quite figured out how to look at him yet, and he understood that. He hadn't figured out how to look at himself either.

"I'm sure," Merrick said. "The front jumped the divide overnight. We have maybe a day before the headwater catchments start dumping. After that, every hour the river comes up a foot, then two, then you stop measuring feet."

Beatrix studied the water as though she could interrogate it. "The descent through the gorge took us six days coming up."

"It'll take three going down. If we run it right."

"And if we run it wrong?"

"Then we won't need to worry about the monsoon," Merrick said, "because we'll have been introduced to the rocks personally."

Juno snorted into her tea. Beatrix almost smiled—a small, grim thing, but real. That was the other strange thing: how quickly these people had folded gallows humor into their daily bread, the way you fold salt into dough. Seven days ago he had been cutting their rope in the dark. Now he was making jokes over porridge and nobody had thrown him overboard yet. He kept waiting for the moment it stopped making sense. It kept not coming.

* * *

They held council on the flat rock above the weir pool, maps spread and weighted with stones, and this was where Merrick earned whatever it was he was earning.

"Everyone knows the main channel," he said, crouching over Odell's charts. "That's the trap. The main channel funnels straight through the Gullet—that slot canyon halfway down—and in flood the Gullet doesn't run water, it runs *pressure*. Whole river compressed into forty meters of rock. Nothing floats through that. Boats get sucked under and spat out as kindling."

"So we portage the Gullet," Ada said.

"In flood? The portage trail follows the channel. It'll be waist-deep by tomorrow afternoon." He tapped a line west of the marked route, a faint pencil ghost only he could see the logic of. "There's another way. In my second season, before everything—we got caught high water once, early melt, and the pilot I was working under, old Vasquez, she cut through the side channels instead. The Braids, we called them. A dozen interlocking channels through the willow flats where the valley widens. Most of them are dead ends. Silt traps. But there's a thread—" his finger traced it, slow, remembering rather than reading, "—a thread that connects all the way through, past the Gullet entirely, and comes back to the main stem below the slot. Vasquez called it the Needle. You thread the Needle, you skip the worst twenty kilometers of that canyon."

Odell leaned in, squinting. "It's not on any chart."

"No chart has it. Vasquez never wrote it down—said a channel like that is only safe while one person alive remembers it, and after that it's just a rumor that kills people." He paused. "She died the year after. So for a long time I was the rumor."

"That's very dramatic," Juno said approvingly. "I'd have put it in a song."

"Can you actually find it?" Beatrix asked. Her voice was level, but he heard the question underneath it, the same question everyone aboard had been quietly asking since the standoff at the weir: *can we trust the man who sabotaged us to navigate us?*

Merrick set down his mug and met her eyes—one of the few times he'd managed it. "Seven years I've walked these ridges keeping people away from the source. Do you know what that means, practically? It means I know this river better than any living soul. Every eddy, every deadfall, every gravel bar that shifts with the seasons. I spent all those years learning the country so I could guard its door." His mouth twisted. "Turns out all that knowledge works just as well pointed the other direction. I'd like to find out what that feels like. Being useful in the open, I mean. Instead of useful in the dark."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Tam, who had been crouched at the edge of the rock testing the current with a stick, rose and came over and looked at the map, and looked at Merrick, and put one blunt finger on the entry point of the Braids and nodded once.

"Well," Juno said. "The boatman has spoken. Democracy's over. Thread the Needle."

Ada laughed—short, surprised, the laugh of someone who'd forgotten she could. Merrick felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

* * *

They ran the first day hard and clean. The boat, refloated and patched after the rockslide, handled beautifully under Tam's hands, and Merrick called the channels from the bow—left here, hug the gravel, watch the sweeper—and each call that proved true bought him another degree of something he hadn't had in seven years: standing.

By midday the rain arrived. Not the monsoon proper, but its outriders—hard gray squalls marching down the valley, soaking them to the skin between bursts of sun that felt like mockery. The river began to change color, going from green glass to the brown of steeped tea, and little tributaries that had been trickles in the morning were hauling sediment by evening.

"Water's up eight inches since noon," Tam said. Two words. A speech, by his standards. He'd been rationing out words more freely since the portage, as if speech were a muscle he was carefully rehabilitating, and every sentence he produced was treated by the crew like a small festival.

"Eight inches in six hours," Merrick said. "Tomorrow it'll be a foot an hour. We sleep in shifts tonight and run at first light."

They camped on a high bench above the willow flats, and Merrick took first watch because sleeping was still a skill he was relearning. He sat with his back against a boulder and listened to the river rise, and Ada came and sat with him, wrapped in an oilskin, saying nothing for a long time.

"My uncle used to say rivers talk in their sleep," she said finally. "That you could hear how much trouble they were in by the sound."

"He was right. Listen—that low grinding under the surface noise? Boulders moving. When a river starts rearranging its own furniture, you want to be gone."

She was quiet again. Then: "You knew him. My uncle. Didn't you."

It wasn't a question, and he didn't insult her by pretending otherwise. "Yes. Tomas Vance. He ran comms training when I first signed on. Taught me morse properly—everyone else let me be sloppy, and he made me do it until my wrist ached." He watched the black water slide past. "He was kind to me when I was nineteen and terrified and pretending not to be. I thought about that a lot, these seven years. How the kindest man on that expedition was the one whose family I hurt worst by staying dead."

"You weren't dead, though. That's the thing I keep snagging on." She hugged her knees. "You were just *gone*, and being gone did nearly as much damage as dying would have. Your name on the casualty list is half of why everyone believed the official story. Half of why Beatrix carried it alone."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned to look at him, and in the dark her eyes caught the last gray light. "Because I've been thinking about it. About what you were doing, all this time. Everyone keeps calling it sabotage, and it was, but—it was also grief, wasn't it? You survived something horrible and the story they told about it was a lie, and you couldn't fix the lie, so you just... guarded the wound. Kept everybody away from it so nobody could poke it."

Merrick found he couldn't speak for a moment. The rain ticked on the oilskin between them.

"Yes," he managed. "That's exactly it. And it was cowardice dressed up as vigilance, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But yes."

"Grief usually is," Ada said, with the terrible simplicity of seventeen-year-olds who have recently buried too much. "Cowardice dressed up as something braver." She bumped her shoulder against his, briefly, and left him alone with the watch and the rising river and the strange, terrifying sensation of being understood.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and the Needle was waiting.

The entrance to the Braids was a gap in the willow wall no wider than the boat, invisible unless you knew to look for the way the current curled *into* it rather than past. Merrick stood at the bow with Tam at the sweep behind him, and the whole crew silent, watching the brown water pile up against the flats.

"There," he said, pointing. "See the foam seam? It bends left before the willows. That's the channel pulling. Aim for the bend."

Tam adjusted. The boat slid through the gap into a world that shouldn't have existed—calm water threading between willow islands, the roar of the main channel muffled to a distant heartbeat on their left. Rain fell soft and steady. Herons lifted off ahead of them, indignant, one after another, like a series of increasingly annoyed sentries.

"It's beautiful," Ada breathed.

"It's a lie," Merrick said. "Every one of these channels looks like this, and eleven of the twelve end in silt bars you can't see until your hull's on top of them. Stay sharp."

For four hours he navigated by memory, and the memory held. Left at the split oak. Right where the two dead snags leaned together like conspirators. Through the narrows where the willows closed overhead and they poled in silence, branches whispering along the gunwales, and Juno whispered, "This is either the loveliest or the most sinister place I have ever smuggled through, and I once hid in a coffin shipment, so the bar is high."

Twice they hit dead ends and had to backtrack, poling hard against the current while the rain thickened, and twice Merrick found the thread again—Vasquez's thread, worn into him like a groove in old wood. At the far end the Braids opened, the willows parted, and there was the main stem of the Veyl below the Gullet, swollen and furious and utterly bypassed.

The crew cheered. Actually cheered—Juno whooping, Odell laughing his dry cartographer's laugh, Ada throwing both arms around Merrick before catching herself and stepping back, pink-faced. Tam simply extended one hand, and Merrick shook it, and the handshake lasted a beat longer than handshakes do.

"Not bad," Beatrix said, coming forward. "For a man who spent seven years practicing being a ghost."

"Turns out haunting is excellent fieldwork."

And that was when the sky ended.

There was no gradual arrival. One moment it was rain; the next the whole southern sky simply *came down*, a gray wall of it, swallowing the ridgelines, and the wind hit the river flat and turned the surface to driven smoke. Merrick shouted the numbers over it—river up a foot already, two by dark, the Needle's exit would be underwater by morning, no going back, no stopping—and saw the words land on each face and be absorbed, and saw no one break.

"We make for the gorge mouth tonight," Beatrix yelled, "and we run the flooded gorge at first light, all of us, together!"

Later, soaked through, hauling the boat higher up the bank while the river climbed hungrily beneath them, Merrick worked beside Ada and Tam and Odell and Juno and Beatrix, seven hands on one hull, and understood with sudden clarity that he had crossed some line in the last hour and could not cross back. He was not guarding the door anymore. He was on the wrong side of it—the right side—with the flood coming and the gorge waiting, and the only way home ran straight through the worst water this river had ever thrown at anyone.

Above the roar, Odell suddenly grabbed his arm and pointed upstream, toward the black shape of the gorge mouth.

Lights. Faint, bobbing, wrong—moving *against* the current, deep in the gorge where nothing should be moving at all.

"Tell me that's a reflection," Juno said.

Nobody told her anything. The lights blinked—twice, pause, twice—and Merrick's blood went cold, because his wrists remembered what his mind had tried to bury: that was signal code. Old expedition code. *Distress. Come.*

From inside the flooded gorge, someone was asking them to come in.
