## 21. Through the Flood

Tam had not spoken in six years, and the river was about to make him wish he'd never learned how.

He stood at the stern of the *Kindly Gull*—a name Juno had painted on the hull as a joke, because the boat was neither kind nor gull-like—and watched the gorge swallow the sky. The monsoon had come down the valley like a debt collector. The water that had been green and patient two days ago was now brown and furious, carrying whole trees sideways, carrying a fence post, carrying what looked disconcertingly like someone's roof.

Merrick stood beside him at the tiller, pointing out channels from memory so old it might as well have been memory of a previous life.

"Left of the split rock," Merrick said. "There used to be an eddy behind it. There is no longer an eddy behind it."

"There is no longer a split rock," Odell said, consulting his map with the calm of a man reading his own obituary. "The rock is downstream somewhere. Possibly in Kessel's Landing by now."

Beatrix crouched amidships, lashing down the last of their gear with knots so tight they'd have to be cut off the boat like tumors. Ada worked beside her, matching her knot for knot, and Tam noticed—not for the first time—that the girl had stopped looking scared around day nine and started looking hungry instead. Hungry for the water. That was either very good or very bad, and he hadn't decided which.

"Tam." Beatrix didn't look up from her rope. "Your read on the current?"

He held up three fingers, then pointed at the gorge mouth, then made a rolling motion with his hand.

"Three minutes to the narrows," Ada translated. She'd gotten good at this. Better than good. Somewhere between the portage and the rockslide she'd learned to read his hands the way other people read weather. "And then?"

He made the rolling motion again. Bigger.

"And then everything rolls."

"Wonderful," said Juno, who was bailing anyway, on principle. "I love a boat with ambition."

* * *

The gorge mouth took them the way a dog takes a thrown stick.

One moment they were a boat; the next they were a leaf in a fist. The *Gull* dropped into a trough deep enough that Tam saw sky only through a lens of spray, then climbed a wave that shouldn't have existed in a river at all—it belonged to the sea, it had gotten lost—and slammed down its far side hard enough to crack something below the waterline.

He felt it through his boots before he heard it. A change in the boat's voice. Every boat has one; Tam had spent six years listening to them instead of talking, and he knew the *Gull*'s voice the way he knew his own pulse.

She was dying.

He caught Beatrix's eye across the chaos and tapped his boot twice, then drew his hand flat across his throat.

Her face did something complicated and fast—grief, arithmetic, decision—and came out the other side as command. "Odell! High ground, left bank, two hundred yards, that shelf above the scree!" She turned forward. "Ada, Juno—packs on, lash them to your backs, not the boat! Merrick, hold her straight as long as you can!"

"How long is as long as I can?" Merrick shouted back.

"You'll know!"

"That's not reassuring!"

"It's not meant to be!"

The shelf came up fast—a gray tongue of stone jutting over the flood, maybe four feet above the water and rising. Beatrix threw a line. It fell short. She threw again, and this time it caught a stunted pine growing sideways out of the rock like a man leaning out a window to yell at the neighbors, and Juno hauled them in hand over hand while the boat ground against stone beneath them with a sound like teeth.

They got Ada up first. Then Juno. Then Odell, who lost his map case in the exchange and made a noise Tam had never heard a grown man make.

Then the *Gull* gave up.

It happened between heartbeats. One moment the hull was under Tam's feet; the next it folded, quietly, almost politely, and the river reached in and took everything. Tam went under with it.

The water was not cold so much as *opinionated*. It had views about where he should be, and none of them were upright. He tumbled through brown murk, snagged something—a branch, a rail, a memory—and tore free, and came up gasping in the lee of the shelf with his hands already finding rock because his hands had always known what to do even when the rest of him didn't.

Beatrix hauled him out by the collar like a kitten.

For a moment they all just lay on the stone, breathing, while the river shouted underneath them about what it thought of their plans.

Then Odell sat up, dripping, and said, "I would like it noted that I saved the inkwell."

"You saved the *inkwell*," Juno said.

"A cartographer without ink is just a man with opinions."

"We're alive," Ada said, half laughing, half crying, wringing out her braid. "We're actually alive."

"Don't celebrate yet," Beatrix said. She was standing at the edge of the shelf, looking downstream, and Tam saw her do the thing she did—the counting thing, the guide thing, the thing that had ruined her once and was now, apparently, going to ruin her again if she let it. "We've lost the boat. We've lost most of the food. We are forty miles from Kessel's Landing with a monsoon still coming down the valley behind us, and this shelf goes underwater when the crest arrives."

"When does the crest arrive?" Ada asked.

Beatrix looked at Tam.

He looked at the water. Looked at the trees on the banks—the debris line, the new wrack marks, the speed of the current against the rocks. Held up eight fingers. Then pointed downstream, and walked two fingers along his palm, slow, slower.

"Eight hours to the crest," Ada translated. "And we can't walk faster than the water."

"So we float," Beatrix said. "We just don't float in a boat."

Everyone looked at her.

"We float," she said, "in whatever's left."

* * *

What was left, it turned out, was a great deal—if you were willing to be undignified about it.

The *Gull*'s wreckage kept arriving at the base of the shelf like tardy guests: a section of hull, both oars, the bail bucket, Juno's entire spice collection in a floating cloud of regret, and—because the river has a sense of humor—one intact crate of the expedition's trade goods, bobbing serenely, containing oilcloth, cordage, and two empty barrels with their lids.

Beatrix directed the salvage like a woman setting a table for a war.

"Oilcloth over the packs. Cordage cut into arm's lengths—I want everyone carrying three pieces minimum. Barrels sealed, stoppered, and tied in pairs." She looked up. "Congratulations. You are now a raft."

"A raft of *barrels*," Odell said.

"People have survived worse ideas."

"Name one."

"The idea that got us up here."

Tam, meanwhile, was doing the work nobody else could see. While they salvaged, he watched the water. Not the flood itself—he'd made peace with the flood—but its *grain*. A flooded river isn't one current; it's a braided rope of them, fast lanes and slow lanes, seams where they touch and boil, and if you can read the grain you can pick your way down it the way a sailor picks a path through reef. Six years of silence had taught him to see it. Silence does that. It leaves room.

By the time the barrels were lashed, he had the route in his head: right seam past the drowned willow, hard left at the church spire of rock, then ride the fast lane down the middle until the gorge bent and the water told them what came next.

He gathered them in a circle on the shelf. Eight hours had become seven. The crest was audible now, far upstream, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat.

He held up two fingers, pointed at the barrel pairs, then at two people each.

"Two per raft," Ada said.

He nodded. Pointed at himself and Beatrix. Then at Ada and Merrick. Then Juno and Odell. Then he pointed downstream, made the rolling motion, and—this was the important part—held his palms flat together and slid them apart: stay together, but not too close.

"Rafts in a line," Ada said. "Close enough to help, far enough apart that we don't tangle."

He nodded again. Then he did something he almost never did. He took Beatrix's hand, turned it palm-up, and traced a line across it—the route, the whole route, seam by seam, turn by turn—pressing each waypoint into her skin like a signature.

She watched his finger the whole way. When he finished, she closed her hand around the invisible map and said, "I've got it. Every mark."

He believed her. That was the strange part. Six years ago he wouldn't have believed any guide with anything, and this one least of all. But he'd watched her count water for twenty days now, and he knew what he knew.

Juno broke the moment, because Juno always did. "If we die," she said, tying down the spice tin to her chest like a breastplate, "I want it noted that my last meal for all of you was excellent, and the review should reflect that."

"Noted," Ada said solemnly.

"Five stars," Odell said.

"Four and a half," said Merrick. "The fish was dry."

"The fish was *perfect*, you absolute—"

"Rafts in the water," Beatrix said. "Now."

* * *

The crest caught them an hour later, in the bend, exactly where Tam had hoped it wouldn't.

He heard it before he felt it—a wall of new water coming down the gorge, taller than the flood, carrying the monsoon's first full argument. There was no outrunning it. There was no beaching. There was only the thing guides say in the last hour of every bad decision: *stay on top of it.*

He screamed it with his hands—both fists pumping overhead, *up, up, stay up*—and whether the others understood or simply obeyed instinct, they all hit the surge facing forward.

The world became brown thunder.

Tam rode his barrel-pair like a horse he didn't trust. Beatrix locked her legs around the cords behind him, one arm around his chest, and shouted nothing at all, because there was nothing to shout and nowhere to shout it to. They surfed the crest's shoulder for what felt like a year. Once, in a lightning-flash gap in the spray, he saw Ada and Merrick's raft ten feet to the left, riding clean, Merrick's arms around the girl like a man holding onto the last good thing in the world. Once, worse, he saw *nothing* where Juno and Odell should have been, for eleven full seconds, and those seconds cost him years.

Then the water spat them all out the bottom of the surge into the relative sanity of the flooded valley below, and Juno's voice came floating across the rain, ragged and triumphant:

"—and ANOTHER five-star review, that's TWO rapids survived, the kitchen remains OPEN—"

"They're alive," Ada called, from the other raft, and started laughing, or sobbing, or both.

Tam put his head back against Beatrix's shoulder and let himself breathe.

* * *

Night fell on the flood and refused to lift.

They ran the dark water by lightning alone—Merrick calling depths from a decade-old memory, Odell confirming against a map he now held inside his shirt against his skin, Tam reading the seams by feel, by the pull of the current through the cords, by the sound the water made against the barrels. Beatrix kept them in formation. Ada kept them fed—hardtack passed raft to raft on a thrown line, eaten in the rain, tasting like victory tastes, which is to say like nothing at all and better than anything.

Somewhere in the smallest hour, Tam's raft swept alongside Ada's, close enough to talk.

"Tam," she said. Her voice was hoarse. Her hands were bleeding where the cordage had run through them all night. She looked seventeen again, and also like she'd never been seventeen at all. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

He waited.

"Why don't you talk?"

The rain filled the pause. He could have given her the easy answer—the wreck, the guilt, the word that died in his throat the day the *Marisol* went down with four souls aboard and his voice went with it. He could have traced it in the air and been done.

Instead he took her bleeding hand, turned it palm-up the way he'd done with Beatrix, and pressed one finger into it. Just one. Pressed hard enough to mean it.

Then he pointed at her. At Beatrix, on the raft behind. At Juno, Odell, Merrick. At all of them, strung down the dark river like lanterns.

Ada stared at his finger on her palm. Understanding moved across her face slowly, the way dawn moves across water.

"You didn't need words," she said. "You needed people worth using them on."

He shrugged, which in Tam's language was a paragraph.

She laughed—wet, cracked, real—and squeezed his finger once before the current drew the rafts apart again.

* * *

Kessel's Landing announced itself at gray dawn as a smell: woodsmoke, wet wool, and Juno's spices somehow still faintly present on every one of them. Then as a light. Then, blessedly, as a dock—with figures on it, shouting, throwing lines that were too short, and finally a fishing skiff putting out into the flood to meet them because the townsfolk of Kessel's Landing had never once in its history let a rescue go to waste when there was an audience.

They came ashore in ones and twos, half-drowned, half-frozen, entirely ridiculous. Juno kissed the dock planks. Odell kissed his map case. Merrick stood ankle-deep in the flood and looked back upriver for a long time at the country that had swallowed him once and finally, grudgingly, given him back.

Tam came ashore last, with Beatrix's hand on his arm and Ada's on the other, and stood swaying on solid ground like a man who'd forgotten what it was for.

A crowd was gathering. Someone had brought blankets. Someone else had brought brandy, which Juno confiscated immediately for medicinal purposes, meaning hers.

And then a voice from the crowd—an official voice, a customs-officer voice, a voice Tam recognized and disliked on principle:

"Is it true? Did they come down from the source? Is there a witness statement? Because there's a man here from the capital, arrived on yesterday's packet, asking after a Beatrix Dravenmoor—"

Tam felt her go stiff under his hand.

But Ada stepped forward, chin up, soaked to the bone, and said clearly, so the whole dock could hear:

"She's right here. And she has something to tell you."

Beatrix closed her eyes. Opened them. And Tam, who had spent six years learning that silence is a choice, watched her choose otherwise.
