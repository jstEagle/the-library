## 22. The Names We Keep

The letter from the capital arrived on a Tuesday, which Ada would remember later because it was also the day the new keel plank came in on the morning barge, and Beatrix made her carry both.

"You take the mail," Beatrix had said, elbow-deep in the boat's open hull, tar to her wrists. "I'm not touching anything official until I've had breakfast. Official things before breakfast have a way of ruining the whole day."

So it was Ada who stood in the yard of the boatworks with the government envelope in her hands—thick paper, wax seal, the kind of correspondence that made the ferry clerks straighten their collars—and read it three times while the river ran silver and loud behind her.

*The Commission of Inquiry into the Veyl Headwaters Disaster has concluded its proceedings. The testimony provided by the survivors of the recent rescue expedition, together with recovered documentary evidence, has established that the flooding which claimed eleven lives seven years past resulted from the catastrophic failure of an unlicensed impoundment structure constructed by the Corvane Supply Company. The findings of the original inquiry are hereby vacated. All professional sanctions against Beatrix Dravenmoor are rescinded in full.*

Rescinded in full. Four words that had taken seven years and one flooded gorge to arrive.

Ada read them a fourth time anyway, just to feel them sit there.

Then she walked down to the slipway, held the letter out at arm's length like something that might bite, and said, "It's here. It's done. You're—you're cleared."

Beatrix looked up from the hull. Tar dripped off her forearm into the sawdust. She didn't reach for the letter right away. For a long moment she just looked at it the way you look at weather coming over a ridge—assessing, measuring, deciding whether to trust it.

"Well," she said finally, and took it, and read it standing in the bottom of a half-gutted riverboat with her hair tied back in a kerchief and pitch under her fingernails. "Huh."

"*Huh?*" Ada heard her own voice crack with indignation. "Seven years! A blacklist, a false name, a whole life thrown away, and all you've got is *huh?*"

"I'm pacing myself." Beatrix folded the letter along its original creases, very carefully, and tucked it into her shirt pocket, over her heart, which Ada was fairly sure she did not do on purpose but did anyway. "If I let myself feel the whole thing at once I'll sit down in this sawdust and not get up until spring."

"Would that be so terrible?"

"Yes. The hull wouldn't finish itself." But her voice had gone rough around the edges, and she turned back to the planking fast, and Ada pretended not to see her press the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes.

The town, of course, had no such restraint about pacing itself.

By noon everyone in Kessel's Landing knew. By evening the Kettle & Anchor couldn't fit another body through the door, and Ada found herself wedged between Odell—who had put on his old guild coat for the occasion, the one with the torn-off insignia, wearing the tear like a medal now—and Juno, who kept refilling people's cups and telling anyone who'd listen that she'd *known*, she'd known from the first crate they pulled out of the water, hadn't she said so, Tam?

Tam, sitting at the end of the bench with a cup of cider he wasn't drinking, nodded once. Which from Tam was a speech.

"They're going to want you back," Odell said to Beatrix across the table, leaning in over the noise. "The guild. The companies. There'll be offers. Contracts. They'll come crawling with charts and apologies."

"They can crawl all they like." Beatrix turned her glass slowly on the scarred wood. "I've got a boat half-planked and an apprentice who still ties off a bowline like she's strangling it."

"It's a *functional* strangle," Ada muttered, and the table laughed, and Beatrix's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling when she was trying not to—which was, Ada had learned, its own kind of smile.

Because here was the truth of it, the one Ada had worked out somewhere in the middle of the flood, lashing gear to salvaged timbers while the gorge roared: the world could hand back a name, but it couldn't hand back the years. Beatrix Dravenmoor had been cleared, and Beatrix Dravenmoor was also gone—not dead, just dissolved, like salt in the river. What remained was the woman at the boatworks who fixed ferries and flinched at official envelopes and had taught Ada, over six weeks of sawdust and tar and patient repetition, how to read water.

That woman wasn't going anywhere. Ada had checked. Repeatedly. With questions about apprenticeship terms that were barely disguised as questions about apprenticeship terms.

* * *

The boat, when they finally got her upright and named, was called the *Second Opinion*.

This was Juno's doing. There had been a vote. Ada had proposed *Veyl's Daughter*, which everyone agreed was beautiful and then quietly ignored; Odell had proposed *Cartographer's Revenge*, which everyone agreed was accurate and then also quietly ignored; Tam had been invited to propose a name and had responded with a long look at the river and a shrug that somehow conveyed *she'll tell us when she's ready.*

"She being the boat?" Ada had asked.

Tam had shrugged again, more elaborately, meaning *obviously.*

In the end it came down to Juno, standing on the slipway with her hands on her hips, surveying the freshly painted hull. "She's the same boat we went up in," Juno said. "Same ribs, mostly same planks, patched and re-floated and full of scars. And what did she turn out to be? Not what anybody said she was. So." She slapped the gunwale. "*Second Opinion.* Because everybody deserves one."

Beatrix had opened her mouth—Ada could see the objection forming, something dry about superstition and names that invite mockery—and then closed it again, and looked at the boat for a while, and said, "Paint it on both sides. If we're going to be laughed at, let's be legible."

They painted it on both sides.

The work filled the weeks the way work is supposed to: steadily, with sore shoulders at the end of each day and something at the bottom of each one that felt almost like peace. Odell came every morning with his restored guild credentials pinned inside his coat where nobody could see them—he'd been reinstated, backdated, with a letter of apology so groveling he kept it folded in his map case "for morale"—and redrew the source valley chart by lamplight, the real one this time, the one with the dam's ghost marked in careful hatching where the flood had scoured it. He'd titled it simply *The Veyl Above the Falls, Corrected*, and when Ada asked him if he wasn't angry, after everything, that the correction had taken seven years, he'd thought about it honestly, the way he thought about everything.

"Angry's a fuel," he said. "Burns hot and leaves nothing behind. I'd rather have the map."

Juno stayed too, though she claimed she was only staying for the cooking arrangements—"You try feeding four people on a riverboat galley and then tell me about adventure"—and hung her smuggler's maps in the boatworks office in a row, framed, like trophies. Some of them were technically evidence of crimes. Nobody mentioned this. The customs officer who had once threatened Beatrix with exposure now bought his coffee from Juno and pretended not to recognize any of the framed coastlines.

And Tam—

Tam talked now. Not much. Not easily. But the words came, one or two at a time, like stones placed carefully across a crossing, and the crew had learned to walk them slowly.

He told them, one evening in October, sitting on the slipway with his boots in the shallows, about the wreck he'd never explained. A passenger ferry, nine years ago. He'd been at the tiller. He'd made a call in white water—the wrong call, he believed, though the inquiry had cleared him—and three people had gone into the river, and one had not come out. He hadn't spoken since, not properly, because the words had seemed like a debt he owed the drowned and speaking them cheaply felt like theft.

"But you speak now," Ada said gently.

Tam watched the current for a long time. "Boat sank," he said finally. "New boat." He tapped the slipway timber beneath them, where the *Second Opinion*'s fresh paint gleamed in the last light. "New words fit new boat."

Ada understood that this was as close as Tam would ever come to saying *you saved me* —and that he meant it for all of them, and that it was enough.

* * *

The first snow didn't come until well into the season's turn, and when it did it fell soft and unhurried, dusting the boatworks roof and turning the river gray-green and quiet. Inside the shed, the stove ticked, and Beatrix stood at the big drafting table with the *Second Opinion*'s season plans spread out, and Ada stood beside her, and for once neither of them was working.

"The commission sent a second letter," Beatrix said. "Did I show you?"

"You showed me. You read it aloud in a voice like you were reading a grocery list."

"It contained a grocery list of sorts. Offers. Consultancy on river safety regulation. A seat on the rewritten inquiry board." Beatrix's finger traced the edge of the chart without quite touching it. "They'd like me in the capital by midwinter. There's talk of a formal ceremony. Restoring my license with—what was the phrase—'*appropriate public recognition.*'"

"What did you write back?"

"That I'd testify in writing, advise by post, and attend no ceremonies, as I had a boat to finish." She glanced sideways at Ada. "Was that wrong?"

Ada considered it seriously, because Beatrix always asked her to consider things seriously now, and that was maybe the strangest and best gift of the whole year—that the great disgraced guide treated her seventeen-year-old apprentice's opinion as cargo worth carrying.

"No," Ada said. "But ask me again in ten years. Maybe I'll want the ceremony vicariously."

"Hm. Ambitious." Something warm moved through Beatrix's voice. "Good. Stay ambitious. Ambition aimed at the right things will keep you honest better than fear ever did."

Outside, snow whispered on the water. Downriver, the relay stations were being rebuilt—proper ones this time, funded by the company's forfeited assets, staffed by operators whose names were posted publicly so that silence could never again be mistaken for accident. Merrick had taken the job of inspecting them. He'd left in the autumn without much farewell, just a nod at the dock and a sentence Ada kept turning over like a river stone: *"Somebody should watch the high ground. Might as well be somebody who knows why."*

He wrote sometimes. Short letters, dated carefully, each one cataloguing a station repaired, a route re-surveyed, a stretch of bank cleared of the old expedition's debris. In the latest one he'd included a pressed sprig of glacial forget-me-not from above the falls, and a line that read: *The headwaters are quiet. They should get to stay that way. Tell Thackeray the record reads true now—I checked it twice.*

Ada had pinned the flower above the drafting table. It had lost most of its color but none of its shape.

"Apprentice," Beatrix said, snapping her out of it.

"Guide," Ada corrected, because they'd settled on that title weeks ago and she guarded it jealously.

"Guide, then." Beatrix rolled up the season plans and tapped them against her palm. "First run of the year goes upriver as far as the Serrated Narrows. Survey work for the new relay line. Two weeks, easy water, a crew of five." She paused, and the pause had a shape to it, deliberate as rope-work. "Any objections?"

Ada ran the tally the way she'd been taught: crew experienced, vessel sound, route known, season early, weather window fair. Then she ran the other tally, the one no chart recorded: the way her hands had stopped shaking in white water sometime during the descent through the flood; the way the word *upriver* now tasted like homecoming instead of dread; the way five people in a battered boat had become, without anyone voting on it, a family.

"No objections," she said. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When we pass the burned relay station, we stop. I want to repaint the marker. Put the old crew's names back on it—all of them. Uncle Aldous too. The ones the official story got wrong deserve to be written right somewhere they can see the river."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. Snow ticked against the shed roof. When she spoke, her voice was steady, but Ada had spent enough months beside her now to hear the depth sounding underneath it.

"That," Beatrix said, "is the best condition anyone's ever attached to a contract with me. Write it into the log. First entry of the season."

Ada took down the logbook—the brand-new one, its spine still stiff—and uncapped the pen, and wrote the date, and the boat's name, and the crew: *B. Dravenmoor, master. A. Vance, guide. O. Fenwick, cartographer. J. Marlowe, cook and navigator. T. Ashen, boatman.* Five names. Every one of them earned twice over.

Below them, the Veyl moved in the gray light, carrying snowmelt down from a source that was finally, truly, allowed to be what it was. The *Second Opinion* sat on her new keel, patched and painted and full of scars, waiting for water.

Ada capped the pen and reached for her coat.

"Come on, then," she said. "She's telling us she's ready."
