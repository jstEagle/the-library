## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Sable doesn't keep time. It keeps distance.

I learned that from my mother, who learned it from the river itself, and I've spent forty-one years confirming it. Ask anyone on the water how long they've been running cargo and they'll tell you in miles — *since the flood year, maybe six thousand miles up and down* — because years are for people with houses, people whose lives accumulate instead of erode. A year on the Sable is just the same water going past the same hull, wearing you down a coat of paint at a time until one day somebody scrapes you back to bare wood and asks your name.

My name is Lior Oyelaran, and my house is a hundred and ten feet of riveted steel named Ada, after nobody in particular, though I let passengers believe it was a wife or a daughter because it makes them treat her gentler.

* * *

Morning on the Ada starts with Sefi's hammer.

Not because anything's broken — something is always broken, but that's not why — but because Sefi believes an engine should be woken the way you'd wake a sleeping bear: loudly, respectfully, and with a tool in your hand. She's been at it since before dawn, down in the engine room where the air tastes like hot iron and diesel sweetness, talking to the main motor in a low voice she'd deny using if you asked. She talks to machines the way other people talk to God. The difference is the machines answer.

"Number two injector's weeping again," she reported through the deck plate, which is to say she shouted it up through the open hatch while I was coiling line on the foredeck.

"Weeping or complaining?"

"There's a difference?"

"With injectors? No." I wedged the coil under its pin. "Run her rich till Corrinne. We'll buy a gasket with the fuel money."

"That's not how money works, Captain."

"It's exactly how money works. Money is a rumor we agree to believe so the gasket store stays open."

From the wheelhouse came the sound of Tomas laughing at his own joke before he'd finished telling it, which is how most of Tomas's jokes arrive — pre-approved by their author. He had the morning watch, feet up on the rail, coffee mug balanced on his chest like he was being buried at sea already. Twenty-three years old, all elbows and optimism, a deckhand who'd come aboard two seasons ago as a fare and never gotten off, mostly because I never asked him to and he never asked to stay. Some contracts are signed that way.

"You know what this river needs?" he called.

"Silence."

"A radio station. Like, a real one. Music, weather, gossip—"

"The Sable has a radio station. It's called shouting."

He laughed again, delighted, and I let him have it. That's most of captaining, honestly. Letting people have things they've already taken.

* * *

We were running light that week — pump parts downstream to Corrinne, dried fish upstream to Halfway Landing, the usual blood of commerce moving slow through the Sable's veins. The flood had come through in the spring and rearranged everything, as floods do. Sandbars I'd known for a decade had picked up and moved like tenants avoiding rent. The channel markers were lies now, every one of them, cheerful orange buoys floating over mud or missing entirely, swept off to decorate some oxbow lake. It meant I worked the wheel by memory and suspicion in equal parts, reading the water's face the way you read a person's — the smooth glassy lie of deep water, the wrinkled frown where current argues with a bar.

I know every mile of this river the way you know your own handwriting, including the places where it's changed and pretends it hasn't.

That's not a boast. It's a diagnosis.

Debts, too, travel the Sable both directions. We owed the fuel dock at Corrinne four months' worth, owed old Marta at Halfway for the fish bait she fronts us against better weeks, owed the Ada herself a drydocking we kept postponing because drydocking means numbers, and numbers mean sitting still, and sitting still means the river gets ahead of you. Everything on the water is deferred. You pay later, or downstream, or in kind. The interest is measured in rust.

And yet. There are mornings — this was shaping into one — when the mist burns off the flooded timber and the herons stand in the drowned willows like an audience waiting for the show to start, and the Ada pushes her blunt bow upriver at eight knots, eight honest knots, and I think: *this is enough.* This is the whole arithmetic. A boat, a crew, a river, a load. Miles, not years.

Then the radio crackled.

* * *

"Sable Queen to any station, Sable Queen to any station." The voice was a woman's, clipped, corporate-polite, the kind of voice that has never once shouted across open water. "Ada, if you're monitoring, please respond."

Tomas looked at me. I looked at the radio like it had personally betrayed me.

"Ada," I said. "Go ahead, Sable Queen."

"Captain Oyelaran?" A pause, the particular pause of someone checking a file about you. "This is Meridian Consolidated, logistics division. We have a contract opportunity. Are you in a position to talk terms?"

Meridian Consolidated. The name went down my spine like cold water finding a leak. They were the drilling concern, the ones with the upstream leases, the company whose barges passed us loaded with equipment going toward the headwaters and empty coming back, always empty, always in a hurry, as if whatever they were taking out of the ground couldn't wait and whatever they'd done to it wouldn't keep.

"We're always in a position to talk," I said, because we were, because the gasket store does not accept rumors.

"Resupply run. Our survey expedition at Kettle Bend, above the Split Rock narrows. Provisions, mail, replacement parts. Standard charter rates plus twenty percent."

Tomas made a noise like a kettle achieving enlightenment. Twenty percent on top of standard rates was the kind of money that paid off Corrinne, paid off Marta, paid for the drydocking and the gasket and possibly a new injector, and left enough over for Tomas to buy a hat he didn't need.

"What's the catch?" I asked. There is always a catch on the Sable. Free cheese is found in mousetraps and company charters.

"No catch. Routine resupply."

"Routine," I repeated.

A hesitation. Small. Professional. "Our expedition has been... difficult to reach by radio recently. Weather in the headwaters, most likely. We'd like confirmation they're supplied and operational. You'd be doing us a service."

Difficult to reach. On a clear-sky week, with the repeater tower at Split Rock broadcasting fine — I knew, because I'd heard the fishing boats chattering on it that very morning. Rivers don't hide radios. Only people do.

"When do you need us loaded?"

"Tomorrow, if possible. First light."

Of course first light. Companies love first light; it makes everything feel inevitable.

"I'll take it under advisement," I said, which on the Ada means *yes*, and signed off before I could hear myself say yes out loud.

* * *

Sefi came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag that had once been a shirt and would soon be a rag again, full circle. She listened to my summary with her head tilted, the way she listens to a bearing that's running rough.

"Twenty percent," she said.

"Plus confirmation of personnel. They want eyes on the camp."

"Nobody pays twenty percent for canned peaches and mail." She tossed the rag onto the workbench. "They pay twenty percent because they're afraid of what the answer is, and they want it delivered by somebody expendable."

"That's a cynical read."

"That's an accurate read. I'm an engineer. Accuracy is the whole job." She looked upriver, toward the narrows, where the water went green and narrow and old. "Lior. When did Meridian last ask anybody for help?"

I didn't have an answer, which was an answer.

Tomas, meanwhile, had done the math out loud, standing at the rail with his arms spread like the figurehead of a much stupider ship: fuel debt gone, Marta square, drydock in the fall, and — I watched him arrive at it — *a payday big enough to matter*. Big enough to be a door. I recognized the look. It's the look of a man measuring his life in miles and realizing he wants, someday, to measure it in something else. Years. A porch. A hat.

"We're doing it," he said. Not asking.

"We're discussing it," I said.

"Captain." Sefi's voice was quiet, which from Sefi is louder than shouting. "If we take this contract, we take everything that comes with it. Company money is never just money. It's a hook."

"Everything's a hook," I said. "The question is whether there's bait worth the hook."

She held my gaze for a long three seconds, then shrugged and went below, hammering, which is how she says *I told you so* in advance.

* * *

That evening I stood alone in the wheelhouse while the Ada rode her anchor in the slack water off Corrinne, lamps coming on along the fuel dock, moths beginning their shift. Upriver, past the last bend I could see, the country turned to flooded timber and silence, and somewhere up in that silence was a camp full of scientists who weren't answering, and a company that wanted them confirmed operational, and twenty percent of a number large enough to fix everything wrong with my life except the thing actually wrong with it.

Because here's the truth I don't put in the logbook: the money wasn't the ache. The ache was older than the offer. Forty-one years on the water and I could name every sandbar between here and the sea, and not one of them knew my name back. A life measured only in distance adds up to nothing no matter how far you go. You can run a river your whole life and still be nowhere when you stop.

I keyed the radio and raised the Sable Queen.

"Meridian, Ada. We'll take the charter. First light."

"Excellent, Captain. Load manifest follows. And Captain—" that small professional pause again, the sound of a file being consulted, "—the expedition leader is Dr. Imka Voss. If you reach her, tell her Meridian looks forward to her report."

*Click.*

I sat with that a while. *If* you reach her. Not *when*. Companies are careless with words only when the words are true.

Below me, Sefi's hammer had gone quiet. Tomas was asleep in the fo'c'sle, smiling at something. The Ada creaked at her lines, patient as ever, ready to carry whatever I put aboard her — cargo, crew, or consequences.

First light, then. Upstream, into the quiet.

I should have burned the contract on the spot. Instead I did what the river taught me: I measured the distance, and I went.
