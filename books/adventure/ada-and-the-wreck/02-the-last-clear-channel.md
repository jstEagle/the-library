## 2. The Last Clear Channel

The Sable River has exactly two moods, and I have learned to distrust both of them. There is the mood where she runs brown and lazy and pretends she's harmless, and there is the mood where she remembers she's older than every country that's ever tried to name her. On the third morning above Kessel Bend, she was wearing the second mood, and the *Ada* was arguing with her about it.

I don't mean the crew argued. The crew always argues. I mean the boat herself — the long shudder through her hull when the current grabbed her stern, the way the wheel went heavy in my hands like a living thing leaning against me. Thirty-one years old, iron-bellied, patched in nine places with steel that didn't match because it came from nine different wrecks, and still the finest vessel on this river, which is a low bar but I'll clear it proudly.

"Log boom at two miles," Sefi called up from the engine hatch, voice flat as ever, as if reporting the weather on Mars. "Company chain across the channel. Two men, one dog."

"One dog," Tomas repeated from the bow, delighted. "What kind of dog?"

"The kind that bites survey crews' resupply captains, presumably."

"Could be a nice dog."

"Tomas."

"I'm just saying we haven't met him yet. Statistically some dogs are nice."

The *Ada* carried four of us in those days: me; Sefi Okonkwo, my engineer, who trusted boilers more than bishops and had earned the right; Tomas Reyes, deckhand, nineteen, all elbows and optimism, who'd signed on in Port Anselm with nothing but a duffel bag and the belief that rivers were romantic; and me, which made three, plus Vess, our cook, who doesn't count as crew so much as weather — constant, essential, impossible to argue with.

We were running the Meridian contract. Resupply for the Halvard Survey, twelve days up into the flooded headwaters where the maps went soft and the radio had gone quiet. Triple rate, half up front, and the money sitting in an escrow account with my name on it like bait on a hook. I'd known it smelled wrong when I took it. I took it anyway, because the *Ada*'s hull inspection was due in spring and hull inspections are how riverboats die of paperwork.

Now the water was rising around us, three feet over seasonal in two days, and the checkpoints were getting less polite.

The checkpoint at Kessel Bend was two Meridian men under a tarp strung between a fuel barge and a dead willow, and the dog turned out to be a rangy yellow thing that looked like it would bite you out of professional obligation rather than malice. One of the men had a company lanyard and a company rifle and the particular boredom of someone paid to stand in rain.

"Commercial traffic halts at the flood line," he said.

"We're not commercial traffic. We're contracted support." I passed down the manifest, keeping my voice at the register I use for customs men and angry wives — reasonable, unhurried, faintly disappointed in them. "Meridian's own paper. You're welcome to radio your office and explain why their supply boat sat at your boom for six hours."

He read it twice. Rain dripped off the tarp edge between us. Behind me I heard Tomas breathing the way he breathes when he's trying very hard not to say anything, which is louder than most people talking.

"Water's high," the man said finally. "You lose the channel above the Narrows, nobody's coming for you."

"That's why they pay me what they pay me."

They dropped the chain. The dog watched us go with what I swear was pity.

Sefi climbed to the pilothouse as soon as we were clear, wiping her hands on a rag that had been black since before I owned the boat. She stood beside me without speaking for a full minute, which for Sefi is a paragraph.

"You lied to them," she said.

"I declined to share information."

"You told them we were turning back at the Narrows if conditions worsened. We are not turning back."

"No," I said. "But it bought us past the boom without a search, and now they think they know where we'll be."

"And do you know what you're doing?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I watched the banks slide by — drowned farms, rooftops standing in brown water like teeth in a gum, a church steeple with the bell still hanging in it, ringing once when a branch knocked it, a sound I will hear until I die. "I know what I'm being paid to do," I said. "It's not always the same thing."

"It should be," Sefi said, and went back down to her engines.

* * *

That night we tied off to a drowned oak, engines idling low, and had the argument. Every voyage has one argument that matters and forty that don't, and this was the one that mattered.

Tomas wanted the payday. He laid it out with his hands, the way he does, standing on the cargo deck in the rain-shadow of the pilot house while Vess's stew steamed under the tarp. "Triple rate. Half already paid. Hull work, new boiler tubes, and we still walk away with enough to winter in Port Anselm like actual people. All we have to do is deliver groceries to some scientists."

"Groceries," Sefi said.

"And whatever else is on the manifest."

"The manifest lists 'one sealed container, return priority.' Return. As in, it comes back with us. To Meridian. Have you considered why a drilling company pays triple rate to move a box it already owns, through a flood zone, from a camp that has stopped answering its radio?"

"Because they're rich and disorganized?"

"Because," Sefi said, with the patience of a woman explaining valves to a child, "the money is a trap. Escrow releases on delivery confirmation. Delivery confirmation requires radio contact with Relay 7. Relay 7 has been silent for nine days. So either the money never comes, or it comes only after someone at Meridian decides we saw what we were supposed to see and nothing else. Either way, we are the only boat on this river heading toward the silence."

I let them go at it. A captain who referees every fight ends up owning all of them. But I listened, and underneath both voices I could hear the other sound — the one that had been growing for two days, the one that wasn't a sound at all. The silence ahead of us. Radio static where there should have been relay chatter. No barge traffic, no fisherman skiffs, no company patrol boats above the Narrows. A river that empty isn't peaceful. It's evacuated.

Vess broke the tie, the way Vess does, without appearing to have entered the argument. "Eat," she said, putting bowls in hands. "Dead men skip meals. Live ones don't get to choose."

In the morning the vote was mine alone to make, because that's the shape of the job. I stood at the wheel with the escrow number in my head and Sefi's arithmetic in my ears and Tomas's face doing its terrible honest hoping, and I turned the *Ada*'s nose upriver.

"Boiler pressure?" I called down.

"Full," Sefi said, and I heard what she didn't say, which was *your funeral*.

* * *

The Narrows took us at noon. The river pinched between bluffs that had been hills last month, and the flood had turned the whole valley into one fast brown muscle. The *Ada* fought it for four hours — Sefi coaxing revolutions out of an engine built for calm water, Tomas on the bow sounding depths with a pole and shouting numbers that got worse the higher upstream we climbed, Vess wedged in the galley door swearing at the stove in three languages.

And through all of it, the radio.

I kept it on the expedition frequency the whole way, volume low, the way you keep a lamp lit in a house where someone died. Static, mostly. Twice, fragments — pieces of the Halvard transmissions, torn and reassembled wrong, the way rivers tear things apart and hand them downstream.

*—do not approach the east channel, I am telling you, do not—*

*—six souls, requesting immediate—*

*—it knows the boats. I don't know how else to say it. It knows the BOATS—*

At dusk the river widened again, briefly, into a drowned bottomland where the water lay flat as poured tin, and for the first time in two days the *Ada* ran easy, and somehow that was worse. Tomas came up to the pilothouse, soaked, grinning the grin he uses to keep fear at arm's length.

"Sounds like they lost their minds up there," he said. "Scientists. Probably just equipment failure and panic. Happens."

"Sure," I said.

"Radio's junk anyway this far up. Interference off the bluffs."

"Sure."

"Lior." He leaned on the rail beside me. "We're still good, right? This is still the plan?"

I looked north, where the channel bent away toward the headwaters and the last of the light. Somewhere up there was a camp, twelve days' run, and six scientists, and a sealed container that a dying man had ordered kept shut, and a silence so complete it had texture, like deep water pressing on your ears.

"Tomas," I said, "go tell Sefi I want full steam at first light. And tell Vess to cook everything. Everything we've got."

"Why everything?"

"Because tomorrow," I said, "we find out what the river's been keeping."

Ahead of us, in the dark, the static on the open channel changed — just for a moment, just long enough — into something that was almost, but not quite, a voice.
