## 3. What the Flood Kept

The Sable stopped being a river somewhere past the Kettle and started being a rumor of one. Water where my charts said timber. Timber where my charts said water. I'd run this stretch eleven years ago with a different boat and a younger opinion of myself, and the river had spent the intervening decade rearranging the furniture out of what I could only assume was spite.

"Captain," Sefi called from the engine room, her voice arriving through the deck grating like a complaint filed in triplicate, "we're drawing two feet over a bottom that should be six."

"Then the bottom's moved, and we'll move with it."

"That's not how displacement works."

"It's how *survival* works. Displacement is for philosophers." I eased the Ada's wheel a half-point to starboard, feeling her answer through the wood the way she always did—reluctantly, then completely, like a cat deciding to forgive you. "Keep her turning over. If she coughs, cough politely."

Tomas stood at the bow rail with the sounding pole he refused to trade for a depth sounder, reading the water the old way, by color and temper. He'd been quiet since the checkpoint at Two Sisters, quieter even than his usual morning quiet, which was already a kind of weather system.

"Mud coming up," he said. "Brown going to copper. That's flood silt, Captain. That's not normal high water."

"I know."

"And that—" He pointed upstream to where the treeline leaned into the current at angles trees don't grow at. "That's not normal anything."

He was right, which was becoming a habit of his I found increasingly inconvenient. The whole headwater country had been drowned and re-drowned; you could see the watermark on the trunks three feet up, then five, then eight, the river unable to make up its mind about how much it wanted to take. Somewhere ahead of us was the Meridian survey camp, nine scientists who had gone silent nineteen days ago, and a contract paying more than the Ada had grossed in any two seasons of honest work.

I don't believe in omens. I believe in invoices. But the river was doing its best to convert me.

* * *

We made the last bend an hour before dusk, and Tomas saw it first because Tomas always sees things first when seeing them will ruin your day.

"Camp's off the waterline," he said. "Way off. That's the supply tent—" he squinted—"floating."

Floating is a generous word. The tent had come loose of its moorings and lay draped over a drowned alder like a shroud somebody had given up on arranging properly. Beyond it, where the charts promised a gravel bench above the high-water mark, there was only brown water moving with a slow, muscular patience, and the tips of things sticking up out of it that might have been fence posts or might have been masts.

I brought the Ada in against the current, easy as easing a splinter out, and put her nose into the lee of the alder stand. The engine died to nothing. And in that nothing, we heard it: the sound a flooded camp makes. Canvas slapping water. Something metal swinging and tapping, swinging and tapping, keeping time like a metronome nobody asked for.

"Radio mast's still up," Sefi said, appearing on deck with grease to her elbows and binoculars she'd liberated from somewhere. She swept the shore once, twice, and went very still in the way that, in Sefi, constitutes screaming. "Captain. The boats."

"What about them?"

"They're all still here. Both survey skiffs, the pontoon—they're sunk at their moorings. Nobody took a boat out." She lowered the binoculars. "Nobody left by water."

Nobody left by land either, as it turned out. We found the camp—or the idea the camp had been—in waist-deep water among the alders. Tents shredded, and I want to be precise about shredded, because I've seen what bears do to canvas and what storms do to canvas, and this was neither. The tears were long and parallel, four of them, spaced like fingers. The mess tent had been peeled open top to bottom and its contents were scattered across fifty yards of floodwater with a thoroughness that looked less like violence than enthusiasm.

The galley cooler bobbed against a table leg. Tomas fished it out and opened it, and then closed it very quickly and set it down and walked ten feet away and stood there with his hands on his hips looking at the water.

"We eating tonight?" I asked him.

"No," he said.

Fair enough.

Sefi found the radio shack half-submerged, its transmitter dead, its logbook sealed in a waterproof case and floating in the drawer like it had been waiting for us. The last entry was nineteen days old. The handwriting started neat and ended like seismograph readings. The final legible line read: *It is not in the samples. It is IN THE RIVER.* Underlined twice. Then a third time, hard enough to tear the page.

"Cheerful fellow," I said, because someone had to say something.

"Lior." Sefi almost never uses my name. "Where are the other eight?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Nine scientists. One shredded camp. Zero boats launched. And no bodies, which after thirty years on this river I can tell you is the worst possible number. Bodies are terrible, but they're *answers*. The river had kept every one of them, and the river does not keep things without intending to use them later.

* * *

We heard her before we saw her—a voice, thin and cracked, coming from the high ground where the equipment shed clung to the slope above the flood line.

"OFF THE WATER. Get OFF the water, get OUT of it, get—"

She came down the bank at us with a fire axe held in both hands, and I will say this for Dr. Imka Voss, whoever she had been nineteen days ago: her stance was excellent. Weight low, axe angled, eyes doing the work of a woman who had decided some time ago that everything not visibly her was hostile. Her hair had gone fully white at the temples, which I'd have guessed was dye if her eyes hadn't confirmed otherwise. She'd been sleeping in her boots and her boots had been sleeping in mud.

"Dr. Voss?" I said, hands wide, standing very still on the Ada's deck. "Lior Oyelaran. Meridian sent us. Resupply contract. We've been trying to raise you for—"

"You answered." Her voice caught on something. "You *answered*. Nineteen days. Nobody answers." The axe point dropped an inch. "Get out of the water. All of you. Now. It knows the boats. It comes up under the boats."

"Now hold on," Tomas began.

"*Under the boats.*" She said it the way you say a thing you have watched happen. "Okafor went in to free the pontoon line on day four. The water didn't even splash right. There was no splash. Things that big make a splash." Her eyes were fixed on the brown current sliding past our hull, tracking it, and I felt the hair rise on my own arms because I realized she wasn't watching the water. She was watching for a shape *in* the water, and she knew exactly how big a shape to watch for.

"Ma'am," I said carefully, "why don't you come aboard, dry off, and tell us—"

"Not aboard. Never aboard. You'll take me away from it and it will follow the crate, it always follows the crate—"

And there it was. Her gaze flicked, just once, involuntary, up the slope to the equipment shed, and I watched Tomas and Sefi perform the same calculation I did, at the same instant, like a crew thinking with one brain.

There was something on that hillside worth guarding with a fire axe and nineteen days of madness.

"Stay here," I told my crew, in the tone that means *stay here*, and went up the bank alone.

* * *

The shed door had been barricaded from the inside with shelving, a generator, and what I sincerely hope was a decoy crate, because it was riddled with axe marks. Voss had followed me up, axe lowered now but still in her hands, hovering at the threshold like a dog torn between defending the house and trusting the visitor.

Inside, behind the last shelf, sat a steel case the size of a sea chest. Military gray. Sealed with a band of welded plate that somebody had clearly done in a hurry with a portable rig—and around it, drawn on the floorboards in what I chose to believe was paint, was a ring of symbols and a border of salt gone damp and gray.

"Dr. Voss," I said. "What's in the box?"

"Samples," she said. Then, quieter, in the voice of a woman correcting herself for the thousandth time: "Evidence. Proof. It doesn't matter what it's called. It matters that Meridian wants it and it must never—*never*—go back to Meridian."

"The company funded your expedition."

"The company caused it." She laughed, a small terrible sound with nothing in it. "Upstream drilling, eighteen months, straight into the deep channels. They cracked something open and then they sent us down to see what came out. We were never scientists to them. We were *witnesses*, and witnesses are only useful until they sign affidavits or stop breathing." She crouched beside the crate and put one hand flat on the steel, the way you'd touch a horse you were calming, or maybe the way you'd touch a thing you were afraid would notice you. "Eight of us came down the river. Ask me where they are. Go on. Everyone asks eventually."

I didn't ask. Some questions you can see the answer to from a long way off, hanging in the water like a shape that doesn't splash.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You're coming aboard the Ada. The crate's coming too—not because I believe you, though I'll be honest, ma'am, the finger-marks on those tents are arguing your side of it—but because I'm not leaving a living soul on this bank overnight. Come dawn, we decide together what's true and what's next. Deal?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Nineteen days alone with a fire axe looked back out of her face, and underneath it, faint as a watermark, the scientist she'd been before.

"If it comes up under your boat," she said, "and it will try, it likes engines, it feels them through the hull—you'll cut the lines and run it aground. Promise me. You beach the boat and you get OUT of the water and you let it have the boat."

I thought about the Ada. About eleven years of her, about what she owed me and what I owed her, and I said, "I promise to consider it," which is captain for *no*, and she knew it, and something in her face said she'd expected exactly that much honesty and accepted it as the best available rate.

* * *

We got the crate aboard by block and tackle, Voss supervising every inch of it like a woman loading a bomb, which, for all I knew, we were. Sefi lashed it to the mainmast step amidships—the strongest point of the hull, chosen without anyone telling her why that mattered—and Tomas cast off the mooring lines while I took the wheel.

Dusk came down purple and thick. The Ada turned her nose downstream, home water, easy water, and for about four minutes I let myself think the hardest part was behind us.

Then Sefi, at the depth sounder, said quietly: "Captain. Bottom's coming up fast. Six feet. Four." A pause. "There's no shoal here. I've got the chart right in front of me."

"Tomas, sound it."

He dropped the pole, and I watched his face do something I'd only seen once before, the night the Ada nearly went down in the Kettle ice. He pulled the pole up out of the water and held it dripping, and he didn't say anything at all, because along the pole's length, below the waterline marks, the wood was scored. Long gouges. Four of them. Parallel. Spaced like fingers.

Beneath our keel, in forty feet of floodwater, something the size of the Ada herself rose up to look at what had come to carry the crate away.

"Voss," I said, very calmly, hand already on the throttle. "Which way to the shallows?"
