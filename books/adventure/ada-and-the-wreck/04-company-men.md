## 4. Company Men

The fueling station at Kilometre 212 was the kind of place the river forgot on purpose—a concrete apron gone soft at the edges, a fuel barge lashed to pilings that leaned like tired men, and a single floodlight that buzzed and flickered as if it were thinking about quitting. We tied up just after dusk, which was later than Lior liked. The captain had a rule about night moorings: never, unless you had no choice or something to hide. I remember thinking, in the way you think things without letting them become thoughts, that we were probably both.

I was on deck coiling lines when the gunboat came.

You hear them before you see them—Meridian boats have an engine note like a wasp trapped in a tin roof, all high whine and no humility. Sefi heard it first, because Sefi hears everything mechanical before anyone else, and she came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag that had been black since before I met her.

"Company," she said. Just that. One word, flat as the water.

Lior stepped out of the wheelhouse with that unhurried walk they do, like the world is a corridor they've already measured. "Cargo run," they said, before anyone asked. "We're nobody."

"We're not nobody," Tomas said, meaning me, meaning my own mouth. "We're the Ada."

"We're nobody tonight." And there was something in their voice—not fear, exactly. Something colder and more patient than fear. I didn't have a name for it yet. I would later.

* * *

The gunboat swung alongside with the arrogance of anything that's never had to worry about its draft. Two men aboard visible, one at the helm wearing a Meridian Consolidated windbreaker over what was very obviously body armor, and one standing at the rail with a loudhailer he didn't need, because the river was quiet enough to hear the fuel barge settling against its fenders.

"Riverboat Ada, this is Meridian security vessel Kestrel. Cut your engines and prepare for inspection."

Lior picked up our own speaker, thumbed the switch, and smiled—I could hear the smile, which should have frightened me more than it did. "Evening. What inspection? We're under charter to the survey office at the headwaters. Resupply contract. Paperwork's aboard if you want to read it."

A pause. On the Kestrel, the man with the loudhailer lowered it and conferred with his partner. Then: "That expedition has been unresponsive for eleven days. Meridian is concerned for its personnel and property. Have you had contact?"

Here's the thing about Lior Oyelaran: they can lie the way other people breathe, without any of the tells—the swallowed pause, the too-quick answer, the eyes sliding off. They stood at the rail in the buzzing light and said, "Contact? Captain, we've been fighting water since Kilometre 90. The river's up half a meter a day up there. If your people are quiet, maybe they're busy staying alive. Or maybe their radio drowned. Radios drown out here every season."

It was beautiful. It was true-shaped. Every word of it was technically defensible, and none of it was true, because Dr. Imka Voss was at that moment sitting in our galley drinking tea she hadn't touched, staring at a steel crate lashed down in our hold like it might apologize to her.

The Kestrel's man considered. "We'll inspect regardless. Standard procedure."

"Standard procedure," Lior repeated pleasantly, "requires a warrant or a charter clause, and requires you to file it with the River Authority, whose nearest office is two days downstream. But you're welcome to come aboard for tea while we wait for the paperwork to catch up with you." A beat. "Fuel first, though. My engineer doesn't pump for armed men. Bad for her nerves."

Sefi, who has no nerves that I've ever found, said nothing, which was somehow perfect.

Another conference on the gunboat. The river held its breath. Somewhere behind me in the dark, I heard Voss's door close—softly, deliberately—and understood that she'd heard everything.

Finally: "Proceed with fueling. Remain at this station until morning. We'll verify your charter with regional offices." And the Kestrel backed off fifty meters and anchored, its running lights burning like a pair of patient eyes.

* * *

Below, in the galley, the crew gathered the way crews do—nobody calling a meeting, everybody showing up. Voss sat in the corner with her hands around a cold mug. Sefi took the bench by the engine-room hatch so she'd feel the deck through her boots. Lior stood, because Lior always stands when there's a decision being made, even if they haven't told anyone it's a decision yet.

"They'll check the charter," Sefi said. "It'll hold. The contract's real."

"The contract's real," Lior agreed. "So we're clean. We resupply nothing, we deliver one passenger and her luggage wherever she asks, and we go home rich."

"And the crate?" I asked.

"What crate?" Lior said, mild as milk.

And that—that was the moment. Not the gunboat, not the lies to men with rifles. That moment, in the galley, when the captain looked at me with those calm river-bottom eyes and pretended, in front of me, that the thing taking up a third of our hold didn't exist. As if I were cargo. As if I were someone to be managed.

"I carried it aboard," I said. "With these hands. I know how much it weighs. I know it hums."

"It hums?" Voss looked up sharply, first real expression she'd shown in two days.

"Cold storage unit," Sefi said quietly. "Battery draw. I noticed the load three nights ago." She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at Lior. "Captain. What are we doing?"

"We're getting paid," Lior said. "Go fuel the boat, Tomas."

* * *

I fueled the boat. Forty minutes dragging hose and watching the gauge climb, forty minutes with the Kestrel's lights on my neck and my blood going hotter with every liter. Because here's what I kept circling back to, out there alone on the apron with the smell of diesel in my nose: Lior lied to the company easily, yes. But Lior also lied to *me*, and the second lie was smoother than the first.

I've been aboard the Ada six years. Six years of sandbars and bad coffee and the captain teaching me the river one mistake at a time—"No, there, Tomas, *there*, feel how the current folds? That's the river telling you where the bar moved." Six years of being trusted with the boat, with the tiller, with the truth. And now there was a secret in the hold humming to itself, and the captain had decided—without saying it, without ever having to say it—who got to carry it and who got to be lied to for their own protection.

I went back aboard wet-handed and angry, and found Lior alone in the wheelhouse, chart spread under the lamp, making small pencil marks in the margins. Not course marks. I know course marks; I've drawn half of them. These were something else—depths, timings, little annotations in the captain's cramped hand along the deadwater reach above Kilometre 230.

"Planning our route home?" I asked.

"Always planning," Lior said, and covered the chart with a logbook, casual as a card trick.

Casual as a card trick. That's what did it. Not the secret itself—secrets keep boats afloat sometimes—but the *ease*. The captain had already decided something. Had probably decided it days ago, back when Voss first came up the gangplank hugging that crate like a coffin. And whatever it was, it wasn't *deliver the crate and get paid*, because you don't chart depths and timings for a delivery. You chart them for something else.

"You could tell me," I said. "Whatever it is. You could just say it."

Lior looked at me for a long moment, and I swear there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "Get some sleep, Tomas. Tomorrow's a long day."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in my bunk listening to the bilge pump cycle and the Kestrel's generator next door, two machines breathing out of rhythm, and I thought about the payday. Fifty thousand, split. Enough to buy out my mother's debt in Port Anselm. Enough to stop being a deckhand and start being something. I'd wanted this contract since the day it came over the wire, argued for it in the galley while Sefi called it a trap. Well, the trap had teeth, and I was lying in one, and the person who'd baited it wasn't Meridian.

Or maybe it was. Maybe Lior was playing some long game I couldn't see, protecting us all in that high-handed way of theirs, deciding our fates like channel markers. I turned it over and over until the shapes blurred. Hero or liar. Protector or owner. The trouble with a captain you trust completely is that you don't notice, until it matters, how much of your life they're steering.

Near midnight I gave up and went up on deck for air, and that's when I saw the light in the wheelhouse still burning, and Lior at the chart table again, and Voss standing beside them, and the two of them talking low and fast over the covered chart, and both of them looking up at once when the deck creaked under my foot.

Three faces' worth of silence. Mine made four.

"Tomas," Lior said calmly. Too calmly. "Couldn't sleep either?"

And downriver, the Kestrel's searchlight swept once across the water between us—slow, unhurried, like something checking on something it already owned.
