## 5. The Crate

I trust three things in this world: a torque wrench, a well-bored bearing, and the sound an engine makes when it's telling the truth. Everything else—people, promises, weather reports—is a machine waiting to fail, and I've spent forty-one years learning to hear the failure coming.

So when Dr. Imka Voss sat down across from me in the engine room at two in the morning and said, "I need to show you something," my first instinct was to check the bilge pump. Not because I didn't believe her. Because believing her was going to cost something, and I wanted to know what state my machinery was in before the world started lying to me again.

She'd been aboard four days. Four days of eating Tomas's terrible rice, sleeping in the forward hold, staring at walls like they owed her money. Lior had told us to leave her alone, and mostly we had, but I'd noticed things. The way she flinched when the depth sounder pinged. The way she wouldn't sit with her back to the river windows. The way she'd asked me—not Lior, not Tomas, me—how thick the Ada's hull plating was, and whether it was uniform along the keel line.

Nobody asks an engineer that question out of curiosity. You ask it because you're doing math you don't want to do out loud.

"I can't show the captain," she said. "Not yet. He'd make the smart choice, and the smart choice is the wrong one."

That, at least, I understood. Lior Oyelaran is the smartest person I've ever worked for, which is exactly why I've never fully trusted them. Smart people calculate. Machines don't calculate; machines *are* calculations, and they still surprise you. A smart person will hand you over to a gunboat if the arithmetic says so, and they'll hate themselves while they do it, and they'll do it anyway.

"So show me," I said.

Voss looked at the crate.

It sat in the corner of the engine room where she'd insisted we stow it, wedged between the spare propeller shaft and the wall, as far from the hull as geometry allowed. Steel, maybe eighty centimeters a side, sealed with some kind of resin around the lid seam that wasn't factory work. She'd sealed it herself, on the bank of the Sable, with whatever the camp had left after everything else went into the water. There were scratches on one face—deep ones, deliberate—and I'd assumed they were from the wrecked camp. They weren't. They were hers. Counting marks. She'd been counting days inside that thing's company, and the count stopped at nine.

"You opened it," I said. "At the camp."

"Once." Her voice did something strange, flattening out, like a signal dropping through interference. "To confirm the samples survived transit. Then I closed it."

"And?"

"And nothing came out. That's not the same as nothing being in there."

I want to be honest about this part: my skin did the thing skin does. But I'm an engineer, and engineers have a protocol for claims that violate the operating manual. You ask for data. So I asked for data.

Voss pulled a folded oilcloth packet from inside her jacket—she'd slept with it, apparently, which explained the smell—and unwrapped it on my workbench, next to my calipers and a gasket I'd been meaning to replace. Inside were slides. Microscope slides, maybe thirty of them, each labeled in handwriting that started neat and deteriorated into something frantic by the last few.

"Meridian Consolidated," she said, "has been running deep-core drilling operations in the upper Sable headwaters for eleven months. Officially, seismic survey. Unofficially—" she tapped a slide "—they cracked open an aquifer system that had been sealed off from the main channel since before the last glacial retreat. Isolated water. Isolated *everything* in the water."

I picked up a slide and held it to the work lamp. There was something on it. Something organic, desiccated now, but structured. Not algae. Not fungus. Not anything with a taxonomy I could have named, and I grew up dredging mussels out of the delta, I know river life down to its manners.

"The flood," Voss said, "wasn't weather. Or it wasn't only weather. Meridian breached the aquifer on a Tuesday. By Friday the headwaters rose two meters with no rainfall anywhere in the catchment. By the following week, the survey team's sonar was reading biomass in the deep channels. Moving biomass. Large."

"How large?"

She looked at me with the flat patience of someone who has already answered this question for people who are now dead. "The sonar maxes out at twelve meters. It returned solid contact at twelve meters."

I put the slide down very carefully, the way you set down a live wire.

"The others," I said. "Your team."

"Three went into the water during the first night. The river took them. We told ourselves it was the flood. Then the second boat went missing with two aboard, moored, engine cold, at anchor, in four meters of dead-calm water. Then Ndaya found tracks on the mudbank. Not animal tracks. Not boat tracks. *Drag* marks, leading out of the water and back in." Her hands were steady. That was the worst part. Whatever shaking was in her had gone somewhere deeper than her hands. "Nine days. I counted because counting kept me from swimming out to join them. Then your captain's boat came around the bend playing music, and I have never in my life been so grateful to hear anything so badly recorded."

Tomas's radio does play music badly. It was somehow good to know she'd heard it too.

"Why me?" I said. "Why tell the engineer and not the captain?"

"Because the captain owns this boat." She said it without accusation, just as fact. "And the company is going to come for the boat, or the crate, or both, and when they do, the captain is going to stand at the wheel and weigh lives against cargo, because that's what captains do. But you—" she pointed at my chest with a finger still stained faintly blue from some dye or other "—you don't own anything. You keep things alive. I watched you rebuild that generator with parts from three different boats. I need someone whose whole profession is deciding what gets saved and what gets sacrificed, and who doesn't flinch at the sacrifice."

"That's a terrible description of engineering."

"It's an accurate one."

I wiped my hands on my rag even though they were clean. It's a thinking gesture. Don't judge me.

Here's the thing about me: I have been burned by believers. My sister joined a river cult when we were nineteen and drowned proving the current loved her back. I have no patience for faith. But what Voss was showing me wasn't faith. It was instrumentation. Slides, sonar logs, core samples, a chain of evidence as clean as a freshly machined surface. And underneath all of it, one claim that couldn't be verified until it killed somebody—which, I'll grant, is also true of every bearing I've ever installed. You run the numbers, you respect the tolerances, and then you commit, knowing the material might have a flaw you can't see.

Meridian drilled. Meridian broke something sealed. Something sealed got out. Meridian wants the proof back in a steel box, and they want the box, and probably everyone who's seen inside it, at the bottom of the Sable.

"They'll sink us," I said. "Once they have the crate. That's the play. Recover property, lose witnesses."

"Now you understand why I couldn't tell the captain first."

"He'd already figured out half of it. He lied to those company men at the fueling station smoother than I've ever heard him lie about cargo manifests. He knows."

"He suspects. Suspecting and knowing are different animals, and he's trying to keep his crew on the side of the river where suspecting keeps you alive." Voss began rewrapping the slides with hands that finally, finally trembled. "But knowing is contagious, engineer. Once you know, you can't unknow, and every choice afterward is made by someone who knows. I'm sorry for that. Truly."

I sat with it for a long moment. The generator cycled. The river muttered against the hull, and for the first time since I'd signed onto the Ada, the muttering sounded less like water and more like traffic.

There was a version of tonight where I told her to take her slides and her monster and sleep in the hold and leave the adults to run the boat. There was a version where I woke Lior immediately and put the decision where it belonged, on the wheelhouse, with the person paid to make it. Both versions were safe. Both versions kept my hands clean.

Instead I got up, crossed the engine room, and moved the crate.

Not far. Forty centimeters, from the wall gap into the space behind the fresh-water tank, where the hull curves double-thick above the keel shoe and any breach would have to fight its way through the strongest plate on the boat before it reached the box. Old habit. When I can't fix a problem, I armor it.

Voss watched me do it and something in her face unclenched for the first time in four days.

"You believe me," she said.

"I believe the data," I said. "You're still on probation."

She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, crooked and exhausted, and I thought: nine days alone on a mudbank with drag marks, and she can still almost smile. Maybe people aren't entirely unreliable after all.

I went up to tell Lior. All of it—the drilling, the aquifer, the twelve-meter contact, the arithmetic of witnesses. The captain listened from the wheel with the river sliding past black under the running lights, and when I finished, Lior was quiet for exactly as long as it takes to make a bad calculation look like a good one.

Then the radio crackled, and a voice I didn't recognize gave our position, our name, and an instruction to prepare for boarding at dawn.

Lior's hand moved toward the throttle before the voice finished talking.

"Hold course," they said, to me, to the river, to nobody. "Sefi—get below. Whatever happens, the tank stays full and the crate stays cold."

I climbed down into the dark, put my palm flat against the double-thick plating, and felt the engine answer me the way engines always do: honestly.

Above me, the Ada turned toward the deadwater bend, and somewhere behind us, low and patient, a gunboat lit its running lights.

* * *

Dawn was going to be arithmetic. I started checking mine.
