## 6. Deadwater

The gunboat came out of the morning mist the way bad news always does—already there, already too close, as if it had been waiting behind the fog for the precise moment I'd run out of luck.

I'd been counting on more warning. A radio squawk, a wake on the water, something. Instead it simply resolved out of the gray: a low black hull with a Meridian Consolidated crest stenciled on the bow, twin diesels idling with that patient, expensive sound money makes when it's decided you're worth the trouble. It was maybe forty meters off our port beam and closing at a walking pace, which is how you know someone isn't in a hurry because they don't think you can escape.

"Captain," Sefi said from the engine hatch, not looking up from her gauges. She'd heard it before any of us. She always did. "Company boat. Two engines, shallow draft, and a deck crane."

"A deck crane," Tomas repeated, coming up the stairs two at a time. He had a coil of line over one shoulder and the particular brightness in his eyes he got whenever trouble looked like it might pay. "For cargo."

"For property," I said.

Voss was below decks with the crate. I'd put her there three days ago and she hadn't come up since except to eat standing at the galley counter like a woman who'd forgotten chairs existed. The crate sat in my hold under a false floor of spare planking and bilge rags, and every mile we'd run since the fueling station I'd been aware of it the way you're aware of a stone in your boot—not painful exactly, just present, just insisting on itself.

The gunboat's loudspeaker crackled. "River vessel *Ada*, this is Meridian survey support *Kestrel*. Reduce speed and hold your heading. We wish to board for an inspection."

Inspection. The word companies use when they mean recovery, and the word they use for recovery when they want to say it smiling.

I held my heading. For about four seconds. Then I eased the throttle back, because the alternative was letting them demonstrate what their bow wave could do to a working boat's hull, and I love the *Ada* too much to make her a lesson.

"That's it?" Tomas said. "We just stop?"

"We just stop," I said, "and we see what they actually want."

"They want the crate."

"Tomas." I kept my eyes forward. "They don't know we have a crate. As far as anyone knows, we're a resupply run that found nothing but drowned tents and came home early. That's the story. Everyone tell it the same way or don't tell it at all."

Sefi wiped her hands on a rag with great deliberation. "And Voss?"

"Voss is cargo," I said, and hated myself for it even as I said it, because I've spent thirty years on this river learning that people are never cargo, that the whole rotten arithmetic of the world runs on treating them like it, and here I was doing it in my own wheelhouse. But she nodded anyway, and went below, and I heard her settle herself next to the crate in the dark like a dog that wouldn't leave a body.

* * *

They boarded us at a bend in the river where the current died completely—the kind of deadwater pocket that forms where the flood had reshaped everything and left a slack green pool behind a point of silt. No current meant no drift, no drift meant no hurry, and the *Kestrel*'s captain clearly knew his business, because he brought us to rest in a place where the *Ada* couldn't go anywhere without making noise about it.

Four of them came across. Three were muscle in company-gray, and the fourth was a woman in a dry, clean field jacket with MERIDIAN CONSOLIDATED embroidered small and neat above the pocket, the way expensive things label themselves quietly. She introduced herself as Oyelaran-catcher number one, though what she actually said was, "Ade Coker, asset recovery," and offered a hand I didn't take.

"Captain Lior Oyelaran," I said. "We're running light. Nothing aboard worth recovering."

"I'm sure." She smiled like a form being filled out. "You answered the distress contract for Survey Team Nine. Where are the survivors?"

"One survivor. Dr. Imka Voss. She's below, sedated—she was in bad shape when we found her. Half-starved, fevered, talking to things that weren't there." This part was easy, because most of it was true. Lies built on true bones stand up better. "No others. We buried what we found."

"You buried them." Not a question. A little tick of her pen.

"The river does the digging. I do the courtesy."

"And equipment? Samples? The team's research materials?"

"Gone with the camp. Flood took the whole site. You can dive for it if you like—I marked it on my chart." I gave her coordinates that were accurate down to the last drowned tent, because accuracy is the best camouflage there is. Let them spend a week dragging the bottom of a flooded valley. Every day they spent there was a day we weren't being followed.

Coker wrote it all down. Then she looked up at me with an expression of mild, professional regret, the kind surgeons have before they say the word *unfortunately*.

"Captain. May I be direct?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

"Meridian knows the survey recovered samples before the camp was lost. We know the samples were packed in a sealed steel transport case, serial-numbered, roughly—" she consulted a card "—ninety centimeters long. And we know your boat departed the disaster site carrying weight in its hold that your draft marks say shouldn't be there." She tapped the *Ada*'s hull gently, almost affectionately. "You're sitting six centimeters deeper than a resupply run should. I count cargo for a living, Captain. It's the only interesting thing about me."

Behind me, very faintly, I heard Tomas shift his weight on the deck. Bad. He shifted again. Worse. Tomas has never in his life met a silence he couldn't improve into a catastrophe.

"Draft marks lie," I said. "We took on water in the rapids. Pumped half the Sable out of my bilge this week."

"Mm." She turned her head slightly. "Your deckhand looks nervous."

"He's twenty-two. Everything makes him nervous. Last month a heron made him nervous."

One of the muscle men laughed—a short bark, quickly smothered—and Coker didn't. She walked to the rail instead, looked down at the deadwater, all that still green glass, and said, conversationally:

"Here's what happens next, Captain. My employer offers a finder's fee for recovered assets. Generous. Enough to retire that boat of yours outright, buy her new engines, new hull plates, whatever she needs. Enough that you never run another cargo you don't choose." She let that sit. Money always wants to sit a while; it likes an audience. "Or, alternatively, my employer notes that the *Ada* is operating on a lapsed salvage license, that her insurance lapsed eight months ago, and that unlicensed vessels in restricted waters are subject to impoundment. With crew detained pending investigation. Indefinitely. Rivers are slow places, Captain. Investigations slower."

I felt it then, the thing I'd been dreading since the fueling station: the trap closing not around the crate but around the crew. They didn't need the crate today. They needed only to make keeping it cost more than any of us could pay.

"There's no crate aboard," I said.

"Then you have nothing to lose by letting my men search." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Refuse, and I'm required to assume the worst. Assume, specifically, that you're a thief. And thieves—" she spread her hands "—well. Impoundment. Detention. All of it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the moment your life splits in two: it doesn't feel like anything. There's no thunder. You're just standing on your own deck in the morning sun, smelling diesel and river mud, watching a company woman wait politely for you to decide what your people are worth, and the whole time some cold clerk in the back of your skull is running numbers.

I thought about Sefi, who'd signed on when no other engineer would take a chance on a woman with her record, and who kept the *Ada* alive with wire and stubbornness and love disguised as maintenance schedules. I thought about Tomas, who came aboard with nothing but arms and appetite and had become, somewhere along the miles, the little brother I never asked for and would now commit crimes for. I thought about Voss in the dark with her crate, holding the only proof that Meridian's drilling had cracked open something in the deep channels that the deep channels wanted very much to keep shut.

And I thought about the math. God help me, I thought about the math.

"Captain?" Coker said.

I looked at Tomas. He was watching me with total, unguarded faith—he genuinely believed I'd never give him up, never give any of them up, that the *Ada* was a country with laws of its own. That faith was the heaviest cargo I ever carried.

"My hold," I said. "Under the false floor. Forward section, port side."

The words came out level. Easy. That's the part I'll never forgive—not Coker, not Meridian, but how easily it came out. Thirty years of river taught me every sandbar and eddy, and apparently none of it taught me how to hesitate.

Tomas made a sound. Small. Like the heron thing, but worse.

"No," he said. "Cap, no, I can take—"

"Hold him," Coker said, and two of the muscle men took him by the arms, and he didn't fight, because he was watching me, waiting for me to say *just kidding, Tomas, stand down, we're all friends here.* And I couldn't say it, because the men holding him had guns slung loose and ready, and because Sefi had come up the stairs and gone very still, and because Voss was below with the truth, and truth doesn't float.

"He comes with us," Coker said. "Cooperation is appreciated, Captain. He'll be returned when the asset is verified intact. Consider him—" she smiled her form-smile "—collateral."

"If you hurt him—"

"You'll do what?" she asked, genuinely curious, and that was the worst question I've been asked in thirty years on the water, because I stood there on my own deck and had no answer.

They took Tomas across the gap between the boats on a gangplank, his arms still held, and he twisted once at the top to look back at me. I expected anger. What I saw was worse: he nodded. Once. Like he understood. Like he was telling me it was all right.

It was not all right. It was the least all right thing I have ever done, and I have done a number of candidates.

The *Kestrel* pulled away with my deckhand aboard and my secret in her hold, and the deadwater closed behind her hull without so much as a ripple of judgment. Still water. Dead water. Water that keeps everything and tells nothing.

Sefi stood beside me at the wheel, arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Sefi is a full-throated scream.

"I bought us time," I said finally. "That's all it was. Time."

"You sold him."

"I traded a location for a life. They'd have taken both otherwise." I gripped the wheel hard enough to hurt. "They'll verify the crate, and then they'll return him, and then—"

"And then they sink us with the evidence aboard," Sefi said flatly. "I read their hull, Captain. Deck crane. Salvage rigging. You don't bring a crane to pick up ninety centimeters of steel. You bring it to lift things *off* a sinking boat."

I stared at the wake the *Kestrel* had left, already smoothing itself out, already pretending none of us had ever been here.

Below us, faint through the deck plates, I heard Voss start to sing. Some survey-team song, half-remembered, tuneless, endless. The song of a woman guarding a box that had just stopped being ours to guard.

I turned the wheel toward the narrows, toward the channel Voss had warned us twice never to enter, and started planning how to steal my own cargo back.

"Get Tomas's kit," I told Sefi. "And the chart of Sandbar's Mood. If we're going to do something stupid, we'll do it on water I know better than they ever will."
