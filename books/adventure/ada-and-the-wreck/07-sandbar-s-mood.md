## 7. Sandbar's Mood

The gunboat was called the *Providence*, which told you everything you needed to know about Meridian Consolidated's sense of humor. She was forty feet of gray steel and bad intentions, twin diesels, a winch on the stern that could pull stumps or people, depending on the day's requirements. I'd been aboard her for six hours, most of them spent tied to a pipe in the engine room, and in that time I had learned three things.

One: the coffee was terrible. Two: the crew of the *Providence* did not talk to prisoners, which meant my charm was going entirely to waste. Three: sandbars have moods, and the one we were anchored behind had woken up cranky.

I could hear it through the hull. A low grinding whisper, like the river chewing with its mouth open. The channel Voss had warned about — the one she'd said no boat should run after dark, the one where the bottom rearranged itself nightly like a drunk rearranging furniture. The *Providence* had tucked herself into the deadwater bend for the night, smug as a tick, and the sandbar out there was already plotting against her.

Good, I thought. Join the club.

My wrists were zip-tied above my head to a stanchion, which sounds dramatic but is mostly just uncomfortable in a way that gets worse by degrees, like a sermon. The engine room smelled of diesel and hot metal and somebody's half-eaten lunch left on a toolbox, which offended me more than the captivity did, honestly. You don't leave food on tools. That's not a rule, it's a religion.

A man came down the ladder around what I guessed was midnight. Big, square-jawed, wearing a Meridian polo under his gun belt, because nothing says *we're just a resources company* like armed men in corporate colors. He didn't look at me. He talked to another man at the foot of the ladder, low, the way people talk when they think the walls are deaf.

Walls aren't deaf. Walls are the best listeners on any boat.

"Crane's rigged for morning," the big one said. "We take the crate off the *Ada* at first light, then—"

"I know the order."

"—then the captain wants the survey vessel scuttled in the main channel. With everything aboard. Manifests, cores, the doctor's notes if they're still on her. Make it look like she foundered. Flood season does half our work for us." A pause, and then, with the casual cruelty of a man discussing weather: "Crew goes ashore first. Mostly."

*Mostly.*

That word sat in my chest like a swallowed hook.

They climbed back up. The ladder rang. The engines idled their idle, patient as a held breath, and I hung there on my pipe doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Here's the thing about me: I'm not the smart one. That's Sefi, who talks to generators like they're shy animals and they answer. I'm not the brave one either — Lior holds that title and all its burdens, and wears it like it weighs nothing, which is its own kind of lie. And I am certainly not the mysterious one, because that's Voss, who looks at the river like it owes her an apology and might be right.

What I am is deckhand. Cargo handler. The guy who ties things down so they stay tied. For four years on the *Ada* I have been a passenger in my own life, going where the current and the captain decided, happy enough, paid enough, belonging enough — and I told myself that was the same as choosing.

It isn't. I learned that tonight, hanging from a pipe, listening to two men plan to sink my home with my family's names still wet on the paint.

*Mostly.*

Lior would come for me. That wasn't hope talking; that was just fact, like water being wet. Lior would come for me and it would cost them, because Meridian would be waiting, and Meridian had guns and a crane and a plan, and the *Ada* had love and a shallow draft. Love doesn't stop bullets. I've checked.

So somebody had to make sure the odds weren't suicide dressed up as rescue. And since nobody else was available, and since I was, apparently, done being cargo —

I started working on the zip tie.

* * *

You learn things as a deckhand that you don't realize you're learning. How rope behaves. How steel rusts from the inside out. How every zip tie has a ratchet head, and every ratchet head can be persuaded, given time and friction and something sharp. My something sharp was a burr of weld slag on the stanchion behind me, rough as a cat's tongue, and I sawed at the plastic for what felt like a season of floods.

The sandbar kept me company. Through the hull came its grinding song, that slow geological chewing, and I found I could set my rhythm to it. Saw on the grind. Rest on the silence. The river teaching me patience the only way rivers teach anything: by making you wait whether you want to or not.

Somewhere above me, footsteps crossed the deck. Somewhere aft, a sentry coughed. Twice during the night the engine room hatch opened and lamplight spilled down the ladder, and both times I went limp and stupid-looking, a man asleep on his feet, which is a skill every deckhand masters by year two.

The tie parted near dawn. My arms came down and screamed about it, blood roaring back into my hands like the river reclaiming a floodplain, and I stood there flexing my fingers and grinning like an idiot in the dark.

Then I stopped grinning, because now came the hard part.

The *Ada* was moored fifty yards upstream, under guard — two men aboard her, I'd counted through a porthole before they'd walked me below. The *Providence* carried six. Even if I freed myself, even if I got word to Lior, six against four, with guns, on open water, was not a fight anybody on my crew would win. We didn't win fights. We won departures. The *Ada*'s whole philosophy was *be somewhere else by the time trouble arrives*, and it had worked for years because Lior knew this river like other people know their own handwriting.

But Meridian didn't. That was the crack in them. They had charts, and charts are lies the river agrees to tell strangers.

And Voss — Voss had told us, back on the *Ada*, in that flat voice of hers, that the channel past the eastern sandbar was unsafe. Not dangerous. Unsafe. She'd said the flood had moved it, that the old channel was dead water over new ground, that the bottom out there turned over every night like something sleeping badly. Lior had nodded and steered wide. Meridian's skipper had looked at his chart, seen deep water marked, and anchored snug against the bar like it was a harbor wall.

Charts don't show what the flood woke up. But Voss knew. And I remembered.

I couldn't fight six men. But a sandbar with a mood could.

* * *

Getting off the *Providence* was insultingly easy, which should have worried me more than it did. The sentry on the stern had fallen into that 3 a.m. trance where a man guards everything and sees nothing, and the winch cable hung slack into the black water like an invitation. I went down it hand over hand, lowered myself until the river took my weight, and let go.

The water hit me like a fist full of ice. Flood-season runoff off the high country, carrying silt and cold and whatever else the flood had brought down from the places drilling had cracked open. I did not think about that last part. I though about swimming.

Fifty yards, upstream along the hull, hugging the shadow of the *Providence*'s flank. Then the open black of the bend, where the current ran lazy but the bottom ran hungry. Voss's voice in my head: *the ground turns over at night.* Fine. Let it turn. I'd been turned over plenty lately myself.

Halfway across, the river grabbed my ankle.

Not current — current pulls, this *held*. Something down there, soft and sucking, the fresh-deposited silt of the new bar closing around my boot like a mouth testing the flavor. I kicked and sank an inch. Kicked again and felt the whole bed shift beneath me, a vast slow shrug, the sandbar deciding whether I was worth the effort.

I've never prayed much. Deckhands keep their religion practical — lucky knots, offerings to the bilge pump, that sort of thing. But hanging there in the cold black with a riverbed chewing on my leg, I made the crew a promise with the serious part of my soul, the part that doesn't joke: get me across, and I will never again mistake riding along for living.

The sandbar sighed. My boot came free, taking most of the sole with it, and I swam the rest on pure spite.

I came up under the *Ada*'s stern, gasping, one shoe lighter and several years older. Above me, the deck I'd swabbed a thousand times, dark and quiet, two guards' cigarette embers glowing at the bow like a pair of patient eyes. Fifty yards away, the *Providence* slept, and beyond her the sandbar went on grinding its slow teeth.

I got a hand on the rudder iron. Pulled myself against the hull. Breathed.

Okay, Tomas, I told myself. You're a passenger no more. Now comes the part where you earn your berth.

I started climbing — toward the guards, toward the crate, toward whatever Lior was going to say when a dripping, half-shoed deckhand came over the rail with a plan involving a sandbar's mood and a very large amount of luck.

Toward morning, and the wreck we were going to make of it.
