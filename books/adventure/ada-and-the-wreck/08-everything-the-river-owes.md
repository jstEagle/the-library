## 8. Everything the River Owes

The storm found us an hour after Tomas didn't.

I'd been standing at the rail of the *Marta's Gift*—a borrowed barge with a borrowed engine and a borrowed future—watching the channel where I'd last seen my deckhand swim into water Voss had called unsafe, which was Voss's way of saying suicidal. The rain came down the way it does on the Sable in the wet season: not falling so much as arriving, all at once, like the sky had been holding it in its mouth for days and finally got tired.

"Captain." Sefi stood beside me, hood up, hands jammed in her pockets. She never shouted over weather. She just appeared at your elbow and spoke at the volume of a woman discussing engine tolerances, and somehow you heard every word. "If he made the far bank, he'll be looking for shelter. There's a fishing camp at Kiln Bend."

"I know."

"You know everything about this river."

"Not everything," I said. "Just enough to be dangerous."

She almost smiled. Sefi's smiles were like her spare parts—rare, hoarded, and only deployed when something essential depended on them.

We'd spent two days doing what fugitives do: moving slow by day, drifting dark by night, keeping off the channels where Meridian's gunboat—the *Consolidator*, because even their menace came with paperwork—might be patrolling. We had no boat worth the name, a scientist who flinched at deep water, a crate we couldn't touch sitting in Meridian's hold, and a hostage who had freed himself through the simple expedient of jumping into a river that ate people.

And yet, standing there in the rain, I felt something I hadn't felt since the Ada left the yard at Corvo Landing.

Hope. Ugly, unreasonable hope.

* * *

Tomas came back to us at Kiln Bend near midnight, or rather he was delivered back, half-drowned and furious about it, by a fisherman who'd pulled him out of a drift net and demanded payment in cigarettes and stories. He paid the cigarettes; I paid the stories; Voss paid in something better—she looked at him, really looked, and said, "You swam the Serpent's Throat," in a voice that made it sound like either an accusation or a knighthood.

He was blue-lipped and shaking, wrapped in every dry blanket we owned, and still he wouldn't sit down until he'd said his piece.

"They're going to sink the Ada," he said. "I heard them. Once they've got the crate secured at the depot, they're going to tow her out to Deadwater Bend and scuttle her with the rest of the 'contaminated evidence.' Captain—they're going to sink her."

The rain filled the silence that followed.

I want to tell you I took it like a captain. That I nodded gravely and began issuing orders. What actually happened is that I sat down on a crate of fish salt and put my face in my hands, because the Ada wasn't a boat. The Ada was eleven years of my life welded to a hull. She was the year Sefi rebuilt her engines from scrap while running a fever. She was Tomas learning to tie a bowline with frostbitten fingers. She was every debt I'd ever outrun and every dawn I'd ever loved, and some company man with a clipboard had decided she was *evidence*.

"Then we take her back," Sefi said.

Everyone turned. Sefi was examining her fingernails as though she'd suggested nothing more radical than changing the oil.

"With what?" I asked. "Our winning personalities?"

"With what we have." She ticked points off on her fingers, because Sefi always reduced catastrophe to inventory. "One gunboat, one shallow-draft river, one captain who knows every sandbar between here and the delta better than the men who charted them, one engineer who knows their boat class has a cooling intake that clogs in silty water, one deckhand who's been aboard the Consolidator for two days and can draw their watch rotations from memory, and one doctor who—" she paused, choosing her words with care, "—who knows what's actually in that crate, and what happens if it gets loose again."

Voss had gone very still by the stove. "You want to open it."

"I want Meridian to lose it," Sefi said. "Permanently. Publicly. In front of witnesses who aren't us."

* * *

We planned it the way families plan things—which is to say badly at first, then well, then with a ferocity that would have frightened strangers.

Here is what we had, laid out on the fisherman's table under a lamp that guttered every time the wind leaned on the shack:

Tomas drew the gunboat from memory on the back of a supply manifest—the *Consolidator*, forty feet of armored ego with a crew of six, twin screws, a draft too deep for the upper channels, and a captain named Ostrander who liked his routines the way Meridian liked its liabilities: contained. Watch changed at six and midnight. Crate stored aft, lashed under tarp. And crucially—a fact Tomas had risked his neck to learn—the Ada herself, under tow, riding light and empty, trailing behind them like a corpse they hadn't gotten around to burying.

"They tow her slow," Tomas said. "Four knots. They think nobody's crazy enough to come at them in this water."

"Nobody is," I agreed. "That's our whole advantage. We are the exception that proves the madness."

Sefi spread the charts—not the company charts, but mine, hand-annotated, twenty years of the Sable's moods recorded in pencil and stubbornness. "Here." Her finger landed on a bend thirty miles downriver, past the fueling station, past the last checkpoint. "Coffin Slough. You told me once it isn't on any chart."

"It isn't, because it doesn't exist most of the year. It's a sandbar wearing a river's clothing. In flood season, the main current cuts across it and it looks like open channel—deep enough to float a gunboat right up until it isn't. There's a hard pan under the silt. Anything drawing more than three feet hits it at speed and stays hit." I tapped the chart. "A gunboat doing eight knots in floodwater would go aground so hard they'd need a mining permit to get free."

"And you can find it in the dark? In a storm?"

"I could find it blindfolded, asleep, and insulted." I looked around the table. "The question isn't whether I can wreck them. The question is what we do in the ninety seconds after."

That was when Voss finally spoke from her corner, and everything shifted.

"The crate," she said. "You keep talking about taking it back. Don't." She came forward into the lamplight, and I saw that she'd slept, finally, and woken up harder. "Meridian drilled into the aquifer under the headwaters. What the flood released—it's in those samples, alive, in suspension. If that crate opens on land, in a depot, in a town, it doesn't stay contained. But in moving water, in flood conditions, diluted past recovery—" She met my eyes. "The river already did it once. Let the river finish it."

"So we don't steal the crate," Sefi said slowly. "We destroy it. At Coffin Slough. While they're aground."

"While they're busy," Tomas corrected, grinning through chattering teeth. "Because here's the beautiful part. When the Consolidator goes hard aground, they're going to have to cut the Ada loose to lighten and kedge off. They'll drop my girl's towline within ten minutes, guaranteed, because Ostrander follows procedure and procedure says save your own hull first. We don't fight them for the crate. We wait for them to hand us the Ada."

The table went quiet in the good way—the way it goes quiet when a plan stops being a gamble and starts being inevitable.

* * *

We argued about who went. Of course we did. That's what family is: people who argue about who gets to be in danger together.

Voss insisted on coming, because the crate was hers—her colleagues' deaths, her evidence, her responsibility—and none of us were fool enough to argue with that particular arithmetic. Sefi came because someone had to keep whatever we were floating in alive, and because, as she put it, "I don't trust machines to anyone else's hands, including yours, Captain." Tomas came because no force on earth or in Meridian's org chart was leaving him behind twice.

And me? I came because the sandbars knew my name, and it was time they earned their keep.

Our vessel was the fisherman's old work skiff, *Marta's Gift*, underpowered, overloaded, and ugly as an argument. Sefi coaxed her engine into sounding healthy; Tomas rigged running lights that showed a lawful tug's pattern; Voss sat amidships with the storm lantern dimmed, hands steady now, watching the water like it owed her an answer.

We cast off at four in the morning, ahead of the worst of the blow, because the worst of the blow was the point. No patrol runs in weather like this. No checkpoints on a night when the river itself seemed to be trying to drown the world.

Thirty miles of black water. Rain like gravel. Somewhere behind us, upstream, the Consolidator plodding downriver with my boat in tow and murder on its manifest.

I stood at the tiller and let my hands do what twenty years had taught them, reading the water the way other men read letters. A boil of current there—silt hanging heavy, bottom rising. A smooth glassy lane there—deceptive, treacherous, lying about its depth the way companies lie about their drilling reports. The river talked to me all night, and for the first time since I'd taken the contract that started all this, I understood what it was saying.

It wasn't saying *run*. It never had been. Every mile, every season, every flood—I'd thought I was restless, a man who couldn't put down roots. But the river had been teaching me the whole time: how to read danger, how to hold a crew, how to know exactly what a thing costs before you pay it.

"You're smiling," Sefi said, appearing at my shoulder.

"Am I?"

"You're smiling like a woman about to do something unforgivable."

"That," I said, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me all week."

Ahead, through the rain, the channel bent toward Coffin Slough, and the water there lay flat and innocent and full of lies. And somewhere in that darkness, running lights low, came the sound I'd been listening for all night: the deep, patient churn of twin screws, and beneath it, fainter, the familiar slap of a hull I'd know anywhere in the world.

My boat. Coming home to me the hard way.

I eased the throttle forward and turned the bow toward the lie in the water.
