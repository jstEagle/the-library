## 9. The Wreck

**Lior**

There is an old saying on the Sable: the river forgives everything except carelessness. I have spent forty years being careful. Tonight I intended to be the most careless man alive.

The storm came up from the southwest like a debt collector, and I welcomed it. Rain hammered the Ada's deck, lightning stitched the clouds together, and somewhere behind us the Meridian gunboat *Vigilant* ran her searchlight through the rain, patient as a bailiff. They had Tomas back two days—swum out of their hands half-drowned and half-frozen, delivered to us at the mouth of Deadwater Bend by nothing but stubbornness and Voss's knowledge of which channels lie about themselves. Now they wanted the crate, and they wanted it badly enough that Captain Reyes himself had come on the radio to make promises.

"Last chance, Oyelaran," he'd said, his voice smooth as silted water. "Deliver the property, walk away rich. Or we take it, and you walk away at all."

I had looked around my wheelhouse—at the chart I'd drawn by hand over twenty years, every sandbar marked in my own cramped script—and felt something settle in me that was not fear. It was more like the quiet a pilot feels when he finally knows what the river wants.

They thought I was running for the border. I let them think it. I turned the Ada up the Narrows, into the throat of the flood, where the water ran high and fast over the shoal every map called the Widow's Teeth because no one who'd found it had lived to rename it.

Sefi stood beside me at the wheel, arms crossed, watching me steer toward the one place on the Sable I'd sworn never to take her.

"You're sure," she said. Not a question. Sefi didn't ask questions when she already knew the answer; she just gave you the chance to say it out loud.

"I've run this bend a hundred times in my head," I said. "High water covers the Teeth by four feet. The gunboat draws six. She'll follow us because she can—on paper."

"And us?"

"We draw five and a quarter." I touched the wheel like a man touching a face. "And I know exactly where the top bar lies."

* * *

**Tomas**

I was on the bow with the lifeboat, and I will tell you honestly: I didn't want to do it. Not the plan—all right, yes, the plan too—but mostly I didn't want to be the one who lived. That sounds stupid now. It didn't sound stupid then, with the rain coming down like the sky was trying to wash the whole river away and Lior standing calm at the wheel, steering us straight at our own funeral.

Voss came out of the cabin with the crate strapped to a raft of empty fuel drums, and she looked worse than ever—hollow-eyed, soaked, gripping that steel box like it was the last honest thing in the world.

"It has to be opened in the water," she shouted over the storm. "Not burned—the company burns evidence all the time. Flooded. Let the river take it apart. Let the river testify."

Sefi had rigged the latch mechanism with a pressure release—of course she had; give that woman a problem and a wrench and she comes back smiling. All it needed was for the crate to go under and stay under, and the current would do the rest.

Lightning flashed, and in its light I saw the gunboat behind us, big and white and certain, eating up the distance. Captain Reyes wasn't chasing us anymore. He was herding us. He thought we were trapped between his boat and the Narrows.

He was almost right.

* * *

**Lior**

I felt the shoal before I saw it—that change in the wheel's voice, the way the Ada began to speak to me through her timbers, telling me what lay beneath. Forty years of conversation, and tonight would be the last of it.

"Sefi," I said. "When I give the word, cut power. Full astern on the port only."

She looked at me. "You're going to swing her broadside."

"I'm going to set her down gentle as I can. On the bar. Across the channel." I swallowed something that tasted like twenty years. "She'll block the Narrows. Nothing bigger than a canoe gets past her until the water drops."

"And after?"

"After, she belongs to the river. Like everything else."

Behind us the searchlight found us, pinned us, and Reyes's voice came over the loudhailer, distorted by wind: "Oyelaran! Hard to port or we fire!"

"They're going to fire anyway," Sefi said.

"Yes. But they'll wait until they're close enough to be sure. And by then—" I put the wheel hard over. "By then it won't matter."

* * *

**Tomas**

They fired. Of course they fired. A rifle cracked from the gunboat's bow and something sparked off the Ada's rail a hand's width from my head, and I stopped feeling sorry for myself real quick.

Lior swung us across the channel like a dancer, and I swear the old woman knew. She heeled, groaned, and then the Widow's Teeth reached up and took her—not with a crash but with a long, terrible grinding sigh, like a giant settling into bed. The deck tilted. The river came over the starboard rail all at once, cold as a confession.

"Now!" Lior roared, and Sefi cut the engines, and the Ada settled onto the bar with her hull across the channel, broadside to the flood, a wall of wreck where a clear passage used to be.

The gunboat never even slowed. I heard her engines surge—Reyes trying to bull past us before we could get clear—and then I heard the sound I will hear for the rest of my life: steel meeting stone at speed. The *Vigilant* hit the Teeth at full throttle, and the sound was like the end of the world conducted by an amateur.

We were already in the lifeboat. Voss had the crate on the drum-raft, tethered alongside, and Sefi was hauling us toward the flooded shallows while the gunboat tore herself open fifty yards downstream, lights dying, men shouting, the river pouring into her like she owed it money.

But the crate—it was dragging. The current had the raft, and the raft had the line, and the line was pulling Voss halfway out of the boat, and then it pulled her out.

* * *

**Lior**

From the tilting deck of my own ship I watched Imka Voss go into the flood after the crate, and I understood that some people cannot let a thing go even when letting go is the only sane act left. She went under. Came up. Went under again, dragged by the drums, and the lifeboat was too far, and Tomas was already diving—

No. Tomas was holding Sefi back, both of them screaming at each other over the storm, and it took me a moment to understand why: Sefi had gone in first. My engineer, my careful, machine-loving Sefi, had gone off the lifeboat into black floodwater after a woman she'd known three weeks, because that is what family does when it finally decides it is one.

The Ada shuddered beneath me. Water was in the engine room now; I could feel her dying through the soles of my boots. There was nothing left to save aboard except the wheel, so I held it, because a captain holds the wheel.

Then the river did the thing rivers do. A wave off the broken gunboat—a backwash, a rolling hump of displaced water—came upstream against the current and took Voss and Sefi both, rolled them like leaves, and slammed them against the Ada's exposed hull.

I went down the ladder faster than I have moved in thirty years, dropped flat on the sloping deck, and caught Sefi's wrist as the water tried to peel her away. She was coughing, clawing, alive. Voss had the crate's tether wrapped twice around her forearm and was going under with it, and the drums were gone, torn loose, and the crate was sinking with her tied to it.

"Tomas!" I bellowed. "The axe!"

He threw it. I don't know how he threw anything accurately in that chaos, but the hatchet came spinning out of the dark and I caught it by the handle, and then I had the worst choice of my life laid out simple as arithmetic: cut the line and lose the evidence, or hold on and lose the woman.

I cut the line.

The crate went down into the deep channel with all its terrible proof sealed inside, and Voss came up gasping, and I hauled her onto the deck beside Sefi, and the three of us lay there on the slanted planks of my sinking ship while the rain came down and the gunboat broke apart downstream and the river carried the crate away into the dark where no company dredge would ever find it.

Wait. No. That's not how it happened. Let me tell it true, because Voss made me promise to tell it true.

I raised the axe. And Voss screamed—not for herself. "The latches! Sefi's latches—they open underwater!" And I understood, and I didn't cut. I let the line run through my bleeding hands, hand over hand, paying it out slow, guiding the crate down along the hull to the deep water beside the wreck, and below me, in the black, I heard it—a soft metallic clunk, then another, as the pressure releases tripped and the seals gave way. The crate opened itself in the flood, and whatever was inside—samples, cultures, the living proof of what Meridian had woken up with its drilling—went out into the Sable in every direction, diluted past recovery, destroyed by the very river they'd poisoned.

Let the river testify. It testified.

* * *

**Tomas**

I got the lifeboat alongside the wreck and we loaded them aboard—Lior last, always last, standing on the Ada's rail like a man seeing a friend off at a station. The old boat was down to the main deck now, water in the cabin, the wheelhouse window glowing one last time from the lamp Sefi had left burning inside. Deliberate, I think. A light left on.

"She was a good boat," I said, because I couldn't say anything else.

"She was the best boat," Lior said. "That's why she could do this."

We pushed off. Behind us the Ada settled by degrees, taking the shoal forever, and the gunboat's survivors were clinging to her broken spine in the rain, and none of them were looking at us, because they were busy learning what the Sable thought of Meridian Consolidated.

We ran the lifeboat down the flooded side-channel Voss had mapped, the one that lied to strangers, and by dawn the storm had spent itself and the river ran brown and fast and clean of everything—no crate, no gunboat, no Ada. Just water, going about its business.

Sefi sat shivering under a tarp with Voss's head on her shoulder. Lior held the tiller and watched the banks slide past, and I saw my captain's eyes shining and pretended I hadn't, because some things you don't mention.

"So," I said, into the silence. "Anybody else suddenly unemployed?"

Lior laughed—a real one, cracked and wet at the edges. "Ashore," they said. "I hear they have rivers there too."

And that was when we saw it, rounding the bend ahead: a fueling dock, a town waking up, and tied at the dock, flying no colors at all, a second Meridian boat. Smaller. Faster. Already casting off.

Already coming for us.
