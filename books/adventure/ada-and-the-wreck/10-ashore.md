## 10. Ashore

The trouble with dry land, I'd always said, is that it doesn't move. You stand on it and it just sits there under your feet like it's got nowhere better to be, and after thirty years of deck that shifts and breathes beneath you, standing still feels less like rest and more like being beached. Which is what I was. Beached. A captain without a boat is a funny kind of animal—still has all the instincts, none of the means.

I'd taken a room above the chandlery in Marrow's Bend, which is the sort of town that exists because the river decided to slow down there and couldn't be bothered to speed up again. Three streets, two churches that hated each other on principle, one chandlery, and a dock long enough for six boats if nobody argued about it. The room had a window facing the water, because I'd learned nothing in fifty years and still paid extra to watch something I'd lost.

The Ada was at the bottom of the Sable by then, four months gone, resting on the shoal I'd put her on with my own hands. They say you can see her mast at low water when the light comes sideways, a black finger pointing at the sky, accusing me specifically. I tried not to look. I looked every day.

* * *

Meridian's cover-up didn't collapse so much as quietly rot through, the way their drilling platforms always should have. Voss had been smart about it—smarter than any of us gave her credit for in those first mad days when she was guarding a steel crate with a boat hook and her teeth. She hadn't put everything in the crate. The crate was the decoy, the thing Meridian would chase and sink and feel victorious about, while the real evidence went out in pieces: three copies of the survey data mailed from three different towns to three different newspapers, a university, and a government hydrology office staffed, as it turned out, by one honest man.

The papers ran it small at first. *Meridian Consolidated Faces Questions Over Headwaters Survey.* Then not small. A junior engineer testified. Then a senior one. Then the drilling logs surfaced, and it turned out the thing in the deep channels—the thing that came up with the flood and took six scientists and nearly took us—had a birth certificate, and Meridian had signed it.

They never named the Ada. That was the strange mercy of it. In all the hearings and the print, our boat was "a civilian vessel contracted for resupply," and we were footnotes, untraceable, scattered upriver and down like seed. Tomas went north to his cousin's. Sefi took a job maintaining locks, of all things, machines that sit still and lift boats, which she claimed was either penance or promotion depending on the day. Voss went back to teaching, because that's what she'd been before Meridian turned her into a witness. And I—

I sat above a chandlery watching a river that no longer needed me, drinking coffee that tasted like an apology, telling myself this was what I'd wanted. Freedom. No debts, no schedule, no cargo manifest. Just miles behind me and none ahead.

It turns out miles were never the point. It took me four months and a Tuesday to figure that out.

* * *

The Tuesday started like any other, gray and damp, the river wearing its winter color of old rope. I was downstairs arguing with Odile the chandlery owner about whether I owed her for the window or the window came with the room, a debate we'd been having amicably since October, when the bell over the door rang and a voice I knew said:

"He's cheating you, missus. He's been cheating everyone since before I met him."

Tomas stood in the doorway dripping rain, broader than I remembered, hair cut short, looking like a man who'd slept indoors regularly and wasn't sure he liked it. Behind him came Sefi, carrying a toolbox out of pure habit, and behind Sefi, hesitating on the threshold like the floor might be a sandbar of uncertain depth, came Dr. Imka Voss.

"You're a hard man to find," Tomas said, "for a man who hasn't moved."

"I'm easy to find," I said. "I'm just not worth finding."

"Both things can be true." He crossed the room and shook my hand like we were closing a deal, which in a way we were, though neither of us knew the terms yet.

Sefi set the toolbox down with a clack that meant business. "Tomas is building a boat."

"Tomas is building a *wreck*," Tomas corrected. "A beautiful wreck. It's taking forever because Sefi keeps inspecting it."

"It's taking forever because you don't know what a keel is for."

"I know what a keel is for. It's for arguing about."

Voss hadn't spoken yet. She was looking at me the way she'd looked at the river in those first hours aboard, when she was half-mad and we were half-afraid of her—like she was reading depths. "I'm teaching now," she said finally. "At the school here. Biology, some geography. The children ask excellent questions. They want to know what's in the river." A pause. "I tell them the truth. Most of it."

"And they sleep fine?"

"Their parents don't. But that's not my department."

Odile, who had been following this exchange like a spectator at a tennis match played entirely over her head, cleared her throat and said, "Are these people staying? Because rooms are upstairs and gossip is free but coffee isn't."

"They're staying," I heard myself say, and then wondered who had said it, because it certainly wasn't the man who'd spent four months perfecting the art of sitting still.

* * *

We fell into it the way water finds a channel—not quickly, not all at once, but inevitably, carving as it goes. Tomas's boatyard was a rented slip two streets over where he was building a forty-foot stern-wheeler from scratch, oak and ironbark, and every morning I found reasons to walk past it, and every morning he found reasons to hand me a tool and complain about my technique. Sefi came weekends, ostensibly to inspect, actually to build, and the engine she was assembling in the corner of the yard was, by her own admission, "overkill for a riverboat and exactly right for ours."

Ours. Nobody said it out loud for weeks. We just kept building it.

Voss taught her classes and came down to the yard in the late afternoons, and somewhere in there she and I developed a ritual: she'd bring tea, I'd pretend to work, and we'd talk about the Ada without talking about the Ada. How the wheel had pulled left in a current. How Tomas used to sing off-key on night watches until the whole crew sang off-key just to drown him out. How Sefi once fixed a fuel pump with a hairpin and sheer contempt for the manufacturer.

"Do you regret it?" she asked me one evening, meaning the boat, meaning the shoal, meaning all of it. "You loved her more than anything afloat."

"I did love her," I said. "That was the problem. I loved her instead of—" I gestured at the yard, at the sound of Tomas swearing at a plank, at Sefi's engine ticking over like a contented animal. "Turns out a boat is just the shape belonging takes when you haven't found the people yet."

Voss smiled into her tea. "You've become a philosopher."

"I've become beached. Same thing."

* * *

The launch was set for spring, when the river came up soft and green and forgiving. Tomas named her before anyone could stop him—*Ada II*, which Sefi called unoriginal and Tomas called tradition and I called, when I thought no one was listening, exactly right.

On the morning of it, the whole town came down to the dock, because a new boat in Marrow's Bend is Christmas, an election, and a scandal all at once. Odile closed the chandlery. Voss brought her students, who swarmed the rails asking the excellent questions children ask—*does it float, how fast, can we live on it*—and I answered each one honestly except the last, which I answered with a smile.

Sefi's engine caught on the second try. Tomas took the wheel first, then surrendered it, grinning, to the only person present with any actual credentials. I put my hands on wood that smelled of sawdust and linseed instead of sixty years of river, and found that it fit them anyway.

We ran down to the bend and back, slow, showing off to nobody, everybody waving anyway. And standing at the wheel with my crew around me—my crew, still, after everything, still—I felt the old ache, the one measured in miles, go quiet for the first time in my life. Not because the miles stopped. Because I finally understood they'd never been the cargo.

Belonging was never the river. It was the people all along. The river was just where we happened to find each other, and it would have been a road, a mine, a city street, if that's what the flood had given us.

Tomas leaned against the rail beside me. "So," he said, in the tone of a man about to ruin a perfectly good ending. "Meridian's assets are being sold off in bankruptcy. There's a gunboat in the lot. Barely used. One careful owner."

I looked at him. He grinned at me.

"Sefi already drew up a refit plan," he said. "Voss says she'll come as far as the headwaters. Says there's science to do. Says somebody ought to check on the shoal."

I looked downriver, toward the Sable, toward the wreck I'd made and the life I'd built on top of it, and felt the old restlessness stir—but lighter now, a tide instead of a wound.

"Well," I said, and pushed the wheel over. "Somebody should."
