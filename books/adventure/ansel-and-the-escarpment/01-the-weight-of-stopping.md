## 1. The Weight of Stopping

The salt flats at dawn were the color of a dead man's eye, and Isandro Marchetti had been looking at them long enough to know every mood they owned. Three days now. Three days since the thief had walked out of the upriver station with something under her coat and no horse worth the name, and three days since Isandro had swung into the saddle after her with the particular dread of a man who suspects he is chasing the wrong thing in the right direction.

He stopped his mare where the trail crossed the old survey line—a seam of darker crust running straight as a ruled page toward the mountains—and crouched to read the ground. Boot prints, small, deliberate. She wasn't hurrying anymore. That was the thing that had been working at him since yesterday: on the first day she had run like a woman with hounds behind her, her tracks long-strided, half-skidding on the salt pan. By the second day she had settled into a walk. And this morning she had stopped here, at the survey line, and stood for a while—both feet planted, weight even, facing the range—before walking on.

Like she was waiting for him to catch up.

"Damn fool," he said aloud, because the flats took sound and gave nothing back, and a man could go strange out here if he didn't hear his own voice now and then.

His radio had been silent since noon yesterday. Not dead—the needle still twitched when he keyed it, the little green eye still glowed—but empty. The upriver band, the one frequency that carried past the escarpment's shoulder, had gone quiet as a held breath. Dispatch answered nothing. He'd tried the low band, the relay at Corriente Wells, even the old emergency channel the rangers weren't supposed to use anymore. Static, all of it, static with a texture he didn't like: too clean, too even, like static that had been manufactured by someone who wanted it that way.

He told himself it was sunspots. He told himself it was the storm front stacking up over the range, all that iron-gray cloud dragging its belly along the peaks. He was good at telling himself things. It was part of the job. A ranger who couldn't lie to himself about the desert wouldn't last a season.

What he couldn't lie to himself about was the water.

* * *

He'd found the first cache at the end of day one, at a wind-scoured hollow where the thief had evidently camped. He'd expected signs of panic—scattered fire rings, abandoned gear, the leavings of someone running blind. Instead: a neat camp, cold before full dark, ash buried properly like a ranger would bury it. And beside the trail, set on a flat stone where no one could miss it, a tin cup with water in it. Clean water, drawn from somewhere, because there was no well within twenty miles of that hollow.

He'd stood over it a long time. Then he'd poured it out, because you didn't drink what a thief left you, and ridden on.

The second cache had been at midday yesterday, tucked in the shade of an overturned gypsum block: two cups this time, and a strip of dried apricot laid across the rim of one like a garnish. Like an offering. Like hospitality. He'd sat his horse and looked at it until the wind changed and the salt dust came hissing across the pan, and then he'd drunk from his own canteen, deliberately, watching the cups as he did it, and ridden on.

This morning there would be a third. He knew it the way you know weather coming. And the terrible thing, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, was that some tired animal part of him was grateful.

That was how they got you, out here. Not with guns. With cups of water.

* * *

By late morning the heat had come up off the flats in visible sheets, and the mountains ahead had gone soft and blue, the way distance lies. The escarpment. Everyone out here oriented by it the way sailors orient by a star—the great wall of rock lifting eight thousand feet out of the desert floor, snow on its crown nine months of the year, holding the headwaters of every river that kept the valley alive. The concession company's territory. The company's roads, the company's pumps, the company's fences, all stitched up the lower slopes like sutures in a wound.

Isandro had a warrant in his breast pocket, folded small, and he'd read it so many times he could recite it in the dark. *Ansel Viret, wanted for theft of company property from the upriver station.* That was all the paper said. Theft of property. No description of the property, which was itself unusual—he'd noticed that the first night, reading it by firelight. Warrants named what was stolen. Horses, payroll, rifles. This one just said *property*, the way you'd describe a body you didn't want to look at.

And the reward notice that had come with it, wired down from the company office instead of the magistrate's court—that was unusual too. Companies didn't post rewards. Companies hired rangers and let the law do the work. Unless the law was slow, or squeamish, or asking questions.

He turned his mare south along the survey line and let her pick her way through the crust, which broke under hoof with a sound like crockery. Somewhere behind him, thirty miles back, was the upriver station where he'd spent four years checking gauges and settling disputes and writing reports nobody read. Somewhere ahead was a thief who walked like she had all the time in the world and carried something worth more than the whole station put together.

Three days of pursuit cost money he didn't have. His contract with the district ran out at season's end—six weeks—and if the passes closed before he brought her back, the warrant went cold and the company found another ranger, a hungrier one. Stopping meant all of that. Stopping meant the flat gray arithmetic of failure. So he rode, because riding was easier than thinking, and the desert rewarded motion the way it rewarded everything else: barely, and not for long.

* * *

He found the third camp an hour before dusk, in the lee of a ridge where the salt gave way to gravel and the first stunted greasewood grew. The camp was the same as the others—cold, buried fire, tidy as a chapel—and the cup was waiting on the stone.

But this time there was a note under it.

A scrap of paper, weighted with a pebble, written in a hand that slanted hard to the right like it was being pushed by wind:

*You're three days behind and losing ground. There's a spring two miles east of the old corral—you've been rationing, I can tell by your camps. Take the water. I'm not your enemy.*

He read it twice. Then he looked east, at the low sun bleeding into the haze, and thought about the old corral—a ruin from the first survey days, decades back, a place so far off any route a sensible fugitive would take that using it meant either ignorance or invitation.

She was steering him. That was the plain truth of it. Every cache, every note, every neat cold camp laid out like breadcrumbs—not carelessness, not arrogance. Navigation. She was leading him somewhere, or leading him along, and he was following, and the worst part was that his badge felt lighter every mile, not heavier, as though the metal itself understood something he hadn't admitted yet.

He should turn back. He knew that too. Radio dead, warrant thin, quarry taunting him across forty miles of salt—any competent officer would call it and regroup at Corriente Wells. Any competent officer would report the jammed frequency, request the wire, wait for instructions.

But dispatch wasn't answering. And the storm over the escarpment was building the way storms did before early snow. And somewhere above the snowline, in country the company maps marked with a single word—*STERILE*, as if the mountain itself had been declared legally dead—there was supposed to be nothing at all.

Nothing but the expedition that went up two autumns ago and never came down. Eleven men, written off in a single paragraph of the company's annual report: *lost to weather, regrettable, operations resumed in spring.* He'd read that paragraph a dozen times over the years. He'd never once asked why the company hadn't sent a recovery party. Dead men on a mountain made poor publicity; live ones made worse.

He filled the cup. He drank. The water was cold and mineral-bright and better than anything he'd tasted in a year, and he hated himself a little for how good it was.

Then he built his own fire, small and smokeless, and sat with the note in his hand while the dark came down over the flats and the first stars pricked through above the black shoulder of the range.

"You're not my enemy," he said to the fire. "Then what are you?"

The wind moved through the greasewood, and far off—or maybe only in his head, maybe only in the place where three days of silence had started making sounds—the radio in his saddlebag crackled once, softly, like a throat clearing before words.

He lunged for it, thumbed the key. Static. But beneath the static, faint as a pulse under ice, something else: a voice, ragged, repeating. Numbers. Coordinates. And a phrase, cut clean off mid-word, that dropped his stomach through the salt and the stone and the whole thin floor of the world:

*"—still alive up here, do you copy, we are still—"*

Then nothing. The band sealed shut again, smooth as water closing over a stone.

Isandro sat very still in the firelight, the radio heavy in his hands, and understood that the desert had been listening all along—and that whatever was speaking from above the snowline had just used the same broken silence the thief had opened, and that neither of them, the thief or the voice, had ever been running away at all.

They were both running toward him.
