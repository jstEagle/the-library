## 2. What Ansel Took

Ansel heard the ranger's horse before she saw him—three days of being hunted had tuned her ears to the particular rhythm of a man who would not quit. Not the loose, water-hoarding walk of a drover, not the scattered percussion of a scout checking sign. This was a metronome. A tired, stubborn, unforgiving metronome, and it was coming up the arroyo toward the dry well where she'd meant to sleep.

She had maybe four minutes. She'd had four minutes for three days now, and she was so tired the world had begun to feel like something happening to another woman, someone she watched from a great height with a kind of detached pity.

The well sat in a fold of badlands the old maps called *Los Ojos Secos*—the dry eyes. Two stone lips of it, ringed with a century of rope-grooves worn into the lip like scars on a wrist. Her father had drunk from this well as a boy, back when it still gave water. Now it held only shadow and the smell of cold mineral dust, and Ansel crouched behind its windward wall with her brother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, feeling its weight the way you feel a broken rib: constantly, and only worse when you moved.

She could run. There was still a little dark left, and the badlands were kind to people who knew them. But she had been running since Cerrito Blanco, and her horse was down to walking pace no matter how she asked, and some animal part of her—the part that had kept her alive through two winters of company country—was done pretending the running led anywhere but here.

So instead of running, she did something that surprised even her.

She built a fire.

Small. A fist of greasewood in the lee of the well, screened by stones, burning low and blue-flamed because greasewood always burned a little blue at the heart, like it was ashamed of itself. She set her dented pot on it. Melted snow from her last cache. Dropped in the last of the dried chiltepín and a strip of whatever the jerky had become after three days of saddle-heat. And when the metronome in the dark finally resolved into a shape—a rider, hat low, rifle slung forward across his chest the way rangers carried them when they expected trouble but hadn't decided whose—she said, without turning around:

"There's enough for two. If you're going to arrest me, you can do it fed."

* * *

He didn't answer right away. He dismounted at the edge of the firelight, which told her he was careful, and he stood there a moment with his hand resting on his horse's neck, which told her he was kind, or wanted to be, and both of those things together made her angrier than a cruel man would have.

"You've been leaving water," he said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "Every camp. A full skin, set out where I'd find it."

"I have."

"Three days I've been chasing you and you've been watering me." He came closer, boots crunching on the alkali crust, and she heard him stop just outside arm's reach, the way men stopped when they weren't sure yet whether they were hunting an animal or meeting a person. "Why?"

"Because you'd have died otherwise." She turned then, finally, and looked at him across the fire. He was younger than his voice. Wind-scoured, gray-eyed, with the particular hollowed look of a man who had been riding on anger and duty and very little food. "That trail crosses forty miles of nothing. The company maps mark three springs. Two of them are dry—they've been dry for six years, ever since the concession company capped them to force the ranchers onto their water wagons at their prices. The third one they poisoned with tailings runoff and never reported. You'd have trusted your map, and your map is a lie, and you'd be dead in a wash somewhere with your badge going green in the sun. So yes. I watered you. I needed you alive long enough to hear this."

She lifted the oilcloth bundle out of her coat and held it between them, over the fire, so the light caught it.

"This is what I took."

His hand went to his belt—not to the gun, she noticed, but to the tin star, an unconscious gesture, a man touching the thing that defined him. "I don't care what it is. It isn't yours."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't mine. It's my brother's. It's eleven other men's. It belongs to every rancher between here and the escarpment who signed a water agreement written in a language designed to be unreadable." She unwrapped it. The ledger was fat, swollen with years of damp and heat, its spine cracked, its corners soft as old bread. "Concession ledger. Upriver Water and Land Company, season of the big survey. Every entry in it is either forged or a confession, depending on how honest you want to be about arithmetic."

"Put it down, miss. Slow."

"It records the actual flows against the claimed flows." She kept talking because if she stopped she would start shaking; she could feel the shaking waiting behind her teeth like a dog behind a door. "They filed for nine thousand acre-feet. They drew twenty-two thousand. They sold the difference back to the very communities whose rights they'd falsified, and when the land board sent an inspector, they bought him a house. That's page sixty-one. There's a house on page sixty-one, ranger. With a porch."

"Ma'am—"

"And then—" and here her voice did break, one clean crack down the middle, and she hated it, hated herself for it, and kept going anyway because the grief deserved to be heard even when it was ugly—"and then there are the last forty pages. The expedition. Twelve men contracted to survey the high escarpment routes, winter permits pending, provisioned for six weeks. The company wrote them off as lost to weather in the second month. Closed the file. Paid out the insurance. Buried them on paper before they were even dead."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a coyote made a sound like a question nobody answered.

"They weren't lost," Ansel said. "Were they."

It wasn't really a question, and the way the ranger's face changed—nothing dramatic, just a settling, like silt in disturbed water—told her everything before he said a word.

"I don't know anything about any expedition," he said.

"You know what your office knows. Your office knows what the company tells it. That's not ignorance, ranger, that's employment." She hugged the ledger back against her chest. "My brother Tomás was the junior surveyor on that crew. Twenty-four years old. He used to write me letters from every camp—terrible letters, spelling all wrong, drawings of mules in the margins. Then the letters stopped, and the company sent a man in a good coat to our door who said *lost to weather* the way you'd say *lost in transit*, like my brother was a crate of hardware. My mother died inside a year. Not officially of grief. Officially of her heart. But hearts stop for reasons, ranger, and mine has been stopping slowly ever since, and it will not stop fully until this book sees daylight."

"So you stole it."

"I *rescued* it." She stood, and the weariness fell off her the way dust falls off a shaken blanket, and for a moment she felt tall, felt lit, felt like the person Tomás had believed she was. "It sat in the company archive in Cerrito Blanco under a lock the clerk opens for anyone with a company pin. Eleven years. Eleven years it sat there while the men who wrote it got promoted, and the families got nothing, and the wells went dry, and the desert got quiet in that particular way deserts get quiet when everyone who might complain has been bought or buried. Nobody was ever going to read it. Nobody was ever going to *miss* it. I read it in one night and I have not slept properly since, and I would do it again, and again, and again."

The ranger looked at the ledger for a long time. The firelight moved on his face and she watched him try to make it simple—thief, property, oath—and watched him fail, and something in her that had been braced for three days loosened half a degree.

"That's a story," he said at last, quietly. "A good one. People in my line hear a lot of good stories."

"It's not a story. It's arithmetic. Check the arithmetic."

"The company will say you forged it."

"The company will say whatever its lawyers tell it to say. That's why I'm not taking it to the company's courts." She stepped around the fire, close enough now that she could see the salt sweat dried white on his collar, and held his gaze. "I'm taking it upriver. To the land board session at season's end, before the renewal filings close. In front of the valley. In front of the widows. In front of God and everybody, ranger, and if you want to arrest me, you'll have to carry me and this book both, and explain to twelve families why the truth weighed more than your oath."

He didn't move. His jaw worked once, twice, like a man chewing on something too large to swallow.

"You understand," he said finally, "that I've spent three days and most of my water catching you. That I have to bring you in. That's the job. That's the whole job."

"I understand the job." Her voice gentled despite herself; she knew what it cost to say what he'd just said, because she'd had oaths of her own once, to family, to ground, to the memory of a boy with mules drawn in his letter margins. "I had a job too. Keeping my mother alive. I failed at it. The difference between us, ranger, is that I stopped pretending my failure was somebody else's success."

Something flickered across his face—wounded, maybe, or recognized—and before either of them could find the next word, his hip radio crackled.

Not static. Not the usual dry hiss of the upriver band, which everyone knew was dead air, had been dead air for years, jammed or abandoned or both. This was a voice. Thin, shredded by distance and interference, barely human—but a voice, saying words that dropped into the night like stones into a well:

*"—anyone—this is Vásquez, escarpment survey—we are—we are still—"*

And then silence, total and absolute, as though the desert itself had clapped a hand over the mouth of the world.

Ansel looked at the ranger. The ranger looked at his radio. Above the badlands, the first hard stars were coming out, cold and indifferent, and far to the north, invisible in the dark, the escarpment stood waiting with whatever it had been keeping all these years.

"Still," Ansel whispered. "*We are still.*"

The ranger's hand closed slowly around the silent radio, and she saw in his face that whatever he had ridden out here to do, it had just died in the firelight between them.
