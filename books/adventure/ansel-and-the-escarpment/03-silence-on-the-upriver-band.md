## 3. Silence on the Upriver Band

The night turned cold enough to crack the mud in the arroyo where Isandro made camp, and he lay awake listening to it, the little reports of shrinking earth, like distant gunfire from a war fought by the ground itself. Three days he had ridden the thief's trail out of the salt flats and into the broken country below the escarpment, and three days he had found water waiting for him at each abandoned camp—a canteen hung on a dead branch, a tin cup set on a flat stone, always full, always clean. It was a message. He had not yet decided what it said.

He slept in pieces. Somewhere past the middle of the night he woke with his hand already on the radio.

It was habit more than anything. The upriver band—the concession company's private frequency, the one every ranger in the district was licensed to monitor—had been silent for two years. Dead air, the dispatchers called it, though everyone knew dead air was never really dead; it was just air nobody wanted disturbed. Isandro had spent six years listening to that band the way a man listens to a house at night, cataloguing its silences, learning which creaks meant weather and which meant someone walking where they shouldn't be. The band had gone quiet the autumn the survey expedition went into the escarpment and did not come out. Nine men. A foreman named Ochoa. A young instrument man whose name Isandro had never learned until this week, when he read it in a stolen ledger: *Tomás Reyes, chainman.*

He had thought about that name all day while following hoofprints north through country where nothing grew taller than a man's conscience.

Now, half-asleep with his bedroll still warm around him, he heard the band breathe.

Not static. Static was the desert's own voice, the white noise of stars and heat and distance. This was different. This was a carrier wave, thin as wire, rising out of the noise floor like a fish breaking dark water—and then a voice inside it, warped and doubled by skip and interference, but unmistakably a voice.

*"—any station, any station, this is Escarpment Survey, camp four, do you copy—"*

Isandro was upright before he knew he had moved, the radio cradled against his chest like something newborn. His hands were shaking. He told himself it was the cold.

The voice was a man's, hoarse, worn down to the grain. Behind it, faintly, other sounds: wind through rock, the clink of metal, someone coughing in a way that suggested lungs long past complaining. The signal surged and faded with the turning of the ionosphere, words surfacing and sinking.

*"—been transmitting on schedule, every—"*

A gap, filled with howling static.

*"—nobody answers. Nobody has ever answered. We know you can hear us. Whoever you are, we know the company took the band off rotation, we've known for—"*

Another gap. When the voice came back it was closer somehow, or Isandro had leaned closer to it, his ear nearly touching the housing, the antenna's shadow lying across his knees like a fallen branch.

*"—eleven of us left. Eleven. We have kept the records. We have kept everything. If anyone receives this, the passes will close within the month. After that there is no descent until spring, and we do not believe we will see spring. Come before the snow. Come before—"*

The transmission cut. Not faded—cut, sheared off mid-word with the mechanical finality of a switch thrown somewhere between the speaker and the sky. Then silence, the old silence, the two-year silence, pressing back in like water filling a footprint.

Isandro sat in the dark with the radio warming his hands and understood, slowly and then all at once, what he had just heard.

They were alive. Eleven men, alive above the snowline, alive for two years while the district filed them under *lost, presumed deceased* and the concession company collected their insurance and refiled their water rights and paved over the whole catastrophe with paperwork. Alive, and transmitting on schedule, and receiving nothing—not because no one could hear them, but because no one was allowed to listen. The band hadn't died. It had been killed. Jammed, or reassigned, or quietly struck from the rotation logs, some clerk's pen-stroke converting nine living men into a rounding error. And tonight, for reasons Isandro would spend the rest of his life trying to understand, the jamming had failed—or been broken through—and the desert had spoken.

He thought of the water left at each camp. Full cups on flat stones. A thief who stole not coin but paper, who moved through the flats like a ghost leaving gifts for her pursuer, who knew things about the escarpment that no one outside the company should have known.

She hadn't stolen the ledger to sell it. She'd stolen it to make someone listen.

And now the desert had listened, and Isandro Marchetti realized, with a horror that came on like altitude sickness—gradual, then total—that staying invisible had never actually been an option. He had told himself for three days that he was pursuing a thief, that the law was simple, that a badge pointed one direction and duty followed it. But the radio in his hands had just informed him that the law itself was a fiction maintained by jammed frequencies and unasked questions. The desert had been listening all along. Every ranger on the upriver band, every dispatcher, every man who logged *dead air* in the station book without once asking why a frequency used by eleven men had gone quiet—they were all of them accomplices to a silence. He had been an accomplice to a silence. He had signed the quarterly band-verification forms himself, six times, his own signature attesting that the upriver channel carried no traffic, when what it truly carried was the sound of men calling into a wall.

The coughing behind the voice. He would hear it for years.

He built a small fire after all, though the light might carry—he no longer cared who saw it. He put water on and drank it too hot and watched the steam climb. Above him the escarpment stood against the stars, a black serration like a jaw, and somewhere up there, above the snowline, eleven men were keeping records. Keeping everything. Waiting for a rescue that had been cancelled without their knowledge.

His oath said: return the thief, recover the property, restore order. His oath had been written by men who believed the paperwork. The paperwork said those men were dead. The paperwork was wrong, and the wrongness had a signature on it, several signatures, and Isandro's own name was not among them—but his silence was.

He looked north along the trail. In the last light before full dark he had seen the hoofprints turn toward the range, toward the old survey routes, toward the only place in the district where a stolen ledger could be matched against living witnesses. She was going up. Of course she was going up. She hadn't taken the truth to the courts or the newspapers; she had taken it to the mountain, because the mountain held the other copy, the proof that couldn't be dismissed as forgery, the men themselves.

And the company would know about the transmission too. Whatever had broken through the jamming tonight would be noticed in some office with electric lights, and riders would move, and the passes would begin to close—not with snow first, but with men.

Isandro kicked dirt over the fire and saddled in the dark, working by touch, his hands remembering what his mind was too loud to direct. Before he mounted he took the canteen from his saddle horn—the one he'd found that morning hanging on the dead juniper, still full, offered like a hand extended across a chase—and drank from it. It tasted of stone and juniper smoke and, absurdly, of kindness.

"Alright," he said to the dark, to the mountain, to the eleven voices the desert had finally let through. "I'm coming."

He rode north, and behind him the upriver band lay silent again, and the silence sounded different now—not empty, but occupied, the way a room sounds after someone has stopped speaking on purpose.

* * *

Near dawn he found the next camp, and this time the gift was not water.

A page torn from a survey notebook lay weighted under a stone beside the cold ashes, the handwriting small and precise, a chainman's hand, a man accustomed to recording distances exactly:

*If you heard the band last night, you know we weren't lying. If you didn't, ride faster. The scouts are two hours behind you and gaining. Take the western fork—the ford is watched.*

Below it, a second line, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*My brother kept records too. Bring your badge if it makes you feel safe. Just bring yourself.*

Isandro stood in the grey light holding the page, listening to the wind come down off the escarpment carrying the first smell of snow, and two hours south, faint as a heartbeat, he heard engines.
