## 4. An Uneasy Truce

*Isandro*

The dry well sat in a bowl of white stone, and for a long time after the ledger lay open between them, Isandro did not move. The wind came off the flats and turned the pages for them, one by one, as if the book itself were impatient to be read.

He had his rope in his hands. He'd carried it three days for exactly this purpose, and now it hung there, coiled and stupid, while a thief with dust in her eyelashes looked at him like he was the one who owed an accounting.

"You're not going to tie me," Ansel said. It wasn't a question.

"I should."

"You won't." She crouched at the well's lip, palms on her knees, watching him the way she'd probably watched her brother board the expedition wagon—knowing already that she couldn't stop it. "Because you read the second page. I saw your face change."

He had read the second page. *Water allocation, revised—upriver allotment reduced to maintenance minimums, downstream users notified of 'natural shortfall.'* Signed twice, countersigned once, stamped with the concession company's ironwheel seal. And beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand: *Expedition survey party dispatched to verify headwater flow. Party of eleven.*

Party of eleven. The escarpment expedition had been written off as lost to weather two winters ago. Isandro had been a ranger then too. He remembered the memorial notice pinned to the station board, the company's condolence payment, the way the file had closed so cleanly it had felt less like grief than like housekeeping.

"Eleven men," he said.

"Ten men and my brother." Ansel's voice didn't crack. That was somehow worse. "The company says weather. The company says the escarpment took them. But this ledger says the company cut their water before they ever climbed, and when the survey found what was really happening upriver—" she tapped the page, "—they stopped sending resupply. You don't lose eleven people to weather in a season you planned for. You abandon them."

The wind turned another page. Isandro looked down at his rope, then coiled it slowly back onto his saddle horn, and hated himself a little for how relieved his hands were to do it.

"I have an oath," he said.

"Yes." She stood, brushing off her knees. "And I have a brother. Let's see which one weighs more by the time we reach the range."

* * *

*Ansel*

She hadn't expected him to believe her. Belief had never been part of the plan; the plan had been to get the ledger into the valley, into the land board's hands, before the permits renewed at season's end—and if a ranger caught her first, well, she'd counted on the arithmetic being simple enough even for an honest man. Eleven dead against one thief. Even law could do that sum, if anyone made it look up from its paperwork long enough to try.

But Marchetti hadn't arrested her, and now they stood facing each other across the dry well like two people who'd arrived at the same funeral by different roads, and belief turned out to be its own kind of problem. Because if he believed her, then he had to act. And if he acted, he'd want to do it his way—by the book, through channels, with forms in triplicate—and the book was exactly what the company had bought.

"The land board sits in six weeks," she told him. "After that, renewal. After that, this ledger is a historical curiosity and my brother is officially weather."

"The board will want verification. Chain of custody. They'll want the original survey records—"

"The original survey records are in the concession office in Vell's Crossing, under seal." She let that sit. "Which is why the ledger isn't enough. It's why I need the routes."

His eyes narrowed. "What routes?"

"The old survey routes. Before the concession, before the permits—the government mappers cut trails up the escarpment's east face. Switchbacks, ledges, a high route above the snowline. My brother studied them. He drew copies. He sent me one." She reached into her coat and produced a folded sheet of oilcloth paper, soft as cloth from handling, covered in her brother's small, precise hand. "If the survivors are alive—if anyone is alive up there—they're above the snowline, because below it is where the company would have let them die. And the only way up before the snows is the way the company doesn't know exists."

Marchetti took the map without touching her fingers. He studied it the way he seemed to study everything—thoroughly, unhurriedly, like a man reading a river for depth. "This is government property," he said finally. "Copying survey charts is a crime."

"So is abandoning eleven people. Pick which crime you'd rather be known for."

Something moved behind his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile might have lived, in a man who'd let the habit go fallow. He folded the map along her brother's creases and handed it back.

"We ride together," he said. "Not because I trust you. Because you know the routes and I know the country, and neither of us reaches the valley alone."

"No," Ansel agreed. "Not trust. Arithmetic."

* * *

*Isandro*

They rode north by afternoon light, the escarpment rising ahead of them like the hull of a drowned ship, blue-grey and streaked with the first chalky hints of snow. Isandro kept half a length between their horses—not close enough to grab, not far enough to lose her—and told himself the arrangement was tactical. He was lying, and he suspected she knew it. Somewhere between the well and the horizon, custody had quietly become companionship, and no form he'd ever signed accounted for the difference.

At dusk he checked his radio out of habit. Static. He tried the upriver band, the one every ranger used, the one that had gone silent two years ago and stayed silent, officially due to atmospheric interference.

Static—but underneath it, faint as a pulse under ice, something else. A rhythm. Three clicks. Pause. Three clicks.

He froze. "Ansel."

She reined in. He held up a hand, and they sat motionless in the failing light while the static breathed. The clicks came again. Three. Pause. Three. Then, threaded through them, almost lost: a voice, or the ghost of one, saying a word that might have been *still* or might have been *starved*, and then nothing, the band closing like water over a stone.

"That frequency's been dead two years," Isandro said. His own voice sounded strange to him. "Company jammed it after the expedition went missing. Said interference. Everyone accepted it."

"Then someone just broke through." Ansel's face had gone pale in the last light. "Someone up there has a transmitter. Someone's been trying."

Three clicks. Pause. Three clicks. Not random. A signal maintained. A signal *kept*, the way you keep a fire, the way you keep a hope you're ashamed of.

Isandro thought of the memorial notice, the condolence payments, the clean-closed file. He thought of his oath, and of what it actually bound him to protect. The desert had been listening all along. It had simply been made to hold its tongue.

"We ride tonight," he said. "Cold camp. No fires."

Ansel was already tightening her girth strap. For the first time since the salt flats, they wanted the same thing at the same speed, and Isandro understood that this—not the ledger, not the map—was the real truce. Two debts. Same dead men. One road.

They rode into the dark, and behind them, far back along the trail they'd left across the flats, a lantern kindled. Then a second. Company scouts, patient as vultures, keeping their distance and keeping their count.

* * *

*Ansel*

She felt them before she saw them—had felt them since noon, that prickle between the shoulder blades that meant being watched by professionals. The scouts didn't close. They didn't need to. Their job wasn't capture; it was report. Every mile she and Marchetti gained toward the escarpment was a mile the concession company's lawyers gained toward an injunction, and every hour the lanterns burned behind them was an hour someone in Vell's Crossing was deciding how far they were willing to go to keep eleven corpses officially buried.

"They'll try the ford," Marchetti said near midnight, when they stopped to breathe the horses at a seep. "River forks ahead. Only crossing for thirty miles. If they mean to stop us short of the range, that's where."

"Then we cross before dawn."

He glanced at her. "You've done this before. Run from men with lanterns."

"My father ran a freight line the company squeezed out. I learned young what their patience looks like." She fed her mare a handful of grain and watched the dark where the lanterns weren't, precisely because they were. "It looks like nothing happening for a very long time. And then it looks like everything happening at once."

Marchetti was quiet a moment. "When we reach the ford," he said, "if it goes wrong—I need to know what you'll do. With the ledger."

"It stays on my body. However that ends."

"And if I ordered you to give it to me? Custody. Safekeeping. My authority?"

She looked at him fully then, and let him see all of it—the three years of petitions, the sealed office doors, the condolence letter signed by a man who'd never learned her brother's name. "Then you'd better be prepared to take it," she said, "and I'd advise you to bring both hands."

To her surprise, he nodded slowly, as if she'd passed some examination he'd been dreading. "Good," he said. "I needed to know you wouldn't hand it over. To anyone. Including me."

Ahead of them the river murmured in its gorge, black and quick, and beyond it the escarpment stood against the stars—a wall, a grave marker, a door. Behind them the lanterns burned on, patient, counting.

Ansel swung into the saddle. Dawn was four hours off, the ford one, and somewhere above the snowline, three clicks and a pause, three clicks and a pause, a signal kept alive by hands she prayed still had the strength to hold it.

"Ride," she said.
