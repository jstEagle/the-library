## 5. The Company's Men

Ansel heard the river before she saw it—a low, mineral mutter under the wind, the sound of water that had traveled a long way to die in the flats. Two days out from the dry well, and the land had begun, grudgingly, to give things back: a stand of stunted cottonwoods gone gold at the edges, a smell of wet stone, the first honest shade they'd seen since the salt.

She reined in on the last rise and let the mare blow. Behind her, Isandro did the same, and for a while neither of them spoke. That was the shape of their truce now—silence held between them like something breakable, both of them careful where they set their feet.

"The ford's called Vado Hondo," Isandro said at last. "Deep crossing. In autumn it runs shin-high if you pick your line." He said it the way he said everything, flat and precise, a ranger's inventory of the world. But she had learned to hear what was underneath it. He was tired in a way that had nothing to do with his body. He kept touching the radio at his hip as though checking a wound.

"And if we don't pick our line?"

"Then the river picks it for you."

She almost smiled. It surprised her, that—the small mechanical twitch of it, like a joint long rusted. She hadn't smiled since the well, since she'd opened the ledger across her knees and watched him read the columns of names and numbers and understand, finally, what she'd been carrying. He hadn't apologized. He hadn't arrested her either. Both omissions sat between them, unexamined, like coals that might yet catch.

They came down to the river through the cottonwoods, and that was when Ansel saw the birds.

Not the right birds. The trees should have held doves, maybe a heron working the shallows. Instead there were crows—six of them, seven—sitting along a dead branch over the ford in a row so deliberate it looked arranged. They weren't cawing. They were watching the water with their heads cocked, patient as clerks.

Her stomach went cold. Her brother Teo had taught her that, years ago, on their one trip up into the foothills together: *when the scavengers go quiet, something has already died, or something is about to.* She'd thought he was being dramatic. He'd been seventeen and full of other people's poetry. Then he'd grown into a man who signed onto an expedition because the concession company paid triple wages and asked no questions, and the poetry had gone out of him, and two years ago the company had sent her a letter with a black border and the word *REGRET* in it, and no body, and no answers, and a ledger full of lies instead.

"Isandro," she said quietly. "The birds."

He'd already stopped. His hand had come off the reins and was resting on the radio—not touching it now, hovering. His eyes moved along the far bank, reading it the way she read a page of figures: fast, total, unsmiling.

"Tamarisk thicket on the east approach," he murmured. "Too thick for this time of year. Nobody camps in tamarisk unless they don't want to be seen from the water."

"How many?"

"If I knew, I'd tell you." A pause. "More than two. Fewer than twenty."

*Helpful,* she didn't say. Instead she looked at the river itself, running brown-green and unhurried over its gravel bed, and understood that the choice had already been made for her somewhere behind her eyes. There was no other crossing for thirty miles north or forty south. The ledger rode against her ribs in an oilskin wrap, and every mile of delay was a mile the company's lawyers gained, a mile closer to permit renewal, a mile deeper into the season's first snows. The men in the tamarisk knew that too. That was why they were here, and not chasing them across the open flats where a ranger's rifle could talk back.

"We could go around," Isandro said, and she heard him testing the words even as he spoke them, hearing how hollow they rang.

"No," she said. "We can't."

He looked at her then—really looked, the first time since the well—and whatever he found in her face seemed to settle something in him. He nodded once, slow, like a man signing a document he hated.

"Then we cross loud," he said. "Middle of the channel, horses close together. If they wanted us dead they'd have shot by now. They want the ledger, and they want it to look like bandits did it." He drew his rifle from the scabbard and laid it across his thighs, casual as a man carrying kindling. "So we give them a reason not to look like bandits. We give them witnesses."

"There are no witnesses out here."

"There's the river," Isandro said. "Rivers carry things downstream. Bodies. Stories." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Company men hate rivers."

---

They hit the water at mid-morning, when the light lay flat on the current and made the far bank a smear of gold and shadow. The river took the horses to the hocks, then the knees, cold enough to make Ansel gasp, the pull of it strong against the animals' flanks. She kept her eyes forward. She counted her own breaths. Somewhere behind her left shoulder, in the tamarisk, men were making decisions about her life, and all she could do was keep the mare's head pointed at the far shore and trust that murder was more expensive than theft.

They were nearly across—hooves finding gravel, water falling away—when the first rider came out of the trees.

He came at a walk, which was worse than a charge. A big man on a dun horse, face wrapped against the dust below the eyes, a company brand on his saddle skirt: the escarpment concession's mark, a stylized peak with a canal cut through it. Four more emerged behind him, fanning out along the bank with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and never once been punished for it. One carried a coil of rope. One carried a jerrican, and the smell of it reached Ansel across the water—kerosene, sharp and wrong among the river smells.

"Ranger Marchetti." The big man's voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. "Didn't expect to see you this far upriver. Thought you were still chasing a horse thief down in the salts."

"You found me," Isandro said. He had turned his horse broadside, putting himself between the riders and Ansel, and his rifle lay along his thigh, muzzle down, a courtesy and a warning at once. "Congratulations. Now get out of my way."

"See, that's the thing." The big man shook his head, sorrowful as a priest. "There's no jurisdiction past the high-water mark on concession land. You know that. Your badge stops—" he pointed with a gloved finger at the waterline, at the dark seam of wet gravel, "—right about there."

Ansel felt the fury come up through her like heat off stone. Not the hot kind. The old kind, the kind that had lived in her chest for two years, banked and fed and tended like a stove fire through every winter since the letter with the black border. She looked at these men—at their clean gear, their new ropes, their kerosene—and she thought: *they burned the base camp too.* She didn't know it, not the way you know a fact. She knew it the way you know weather coming. Companies burned what they couldn't bury. It was in the ledger's margins, in the missing pages, in the way the supply requisitions stopped six weeks before the expedition was declared lost.

"They're going to burn our supplies," she said. Not to Isandro. To the big man. Loudly, so the others could hear. "Right here, in front of a ranger. Because a piece of paper scares them."

The big man's eyes flicked to her for the first time, and she saw the assessment in them—quick, professional, dismissive. A woman, a stolen horse, a thief. Nothing.

"Ma'am," he said, "you're carrying stolen property. We're recovering it. The ranger will confirm the circumstances however he likes afterward." He nodded at the jerrican. "Paper burns. Horses swim. Everybody walks home. That's the generous version."

"And the other version?" Isandro asked.

The big man smiled under his face-wrap. "The other version's why we brought rope."

What happened next, Ansel would remember all her life in fragments, the way you remember a rockfall—by sound and impact rather than sequence. Isandro moving his horse forward a half-step, buying ground. The rope man swinging down from his saddle. The jerrican arcing, glinting, landing in the middle of their packed supplies with a sound like a dropped drum, kerosene blooming dark across the canvas. A match. Flame taking the canvas with a soft whump that she felt in her teeth. Her bedroll going up. The spare ammunition cooking off in its box, cracking like knucklebones. The mare screaming and her hauling the animal's head around, away from the fire, away from the water, her hands doing it without her.

And through all of it, the ledger against her ribs, dry inside its oilskin, and her own voice inside her skull saying, over and over, with terrible calm: *they showed you. They just showed you everything.*

Because that was the thing the fire taught her, standing there in the smoke while five men watched her belongings turn to ash with the patience of executioners: she had spent two years thinking she was smuggling a secret. She wasn't. She was carrying a verdict that hadn't been read aloud yet. And the company knew it—that was why the riders, why the rope, why the kerosene instead of bullets. If the ledger surfaced before the permits renewed, the concession collapsed. Not weakened. *Collapsed.* Every falsified right-of-way voided, every water claim reopened, every signature on every buried report dragged into the light with the expedition's true dates attached. The men in the tamarisk weren't thugs. They were the last dam holding back a flood, and they knew exactly how deep the water ran.

"Isandro," she said. Her voice came out level. That surprised her most of all. "Look at them."

He was looking. He had his rifle half-raised and five rifles covering him, and he was looking at the burning supplies, at the branded saddles, at the big man's pleasant face, and she watched something move through him that she recognized because she had lived inside it for two years. The moment the institution stops being a shelter and becomes a wall you're standing on the wrong side of.

"This is evidence," Isandro said quietly. "You're destroying evidence in front of a sworn officer."

"We're managing a range improvement," the big man said. "Write it up however you want, Ranger. By the time any board reads it, the permits will be renewed and your report will be a curiosity." He turned his horse. Paused. Looked back at Ansel, and this time there was no dismissal in it, only a flat professional regret, like a man declining a purchase. "Give us the paper, ma'am. Walk away rich. Or keep it, and walk up into those mountains after the snows start, and we'll collect it off your body in the spring. Either way it comes back to us. Only question is what condition it's in, and whether you're attached to it."

Then they were gone, back into the tamarisk, unhurried, leaving the fire and the smoke and the crows, who had risen at the flames and now settled again on their dead branch, resuming their watch.

* * *

They stood in the shallows until the fire burned itself down to black canvas and ash. There was nothing to save. Ansel catalogued the losses with a strange detachment—bedroll, spare rations, the ammunition, the spare boots she'd traded half her water for in the last town. Gone. What remained was what they carried: the ledger, two days of food, three days of water, two rifles, and the mountains, still four hard days south, wearing their first white caps like a warning.

Isandro waded ashore and stood over the ashes for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed. All the ranger's flatness had burned out of it, and what was left was quieter and much more dangerous.

"I'm going to write this up," he said. "Every name I saw. The brand. The threat. It won't matter—it'll be filed under curiosities—but it will exist. A record. That matters to me."

"Good," Ansel said. "Write it up. And then help me plan, because writing is done."

He turned. Raised an eyebrow.

She walked to the water's edge and crouched, and began, with a stick, to draw in the wet gravel: the river, the range, the southern pass, the gorge. Her hand didn't shake. Two years of fury had finally finished refining itself into something with an edge, and it felt less like anger now than like clarity, cold and load-bearing.

"They think we're running," she said. "Let them. Runners take roads, and roads have fords, and fords have men with kerosene. So we stop running." She drew a line up out of the gorge country, west of the pass, into terrain no map in the company office bothered to mark correctly. "Teo surveyed these routes. He wrote me about them—old stock trails from before the concession, goat paths above the tree line. The company's riders know the passes. They don't know the mountain. My brother did."

"That route kills people in good weather," Isandro said. "And the weather's turning."

"Yes," Ansel said. She snapped the stick and threw the halves into the river, where the current took them south, toward the valley, toward the land board, toward everything waiting at the end of this. "It is. Which means the company's riders will hole up and wait for it to pass. Men who fight for wages hide from storms. We won't have that luxury—we'll be in the storm, and the storm will be ours."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she hadn't seen him do: he unbuckled the radio from his belt and set it on a stone between them, open, its dead dial facing the sky.

"Upriver band's still jammed," he said. "But someone broke through it once, at the escarpment. Someone up there is alive, and they've got a transmitter, and they tried to reach us." His jaw tightened. "If they try again, I want to be listening. And if we're climbing into the high country anyway—" he met her eyes, "—then maybe we're not just delivering your ledger. Maybe we're answering a call that's been waiting two years."

Above the river, the crows lifted all at once, silent, and wheeled south toward the escarpment, and Ansel watched them go and felt the last of the runner fall away from her like burnt canvas.

"Then we climb," she said.
