## 6. Foot of the Escarpment

The range announced itself three days before we saw it.

First it was a bruise on the horizon, blue-black where the sky should have gone gold at dusk. Then the wind changed—came down off the heights carrying a smell I hadn't breathed since I was a boy on my father's survey crews: cold stone, wet iron, the green rot of things growing in shadow instead of sun. The desert had no smell like that. The desert smelled of salt and dust and honest heat. This was something older, patient, waiting.

Ansel rode ahead of me with her hood up and her shoulders set like a woman walking into a courtroom. She had barely spoken since the ford, since the company riders burned our spare supplies and left us with what we could carry on two horses and one mule. But she'd stopped running. That was the change I kept noticing, the way you notice a shifted weight in a pack. Before the ford she'd moved like prey. Now she moved like someone with a destination, and every mile east put more iron in her spine.

I rode behind her because I didn't trust myself to ride beside her yet. My badge sat against my chest under the coat, and all day it had felt less like tin and more like a stone I was carrying in my mouth.

* * *

The storm found us at dusk on the last day, rolling off the escarpment's shoulder in a wall of gray that ate the light whole. Rain came sideways, then turned to sleet, ticking against the rocks like gravel thrown by a giant hand. We hunched through it, horses' ears flat, following Ansel's memory of an old survey track that climbed out of the dry wash and into the foothills where the junipers began.

"There," she shouted over the wind, and pointed.

The base camp sat in a bowl below the first true cliffs, sheltered from the north wind by a spur of black rock. Even at half a mile I could see it was wrong. Company camps were tidy things—tents staked square, gear racked, flags flying so the air service could find them. This camp looked like a body. Canvas sagged in long tears. A supply sled lay half-buried in blown sand, its runners pointing back the way we'd come, as if it had been trying to leave.

We made a cold camp at the edge of it. No fire—the storm would hide the smoke, but the light would carry, and I no longer believed we were unwatched. Ansel saw to the animals while I walked into the ruin with my lantern held low.

The first tent I entered still had bedrolls in it. Two of them, side by side, blankets folded back as if the sleepers had risen that morning and meant to return. A tin plate sat on a crate with a fork resting across it. Dust lay thick over everything, thick enough to write in, and nobody had written in it for a long time.

People don't abandon camps like this. People abandon camps when they're leaving in a hurry, or leaving dead. This was neither. This was a camp left the way you'd leave a room you expected to come back to—and then never came back.

"Isandro." Ansel's voice from the mess tent, stripped of everything but the words themselves. "You need to see this."

* * *

The mess tent had been the expedition's heart, and whoever left it had left its records behind—or been made to. A field desk stood against the pole, drawers hanging open, papers scattered and curled with damp. Ansel stood over it holding a page in both hands, and in the lantern light her face had gone the color of the sleet outside.

She handed me the page without a word.

It was a ration ledger. Dated entries, careful surveyor's handwriting, columns of numbers. And the dates ran wrong. The Marlowe-Concession expedition—the one the company had written off, the one whose memorial plaque I'd ridden past in the valley town every season of my service, *lost in the high country, spring of the bad year*—the dates on this ledger ran four months past the official loss date. Four months past the day the company had told the land board there were no survivors to rescue.

I turned the page. Another. The entries continued, thinner rations, thinner handwriting, but continuing. Someone had been alive up here, keeping books, keeping count, for a hundred and twenty days after the company closed the file.

"They knew," Ansel said. Her voice came out flat, which was worse than if it had broken. "The rescue party. There was a rescue party—isn't that always the story? They came within ten miles of here. My mother paid for the search herself before the company called it off." She took another sheet from the desk, a carbon copy on flimsy paper, and read it aloud like a man reading his own sentence. "*Request permission to extend search grid westward. Survivors' signal fires observed on the second bench. Requesting resupply drop.*" She looked up. "There's a response stamped on the bottom."

I read it. I made myself read it twice, because the first time my eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a thing too bright to look at.

*Search concluded per concession terms. No further expenditure authorized. Do not log signal observations.*

Do not log signal observations.

The storm pressed against the canvas. Somewhere out in the dark the mule stamped and complained, alive and warm and fed, and I thought about the men who had written that order sitting in a valley office with a stove going, deciding that fires on a mountainside were a line item rather than lives.

"I'm a ranger," I said, and heard how stupid it sounded even as I said it. "Eleven years. If a distress call comes in on any band, any frequency, we go. That's the whole oath. Everything else is decoration."

"Your radio," Ansel said quietly. "The upriver band. You told me yourself—it's been dead for years. Dead frequencies don't die on their own, Isandro. Somebody killed them. Somebody has been standing on this band for two years, sitting on these signals, filing these orders." She gestured at the tent, the ruined camp, the mountain above us vanishing into cloud. "And your badge is the thing that makes it all legal. Every permit, every boundary, every 'no further expenditure authorized'—it all runs through rangers like you. You're not the law out here. You're the company's signature."

I wanted to argue. God help me, I stood there in a dead man's mess tent with his ration book in my hands and I wanted to argue, because the alternative was to feel eleven years of my life turn over like a log in a fire and show the rotten underside.

Instead I knelt and went through the rest of the desk.

* * *

What we found in the next hour, by lantern light while the storm hammered the escarpment above us, was not the record of a lost expedition. It was the record of an abandoned one.

A surveyor's journal, water-stained but legible, kept by a foreman named Aldous Wren—a name I knew from the memorial plaque, third from the top. His entries started in competent field shorthand and got plainer as the weeks went on, as if he'd stopped writing for the company and started writing for whoever might someday read it.

*Day 12. Bridge washed out. Sending Ruiz down for help. He should reach the valley in six days.*

*Day 19. No sign of Ruiz. Hobb says he saw riders on the lower trail but they did not turn toward us.*

*Day 31. Second fire built on the bench. Green boughs for smoke. If they can see us and will not come, then they have been told not to.*

*Day 44. We are being counted, not rescued. Rations for nine weeks if we're careful. Vess has the ledgers. Whatever happens, the ledgers must go down the mountain. Not us. The ledgers. They can survive without us. The truth cannot.*

*Day 60. Cold now. We talk about the valley like children talk about summer.*

The last entry was dated five months after the loss date. After that, nothing. Nine names on the memorial plaque, and at least some of them had lived months past their own deaths, watching the lower trail for riders who had orders not to turn toward them.

I sat back on my heels in the ruins of their table and understood, finally and completely, what Ansel had stolen and why she'd run west instead of south to the courts. Because there were no courts for this. There was only the desert, and the mountain, and whoever was strong enough and stubborn enough to carry the truth up over the top of the world and bring it down the other side before the men who wrote *do not log signal observations* could bury it deeper than they'd buried the dead.

My badge hung against my chest, and for the first time in eleven years it weighed exactly what it was: a piece of stamped metal that had spent years certifying a lie.

"Vess," Ansel said. She'd gone very still, the way she went still when something mattered most. "That's my brother's name. Tomás Vess-Anselmo—he signed with Mother's name so the company wouldn't know whose son they'd hired." Her hand flattened against the journal page, over the words *the ledgers must go down the mountain.* "He kept a second copy. He always kept copies. He used to say paper was the only honest witness because it couldn't be promoted."

"Then he may have carried it higher," I said. "If they went up—above the snowline, into the caves—"

"Nobody survives a winter above the snowline."

"No," I agreed. "Nobody survives a winter alone." I looked at her, and the storm roared down the cliffs, and I said the thing I'd been circling since the dry well, the thing that had no road back once spoken. "But six men can survive anything a mountain builds if they keep a fire and keep faith. And your brother kept books. Bookkeepers plan."

Outside, the sleet had thickened into true snow—the first snow, weeks early, the sky spending winter's coin all at once. It came down through the lantern glow in long silent strokes, filling the tracks we'd made, erasing the trail behind us as if the mountain had decided we were only ever going forward.

Ansel stood in the torn doorway of the mess tent with the journal hugged to her chest, looking up at where the cliffs vanished into white.

"The switchbacks start at first light," she said. "If we're not above the second bench before the snow gets deep, we'll be joining them."

I banked the lantern, and in the dark I took the badge off my chest—not to throw it away, not yet, but to hold it in my fist, feeling its edges, deciding what it was for. Above us, somewhere in the white roar of the escarpment, six ghosts were keeping a fire.
