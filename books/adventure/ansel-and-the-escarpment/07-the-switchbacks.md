## 7. The Switchbacks

The mountain did not announce itself. It simply arrived, the way grief arrives—one morning the horizon was salt and shimmer, and the next it was stone, gray-blue and enormous, wearing a collar of cloud that had no business being there before October.

Isandro stood at the foot of the first switchback with his hands on his hips and his breath already short, though he hadn't climbed anything yet except out of the saddle. The escarpment rose above them in stacked walls of fractured granite, cut by the old survey crews into a staircase of switchbacks that zigzagged up the face like a scar someone had tried to sew shut. Twenty years of wind had chewed at the retaining walls. In places the trail was less a path than an argument between the mountain and the men who'd insisted on crossing it.

"Snow's early," Ansel said beside him. She was looking up, past the third bend, to where the clouds hung low and wrong-colored, bruise-purple along their bellies.

"Snow's always early somewhere." He said it because a ranger learns to say such things, to keep the people around him calm, but even as the words left his mouth he could smell it coming—that clean, mineral cold that meant the sky had made its decision. "We make the fourth camp by dark or we don't make it at all."

They didn't make the fourth camp by dark.

The trouble started at the second switchback, where a rockslide two winters old had eaten half the trail and never given it back. Isandro went first, testing each stone with his boot heel, listening for the hollow note that meant nothing underneath. Behind him came Ansel with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat, and behind her the three survivors who had come down from the high cave—Vasquez with her frostbitten fingers, the boy Tomás carrying more than his own weight, and old Rennick, the dying foreman, who rode a mule they'd lashed together from rope and stubbornness and refused, absolutely refused, to be left behind.

Halfway across the slide, Isandro heard the whistle.

Two notes, low, repeated. Company signal. He knew it from his training years ago—the way you know a hymn from a church you stopped attending—and his body reacted before his mind gave permission: he dropped flat against the slope, hand up, and felt Ansel fold down beside him without a word being spoken.

They came over the ridge above the third bend. Four riders, dismounted, moving with the unhurried competence of men paid by the day. One carried a rifle with a scope that caught the last light and threw it back like a small cold star.

"They're cutting us off above," Ansel breathed. "The switchbacks funnel right into them."

Isandro looked at the trail ahead—the narrow shelf, the drop-off falling away into shadow on his left, four hundred feet of air ending in talus—and then at the cliff wall on his right. And there, where the granite had sheared away in some ancient collapse, he saw it: a seam. A ledge. Barely a hand's width of stone running laterally along the face, unmarked on any survey he'd ever read, the kind of route that existed only because goats were too stupid to know better and men too desperate to care.

"That's not a trail," Ansel said, following his eyes.

"It's a way through."

"It's a way off the mountain."

"Both things can be true." He was already unbuckling the rope coil from his pack. "Riders can't take it. That's the whole point."

* * *

They moved in the blue hour, when the light went thin and everything sharp became uncertain. Isandro went first along the ledge, driving pitons into cracks that may or may not have been kind, paying out rope behind him. The stone under his boots was slick with rime, and the wind came up the escarpment face in long slow shoves, patient as a debt collector, testing him at every step.

Below, the drop opened like a throat.

He did not look down. This was a discipline older than his oath: look at your feet, look at the crack, look at the next hold. The world shrinks to what is directly beneath you, and somehow that is enough. He heard the others behind him—Vasquez's controlled breathing, Tomás's small involuntary sounds, Rennick's mule protesting in a language all mules share—and he kept moving, kept placing iron, kept the line taut and true.

The snow began at the fifth pitch. Not falling so much as arriving—a white static filling the air, erasing the trail below, the riders above, the entire lower world. Within minutes his shoulders wore a thin coat of it. His fingers went from aching to absent. He drove another piton, tested it, and thought: *four more pitches, maybe five, then the ledge widens and we're around the shoulder and out of their sight.*

He was wrong about how many pitches. He would learn this the hard way.

At what should have been the final traverse, the ledge simply ended. A slab the size of a chapel door had let go sometime in the last century, taking the route with it, leaving a gap of perhaps eight feet with nothing below but the long fall and nothing above but blank wall. On the far side, the ledge resumed, wide and welcoming, mocking him.

"We go back?" Vasquez called forward, her voice shredded by wind.

Back was the riders. Back was the rifle scope catching starlight. Back was two years of dead men waiting in a cave while the world filed paperwork.

"No," Isandro said. "We cross."

* * *

He anchored the rope at the last good crack and rigged the tension line across the gap, and then he understood—with the particular clarity that comes to a man when the arithmetic finally finishes—what the crossing would cost. Someone had to go first, unanchored, to set the far anchor. Someone had to hang on a single line over four hundred feet of nothing while the wind pushed and the snow blinded and his hands, already past feeling, had to do the one thing hands exist to do: hold.

"I'll go," Ansel said.

"You'll carry the ledger."

"That's why I should go. If I fall, it falls with me, and the company wins either way—"

"If you fall, I lose the only person on this mountain who knows where we're going." He said it flatly, without drama, the way you state a fact about weather. Then, because she deserved more than weather-talk: "And because I've spent three weeks chasing you across the desert for something I believed was theft. Let me spend five minutes proving what it actually was."

She stared at him. Snow gathered in her hair, in her lashes. Behind her, Rennick coughed the wet deep cough of a man rationing his remaining breaths.

"What was it actually?" she asked quietly.

"A rescue," Isandro said. "Late. But a rescue."

He clipped in, checked the knot twice, and stepped off the ledge.

* * *

There is a version of this story he would tell later, in the valley, by firelight, and in that version the crossing takes thirty seconds and he barely remembers it. The truth was longer and worse. The truth was that the wind hit him at the midpoint like a hand shoving a door, and the rope sang, and his boots skated on ice-glazed stone, and for one full, endless moment he hung over the void by his arms alone, watching the snow swirl up past him from the darkness below as if the mountain were exhaling.

His left hand slipped.

He felt it go—not a dramatic tearing loose but a slow, quiet betrayal, his grip opening finger by finger like a flower deciding to bloom—and in that suspended instant, swinging above four hundred feet of dark, Isandro Marchetti thought with perfect calm: *so this is what the badge weighs.* Not the tin itself. The years. The oaths spoken to institutions that had stopped listening. The reports he'd signed that said *lost, presumed dead* about men who had written *still alive, still waiting* in a cave above the snowline. All of it hanging from his hands, and his hands letting go.

Then his right hand closed instead.

It was not courage. It was simpler than that, and harder: somewhere behind him on that ledge was a woman carrying her brother's handwriting, and three strangers who had chosen to descend rather than die waiting, and if he let go now, the mountain would take all of them one after another like beads off a snapped string. His loyalty had spent twenty years pointed at a star on his chest. It took one slip in the snow to turn it around.

He hauled himself hand over hand, screaming without sound, and his boot found the far lip, and he rolled onto the ledge and lay there while the snow fell on his back and his lungs burned like furnaces fed with glass.

Then he anchored the line, and pulled it taut, and shouted the word that mattered:

"Cross!"

* * *

Ansel came first, quick and light, refusing to look down. Then Vasquez, slower, her ruined hands hooked in the rope like grappling irons. Then Tomás, crying without shame, which Isandro privately considered the correct response to that gap. Last came Rennick's mule, blindfolded and led by a rope sling, the old man himself crossing on the tension line with a strength that had nothing to do with his body and everything to do with what he was still carrying: the second ledger, the truth, the names.

When they were all across, Isandro sat with his back to the wall and his hands in his lap and discovered he could not close his left one. The fingers moved when he told them to, mostly, but the grip was gone, scooped out of him like water from a cracked cup. He flexed it and watched it fail and felt, strangely, no fear at all. Only a vast and unfamiliar lightness, as if by nearly losing his hold on the mountain he had finally let go of something heavier.

Ansel knelt beside him and wrapped his hand in a strip of blanket, working with the efficiency of someone who had bandaged miners before.

"You couldn't have known the ledge was broken," she said.

"I knew the odds." He watched the snow thicken, erasing the ledge behind them, erasing the route, erasing any possibility of retreat—which meant the company riders, wherever they were, faced the same blank wall of weather. Which meant, for tonight, they lived. "I offered anyway. No apology coming."

"I wasn't going to ask for one." She tied off the bandage and looked at him, and in the white gloom her eyes were the darkest thing on the mountain. "Why? You could have turned me in at the dry well. You could have walked away at the ford. Why climb?"

He considered the question honestly, because she had earned honesty.

"Because I swore an oath to protect the people of this territory," he said. "I just spent twenty years protecting the wrong ones."

Above them, through a tear in the clouds, the higher peaks showed themselves for the first time—white, immense, indifferent—and below the tear, faint as a struck match in a dark room, a light blinked twice from a slope across the valley.

Not the company's lanterns. The company used steady lights.

This one blinked. Twice. Paused. Twice again.

Someone up there knew the old survey signals. Someone up there was counting them through.
