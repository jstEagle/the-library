## 8. Above the Snowline

The cave breathed warmth before they saw it.

Ansel felt it first as a wrongness in the air—a thread of woodsmoke threading through the iron cold, so faint she thought her mind had finally begun to invent kindnesses. Then Isandro stopped ahead of her on the ledge, one hand raised, and she saw it too: a smudge of gray rising from a crack in the rock face forty feet above, curling into the wind and shredding like something trying to speak.

"Smoke," he said, as if the word might break.

"Two years," Ansel said. "You said the expedition was written off two years ago."

"I said the company wrote them off." He didn't look at her. His breath came out in white plumes that the wind took sideways. "I never said anything about dying."

They climbed the last pitch with their hands bare because mittened fingers couldn't find the holds, and by the time they hauled themselves over the lip of the ledge Ansel's palms were bleeding and she didn't care. The cave mouth was half-hidden behind a curtain of wind-carved ice, wide enough for one body at a time, and warm air pushed out of it like an exhalation—dust and smoke and tallow and something underneath all of it, human, unwashed, alive.

She went in first. Isandro let her.

* * *

Inside, the dark resolved slowly. A fire pit ringed with stones, banked to embers, its heat reaching maybe eight feet in any direction. Beyond that, shapes: bedrolls flattened to the color of the stone, crates pried open and emptied, a tin stove, coils of wire, a signal mirror hanging on a nail. And people. Four of them, maybe five—she counted twice and got different numbers because two of the shapes were so thin they seemed more shadow than man. They had turned toward the light at the cave mouth, and none of them moved, and none of them spoke, as if visitors were a thing they had trained out of believing.

Then a voice from the back, cracked and low: "Well. Either the company's finally come to finish it, or we've gone mad enough to share a hallucination."

An old man pulled himself upright against the wall. He was gaunt as a wintered coyote, beard gone gray-white, one leg wrapped in bindings that had been changed and changed again until the cloth itself looked scarred. But his eyes were clear, and they fixed on Ansel with an attention that made her spine straighten.

"You're not company," he said. "Company doesn't climb."

"No," Ansel said. Her voice came out strange to her own ears. "We're not."

"Then who?"

She should have said ranger first, or thief, or any of the true things. Instead what came out was: "My brother rode with you. Tomás Vela. Second survey team."

The silence that followed had weight, the kind that presses down on the chest. One of the thin shapes by the fire made a sound—not quite a word—and covered its mouth.

The old man closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, but steady. "Tomás Vela," he repeated. "He talked about you. Every night, near enough. Said his sister could read a ledger better than the land board's own clerks. Said you'd come looking someday."

"He's dead," Ansel said. It wasn't a question. She had known it for two years the way you know a bone is broken even through the cast—the knowledge lived in her body, not her mind. But saying it aloud in this place, among these men, made it real in a way grief had refused to be until now.

"He is," the old man said simply. "Eighteen months back. The coughing sickness. We did what we could." He tapped his own chest. "I'm Aurelio Sandoval. Foreman. What's left of one."

* * *

They gave them tea made from pine needles and melted snow, bitter and hot, and Ansel held the cup without drinking because her hands needed the work. Around the fire the survivors assembled themselves—Sandoval; a young surveyor named Pilar whose frostbitten fingers ended at the second knuckle; two brothers, the Okafors, who had been the team's riggers and now moved like old men though they couldn't have been past forty. Five left, out of fourteen who'd ridden up the escarpment trail in the spring two years ago.

"We heard the radio," Isandro said quietly. "Three days ago. Someone broke through the jamming."

Pilar laughed—a dry, disbelieving sound. "Broke through. We've been transmitting every week since the first winter. On schedule, like church." She lifted her ruined hand. "We built the aerial ourselves. We figured someone upstream was listening and choosing not to answer. Then three days ago somebody answered back, and I nearly fell off the ledge."

"That was me," Ansel said. "I've been carrying a repaired field set. I found the frequency in the ledger."

"The ledger." Sandoval's head came up sharply. "Whose ledger?"

"The concession company's. Stolen from their district office. Water rights falsified going back nine years. And your expedition—" Ansel's throat tightened. "Written off as lost. All fourteen names. Filed under natural causes before anyone ever went looking."

Around the fire, the survivors exchanged looks that carried two years inside them. It was the youngest Okafor who spoke, his voice flat as the salt flats. "We knew. Once the resupply didn't come and the passes stayed quiet, we knew they weren't coming. You don't forget fourteen men up a mountain. You forget them only if you want to."

"They wanted to," Ansel said.

"They wanted the water," Sandoval corrected. "That's the thing you have to understand, girl. The concession's whole claim rests on those rights being exercised—water drawn, acreage served. If the survey found the aquifer mapped wrong, if the yields came in short, the permits die. So the survey had to either lie or disappear." He smiled, and there was no humor in it, only an old exhausted contempt. "We wouldn't lie. Tomás wouldn't lie. So we disappeared."

* * *

It was after that, while the Okafors fed the fire and the wind outside rose to a long wolfish howl, that Sandoval asked to see her ledger. Ansel unstrapped it from her pack—the concession book, water-stained at one corner, heavy as a brick—and put it in his hands.

He turned the pages slowly, lips moving over figures he clearly already knew, and then he stopped, and his hands began to shake.

"This isn't the only copy," he said.

Ansel went still. "What?"

"There were two books. Company rule—one for the office, one for the field, so the clerks could reconcile against our entries. That's how they caught us out, see. Our field book told the truth. Their office book told the story they'd already sold downstream." He looked up at her, and his eyes were burning now, fever-bright or grief-bright, impossible to say. "When we understood what was happening—that no one was coming—we hid ours. Tomás kept it. He said—" Sandoval's voice caught, steadied. "He said if his sister ever came looking, she'd know where."

The cave tilted. Ansel heard herself ask, very calmly, "Where," and heard the calm crack down the middle of the word.

Sandoval reached beneath his bedroll and drew out a oilcloth packet, soft with handling, tied with wire. He set it in her lap with both hands, the way you hand over something sacred or dangerous, and sometimes both.

"Under the third cairn on the ridge saddle," he said. "But he moved it, last autumn, when the rockfall shifted. Into the lining of my coat, after—" He stopped. Started again. "After he died, I kept it. Two years I've slept on it. My hip has a dent shaped like a book."

Ansel untied the wire with fingers that would not obey her. Inside the oilcloth: a surveyor's field ledger, canvas-bound, corners worn round, and across the cover in pencil, in a hand she knew better than her own—knew from grocery lists and letters home and the margins of her schoolbooks when he'd helped her study—

*T. Vela — Field Book No. 2 — If found, tell Ansel.*

She opened it and her brother's handwriting walked toward her line by line, day by day, and the cave, the fire, the wind—all of it receded until there was only the page.

*May 14. Aquifer readings confirm the concession maps are fiction. Yields less than a third of filed estimates. Told Sandoval we report honestly or not at all.*

*June 2. Resupply overdue 40 days. Radio answered once tonight by a voice that would not identify itself, then silence. Pilar thinks the band is jammed. I think she's right.*

*July 19. Rationing to half. Men are angry. I am angrier. Somebody signed our death warrant in triplicate and called it weather.*

*August 30. Fourteen of us. Eleven of us. Burying men takes longer than surveying.*

*September 12. If anyone reads this: we did not get lost. We were left. The water rights are false, the maps are false, and the men who signed them are warm and fed tonight while we eat snow.*

*Last entry, October 3, the writing gone thin and wavering:* Tell Ansel the truth keeps. Truth keeps better than we did.

Ansel did not remember starting to cry. She only became aware of it the way you become aware of rain—suddenly, everywhere, long after it began. She held the book against her chest with both arms, and around her the fire popped, and nobody told her to be quiet, and Isandro Marchetti sat across the flames with his hat in his hands and his head bowed, a ranger watching the law he served stand revealed in its own handwriting.

* * *

Later, when the fire had been rebuilt and the shaking had slowed, Sandoval leaned close. His breath rattled like gravel in a tin cup.

"How far to the valley?" he asked.

"Four days down, if the weather holds. Three if it doesn't hold and we take the gorge."

"And the permits?"

"Expire at season's end. If the ledger reaches the land board before renewal, the concession dies." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "There's an injunction filed. Riders on the southern pass. The company knows what we're carrying."

Sandoval nodded slowly. Then he did something that frightened her more than the riders, more than the storm: he began pulling on his boots.

"Foreman," Pilar said sharply. "Your leg."

"My leg has been dying for eighteen months," Sandoval said. "It can finish dying on the move." He gripped the cave wall and stood, swaying, upright, terrible and magnificent in the firelight. "Two years we waited for someone to come. Now someone has. I'll not greet them sitting down." He looked around the fire at each of them—the Okafors, Pilar, the embers reflected in five pairs of eyes. "Pack everything. The book goes down the mountain tomorrow. All of us go with it."

One of the Okafors hesitated. "And if the mountain says no?"

"Then the mountain buries us beside the truth instead of above it," Sandoval said. "Either way, we stop hiding."

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does before a real storm gathers its full voice—and in that sudden, ringing silence, Ansel heard it: voices on the wind, distant, below them on the switchbacks. Not the wind's making.

Riders. Climbing.
