## 9. Race Before the Passes Close

The radio woke them an hour before dawn, and it was not the voice Isandro had been listening for.

He had kept the set tuned to the valley band all night, wrapped in his coat against the cold coming off the glacier, and when the crackle resolved into speech he sat up so fast his spine cracked like a green branch. The voice was clipped, official, a man reading from paper: *By order of the land board, sitting in emergency session at the petition of the Meridian Concession Company, all persons bearing documents pertaining to concession 114 are enjoined from—*

Ansel was already awake. She had been awake, he suspected, most of the night, sitting with her back to the cave wall and her brother's ledger open on her knees like a book of scripture she could not stop rereading.

"They filed," she said.

"They filed." Isandro turned the volume down until the injunction became weather, distant and meaningless. "Which means they know we're coming down."

"Which means someone in the valley is still listening too." She closed the ledger. Her hands were cracked from the cold, the knuckles split, and she flexed them as though testing whether they would hold what she meant to ask of them. "Injunctions take a day to serve. A day to ride out to the southern pass and stand there looking official. We can't beat them through the pass."

"No."

"But there's another way down."

Isandro looked at her. Above them the cave mouth framed a sky going from black to iron, and beyond that the escarpment fell away in shelves and hanging snowfields, and somewhere below all of it the river ran—the same river whose water rights were falsified line by line in the book she carried, running now with autumn melt, running high.

"The gorge," he said.

"The gorge."

"The gorge killed survey crews before your brother was born."

"And the pass has company riders in it, and behind us the weather is closing like a door." Ansel stood, stiffly, and looked down the length of the cave where the survivors lay in their row of bedrolls—seven of them now that the dying foreman, Voss, had insisted on being helped upright, seven people who had spent two years above the snowline waiting for the world to remember them. "I didn't climb this mountain to be served papers at the bottom of it."

* * *

They broke camp in the grey half-light, and it went faster than Isandro believed it would, because the survivors moved like people who had rehearsed leaving for two years. Packs had been packed since spring. Ropes were coiled and hung. The woman called Reyes—who had been the expedition's medic and had kept seven hearts beating on starvation rations and melted snow—distributed the last of their food without ceremony, dividing it by weight rather than want, and no one argued.

Voss came last. He could not have weighed more than ninety pounds; the mountain had eaten him slowly and left the frame. But he walked to the cave mouth on Reyes's arm and stood looking down at the clouds stacked against the lower slopes, and when Isandro offered to carry him he refused with a shake of the head that cost him visibly.

"I'll walk to the edge," Voss said. "After that you can drag me. I've been dragged before."

"What's at the edge?" Isandro asked.

"The truth about the gorge." The old foreman's breath smoked. "You're thinking of the river route. Everyone thinks of it and everyone dies trying it, because they go where the water goes. You don't go where the water goes. There's a ledge—a maintenance cut, from when the company surveyed the dam site twenty years ago and then buried the survey because the rock wouldn't hold. It runs above the flood line for six miles. My crew cut steps into it." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile, all grief and iron. "We cut them hoping someone would come up. Now we'll use them going down. The company built our road out after all. They just never signed the paperwork."

Ansel laughed—a short, hard sound with nothing of humor in it—and slung the ledger's oilskin wrap across her chest. "Then let's not keep them waiting."

* * *

The first three miles were the kind of descent that punished pride. Snow had fallen in the night and lay in soft treacherous pillows over scree, and each of them kicked steps for the one behind, and the rope went from wrist to wrist down the whole line of nine so that no single fall could be a private matter. Isandro led. He had decided that without discussing it, and Ansel had let him, and between them they carried the injured man, Teller, whose ankle had gone bad in the scout attack, lashed in a litter of pack frames between two stronger backs.

By midmorning the snow gave over to rain, and by noon the rain had found the river.

They heard it before they saw it—a sound like continuous thunder rolled thin, like the range clearing its throat—and then the trees opened and there it was below them: the gorge, deep and green-black, the river swollen with melt and storm, white water standing up in ridges like the hackles of some animal too large to name.

"There." Voss pointed with a chin that trembled from more than cold. A seam in the far cliff face, a horizontal line cut by hand and time, vanishing downstream into spray. "The ledge starts across the ford. And the ford—" He stopped.

Isandro finished it for him. "The ford is under that."

The crossing should have taken an hour. It took five.

They went in pairs and threes, roped to an anchor Reyes drove into the bank with a piton hammer that had crossed the escarpment twice. The water hit at the thigh and then the waist and then, in the channel's heart, at the chest, and it did not push so much as *decide*, the way stone decides, and every crossing was a negotiation conducted in held breath. Isandro went first with the rope, and the river took his feet twice and gave them back grudgingly, and when he dragged himself onto the far rocks he lay there a moment with the water peeling off him in sheets, listening to his own heart argue.

Then Ansel came, with the ledger held high and dry inside her jacket, and she crossed the way she did everything—jaw set, eyes forward, as if the river were a bureaucracy she intended to outlast.

Then the others, one by one. Teller, hauled across half-floating, screaming once when the litter swung and his ankle struck a boulder. Reyes, who sang—actually sang, low and steady, some work song from the lowland orchards—to keep the rhythm in the rope-line, and the song did more than any command could have. Old Ferro, who wept openly from fear and crossed anyway. The boy, Pell, seventeen and frost-scarred, who had been a cook's assistant when the expedition went in and was now the strongest climber among them, and who went last of the survivors because he insisted on carrying double.

And Voss.

They tried to make it easy for him. They rigged the litter again, offered to float him, and he shook his head and gripped the rope with both ruined hands and stepped into the current like a man stepping into his own history. Halfway across the river rose—it came down the gorge in a brown shoulder, some tributary unloading its storm—and it took him off his feet, and the whole rope-line snapped taut, nine bodies leaning into the pull, strangers and thieves and rangers and ghosts all leaning together, and Isandro felt it then, unmistakably, the thing he would remember for the rest of his life: the weight of every one of them arriving in his hands at once, and holding.

They pulled him out coughing on the far bank, and he lay on the stones and laughed until it turned to something else, and Reyes knelt over him and did not say what her face said.

"We're close," Voss managed. "Two miles of ledge. Cut steps the whole way. I counted them every morning for two years. Four hundred and eleven." He gripped Isandro's wrist. "Count them with me. I want to hear it out loud, just once, going down instead of up."

* * *

The ledge held.

It was narrow—two feet in places, eighteen inches in others—and wet, and the river roared sixty feet below with a patience that felt personal. They went in single file, rope running the whole length, and the steps Voss's crew had cut were there exactly as promised, worn but true, each one chiseled with a small mark like a tally. Ansel, bringing up the rear, touched each mark as she passed. Somewhere around the two-hundredth step Isandro heard her begin to count under her breath, matching Voss's cracked voice ahead of her, and soon the whole line was counting, quietly, a litany against the roar: *two-sixty-one, two-sixty-two,* boots finding stone cut by dead men for living ones.

At step three-ninety the ledge ended.

Not collapsed—*ended*, sheared away by some old slide, a gap of perhaps twelve feet with the river boiling underneath. Beyond it the final steps continued, visible, mocking.

Rope, then. They had one length long enough. Isandro anchored it, swung it across, and Pell went first, hand over hand above the flood, and made the far side gasping. Then the others, one at a time, the pendulum of each swing carrying them out over the water while the line below counted and prayed in the same breath.

Teller went across unconscious, which was mercy. Reyes went singing. Ferro went with his eyes shut. Voss went last of the survivors, swinging slow, and landed on the far side and turned around and looked back at the gap and at Isandro and Ansel still on the near side, and his face in the grey light was suddenly urgent.

"The ledger," he called over the river. "Send it next. Not yourselves—the *ledger*. If only one thing crosses this gorge today, it's that."

Ansel unwrapped the oilskin without hesitation. She weighted it, tied it fast, and Isandro swung it out, and it arced over the gap—

—and the rope, frayed by eight crossings against raw rock, parted at the anchor.

No one screamed. That was the strangest part, the thing Isandro would carry: the silence of it, nine people watching the ledger drop toward the white water, and the only sound the river itself, patient, certain, opening to receive the truth of concession 114 the way it had received everything else the mountain ever let fall.

Then Pell moved.

He went over the edge of the far ledge without a rope, without a word, diving—not falling, *diving*—and caught the oilskin bundle one-handed in midair, an arm's length above the flood, and slammed into the water and was gone, and the counting stopped, and the world narrowed to the place where he had vanished.

Three seconds. Five. The river shrugged and rolled on.

Then a hand broke the surface at the eddy below the slide-rock, gripping the ledger high, and Pell's head came up after it, streaming, alive, laughing—a wild, impossible laugh that the whole rope-line answered with a sound that was not quite cheering and not quite weeping.

Ansel's knees went. Isandro caught her elbow, and for a moment neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to add to that.

"He caught it," she finally said, stupidly, wonderfully. "He actually—"

"Get across," Isandro said, and pushed the spare rope into her hands, and his own voice was not entirely steady. "Because I just realized something, and I need you across this gap before I say it out loud."

She looked at him—at the set of his jaw, at the direction his eyes had gone, downstream, where the gorge widened toward the valley and where, faint through the rain and the roar, carried on the wind off the low country, came a sound none of them wanted to hear.

Engines. More than one. Coming up the valley road toward the gorge mouth.

The company hadn't only blocked the southern pass. They'd thought of the river too.
