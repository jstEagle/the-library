## 10. What the Valley Decides

The gorge gave them the valley the way a river gives up its dead—slowly, grudgingly, in pieces.

Isandro felt it before he saw it: the change in the air, the way the wind that had screamed down the canyon all morning suddenly lost its teeth. The water they'd been hauling through for two days shallowed around their knees instead of their waists, and the walls of gray granite fell back on either side like hands opening. Ahead, through the last stand of cottonwoods gone gold at the crown, the valley floor spread out flat and brown and ordinary, and Isandro had to stop walking just to look at it.

He had crossed that floor a hundred times. He had ridden it on patrol, bored, half-asleep in the saddle, thinking of nothing but his next meal and whether the radio static would clear by nightfall. He had never once thought of it as a country worth fighting to reach. Now it looked like the far shore of an ocean.

Behind them, high up where the gorge pinched into white water, the weather was coming down. He could hear it—a low continuous roar, not thunder, not the river, but snow sliding off a thousand ledges at once, the passes closing like a book. Ansel stood beside him with her brother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, both arms around it the way you hold a child, and she was listening too.

"It's done," she said. "Nobody's following us now."

"Nobody was following us since the ford," Isandro said. "Vasquez's riders turned back when the river came up." He didn't say what they were both thinking: that the company didn't need riders anymore. That whatever happened next would happen in rooms, on paper, in the slow bloodless language of permits and renewals—and that the ledger under her arms was the only weapon that spoke that language.

The survivors came up behind them in a strung-out line, roped together, moving the way people move when they have been moving too long and have passed through exhaustion into something quieter. Nine of them. Two years above the snowline, and nine still breathing. The foreman, Okafor, rode lashed to a litter between two mules because his lungs had begun to drown in their own fluid three days ago and he refused, absolutely refused, to let that stop him from being present at whatever came next. His eyes were open. They had been open the whole way down.

* * *

The land board sat in the old courthouse on the valley's one street, a building with a false front and a bell tower that hadn't rung since the company bought the fire brigade. Word traveled faster than they did; by the time they walked up the boardwalk, half the town was on the porches, and the other half was already inside, standing along the walls because every chair was taken.

Isandro had imagined this moment for six days—for longer than that, if he was honest, since the dry well, since Ansel had opened the ledger and shown him the columns of names written off as line items. In his imagination there had been shouting. There had been the company's lawyer, sleek as a wet stone, and the board members looking at their hands, and some procedural wall that truth could not climb.

There was none of that. There was a room that smelled of dust and woodstove smoke, and a chairman named Reyes who had known Isandro's father, and a silence so complete that when Okafor asked to be propped upright in his chair, the sound of the mule harness creaking as they lifted him carried to the back wall.

Ansel unwrapped the ledger and set it on the table, and the oilcloth made a sound like a shot in all that quiet.

She read for forty minutes. She read the falsified gauge readings, the water rights filed against flows that had never existed, the diversion schedules that starved the lower valley so the upper concession could look green on paper. She read the requisition orders—the ones showing the company knew the expedition was stranded above the snowline in the first week of October, two years ago, and chose to file them as lost rather than fund a rescue that would have exposed the illegal diversions the expedition had surveyed. She read her brother's name. She read it in the margin of a supply manifest, in Okafor's hand: *R. Vidal insists we keep surveying. Says the numbers will matter someday. I believe him.*

Her voice held until that line. Then it didn't, and nobody in the room moved, and Isandro stood against the wall with his badge heavy on his chest and understood that he was watching something rarer than justice happen. He was watching the record change. Not a verdict—verdicts could be appealed, bought, waited out. A record. The plain fact of what had occurred, entered into the book where the valley kept itself.

The company's man arrived at the hour mark. Isandro recognized him from the ford—one of Vasquez's lieutenants, out of uniform, carrying an injunction signed by a judge two hundred miles away who had never seen the escarpment. He laid it on the table beside the ledger and said the words *irregularly obtained* and *chain of custody* and *the board has no jurisdiction*, and Reyes let him finish, and then Reyes said:

"Son, the permits expire Friday. Renewal requires a finding that the concession operated within its rights. Look around this room and tell me how you think that finding goes."

The lieutenant looked. He looked at Okafor, upright in his chair, breathing in shallow whistles. He looked at the nine survivors ranged along the wall, sunken-eyed, frost-scarred, alive. He looked at the standing-room crowd of farmers and ditch-tenders and widows, and among them, Isandro knew, were mothers and fathers of the eleven who had not come down. Then he picked up his injunction, folded it once, and left without another word, and outside the window they could see him standing in the street for a long moment before he walked toward the telegraph office—not to fight, Isandro thought, but to tell Vasquez the war was over.

* * *

Friday came gray and windless. The permits expired at noon, unrenewed, and at ten minutes past noon the company's flag came down over the pump station on the north bench, and nobody put anything else up, and that was somehow the rightest thing Isandro had ever watched a piece of cloth do.

It was also, he understood, the moment the law remembered him.

Sheriff Duarte found him at the corral behind the boarding house, where he was checking hooves that didn't need checking. Duarte was sixty and had held his office through the company's best years by perfecting the art of being elsewhere, and he came at Isandro now with his hat in his hands and his eyes anywhere but Isandro's face.

"Warrant came down on the telegraph this morning," Duarte said. "Theft of corporate documents. They want the woman held for transport when the pass reopens."

"When the pass reopens," Isandro repeated.

"April. Maybe May." Duarte turned his hat in his hands. "You brought her in, Marchetti. You're the ranger of record. It's your—" He stopped. He looked at the corral rail. "I'm not going to pretend I don't know what you're going to say."

"She saved nine lives off that mountain," Isandro said. "And the eleven that didn't come down—the only reason anybody will ever know their names is that paper she carried. Two years the company wrote them off as weather. She carried them out on her back through a blizzard." He took a breath. "I've worn this badge eight years, Amos. I believed in it. I still believe in what it's supposed to be. But it wasn't supposed to be the company's errand boy, and if arresting her is the job, then the job and I are finished."

"You'll lose your pension."

"I'll lose my pension."

Duarte nodded slowly, the way men nod at funerals. Then he held out his hand, palm up, and waited, and Isandro understood what he was asking—that it be given, not taken. That the surrender be a choice, witnessed, on the record.

Isandro unpinned the badge. The brass was cold and lighter than it should have been after eight years; he had expected it to weigh like an anchor and it weighed like a coin. He set it in Duarte's palm.

"For what it's worth," Duarte said, closing his fingers over it, "the board met this morning before the warrant came. They voted to hire a new ranger for the valley. Someone local. Someone who knows the escarpment routes." He almost smiled. "Pay's worse. Paperwork's worse. But the oath's shorter, and it doesn't mention the company at all."

* * *

They buried the eleven on the south slope in the first week of the thaw-that-wasn't, while the high country still smoked white above the tree line. The whole valley came. Ansel read her brother's final entries aloud, the ones from the cave, the ones that had confirmed everything the ledger promised—dates, gauge readings, the decision made in a warm office two hundred miles away to let cold do the company's work. When she finished, Okafor, who had survived long enough to see the permits voided and died four days later with the window open and the mountain in view, was lowered into the ground beside the men he had tried to bring home.

Afterward the crowd drained away toward the valley, toward food and woodsmoke and the ordinary business of a place learning to govern itself again, and Isandro stayed on the slope with Ansel, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

The light was doing what desert light does at the end of an afternoon—pouring gold across the flats, turning the salt pans to hammered metal, laying the shadows of the two of them out long and thin across the graves. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and quit. The wind came up the slope smelling of sage and coming snow.

"They'll want you for the new ranger," Ansel said finally. "Reyes told me. He wanted to know if I'd vouch for you."

"What did you tell him?"

"That you chased me three days across the salt flats and never once cut corners." She looked at him sideways, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—not grief, not defiance, just a tired, unguarded thing, almost young. "That you stopped at every camp I'd left water at and drank it anyway, knowing it might be poisoned, because you'd decided I wasn't the kind of person who poisoned wells. You decided that about me before you knew anything true about me. You just decided it."

"You left water at every camp," Isandro said. "I figured a thief who shares his water in a drought has a reason for what he stole."

"And if I hadn't?"

"Then I'd have arrested you at the well and been wrong, and the eleven would still be a line item." He shrugged. "The badge didn't teach me that. The desert did. The badge just got in the way."

She laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first he'd heard from her, and it seemed to surprise her more than him. Then she sat down in the grass between the graves, deliberately, heavily, the way you sit when you have finally, actually stopped, and after a moment Isandro sat down beside her.

Below them the valley went on lighting its lamps, one by one, unhurried, as though it had all the time in the world—as though, for the first time in either of their memories, it did. The first stars came out over the escarpment, over the switchbacks and the ledge route and the cave above the snowline, and the mountain held its snow and said nothing, keeping its own records now, needing no one.

"I don't know how to rest," Ansel said. It wasn't a complaint. It was a confession, offered plainly, the way she'd offered everything else.

"No," Isandro agreed. His whole body ached in places he hadn't known he owned, and somewhere below someone was frying onions, and tomorrow there would be paperwork, and a short oath, and a valley full of ditches and disputes and small honest troubles that belonged entirely to the people who lived among them. "Neither do I."

He leaned back on his hands and watched the lamps come on.

"We'll figure it out," he said. "We've got until spring."
