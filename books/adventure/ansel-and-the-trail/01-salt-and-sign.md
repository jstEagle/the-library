## 1. Salt and Sign

The Ashen Flats did not forgive trackers. They barely tolerated the living.

Mara Okafor had crossed them eleven times in her years as a ranger, and each crossing felt like walking through the memory of a fire—grey dust to the horizon, fine as flour, that rose in slow ghosts around her boots and settled again on her shoulders like a second skin. The sun here was a white coin behind a veil of ash-haze, casting no shadows worth reading. Wind came in long, low breaths from the south, carrying the smell of salt and old stone and, faintly, of the river she could not yet see.

She knelt where the ground told her a story.

Three days old, this print—no, two and a half, the edges still crisp where the wind hadn't yet chewed them soft. A woman's boot, narrow heel, weight rolled slightly outward on the left foot. Favoring an old ankle injury, or developing one. Carrying perhaps forty pounds, judging by the depth of the impression and the way the dust had compressed in a single sharp press rather than a rolling stride. Moving fast. Moving alone.

Moving *deliberately*.

Mara rose and walked on, letting her eyes do what they had always done better than her words: read. A broken salt-crust shelf two hundred paces on, snapped at knee height—a handhold, used once, not twice. A scatter of chert flakes beside a dead fire ring, knapped in haste but with a skilled hand. A single strand of dark wool caught on a thorn of greasewood, dyed the deep indigo that came from only one place: the river towns downstream, where the Kessarin still ran shallow and bitter with whatever the guild allowed it to carry.

The thief was heading north. Toward the source.

Everyone else who had looked at this trail—and there had been others; Mara had found their spoor too, guild men in matched boots moving in nervous clusters—had lost it within the first day. The Flats erased everything. But trails were not only marks in dust. They were choices, and choices left patterns, and patterns could be predicted by anyone willing to think like the person making them.

Mara thought like the thief now. Thirsty, alone, heading for the canyon country where water still moved. She would travel at dawn and dusk, rest through the killing midday under whatever shade the land offered—which meant the leeward side of the basalt ribs that broke the Flats like the spine of some buried animal. She would ration, which meant she would stop at the seep Mara herself had used on her last crossing, the one hidden beneath a tilted slab of sandstone that no guild map bothered to mark.

Mara reached the seep at dusk. The sand around it was disturbed—but not by drinking.

Someone had arranged stones there. Seven of them, small and grey, set in a curve against the slab's base. To any other eye: nothing. Scree. Wind-work.

To Mara's eye, it was a sentence.

Seven stones, curved like a crescent opening north—the old ranger sign for *follow*. But beneath it, half-buried, a single chip of blue-glazed pottery. River-town ware. And pressed into the dust beside it, deliberate as handwriting, the print of a thumb dragged twice through wet clay: two lines, parallel.

The sign for *Okafor*.

Mara stood very still. Around her the dusk gathered, purple and cold, and somewhere far off a rock wren said its dry piece and fell silent.

No one used that sign anymore. It belonged to the old training codes, the ones the guild had retired a decade ago when they streamlined their signals, when they stopped teaching rangers to leave messages for each other and started teaching them to leave none at all. *Invisibility is safety*, the new manuals said. Mara had helped write the revision. She had argued for it, eloquently, in the guildhall at Saren Ford, and she had gone home afterward and not slept, because she understood exactly what she had voted to bury.

Only three people alive had learned the old signs before they were retired. One was dead. One was Sefu Adeyemi, who was many things but never subtle, and who would have simply sent a letter.

Which left the person standing the prints' owner: a woman, favoring her left ankle, moving fast toward the glacier country, leaving messages in retired code addressed to Mara Okafor by name.

*Someone wants to be found.*

Mara crouched and touched the stones. They were warm still, holding the day's heat. The thief had passed within hours.

Her first instinct—the instinct she had trusted for ten years, the instinct that had made her name and kept her alive—was to turn around. Being found was a door she had nailed shut long ago, board by board, and she knew precisely what waited on the other side of it. Not danger. Danger was simple. What waited was *recognition*: someone standing in front of her holding up a mirror made of her own past, saying *I know you, I know who you were before you decided to be no one.*

She had spent a decade perfecting the art of vanishing—not just from pursuers but from memory itself. No home. No letters. Crews assembled for a season and dissolved without farewell. Even her name, spoken aloud, felt like something borrowed. And now some ghost from before the nailing-shut was out here in the ash, leaving her breadcrumbs, calling her by a sign that hadn't been used since—

Since the last time she'd trusted someone enough to be known.

Mara filled her flask at the seep, watched the water run thin and mineral-bright over her wrist, and made herself think it through like a professional, because the professional was all she had left.

If the thief wanted to be caught, why steal the ledger at all? Why run north into country that killed the unprepared? Unless the running wasn't flight. Unless it was an invitation with teeth—an itinerary written in bootprints, designed so that only one tracker in the world could follow it, and that tracker would arrive at the end of the trail already committed, already deep, already unable to turn back without knowing.

It was elegant. It was patient. It was, she admitted with a cold twist in her chest, exactly the kind of thing *she* would have done, ten years ago, to get a message to someone who refused to receive letters.

Which narrowed the field further than she wanted it narrowed.

Mara banked her small fire in a hollow of stone, ate hardtack and dried figs, and lay watching the veiled stars wheel. Sleep came in shallow pulls. In the grey hour before dawn she dreamed of a training yard in Saren Ford, of a younger woman laughing and dragging her thumb through mud—*two lines, Okafor, so even the wind knows your name*—and woke with grit in her teeth and her heart going hard.

She broke camp before light.

* * *

Two more days. Two more sentences in stone and broken branch.

At the basalt ribs, the trail grew bolder—or its author did. A cairn built wrong on purpose, three stones stacked with the top one split, the old sign for *hurry*. A greasewood branch stripped and laid pointing north-northeast, toward the Kessarin's upper canyons rather than the easier eastern route. And once, pressed into a pan of baked-hard mud beside a dry wash, a full handprint. Left hand. Small scar across the base of the thumb, pale and old.

Mara looked at that handprint for a long time.

She knew hands the way priests knew scripture. She had held that one, once, across a map table, both of them arguing about a patrol route while the guildhall bells rang for some festival neither of them cared about. She knew the scar. She had asked about it, years ago, and gotten a laugh and a deflection, and had loved the deflection, and had hated herself for the loving.

*Nara.* The name surfaced like a body coming up through dark water.

Nara Vess, who had stood beside her in the yard and in the field and finally, catastrophically, in the guildhall the day the revision passed—the day Mara chose the new silence over the old language, over her, over everything they might have been. Nara Vess, who had walked out of Saren Ford the same week and out of every record thereafter, so thoroughly that Mara had let herself believe she was dead, because believing that was easier than believing she had simply learned to vanish better than Mara ever had.

And now she was two days ahead, favoring an ankle, carrying a stolen ledger toward a glacier, and signing her work like a woman with nothing left to lose except being found by the wrong person.

Or the right one. Mara couldn't tell anymore which fear was larger.

She climbed the last rib of basalt as the sun went down behind the haze, and from the crest she saw it: far north, a thin silver thread stitched down the throat of the world. The Kessarin, running out of the high country toward towns that were dying of its absence. Beyond it, hazed to a rumor, the white shoulders of the glacier country, where calving season would soon close every route like a fist.

Campfire smoke rose from the canyon mouth below—one fire, small, shielded. Careless by ordinary standards. Deliberate by hers.

Mara sat down on the cold stone and did not descend. She watched the smoke until full dark, feeling the whole decade of her careful invisibility press on her like the ash that covered everything, fine and grey and impossible to brush away entirely.

Tomorrow she would walk down into that canyon and come face to face with the one person alive who could say *I know you* and mean all of it. Tomorrow the quarry would turn around, and there would be no more signs, no more code, no more distance.

Tonight, she allowed herself one last hour of being no one.

She took out the scrap of blue-glazed pottery she'd carried since the seep, turned it in her fingers, and almost laughed. Ten years of erasing herself, undone by seven stones and a thumbprint in mud.

Below, the fire flickered. The river murmured in the dark, keeping whatever secrets it kept.

Mara slept badly, and at first light she went down to be found.
