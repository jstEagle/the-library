## 2. The Rival's Ledger

The border town of Halvatt had two streets, three wells, and one rule: nobody asked anybody anything. Mara liked it the way she liked most places—briefly, and from a distance.

She came in at dusk with the dust of the Ashen Flats still in her teeth, paid for a room at the Wayhouse of the Two Rivers with coin that had no history, and was halfway through a bowl of barley soup when Sefu Adeyemi sat down across from her without being invited.

He looked older. That was the first thing. The second thing was that he'd found her anyway.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said, not looking up from her soup.

"So are you." He turned his cup over on the table, an old ranger's signal—no drink, no time, talk only. "Funny how many dead people there are in this business."

Mara kept eating. The soup was bad, oversalted, but it was hot, and heat was worth more than taste out here. Around them the wayhouse murmured its evening murmur: freight haulers arguing about axle grease, a pair of well-diggers asleep in their chairs, a child chasing a moth against the lamp glass. Nobody looked at Sefu. That was deliberate. He'd always had a talent for making himself unremarkable when he wanted to be, which had annoyed her for fifteen years, because he was not actually remarkable-looking at all—broad, gray-shot, weathered like everyone else—and yet somehow he filled every room he entered.

"Three days," he said. "That's how long it took me to find you. Three days, after ten years of nothing."

"Then you weren't looking hard enough before."

"I wasn't looking at all before." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register rangers used for things that mattered, low and even, pitched to carry exactly one table's distance and no further. "Someone stole a ledger from the guild archive at Kessarin Hold."

Mara set down her spoon.

* * *

Ten years earlier, in the training yard at Saltmere, under a sky the color of hammered tin, Sefu Adeyemi had stood over her with a practice staff and told her the truth about herself as though it were a diagnosis.

"You vanish," he'd said. "Not just in the field. In the yard, in the mess hall, in your own head. I give you an order and you're gone before the words finish leaving my mouth. You're the best tracker we've produced in a generation, Okafor, and you are going to waste it, because you think disappearing is the same thing as surviving."

"And you think standing still is the same thing as strength," she'd said, and swept his legs out from under him.

He'd lain in the dust a moment, breathing, then laughed—that short, unwilling laugh he used when something hurt him and pleased him at once. "There it is," he said from the ground. "You know what the founders wrote? First charter, before they sealed it away? *A ranger is a name given, not a name taken.* We exist because the river towns need us to exist. Visibility isn't vanity, Mara. It's the whole job."

"Visibility gets people killed."

"Invisibility gets rivers killed." He'd gotten up, dusted off his knees, and looked at her with that expression she hated—the one that said he could see all the way down into her and wasn't impressed by what he found there. "One day you'll have to choose which failure you can live with. I hope, for your sake, you choose slowly."

They hadn't spoken since the year she left the guild. Ten years. And now here he was in Halvatt, turning his cup over on a scarred table, asking her to come home by never once using the word *home*.

* * *

"A ledger," Mara repeated now. She pushed the soup away. Her appetite had gone somewhere cold. "What kind of ledger?"

"The old kind. Water rights. Grants, transfers, signatures." Sefu's eyes were steady on hers. He wasn't performing reluctance; he was performing calm, which was worse. "It covers the founding decades. It shows who the guild sold rights to, when, and what they took in exchange."

"That archive is sealed. Warded. Nobody walks out of Kessarin Hold with a founding ledger."

"Somebody did. Eleven days ago. And now the guild council has convened an emergency session, and the archivists have been dismissed from the lower vaults, and two wardens I've known my whole life have stopped answering letters." He let that sit. Outside, the wind came down off the flats and worried the shutters of the wayhouse, a dry rattling like bones in a sack. "They're going to bury it, Mara. Whatever's in that book, they'd rather burn the past than face it. And if they find the thief first, we'll never know what we lost."

"*We*," Mara said. "Interesting word, coming from you."

Sefu's jaw tightened. There it was—the resentment, surfacing like a stone in shallow water. Fifteen years of it, going both directions. He'd reported her disappearance as desertion rather than resignation, she'd learned later; a small cruelty, or maybe a large kindness, depending on how you read it, because a deserter was hunted and a ghost was merely mourned. She had never decided which he'd intended.

"You want to say it," he said. "So say it."

"You spent a decade telling me the guild was worth belonging to. Worth being seen by. Now you come to a border town to hire a ghost, because the guild you venerated sold water out from under dying towns and you can't ask anyone inside its walls to help you prove it." She folded her hands on the table. "You need someone invisible, Sefu. You need exactly what you spent fifteen years telling me was a character flaw."

"Yes," he said simply.

She had prepared herself for argument. Argument she knew how to survive. The plain agreement knocked something loose in her chest, and she covered it by reaching for her cup and finding it empty.

"The thief went upstream," Sefu continued, quieter now. "Along the Kessarin. Toward the source."

"No one goes toward the source. The calving season starts within the month. After that, the shelf routes close until next spring—if they open again at all. The ice has been pulling back for years."

"I know the calendar, Mara. So does the thief, presumably. Which means either they don't care about dying up there, or they're in a hurry to reach whatever they think is waiting at the glacier." He drew something from inside his coat and laid it on the table between them: a strip of paper, copied small and neat in a clerk's hand. A fragment. She read it twice.

*...bound to serve every town upon the river, from source to salt, in perpetuity, and to hold the waters in trust...*

"What is this?"

"A page from the charter. The original charter—the one the founders sealed in the glacier vault forty years ago. This copy survived in a private collection. I bought it at considerable expense." His finger tapped the paper once. "*In perpetuity*, Mara. If the guild's own founding law says what I think it says, then every rights transfer in that stolen ledger is not just corruption. It's betrayal. Legal, documented, provable betrayal."

Mara looked at the fragment for a long time. The lamp flame bent in a draft, and the ink seemed to move, the old words swimming like fish under ice. From source to salt. Every town. She thought of the settlements she'd passed through in ten years of vanishing—the tanker queues, the bought magistrates, the wells gone brackish while guild barges ran fat and scheduled upriver. She had seen it. She had seen it and told herself it wasn't her river anymore.

Duty dressed up as choice, she thought. That was all this was. Sefu hadn't come to offer her a job; he'd come to make sure that when she refused, she'd know exactly what she was refusing.

"You hate me," she said. "You've hated me for ten years. Why me?"

Sefu was quiet long enough that the child chasing the moth bumped their table, apologized, darted off. When he spoke, his voice had roughened.

"Because whoever stole that ledger wanted it found. They left the archive warden alive. They left a trail a first-year could follow as far as the flats—and then the trail vanished, which means they're good, or they wanted to be caught by someone better." He met her eyes. "And there is no one better than you. There never was. That was never the problem between us, Mara. The problem was everything else."

* * *

She walked the edge of town that night, because sleep wouldn't come and because Halvatt's single street held too many ears. The moon was a thin paring over the eastern mesas. Down the dark slope, the Kessarin ran—a sound more than a sight, a cold muttering in the black, the only honest voice in the territory. It had run through every scene of her life: Saltmere's training yards, her father's orchard two days' walk downstream, the glacier she'd seen once from a ridge at nineteen and never forgotten, blue-white and patient as a closed fist.

Sefu found her at the ford, because of course he did. He'd always been able to find her when she didn't want finding. It was the one talent of his she'd never managed to resent properly, though God knew she'd tried.

"You haven't said no," he observed.

"You haven't asked."

"I'm asking." He stopped beside her at the water's edge. The current pulled at the stones with small persistent fingers. "Come with me as far as the hold. Help me pick up the trail. After that—" He spread his hands. "After that, choice. Real choice. I'm not the guild, Mara. I'm just a man who read a piece of paper and couldn't sleep afterward."

"The guild will call this treason."

"The guild called your leaving desertion. Their vocabulary has never been very accurate."

Despite herself—despite fifteen years and ten of silence—Mara felt her mouth twitch. She smothered it. Some things, once started, couldn't be stopped, and smiling at Sefu Adeyemi was one of them.

"I'll need a crew," she said. "People who can move unseen. A forger, if we're crossing warden country. A climber, for the shelf approaches. Someone who can cook trail food that won't kill us." She paused. "And someone who knows those waters. Really knows them. Not maps—waters."

Something flickered across Sefu's face, quick as a fish turning. "I might know a name," he said. Too quickly. Too smoothly.

Mara filed it away, the way she filed everything: in the deep cold place where trails waited to be followed. "Dawn," she said. "I leave at dawn. With or without you."

"With me," he said. "At dawn."

He walked back toward the wayhouse, his silhouette dissolving into the town's thin lamplight. Mara stayed at the ford a while longer, listening to the river talk to itself in the dark, saying things it had been saying since before the guild existed and would say long after.

From source to salt, the charter had promised. In perpetuity.

Upstream, somewhere in the high white silence, a thief was carrying a book full of broken promises toward a vault of ice—and the season that would seal the route forever had already begun to count itself down.

She had three crew members to find, and one of them, she suspected, would be the hardest conversation of her life.

Old Tam was going to say no. She was almost certain of it.

She was wrong.
