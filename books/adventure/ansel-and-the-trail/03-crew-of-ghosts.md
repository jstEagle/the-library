## 3. Crew of Ghosts

Ilya counted the money twice, then a third time, because the third count was the one that mattered. Not the amount—though it was generous, insultingly so—but the way it was folded. Rangers folded bills in thirds, crisp as scripture. Sefu Adeyemi had folded his in halves, soft and creased, the way merchants did, the way men who had stopped pretending to be anything but what they were.

He sat in the back room of the dye-house in Vell's Crossing with the coins and notes spread before him on a table scarred by forty years of vats and lye, and he thought about names. That was the trade, really. People thought forgery was about hands—about the wrist's arc, the nib's pressure, the little tremor of authenticity you could fake with a pinprick in the right place. But it was about names. A signature was only a name that had learned to bleed.

His own name had bled enough for one life. Ilya Voss had died eleven years ago in a customs house fire in Marrow Bay, along with a ledger of shipping manifests that would have hanged three harbor magistrates. The man who walked out of that fire wore a different face and answered to whatever the room required. He had been Ferro the pigment-broker. He had been Master Ellun, tutor in fine penmanship to the sons of garrison officers. For two years he had been nobody at all, which was the most restful name he'd ever worn.

And now Mara Okafor wanted him to be Ilya again.

She hadn't said it that way. She never said things that way. She had stood in his doorway at dusk with the ash-light behind her, looking like a woman who had walked out of a story people told to frighten children, and she'd said: *I need someone who can make paper lie.* And then, when he'd laughed: *Not for coin this time. For the river.*

The river. As if that meant something. As if a river could hold a debt.

But he had looked at her hands—scarred, steady, resting easy at her sides the way no guilty person's hands ever rested—and he had heard himself say yes before the better part of him could object. That was the trouble with Mara. She made refusal feel like a smallness you'd have to carry afterward, heavier than any risk.

He swept the money into a leather roll and began packing his kit. Inks first: the iron-gall that aged honestly, the walnut that lied beautifully, the aniline blues he kept wrapped in oilcloth because they were the color of confessions. Pens, nibs, blotters, the brass scale for weighing paper by its grain. Last, from a false bottom beneath the floorboards, a folder of blank credentials—warden's warrants, guild passes, a magistrate's travel writ—all empty, all waiting for names.

He paused over the warden's warrant. The guild's seal was a river splitting into three branches, embossed deep enough to catch candlelight like water catching sun. He had forged that seal four times in his old life, and each time he'd told himself the same thing: *paper doesn't drown anyone.* Paper just made drowning easier for other people.

Outside, the dye-house yard was loud with evening—the hiss of cooling vats, women calling the day's last colors across the drying lines, indigo and madder hanging like captured sky. Ilya pulled his hood up and became, provisionally, a man named Corren, a journeyman scribe heading upriver for work. Provisionally. Names were clothes; you changed them when they got dirty.

He was three streets gone before he admitted what had actually decided him. Not the river. Not even Mara.

It was that Sefu Adeyemi's money had been folded in halves—and Sefu Adeyemi had once signed a complaint against Ilya Voss that sent two innocent clerks to prison while the real thief walked. Ilya had spent a decade making sure no document bearing his true hand ever surfaced. Now he was going upstream, toward the source of every document there was, carrying a folder full of lies into the one place in the world where the truth had been sealed in ice.

Either it would bury him or free him. With Mara Okafor, those were usually the same road.

* * *

Dessa climbed the water tower at Vell's Crossing the way other people checked their reflection—not out of vanity, but to see who was looking back.

From the top, the town arranged itself honestly: flat roofs furred with dust, the canal cut through its middle running low and greenish, the guild tanker parked at the depot like a fat black beetle everyone pretended not to stare at. Beyond the rooftops, the Kessarin glittered in its channel, wide and slow here, deceptively generous. Upstream it narrowed into canyon country. Upstream was where they were going.

She hooked her elbow over the tower rail and let her boots dangle over ninety feet of nothing, which was the only altitude at which her thoughts ever settled.

Her mother had climbed too. Not towers—crates, scaffolds, the loading rig on the tanker line at Sorrel Ford, hauling herself up hand over hand because the foreman paid climbers by the height and Dessa's mother had a daughter to feed and a well that had gone dry the year the guild renegotiated the water rights. Dessa had been nine. She remembered the exact sound of the pump sucking air—that wet, choking gasp, like the town itself trying to breathe through a straw—and she remembered her mother coming home with rope burns and silver coins and saying, brightly, *we're lucky, we got picked for the tanker crews,* as if luck were a thing you could say fast enough to make true.

Her mother was dead now. The lungs, mostly. Dust and diesel and thirty years of climbing in the heat. And Sorrel Ford still stood, still pumped air, still queued its children at the tankers every morning with their tin cups, and the guild's magistrates still called it a model settlement in their reports.

So when Mara had come to her—no pleasantries, just a map unrolled across a tavern table and one finger pressed on the glacier at its head—Dessa had read the map upside down, the way climbers do, and said yes before Mara finished the sentence.

She hadn't told the others why. Let them think it was the money, or the climb itself—the glacier route was legendary among the few who talked about it at all, a vertical cathedral of blue ice that no one had scaled since the calving seasons turned murderous. Let them think she was chasing glory. Glory was a good disguise. She'd learned that watching her mother spend her whole life being called lucky.

Below her, a figure crossed the depot yard and stopped at the tanker. Guild gray coat. The driver handed down a manifest to the station clerk, and even from ninety feet Dessa could see the clerk's posture change—the little bow, the hat coming off. Bought towns bowed like that. She'd grown up inside the bow.

*We're going to take something from them,* she thought, testing the idea the way she tested a handhold, putting weight on it slowly, ready to drop away if it held nothing. *We're going to take the one thing they can't buy back.*

Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady at height. It was ground level where she shook.

She swung off the rail and descended the ladder rung by rung, fast, the wind rising around her, and at the bottom she looked upriver once—toward the canyons, toward the ice, toward whatever the founders had buried where the water was born—and felt the strange calm come over her that always preceded a fall or a flight.

* * *

Tam burned the soup.

He hadn't burned a soup in thirty years, and he stood over the pot in the crew's rented kitchen feeling the scorch-smell wrap around him like a hand on the back of the neck, and he understood that it wasn't the soup at all. It was the word *guild*, spoken aloud that afternoon in Mara's flat voice, that had put his hand wrong on the ladle ever since.

Forty years ago, Tamuel Ash had cooked for the founders' last expedition. He'd been young then, strong in the wrists, and he'd carried more than food up the Kessarin—he'd carried the sealed case itself for three days, strapped to his back above the cookpot, because the founders trusted no one else's shoulders on the ice. He had stood in the vault while they set the charter into the glacier's heart, and he had heard Warden-Founder Essik say the words Tam had never stopped hearing since:

*Let the ice keep our promise until we are worthy of it again.*

And Tam had believed—gods, how he had believed—that sealing it was mercy. That a guild founded to serve every town on the river could forget its founding gently, could grow its gray coats and its bought magistrates without the shame of its own first words hanging over it. Forgetting as kindness. He had cooked for wardens for twenty more years and told himself the story every morning over the porridge: *the ice remembers so we don't have to.*

Then the wells went dry. Then the tankers came. Then the children with the tin cups, and Tam, retired, cooking in charity kitchens downriver, ladling thin stew to towns his own hands had helped seal shut.

He scraped the burnt pot in silence and set out bowls anyway, because feeding people was the one prayer he still trusted. The others drifted in—Ilya first, precise and watchful, setting his kit down like a surgeon laying out instruments; then Dessa, smelling of height and wind, taking the corner seat with her back to the wall; then Nara's empty chair, which no one mentioned, because the thief was still ahead of them somewhere up the flats, leaving signs like breadcrumbs for a girl who hated being led.

Mara came last. She always came last. She stood in the doorway a moment, reading the room the way she read a trail, and Tam saw her notice the burnt smell and file it away without comment. That was the worst of her, sometimes—she missed nothing, and mercy was hers to give or withhold, and you never knew which until after.

"Eat," Tam said gruffly, serving the unburnt parts. "Upstream there'll be nothing but what I carry."

"You've been up there." It wasn't a question. Dessa said it quietly, watching him. "You knew the waystation markers on Mara's map. You touched them like old friends."

The kitchen went still. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

"I cooked for the guild," Tam said. The truth came out of him like a splinter, slow and sideways. "A long time ago. Before your mothers were climbing."

"Then you know the wardens' patrol signs," Ilya said, and his voice had gone smooth and professional, the voice of a man pricing a document. "Current ones. The countersigns."

"Some." Tam kept his eyes on the bowls. "The river changes its rules. So do the wardens."

"And the vault," Dessa said. "The glacier vault. Is it real?"

Tam looked at her then—at the climber's hunger in her face, the same hunger he'd seen on the founders when they spoke of sealing their promise away—and something in his chest cracked along an old seam.

"It's real," he said. "I helped close it."

Mara set down her spoon. Around the table, three ghosts looked at a fourth, and the fire ticked in the stove, and outside the wind came down off the flats carrying the faint mineral smell of snowmelt from a country none of them could see yet.

"Then tomorrow," Mara said, "you lead."

Tam nodded, because there was nothing left in him that could refuse, and because some traitor part of him—the part that had carried the case on its young strong back—had been walking upstream for forty years already, and was, at last, going home.
