## 4. Downriver Towns

The Kessarin ran thin as a lie by the time they reached Harrow Bend.

Dessa had known it would. She'd grown up on a river like this one, a hundred miles east and twenty years gone, and she knew the arithmetic of thirst: how a river that fed ten towns could be divided among three, and how the seven left over learned to stop asking why. But knowing was different from seeing. Seeing was a stone in the boot you couldn't shake out.

They came down off the ridge at first light, four of them and a thief, moving single file through saltbush that crackled underfoot like old paper. Below, the town lay in its bowl of dust—flat-roofed houses the color of the ground they sat on, a market square with awnings faded to the pink of sun-bleached bone, and cutting through it all, the river. Or what the maps still insisted on calling a river.

"You said there'd be water," Nara said behind her.

"I said we'd follow the river," Dessa answered. "I didn't say it would follow us."

Mara held up a fist and they stopped. She studied the town the way she studied everything—without hurry, without expression, her eyes doing the work her face refused to do. Then she pointed to a windmill at the town's edge, its blades hanging slack.

"No windmills turning," she said. "No wash on lines. No smoke from the bakeries." She looked back at them. "We go in quiet. We buy what we can carry. We don't talk about why we're here."

"And if someone asks?" Ilya asked.

"Then we're surveyors," Tam said. He said it mildly, the way he said everything, but his hands were steady on the mule's lead and his eyes were not. "Water surveyors for the guild. Nobody questions a guild badge. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it."

* * *

Harrow Bend had a well once. Dessa found it in the square, ringed in stone worn smooth by generations of palms and pails, and now capped with an iron plate bolted down and stamped with the guild's mark—a circle bisected by a wavy line, the sign of the water-bearers, cast in metal that had cost more than the well itself. Someone had scratched words into the plate's edge, small and furious, half filled with rust. Dessa crouched and read them with her fingertips before her eyes caught up.

WE PAID. WE PAID. WE PAID.

She stood too fast and had to put a hand against the stone rim. The stone was warm. Everything here was warm; warmth was all the sky gave anymore.

"They capped it eight years ago," said a voice. An old woman sat in the shade of the assay office across the square, shelling beans into a bowl, her hands brown and knuckled as roots. "Guild man came with papers. Said the aquifer was failing. Said capping it was for our own good." She smiled without any joy in it. "Same year the new wells went in at Vell's Crossing, upstream. Guild wells. You can see the towers from the ridge on a clear day."

Dessa crossed the square and bought beans from her, because that was something to do with her hands, and because the woman's eyes had the particular shine of someone who hadn't been listened to in years and had a great deal stored up.

"Surveyors?" the woman asked.

"For the guild," Dessa said, and hated how easily it came out of her mouth.

"Mm." The woman looked at the badge Ilya had forged, then at Dessa's face, and whatever she saw there made her lower her voice. "Then tell them this. My granddaughter walks two miles each morning to the tanker road. Two miles there, two miles back, carrying what she can. She's nine. There are forty children in this town who do the same, and the magistrate tells us the schedule is fair." She cracked a bean pod with terrible precision. "He drives past them every morning. In a cart with a canvas top. Guild canvas."

* * *

They saw the children at midday.

The tanker road ran along the dry channel downstream of town, and the water came up it once a day in a convoy of three wagons drawn by mules and escorted by two riders wearing guild gray. The children came out to meet it like gulls after a fishing boat—running, jostling, forming lines with the grim competence of kids who had never known anything else. Each carried containers: dented cans, clay jars wrapped in sling-cloth, plastic bladders that had been patched so many times they were more patch than bladder.

Dessa watched a girl of about nine fill her jars and stagger upright under them. Watched her set her jaw the way climbers do at the start of a hard pitch—the calculation of weight against distance, the decision to simply begin. The girl walked. The line moved forward. Nobody cried, which was somehow worse.

"That's enough," Dessa said.

Nobody had asked her anything. She said it anyway.

"Dessa." Mara's voice, low.

"I said that's enough."

"It is," Mara agreed, which stopped Dessa colder than an argument would have. Mara turned to face her fully, and behind her the noon light made the dust glow like something burning. "It is enough. And there are eleven towns between here and the delta, and we have five days of food and a job that only works if we stay invisible. So tell me what you want to do with your anger, and make it something that doesn't die with us on this road."

Dessa had no answer. That was the truth of it. She wanted to break the iron plate off the well. She wanted to ride to Vell's Crossing and put a pick through the guild's new pumps. She wanted, most of all, to go back twenty years and stand in her mother's doorway while the guild men rolled up their maps, and say something, anything, instead of watching from the stairs the way a child watches.

"We keep moving," she said finally.

"We keep moving," Mara said. "But we see it. All of it. That's not nothing, Dessa. Seeing is the job. It's always been the job."

* * *

That night they camped in the ruins of a ferry crossing, where pilings stuck out of the dry channel like the ribs of something that had died standing up. Tam cooked flatbread over a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, and the smell of it pulled the crew together the way gravity pulls water—without effort, without discussion.

Nara sat apart, as she always did, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth in her lap. But when Dessa passed her on the way to check the mules, the thief spoke without looking up.

"The magistrate at Harrow Bend," Nara said. "You want to know how he was bought? I've seen the ledger entries. It wasn't coin, mostly. It was water. A guaranteed allotment for his family compound, in perpetuity. They don't bribe with money out here." She finally looked up, and her eyes were very tired. "They bribe with the thing everyone else is dying of. How do you prosecute a man for accepting the only medicine?"

Dessa stood in the dark and thought about her mother's town, where the magistrate had also driven a cart with a canvas top, and where her mother had also said *the schedule is fair* right up until the year she stopped saying anything at all.

"I'm going to sleep," Dessa said.

But she didn't sleep. She climbed instead, because climbing was what she did with feelings too large for her body—took the rope she always carried and went up the tallest ferry piling, and sat on top of it with her knees drawn up, looking north along the dark seam of the riverbed toward the mountains she couldn't yet see.

Somewhere up there was a glacier. Somewhere up there was a vault, and inside the vault, if Nara was telling the truth, a piece of paper that said *every town*, that said *all who drink*, that said the words the founders had sworn and the guild had spent sixty years unwriting. A piece of paper. Her whole life had been bent around things heavier than paper.

Below her, Tam banked the fire. And she heard him say—not to her, not loudly, almost to himself, in the tone of a man setting down something he'd carried too long:

"I helped seal it, you know. Forty years back. I was there when they closed the ice."

Dessa went very still on top of the piling, the night wind moving through her like water through a channel.

Upstream, she thought. Everything they needed was upstream. And so, apparently, was everything Tam had buried.

She climbed down and went to wake Mara.
