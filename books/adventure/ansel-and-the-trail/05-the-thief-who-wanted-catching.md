## 5. The Thief Who Wanted Catching

The trail changed on the third morning above the salt.

Mara had been reading it the way she read everything—sideways, half-asleep, letting her feet do the thinking. The thief's prints were small, narrow-heeled, weighted toward the outside edge like a woman who walked with her weight forward, always ready to run. For two days those prints had been careful. Deliberate. They crossed rock where rock would hold them, kept to the shade side of arroyos, doubled back through a dry wash to break the line of travel. A professional's trail. Mara had followed worse for less pay and felt nothing but the flat satisfaction of competence meeting competence.

Then, somewhere in the gray hour before dawn, the trail stopped being professional.

It started leaving things.

A twist of blue thread snagged on a thornbush—dyed wool, the kind sold in border markets, the kind Mara herself had worn as a girl before she learned that color was a debt you paid for later. Then a cairn of three stones stacked on open ground where no one needed a marker, because no one could get lost in country this flat. Then, most damning of all, a boot print pressed deep into a pan of clay silt and left there—*left there*, facing back along their own line of approach, like a signature at the bottom of a letter.

Dessa crouched over it, squinting. "She knows we're behind her."

"She's known since the flats," Mara said. "Maybe before."

"Then why leave us a road?"

Mara didn't answer. She was looking at the print again, at the narrow heel, the outward roll, and feeling something cold walk up her spine on small familiar feet. Because she knew that gait. She had tracked it across three hundred miles of badlands once, years ago, when it belonged to a girl who ran messages for the guild and laughed too loudly around campfires and taught Mara that silence could be a gift you gave someone rather than a wall you built against them.

No. That was coincidence. Small-footed women were common enough. The desert was full of people who walked like they were about to run.

But the blue thread—

She said nothing. She broke camp without breaking stride, and they climbed north out of the clay pans into the first red shoulders of the canyon country, and all day the trail taunted them. Markers now, unhidden. A strip of cloth here. An arrow scratched into stone there, pointing upstream, toward the Kessarin's narrowing throat. By afternoon Mara had stopped pretending she was tracking anything. She was being *escorted*.

Ilya noticed first, because Ilya noticed everything that touched paper or pattern. "This is wrong," he murmured, walking beside her, his voice pitched under the wind. "Thieves don't do this. Thieves want distance. This one wants—" He hesitated, choosing the word with a forger's care. "*Company.*"

"Then we'll disappoint her," Mara said, and heard how thin it came out.

Tam, trudging behind with the mule, said nothing at all. But old Tam's silences had texture, and this one had the texture of a man watching someone walk into a room he'd left long ago.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen spire, no fire, cold rations. Mara took first watch and sat with her back to the stone and let the dark come down over the canyon like a lid. Somewhere below, water moved—the Kessarin, running shallow and stubborn through its gorge, carrying snowmelt from a source none of them had seen. She listened to it the way you listen to a stranger talking in another room: present, unintelligible, patient.

The thief came at moonrise.

Mara heard her forty yards out—not the footsteps, which were nothing, but the *absence* of footsteps, a pocket of quiet moving through the night noise where a body should have made some. Ten years of ranger work had taught Mara's ears that trick. She didn't reach for her knife. She sat very still and let the quiet come to her, and when it arrived at the edge of the fireless camp and stopped, she spoke into the darkness without turning her head.

"You're loud," she said. "For someone who spent two days being invisible."

A pause. Then a laugh—low, rueful, and Mara's whole body went rigid, because she knew that laugh. She had slept ten feet from that laugh through one hard winter and half a spring, back when she still believed she might be someone who stayed.

"Nara," she said.

"Hello, Mara." The shape detached itself from the rocks and came closer, hands visible, empty. Moonlight found her face: thinner than Mara remembered, a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there before, eyes still quick and dark and unbearably awake. "You look terrible."

"You're sitting in my camp without permission."

"I'm standing in your camp. And technically it's public land." Nara lowered herself onto a rock an arm's length away, deliberate, unhurried, showing Mara exactly how much she trusted Mara not to gut her. It was either trust or a very good performance, and Mara hated that she couldn't tell. "You got my markers."

"I got your breadcrumbs. You led four armed people across sixty miles of open ground like children following sweets." Mara finally turned to face her fully. "Why?"

Nara looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw in Mara's face made something in her own expression settle, like sediment finding bottom.

"Because I tried doing it alone," she said simply. "And alone doesn't work. I stole the ledger because someone had to. I've been carrying it for five months, Mara. Five months of sleeping in drains and eating cold and watching every road for guild wardens, and do you know what I discovered?" She spread her hands. "One woman with a stolen book is a thief. One woman with a stolen book and a crew and a plan is a reckoning. I don't have a crew. So I made a trail only you could read, and I waited to see if you'd come."

"You could have sent word. A letter. A—"

"A letter? To you?" Nara's laugh again, softer now, and it hurt more than the first one. "Mara Okafor, who answers to no name and sleeps facing exits and hasn't answered a message from anyone in ten years. No. Letters bounce off you. Trails don't. You can't resist a trail. It's the one honest thing about you—you always follow it to the end."

Mara should have denied it. Some part of her, the part that had spent a decade sanding herself smooth of handles, reached instinctively for denial. But the night was cold and the river was talking to itself below them and here was Nara, real as weather, looking at her with the particular patience of someone who had already lost everything worth protecting and so had nothing left to lose.

"The ledger," Mara said. "Show me."

Nara hesitated—and Mara watched her hesitate, watched the flicker of calculation, the last professional reflex. Then she reached inside her coat and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, bound with waxed cord, and set it on the stone between them like a card played face-up.

Mara unwrapped it by feel. Inside was a ledger—old, heavy, its leather cracked and scuffed, its corners rounded by decades of handling. She opened it to the middle. Columns of figures, faded brown ink, dates going back past her grandmother's time. Water rights. Allotments. Names of towns she knew, towns she'd ridden through, towns whose wells she'd seen dry. And beside the entries, in a different hand, later additions: transfers, sales, signatures. The guild selling what it had sworn to hold.

"It goes back to the founding," Nara said quietly. "The original charter's sealed in the glacier vault at the source—I've read the references, everyone in the old records says so. The charter binds the guild to serve every town on the river. Free water, guaranteed passage, warden protection for the low settlements. And then this—" she tapped the ledger's later pages, "—is what they did instead. Sold the rights downstream. Bought magistrates. Let the tankers move in. The founders' promise is sitting in that ice right now, and if it comes down off that mountain, the whole rotten arrangement comes down with it."

"And you can't get there alone."

"I can't get there at all. The calving season starts within weeks. After that, the shelf route closes—maybe for years, maybe forever. I need climbers, a guide who knows the wardens' patterns, someone who can talk us past checkpoints." Nara's eyes went to the sleeping shapes of the crew, then back. "You have all of it. I've been following your reputation for months, Mara. The ranger who vanishes. The one the guild can't find because she taught them everything they know about finding."

"That reputation exists because I stay gone," Mara said. Her voice came out flat and cold and she was grateful for it. "You're asking me to walk into the one place in the world I can't disappear from. The source. The vault. Guild ground older than the guild. You're asking me to be *seen*."

"Yes," Nara said. "I am."

The silence stretched. Below them the river ran on, indifferent, carrying its meltwater down to towns that didn't know why their taps had gone to dust. Mara held the ledger in her lap and felt its weight—not the paper's weight, the other kind, the kind that bent spines and ended friendships and turned invisible women into names on warrants.

Betrayal, she thought. She had been hired to catch a thief, and the thief had turned out to be the only person in this whole affair telling the truth. Sefu wanted the ledger recovered and buried; the guild wanted it destroyed; and Nara—foolish, brave, impossible Nara—wanted it carried to the top of the world and read aloud while the ice gave way.

Every certainty Mara had built the trip on lay in pieces around her camp, and the pieces fit together into something she didn't want to look at.

"If we take you," she said slowly, "you follow my rules. My route. My timing. And when this is done—if it's done—you vanish. Same as me. No names, no credit, no—"

"No," Nara said.

Mara stopped.

"No," Nara repeated, gentler now. "That's the whole point, Mara. That's what I've been trying to tell you since the blue thread. Vanishing is how they won. Every witness who disappeared, every record that got buried, every ranger who decided staying invisible was the same as staying safe—that's the guild's whole strategy, and we've been playing their game for free. I'm not doing this to disappear after. I'm doing this so that *staying hidden stops working*. For them, and—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—for us."

Mara looked at her for a long time. The moon had moved; the canyon shadows had shifted; somewhere upslope Dessa was awake and listening, and had probably been awake the whole time, because Dessa missed nothing.

"The crew votes at dawn," Mara said finally. "Not me. Them. If any one of them says no, you walk, and I take the ledger, and that's the end of it."

"Fair," Nara said. And then, as she rose to go, lighter: "For what it's worth—they'll say yes. Your climber grew up drinking tanker water. Your cook served the guild for twenty years and has never once told you what he did there. Ask yourself why a man like Tam signed on for a trip to the source."

Mara went still. "What do you know about Tam?"

"Enough to know he helped bury something up there," Nara said. "And enough to know that men who help bury things eventually want to dig." She melted backward into the dark, and her footsteps, when they came, were audible this time—deliberately audible, a courtesy, a promise.

Mara sat alone with the ledger until the stars began to fade, and the river talked, and the ice far to the north groaned in its sleep like something enormous turning over, getting ready to wake.
