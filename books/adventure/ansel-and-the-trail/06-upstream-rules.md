## 6. Upstream Rules

The river changed its mind about being a river the morning they entered the canyon country.

Ilya noticed it first as a sound—or rather, the loss of one. For six days the Kessarin had talked to them, that low constant murmur of water over gravel, and now, as the walls rose on either side, the voice went strange. Echoes arrived late. The current deepened and slowed, moving with the patience of something that knew it would outlast everything touching it. Between the canyon walls the water ran green-black, cold enough to ache, and when Ilya leaned over the gunwale of their borrowed skiff he saw his own face looking back at him from three feet down, sharp-edged and honest in a way he did not care for.

"Stop making faces at yourself," Dessa said from the stern, though she was smiling. "You'll frighten the fish."

"There are no fish," Tam said from the bow, where he'd spent most of the morning silent, which for Tam was its own kind of weather report. "Not this far up. The guild netted them out decades ago. Said the spawning runs interfered with the gauge stations." He said it flatly, the way a man reads a list of grievances he's memorized so long ago they've worn smooth.

Ilya filed that away. Tam had been filing things away all trip—small corrections, small knowledge—and each one landed like a stone dropped down a well whose bottom none of them had found yet.

Mara stood amidships with her eyes on the walls. They were red and black banded stone, rising two hundred feet, close enough in places that Ilya could have touched both sides with an oar if he'd been fool enough to try. Ledges cut into the rock every few hundred yards, old ones, mortared with a craft nobody used anymore, and on some of them stood iron posts where signal lamps had once hung.

"Warden country," Mara said quietly. "From here to the source, we're visible whether we like it or not."

* * *

They made camp that night in a side slot canyon, hauling the skiff up onto a gravel bar and dragging brush over it. Nara built the fire small, feeding it dry willow, and cooked nothing because cooking smells traveled. Tam handed out cold biscuits and dried apricots and apologized for both, which was his way of apologizing for everything.

It was Ilya's watch when the lamp came on.

He saw it before he understood it—a smudge of amber light high on the eastern wall, maybe a mile upstream, blooming against the stars and then settling into steadiness. A signal lamp, lit on one of those ancient ledges. It burned for the count of thirty, then went dark. Ten heartbeats later, another light answered from somewhere around the next bend, farther up.

Relay. He'd forged enough guild documents to know what a relay chain meant: someone was passing word along the line, station to station, toward the source or away from it. And there was only one direction anything could be traveling tonight.

He woke Mara without a word. She came awake the way she always did—no gasp, no movement, just her eyes opening as if they'd never really closed.

"Lamps," he breathed. "Two of them. Upstream."

She listened to the silence as if it might repeat itself. Then: "How fast?"

"If they're running the old relay intervals? Word reaches the glacier vault by tomorrow night."

Mara looked up at the black walls, and Ilya watched her do the arithmetic he couldn't—the distance, the current, the number of wardens a guild frightened enough to light lamps might put on the water.

"Then we stop being ourselves," she said.

* * *

By gray dawn they were dressed as the law.

This was Ilya's part, and he hated how good he was at it. From the warden patrol boat they'd taken apart at the border town—Sefu's parting gift, though Sefu had called it a loan—he'd salvaged two uniform tunics, a brass badge stamped with the Kessarin watershed mark, and a leather dispatch wallet containing three blank credential slips printed on watermarked stock. Real stock. Stolen stock. The kind of thing that got a forger hanged if he was caught with it, which was why he'd carried it sewn into his bedroll for six days, feeling it against his spine like a second heartbeat.

Now he sat on a flat rock with the slips spread before him, working by the last of the starlight, and the crew crowded around him in a half-circle no one wanted to admit was anxious.

"The hard part isn't the writing," he murmured, dipping his pen. "Anyone can copy a hand. The hard part is the paper knowing things it shouldn't." He tapped the watermark. "Guild stock is dated by batch. This batch was milled eleven years ago, which means any credentials written on it have to predate—" his pen paused, "—the reorganization. Which means the signature has to be someone who held office then and doesn't now. Someone dead, ideally. Dead men countersign beautifully."

"You've done this before," Dessa said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've done worse before. This is practically honest work. I'm only inventing the past instead of stealing someone's future." He drew the first stroke of a name—*Warden-Captain Ede Voss*—and let his hand remember a man he'd met exactly once, in a customs house in Sel Maro, a man whose signature had been all downward strokes and impatience. "Voss drowned eight years ago. His ink is still valid. Ink outlives intent; that's the whole rotten architecture of paperwork."

Tam made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough.

"Something funny, old man?"

"Voss." Tam shook his head slowly. "I signed his supply requisitions for six years. He pressed so hard his pen went through the page. You'll want to write on something soft underneath or you'll tear the slip."

Ilya stared at him. Around the circle, everyone else did too.

"How do you know that?"

Tam looked at the fire, or through it. "Because I was there," he said simply. "Sel Maro depot. Fourteen years, cook's rank, then quartermaster's clerk. I know Voss's hand better than my own. I know the countersign cipher they were using in the year your paper says this was written, because I helped change it." He finally looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were terribly tired and terribly clear. "You're forging warden credentials, boy. I'm the closest thing to a real warden on this river. If you want them to survive contact with an actual checkpoint, you'll let me hold the pen."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, in the low fierce voices of people who had learned that sound carries off water. Mara settled it the way she settled everything—not with a speech but with a decision that arrived fully formed, like a stone dropping into still water.

"Tam writes. Ilya checks. Nobody else touches the slips." She turned to Tam. "And you tell us everything else you know about these waters. Every station, every password rotation, every place a patrol likes to nap. Starting now."

So Tam told them, sitting with his hands around a cup of nothing-hot tea, while the canyon lightened from black to bruise to pale gold above the eastern rim.

There was a checkpoint at the Narrows, he said, where the river pinched to forty feet and the guild kept a floating boom of chained logs. Wardens boarded every vessel. Credentials were checked twice—once by the boom sentry, once again by the station officer, who was a woman named Corrag Hale whom Tam had trained himself, God help them all, and who still wrote her reports in the cramped leftward slant he'd taught her.

"And the countersign?" Ilya asked, pen hovering.

"Changes monthly. But Hale is lazy about it—was lazy, twenty years ago, and pride like hers doesn't reform. She uses the month plus the station number. This is the fourth month." He counted on his fingers, lips moving. "*Kessarin-four.*"

"That's it?" Dessa said. "That's the whole secret?"

"That's the whole secret," Tam said. "That's always been the whole secret. Guilds don't run on cleverness, girl. They run on habit. Habit is cheaper."

Ilya thought about that all through the morning as they drifted down the long green miles toward the Narrows, and it sat in him like a swallowed stone. Habit is cheaper. He had built his whole life on breaking habit—new names, new hands, new towns every season—and here was Tam, who had spent fourteen years inside the machine and walked out of it carrying its habits in his head like shrapnel. The past wasn't behind them. It was upstream. It was waiting at a boom of chained logs with a clipboard and a lantern, wearing a face Tam had taught it.

* * *

They heard the Narrows before they saw it: a deepening of the current's voice, a cold breath off the water, and then the boom itself—black logs chained end to end across the green channel, with a plank platform mid-river where two wardens stood with pole-lamps unlit against the afternoon glare. Behind them, built into the canyon wall, the station: a door of grey timber cut straight into the rock, a chimney trickling smoke, a bell on a post.

Mara steered the skiff in slow, unhurried. She wore Voss's tunic now, sleeves rolled, badge polished with ash. Ilya sat beside her in clerk's grey, the credential wallet open on his knees, his heart doing arithmetic it refused to finish.

"Halt the boat." The nearer warden's voice bounced off four different walls. "State your business and your station."

"Supply inspection, running upstream to the source gauges," Mara said, bored and crisp. "Credentials."

The warden took the slip Ilya handed up, and Ilya watched him read it and watched the moment hang—that terrible suspended moment when all the ink in the world weighs nothing—and then the warden passed it to the platform without looking up and called out, "Countersign."

"*Kessarin-four,*" Tam said from the bow, in a voice Ilya had never heard him use: clipped, official, faintly irritated, the voice of a man who had said those words ten thousand times and expected the world to move faster.

A pause. Then, from the rock-cut doorway, a woman's voice—older, suspicious, familiar with its own authority: "Who gave that countersign? Step forward."

Tam stepped forward into the light.

What happened next took four seconds and lasted a year. The woman in the doorway—grey-haired, square-built, with Tam's own cramped leftward slant written all over her posture—stared at him, and her hand moved toward the bell, and Ilya's whole life narrowed to the space between her fingers and the rope—

"Quartermaster Adeyemi's compliments," Tam said loudly, before her fingers closed. "Special audit. He said you'd want it quiet, and he said you'd say the seal was wrong, and he said to tell you—" Tam paused, delicately, cruelly, "—that you still hold your pen like a fishhook, and he'd know your reports anywhere."

Corrag Hale's hand stopped.

For a long moment the only sound was the river, patient under the boom, going about its endless business. Then Hale laughed—one short bark, unwilling—and stepped aside.

"Audit," she said. "Of course. An audit. Forty years I've asked for a new boom chain and they send me an audit." She waved them through as the wardens swung the logs aside. "Go on, then. And tell Adeyemi his seal *is* wrong. It's been wrong since the reorganization and nobody upstairs can read."

The channel opened ahead of them, narrow and deep and shadowed, running north toward the ice. Ilya let his breath out slowly and discovered his hands were shaking, and that Tam's were too, though the old man hid it better, folding them in his lap like a man closing a book he hoped never to reopen.

"You knew her," Ilya whispered. "You didn't just know her handwriting. You knew *her*."

"I trained her," Tam said. His eyes stayed fixed upstream, where the canyon bent away into blue shadow and cold. "And she'll send a report tonight, because she's diligent, and the report will say an audit party passed through. And when the guild learns there is no audit—" he exhaled, long and slow, "—they'll know exactly which ghost came home."

Above them, high on the western wall, a signal lamp lit. Held. Went dark.

Ten heartbeats later, the next one upriver answered.
