## 7. What Tam Buried

The waystation announced itself before we saw it: a smell of old cedar and older smoke, rising off the canyon wall like a memory the stone couldn't hold. I smelled it from a half-mile out, and my knees went soft on the trail, and I told myself it was the climb.

It was not the climb.

Forty years is long enough to forget the shape of your own guilt, but not its smell. Cedar shingles, pitch-sealed, guild-stamped. The Kessarin Company built these stations every twenty miles from the delta to the source—rest houses for the water wardens, back when the wardens were still something other than hired muscle. This one had been called Meridian Nine. I knew that the way you know a scar's name.

We came up on it at dusk, the five of us strung along the river trail like beads on a fraying cord. Mara first, of course, moving through the broken country the way she moved through everything—as if the land had agreed in advance to pretend she wasn't there. Then Dessa with her ropes coiled across her chest like bandoliers. Nara walked behind her, hands loose, eyes everywhere, a woman who had spent months being hunted and hadn't yet remembered how to stop flinching. Ilya brought up the rear with his satchel of papers and inks hugged tight as an infant.

And me. Old Tam, sixty-eight years of him, carrying a cook's pack and a warden's memory, both heavier than they had any right to be.

"There," Mara said, though there was nothing to say it about yet. Then the station swung into view around a shoulder of red rock, and there it was.

Meridian Nine sat on a shelf above the river like a bird's nest abandoned mid-building. Half the roof had gone. The porch sagged where the posts had rotted through. But the walls stood, and the great cedar door with its iron ring still hung true, because the guild had built for centuries—the guild had always assumed it would last for centuries.

I stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Tam?" Dessa said.

"I'm coming." My voice came out gravelly. "Legs are old."

They were not the problem. The problem was that I had slept under that roof for three winters of my youth, and eaten at that table, and helped carry the charter upriver from this very porch, wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heart, while the founders' seals went cold in my hands.

Mara looked at me a moment too long. She had been looking at me too long for three days now, ever since the canyon narrowed and the warden markers started appearing on the stones. She was a tracker; she read trails in dust and bent grass and the absence of birdsong. I wondered what she read in me.

We made camp in the station's main room, which still held its long table and the iron stove, cold for decades but sound. Dessa got a fire going. Ilya spread his maps. Nara sat by the window with the ledger on her knees—not reading it, just resting her hand on it, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal you didn't entirely trust.

I cooked. It was what I did. Barley and dried lamb and the last of the onions, and the smell filled the ruined room and made it briefly, treacherously, into a home again. That was the cruelty of cooking: it made anywhere feel like somewhere you could stay.

Mara ate standing at the window, watching the dark come down over the river. When the others turned in, she didn't.

I washed the pot. I dried it. I set it down, and then I picked it up again, because my hands needed work or they'd start shaking.

"You knew this place," she said, without turning around.

"I told you. Guild man once. Cook."

"You told me you cooked for them." Now she turned. Firelight caught half her face; the other half stayed in shadow, which was fair, since that was how she liked to live. "You never told me you knew the waystations. You never told me you could name the warden codes on those markers before Ilya finished copying them. You've been navigating by a map inside your own head, Tam, and pretending it's luck."

The pot was dry. I set it down and left my hand on it anyway.

"Sit down, girl," I said.

* * *

She sat across the table from me, and I poured us both the plum brandy I'd been hoarding since the flats, and I looked into the fire because looking at her would have been worse.

"Forty-one years ago," I said, "I was twenty-seven, and I believed everything. That's the part people don't understand about young men. Not that we're wicked—we're credulous. Someone hands you a story with clean lines in it, and you build your whole life inside it without checking whether the walls are real."

"The charter," she said.

"The charter." The word tasted like glacier melt, cold and mineral. "You know what they tell rangers now—that the founders sealed their original compact in the ice as a sacred trust. A relic. Something to venerate." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "It wasn't a relic, Mara. It was a burial. We buried the truth in the ice and called it honoring it."

The fire popped. Upstairs, or what remained of upstairs, a beam ticked as it cooled.

"I was on the carrying party. Six of us. The founders were dead by then—all but one, and she was old and frightened of what the new council had become. The council had already begun selling. Little sales at first. A stretch of bank here, a pumping right there. Always legal. Always signed. And always, always, a clause saying the affected towns had been duly consulted, when no one had consulted so much as a well-keeper." I turned the cup in my hands. "Old Sarrat—I carried her chair up the glacier myself, she was too weak to walk it—she gave us the charter and told us to seal it where the guild could never conveniently find it again. 'Let the ice keep the promise,' she said. 'Ice doesn't take bribes.'"

"And you did it."

"We did it. And I told myself it was mercy." My voice cracked on the word and I let it. "That's the confession, girl, if you want it plain. Not that I hid the charter. That I was glad to. The towns downstream were already hurting. Dry wells, bought magistrates—you've seen it, you've all seen it, Dessa grew up inside that wound. And I thought: if the proof comes out, the guild tears itself apart, and who keeps the high passes open then? Who runs the snowmelt gates? Forgetting felt like kindness. Forgetting felt like keeping the peace." I finally looked at her. "I chose the peace of the grave over the life of the truth, and I have been cooking meals for forty years to keep my hands from remembering what they carried."

Mara was quiet a long time. Outside, the Kessarin ran over its stones, patient as anything that has all the time in the world.

"Why tell me now?" she asked. "Not at the flats. Not when Ilya forged the warden seals. Here."

"Because tomorrow we cross onto the shelf ice, and after that there are no more waystations, no more trails, nothing but the glacier and what it remembers." I set down the cup. "You need to know that I can find the vault, Mara. Sarrat made us memorize the approach—landmarks, bearings, the cairn line under the ice. No living soul knows it but me. The wardens don't know it. The council doesn't know it. They sealed their own crime so well they lost the address." I smiled, and it hurt. "But you also need to know what I am. I'm the man who decided once, all alone, that other people couldn't bear the truth. If I decide it again—if some night on the ice I think the crew can't survive the storm and the secret both—" 

"You won't," Mara said.

"You can't know that."

"No," she agreed. "But I watched you feed us for three weeks. I watched you give Nara the biggest portions when she was weakest and pretend you weren't doing it. I watched you stand between Ilya and that warden patrol with nothing but a ladle and a lie." She leaned forward, firelight moving in her dark eyes. "A man shows you who he is by what he carries for other people, Tam. You've been carrying this forty years. You didn't bury it deeper tonight. You dug it up."

I looked away, because an old man is allowed that much.

"If I lead you wrong," I said, "we die on the ice. If I lead you right, we dig a corpse out of the glacier and show it to the world, and the world will not thank us. The guild will fracture. The passes will go unkept for a season, maybe two. Towns will suffer before they're saved. That's the arithmetic, girl. I've done it every night for forty years and it never once came out clean."

"It doesn't have to come out clean," Mara said. "It has to come out true."

* * *

She woke the others. Of course she did—she wasn't a woman to sit on a thing that size alone, and I loved her for it even as my stomach dropped.

Dessa heard me out with her jaw set hard, thinking of tankers and dry taps and children hauling water in the town she wouldn't name. Ilya heard me out with his fingers steepled, already calculating what a verified founder's charter would do to every document he'd ever forged, every forgery he'd ever feared. Nara heard me out and simply nodded, once, slow, like a door closing on a room she'd lived in a long time.

"So you're our guide," Dessa said. "Not our cook."

"I've been your guide since the flats," I said. "You just didn't know what I was guiding you toward."

Mara stood at the window again, looking north, where the canyon walls fell away and the white shoulder of the high country showed itself under the stars—vast, blue-white, indifferent. The glacier. Tomorrow's road.

"One condition," she said, and her voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she was most dangerous. "On the ice, you tell us everything as you remember it. Every bearing, every landmark—and every doubt. No more deciding alone what we can bear. That's how the guild got here, Tam. Men deciding alone."

"That's the whole of my sin," I said, "and you're welcome to it. I'll leave it on the mountain."

Nara laughed softly from her corner. "Nobody leaves anything on that mountain. It keeps everything. That's rather the point."

We turned in. I lay on my bedroll under the broken roof with the stars showing through, and sleep took its time coming, and when it came it brought Sarrat's voice, thin and cold as the air at altitude: *Let the ice keep the promise.*

In the grey hour before dawn I woke to Mara shaking my shoulder, and her face in the early light told me everything before she said a word.

"Smoke," she said. "Downstream. Warden outriders, moving fast—and Tam, they're not following our trail." Her eyes went to the glacier, white and waiting in the first sun. "They're heading for the source. Somebody told them where we're going."

Somebody who knew the way.

Somebody besides me.
