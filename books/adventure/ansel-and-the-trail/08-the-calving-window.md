## 8. The Calving Window

I had never heard ice die before.

Everyone thinks it's silence up here—the high Kessarin, the white throat of the world—but that's a lie told by people who've only ever seen glaciers from the valley floor. Up close, the ice is never quiet. It ticks. It groans like an old man turning over in his sleep. It sighs through its crevasses with a sound I can only call breathing. And when a piece of it lets go—when the mountain finally shrugs off a shoulder of itself—the crack comes first, a whipcrack that goes through your boots and your teeth and the small bones of your ears, and then the roar, and then the long rolling thunder as the calved mass slides into the meltwater lake below and sends up a wave that will travel the whole river to drown someone's fields.

I grew up downstream of that sound. I knew it before I knew my own name.

Now I was walking toward it.

* * *

We came out of the last canyon at grey dawn, six of us strung along a goat track no wider than a coffin: Mara in front reading the ice the way she read everything, Dessa behind her with her climbing rope coiled across her chest like a bandolier, then Tam leaning on his staff, then me, then Ilya clutching his forged warden credentials against his ribs as if paper could save him from what was coming, and Sefu at the rear, silent, watching our backs for the wardens he swore wouldn't follow us this far.

He'd been wrong about that already once. We all remembered the smoke signal on the ridge two nights back.

The shelf opened before us like a held breath—a mile of glacier tongue spanning the gorge where the Kessarin poured out from under the ice, blue-white and ridged and laced with crevasses that showed their teeth only when the light hit them sideways. Beyond it, the source. The vault. The charter, if Nara's stolen ledger had told the truth, and it had; I'd read every page of it twice by firelight, and I'd stolen it precisely because it told the truth.

"Calving season started early," Mara said. She didn't look at us. She was looking at the far wall of the glacier, where a serac the size of a granary leaned out over the route like a drunk over a well. "Tam?"

Tam spat, considered the spatter in the snow, and said nothing for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was the flat one he used for hard truths. "Three days. Maybe four. After that, that wall comes down and takes the crossing with it. There won't be another window until next spring, and by next spring—" He stopped. He didn't need to finish. By next spring, whatever was left of the downriver towns would be gone or sold, and the guild would have buried the charter under forty more years of forgetting.

"Then we cross today," Mara said. "Light loads. Rope spacing wide. Nobody stops on the ice for anything."

Dessa laughed, short and sharp. "You say that like stopping will be a choice."

"It never is," Mara said. "That's why you don't plan on it."

* * *

The first hour was almost kind.

The ice creaked under us, but it held. I walked where Tam told me to walk—he knew these waters, these ices, from his guild years, and he called out the safe lines in a low chant, half prayer, half memory: *left of the blue seam, right of the buried stone, mind the hollow where the melt sings.* I learned to hear what he heard. Water under the ice has a voice, and it tells you where the ice is thin. Where the melt sang loudest, we went widest.

I kept thinking about my mother hauling water in Halven's Ford, three miles each way from the tanker wagons, paying the guild's water-tithe with coins she earned carrying other people's laundry. I kept thinking: *under your feet is the water she never got.* It made me walk faster. Dessa put a hand on my shoulder once without a word, and I hated how much it steadied me.

We were two-thirds across when the mountain spoke.

Not a groan. A sentence. A long, articulate cracking somewhere deep in the glacier's spine, and every one of us froze except Mara, who shouted, "Move—*move now*—" and the serac on the far wall shed its first piece, not the whole mass, just a warning, a house-sized block that fell inward with a boom I felt in my sternum, and the wave it sent across the shelf lifted the ice beneath our feet like a sheet being snapped flat.

I went down. So did Ilya. Tam stayed upright somehow, planted like a fencepost, and hauled me up by the collar with a strength that had nothing to do with his years. "Run," he said. Not walk. Run.

So we ran, on ice, roped, toward a crossing that was closing exactly as promised.

* * *

They hit us at the narrows.

Later, I would understand how they'd done it—how a warden patrol had cut the canyon ahead of us and come up onto the shelf from the eastern moraine, moving fast on ice they'd trained on since childhood. In the moment there was no understanding. There were just figures in grey rising out of a pressure ridge fifty yards ahead, between us and the far shore, and the flat crack of a signal horn, and Sefu's voice behind us saying one word: "*Ambush.*"

Four wardens. Maybe five. They didn't draw steel—they didn't need to. One of them carried a coil of line with a weighted end, and he began swinging it, and I understood with a cold clarity that they meant to take us alive, here, on the ice, and drag us back downriver to a trial that would end with the ledger burned and all of us forgotten in some warden cell.

Mara did the thing I still can't decide whether to admire or fear. She ran *toward* them.

"Split!" she shouted. "Dessa, with me—high line! Rest of you, low route, *go!*"

And because her voice has always sounded like the voice of someone who has already survived worse than this, we obeyed. Tam pulled Ilya and me left, down into a trough between pressure ridges, while Mara and Dessa went right, up onto the spine of the shelf, straight at the wardens' line.

What happened next happened fast and happened badly, which is the way most true things happen.

The warden with the weighted line swung it at Dessa as she came up the ridge. She ducked the first pass—I saw it, I saw the weight whistle past her head—and then the ice chose that moment to speak again, that deep articulate cracking, and the ridge she stood on shrugged. Just a little. Just enough. Her foot skated, her arms windmilled, and the second swing of the line caught her across the shoulders and took her off her feet, sliding toward a crevasse whose blue mouth opened beneath her like something patient finally getting its wish.

Her rope caught. That's the only reason I'm alive to tell it, and the only reason she is—that Mara had tied her own harness line to Dessa's at the last anchor, against all sense of load distribution, because Mara does not trust ice and does not trust luck and trusts, apparently, herself. The line snapped taut. Mara threw herself flat and wrapped the rope around her forearm, and I watched the skin of that arm go white and then red as Dessa's weight dragged her toward the edge, inch by inch, the two of them connected by a cord and forty feet of dying ice.

The wardens hesitated. I will think about that hesitation for the rest of my life. They had orders, surely—take them, stop them—and instead they stood on their ridge and watched a woman nearly be swallowed by the glacier, because even men who serve a lie have some animal part of them that knows when the truth is bigger than they are.

Or maybe they simply saw what we all saw next: the serac coming down.

No warning crack this time. No sentence. The whole mass let go at once, ten thousand tons of it, and the world turned to thunder. I remember Ilya screaming something. I remember Tam throwing his body over mine. I remember the wave.

It hit the shelf like the hand of something enormous and indifferent. The ice heaved. The crevasse under Dessa flexed open and shut like a gill. And when the roar finally rolled away downriver toward all the towns that would feel its echo, the wardens were gone—not dead, I think, though I couldn't swear to it—simply gone, their ridge collapsed into rubble, their ambush buried under the very route they'd been sent to close.

The route was closed. Behind us, the way we'd come was a field of broken slabs and open water. Ahead, a narrow bridge of surviving ice led to the far moraine, maybe eight feet wide, groaning with every breath of wind.

There was no retreat. There was only forward, and forward was barely there at all.

* * *

We got Dessa out by inches.

Mara hauled; Tam anchored; I crawled out on the rotten lip of the crevasse and gripped Dessa's wrist so hard I left bruises, and told her, over and over, things I don't remember saying. When she finally came over the lip she was bleeding from the shoulder where the weighted line had struck her, and shaking, and laughing—a thin, wild, terrified laugh that I understood completely, because terror and relief are the same animal wearing different coats.

"My pack," she said first thing. "My pack's in the crevasse."

"Forget the pack," Mara said.

"The pitons are in the pack."

Mara closed her eyes for one breath. Then: "We'll manage."

We inventoried what the mountain and the wardens had taken between them: Dessa's gear, half our food, both of Ilya's spare credential sets, and—when Tam checked the oilcloth bundle he'd carried since the waystation—one of the two flares. What remained was the ledger, dry inside my coat against my heart; the charter vault, a quarter mile on; a wounded climber with no equipment; and a bridge of ice eight feet wide that the glacier was actively eating.

Sefu came up the line from the rear, grey-faced, and looked at the ruined crossing behind us, and said quietly, "They'll report the route lost. No one else will try it. We're committed."

"We were always committed," Mara said. "That's what commitment means."

She looked at me then—directly at me, the thief who'd wanted catching, the stranger who'd become crew—and something passed between us that I'm still learning the shape of. Trust, maybe. Or the recognition of two people who had both spent years invisible and were done with it.

"Nara," she said. "How steady is your nerve?"

I looked at the bridge. I looked at the serac wall, already leaning farther than it had been an hour ago, shedding small pieces of itself like a man tearing up letters. I thought of my mother's cracked hands. I thought of the charter under the ice, waiting forty years for someone stubborn enough to come get it.

"Steady enough," I said.

"Good." Mara turned toward the far shore, where the glacier's face rose in a wall of ancient blue, and somewhere inside it, a door that the founders had sealed and the guild had hoped the world would forget. "Because I can hear it opening," she said, "and I don't mean the ice."
