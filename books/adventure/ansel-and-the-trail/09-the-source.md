## 9. The Source

The ice sang.

Mara had heard it all her life in small ways—the tick of frost in canyon walls, the groan of a frozen seep giving way at dawn—but nothing like this. Here, beneath two hundred feet of glacier, the cold itself had a voice. It moved through the vault like a congregation murmuring before a service, low and vast and patient, and every few minutes it rose into a note so pure it seemed less sound than light.

They had come down through the blue throat of the ice on ropes Dessa had cursed the whole way, Nara leading with the warden's seal she'd stolen months ago pressed to her palm like a talisman. Now they stood in a chamber no map admitted to: a hollow the founders had carved—or found; Tam swore it was found—and floored with stone older than any guild, older than any town on the Kessarin.

Torches were useless here. The ice gave its own light, a green-blue luminescence that came from everywhere and nowhere, pooling in the hollows of the walls, catching in their breath until each exhale was a small ghost leaving them.

"There." Tam's voice cracked on the second word.

At the center of the chamber stood a pillar of clear ice, taller than a man, and inside it—sealed as if the river itself had agreed to keep the secret—a case of black iron, banded in silver gone dull with age. The charter. Forty years Tam had carried the memory of helping to bury it, and now he stood before it with his hat in his hands like a man at a graveside.

Mara approached it slowly. Her ranger's instincts, honed on ten years of reading dust and bent grass and the absence of footprints, told her what her eyes already knew: no one had touched this place since the sealing. The ice around the iron case was unbroken, unclouded. Whatever the guild had become downstream—whatever bought magistrates and dry wells and tanker lines of children—it had never come back here to look at what it had been.

*We sealed it because we were ashamed,* Tam had said at the waystation. *No. We sealed it because we were afraid.*

She put her bare hand against the ice. It burned with cold, but she held it there, letting the pain sharpen her.

"Mara." Ilya stood near the entrance passage, ears cocked toward the sounds above. "The shelf is moving. Calving's started early—I heard three go since we came down. This window is closing."

"Then we work fast."

Dessa had brought the tools—chisels, wedges, a small charge of black powder she'd carried wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping snake. Mara looked at the powder and then at Tam.

"You helped seal it," she said. "You should help open it."

The old cook's hands shook as he took the chisel. But they steadied when they touched the ice, and Mara understood that too: some hands remember.

* * *

They worked by rotation, the way miners work, Dessa calling the rhythm, Nara hauling chips away, Ilya keeping watch at the passage mouth with his forged credentials folded uselessly in his pocket—forgeries meant nothing down here, where the only law was the ice. The green light shifted as they cut, throwing their shadows huge against the walls, so that five people looked like twenty and the chamber seemed full of everyone who had ever sworn an oath on this river.

An hour in, Mara's arms burned and her fingers had gone past pain into a strange wooden numbness, and still the case lay behind six inches of ice. She thought of the towns downstream. The girl in Marrow Bend who'd shown them the dry well, dropping a pebble down it and waiting, waiting, for a sound that never came. She thought of Sefu, and her chest did something complicated and unwelcome.

She had loved him once. Not the way the ballads said love went—not singing, not sweetness—but the way two people who are both fast and proud and alone can love each other: as recognition. He had been the only one who could read her trails, and she the only one who could read his. When the guild had asked them both to become invisible—to become the rangers' hidden hand, unseen, unrecorded, deniable—he had said yes faster than she had. And when she had finally walked away into the Ashen Flats and let the desert erase her name, he had been the one thing she regretted erasing.

Now his voice came down the passage ahead of him.

"I heard the calving from the ridge. I've been following your trail for nine days, Mara, and you didn't even try to hide it."

She turned. He filled the passage mouth, ice-dust on his shoulders, his dark face drawn tight, and behind him two wardens with crossbows leveled—not at her, she noted, but at the space between her and the pillar, as if the truth itself needed containing.

"Sefu."

"Don't." He raised a hand. "Don't say my name like we're meeting at a well. You know why I'm here."

"The ledger stays buried. That's the message?"

"That's the *wisdom*." He came closer, boots crunching on ice chips, and lowered his voice though the wardens could surely still hear. "Mara. Listen to me. The rangers exist because no one knows where we are or what we hold. The day the guild's shame is public, the charters burn, the wardens scatter, and every magistrate with a grudge comes hunting us. We protect the river *because* we're ghosts. Kill the ghost and you kill the protection."

"And the towns?" Her voice came out flat and cold as the vault floor. "Marrow Bend? The children hauling water from tankers while the guild sells their birthright by the barrel? Is that what the ghost protects?"

"The guild will reform itself—"

"The guild has had forty years." She gestured at the pillar. "It hasn't even come to *look*."

Behind her, the ice made a sound like a giant clearing its throat. Everyone froze. Somewhere far above, something the size of a hill let go of the mountain and fell into the river, and the concussion rolled through the glacier and set the torchlight—no, the green light—flickering like a frightened animal.

"We don't have time for this," Dessa said quietly.

Sefu stepped forward and put his hand flat against the pillar, mirroring her, and for a moment they stood like that, palm to palm through six inches of ancient ice, the way they had once stood back to back on watch fires in the Flats, trusting each other with their sleep.

"If you open this," he said, "you end us. You end what we were to each other. There's no coming back from being seen, Mara. Not for the guild. Not for you. Not for me."

"I know." Her breath fogged between them. "I spent ten years learning exactly how invisible a person can be. And do you know what I found at the bottom of it, Sefu? That invisibility is just another word for letting it happen. Every dry well. Every bought magistrate. Every year of silence. I let it happen. You let it happen. We were *good* at it."

He closed his eyes. "I loved you."

"I know." And her voice almost broke, and she let it, because hiding it would have been one more kind of lying. "That's why it has to be me. If anyone else opened this, you could hate them. You could make them the traitor and keep your ghost intact. But me—you'll have to look at me and understand that some things matter more than being safe."

She stepped back. "Dessa. The charge."

"No—" Sefu's hand went to his knife, and stopped there, and did not draw it. That was the thing about Sefu: he would threaten forever and strike never, when it mattered. "Mara. Please."

"Set it low," Mara said. "Just enough to crack the shell. Tam—"

But Tam was already moving. The old man pushed past them all, planted himself before the pillar, and began to sing.

It was a guild song, one Mara had never heard—a founder's hymn, low and rolling, in the old river-tongue—and as he sang, he laid his palms against the ice as if greeting an old friend. *Water remembers,* the words went, in Tam's cracked translation afterward. *Water remembers what men agree to forget.*

The charge cracked the shell. The ice split in a white bloom, and the black iron case slid free into Tam's waiting arms, trailing meltwater like something born.

And the glacier screamed.

Not the singing anymore—a tearing, grinding roar, the calving season arriving all at once, the whole mass of the ice deciding, in the same moment the charter came free, that its long keeping was over. Cracks raced across the ceiling in branching veins of white. The green light guttered.

"Run!" Dessa seized the rope. "All of you, the passage, NOW—"

Sefu grabbed Mara's arm. For one heartbeat they faced each other in the failing light, the case between them, the vault dying around them.

"Read it," she shouted over the roar. "When we're out—read it aloud, Sefu. To the wardens. To everyone. Or everything we were dies in here with the ice."

His grip tightened. Above them, a seam in the ceiling yawned wide, and the first block of the vault's roof began, slowly, terribly, to fall.
