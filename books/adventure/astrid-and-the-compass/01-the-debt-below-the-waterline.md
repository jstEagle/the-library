## 1. The Debt Below the Waterline

The Alder River ran low and mean that September, and Jude Fairweather read it the way other people read the faces of old friends — for what it was hiding.

She stood at the wheel of the *Marrow*, one hand on the brass rail, the other resting easy near the throttle, and took her cargo of sawn cedar down through the Narrows with the sun still an hour from clearing the eastern ridge. Mist lay on the water in long gray ropes. Where it thinned, she could see the current working: a seam of darker water below the first bend where the channel deepened, a faint V of ripples upstream of Miller's Rock where the flow split around something submerged. The chart called that stretch eleven feet. The chart was three years out of date and optimistic by two. Jude knew because she'd sounded it herself in spring, hanging off the bow with a weighted line while Marsh watched the engine and made jokes about her wanting to drown twice in one lifetime.

"Easy water," said Marsh from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime around midsummer. "You're steering like it's going to bite."

"It always bites," Jude said. "It's just polite about when."

He laughed. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like dock pilings and a face that folded into good humor the way paper folds into a pocket. Twenty years he'd run engines on this river, fifteen of them beside her, and he'd never once asked her to explain why she treated every mile of open water like a debt coming due.

Because it was one. That was the truth under everything, the ballast she never spoke of. She owed the river a man's life, and the river did not take payment in coin.

The *Marrow* slid past the drowned alder at Hartley Bend, its bare branches combing the surface, and Jude gave it the wide berth she always did. Not because it was a hazard — though it was — but because of what had happened four miles downstream of it, six years ago, on a night of rain so hard the river seemed to come apart into separate rivers that hated each other.

Her old boat had been the *Fair Weather*. Her father named her, back when the name was a joke and not a prayer. Forty feet of shallow-draft freight hull, honest and ugly, and on the last night of her floating life she'd been carrying too much weight too high in a flood crest, because Jude had promised the mill a delivery and Jude did not break promises to men who held her mortgage. The Okonkwo family held her mortgage. That was how it started, anyway — the ledger between them.

Tobias Okonkwo had been twenty-three. He'd hired on that season as deckhand, all elbows and grin, saving money to study engineering in the city. His mother Ada had packed him food for the run. His father had shaken Jude's hand at the dock and said, *Bring him back better than you found him.*

The *Fair Weather* went down at the mouth of Coyle Slough in ninety seconds flat. Jude got three of her crew onto the roof of the wheelhouse before the hull rolled. Tobias went back inside for the pay chest — the crew's wages, which he'd insisted on keeping dry because he was that kind of boy — and the river closed over him like a book.

They found him two days later, nine miles down. They never found the chest.

Jude testified at the inquiry with her hands flat on the table so no one would see them shake. Overloaded. Flood stage. Known risk taken knowingly. The examiner used the word *negligent* and the word *tragic* in the same sentence, as if they could share a sentence without one of them drowning the other. No charges. There were never charges, on rivers. Rivers don't testify.

But Ada Okonkwo had stood up in that room, small and straight-backed in her black dress, and looked at Jude the way you look at a hole in the ice where your child went in. And she had said only: *"You will carry this."*

Not a curse. A commission.

So Jude carried it. She sold what was left of the wreck, borrowed against her father's name, and built the *Marrow* plank by plank with money the Okonkwos did not lend her, though God knew she'd offered them every kind of stake. She refused their investment and accepted their interest payments instead — the real ones, the ones the bank charged, plus a tithe she invented herself and paid quarterly to Ada in person, in cash, in an envelope she never let anyone else touch. Fourteen years of payments on a debt that could not be discharged, because the principal was a boy at the bottom of a slough and no court could garnish a soul.

She'd told herself once, in the early years, that she'd pay until the envelope stopped feeling like blood money. It still felt like blood money. She kept paying.

* * *

By noon the *Marrow* was through the lower flats and riding the long green reach above Corben's Landing, where the valley opened out and the water spread lazy as spilled tea. Jude worked the wheel in small corrections, half-listening to the engine's even heartbeat, letting the river do most of the labor. This was the part of the job strangers envied — the sun, the drift, the sense of going somewhere without effort. Strangers didn't see the reading underneath it. Every eddy told you what the bottom was doing. Every change in the sound of the water against the hull was a sentence in a language the river had been speaking since before there were charts, and Jude Fairweather understood more of it than any pilot on the Alder, and none of it mattered, because understanding had not saved Tobias Okonkwo and would not save anyone else either. Competence was just guilt wearing work clothes. She knew that. She put the clothes on every morning anyway.

At Corben's Landing they took on crates of nails and off-loaded the cedar to a yard foreman who complained about the price and the weather and the government in roughly equal measure. Marsh went to argue with the fuel pump. Jude signed papers in the landing office, and while the clerk counted change she caught herself looking north upriver, to where the Alder narrowed between bluffs and climbed toward the high country, toward the gray line on every map where the surveyed world simply stopped.

The Weir Line. Nobody ran above it. The law forbade it — some old statute about navigation hazards and treaty waters that nobody enforced because nobody needed to; the river itself did the enforcing. Above the Weir the channel steepened, the gorge closed in, and the water came down cold and fast off country no chart covered. Pilots had a way of talking about it the way sailors talk about the Roaring Forties: with respect, with jokes, and with a firm agreement to never find out.

Jude hadn't been above the Weir since before the accident. Before that, she'd gone up sometimes with her father, in the years when he was still alive and still whole, when he'd stand at the rail with his strange brass compass in his palm and watch the needle swing away from north entirely, pointing off sideways into the hills, and say nothing about it at all.

She'd inherited the compass along with his debts. It sat in her cabin drawer now, wrapped in oilcloth, and she did not look at it, the same way she did not look at the drowned alder at Hartley Bend, the same way she did not look at Ada Okonkwo's eyes when she handed over the envelope. There was a whole geography of things Jude Fairweather did not look at, and she navigated around them the way she navigated around snags — by feel, by habit, by never quite admitting the hazard existed.

"You're doing the thing," Marsh said, appearing at her elbow with two cups of coffee from the landing house, both terrible.

"What thing."

"The looking-upriver thing. You get a line right here—" he tapped the space between his own eyebrows— "like the river owes you an argument."

"The river doesn't owe me anything," Jude said. "It's the other way around."

Marsh grunted and drank his coffee and didn't push, which was one of the ten thousand reasons she kept him.

* * *

They tied up for the night at Mile Forty, a shelf of gravel and mooring posts below a bluff, and Jude was checking the lashings in the last gray light when she heard the truck.

It came down the switchback road slow, headlights off, and stopped at the top of the bank, and a woman got out of it and stood there a moment looking at the boat the way you look at a church you have bad news for. Even in the dusk Jude knew the walk. Small steps, straight spine, a coat buttoned to the throat regardless of the season.

Ada Okonkwo came down the bank path with a lantern in one hand and a canvas satchel in the other, and Jude felt the envelope ritual rearrange itself in her chest — except it wasn't quarter day, and widows carrying satchels at dusk do not climb down to riverboats for envelopes.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Jude said. "You should have sent word. I'd have come up."

"I needed to see the water," Ada said. Her voice was level, careful, the voice of a woman who had spent fourteen years refusing to cry in front of the person responsible. "I needed to see if you were on it."

"I'm always on it."

"Yes," Ada said. "I know. That is why I'm here."

She stopped at the edge of the lamplight spilling from the *Marrow*'s cabin windows, and she set the satchel down between them on the gravel like a weight on a table, and Jude saw that her hands were trembling and that everything else about her was not.

"My husband is dead," Ada said. "Or he is worse than dead, which is lost. And every office in this territory has told me no. The survey service says the water above the Weir Line is outside their jurisdiction. The magistrate says the expedition signed waivers. The company men smile at me and ask if I am quite sure Elias didn't simply run off." Something moved behind her face then, quick and cold. "Elias Okonkwo did not run off. Elias went up that river three weeks ago with five men and a contract, and four of those men were afraid of exactly one thing, and it was not the rapids."

Jude heard herself say, "What are you asking me?"

Ada lifted her chin. In the lantern light her eyes were dry and enormous and absolutely without mercy, and they were the same eyes that had stood up in an inquiry room fourteen years ago and commissioned her.

"I am asking you to go back upriver, Captain Fairweather. Past the Weir. Where you swore to me once — swore on your father's grave — that you would never go again."

The river talked against the gravel below them, patient, unhurried, saying nothing at all.
