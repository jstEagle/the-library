## 2. What the Widow Bought

Ada Okonkwo did not sit down. She stood in the pilothouse of the *Maggie B* with her hat in her hands and her back straight as a mooring post, and she let the silence do its work while Jude pretended to study a fuel manifest she had already read twice.

"Three weeks," Ada said at last. "Twenty-two days since his last letter came down from the Weir Line. He wrote that they'd made camp above the narrows and that the river up there ran like something young. Like it hadn't learned to be a river yet." Her voice didn't break. It narrowed instead, the way water narrows before it drops. "Then nothing."

Jude set the manifest down. "The survey office will have sent someone after him."

"The survey office sent him." Ada's mouth went flat. "Elias led six men past the Weir Line on a contract Corliss Hale signed personally — timber money, though Elias wouldn't say what they were really mapping. When he stopped writing, I went to the River Authority and asked them to send a patrol boat up. They told me the Weir Line is closed by statute. Closed by law, Jude. They told me no licensed vessel may pass above the weir, and if a man is fool enough to go past it himself, then the river owns what it takes." She turned her hat slowly in her hands. "They said that to my face. A woman whose husband is somewhere above that line, alive or dead, and they quoted me the statute like scripture."

"They'd say the same to me."

"They would." Ada looked at her then, directly, and Jude felt the look land somewhere below her ribs where excuses lived. "Which is why I'm not asking the Authority. I'm asking you."

"I don't run passengers anymore."

"I'm not asking for passage. I'm asking you to find my husband." She laid an envelope on the chart table — heavy paper, heavy with whatever was inside it. "There's enough there to cover your boat, your fuel, your engineer, and your silence about who hired you. And there's more waiting when you bring him back. Or bring back word of him. Either one. I only need to know."

Jude didn't touch the envelope. Outside the pilothouse windows the Alder ran brown and patient toward the sea, and the evening light lay on it like oil. Twenty-two days. In twenty-two days a man could be anywhere between the weir and the glacier head — or nowhere at all.

"You know what's above that line?" Jude said. "Nobody does. That's the whole point of the line. No charts past the weir. No soundings. The old pilots called it the Blind Water because the maps just stop, like the cartographers got to the edge of their nerve and quit."

"Elias wasn't afraid of blank places on maps," Ada said. "He used to say a blank place was just a truth nobody had bothered to write down yet."

*Or a grave nobody had bothered to dig.* Jude kept that behind her teeth. The compass hung in her pocket against her hip, and she was suddenly, acutely aware of it — her father's brass compass, the one that pointed wherever it pleased, the one that trembled when she lied. She wasn't lying now. But she was close to it, circling the lie the way a hawk circles a henhouse, and she could feel the needle stirring in its housing like something waking up.

"I'll need until morning," she said. "To load. To think."

Ada put out her hand, and after a moment Jude took it. The widow's grip was dry and strong and did not linger. "Thank you, Captain Fairweather."

"Don't thank me yet."

"No," Ada agreed, gathering her hat. "I'll thank you when you come back down. With him or without him." At the top of the gangplank she paused, not turning around. "He spoke of you once, you know. After he met you at the harvest fair, years ago. He said you were the best pilot on the Alder and the saddest man he'd ever shaken hands with. He hoped someday you'd tell him why." She stepped down onto the dock without waiting for an answer, which was a mercy, because Jude had none that would survive being spoken aloud.

* * *

She loaded through the dark hours because sleeping was worse than working.

By lantern light she took on cordwood and canned goods, rope and lamp oil, the spare propeller shaft wrapped in canvas, medical kit, flare case, the long spool of signal wire her father had sworn by and she had never once needed. The *Maggie B* sat low in the water by three a.m., riding heavy and sure, and Jude walked her decks checking lashings with hands that knew every cleat and seam without looking. This was the part of the work she loved and would never admit to loving: the boat becoming ready, becoming capable, becoming more than the sum of her own doubts.

Marsh found her at the fuel dock at first light, as she'd known he would. He came down the boardwalk with his coat collar up and his toolbag over his shoulder and the expression of a man arriving at a fire he'd been smelling smoke from all week.

"Heard you took a contract," he said.

"Heard wrong."

"Heard you took a contract upriver." Marsh stepped aboard without invitation, the way he'd been stepping aboard her boats for fifteen years, and set his bag by the engine hatch. "Past the Weir Line. Which is either a rumor started by idiots or a plan started by you, and I've met the idiots."

Jue coiled a line and didn't answer.

"Ada Okonkwo," Marsh said. Not a question.

"Elias Okonkwo," Jude corrected. "She's buying a search. I'm selling one. That's the whole transaction."

Marsh studied her across the deck, and his face did the thing it always did — went kind and unreadable at the same time, like a locked door with flowers in the window. "You swore off that water."

"People change."

"Not you. You just get quieter about things." He crouched and opened his toolbag, began sorting wrenches by size with the economy of long habit. "I'll come. You know I'll come. But I want you to say it plain, Jude. Say why we're going, so when it goes sideways we both remember whose idea it was."

The compass pressed against her hip. She thought about saying *for the money*, and felt the needle shiver. Thought about saying *because Ada asked*, and felt it tremble harder, rattling faintly in its brass housing like a moth against glass. The truth was underneath those, down where she kept it: that she owed the Okonkwo family a debt no ledger held, that Daniel Okonkwo had drowned off her old boat eight years ago with her hand on the wheel, that she had spent every day since proving herself careful, competent, safe — and that none of it had paid anything, because guilt dressed up as competence was still guilt, just better tailored.

What she said was: "Because somebody has to go, and I'm the best pilot on the Alder."

That much was true. The needle settled.

Marsh heard what she didn't say — he always had — but he only nodded and swung his bag into the engine room. "Cast off when you're ready, Captain. I'll have her heart beating inside the hour."

* * *

She opened the compass in the pilothouse while the town still slept.

Her father had left her two things when he died: the *Maggie B*, and the compass, and everyone who knew him agreed the second was worth less than the brass it was cast from. It was an old surveyor's instrument, heavier than it should be, with a hinged lid worn smooth by a thumb that wasn't hers. The face bore two needles' worth of markings — the standard cardinal points, and beneath them a second ring of fine engraved characters her father had never explained, worn nearly to nothing.

And it lied. That was the thing. It pointed north when north suited it, and other times it pointed elsewhere — up a side channel she'd have sworn was a dead end until she ran it and found deep water; toward a stretch of bank the night her engine seized and she needed, badly, to make shore. Once, memorably, it had pointed straight at a man on the dock at Harrow Bend, and she'd followed its insistence into a conversation that uncovered a cracked rudderpost before it killed her. Her father's compass pointed not north but at whatever she most refused to see. She'd decided that was superstition. She'd also, quietly, never gone anywhere without it.

Now she held it flat on the chart table, over the great blank space above the Weir Line where the mapmakers had quit, and watched the needle.

It swung. Settled. Swung again — not hunting for magnetic north, which it ignored entirely, but casting about like a dog scenting wind. Then it steadied, quivering, aimed upriver and slightly east, into the blank.

Toward Elias Okonkwo, if there was any sense in the world. Toward whatever Ada's money couldn't buy and the Authority's statute couldn't reach.

Jude sat with it a long moment, the needle trembling its small silver tremble, and tested herself the way she sometimes did, the way a pilot taps a gauge to see if it's stuck. *I'm going upriver for the money.* The needle jumped, agitated, dishonest-adjacent. *I'm going because it's the right thing to do.* It shivered again, gentler. Closer, but not true, not quite. She exhaled through her nose.

*I'm going because I owe them.*

The needle went still as a nail.

There it was, then. She closed the lid on it, on all of it, and slid the compass back into her pocket where it rode against her hip like a small accusing heart. Below decks, the *Maggie B*'s engine caught and settled into its working rhythm, and Marsh's shadow moved across the engine-room skylight, and the eastern sky over the Alder went the color of hammered tin.

Jude rang the bell twice, cast off the lines, and took her boat upriver — past the last buoy, past the last farm light, toward the gray stone span of the old weir where the law ended and the Blind Water began.

The needle in her pocket did not waver once.
