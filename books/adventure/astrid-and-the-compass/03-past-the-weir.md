## 3. Past the Weir

The Weir Line showed itself an hour after midnight, and it showed itself the way a scar shows itself: not as a thing, but as a place where something had been taken.

Jude cut the running lights at half past twelve and let the *Marrow* drift. Below her in the engine well, Marsh worked with a rag and a whisper, keeping the bearings quiet, and Sefu crouched amidships with his hood up, watching the water like a man reading scripture he didn't believe anymore. The moon was a fingernail paring behind cloud. Good. Jude wanted the dark thick enough to chew.

Ahead, the river narrowed between bluffs of black granite, and strung across that throat — low, unlit, official — ran the Weir Line itself: a chain of steel buoys linked by cable, each one stenciled with the same three words in flaking white paint.

CLOSED WATERS. BY ORDER.

By whose order, nobody said. That was the trick of it. The closure had come down eleven years ago on paper bearing too many seals and no signature anyone could find, and every chart since then simply stopped. Past the buoys, the Alder River became a rumor. Boats that went up did not come back down, or came back down as stories, and stories were cheaper than search parties.

"You're sure about this," Marsh said. It wasn't a question. He'd been saying it all day in various grammatical arrangements, and now he'd run out of grammar.

"No," Jude said. "Run her slow."

She took the wheel herself. There were places she trusted no one else's hands, and this was going to be all of them at once. She eased the throttle forward until the *Marrow* crept upstream against the current at barely steerageway, hull kissing the eastern bluff where the current slackened in the rock's cold shadow. The buoys rode their cable thirty yards off the port bow, black shapes bobbing, patient as a row of closed mouths.

A hundred yards short of them, the patrol launch's engine came up out of the south.

Jude heard it before she saw it — the flat, bored throb of an outboard being run by someone who expected to see nothing. She killed the engine entirely and held up one fist. Marsh froze below. Sefu went flat among the cargo netting. The *Marrow* swung broadside to the current and began, gently, terribly, to drift downstream toward the line they'd spent four hours sneaking up to.

The launch rounded the lower bend with its searchlight sweeping the water in long lazy arcs, the way a man pats his pockets for keys he knows are in his hand. Jude counted seconds. The drift would carry them into the light in forty. Thirty-five. She looked at the oars racked along the gunwale — useless, at this tonnage, this current — and then at the cable itself, sagging between its buoys not two boat-lengths ahead.

"Marsh," she breathed. "Grapple line. Now."

He understood without being told, which was why she'd hired him, which was why she'd forgiven him things over the years that other men would have been buried for. The grapnel went over quiet as a fish rising. Jude caught the cable on the second swing, snubbed the line around the aft cleat, and the *Marrow* checked her drift with a groan of rope, hanging in the current like a moth pinned to velvet — directly beneath the patrol launch's path, directly beneath the Weir Line, breaking the law at a speed of zero knots.

The searchlight passed over them twice. Both times it caught nothing but the cable, the buoys, the black water, and the black bluff, because the *Marrow* was painted the color of everything Jude had ever wanted to hide behind. The launch's radio crackled something about a quiet night. The engine faded south.

Nobody breathed until the sound was gone. Then Sefu lifted his head from the netting and said, in the tone of a man reporting weather, "They'll be back on the hour."

"Then we won't be here on the hour." Jude paid out the grapple line hand over hand, walking the *Marrow* up to the cable itself, and Marsh leaned out with a bolt cutter and opened the river the way you'd open a letter you already knew was bad news. The severed ends sank. The buoys drifted apart, and through the gap the current changed voice — deeper, colder, older — and the *Marrow* went through.

* * *

No chart covered what lay above the Weir Line. Jude had known that the way you know a stove is hot, abstractly, and knowing it was different from steering into it. For the first ten miles the river behaved, wide and brown-green under the stars, and she let herself think the stories were just stories, the way you do.

Then the valley closed.

The bluffs drew in until there was hardly room for the river and the moonlight both, and the water turned from green to gray to a gray so pale it seemed lit from underneath, glacial flour suspended in the current, the ground-up bones of mountains that had been dying since before there were names for them. The temperature dropped as though they'd sailed into winter's private holding. Frost feathered the rails. Sefu pulled his hood tighter and said nothing, which from him was a paragraph.

And the river got faster. Not rapids — not yet — but a hurry, a sustained muscular urgency, as if the whole river had remembered somewhere it was supposed to be and had been trying to get there for ten thousand years. Jude found herself working the wheel in constant small corrections, reading water she'd never read, water that read *her*, and the strange thing — the thing she wouldn't tell Marsh or Sefu or anyone — was that some animal part of her loved it. This was the water her father had talked about in the last year of his life, when the drink had loosened whatever gate kept his stories in: *up past the Line, girl, where the river's still honest.* She'd thought it was drunk talk. The river was honest. That was exactly what frightened her.

In her coat pocket, her father's compass sat against her hip like a second heartbeat. She'd checked it at dusk out of habit and found the needle doing something no compass should: not pointing north, not pointing anywhere, but trembling — shivering in its housing like a dog hearing a whistle only it could hear. She checked it again now, by feel, in the dark. Still trembling. Still pointing, as near as she could tell, straight up the throat of the gorge.

Toward whatever she most refused to face. Her father had written that on the card taped inside the lid, in his looping, half-literate hand, and she had decided years ago it was a joke, a tinkerer's whimsy, a dead man's last laugh. She was beginning to suspect it was instructions.

"Dawn in two hours," Marsh called softly from the engine well. "There's supposed to be a cache around mile fourteen, if your widow's information holds."

"It holds." Ada Okonkwo had given him coordinates copied from Elias's field book, along with a photograph of her husband squinting into sun, and a look that Jude carried now the way sailors carry ballast. "Starboard bank, below the talus fan. He marked it safe landing."

The talus fan announced itself as a pale spill of broken rock sliding down from a shoulder of the bluff, and beneath it, tucked into a fold of granite that might have been carved for the purpose, a shelf of gravel flat enough to land on. Jude brought the *Marrow* in against the current with the tenderness of a woman setting down something breakable, and they made fast to a boulder the size of a chapel.

The cache was a steel drum sunk halfway into the gravel, camouflaged with stone, and it had been opened the way a safe is opened by people in a hurry — lid pried, contents scattered, no care taken to hide the crime because the criminals hadn't expected witnesses to survive. Tents gone. Rope gone. The rifle case empty. A scatter of rice gone moldy in the frost, and tin cans punched through, deliberately ruined, so that even a starving man following this trail would find nothing.

"Somebody wanted them stranded," Marsh said quietly. "Or somebody *was* them, and wanted whoever followed stranded."

Sefu crouched at the edge of the wreckage and did not touch any of it. His stillness had a quality Jude was learning to read; it wasn't calm, it was recognition. "This is Hale's work," he said. "Not the crew's. The crew would have taken the food even ruined. This is a message."

"To who?"

"To whoever came next." He looked up at her, and in the pre-dawn gray his eyes were very old. "To say: turn back."

Jude turned back — into the drum. She hauled it upright and tipped it, letting the last grit run out, and that was when her fingers found what the ransackers had missed: a false bottom, a plate of steel welded an inch above the true base, warped just enough that the pry bar had skipped over it. She worked it loose with her knife.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a journal. Elias Okonkwo's journal — she knew the name inked inside the cover, knew the careful surveyor's lettering from the sample pages Ada had shown her — with perhaps twenty pages remaining, the rest torn out. And folded into the binding, a single loose page, written in a hand that had deteriorated across its length from precision to urgency, the last lines pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*Day 19. Crossed the moraine above the falls. Found the boundary stones — the original ones, 1871 survey, hidden under slide rock where someone hoped they'd stay found. The source basin is NOT Halloway land. It never was. The deed Hale's lawyers filed in spring is built on a falsified township plat — I have the original plat numbers, I have the stones photographed, I have enough to bury them.*

*I told the crew tonight. Two are with me. Voss is not. Voss has been radioing our position every night at nine. God forgive me, I led us into a company town and called it wilderness.*

*If anyone finds this: the truth is at the source. Go to the source.*

Jude read it twice. The frost was on the page by the end of the second reading, her breath freezing on the paper, and she realized her hands were shaking — not from cold.

Halloway Timber. The name meant money the way gravity meant falling. Half the county's mortgages, half the county's judges, and now, apparently, a forged deed sitting like a landmine under the headwaters of the entire river she'd spent her life on. Elias Okonkwo had found it, and Elias Okonkwo had gone silent, and the authorities wouldn't search forbidden waters, and suddenly the word *forbidden* had a very specific owner.

"Well?" Marsh said.

Jude looked up the gorge. The river ran fast and pale toward a horizon where, faint as breath on glass, stood the white shoulders of mountains that fed it all. Somewhere up there was a man alive or dead, a truth buried or waiting, and a company that had already sent one message in a ransacked drum and would not need to send a second.

In her pocket, the compass needle had stopped trembling. It lay steady and certain, pointing upriver, pointing at the source, pointing — she understood with a clarity that felt like falling — at the exact center of everything she had spent eleven years refusing to look at.

"We go on," she said.

Behind them, far downstream, thin across the miles, a patrol launch's engine started up.
