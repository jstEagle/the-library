## 4. The Man in the Reeds

Sefu had learned to count his heartbeats instead of hours. Hours were a luxury the reeds did not allow.

He lay in the shallows where the river slowed to think, half-submerged in water cold enough to slow the bleeding, and he counted. Sixty beats. A hundred. The mud held him like a hand. Above him the reed stalks stood in their thousands, gray-gold and whispering, and through their lattice he could see the far bank, the black line of timber, the sky going the color of old tin.

Three days since he'd crawled out of the wreck of the survey camp. Three days since Voss had put two rounds into the water beside Elias's head and said, *walk or swim, old man, makes no difference to me.* Three days since Sefu had run — not walked, run — leaving the others shouting his name behind him, and had not stopped until his lungs tore and his legs gave out here, in this drowned field at the river's edge, where no one with any sense would look.

His left leg was the problem now. Something in it had gone wrong below the knee, a deep wrongness that throbbed in time with his pulse, and the wound he'd taken scrambling down the scree had opened twice more since. He'd bound it with strips of his shirt and packed it with river clay because his grandmother had sworn by clay, and because there was nothing else. Fever came and went like weather. When it lifted, he counted heartbeats. When it fell, he dreamed of the source pool, blue as a piece of fallen sky, and of Elias Okonkwo's face when he'd held up the deed papers and understood what they meant.

*They sent us up here to find it for them,* Elias had said. *Not to map it. To claim it.*

Then Voss had come out of the tent with a lantern and a pistol, and the expedition had ended the way expeditions end — quickly, and in the dark.

A sound brought him up out of the fever-shallow. Not an animal. Engines. Low, throttled back, coming downriver against the current's logic — which meant someone who didn't care about fuel, or someone searching. He pressed himself deeper into the mud. Through the reeds he watched the channel, and after a long while a boat slid past: small, patched, running dark except for a hooded lamp at the wheel. Not Hale's launch. Hale traveled loud, with armed men standing along the gunwales like fence posts.

This boat moved like its pilot was part of it. It went past, hesitated — the engine note dropped — and began to turn.

Sefu thought about staying under. He thought about how easy it would be, how the mud would take him and the river would carry the story downstream without him, and how no one would ever know what was written on those deed papers except the men who'd stolen the land. Then he thought of Elias, somewhere upriver, alive or not, and of Ada, who had kissed her husband's cheek at the Weir dock and told him to bring her back a stone from the source.

The boat bumped something in the shallows. A voice swore — a woman's voice, low and irritated — and a pole swung out, probing.

Sefu raised one arm out of the reeds.

The shouting started immediately.

* * *

Jude had nearly put the pole through the man's skull before she understood he wasn't attacking her. He was barely holding on to anything at all. He came up out of the reeds like something the river had decided to return — mud-caked, skeletal, one leg dragging, eyes burning out of a face gone hollow — and when Marsh hauled him over the gunwale he collapsed onto the deck plates and lay there shaking.

"Water," Jude said, and Marsh brought the canteen. The man drank like it was a fight he intended to win.

"Easy," Marsh said. "Slow, friend."

"Sefu." The name came out cracked. "My name is Sefu Mbeki. I was guide for the Okonkwo survey." His eyes found Jude's and held them, and she saw the fever in them but also something underneath, something level and deliberate. "You're looking for them. That's why you're here. I can see it in how you're rigged — extra lines, spare fuel lashed forward. You're a search boat."

"I'm a cargo boat," Jude said.

"You're a search boat," he repeated, "running the Weir Line at night, which no cargo boat does unless it's paid to or desperate." He coughed, wet and ugly. "Either way. I'm what's left of your answer."

Marsh looked at Jude. Jude looked at the man bleeding on her deck, and felt the compass in her coat pocket do something she'd never felt it do — swing hard, once, like a struck bell, and settle pointing directly at him.

"Get him below," she said. "And get that leg cleaned properly. If he dies on my boat I'll never hear the end of it."

* * *

By lamplight in the cabin, with the boat tied off in a side slough and Marsh playing surgeon with the good needle and the last of the boiled linen, Sefu talked. He talked in pieces, the way men talk when the story is too heavy to lift all at once.

"Hale hired us through a shell company. Corliss Vale Survey Associates — you see the joke? Vale, Hale. He thought it was funny." Sefu's mouth twisted. "Elias didn't. Elias read everything. Contracts, charters, the deed abstracts Hale's lawyers sent along 'for reference.' He read them the way I read water. And halfway up the gorge he called us together and showed us what we were really doing."

"Which was?"

"Finding the boundaries." Sefu's hands made a shape in the air, a rectangle. "The Halloway Timber Company holds title to land above the Weir Line — prime cedar, thousands of acres, deeded forty years ago. Except the deeded parcels don't touch the river. They're drawn around it. Because the original surveyors couldn't get above the falls, so they guessed, and their guess was generous — to the company. Elias figured out that if the true source sits where the old headwaters maps say it does, then the deeded boundary is wrong by six miles. Six miles of river country that belongs to nobody — or to everybody — but that Halloway has been cutting the edges of for a decade anyway. Timber off land they don't own. Elias found the cut stumps. He measured them. He wrote it all down."

"And Hale?" Jude asked, though she already knew. You could always tell a man's business by what he sent armed men to protect.

"Hale is the money behind Halloway's expansion. The upper claims are his next fortune. We weren't a survey, Captain. We were deniable scouts. If we found the source and reported it quietly, Hale's lawyers would amend the deeds before anyone knew there was a question. And the men who objected—" Sefu stopped. The lamp flame leaned in the draft. "Voss objected loudest at first. Then he stopped objecting. I don't know what Hale's people said to him. I know what he did with the pistol."

"Who else is alive?"

"Elias. Maybe. They gave him the choice — walk back down and sign an affidavit saying he found nothing, or stay. He stayed. Voss let him keep the documents because Voss is stupid, or because Hale wants them where he can watch them burn in front of witnesses. There's a camp at the meltwater chute. Armed men. They're waiting for something." Sefu's voice dropped. "They're waiting for the calving season. Two days, maybe three, when the ice dam opens the chute and the river runs high enough to bring a boat down from the source pool. Whatever happens up there has to happen soon."

The cabin was quiet. Outside, the river muttered over its stones, patient as ever.

Marsh sat back on his heels, needle work done, and said, "I've known Sefu twelve years. Ran the Muskeg trade with him before either of us could afford boots that fit. He carried my brother four miles on his back after a moose gored him. If he says a thing happened, it happened."

"That's very moving," Jude said. "It's also exactly what a man would arrange to have said if he wanted aboard a boat headed upriver."

"Captain." Sefu pushed himself up on one elbow, and even feverish there was iron in it. "Search my things. I have nothing. I have a knife and a dead man's hat. If I were Hale's man, would I have lain three days in a frozen swamp waiting for a boat that might never come? Would I have given up the chute, the calving window, the camp positions — everything — to strangers?" He laughed, short and bitter. "I have nothing left to sell. That is the only honest currency I have."

Jude looked at him a long moment. Then she took the compass out of her pocket and set it on the table between them, brass case catching the lamplight, the needle trembling inside its glass like a captive bird.

"My father's," she said. "It doesn't point north. It points at whatever the person holding it most refuses to face. Don't ask me how; I've stopped asking." She turned it so the needle faced Sefu. "Right now it's pointed at you. Which means either you're lying to me, or you're the thing I don't want to deal with. Both are possible."

"And if I hold it?" Sefu asked.

"Then we'll see what you're hiding from."

Sefu reached out slowly, as if the compass might bite, and closed his muddy fingers around the case. For a moment nothing happened. Then the needle swung — not toward him, not toward Jude, but upriver, steady and certain, into the dark gorge where the source waited, and held there without a tremor.

"It points at Elias," Sefu said quietly. "Or at what he's carrying. Either way—" he set it down, and his hand was shaking now, only now, "—it agrees with me. Go up. Get him. Get the papers. Before the chute opens and Hale's men do whatever they're paid to do."

Jude looked at the needle. Steady as a nail. In three weeks of running this river with the thing in her pocket, it had never once pointed anywhere useful — only backward, always backward, at the wreck of her old boat and the boy who'd drowned beneath it and the debt she carried like ballast. Now it pointed forward, and she hated how much lighter that made her feel, and how much she distrusted feeling light.

"All right," she said. "Here's how it goes. You ride in the cabin, you touch nothing, and if I catch you signaling anyone on this river, I'll tie you to the anchor and introduce you to the bottom. Marsh vouches for you, and Marsh has never lied to me about anything except cards. That buys you passage. It doesn't buy you trust."

"Trust is for people with time," Sefu said. "We don't have time."

"No," Jude agreed, and stood, and felt the boat rock gently beneath her like something waking up. "We don't."

She stepped out onto the deck. The night had gone clear and hard, stars in their thousands, and upriver the gorge opened its black throat between the walls of the world. Somewhere up there, a broken man was sitting on the truth, and armed men were camped around him, and the glacier was groaning toward its brief, catastrophic opening.

Behind her, from the cabin, Marsh's voice, low: "Jude. You need to see this. There's a launch lamp coming up the slough. Running dark."

Jude turned back toward the water, and the compass in her hand swung hard toward it.
