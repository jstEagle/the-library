## 5. Hale's Launch

The past does not chase you. That is the lie Sefu had told himself for three weeks, hiding in reeds and eating raw fish and telling himself he was waiting, not fleeing. The past waits. It is patient as water. And then one morning you look over your shoulder and it has a motor.

He heard it first — a low, even thrum beneath the river's voice, too steady to be water, too distant to be thunder. He was standing at the stern of Jude Fairweather's boat with a cup of tea going cold in his hands, watching the gorge walls slide past, when the sound arrived like a name being called from far away. His spine knew it before his mind did. He set down the cup very carefully, the way a man sets down something he might need both hands without.

"Jude," he said.

She was at the wheel, shoulders loose, reading the water the way other people read letters from home. "Mm."

"Behind us. Listen."

The thrum again, closer now, threading under the current's noise. Marsh looked up from the engine housing, wrench frozen mid-turn. Even Ada's boy — no, there was no boy aboard; that was another life, another boat — even the river itself seemed to lean away from the sound.

Jude killed the engine. In the sudden quiet the gorge opened its throat: water over stone, wind through the pines, and behind them, unmistakable now, the flat mechanical heartbeat of a launch built for speed and carrying weight.

"Could be anyone," Marsh said. "Fisheries patrol. A trader running—"

"Patrols don't come past the Weir," Sefu said. "That's the whole point of the Weir." He turned. The river bent behind them around a shoulder of granite, and for now the bend hid them, but bends are only mercy on loan. "It's Hale's launch. Forty feet, twin screws, a mounted gun on the foredeck if he's spent what I think he's spent. I've been on it. I helped fuel it."

The words came out of him flat and gray, and once they were out he could not call them back, so he kept going, because Jude was looking at him with those eyes that weighed men like cargo, and he would rather she weigh all of him than only the parts he was proud of.

"You want to know how Elias died," Sefu said. "You deserve to know how he lived first. Both of those things happened on that launch, in a way. Everything did."

* * *

They had left in high summer, eleven of them, with Hale's money in the expedition account and Hale's blessing in their ears. *Survey work*, Corliss Hale had said, smiling his banker's smile across the mahogany of his office, the window behind him full of the city he owned half of. *Above the Weir Line. Purely scientific. The company wants good maps, Mr. Okonkwo, nothing more.*

Elias Okonkwo had come back from that meeting glowing. Sefu remembered it exactly: Elias on the dock, rolling charts under his arm, saying, *Sefu, do you understand what this means? Someone with money finally cares about what's up there.* Sefu had known the upper river since he could walk — his mother's people fished the tributaries before the deeds and the fences came — and he had felt the old cold feeling, the one that says *a gift from a rich man has a hook in it*. But Elias was his friend, and the pay was real, and hope is a powerful solvent. It dissolves suspicion faster than anything.

For six weeks it was good work. Honest work, even. They ran lines, shot elevations, sampled the water where the glacier milked the river white. Then, in the third week of August, Elias found the file.

It was in the survey hut at the source camp, in a locked case that was supposed to hold only instruments. One of Hale's own men — a surveyor named Brandt, a decent man having a crisis of conscience — unlocked it for Elias at two in the morning and then sat shaking by the stove while Elias read.

Sefu had been asleep. He woke to lamplight and Elias's face, which he had never seen like that before and never saw again after. Not fear. Something older than fear. Elias held out a document covered in stamps and signatures, and his hand was steady, and his voice was not.

"The source lands," he said. "The whole watershed. Deeded to Halloway Timber twenty-two years ago. Except the deed is false, Sefu. The signatures are forged — I know the clerk who supposedly signed, he was twelve years old and living two hundred miles downstream. The boundary markers were moved. The whole thing is a fiction, and everything above the Weir Line belongs to people who don't even know it was taken." He laughed, a terrible bright sound. "And we're drawing the maps that make it legal. Hale isn't funding science. He's funding the erasure. We're the pen."

Brandt said, quietly, "There were others who found things. They went home early. All of them."

"Home," Elias said. "Did anyone write?"

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and the silence in the hut got very large.

* * *

After that, the expedition turned on itself the way a body turns on an infection — or turns on its own blood, depending on whose side you believed you were on. Hale's men had been seeded through the crew from the start; Sefu understood that now. Voss, the first mate, who began locking the radio. Kessler, who counted the rations and reported who ate with whom. Within days Elias was eating alone. Within a week, the vote was called.

*We finish the survey,* Voss announced around the cook fire. *Mr. Hale's contract stands. Anyone with objections can take the long walk home.*

Elias stood up. Sefu could still see him — the firelight, the glacier breathing cold off the ice above the tree line, Elias's shadow thrown huge against the tent canvas. "I'm taking the documents home," Elias said. "All of you are welcome to come. When this reaches the newspapers, every one of you will have to explain what you did in these weeks. Come with me and you'll be on the right side of that explanation."

Voss hit him with a lantern. Sefu never learned whether it was planned or temper; it hardly mattered. What mattered was what happened after, because after, there were guns, and the guns had been in the supply crates marked *geological samples* all along.

They buried two men above the tree line before Sefu ran. Brandt was one of them. The other was a boy named Tomas who had signed on to send money to his mother and had never held a gun before and wept the entire time, and was made to hold one anyway.

Sefu ran at night, down the scree, into the timber, with copies of the deed documents sewn into his coat lining because Elias pressed them on him at the last moment — Elias, bleeding, holding him by the collar: *Not me. They'll watch me. You run, Sefu. You get it downstream. Promise me.* And Sefu had promised, and then he had hidden in the reeds for three weeks instead, because the lower river was Hale's country too, and every dock had eyes, and fear is a patient landlord that lets you stay as long as you keep paying in excuses.

Then a widow with iron in her spine had bought a riverboat captain, and the captain had pointed her bow upriver, and here he was.

* * *

"—and that's the shape of it," Sefu finished. The launch's engine note had grown teeth. "Elias may be dead. I don't know. I ran before I knew, and I've hated myself efficiently ever since."

Jude didn't answer at once. She was looking back along the bend, and Sefu watched her do the arithmetic of the river — distance, current, the draft of her boat versus theirs, the fog lying in the side-canyons like spilled milk.

"Fog bank ahead, maybe a mile," she said. "Where the gorge opens at the old burn. It sits low and it sits thick this time of morning, and it doesn't lift till the sun gets over the ridge." She glanced at Marsh. "How's our fuel?"

"Bought us a day, maybe less, if we run her hard," Marsh said. His face had gone the color of dishwater, and Sefu noted that, filed it away. Everyone aboard had something folded up inside them. Sefu was simply the only one who'd stopped pretending his was small.

"Then we spend it," Jude said. "Sefu — the launch. Draft? Shallow enough to follow us into the fog?"

"Shallow enough. But Hale's pilot is a man called Renner, and Renner won't run blind water at speed. He'll slow down. He'll listen." Sefu paused. "And they'll expect us to hide. So don't hide us. Do the opposite."

Something moved at the corner of Jude's mouth — not quite a smile, more like a door opening a crack. "Go loud."

"Loud," Sefu agreed. "Renner trusts his ears. Give him ears full of lies."

What followed took four minutes and cost Sefu ten years of his life. Jude ran the boat wide open toward the fog bank, then, at its edge, swung hard along the burn-scarred shallows where the water hissed over gravel bars — a roar of white noise to drown their own engine — cut the throttle, and let the current carry them silent into the fog while Marsh banged the hull with the wrench, twice, three times, echoes flying off the canyon walls like ghost boats going everywhere at once. Behind them the launch's engine surged, hesitated, surged again, hunting. A voice carried faintly over the water, giving orders. A single rifle shot cracked and went nowhere, hitting nothing but nerve.

Inside the fog the world shrank to a circle ten feet wide: Jude at the wheel reading the current by feel, calling depth to Marsh in a low flat voice, Sefu at the bow with a boathook probing the dark like a blind man's cane. The cold came off the water and into the bones. Once, impossibly close, the launch's wake slapped against the fog wall beside them — so near Sefu could smell diesel and hear a man cough — and then it slid away, chasing an echo down the wrong channel.

They came out the far side twenty minutes later into hard sunlight, alone on the river, the gorge empty ahead and astern.

Nobody cheered. Sefu found he couldn't unclench his hands from the boathook. Jude throttled up gently and set their course, and it was Marsh who finally spoke, staring back at the dissolving fog with an expression Sefu couldn't read.

"They'll pick up our wake below the narrows," Marsh said. "Hale doesn't quit, Sefu. Whatever you think you know about him — it's worse."

"I know," Sefu said. He looked upstream, toward the source, toward the promise he had made a bleeding man in the cold. "But he's not the only one who doesn't quit anymore."

Ahead, the river narrowed between walls of black stone, and somewhere beyond them, high and white and waiting, the glacier held whatever was left of the truth — and possibly, if the river was merciful, a man still breathing.
