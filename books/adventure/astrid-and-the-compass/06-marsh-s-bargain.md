## 6. Marsh's Bargain

Jude woke to the sound of the river and nothing else.

That was the first wrong thing. The *Alder Queen* had a voice at night — the tick of cooling iron in the boiler housing, the creak of Marsh's cot through the bulkhead as he turned over, the soft percussion of his boots when he got up twice a night to check the fuel lines. Twenty years on the water had taught her that silence was never empty. Silence was a room with something standing in it.

She lay still in her bunk and listened harder.

No tick. No creak. No boots.

The engine room hatch stood open when she came down the ladder, and the cold coming off it was the wrong kind of cold — river cold, but also emptiness cold, the draft of a space that had been left open deliberately. She struck the lantern. The fuel reserve — two drums they'd carried past the Weir Line because there would be no buying more above it — was gone. Not drained. Gone. The skid marks of the hand truck scored pale lines in the grime of the deck plates, running from the drum cradle to the aft cargo door, which hung open onto black water.

On the chart table, weighted down with the brass engine-oil can he'd used as a paperweight for twenty years, lay a folded page torn from Elias Okonkwo's journal. She recognized it because she'd spent half the night before reading it by lamplight while Marsh pretended to sleep. The survey coordinates. The Halloway Timber Company's seal on the deed copy. The line Elias had underlined twice: *The source lands were sold before we ever set out. We are not mapping them. We are inventorying stolen goods.*

Except this page wasn't the one she'd read. This page was blank where the figures should be — no, not blank. Cut. Razor work, neat as surgery, the heart of the evidence excised and taken.

Marsh hadn't just run. He'd taken the proof with him.

"Sefu." Her voice came out flat, and she hated how steady it sounded, how competent, as if she were reporting weather instead of ruin. "Sefu, wake up."

He was already awake. He stood in the companionway with his pack over one shoulder, and when he saw her face he didn't ask. He'd been a guide long enough to read a camp the way she read water.

"He's gone," Sefu said.

"The drums, the pages, the man. All of it."

Sefu came down into the cabin and looked at the chart table for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "I told you."

"You told me you didn't trust him. That's not the same as telling me he'd sell us." Jude heard her own voice climb and forced it back down. "You vouched for him. In the reeds, when I wanted to leave you on the bank, it was Marsh who said take him aboard. You've both been vouching for each other since the Weir Line, and now my fuel is on Hale's launch and I'm sitting in a boat that weighs as much as a church and moves like one."

"Then we walk," Sefu said.

"We're forty miles from anywhere, in gorge country, with winter coming down off the ice fields—"

"And Hale's men are forty miles behind us with a fast launch and full tanks, and every hour we sit here arguing, they're closer." Sefu set his pack down on the bench. His hands were steady. Hers weren't, and she put them in her pockets so he wouldn't see. "Captain. I have walked this river's spine since I was twelve years old. There is a path along the east wall of the gorge — goat track, then survey cut, then nothing but stone and nerve. It passes above the cache where your boat's spare gear sits. It reaches the glacier country in three days if the weather holds and four if it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we learn what Elias learned about waiting."

* * *

She nearly turned back. She wants that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the river keeps — that at dawn, standing on her own afterdeck with the mist rising off the water like breath off a wound, Jude Fairweather stood with her hand on the wheel and seriously considered pointing the dead-weighted *Alder Queen* downstream and running until the Weir Line was behind her and the whole rotten business belonged to other people.

It wasn't fear of Hale. Or it was, but fear had never stopped her before; fear was just ballast, something you trimmed for. What stopped her was simpler and uglier. If she went on, she would find Elias Okonkwo or his body, and either way she would have to stand in front of Ada Okonkwo afterward and say *I found your husband because you paid me,* and the debt beneath the debt — the one with Daniel's name on it, the boy who drowned when her old boat went down with her at the wheel — would come due again, with interest, in a currency she couldn't print.

Turning back wasn't cowardice, she told herself. Turning back was prudence. A captain protects her vessel. A captain doesn't spend lives on lost causes.

The compass sat in her coat pocket, her father's strange inheritance, the needle that pointed not north but toward whatever she most refused to face. All night it had swung wild whenever she spoke — steadying when she named the truth, shivering when she dressed it up. She took it out now, cupped in her palm on the empty afterdeck, and watched the needle tremble like a trapped thing.

"Prudence," she said aloud.

The needle shivered hard against its glass.

"I'm turning back because it's the smart play."

Shivering. Worse. The glass buzzed faintly under her thumb.

"It's because I'm afraid."

Steadier. Almost level. Pointing upriver, up-gorge, toward the white shoulder of the glacier just visible between the canyon walls.

And then, because rock bottom has a way of making honest people of us, she said the rest of it.

"I let Daniel drown." The words came out of her like splinters, one at a time. "Not the way Ada thinks — not drunk, not careless. I froze. Four seconds, maybe five, with the line in my hand and him in the water, and I froze because throwing it meant admitting the boat was lost, and I couldn't admit it, so I did nothing, and he died in the time it took me to be brave enough to fail." Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "I came upriver to prove I'm not that woman. Not for Ada. Not for Elias. For me. Because if I turn around now, I'll be her forever — the captain who watches people drown while she does the arithmetic."

The needle settled. Dead steady. Pointing upriver like a finger.

Jude stood there a long moment with the compass in her hand and the truth sitting in her chest where the guilt had been, and found — to her fury — that it weighed less.

"All right," she said to the river, to the needle, to the drowned boy whose face she hadn't let herself see in six years. "All right."

* * *

They stripped the *Alder Queen* to what backs could carry: rope, flares, the tin box of rations, Elias's journal with its mutilated pages, the boat's papers sealed in oilcloth and buried beneath the floorboards where only a captain would think to look. Jude wrote a note in case anyone ever came this way again — *Vessel abandoned above Mile 40, gorge east side, crew proceeding overland, date* — and felt absurd doing it, leaving a message in a bottle for a world that might not exist yet.

At the gangway Sefu paused and looked back at the boat.

"She'll keep," Jude said. "Iron hull. She's sunk once already and floated anyway. Stubborn as her captain."

"That," Sefu said, "is what worries me about you both."

The path began where a survey crew had blasted a shelf into the gorge wall thirty years ago and the mountain had been taking it back ever since. They climbed single file, the river dropping away below them until it was a green ribbon stitched through gray stone, and by noon Jude's hands were bleeding through her gloves and she had stopped thinking about anything except the next hold, the next step, the next hold. It was a mercy. Grief and betrayal were luxuries for flat ground.

They made camp at dusk in the mouth of an old mine adit, and it was there, splitting kindling with a rock, that Sefu finally said what he'd been carrying since morning.

"Marsh taught me to read the current above the Weir," he said. "First night out. He sat with me four hours, drawing it in the condensation on the window. Nobody had ever—" He stopped. Set the kindling down. "I know what it looks like. But Captain, I knew that man, and men don't fake twenty years of friendship to steal two drums of fuel."

"Men fake less for more," Jude said. "Hale's price must have been high."

"Or the price was something money can't buy." Sefu fed the sticks into their small fire and watched it catch. "Elias trusted the wrong man too. Corliss Hale didn't buy our expedition with threats. He bought it with rescue — Elias's daughter was sick that winter, and Hale paid the hospital bills nobody else could. Elias spent two years trying to pay it back before he understood what the repayment was for." He looked up at her across the flames. "What does Marsh owe, Captain? Who did he love that needed saving?"

Jude opened her mouth to say *nothing, no one, Marsh had no one* — and stopped. Because there had been a letter last spring, passed to her at the dock without explanation, postmarked from the coast, in a doctor's careful hand. Marsh had kept it folded in his toolbox ever since. She'd never asked. On the river you didn't ask; everyone hauled some weight below the waterline.

She thought of the neat razor cuts in the journal page. Of the missing figures — but only the figures. The accusation, the seal, the names of the guilty: all left behind for her to find. A thief burns the evidence. A man planning something else leaves you the story and takes only the part he needs.

"Three days' walk," she said slowly. "If he sold us, Hale knows exactly where we are and we're dead in the gorge. If he didn't sell us—" She shook her head. "Then he's playing a game I can't see, with stakes I can't afford, and I'm going to strangle him with my own rope either way."

The fire cracked. Above the adit's mouth, between the black walls of the gorge, the glacier's shoulder caught the last light and burned white, and far up there — so faint she might have imagined it — something flashed. Once. Twice. Three times.

Evenly spaced. Deliberate.

Sefu was on his feet, staring. "Signal mirror," he breathed. "From the ice fields. Someone knows we're coming."

Jude pulled out the compass. The needle didn't waver. It pointed straight up the gorge, straight at the flashing light, steady as a heartbeat — and beside it, three days' hard climbing away, something on the glacier was calling them forward, or calling them in.
