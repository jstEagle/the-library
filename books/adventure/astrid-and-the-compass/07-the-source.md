## 7. The Source

The trees gave out the way a voice gives out at the end of a long shout — not all at once, but thinning, cracking, until there was nothing left but the memory of green.

Sefu had walked this slope once before, three months ago, with eleven companions and a client's money in his pocket and no idea he was walking into a grave. Now he climbed it again with only Jude Fairweather beside him, and his body remembered every step the way a scar remembers the knife. The altitude pressed on his chest like a hand. His ribs, still knitting from the beating Hale's men had given him, sent a white flare of pain up his side with every hundred yards, and he did not let himself stop, because stopping was how you died up here. He'd learned that too.

Jude climbed like she rowed — steady, economical, furious at nothing and everything. She hadn't spoken in two hours. Neither had he. There was a language between them now that didn't need words: the angle of her chin when she wanted him to look at something, the flat of his palm raised when he heard movement that wasn't wind. They'd been speaking it since the gorge, since Marsh's empty bedroll and the missing fuel, since Jude had stood on the riverbank with her father's compass trembling in her fist and finally said aloud the thing she'd been carrying for years.

*I came to prove I'm not a coward,* she'd said. *That's the truth. That's where the needle points.*

Sefu had said nothing then. He was thinking of it now, because the needle had gone still as a dead man's pulse, and stillness in that compass meant one thing only: they were close to whatever Jude Fairweather most refused to face. He wondered what shape it would take. A body. A grave. A truth about Elias Okonkwo that neither of them wanted spoken.

The glacier appeared around a shoulder of black rock without warning, the way grief does.

It filled the head of the valley wall to wall, a river of ice frozen mid-fall, blue-green in its depths and scoured white on top, cracked everywhere with crevasses that glowed like veins. Above it the sky was so clean and hard it looked cut from glass. And below the ice tongue, cradled in a basin of gray stone, lay the source pool — water so still and so palely turquoise it seemed less like water than like light that had forgotten how to move.

Sefu stopped. He couldn't help it. Even knowing what waited here — even carrying what he carried — the sight took him at the knees.

*Elias stood right there,* he thought. *Right there, on that flat stone, when he first saw it.*

"The source," Jude said quietly. Not a question. She was reading the water the way she read everything — for current, for danger, for the lie underneath. "All this," she said, "and they deeded it away with a signature."

"Two signatures," Sefu said. "And one of them forged."

He told her then, because she'd earned it and because the pool made lying feel obscene. How the Halloway Timber Company's deed claimed title to the entire upper basin through a survey that had never been run. How Elias had found the original field notes in a government archive — notes proving the boundary markers had been placed by men who never left their office in Corliss Hale's pay. How Elias had copied everything, twice, and hidden the copies apart from each other, because he was the kind of careful man who understood exactly what careful men get killed for.

"And Hale knew," Jude said.

"Hale always knew." Sefu watched the light move on the pool. "We weren't an expedition, Captain. We were deniability. He sent us upriver to find anything that could contradict the old survey before the land transfer went through — and to die politely if we found it instead."

* * *

The survey hut sat on the eastern shore of the pool, half-rotted, its tarpaper skin peeled back like old bark. Sefu had helped pitch it in June, back when the expedition still believed in itself. Seeing it again was like seeing a photograph of someone before their funeral.

Jude saw the smoke before he did.

A thread of it, thin as spider silk, rising from behind the hut's broken leeward wall. Not a campfire's smoke — too small, too disciplined. Someone who knew how to stay alive alone in cold country.

Sefu's heart went off like a signal flare. He grabbed Jude's arm and pointed, and for a moment they were both frozen, two hunters afraid the animal might be a ghost.

Then the door scraped open, and Elias Okonkwo came out into the daylight.

He was thinner than the man Sefu had guided upriver. Thinner, grayer, moving like a man whose bones had been rearranged by violence and set badly. But he was upright. He was breathing. And when his eyes found Sefu across the hundred yards of gray stone, he made a sound Sefu had never heard from him before — not a word, just a raw syllable, the sound a man makes when hope arrives too late to be trusted.

They reached each other at a stumbling half-run. Elias gripped Sefu's shoulders, then his face, then stepped back as if to check he wasn't a mirage, and laughed — a broken, disbelieving laugh that turned halfway through into something else entirely.

"You're alive," Elias said. "God forgive me, Sefu, I thought — when you ran, I thought they'd caught you. I thought I'd signed your death warrant too."

"You saved my life," Sefu said. "You told me to go. You told me *run*, and I ran, and I have hated myself every day since—"

"Don't." Elias's voice cracked like river ice. "Don't you dare apologize for living. I've had enough of men dying for my sake."

Jude had hung back. Sefu saw why when he turned: she was staring at Elias Okonkwo with an expression Sefu recognized from his own mirror. She had taken a widow's contract to find a drowned man, and she had spent the whole climb rehearsing how she'd tell Ada the truth. The truth was standing in front of her, alive, and it had knocked every word she owned out of her hands.

"Captain Fairweather," Elias said, extending a hand that shook. "Ada hired you?"

"She did." Jude took the hand. Her voice came out low and rough. "She thinks you're dead, Mr. Okonkwo. Three weeks of thinking it. I'm going to enjoy being the one to fix that."

"Not yet." All the joy went out of Elias's face at once, the way sun goes off a mountain when cloud crosses it. "Not until you hear what I have to say. Because if you take me down this river the way things stand, all three of us will be dead inside a day, and the documents will be ash, and Hale will own the source forever."

* * *

Inside the hut, among rusted instruments and a cot stained with three months of solitary living, Elias spread his proof on the floor: oilcloth-wrapped bundles of paper, copied in a careful draftsman's hand, cross-referenced and annotated. The forged survey. The archive records. Letters between Hale and the land commissioner that Elias had photographed by candlelight in a locked records room while Hale's men drank downstairs.

Jude read like she steered — fast, total, missing nothing. When she looked up, her eyes were bright and cold.

"This hangs him."

"It should." Elias lowered himself onto the cot with the care of a much older man. "But paper doesn't hang anyone, Captain. Paper has to reach someone with the courage to print it. And between here and a printing press lies—" he gestured vaguely southward, downriver, down everything, "—the chute."

Sefu felt his stomach drop. He'd been avoiding the thought since the tree line. Now Elias said it plainly.

"The meltwater chute. You've seen the glacier calving?"

"I've seen it," Sefu said. "In June. The ice dam at the outlet groans for days and then lets go, and the pool drains down the gorge in a flood you could float a church on. For maybe two days the chute runs deep enough to navigate. After that it drops to a trickle and the gorge is a coffin."

"Two days," Elias agreed. "Maybe less this year. The calving started early — I've been listening to it every night. And Hale knows the schedule better than I do, because Hale's engineers built the original timber flume through that gorge sixty years ago. He knows exactly what the meltwater opens, and exactly when."

Jude went very still. Sefu watched her arrive at it a half-second before Elias said it.

"He's waiting for us."

"He's *camped* at the chute." Elias's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Six men, two launches, and a radio. They've been there nine days. They're not guarding the chute against us, Captain — they don't know I'm alive. They're guarding it against the *water*. Because the moment the melt floods that gorge, any boat on this river can come up and carry my evidence downstream past the Weir Line, and Hale's whole empire becomes a court exhibit. So he'll hold the choke point until the window closes. And if a certain riverboat captain happens to come paddling into his gunsight during those two days—"

"He gets to bury the evidence and the witnesses in the same flood," Jude finished.

Silence. Outside, faint as a heartbeat under the floorboards, came the sound Sefu had been hearing for two days and trying not to name: a deep, rhythmic concussion rolling down from the glacier, like the mountain clearing its throat. Calving. The ice dam was already beginning to fail.

"So we wait for the chute and walk into his guns," Jude said slowly, "or we sit here until the window closes and the evidence dies with us." She looked at the compass in her open palm. Its needle lay steady, pointing not north but at the hut's far wall — at Elias, at the papers, at the thing she'd crossed a forbidden river refusing to face. "There's a third option," she said. "There's always a third option. It's just usually the one that costs the most."

Elias and Sefu looked at her.

"Hale's watching the chute," Jude said. "So we don't come down the chute. Not first. We make him come to *us* — or we make him believe we already have. I've got a boat cached below the gorge, an engineer I no longer trust, and a talent for running water nobody thinks is runnable." A grim ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Mr. Okonkwo, how do you feel about going home the loud way?"

Before Elias could answer, Sefu raised a hand. He'd been listening past them, down the valley, toward the chute — and beneath the glacier's slow thunder he had caught something new. An engine. Faint, wrong, approaching.

Not from downriver.

From above the pool, along the ridge trail — the one route in or out that Hale's men shouldn't have known existed.

"They found another way in," Sefu said, and reached for his pack. "Captain — whatever the third option is, it just got shorter."
