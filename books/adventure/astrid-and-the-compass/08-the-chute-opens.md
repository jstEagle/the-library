## 8. The Chute Opens

The engine sound died before dawn, which was worse than if it had kept coming.

"They've stopped where they can watch the chute," Sefu said, crouched at the lip of the ridge with binoculars he'd taken off a dead man two years ago and never explained. "Four of them. Maybe five. They're not moving because they don't have to. They know the ice is going to do their work for them."

Jude lay beside him in the scree, her breath fogging, and looked down at what three weeks of climbing had bought them a view of: the meltwater chute, a slot of gray-green water cut between walls of blue ice, dammed at its head by a tongue of the glacier itself. Above it, the source pool sat flat and pale as a coin. Below it, six hundred feet of falling water threaded through rock that no chart had ever named.

And moored in the cache cove below the chute — *her* boat. The *Alder Rose*, hauled out on skids by Elias's crew last spring, tarped and hidden, waiting like a promise someone else had made on her behalf. From this height she could see the tarp had slipped. The hull gleamed wet. Hale's men would have found it already. They'd left it there because a boat without fuel was just a coffin with good lines.

"Two days," Elias said behind them. He'd come up the ridge slower, favoring the leg they'd splinted with a survey stake, but his voice was steady in the way of men who have already decided how they'll die and find it restful. "Maybe less. When the calving starts, the dam groans for an hour, then it goes. The chute fills fast — faster than you'd think. You'll have one window. One."

"And when it closes?"

"It closes like a door," Elias said. "Not gradually. All at once."

Jude rolled onto her back and looked at the sky, which was doing that high-altitude thing of being too blue to trust. Her father's compass hung at her throat inside her shirt, and she didn't need to take it out to know what it was doing. It hadn't steadied since Marsh walked off into the dark with her fuel and her father's journal pages. It spun slow, sick circles against her sternum, like something drowning.

Except that wasn't true anymore either, was it. She'd admitted the truth to herself on the gorge trail — that she hadn't come for Ada's money or even Ada's grief, that she'd come because she needed the river to tell her she wasn't the woman who let Daniel Okonkwo drown while she saved herself. And the needle had gone still as a held breath ever since. It pointed now, steady and merciless, straight down the chute.

Toward the boat. Toward the water. Toward the thing she most refused to face, which had never been Hale or the Weir Line or any man's gun.

It was the rapid itself.

* * *

They spent the day preparing the way soldiers prepare — badly, quickly, with jokes that fool no one.

Elias had copies of everything. Deeds, surveys, correspondence with the land office, letters in Corliss Hale's own hand arranging the "acquisition" of forty miles of riverhead from families who had never sold. He'd wrapped the originals in oilcloth and sewn them into the lining of Sefu's pack, and duplicates into Jude's jacket, because — as he put it — "paper should travel the way seeds do. Widely, and expecting some of it to be eaten."

The flares came from the expedition's signal kit, eleven sticks of them, and Sefu laid them out on a flat rock like a card sharp showing his hand. "I can hold the ridge trail for maybe twenty minutes," he said. "Flares, noise, looking like more men than we are. After twenty minutes I'm going to start running downhill very fast."

"You're not holding anything," Jude said. "You're both getting clear the moment I cast off. That's the whole plan. That's the only plan."

"Captain." Sefu smiled, and it was the first time she'd seen him smile since the reeds. "You are about to run a rapid that has killed every living thing that ever tried it. Do you really want to spend your last argument on us?"

She wanted to say yes. She wanted to say a great many things. What she said was: "Twenty minutes. Then you run."

At midday the glacier spoke.

It wasn't thunder, exactly. Thunder comes and goes. This was a note — deep, structural, felt in the teeth and the back of the knees — that rose out of the icefield above the pool and did not stop. Snow slithered off the seracs in long white ribbons. Birds she hadn't known were there lifted off the scree all at once, a single startled exhalation of wings.

"The dam's talking," Elias said. He was pale. "That's the hour. Go, Jude. *Go.*"

She went.

* * *

Down the ridge she ran the way she'd run riverbanks all her life, reading ground three steps ahead, letting her legs make the small decisions so her mind could hold the large ones. Behind and above her, Sefu's first flare went up with a sound like a struck match the size of a church, and red light washed the snow, and men began shouting on the trail in voices that had expected an easy morning.

The cache cove opened below her, and there was the *Alder Rose*, and there — sprawled around her like men guarding a corpse — were two of Hale's crew with rifles across their knees, watching the chute instead of the boat, because who in God's name would come *down* to a boat with no way to run it?

Jude came down to it.

She hit the first man from the rocks above, all her falling weight and the climbing axe Sefu had insisted she carry, and he went down hard and stayed down. The second got his rifle halfway up — and then a flare burst directly over him, blinding white, and Sefu's voice came off the ridge yelling orders in three languages to a squad that didn't exist, and the man flinched upward into the light, and Jude's second blow was enough.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, the way you notice weather, and then she made them stop.

The tarp came off. The fuel — a reserve drum Elias's crew had cached under the floorboards, because Elias Okonkwo planned for betrayal the way other men planned for rain — went into the tank. The engine caught on the third turn, coughed, caught again, and settled into that idle she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Above the chute, the glacier's note climbed half an octave.

She took the wheel. The compass hung out of her collar now, swinging free, and its needle pointed dead ahead down the boiling gray slot of the chute, and Jude Fairweather — who had not gone upriver in four years, who had not slept a whole night since the *Larkspur* went down, who had built a life the size of a cargo manifest so she'd never have to look at the water and see a boy's face — put her hands on the wheel and throttled up.

"Terror transmuted into purpose," she said aloud, to no one, because her father used to say it about storms, and then the chute took her.

* * *

The first hundred yards were just speed — the meltwater surge lifting the hull like a hand, the ice walls flickering past close enough to touch, spray coming aboard cold as judgment. She'd imagined terror. What she got instead was clarity, terrible and clean, the old gift of the river: every eddy line visible, every hole readable, the current laying itself out before her like text she'd been born knowing how to read.

Then the first rapid stood up in front of her.

It came out of the chute's narrowest point, where a fallen serac pinched the channel to perhaps twice the boat's beam, and the water through the gap was standing nearly vertical, folding over itself, white from wall to wall. Below it, the tailrace hammered into a wave train that looked like the river trying to climb into the sky.

The ice groaned overhead. A chunk the size of a wagon calved off the right wall and hit the water ten feet astern, and the shockwave slapped the *Alder Rose*'s transom and shoved her toward the gap whether she willed it or not.

So she willed it.

She squared the bow, found the tongue — the smooth V of green water threading the fold — and drove the boat into it with both hands locked and her heart going like a drum in a parade. The world became noise and white. The bow rose until she was looking at nothing but sky, hung there in a silence that lasted a year, and then the hull slammed down the far side and the wave train took her, throwing the boat left, right, burying the rails, tearing the tarp cleat off the deck and sending it spinning away downstream like a coin into a well.

She steered by feel. By faith. By the compass needle pressed cold against her breastbone, pointing down, always down.

And then — open water. The tail of the rapid spat her out into the broadening channel below, and the chute walls fell back, and the *Alder Rose* surged forward into current that was running fast but honest, runnable, *hers*.

Behind her, the glacier finished its sentence. The whole headwall of the dam let go at once — not a calving but a collapse, a thousand tons of blue ice dropping into the surge — and the sound came down the canyon after her like the door Elias had promised, slamming shut. In her mirror she watched the white wall of it swallow the chute she'd just come through, watched the channel begin to drown, watched the window close.

On the ridge above, two flares went up together, red and red, and she understood: Sefu and Elias, running, alive, driving Hale's men away from the water and toward nothing.

She had done it. She was through. The documents rode against her ribs; the truth rode in the boat; the river carried all of it downstream toward Ada, toward the newspapers, toward the debt—

The engine stuttered.

Once. Twice. Caught. Stuttered again, and this time did not catch, and the *Alder Rose* went quiet on the current, drifting, while from the bend ahead — from the flat water where a launch could wait — came the low, satisfied growl of a much larger engine turning to meet her.

Corliss Hale had not waited at the chute after all.

He had come down to the only place a riverboat must arrive.
