## 9. Compass Point

The chute was closing when Hale boarded.

Jude heard him before she saw him — the flat slap of a hull against hers, a grapnel hook singing through the air, and then the jolt that nearly tore the wheel from her hands. His launch came alongside like a predator flanking a wounded thing, riding the same dying meltwater, and three men in oilskins swung aboard before she could answer.

She had the boat through the first rapid. That was all she'd had time for. The *Alder Rose* — Ada's money, Marsh's engine, her father's compass lashed to the wheel housing — was committed now, running the throat of the chute with the ice walls leaning in on both sides like a cathedral collapsing in slow motion.

"Hold her steady, Captain!" Hale's voice carried over the roar. He stood at the rail of his launch, gray-haired and immaculate even here, even now, as if the river were a boardroom he'd simply decided to redecorate. "You're carrying stolen property. I only want what's mine."

"Nothing up here is yours!" she shouted back, and the compass needle — she saw it, she *saw* it — swung true and hard toward him. Toward the thing she most refused to face. Not the ice. Not the water. The fact that she had known men like Hale her whole life, had taken their cargo, had kept their silence, and had never once said *no* out loud.

The second rapid took them.

It came out of the chute's narrowest point, where a calved block had grounded itself mid-channel and the water piled against it in a standing wave the height of a chapel door. The *Alder Rose* climbed it, hung, and came down so hard that Jude's knees buckled and the wheel spun free, and somewhere aft a crate of Elias's documents tore loose and skidded across the deck.

One of Hale's men went for it.

And Marsh came out of the engine room like a man rising from his own grave.

* * *

She had believed him a traitor for six days. She had cursed his name in the gorge, had told Sefu he'd sold them all for a tank of diesel, had carried the betrayal up the mountain like a stone in her boot. Even when he'd reappeared at the cache below the chute — breathless, unrepentant, claiming he had a plan she wouldn't like — she'd let him aboard only because the engine wouldn't run without him.

Now she understood the plan.

Marsh caught the document case one-handed, spun, and held it over the rail above the boiling water. "Corliss!" he bellowed. "You want to know what I've been doing since I took your money? *Listening.*"

He raised his other hand. In it was the tape recorder — the little brass-and-bakelite machine Jude had seen a hundred times in his kit and assumed was for logging engine hours. The reel was turning. It had been turning, she realized, since the moment Hale's men came aboard.

"Every word," Marsh called. "The deed fraud. the survey you buried. What you told your boys to do to Elias Okonkwo's crew. It's all on tape, Corliss. It's been on tape since the night you bought me at the fuel dock."

Hale's face did something terrible and quiet. "You're a dead man, Marsh."

"Probably," Marsh said. "But dead men's tapes have a way of washing up downstream."

The first gunshot took the wheel housing apart an arm's length from Jude's head.

* * *

After that, the world was noise and water.

She threw herself flat as the second shot shattered the compass glass — her father's compass, the needle still turning inside the cracked dome, still pointing at Hale, still pointing at the truth — and then the chute's third rapid arrived and there was no more time for anything except survival.

The ice dam above them was failing. She could hear it — not a sound so much as a *pressure*, a groan that came up through the hull and into her teeth, the sound of a mountain deciding. When it went, the chute would flood and then choke with ice, and everything between the walls — her boat, Hale's launch, every man on both — would be ground to nothing against the narrows below.

"Hold on!" she screamed, to Marsh, to Sefu at the stern with a flare pistol, to Elias huddled in the wheelhouse with the copies of the deed strapped to his chest like a life vest. "Everybody hold on!"

Hale's launch tried to come alongside again. The grapnel caught her rail. Two of Hale's men hauled, and the *Alder Rose* slewed sideways toward the ice wall, toward the black water shearing off the glacier's face, and Jude understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful that she was going to die here, in the water, again, and that someone she owed would pay for it again.

Then Marsh let go of the rail.

He crossed the tilting deck in three strides, put his shoulder into the grapnel line, and cut it — but the men on Hale's launch had hooked a second line, and there was no time to cut that one too. So instead of cutting it, he *pulled*. He threw his whole weight against the line, dragging the two boats apart instead of together, dragging himself over the rail and down onto Hale's deck in the same motion, a trade no one had asked him to make.

"Marsh!" His name tore out of her.

He looked up at her once. He was smiling. He had that same smile from thirty years ago, two kids running cargo they shouldn't have been running, and he shouted something the river ate whole — she would spend the rest of her life deciding it had been *tell Ada I'm sorry, and tell her husband's widow the truth* — and then the ice wall calved.

The whole face of it, a hundred feet of blue-white, came down between the boats, and the wave it raised picked up Hale's launch like a toy and drove it into the narrows. Jude saw men in the water. She saw Hale clinging to his rail, his immaculate coat finally soaked through, his mouth open in a scream she couldn't hear. She saw Marsh, still on the deck, still holding the tape recorder above the water as the wave closed over him — holding it up, up, *up*, offering it to the sky like evidence at a trial.

Then the white water took everything.

* * *

She ran the last rapid blind.

There was no wheel — the housing was splinters — so she steered with the engine, Marsh's engine, his hands' work even now, feathering the throttle left and right while the *Alder Rose* surfed the flood surge down the chute's final throat. Sefu braced Elias in the wheelhouse. The compass, cracked and bleeding glass, lay in her palm instead of its housing, and she held it the way you hold a dying man's hand, and its needle never wavered.

It pointed downriver. It pointed *out*.

The chute spat them into the broad gray light of the lower river, and the ice dam closed behind them with a sound like the world's largest door slamming — the meltwater dying in minutes, the navigable window shutting for another year, the whole cathedral of it collapsing into silence.

Jude stood at the broken wheel and shook.

* * *

They found the tape recorder a mile downstream, snagged in the reeds. Marsh's body they never found at all, and Jude could not decide, standing in the shallows with the machine dripping in her hands, whether that was mercy or theft. The reel inside was intact. She played it there in the reeds with Sefu and Elias standing silent beside her, and Hale's voice came out of the little speaker — calm, reasonable, monstrous — laying out the deed fraud, the deniable expedition, the men he'd sent upriver to disappear.

Elias sat down in the water when it finished. He didn't cry. He just sat, a man hearing the death of his crew confirmed by the killer's own voice, and Sefu put a hand on his shoulder and left it there.

Jude looked at the compass. The glass was shattered. The needle rested easy for the first time since her father had put it in her hands — no trembling, no pulling, just a quiet, level insistence on the truth of where she was.

She still owed the Okonkwos. That debt had not been paid in money, and it would not be paid in money now.

But she had something better than money, and it was wrapped in oilcloth in the wheelhouse, and there was a widow three hundred miles downstream who had hired her to find a truth and deserved to hear it from the woman who carried it.

Jude wiped the tape dry, stowed the recorder beside Elias's documents, and took the wheel.

"Ada first," she said. "Then the newspapers. Then we burn Hale's whole rotten world down to the waterline."

The *Alder Rose* turned her bow downstream, and the river — wide, slow, and finally, mercifully ordinary — carried them home.
