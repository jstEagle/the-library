## 10. Downstream

The river forgave nothing, but it forgot everything. That was the bargain Jude had always understood it to offer, and she had spent six years believing she'd made a different one.

She stood at the wheel of the *Alder Rose* on the third morning below the Weir Line, watching the water run wide and brown-gold in the early light, and felt the strange weightlessness of a boat that was no longer carrying anything but itself. No documents sewn into oilskin. No tape reel wrapped in her spare shirt. No dead man's survey notes pressed flat beneath the chart table where they had ridden the whole way down like ballast. She had handed all of it over at the *Herald-Dispatch* offices two days ago — every page, every recording, Elias Okonkwo's careful hand describing a boundary stone that had been moved forty years ago and never moved back — and the moment the editor's clerk signed for the lot, Jude had walked out onto the street and discovered that her legs didn't know what to do with her.

The newspapers had done what newspapers do. By yesterday morning Halloway Timber's name was in every headline from Alder Bend to the coast, and by yesterday afternoon Corliss Hale's lawyers were issuing statements that read like men shouting into a rising flood. The fraudulent deeds were already collapsing; the registrar had suspended them pending inquiry, which everyone understood to mean forever. There was talk of a trust, of the source lands held in perpetuity, of Elias Okonkwo's name spoken in the legislature by men who had never once said it before.

Jude had read none of it past the first column. She couldn't. Every time she saw his name in print she heard Sefu's voice saying *he was alive when I ran*, and she heard her own silence answering.

Now the river carried her south through country she knew better than her own hands, and the compass sat on the ledge beside the wheel, its needle resting easy for the first time since her father had put it there. Not pointing north. Not pointing anywhere. Just lying quiet in its brass housing like something finally allowed to sleep.

* * *

Ada Okonkwo was waiting at the Fairweather dock when Jude came in on the evening tide, and Jude knew before she tied off that this would be the hardest run of the season.

Ada stood the way she had stood the night she'd hired him — no, hired *her*; Jude corrected herself even now, three weeks into being honest about small things — upright, composed, a woman who had learned to make dignity do the work of armor. But there were new lines around her mouth, and when Jude stepped down onto the planks, Ada did not speak first. She looked past Jude at the boat, at the patched hull, at the scorch along the starboard rail that no amount of paint would hide, and Jude watched her count everything that was missing from it.

"Where is he," Ada said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He's at the hospital in Alder Bend." Jude kept her voice level. "Broken leg, frostbite in two fingers, a fever they're still fighting. But he's alive, Mrs. Okonkwo. He's alive, and he's asking for you."

Something moved through Ada's face like current under ice — hope and fury tangled so tightly together that neither could surface alone. "Three weeks," she said. "I buried my husband. I sat shiva over an empty box because they told me—"

"They told you what Hale wanted you told." Jude took a breath. This was the part she had rehearsed on the whole long run downriver, and every rehearsal had failed, because there was no version of this that could be made clean. "Mrs. Okonkwo, I'm not going to apologize to you. I want you to hear why."

Ada's eyes came up sharp. "You have some nerve—"

"I do. I've had it for six years." Jude planted her feet on the dock her father had built, on the wood she'd been avoiding looking at since the day she'd come home without Daniel Okonkwo aboard. "When your son died, I told everyone — including myself — that it was weather. A squall that came up fast. And part of that was true. But the other part, the part I kept below the waterline, is that I pushed us out that morning because I was behind on a fuel payment and I didn't want to lose the contract. I read the sky wrong because I wanted to read it wrong. Daniel asked me to turn back twice. Twice. And I told him the *Rose* could take worse than she could give."

The dock was very quiet. Somewhere down the shore a loon called, and the water slapped the pilings in the old patient rhythm.

"I let him drown," Jude said. "Not with my hands. With my pride. And I have spent six years paying money to your family because money was the only debt I could pay without ever having to say that sentence out loud. Your husband went upriver hunting the truth about stolen land, and I went upriver after him partly for you, partly for the contract — and partly because I needed to prove I wasn't the coward who let a man die for wanting something too much. That's the truth. You paid me to find Elias. What you actually bought was the first honest thing I've done since the night your son went into the water."

Ada was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had gone low and rough. "Elias knew. About Daniel. He knew, and he still came to you." She shook her head slowly. "He said a person who hides from a thing will drown in it eventually, and a person who faces it might swim. He said you were swimming, Jude. He said it the day he left. I thought he was being kind."

"He wasn't being kind," Jude said. "He was being right. That's worse."

And then Ada Okonkwo did the thing Jude had braced against for six years and never believed would actually come: she crossed the dock and took Jude's scarred hands in both of hers.

"The debt is forgiven," she said. "Not because it's paid. It will never be paid — you're right about that, and don't you dare pretend otherwise. It's forgiven because you finally stopped hiding from it, and a debt hidden grows in the dark like mold, and a debt spoken—" Her voice caught. "A debt spoken can be set down. Elias is alive. Bring me to him tomorrow, and we will call the whole account closed, and you will sit at our table, and you will not flinch."

Jude found she could not speak at all. She nodded instead, and the compass in her pocket — she'd carried it ashore without thinking — lay against her hip with its needle resting easy, and she understood at last what her father had been trying to tell her all those years: that the thing you most refuse to face is not a punishment. It's a heading.

* * *

They went up to Alder Bend together the next morning, and Jude waited outside the ward while Ada walked down the aisle of white curtains toward the bed by the window, and she did not need to see the reunion to know how it went. She heard it anyway — the city couldn't have drowned it out — Ada's cry, low and cracked, and then Elias's voice, ruined and rusted and unmistakably alive, saying his wife's name like a man surfacing from deep water.

Sefu found her on the hospital steps, moving carefully on the leg the ice had nearly taken, and lowered himself down beside her without asking.

"Marsh's funeral is Thursday," he said. "His sister's coming up from the coast. They're burying the tape player with him. His idea, beforehand — he wrote it in the letter he left. Said if he was going to die for a recording, the machine ought to get a plot too." Sefu huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Vain to the last."

"He saved my life," Jude said. "After selling me out to save it. I still haven't worked out which one of those was the real Marsh."

"Both. All of him, all at once. Like the rest of us." Sefu looked out at the harbor, where the morning ferries were cutting their white wakes. "Hale's people offered me a deal yesterday. Quiet settlement, my silence, a number with a lot of zeros. I told them where they could put their zeros." He glanced sideways at her. "You'll be asked too. They'll come at you through your license, your debts, your boat. Hale doesn't forgive, even ruined."

"Let him come," Jude said, and found that she meant it, and found further that meaning it cost her nothing — that somewhere between the calving glacier and this sunlit step, fear had quietly packed its things and left.

* * *

A week later, on a bright cold morning with the first true bite of autumn in it, Jude took the *Alder Rose* out alone past the breakwater and opened her up on open water.

There was no cargo in her hold. No contract in her pocket. For the first time since she'd inherited the boat and the debt and the name, Jude Fairweather was running the river for no reason at all except that the river was there, and she was her captain, and the sky had that washed, enormous clarity that comes after weather.

She stood at the wheel with the wind pulling at her collar and let the boat run. Behind her the town shrank to smoke and steeples. Ahead the water stretched silver to the horizon, uncharted by anything except her own two eyes, and the compass sat on the ledge beside her with its needle lying still — not pointing at her guilt, not pointing at her father's ghost, not pointing anywhere but forward, which was, she understood now, the only direction it had ever truly meant.

Somewhere upstream, above the Weir Line, the source pool lay blue and ancient under the glacier, held in trust now for everyone, its secret given back to the light. Somewhere downstream, the papers were printing Elias's words in ink that would not wash out. And here, in the middle, on the open water, a woman who had spent six years drowning in a sentence she wouldn't say had finally said it, and the river — which forgets everything, which forgives nothing, which carries all of it regardless — carried her on.

Jude Fairweather put her hand on the wheel, and the *Alder Rose* ran clean and true toward the horizon, and for the first time in as long as she could remember, the captain was not running from anything at all.

She was just going where the water went.
