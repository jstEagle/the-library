## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The sea off Grayhaven Point is at its most honest an hour before dawn — cold enough to kill a lie, dark enough to hide one. I've always liked it best then. No gulls screaming their opinions, no harbor master wanting fees, no crew asking questions. Just me, the water, and whatever the water has decided to keep.

I went over the side of the *Kestrel's Daughter* with my knife in my belt and my lungs full of nothing, which is how I prefer to start anything important.

The Corvane lay forty fathoms down on a shelf of rock that the charts called "unremarkable seabed." The navy's survey — the one they'd published after the wreck, all tidy ink and official stamps — marked the site with a single small cross and no name. Ships get names. Graves get names. A cross with nothing under it is neither, and everyone in Grayhaven knew what it meant without anyone saying so: *don't look here.*

So naturally, we looked.

Not because we were brave. Because we were broke, and broke is braver than brave ever has to be.

* * *

Let me tell you about the rule, since you'll be hearing about it whether I mention it or not. Every salvage crew that works navy water learns it eventually, usually from someone who learned it the expensive way. We never wrote it down. Writing things down is how you give them to other people, and some things you don't want to hand over.

The rule is this: never ask what the navy won't discuss.

It sounds simple. It isn't, because the navy won't discuss a great many things, and half of them are cargo worth more than your ship. A frigate goes down with powder and pay-chest aboard, the Admiralty sends divers or seals the site, fine, that's discussed-adjacent. But when a frigate goes down carrying something the manifests don't list, and the survey chart comes back with a blank where the cargo should be, and the blank has been initialed by three different clerks who all retired within the year — that's not discussed. That's *buried*, and buried things have a way of being heavier than gold.

Our mentor, Tam Voss, used to say the rule out loud every time we anchored over navy water, like a prayer or a warning, depending on his mood. *"We lift what sinks, we sell what lifts, and we never ask what the Crown wants forgotten."* Then he'd spit over the rail, which was as close to liturgy as Tam ever came.

He's been dead eight years, and I still hear him say it. Mostly when I'm doing something he'd have slapped me for.

* * *

The descent was clean. Cold water, good visibility for once — the kind of morning where you can see the wreck's outline before you're halfway down, a long dark spine resting on the shelf like something that lay down to sleep and forgot to wake up.

The Corvane had been down eleven years. Storm-driven onto the shelf, they said, though storms don't usually drive a frigate sideways into a position that could only happen if someone had cut her anchor cable first. But that was a question, and questions about the Corvane weren't for asking. See above. Rule one.

She'd broken her back amidships. Her stern section sat mostly intact, listing hard to port, gunports gaping like the mouths of drowned men mid-word. Coral had taken her slowly, patiently, the way coral takes everything — she was wearing a coat of white and rust now, less a warship than a reef that remembered being one. Fish moved through her in silver rivers. A grouper the size of a barrel watched me descend with the flat disapproval of everything down there that has outlasted better swimmers than me.

I found the gap in her hull where the storm had peeled her open like fruit, and I slipped inside.

Wrecks have a smell even underwater. Copper and rot and old wood gone soft as bread. And underneath that, if the wreck is old enough and sad enough, something else — a stillness that isn't empty. Eleven years of dead men's air, pressed into the timbers. You learn to breathe around it.

My lamp found the great cabin first. The captain's quarters, or what was left. A chair fused to the deck by weed. A chest burst open decades ago, its contents long since scattered or taken. Nothing here. The navy's own divers had been through the accessible parts within the year of the sinking — we'd found their tool marks on our first survey dive, neat chisel cuts in the lead sealing, official as a signature. They'd taken what they wanted people to think there was.

Which left the parts they hadn't bothered with. Or hadn't known about.

I worked aft along the orlop deck, following the slope of the hull, past collapsed bulkheads and the soft grey shapes of things I didn't look at too closely. Diving a wreck is a negotiation with the dead. You take what they offer, you leave them their privacy, and you don't linger where the lingering would be rude.

Forty minutes down, and my air was a clock ticking in my chest. I had maybe fifteen minutes of work in me before Bram started hauling on the signal line like a fisherman who thinks he's hooked the sea floor.

That's when I saw the deck plate.

* * *

Here's the thing about salvage work that nobody puts in the ballads: it's mostly pattern recognition. You spend years learning what wrecks are supposed to look like — where cargo sits, how wood fails, how iron corrodes, how men organize their ships. And then one day you see something that doesn't fit the pattern, and your whole body knows it before your mind does. Your hands go cold. Your heart does something strange and slow, like it's trying to be quiet.

The deck plate was wrong.

Every plate on the orlop deck was iron, riveted, corroded to lace. Except this one. This one was bronze, and bronze doesn't come standard in a frigate's flooring. Bronze is for instruments, for fittings, for things that matter. It was also newer — or rather, less old — its edges still sharp under a thin skin of green, and it had been set *into* the deck, flush, deliberate, by someone working with time and care.

Someone had installed a door in the floor of a warship, and then the warship had conveniently sunk on top of it.

I hung there in the green dark with my lamp shaking slightly — from the current, I told myself — and I heard Tam's voice clear as a bell across eight years of silence. *Never ask what the Crown wants forgotten.*

And I heard the other voice, the one that pays the crew, feeds the crew, keeps the *Kestrel's Daughter* afloat in both senses. The voice that knew we had six weeks before the season turned, that the *Daughter* needed a new foremast, that Sefa's daughter needed a surgeon's fee paid, that Bram's debts had grown teeth. The voice that sounded suspiciously like mine and never shut up.

I got my pry bar under the plate's edge.

Bronze gives you a fight when it's been sitting in salt water for eleven years, but whoever made this door had made it to be opened — there was a lip, a hidden hinge-line, a mechanism that gave with a grinding shudder and a bloom of silt that turned my lamplight to milk. I waited for it to settle, waving my hand through the cloud like a woman trying to read through smoke.

Below the plate: a cavity, lined in lead. Lead-lined, in a wooden ship. Someone had spent real money making sure whatever sat in that cavity would sit dry and safe through fire, flood, and time.

In the cavity: a strongbox. Iron-banded, sealed with wax that had gone the color of old teeth, stamped with a seal I didn't recognize and then did, all at once, the way you recognize a face from a wanted poster. The Admiralty's cipher office. The little crown over the crossed keys.

Navy seal. Navy cavity. Navy nothing-on-the-chart.

My hands were steady now, which was worse than the shaking. Steady hands mean you've already decided.

I got my fingers under the box. It was heavy — sixty pounds, maybe seventy, dense as sin — and I remember thinking, with the detached clarity that comes forty fathoms down on a short air clock, *this is either the best thing we've ever lifted or the last thing.*

I got it free of the cavity. I got it turned. I got my arms around it the way you hold a sleeping child you're afraid will wake.

And that's when I touched the other thing.

* * *

It was under the box. Set into the lead lining, flat against the hull-side of the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth that had held better than any cloth has a right to after eleven years underwater. My fingers found it by accident, reaching for purchase, and even through the wrapping I knew the shape of paper. Folded paper. A lot of it. Bound.

You have to understand — I almost left it. I want that on the record, wherever records go when there's no one to read them. The strongbox was salvage. Salvage is what I do; it has rules, even unwritten ones, and one of them is that you take the prize and go. The papers were something else. Papers in a lead-lined secret compartment beneath a cipher-sealed strongbox on a wreck the charts wouldn't name aren't cargo. They're a message. And messages in bottles are addressed to whoever's fool enough to open them.

But the oilcloth was tied with cord, and the cord was naval issue, and my fingers — steady, traitorous, already-decided fingers — were hooking under the knot before my mind finished voting.

I took it. Of course I took it. I'm telling this story, aren't I? People who leave things behind don't get stories. They get quiet lives and untroubled sleep, and I've never managed either.

The package went into the net bag with the strongbox, and I went up.

* * *

The surface took me back the way the sea takes everything — roughly, and without apology. Bram hauled me over the gunwale while Sefa wrestled the bag aboard, and the two of them got one look at my face and stopped talking mid-sentence, which for those two is a nautical miracle.

"Find it?" Bram asked. He's our big man, our muscle, our gentle giant with gambling debts in four ports, and he asks questions the way a dog asks to be fed — hopefully, and braced for disappointment.

"I found it."

Sefa crouched by the bag, dripping seawater off her braids, and looked at the strongbox the way you look at a snake that might be a rope. "That's a cipher seal," she said. "That's an *Admiralty* cipher seal."

"It is."

"On a wreck with no name."

"There's a cross on the chart," I said. "Crosses count as names if you squint."

Bram laughed, then caught Sefa's expression and stopped, the laugh dying in his throat like a gull hitting glass. Sefa wasn't looking at the box anymore. She was looking at me, and behind her eyes I could see the rule assembling itself, word by word, the way Tam used to say it.

"Astrid," she said carefully. "What else is in the bag?"

I looked down at the oilcloth bundle, water-dark, innocuous, sitting in the bilge of my own ship like it had always lived there. Like it had been waiting eleven years at the bottom of the sea for exactly one fool to come along.

"Paperwork," I said.

Nobody laughed at that either.

* * *

We weighed anchor and ran for Grayhaven with the dawn coming up behind us, turning the water to hammered copper, and I stood at the stern rail with the wind in my teeth and told myself a story. The story was simple: we'd lift the box, sell it discreetly through channels that didn't ask names, pay the crew, re-rig the mast, and be gone before the season turned. One more port. One more season. That was always the plan. It had been the plan in nine ports running, and I was starting to suspect the plan was less a plan than a habit with a schedule.

Because here's the truth I didn't say out loud, even to myself, especially to myself: I don't stay anywhere. Not ports, not crews longer than a contract, not lives. Staying is just sinking in slow motion, and I've seen what the bottom looks like. I move because moving is the only direction that's never lied to me.

And yet.

I kept looking back at the wreck's bearing, that low dark line of coast already sliding astern, and I thought about the deck plate, and the cavity, and the care someone had taken — real care, expensive care, *afraid* care — to hide a bundle of paper at the bottom of the sea. Somebody had put that there believing it would never be found. Or believing that if it ever *was* found, it would be found by someone who needed it.

Eleven years is a long time to wait for a fool.

By the time Grayhaven's harbor light swung into view, I'd made two decisions. First: we say nothing about the papers. Not to the harbormaster, not to the tavern, not to each other more than strictly necessary. Second—

Second, I didn't let myself finish the thought, because finishing thoughts is how you end up staying places.

The *Kestrel's Daughter* slid into the harbor with the morning bells ringing, and the first thing I saw on the quay, leaning against a bollard with his arms crossed and his smile already loaded, was a man I'd spent eight years not thinking about.

Cyrus Amberdale.

And he was looking at me like he'd known exactly where I'd been this morning — like he'd known before I did.

Well. There's the rule, and then there's the exception, and I had a bad feeling I was about to learn which one he was.
