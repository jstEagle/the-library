## 2. Salt Debts

There are three kinds of debt in a port town: the kind you owe the chandler, the kind you owe the tavern keeper, and the kind you owe to people who never say what it's for. The first two can be paid in coin. The third is paid in favors, and favors are just debts with better manners.

I'd spent the morning paying the first kind.

The chandler's boy counted out my coins twice, licked his thumb, and counted them again, which told me everything about how the *Kestrel's Lament* — no. Wrong ship. Old habit. The *Vane's Fortune* was named after me because I won her in a card game and have never once been allowed to forget it. The chandler's boy counted my coins twice because word travels fast in Saltmere that Astrid Vane came into money, and money in Saltmere is like blood in water. Everything with teeth comes to see.

"Even," I said, when he finished the second count.

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure. Unless you'd like me to count it a third time and find it short."

He didn't. He took the coins and gave me a receipt written on paper so thin I could read the grain of the counter through it, and I folded it into my coat next to the little iron key that opened the strongbox I'd pulled off the Corvane two days ago. The key had been sitting against my ribs ever since, warm as a coal, heavier than any iron has a right to be.

The survey charts don't show a strongbox on the Corvane. I've checked three times since. The navy's charts show her holds, her masts, the scatter of her guns across the sand like dropped cutlery. They show everything a salvage crew could want and nothing they couldn't. A sealed box bolted under the captain's cabin floor isn't on there, and neither is the fresh chain wrapped around it, nor the way the wood around the bolts had been cut by hands, not storm.

Never ask what the navy won't discuss. That's the rule. It's kept us fed for six years.

But rules are like anchors — they hold until the storm decides otherwise, and something about that box had been rattling around in me like loose tackle ever since I touched it. Not the box itself. Boxes are boxes. It was the chain. Someone had chained that thing down *after* the ship went down, and the only reason to do work underwater like that is if you're afraid of what happens when it comes up.

I put the thought away the way you put away a knife you might need later. Carefully. Edge down.

* * *

By noon I'd cleared four more debts. Okafor's tab at the Rope & Anchor, which was less a tab than an archaeological record of his optimism. The two months' back rent on our gear shed. The doctor who'd stitched Bram's arm last spring and had been sending increasingly creative invoices ever since — the last one included a charge for "moral injury to the needle." And the widow Pell, who'd been carrying our fresh-water casks on credit through the whole slow season because she liked us, or liked Bram, which amounted to the same economy.

She wouldn't take the full amount.

"Astrid." She pushed half the coins back across her kitchen table. Her hands were flour-white and knuckled like coral. "You'll take it as a loan, not a payment. A woman who pays all her debts at once is either leaving or dying, and I've buried enough of both."

"That's grim even for you."

"I'm old. Grim is the last honest thing I own." She looked at me over the coins, and her eyes were shrewd and kind in equal measure, which is a combination I've never learned to defend against. "You got money from somewhere. Money like this doesn't wash ashore. It gets thrown."

"It's salvage pay. Honest work."

"Mm." She said it the way you'd say *mm* to a child explaining that the dog ate the ledger. But she took the coins, finally, all of them, and pressed a heel of bread and a wedge of cheese on me besides, because refusing food in Maren Pell's kitchen is a sin even the church doesn't have a name for yet.

Walking back down to the harbor, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The crew's debts were clear. All of them. For the first time in six years, every soul aboard the *Fortune* owed nothing to anybody in Saltmere, which meant that come the end of the season, they could walk away from me clean.

That should have felt like a gift. Instead it sat in my chest like swallowed ballast, and I couldn't have told you why, except that a crew with no debts is a crew with choices, and I've learned the hard way that when people get choices, some of them choose the door.

The harbor clerk's office had a line out the door, so I leaned against the pilings to wait, and that's where Tam found me. Tam is our youngest, sixteen, all elbows and opinions, with a talent for reading currents that borders on the supernatural and a talent for staying quiet that doesn't exist at all.

"Astrid. Astrid. There's a man at the office asking about us."

"There's always a man at the office asking about us. Usually about the time we knocked over his crab pots."

"Not like this." He'd gone pale under his freckles, which takes doing; Tam is mostly freckle. "He had papers. Official ones. Red ribbon. He asked the clerk for our charter file, and the clerk went gray, Astrid. Gray like fish belly."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing my mother's footsteps. "What else?"

"He said — he said the license was 'under review.' He said we weren't to take any contracts until the review concluded. And then he looked at me like he knew my name before he asked it, and he did know it, Astrid. He knew everyone's name."

I crossed the harbor fast enough that Tam had to trot. The line outside the clerk's office parted for me, which told me my expression was doing its work. Inside, behind the counter scarred by forty years of quarrelsome captains, sat Master Ellery Voss, the harbor clerk, a small damp man who owed me nothing and feared me slightly, and beside him stood the man with the red ribbon.

He was navy. You can always tell, even out of uniform — they stand like the building owes them rent. Gray coat, good boots, a face like a closed ledger. He held our charter file open in one hand and didn't look up when I came in, which was its own message.

"Master Voss," I said pleasantly. "Who's your friend?"

"This is—" Voss began.

"An auditor," the man said. His voice was dry paper. "Port authority. You're Captain Vane."

"I'm whoever you need me to be if it gets this over faster. What's wrong with my charter?"

"Nothing is *wrong* with it." He turned a page with one finger, savoring it. "It's under review. Standard procedure. Irregularities in the vessel's registration history. Questions regarding prior salvage claims. Until the review concludes, the *Vane's Fortune* may not legally contract for salvage work within territorial waters."

"Prior salvage claims." I let that hang. "Such as?"

"That would be part of the review." He closed the file. For the first time he looked at me, and his eyes were the flat gray of a sea before weather. "You'll receive notice. Good day, Captain."

He walked past me close enough that I caught his scent — ink and wool and, underneath, faintly, the tallow of naval ship's candles. He paused at the door without turning around.

"For what it's worth, Captain Vane, the review was not my idea. I'm told it comes from above my head. Considerably above."

Then he was gone, and Voss was mopping his forehead with a handkerchief that had given up sometime around noon, and I was standing in a harbor office holding a receipt for paid debts and feeling, for the first time in years, like a woman standing on deck while the water rises.

Someone wanted us grounded. Someone with enough braid to make port clerks sweat through their coats. And they'd moved within two days of my dive on the Corvane, which meant either the world's most remarkable coincidence, or someone had been watching the wreck.

I bought a pot of bad coffee from the stall outside and drank it standing up, watching the masts sway against a sky going the color of pewter, and I thought: the sea is safer than the shore. The sea only tries to kill you honestly.

* * *

Bram Tolliver had been a navy man once, which is why he wasn't anymore, and why he noticed things the rest of us missed — the way a man's weight shifts before he reaches for a weapon, the particular silence a crew goes into when fear starts moving through it like bilge water finding the low spots.

He noticed it that evening in the Rope & Anchor.

They were all there, the *Fortune*'s people, because the *Fortune*'s people were always there when there was coin behind the bar, and tonight Astrid was buying. That alone would have raised eyebrows. Astrid buying rounds was like the tide running backward — notable, and a bad sign for somebody.

But it was more than that. Bram watched Okafor sit with his back to the wall instead of to the room. Watched Tam keep touching the knife he'd started carrying three weeks ago and hadn't mentioned to anyone, though Bram had seen him practicing draws behind the gear shed. Watched little Sefa, their navigator, fold and unfold the same scrap of chart until it went soft as cloth.

Fear doesn't announce itself. It just changes how people hold their cups.

"The license," Sefa said, low, when Bram slid into the seat beside her. "Is it true? Tam says—"

"Tam says a lot of things."

"He said a navy man knew our names. All of them. Before anyone told him."

Bram turned his tankard a quarter-turn on the table, the way he used to turn his spyglass, and considered lying to her. He was good at lying; the navy had taught him that too. But Sefa navigated by stars she could barely see, and she had a nose for false bearings.

"It's true," he said.

"What kind of review needs everyone's name?"

"The kind that isn't a review." He kept his voice under the tavern noise. Around them the crew laughed too loudly and drank too fast and didn't look at each other. "I've seen this before. In the fleet, before I — before. When command wants a crew broken up quietly, they don't arrest anyone. Arrest makes noise. They audit you. Ground you. Review you until the crew starves and scatters on its own, and then there's no crew left to ask questions about anything."

"Questions about what?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, surfacing at last, the one they'd all been not-asking since Astrid came up from the Corvane with salt still drying white in the creases of her hands and something shut down behind her eyes that hadn't been there at dawn.

"You felt it too," Bram said. "On the wreck."

Sefa looked into her cup. "The charts were wrong."

"The charts were *edited*. There's a difference. Wrong is an accident. Edited is a decision." He drained the tankard and set it down with a click that sounded, in that moment, exactly like a lock turning. "Six years I've sailed with that woman. She pays debts, she keeps the rule — never ask what the navy won't discuss. But something down there broke the rule, whether she knows it yet or not. And now the navy's come ashore after us, polite as tax collectors, and I'll tell you what I learned in eight years of service, Sefa: the navy is never polite unless it's afraid."

Across the room, Astrid stood to buy another round, and the crowd made room for her the way water makes room for a keel, and Bram saw that she was smiling at them, joking with Tam, pressing coins into the widow Pell's boy's hand for the bread — and that none of it reached her eyes. She was performing captain the way he'd once performed lieutenant, and he recognized the performance because he'd invented half its gestures.

Okafor drifted over, big and slow as a mooring buoy. "Bram. Straight question."

"Those are the only ones worth answering."

"If the license dies — if we're grounded through winter — what do we do? Some of the lads have families now. Tam's got a mother in Copperlane. We can't sit in port eating savings till some clerk finishes his *review*."

"We could take private work," Sefa said carefully. "Non-charter. Smuggling runs, escort—"

"On a grounded ship? Every harbor master from here to the capital would have our hull number. We'd be sunk before we cleared the buoy line." Bram rubbed his jaw. "Unless someone offered us work that came with its own protection. Its own... patron."

The word sat on the table among them like a live coal.

"You think that's why she's spending?" Okafor said. "Paying everyone off? Like she knows we'll need to run?"

"I think," Bram said slowly, "that Astrid Vane has been waiting six years for the other boot to drop, and she heard it coming before any of us did. I think she's clearing our debts so that whatever comes, we can choose freely. And I think—" he stopped, because the tavern door had opened, letting in a blade of cold air and a figure in a long gray coat, and every instinct the navy had trained into him stood up and saluted.

The auditor from the harbor office. In a tavern full of his crew. Alone, unarmed-looking, ordering nothing.

He walked the length of the room without hurrying, and Bram marked how every one of the *Fortune*'s people went quiet in sequence, like lamps being snuffed. The man stopped at Astrid's table. He set a single card down on the wood, face up, and said something Bram couldn't hear — three words, maybe four, delivered with the flat courtesy of a summons.

Astrid looked at the card. Bram watched her go very still, the stillness of deep water, and then she picked the card up and turned it over, and whatever was written on the back made her laugh — one short, hard sound with no floor under it.

The auditor left. The door swung shut. The tavern noise came back in a rush, too loud, everyone talking at once to fill the hole he'd left.

Bram was already moving. He reached her table as she was feeding the card to the candle flame, watching it curl.

"Astrid. What did he say?"

She watched the card burn down to nothing in the saucer. When she spoke, her voice was light as rigging hum, which is how Bram knew it was anything but.

"He said, 'He looks forward to meeting you.'" She brushed the ash off the table with the side of her hand. "No signature. Just a crest on the front. An anchor and three stars."

Bram felt the bottom drop out of his stomach, because he knew that crest. Every ex-navy man does. Three stars above an anchor — that's a commodore's pennant, and there was only one commodore whose reach ran this far up the coast.

"Halloway," he said. "That's Halloway's mark."

"Yes," Astrid said, and smiled her market-day smile at him, the one that had talked creditors and harbormasters and customs men into good humor for six years, and behind it her eyes were doing calculations that had nothing to do with money. "It seems the navy wants to hire us after all. Isn't that funny? We spend our whole lives keeping clear of them, and they come looking anyway."

"And if we say no?"

"Then we stay grounded until the review concludes." She stood, dropped a last handful of coins on the table for the barman, and gathered her coat. "And reviews conclude when the reviewer loses interest. Commodores are very patient men, Bram. It's practically the whole job."

She walked out into the dark toward the harbor, and Bram stood there with the crew's eyes on him, waiting for their answer, and understood suddenly that the debts she'd paid today hadn't been coin at all. She'd been buying them their freedom to choose — because tomorrow, someone was going to offer them a choice no free men should have to make.

Outside, the wind had come around to the north, and it smelled of snow, though it was weeks early for snow, and the sea beyond the mole lay flat and black and patient as a held breath.
