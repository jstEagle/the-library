## 3. An Old Rival's Coin

The tavern was called the Drowned Gull, which was either an honest admission or a warning, depending on how much you'd had to drink. I'd had nothing, so I took it as a warning and sat with my back to the wall anyway.

Bram found me before the ale did. He slid onto the bench across from me the way he does everything — slowly, like a man who's learned that sudden movements in life tend to be followed by unpleasant ones.

"He's here," Bram said.

"I know."

"You can't know. He only just walked in."

"Bram." I turned my cup a quarter turn on the table, a habit I have when I'm deciding whether to break something. "Cyrus Amberdale has walked into every port I've been in for eight years, usually three days after I arrive, usually wearing that coat he thinks makes him look respectable. If I didn't know better I'd say he was following me."

"Maybe he is."

"Then he's worse at it than he is at everything else, and he's very bad at everything else."

Bram grunted, which from Bram is a full paragraph of agreement. Behind him, the door swung open again to let in the harbor wind, and there he was — Cyrus Amberdale, framed in the lamplight like a man posing for a portrait of himself he couldn't afford. The coat was new. That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that worried me, because Cyrus had never in his life spent money on clothes when there was debt to be paid, drink to be bought, or a sister to be quietly sent coin for. A new coat on Cyrus Amberdale meant one of two things: he'd come into money, or someone had dressed him for the part he was about to play.

Neither possibility improved my evening.

He crossed the room without hurrying. That was new too. The old Cyrus came at things fast — deals, dives, decisions, disasters. This one moved like a man who'd been told exactly where to walk and had decided that obeying looked better than resisting.

"Astrid." He said my name the way you'd set down something fragile on an unsteady table.

"Cyrus." I didn't stand. "You're blocking the light."

"There isn't any light. You picked the darkest corner in the room."

"I pick all my corners carefully. Sit down if you're sitting. Stand there if you're performing."

Something flickered across his face — the old humor, surfacing for air before going back under. He sat. Up close he looked like a man who hadn't slept properly in a month, though he'd have fought anyone who said so. There was a crease between his brows that hadn't been there at Kestrel Shoals, and gray coming in at his temples that he probably thought made him look distinguished and actually just made him look tired.

"You look well," he said.

"Liar. What do you want?"

"Straight to it, then."

"You came to my port, in my season, wearing a coat that costs more than my mizzen. You didn't come to ask after my health."

He set a leather folio on the table between us. Not pushed it — set it, gently, the way you'd set down a live thing you hoped would stay calm. Then he folded his hands on top of it, which was either restraint or a man keeping a lid on something.

"One job," he said. "One dive. One crate off one wreck. Triple rates, paid half up front, crew's debts cleared as a signing bonus — yes, I know about the debts, Astrid, I know what you did at the chandlery this morning, I make it my business to know where your money goes, don't look at me like that, it's not romantic, it's professional."

"It's neither. Whose wreck?"

And there it was. The pause. Small — a heartbeat, maybe less — but I've spent twenty years reading water and men, and both of them telegraph before they turn. His eyes went to the folio instead of to me.

"The coordinates are in the contract," he said.

"Cyrus. Whose wreck?"

He opened the folio. Turned it around. Slid a single sheet across the scarred wood, and I looked down at a column of figures — latitude, longitude, depth, bearing — written in a clerk's neat hand, and my stomach dropped through the floor, through the cellar, through the harbor mud, all the way down to forty fathoms where it belonged.

I knew those numbers.

I knew them the way you know a scar. Not because you study it, but because it's yours, and it aches when the weather changes. Eight years I'd carried them, and I could have recited them drunk, asleep, or drowning.

"Kestrel Shoals," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "You're asking me to dive Kestrel Shoals."

"I'm offering—"

"You're *asking*." I looked up at him. "Say the whole sentence, Cyrus. Say it out loud in this room full of people who don't know what those numbers mean, and watch what my face does."

He held my eyes. Credit where due — most men would have looked away. "There's a crate on the Corvane's orlop deck, aft of the powder magazine. Sealed, naval-stamped, logged as diplomatic correspondence. I need it brought up intact. That's the job. That's the whole job."

"And the coordinates being Kestrel Shoals is a coincidence."

"The Corvane went down at Kestrel Shoals. That's geography, not coincidence."

"The Corvane went down at Kestrel Shoals because *we* put her there—" I stopped. Lowered my voice. The tavern noise closed back over us, dice and argument and a fiddle tuning up badly in the corner. "Eight years ago we swore on Halvard's grave that we'd never work that water again. You swore loudest. You cried, Cyrus. I'm the only one who saw it, and I never told anyone, and this is what you bring me? A contract with his grave in it?"

"I bring you triple pay."

"Don't."

"I bring you enough to refit the *Kestrel* proper, pay your crew a year's wages each, and buy back whatever piece of your soul you think you lost—"

"*Don't.*"

The fiddle player hit a wrong note and winced. Somewhere behind me a bottle broke and somebody laughed. Ordinary sounds. The world carrying on, indifferent, while two people who used to be one thing sat across a table pretending to be strangers.

Cyrus leaned back. Ran a hand over his jaw. When he spoke again his voice was quieter, and that frightened me more than the numbers had.

"Astrid. I know what I'm asking. Do you think I don't? I've had eight years to think about Kestrel Shoals, and I promise you I've thought about it more than you have, because you got to hate me cleanly and I got to keep the log." He tapped the folio once. "This job pays enough that no one on your crew ever has to take a dirty contract again. Including you. One crate, and then you're free. Truly free. No more seasons, no more running, no more watching the northern wind and wondering which port will still have you in spring."

"That's not freedom, that's a bribe."

"Everything's a bribe. The question is whether it buys something worth having."

I should have thrown the contract in his face. Every instinct I trust said throw it, walk out, let the sea keep its dead and its secrets both. But Bram was at the bar, and Tally was at Bram's elbow, and Josper and the twins were at the corner table pretending not to listen with the total concentration of people listening to every word. Six people whose debts I'd paid yesterday with money I'd scraped for four years. Six people who'd follow me into anything except, perhaps, poverty — and why should they? I'd never asked them to.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, the thing I didn't say: *he* was under that water too. Halvard. Eight years at forty fathoms with the thing that killed him, and some traitorous part of me wanted to go down and see it. Wanted to put my hands on that wreck and take something back.

"Half up front," I said.

Cyrus blinked. Whatever speech he'd prepared for my refusal, he hadn't prepared one for my surrender. "What?"

"You heard me. Half up front, in coin, tonight, before my crew hears a word about coordinates. And I choose the dive team. And if I surface and say the job's off, it's off, and you eat the loss, and you never darken my horizon again. Those are my terms."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind his eyes that looked horribly like relief. Like a man who'd braced for a fight and been handed a rope instead. Then it was gone, smoothed away under the merchant's mask, and he nodded.

"Done," he said. "Astrid—"

"Don't thank me. Don't apologize, either. You haven't earned the apology and I haven't earned the thanks, and we'll both feel worse if we pretend otherwise." I stood, took the contract, and folded it into my coat next to the papers sewn there — old habit, keeping everything important against my skin. "We sail on the morning tide day after tomorrow. Weather window's closing."

"I know," he said. "The snows are early."

"They always are, when it matters."

I walked out past Bram, who read my face the way he reads weather and asked no questions, which is why he's still my first mate. At the door I paused, because I'm weak, and looked back.

Cyrus hadn't moved. He sat exactly where I'd left him, hands flat on the empty table, staring at the wood grain as if the coordinates were written there. And I swear to you — I swear on Halvard's grave, which is the only oath I have left — that he looked less like a rich man buying a salvage job than like a prisoner checking the bars of a cell he'd only just noticed he was standing in.

Then the door shut between us, and the cold took me, and I told myself I'd imagined it.

* * *

*Cyrus Amberdale counted the coins twice, though he'd counted them already at the countinghouse, and again on the walk over.*

*It wasn't the money he didn't trust. It was himself. A man can talk himself into anything given enough quiet, and Halloway had made sure Cyrus had very little quiet these days.*

*She'd said yes. He turned the word over like a stone found in a pocket — she'd said yes, and it should have felt like victory, and instead it felt like the moment on a dive when the line goes slack and you realize the anchor's dragged and nobody above knows yet.*

*Because she'd said yes too easily. Eight years of hatred, and she'd taken the contract in under an hour, and Cyrus Amberdale was many things — coward, liar, the man who'd surfaced alone at Kestrel Shoals — but he was not fool enough to believe he was persuasive. She'd said yes because of the debts, maybe. Because of the crew. Or because of Halvard, because grief is a current and it pulls even the strongest swimmer back to the wreck.*

*Or because she'd find out anyway. That was the thought he kept stepping around and kept landing on. Astrid Vane found things. It was her gift and her curse and the reason he'd loved her, and if there was a secret within a hundred miles of her, she'd eventually hold it up to the light and ask it its name.*

*Halloway's voice came back to him, smooth as bilgewater: Your sister's creditors are patient men, Mr. Amberdale. But patience is a perishable commodity. Bring me the crate, and everyone's accounts settle. Fail, and — well. Debts are such heavy things to carry. They sink a body, sometimes.*

*They'd shown him the letters. They always showed him the letters. Liana's handwriting, her debts itemized, and beneath them other papers he wasn't meant to read closely and had read closely anyway — manifests, requisitions, a list of names with a red line drawn through the whole column. He hadn't understood the red line. He suspected he would.*

*He ordered another drink he didn't want and didn't touch it. Across the room, Bram watched him with the flat patience of a man waiting for a storm to declare itself.*

*Three days, Cyrus told himself. Three days to the shoals, one dive, one crate, and then it was done and Halloway had no further use for him and Liana was safe and Astrid — Astrid would hate him forever, but she'd be alive and rich and free, and hating him was a burden she could afford.*

*It was a good plan. It was even, mostly, true.*

*What he did not let himself think — what he shoved down deep, past the red-lined names, past the slack anchor line, into the dark where he kept everything he couldn't afford to know — was the question the clerk had asked him, offhand, stamping the contract: Funny thing, Mr. Amberdale. Why would the navy need a civilian diver for Corvane salvage? We've got our own crews.*

*Why indeed.*

*He finished the untouched drink in one swallow and went out into the early snow, and behind him the sea lay flat and black and patient, holding its breath, holding its dead, holding its answer.*
