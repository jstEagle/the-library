## 4. What We Swore

Eight years is a long time to carry a grudge, and I'll tell you the truth about grudges: they're lighter than grief, which is why people choose them.

I was twenty-three the summer we worked Kestrel Shoals, and I thought I knew everything a diver needed to know. My mentor, Silas Vane — no relation by blood, but he'd signed my papers when I was fourteen and orphaned, and in the salvage trade that makes you family whether you like it or not — used to say I had two settings: reckless and asleep. He said it fondly. He said most things fondly, even the ones that turned out to be warnings.

The Kestrel job came to us through a broker in Saltmere with soft hands and a ledger he kept too close to his chest. A merchantman called the *Providence's Grace*, gone down on the shoals eleven years prior, carrying silver plate and, if the broker was to be believed, a strongbox of letters of credit worth more than the rest of the cargo combined. Triple shares for a six-day job. Silas read the contract twice, and I watched his thumb stop on one line — *all recovered materials to be surrendered to the client at completion* — and I watched him sign anyway.

"Why?" I asked him that night on deck, while Cyrus coiled line nearby and pretended not to listen, which was how Cyrus listened to everything.

"Because debts don't drown," Silas said. "People do."

I thought it was just one of his sayings. He had forty of them, and like most men's wisdom, half of it only made sense afterward.

* * *

Kestrel Shoals earned its name honestly. The rocks stood off the coast like the ribs of something enormous that had died standing up, and the water between them moved with a violence that had nothing to do with weather. The tide ran through those channels twice a day like a fist through a glove, and every local fisherman we spoke to told us the same thing: dive the slack or don't dive at all. Slack water at Kestrel lasted maybe forty minutes. The broker's charts said otherwise. The broker's charts said the channels were "navigable at all tides," which is the sort of lie that gets written by someone who has never been cold in his life.

Silas didn't trust the charts. That matters. I want that on the record, wherever records go after we do. He didn't trust them, and he said so, and he planned around them anyway because the contract had penalties for delay and because — this is the part I've chewed on for eight years — because Cyrus argued for going.

Cyrus was twenty-five then, all elbows and certainty, the best line-tender I ever worked with and the worst gambler, which are more related professions than people admit. He'd run the numbers on the tide tables himself, sitting up three nights with a lamp and a slate, and he came to Silas with figures that showed a working window of nearly two hours on the morning ebb.

"The tables are wrong," Silas said.

"The tables are the Admiralty's own," Cyrus said. "Printed in Corvane Harbor. You want to argue with the Admiralty?"

I remember thinking, even then, that there was something hungry in the way he said it. Not greedy — hungry. There's a difference. Greed wants things. Hunger wants to stop being hungry, and it will eat anything placed in front of it, including the hand that feeds it.

Silas looked at me. That's what I can't forgive myself for, if we're keeping the full ledger. He looked at me, the way he always did before a decision, asking without asking whether I was ready, and I said, "Tables look fine to me." Because I was twenty-three and wanted the triple share and wanted, more than anything, to be the kind of diver who trusted her numbers over an old man's weather-sense.

So we dove the morning ebb, and the Admiralty's tables were wrong, and everything that happened afterward happened inside that mistake like a pearl inside an oyster.

* * *

The wreck lay in a channel between two of the shoal teeth, keel-up, her hull furred with eight years of weed. Beautiful wreck, actually. I still dream about her sometimes — the way the light came down through the channel in bars, the way the fish hung in the current like ornaments on a tree that grew sideways. Silas went down first with me on the air line, and Cyrus tended from the boat with Tam Osgood, who was crew then, before the drink took him, and a boy named Pell whose whole job was watching the water move.

First day went clean. We brought up plate, candlesticks, a captain's chest of coin. Second day, the strongbox. It was lashed under what remained of the great cabin, iron-banded, heavier than two men could argue with, and when Silas got his knife under the hasp and pried it open on the seabed — against procedure, but Silas believed in looking before lifting — I saw his face change behind the faceplate.

Not greed. I want to be clear about that too, because the story that got told later, in taverns, in the hearing that never officially happened, was that Silas Vane saw treasure and lost his head. What I saw was fear. He held up a sheaf of papers, waterlogged but legible, and he looked at me through the glass, and he shook his head slowly, the way a man shakes his head at a wound he already knows is fatal.

He put the papers back. He closed the box. And he made the signal for ascent — not the signal for *found it*, but the signal for *we're done, everyone up*.

We were thirty feet from the channel mouth when the tide turned.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about drowning, or rather here is what nobody tells you about almost drowning, which is the only version anyone survives to describe: it isn't loud. The books and the ballads make it a storm, a struggle, a screaming. But the sea doesn't raise its voice. It just takes hold. One moment the current is a suggestion; the next it has your ankle, your waist, your whole body, and it is walking you out to sea at the speed of a trotting horse, and your line goes taut, and then your line goes *tight*, and then your line goes silent in a way that lines should never go.

Mine held. Cyrus had belayed it around the mast step, and whatever else is true about that man — and so much else is true — my line held because he tied it the way he ties everything, twice, and checks it with his whole weight.

Silas's line was fouled. He'd cut it himself, I learned later, or I believe I learned it later, from the one other person who saw it happen. The current had wrapped it around a spar of the wreck, and rather than spend his last minutes fighting rope, he'd drawn his knife and given himself to the water clean, because a fouled line means the tender above gets dragged in after you, and there was a boat up there with three living people in it.

He was fifty-one years old. He had taught me to equalize my ears in a rain barrel when I was fourteen. His last words to me, shouted through the water as the current took him, were not my name and not a prayer. They were: *"Count!"*

He meant count the divers. He meant make sure everyone comes home. He meant the thing he'd been saying since I was a girl: the cargo is replaceable, the crew is not.

I counted. Two. Me, and no one.

* * *

Cyrus surfaced alone.

That's the sentence, isn't it? Eight years, and it's still just the one sentence. He came up the line with the salvage log strapped inside his jacket — the log, the worthless paper record, the thing with the client's seal and the inventory list, the thing the broker would need to pay out — and his hands were bleeding where the line had burned through his gloves, and he climbed aboard, and he looked at the empty water, and he said, "He's gone. The current took him. I couldn't—"

And then he was sick over the rail, and then he sat down, and then he did not say anything else for six hours, and I have spent eight years trying to decide what that silence contained.

Here's what I know. Here's what I've assembled, piece by piece, the way you assemble a wreck map, one honest fragment at a time:

I know the current at Kestrel runs outward, toward open water, and Silas's body was never found, and bodies that run outward are rarely found, and none of that proves anything except that the sea keeps its receipts.

I know that Cyrus surfaced with the log, which was in the cabin locker, which was nowhere near Silas's position when the tide turned, which means that in the last minutes — the minutes when Silas was cutting himself free and shouting *count* — Cyrus went back for paper.

I know that when we made port, the broker paid out in full, triple shares, no argument, and that Cyrus took his share and mine both to Silas's sister in Corvane Harbor, and that she refused it, and that he left it anyway, in an envelope, unsigned, and that she used it to buy a headstone that says *LOST TO THE SEA, BELOVED BY HIS CREW*, and that I have never once visited it without also wanting to visit a certain tavern in Saltmere and break a bottle over a certain man's head.

And I know — this is the part that ruins the clean story, the part I keep trying to throw back and keep finding in my pocket — I know that Cyrus Amberdale dove back into that channel. Tam Osgood told me, two years later, drunk and dying and past caring about loyalties. Told me that when Silas's line went slack, Cyrus stripped his coat and went over the side into a current that was running like a millrace, on a breath, with no line of his own, and came up coughing blood, and said one word, which was "Gone," and then asked for the log, because someone had to account for the dive, because the client would need it, because —

Because why? That's the question, isn't it. Because the contract demanded it? Because panic makes people cling to procedure the way drowning men cling to spars? Or because some cold little clerk in the back of his skull understood that the log was the difference between a tragedy and a total loss, and Cyrus Amberdale has never once in his life been able to let a thing be a total loss?

Tam said he went in after him. Tam swore it on his mother. But Tam also swore the drink wasn't a problem, so.

That's the shape of it. That's the knot I've worried for eight years: a man who may have tried to save my mentor and certainly chose the paperwork over the search, who surfaced alone with the one thing that proved we'd done the work, and who could not — would not — explain, in any words that survived contact with my anger. Every time I cornered him, he gave me the same answer, flat as a calm sea: "I did what I could. It wasn't enough. Hate me if it helps."

Hate him if it helps. As if hate were a tonic. As if I'd take it for my health.

* * *

We buried the empty coffin because his sister needed somewhere to stand. I gave the eulogy. I don't remember what I said. I remember that Cyrus stood at the back, hat in his hands, and that he did not come forward when the others did, and that I hated him for that too, for knowing he wasn't welcome and coming anyway, for standing exactly far enough away that I couldn't pretend he hadn't come.

We dissolved the crew within the month. Tam to his bottle, Pell to a coastal packet, me to whatever port would stamp my papers. And Cyrus — Cyrus found other partners, better-funded ones, and started climbing, the way he always climbed, rung by rung, each job bigger, each partner richer, until the name Amberdale meant something in the salvage trade and the name Vane meant a dead man and a woman who wouldn't work the eastern shoals.

We made one vow, the two of us, the night before we parted ways forever. It wasn't dramatic. There was no storm, no clenched fists. We were in the tavern in Saltmere where the broker drank, and he came and sat across from me uninvited, and he pushed a coin across the table — my final share, the one I'd refused — and I looked at it, and I looked at him, and I said, "Never again. Not Kestrel, not anything like it, not for any money on this coast. Swear it."

And he said, "Sworn."

And that was the whole ceremony. No blood, no gods. Just two liars agreeing on a truth neither of us believed would hold, because the truth underneath it — the real truth, the one we were both too young and too proud to say — was that we had killed a good man together, between the two of us, with our fine tables and our trust in printed numbers, and that the only penance either of us could imagine was to never stand on the same deck again.

I kept the vow for eight years. I kept it through lean seasons and fat ones, through offers that would have set me up for life, and I told myself it was principle, and mostly it was, and the rest of it was that I couldn't look at a chart of those shoals without hearing *count*.

And now here he comes walking into the Gull's Rest with salt spray still on his boots and a contract in his coat, and he sets it on the table between us, and I look down at the coordinates written in his neat clerk's hand, and I know them before I finish reading them. Of course I know them. I could draw those shoals blindfolded, from memory, from the inside.

Kestrel Shoals. Triple pay. One dive.

He kept his eyes on mine while I read it, and his hands were steady on the table, and only a man who has known him as long as I have would see that his thumbs were pressed white against the wood, the way a man presses when he's holding himself to a chair by force.

"You swore," I said.

"I did," he said. "And I'm here to break it, and I need you to hear me out before you put that knife in me, because—" He stopped. He actually stopped, Cyrus Amberdale, who talks like the tide, constant and unstoppable. He looked at the door of the tavern, once, quick as a blink, and when his eyes came back to mine there was something in them I had never seen in eight years of hating him.

Fear.

Not of me.

"Astrid," he said quietly, "they're watching this tavern right now. So smile, order another round, and let me buy you a drink — because the job I'm about to offer you isn't the job I was sent to offer you, and the man who sent me cannot know the difference."
