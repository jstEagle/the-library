## 5. The Commodore's Shadow

I told myself I wasn't following him.

That was a lie, and I'm a decent enough liar to know one when I'm wearing it. I'd told better lies — to harbormasters, to customs men, to myself on more occasions than I care to count — but this one sat poorly in my mouth, like a fishbone swallowed sideways.

The truth was simpler: Cyrus Amberdale had walked out of the Saltgull Tavern with a contract in his coat and something wrong in his shoulders, and I wanted to know what it was. A man who has known you for fifteen years learns your tells. Apparently I'd learned his, too, even after eight years of trying very hard not to look at him at all.

He moved through Port Calloway like a man being followed already. That was the first thing that snagged my attention. He kept to the lamp-lit side of the street where the watchmen walked their rounds, ducked once beneath an awning when a pair of navy officers strolled past, and took a route to nowhere sensible — three turns he didn't need, a cut through the chandlery yard that added ten minutes. Either he was meeting someone he didn't want seen with, or he'd grown paranoid in his old age. Both, as it turned out. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

I followed the way I dive: slow, patient, breathing through the pauses. I'd traded my sea coat for a hooded fishwife's cloak borrowed off a drying line behind the tavern — I left two coppers in its place, because I steal from navies and admirals, not from washerwomen — and I kept to doorways and shadowed eaves while the fog came off the harbor in gray sheets. Port Calloway in autumn is a town built of salt-rotted timber and secrets, and both of them smell about the same.

He went up. That was the second thing. Nobody goes *up* in Port Calloway unless they're going somewhere expensive or somewhere hidden, and the cliff road above the customs house was both. The houses there stood back from the lane behind iron gates, white-shuttered, facing the sea like they owned it. Most of them belonged to shipping magnates and retired captains who'd sold their honor at a fair price and liked to watch the water they'd sold it beside.

Cyrus stopped at the third gate. The one with the naval pennant hanging limp in the dead air.

I knew that house. Every diver in Calloway knew it, the way sailors know the reef that ate their friend's boat. Commodore Halloway. Commandant of the Southern Survey — the office that charted wrecks, licensed salvage, and decided which parts of the sea officially existed. If a wreck wasn't on Halloway's charts, then legally it had never sunk, and anyone who said otherwise could find themselves charged with salvaging naval property from a ship that was never there. It was elegant, the way a noose is elegant.

And now Cyrus Amberdale was standing at his gate like a tradesman come to collect a debt.

I slipped through the gap between the wall and a fig tree gone wild over the neighbor's fence — the rich never maintain their hedges, another small mercy of the idle class — and found myself in a garden all clipped boxwood and gravel paths, with a glass conservatory glowing amber against the dark. Voices carried from inside. Not loud voices. The careful kind.

I pressed myself into the shadow of the conservatory wall, among the ferns they were keeping alive at obscene expense this far north, and listened.

"—the terms are unchanged." Halloway's voice. I'd heard it twice before, from a distance, at licensing hearings where salvage crews got ground into paste by procedure. A voice like a ledger: everything in its column, nothing wasted. "You have until the first snows. After that, the pass closes, and the window closes with it."

"And if I don't take the job?" Cyrus. Flat. Careful.

"Then Mrs. Amberdale's creditors will learn where she is."

The silence that followed had weight. I felt it through the glass.

"You said the debts were settled," Cyrus said at last. "You said—"

"I said they were *managed*. There is a difference, Mr. Amberdale, and you are standing in it." A chair creaked. Something liquid moved in a decanter. "Your sister owes four hundred crowns to men who answer to me, and she is living under a name that would not survive five minutes of honest inquiry. Shall I recite the name? It's rather touching. She chose it herself."

"Don't." The word cracked out of him like a whip over ice. I'd heard Cyrus angry before — at me, mostly, and usually because I deserved it — but I'd never heard him *afraid*. This was afraid wearing anger's coat.

"There it is," Halloway said softly. "There's the man I bought. You'll forgive the indelicacy, but I find it useful to remind us both of the arrangement's true shape. You are not my contractor, Mr. Amberdale. You are my instrument. Instruments that perform well are maintained. Instruments that do not—" a pause, unhurried, a man savoring— "are replaced. And I have other instruments. What I do not have other of, is you. You're the only soul alive she might work for without asking questions. That is your entire value. Guard it."

My hands had closed into fists without consulting me. The fern fronds trembled where my shoulder pressed them. Somewhere in the garden a night bird called, stupid and oblivious, and I thought: *she*. He has a sister. Eight years of knowing the man, of diving with him, bleeding with him, hating him — and I never knew he had a sister. Or maybe I'd known and buried it, the way you bury everything about a person once you've decided what they did to you. Grief makes a lazy historian.

"What's in the crate?" Cyrus asked. Quiet now. Dangerous quiet.

"Naval property. Misplaced records."

"Cyrus Amberdale doesn't risk his neck for misplaced records, and neither does Astrid Vane. She'll want to know."

"She will ask nothing, because you will tell her nothing worth asking about. Triple pay buys a great deal of incuriosity in your trade — I've read her file." Papers shuffled. "But since you raise the point: tell her it's ledgers. Contraband accounts. Something embarrassing to a customs official. Let her believe she's stealing from a thief. People swallow that story whole; it flatters them."

"And if she dives down and opens it anyway? Because she will. You know she will. She opened my sea chest when we were nineteen because she thought I was hiding extra rations."

A dry sound from Halloway that might have been laughter in a poorer man. "Then she'll have learned something true, and she'll be holding the only copy of it in the world, and she'll be standing on a boat full of witnesses I can arrest whenever it suits me. Mr. Amberdale, I have planned this recovery for six years. Do you imagine I've left anything to chance? To *her*?"

Six years. The number went into me like a splinter working toward the bone. Six years ago the Corvane went down. Six years ago the survey charts were drawn — and redrawn, I remembered suddenly, redrafted twice in one season, which no one had thought strange at the time because no one thinks anything strange when the navy does it. Six years of a commodore waiting for the right instrument.

"The crew," Cyrus said. "When it's done. They walk away clean. That was in the contract."

"That was in the contract," Halloway agreed, and the smoothness of it made my skin crawl, because I've heard men repeat terms back like that before, and it always means the same thing: *I am saying the words you need to hear so that you will do the thing I need done.* "Provided the recovery is complete and discreet. Discretion, Mr. Amberdale. That word is doing a great deal of work tonight. See that it does the same aboard her ship."

Chairs. Footsteps. I flattened into the ferns as the conservatory door opened and Cyrus came out alone, hat low, walking fast — too fast, a man trying to outrun the inside of his own skull. He passed within arm's reach of me. In the spill of lamplight through the glass I saw his face, and it was worse than fear. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he already knows comes out wrong.

Then he was through the hedge gap and gone, and I was alone in a commodore's garden with my heart going like a drum in a parade.

I should have left. That's the honest accounting. I had what I came for — more than I wanted. The job was coerced. The cargo was a lie. The man holding Cyrus's leash wore admiral's braid and drank good brandy while he did it. Any sensible woman takes that knowledge home, burns the contract, and sails for friendlier water.

But I stayed one minute longer, crouched in the ferns, because through the conservatory glass I could see Halloway still seated, and he was reading. Not the contract papers. A slim folder, older, its corners soft with handling, tied with the flat black ribbon the Survey Office uses for sealed files. He read it the way a man reads a letter from a dead friend. And on the folder's spine, stamped in faded navy ink, I caught three letters before he closed it away in the desk drawer:

*C-O-R.*

The Corvane.

He turned the lamp down. I went over the wall and didn't stop going until the harbor lights were below me and my breath was tearing ragged in the cold, and all the way down the cliff road one thought kept pace with me like a gull after a fishing boat:

*He's not recovering the cargo. He's burying it. And he needs us to dig the grave.*

* * *

Cyrus's account, since he insists on giving it, and since a story with only one witness is just a rumor with ambition:

He knew she was in the garden. He'd known since the chandlery yard, actually — Astrid Vane moves like the tide, silent and inevitable, and he'd spent fifteen years learning the sound of her. When he'd paused under the awning it wasn't to avoid the navy officers. It was to give her time to get close enough to hear what he needed her to hear.

That's what he tells himself, anyway, lying awake afterward in his rented room above the ropewalk. That some part of him had engineered it — the clumsy route, the obvious gate, the pause at exactly the right moment — so that she would know the truth he couldn't say aloud. Because a man who can't confess can at least arrange to be overheard, and call it conscience.

It's a comfortable story. He doesn't entirely believe it. The truth is muddier, the way truths usually are: he hadn't planned anything. He'd simply stopped being able to carry it alone, and his feet had betrayed him the way feet do.

He sat in Halloway's conservatory and let the commodore recite the arithmetic of his ruin, and he watched the man's hands — soft hands, ringed hands, hands that had never held anything heavier than a pen — and he understood with terrible clarity that this was what power looked like up close. Not brawn. Not even cruelty, particularly. Just a man with a drawer full of folders and the patience to wait six years for a paper to be fetched out of the sea by someone else's hands.

His sister. Lissa, living under a false name in a fishing town two hundred miles south, believing the debts settled, believing her brother's letters when he wrote that everything was fine. Four hundred crowns. Halloway hadn't even needed that much rope — the debts were real but modest, survivable. What made them a leash was the name she'd chosen, and why she'd chosen it, and what Halloway knew about the night in Kestrel Harbor eight years ago that Cyrus had told exactly one person, drunk, weeping, certain it would go to the grave.

It had gone to the commodore instead. Everything went to the commodore eventually. That seemed to be the natural order of things, like water finding low ground.

*Instruments that perform well are maintained.*

He walked back down through Port Calloway with the contract burning in his pocket and rehearsed the lie he'd feed her tomorrow: contraband ledgers, a corrupt customs man, easy money. He rehearsed it until it sounded almost true, and hated himself a little more with each repetition, and noticed — this was the worst part, the part he'd drink about later — that some cold practical corner of his mind was already planning around her. Calculating tides. Calculating which questions she'd ask and which answers would hold. Treating her, in the privacy of his own head, the way Halloway treated everyone: as an instrument.

*You are becoming him,* said some voice in him that sounded like their mentor, old Tam Vane — no relation to her, whatever the port gossips enjoyed claiming — dead eight years at the bottom of Kestrel Shoals with a salvage line around his ankle.

*I'm becoming a man whose sister stays alive,* he answered the voice. *Come back when you've got a better offer.*

The voice, being dead, declined to negotiate.

At the corner of Rope Lane he stopped. Below him the harbor lay black and oily, masts swaying, and among those masts rode the *Kestrel's Luck* — her ship, still hers no matter what the license review said — riding at anchor like a promise neither of them had ever managed to keep.

She'd follow him. He'd known she would; she'd probably already heard everything worth hearing. Which meant that by morning she'd know the job was poison, and a sane woman would refuse it, and the payment wouldn't matter, and Lissa would hang in the wind of Halloway's whim.

Unless the pay was high enough to make sanity expensive. Unless the lie was good enough. Unless he looked her in the eye tomorrow and lied to her one more time, cleanly, thoroughly, the way he'd lied at Kestrel Shoals when he surfaced alone with the log and let the world believe what it wanted to believe.

Eight years ago he'd chosen himself and called it survival.

Tonight, walking down through the sleeping town with a commodore's leash around his throat, Cyrus Amberdale understood that he was about to choose again — and that whichever way he chose, he'd lose something he couldn't dive down and recover.

He just didn't yet know that by morning, the choice would be taken out of his hands entirely — because Astrid Vane had already read the contract he'd left on the tavern table, and had already found, tucked in its folds, the one thing Halloway's careful plans had never accounted for.

Her mentor's handwriting, on a page twenty years old, in the margin of the survey chart.

*Ask about Marrow Bay.*
