## 6. One Last Dive

I told myself I'd sleep on it. That was a lie the way most of my promises to myself are lies — decorative, easily dusted, never meant to hold weight.

The truth was that I'd decided before Cyrus Amberdale finished setting his purse on the tavern table. I'd decided somewhere around the moment he said *triple pay* and my mind did arithmetic without my permission: Bram's debt to the chandlery, Sefa's to the loanhouse, the two years of back wages I owed Tally and Oskar, the charter review hanging over us like a storm glass gone dark. Triple pay cleared all of it. Cleared it and left enough over to refit the *Kestrel's Daughter* — no. Bad name for a ship now. The *Vigil*, maybe. Something with teeth.

But deciding and admitting you've decided are different animals, and only one of them can be shot.

So I slept on it, badly, in a room above the Saltcellar with a window that whistled in E-flat whenever the wind came off the harbor, which was always. And at dawn I went down to the wreck again — alone, without telling Cyrus, because if there was one thing eight years of not trusting him had taught me, it was to check his work.

* * *

The Corvane lay where she'd lain for eleven years, on her beam ends in forty fathoms of cold green nothing, her masts fanned out across the seabed like the fingers of a drowned giant. Most wrecks settle into themselves. This one looked like she was still trying to get up.

I went down on the morning tide with just enough light to work by. Forty fathoms is past the edge of what a sane diver does twice, and I was doing it twice in twelve hours, which told me something about how much sanity I had left in inventory.

The strongbox sat where I'd found it yesterday, wedged under the fallen quarterdeck, half-buried in silt that moved like slow smoke when I brushed it away. Iron banded, lead-lined, and stamped with the Admiralty cipher — the same cipher that was conspicuously absent from every survey chart of this wreck. I'd dived navy losses before. They carry rum, pay chests, dispatches, sometimes guns. What they don't carry is sealed strongboxes bolted to the frame below the waterline, where nobody could reach them in an emergency. You bolt something down like that when whatever's inside matters more than the men above it.

That thought had been sitting in my chest since yesterday, cold as the water. It got colder now.

Because here's the thing about the Corvane that the charts didn't say and the harbor pilots wouldn't discuss: she wasn't lost to weather. Her stern was stove in from the inside out — blown, from within, the night she went down. Eleven years ago the story was mutiny. Then the story was nothing. The navy struck her from the rolls so quietly that half the clerks in Port Calloway didn't know she'd ever existed, and the other half had learned not to.

My crew has one rule older than any of them: never ask what the navy won't discuss. I made that rule. I enforced it with fines. And here I was, alone at forty fathoms, asking.

I put my palm flat against the strongbox lid. Just to steady myself while I rigged the lift lines. That's what I told myself. Steadying.

The iron was furred with rust and something white — coral, or calcification, or — I wiped it clear and my heart did something unprofessional.

Scratches. Inside the corrosion, fine lines, deliberate, cut with a knife point or a nail before the rust swallowed them. Letters. I scraped with my belt knife until my arms burned and the air in my helmet tasted of copper and fear, and I read:

**M. BAY — THEY WERE PASSENGERS**

Four words. Cut by someone who knew they were dying, or knew they might be, and wanted the iron to remember what the paper wouldn't.

I don't remember deciding to surface. I remember the light getting brighter, and my hands shaking on the lines, and Bram's face when I came over the rail gray as old dishwater, and him saying, "Cap? Cap, what's down there?" and me hearing myself answer, "Nothing. Nothing we haven't already sold."

* * *

Cyrus met me at the Saltcellar at noon, because of course he did; the man had a genius for appearing exactly where I least wanted him, which I suppose is one definition of history.

"You dove again." He said it flatly, watching my face. "Alone."

"I like my own company."

"Nobody likes their own company, Astrid. They just haven't found anyone worse." He pushed a mug toward me. I ignored it. He sighed, and for a moment — a single treacherous moment — he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. "You found the box."

"I found the box."

"And?"

"And I'm going to ask you a question, Cyrus Amberdale, and I want you to look at me when you lie, because I've earned at least that much theater." I leaned across the table. "What does Commodore Halloway think is in that crate?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, shuttered, gone. Eight years ago I would have missed it. Eight years of hating a man teaches you his face better than eight years of loving him ever did.

"Dispatches," he said. "Naval correspondence. Sensitive material from a decommissioned loss. That's all Halloway told me, and Halloway doesn't repeat himself."

"Hm," I said. "And the fact that your employer wants this recovered so badly he's grounding my charter and squeezing my crew through three separate port offices — that's just administrative enthusiasm?"

"Astrid—"

"Because here's what I know, Cy." I kept my voice pleasant, conversational, the voice you use for discussing the weather right before you gut someone. "I know the Corvane blew from the inside. I know her charts were burned. I know there's a man's dying handwriting on her strongbox that says *they were passengers* — passengers, Cyrus, plural, on a warship carrying contraband nobody will name. And I know you. I know what you look like when you're being squeezed, because I watched you wear that exact face for six months before Kestrel Shoals, and I was too young and too stupid to read it then."

He went very still. Stillness was always his tell. A guilty man fidgets; a trapped one freezes.

"So here are my terms," I said. "We take the job. One dive, one crate, triple pay, as agreed. But we do it my way — my crew, my boat, my timetable. And when it's done, you tell me who's holding the leash. Not why. Who. That's the price of admission, and believe me when I say it's cheap, because the alternative price is I sail out of this harbor tonight and you can explain to your commodore why his crate is still on the bottom."

For a long moment he said nothing. The tavern noise washed around us — dice, arguments, a fiddle badly mistreated — and Cyrus Amberdale sat inside it like a stone sits in a river.

"You'll regret knowing," he said finally.

"I already know," I said. "I just don't know the name yet. Names make it real. Give me the name."

He looked down at his hands. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the fiddle. "*After.* After the dive. That's the best I can give you, Astrid, and I'm giving you that much because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because you deserve to walk in with your eyes open, even if I can't draw you the map."

It wasn't the name. But it was more than I'd expected, and less than I needed, and exactly like him: a payment made in a currency he chose, at an exchange rate he set.

"Done," I said, and hated how easily the word came.

* * *

I gathered the crew that evening in the *Vigil*'s — no, the *Kestrel's Daughter*'s — main cabin, and I gave them the choice straight, because whatever else I am, I have never spent my people like coin.

"One dive," I told them. "Forty fathoms, known wreck, known hazards. Triple pay, split full shares, and every debt on this crew paid off by nightfall tomorrow. After that, we're clean. Clean enough to refuse anything, forever."

Bram chewed his pipe stem. Sefa counted on her fingers, which she did even when the numbers were simple, because counting calmed her. Tally, youngest of us, just watched me with those sharp harbor-rat eyes.

"And the catch," Bram said. It wasn't a question. There's always a catch; Bram had been catching them longer than I'd been alive.

"The catch is the client," I said. "And the client's client. And the fact that our charter is under review by people who'd very much like us tied to the dock until we rot. This job makes that problem go away. Or it makes us the problem. I genuinely don't know which."

Silence. The lamp swung a little with the tide.

"I owe the chandlery four hundred crowns," Bram said at last. "They took my tools as surety last week. My father's tools." He knocked the pipe out against his boot heel. "One dive, Cap. If it's one dive, I'm in."

Sefa finished counting something that needed no counting. "The loanhouse has my sister's name on my debt," she said quietly. "If I default, they go after her. One dive."

Tally shrugged, seventeen and immortal. "Beats honest work."

Oskar, who was oldest and had survived two navies and one marriage, said nothing at all. He just looked at me for a long time, the way old divers look at water they don't trust, and then he nodded once, slowly, and began checking the air lines.

That's the thing about debts. Nobody chooses them honestly. You choose the paying back, or you don't, and everything else is just weather.

* * *

We went down on the third morning, at slack water, in a flat calm that felt less like luck than like held breath.

I took the descent myself with Oskar on the second line and Tally working the winch above. Forty fathoms. The green dark. The Corvane waiting for us with her fingers spread across the seabed, patient as a grave, which is what she was.

Rigging the strongbox took two hours of bottom time we could ill afford. We slung her in chain and canvas, padded her corners, and when the lift bags took the strain and the box rose out of the silt in a slow bloom of disturbed sediment, I felt something turn over in my chest — not triumph. Dread wearing triumph's coat.

Because underneath, where the box had sat for eleven years, pressed into the mud by its weight, was the shape of something else. Smaller. A document case, rotted nearly to lace, and scattered around it — I made myself look, made myself count — buttons. Uniform buttons, naval pattern, and small ones besides. Small ones.

Passengers. Women and children, maybe, or boys too young for their buttons to mean anything. On a warship that officially never sailed, carrying cargo that officially didn't exist, sunk by an explosion that officially never happened.

*M. Bay. They were passengers.*

I came up slower than I needed to, letting the pressure ease out of me along with something else I couldn't name, and when I broke the surface the sky was the color of unpainted wood and Cyrus was leaning over the rail with his hand already out, and I let him pull me aboard because some habits are deeper than hate.

"It's up," I said. "Get it secured. And Cy—" I caught his sleeve. "When we open that crate, you're standing next to me. Whatever's in it, you see it first with me watching. Those are the terms now."

He didn't argue. That frightened me more than arguing would have.

* * *

We opened the crate in the cabin that evening, hatch battened, lamp turned low, all five of us and Cyrus crowded around a table built for four. The lead lining had kept the salt out. The lock surrendered to Tally's picks in ninety seconds, which told me the navy had trusted secrecy more than steel — fair enough; locks only matter when someone's allowed to look.

Inside: oilcloth packets, wax-sealed, dozens of them. Dispatches, yes. Naval correspondence, yes. Halloway hadn't lied, exactly.

He just hadn't mentioned what the correspondence was *about*.

Depositions. Sworn statements, dated the week before the Corvane sailed, taken from survivors of Marrow Bay — a fishing village on the far coast whose name I knew only as a place the charts marked with a church symbol and no depth soundings, which should have been my first clue years ago. Statements describing soldiers coming ashore under a flag of parley. Describing what happened after. Names of the dead, page after page, in clerk's handwriting, neat as ledger entries. Orders countermanding the garrison commander's restraint — orders signed, in a hand I recognized from a dozen prize-court documents, by **R. Halloway, Commodore**.

And beneath it all, a passenger manifest. Civilians embarked at Marrow Bay under naval protection. Witnesses, being carried to the capital to testify. Carried aboard the Corvane, which blew from the inside out eleven years ago, and whose loss was struck from the records so cleanly that even the grief had nowhere legal to live.

Nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, the harbor slapped against the hull, indifferent as ever.

"They weren't smuggling anything," Sefa said at last, very quietly. "They *were* the something."

"Yes," I said.

Bram set his pipe down unlit, which I had never once seen him do. "This isn't salvage."

"No," I said. "It's evidence."

I looked at Cyrus. He was staring at the manifest with an expression I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to. Relief and horror in equal measure, the face of a man whose worst guess has just been confirmed.

"You knew," I said. Not a question. "Not the details. But you knew it was worse than dispatches."

"I knew Halloway was afraid of what was down there," he said. "I've been afraid of him for two years, Astrid. Fear gives you a good ear for other people's fear." He closed his eyes. "I told myself it was money. Papers. Something shameful but survivable. Everyone tells themselves that. It's how the work gets done."

"Well." I folded the topmost deposition closed, carefully, the way you handle something that might still be alive. "Here's how the work gets done now. We deliver your crate, Cy. Every seal intact, exactly as contracted, because triple pay clears my crew and I keep my bargains even with devils. And then—"

I stopped. Because I didn't know what came after *and then*, and everyone at that table heard the stop.

"And then?" Bram prompted.

"And then I decide whether I'm a salvager or something else," I said. "Ask me tomorrow."

Outside, the tide turned. I felt it in the hull before I heard it — that long, subtle shift in the water's intention, the whole harbor beginning, imperceptibly, to move toward the sea.

And beneath the quay, where the lamplight didn't reach, a navy longboat slid past on silent oars, making for the roadstead. Making, I would learn soon enough, for us.

But that night I only stood at the stern rail with the papers locked in the strongbox below my feet, and felt the dread in my chest stop sloshing around like bilgewater and start, instead, to set — hardening, grain by grain, into something with edges. Something you could steer by.

Purpose, the priests call it. Divers call it ballast.
