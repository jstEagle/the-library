## 7. Betrayal at Anchor

### Astrid

There is a particular quality to a night when everything goes wrong, and it is not the quality you'd expect. It isn't storm-dark or ominous. It's calm. The water lies flat as poured pewter, the anchor holds sweet in the mud off Kestrel Shoals, and the crate sits lashed in our hold like a sleeping dog that hasn't yet decided whether it likes you. The stars are out. Somewhere a nightjar is making its idiot call from the cliffs. And I am standing my watch on the deck of the *Salt Lark* thinking, for no reason I can name, *this is too easy.*

I've learned to trust that thought the way other people trust their mothers.

The crate came up clean on the third dive. That should have been the first warning — nothing Cyrus Amberdale has ever promised me has come up clean on anything less than the fifth attempt. But the strongbox had been there, just where his charts said, and inside it the documents wrapped in oilcloth and wax, dry as the day some clerk with cold hands sealed them aboard the *Corvane*. We hadn't opened them past the first glance. Bram wanted to. I said no. Some instinct older than greed told me that whatever was in those papers, I didn't want to know its face until I was paid and gone.

So we'd weighed anchor at dusk, run up the coast to the sheltered roadstead below Grayfell's shoulder, and waited for Cyrus to come out with the balance of the payment. He was due at midnight.

It was half past eleven when I saw the boat.

Not Cyrus's gig. A navy launch, oars muffled, running dark toward us out of the black mouth of the bay. I counted heads by the way the water broke around them. Twelve. Twelve men don't come to pay a salvage crew. Twelve men come to make sure a salvage crew never talks.

"Bram," I said quietly, and he was beside me before I finished the word — the man moves like a cat that's been paid to be discreet. "Wake them. Quietly. No lamps."

He looked at the launch, then at me, and I watched him do the arithmetic every sailor does: how far to the dinghy, how heavy is the sea, how much of this is survivable. "Astrid—"

"I know."

"We could still—"

"I *know*, Bram. Go."

What I did not say, what I would not let myself think, was that the dinghy held four comfortably and we were six, and that the papers sewn into the lining of my coat — because yes, I had opened them after all, alone, two nights ago, by lamplight, and read enough to understand that I was now carrying something worth more than the ship beneath my feet — were not something I intended to leave floating in a navy harbor.

The launch hooked onto our chains with a grunt of wood on iron. Boots hit the deck. Lanterns bloomed, three of them, and in their light stood a marine lieutenant with a voice like a hatch cover slamming.

"By authority of the Admiralty! This vessel is seized for salvage of Crown property. All hands on deck, hands visible, and no one bleeds tonight unless someone makes it necessary."

Bram appeared at my shoulder, and behind him the rest, blinking and rumpled and frightened in the various ways frightened men arrange themselves — Tam sullen, Odile too still, young Pell looking like he might be sick over the rail. Six of us against twelve marines with loaded muskets and the law on their side, which is the worst arithmetic there is.

And then the second boat came alongside, and Cyrus climbed over our rail into the lantern light, and my heart did something complicated and unforgivable.

He wouldn't meet my eyes.

"Cyrus Amberdale," the lieutenant said, "you will identify the crew manifest and confirm the recovered property."

"Aye, sir." His voice was steady. That was the thing about Cyrus; his voice was always steady, even the time he'd been gut-shot and talking me through stitching him. "That's Astrid Vane, master. Bram Hodge, mate. Tam Ossler, Odile Ferrin, Pell Marsh. Crew of six." He recited us like cargo. Like a manifest. Like men he'd never eaten breakfast with, drunk with, buried a mentor with.

"Amberdale," I said, and my own voice surprised me by coming out level, almost pleasant, "is the payment included in the seizure? Or do I invoice the Admiralty?"

"Madam Vane will accompany us," the lieutenant said. "The rest are detained pending inquiry."

"Inquiry into what?" Odile asked. "We pulled a box out of the mud. Since when is mud Crown property?"

"Since the Crown says so. Bind them."

They took my arms. I let them — there were twelve of them and one of me and the coat was the whole point now, the coat with its new lining and its secret seams, warm against my ribs like a second heartbeat. I let them walk me to the rail. I let them think I was beaten, because beaten women get handled loosely, and loosely is all I needed.

I looked at Cyrus one last time. He was staring at the deck. Eight years ago at Kestrel Shoals he had surfaced alone, and I had spent eight years telling myself there had been no choice, that the sea had made the decision, that he had surfaced because surfacing was all he could do.

Now there was no sea to blame. There was only a lantern-lit deck and a man who could have said *no, sir, not her, take me instead* — and didn't.

"You could have just robbed us," I told him. "Would've been kinder."

His jaw worked. "Astrid—"

"Don't." I said it softly, which somehow made it worse, and I saw him flinch, and some small vicious part of me was glad, and the larger part of me hated both of us.

Then the lieutenant said, "Careful with her at the rail, she's a swimmer," and the universe handed me my opening like a tipsy friend handing you a knife.

I went over the side before the sentence finished.

The water was cold enough to stop thought. Good. Thought was a luxury. I swam down and away, under the keel, into the black, with the lantern light breaking above me in shattered coins and the sound of shouting arriving late and warped through ten feet of seawater. My coat dragged at me. The papers pressed against my ribs. I kicked until my legs burned and came up fifty yards off, behind the launch's wake, treading water in the dark like a piece of driftwood that had opinions.

Behind me, the *Salt Lark* blazed with lanterns and confusion. Someone had noticed the empty spot where I'd been standing. Someone was shouting my description, which was pointless, because my description from the water is: *nothing*.

I turned south, toward the coast, toward the fishing lights strung along the cold shore like a necklace someone had dropped, and I swam.

I did not look back at Cyrus. That was the hardest stroke of the night, and I have swum through worse water than this.

### Cyrus

She went over the rail like she'd rehearsed it, and maybe she had — Astrid rehearses escapes the way other women rehearse dances — and I stood in the lantern light and listened to Lieutenant Corven swear and order boats launched, and I felt the specific, exquisite relief of a man who has just survived something, and the specific, bottomless shame of knowing what surviving cost.

Identify the crew. That was the price. Halloway had been very clear. *You will identify them, Amberdale, personally, by name, or your sister's creditors will learn where she sleeps, and the second set of papers on my desk will find their way to a magistrate.* Clear as a chart. And I had done it. I had stood on the deck of the ship where I'd been happier than anywhere in my life and read off the names of the people I loved best, in a voice that didn't shake, because my voice never shakes, God rot it, my voice has never once shaken when it most damn well should.

"They'll run boats till dawn," Corven said beside me, watching the black water. He was younger than I expected, and there was something wrong with his face — not cruelty; reluctance. A man doing a job he'd already begun to regret. "If she's a strong swimmer she might make the shore."

"She's the strongest swimmer I've ever known," I said.

Corven glanced at me. Something passed between us, brief and unspoken, the way things pass between men who are both lying to themselves. Then he turned away to shout orders, and I was left alone at the rail of a seized ship, among a crew in irons who would not look at me either, and the nightjar was still calling from the cliffs like nothing in the world had changed.

Tam spat at my boots as they led him past. I deserved it. I'd have spat too.

Bram stopped, though. Bram, who never wastes a motion, leaned close as the marines hustled him by, and said, very quietly, "She'll live. Question is whether you will," and then he was gone into the hold, and I stood there turning the sentence over like a coin I wasn't sure was gold.

Below decks, the crate sat where we'd lashed it. The lieutenant's men would open it by morning. They'd find the documents — the real ones, the ones about Marrow Bay, the massacre, the orders signed in a commodore's neat hand — and they'd carry them back to the man who signed them, and the truth would go into a furnace, and the world would go on not knowing, and I would go on being paid in the only currency Halloway deals in, which is the lives of people you love, spent slowly.

Unless.

I looked at the water. Boats were pulling toward shore, lanterns sweeping. She'd have gone south, along the coast, to the fishing villages — Maren's village, if she remembered Maren, and Astrid remembers everything. Six hours' head start if I moved now. Less if I hesitated, and hesitation was the one luxury I'd been indulging in for eight years, and look what it bought.

My sister's face rose up in me, unbidden — Nell, laughing in the kitchen in Port Sallow, before the debts, before Halloway's men started appearing outside her window like weather. If I ran, they'd ruin her. If I stayed, they'd ruin everyone else, one at a time, forever, because men like Halloway never stop spending.

*Astrid,* said the part of me that had surfaced alone at Kestrel Shoals and had been drowning ever since. *Choose.*

For once — for the first time since I was twenty-six years old and swimming away from my mentor's last bubble — I chose.

I went below, under the bored eye of a marine who thought I was fetching my coat, and I did fetch my coat, and under it, from the false seam in the crate's lining where I'd hidden them an hour before the raid — because I had known, God help me, I had known the moment I saw the launch, some part of me had always known — I took the route map. Halloway's pursuit orders. The roads, the checkpoints, the names of the trackers, the schedule of the snows.

Then I walked to the rail, waited for the sentry to turn toward the shout at the bow, and went into the cold black water after the woman whose crew I had just sold.

The sea took me like an old debt. I swam south.

### Astrid

The fishing village of Coldharbor Row consists of nine houses, one church, and a great many nets, and at two in the morning it consists entirely of one widow named Maren holding a boathook and asking me, in a voice like gravel in honey, why I'm dripping on her steps.

"Maren," I said, through chattering teeth. "It's Astrid. From the *Kestrel* run. Eight years ago. You fed me fish stew and told me my mentor was a fool and you were right."

The boathook lowered an inch. "You look worse than you did then."

"I feel better than I look, which should tell you something about how bad I look."

She got me inside, got a fire going, got brandy into me with the brisk mercy of a woman who has pulled drowned sailors out of the sea for forty years and has no patience for drama. Only when my hands stopped shaking did she nod at my coat, hanging steaming by the fire, and say, "Navy after you?"

"Navy ahead of me, behind me, and probably beside me by noon."

"And whatever's in that coat is why."

I looked at her. She looked back, sixty years old and unimpressed by death, having clearly outlived several husbands and all fear. "Maren," I said, "there are papers in this coat that can hang a commodore. And I'm the only person alive who knows where they are."

She snorted and ladled more stew. "Then you'd best not die here. Waste of good sewing."

Outside, far down the coast, a light was moving along the shore road. Searching. I pulled the coat closer and started planning six days of mountains between me and the capital, and the snows that were coming early this year, and the man I had left kneeling in lantern light on the deck of my ship.

I hated him. I told myself I hated him, thoroughly and permanently, and I believed it the way you believe a rope will hold — right up until you put your weight on it.

The light on the shore road went out.

Someone, somewhere behind me, had also chosen to swim.
