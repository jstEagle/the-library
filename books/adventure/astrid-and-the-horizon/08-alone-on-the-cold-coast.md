## 8. Alone on the Cold Coast

The sea spat me out like something it was ashamed of.

I woke on my stomach in a wrack line of kelp and broken crab pots, with a gull screaming overhead as if I owed it money. For a long moment I didn't move, because moving required reasons, and I had none left. The cold had gotten into my bones and set up housekeeping. My left shoulder announced itself with every heartbeat — wrenched, maybe worse, from hauling myself over the Corvane's rail and into black water while marines' boots thundered on the deck above.

The papers were still there. That was the first coherent thought I managed: *the coat, check the coat.* I rolled onto my side — an operation that cost me ten minutes and most of my dignity — and pressed my hand flat against the lining. The stiff little rectangle of sewn-in canvas was there, snug against my ribs, holding its secret the way I'd once held my breath under the keel of a listing hull. Forty pages of navy paper that forty people had died to keep quiet about, and they'd survived a night swim in the Cold Coast surf because I'd stitched them in with sail twine and paranoia two winters ago, back when I trusted no one enough to leave documents loose in a satchel.

Paranoia, it turns out, is just foresight with a bad reputation.

I lay there and took inventory the way you do after the sea tries to keep you. One diver, half-drowned. One shoulder, ruined for climbing. One set of clothes, salt-stiff and stinking of weed. One crew, arrested at anchor. One partner — *former* partner, I corrected myself, and then, more honestly, *one Cyrus* — who had stood on that deck with a lantern in his hand and pointed his chin at me when the marine lieutenant asked which one was the salvage master.

He hadn't even said my name out loud. He'd just looked at me across twenty feet of wet deckplanking, and his face had done something complicated and terrible, and then he'd given me up with a nod, the way you'd hand over a borrowed tool you'd already broken.

And I'd gone over the rail rather than let them take the coat off me.

So here I was. Alive, which felt less like a victory than a clerical error.

I got up. It took three attempts and involved language that would have gotten me fined in any respectable port, but there are no respectable ports on the Cold Coast, only fishing villages hunched behind their breakwaters like old men avoiding eye contact. I started walking along the shingle, because walking was the only thing I knew how to do that didn't require thinking.

The sun came up gray and apologetic over the water. Gulls argued over something dead. My boots squelched. Somewhere behind me, miles south, Halloway's men would be discovering that the woman they'd arrested had vanished overboard between one wave and the next, and somewhere north of that discovery, a commodore's career depended on finding her before she found a magistrate. I knew how this worked. I'd spent my whole adult life being the person who found things other people wanted lost. Now I was the thing.

It's a strange feeling, being your own salvage. You know exactly what you're worth to the people looking for you, and it isn't much — a body is easier to explain than a witness.

* * *

The village appeared around a headland an hour later: a dozen houses, a drying shed, nets hung like funeral banners, boats hauled up on skids with their bellies showing. Smoke from chimneys stood straight up in the windless morning, which meant the weather was either perfectly calm or saving itself for something spectacular. On the Cold Coast, bet on spectacular.

I'd meant to walk through it. That was the plan — such as it was — keep to the cart track, stay unremarkable, put distance between me and the water before anyone started asking why a drowned woman was touring the shoreline. But my shoulder had other opinions, and halfway past the drying shed the world did a slow, courteous tilt, and the next thing I registered was gravel against my cheek and someone saying, "Well, you're not one of mine," in a voice like a door opening onto a warm room.

She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a barrel and twice as sturdy, with forearms that had clearly ended arguments with fish and men alike. Gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a fishhook. She crouched over me without fear, which told me two things: this village didn't get strangers often, and she wasn't the type who scared easy.

"Can you stand?" she asked.

"I can lie here convincingly."

"Hmph." She got a hand under my good arm and hauled, and I mean *hauled* — I came off the ground like a net full of haddock. "Maren," she said. "You'll be wanting soup before you'll be wanting questions, so we'll do it in that order."

"Maren," I said, hanging off her shoulder like a wet coat, "you should know I'm trouble."

"Everyone's trouble or dead, dear. Soup first."

Her house sat at the end of the row, closest to the sea, which told me she'd buried a husband who'd wanted to hear the water even indoors. Inside it smelled of peat smoke, tar, and bread — the specific smell of a life maintained alone and without complaint. She sat me by the fire, cut my coat off my bad shoulder with a knife sharp enough to shave with, and clucked over the bruising like it was a personal insult.

"Fell down some stairs," I said.

"On the beach?"

"The beach has stairs now. Very modern."

She looked at me the way Bram looks at a knot he doesn't trust. "I've lived on this coast forty years," she said. "I've pulled eleven drowned men out of that surf. Nine of them lied to me about how they got there. The soup doesn't care. Eat."

I ate. It was fish soup, thick enough to stand a spoon in, and it may have been the best thing I have ever tasted, though I grant that nearly drowning biases a review. Somewhere in the second bowl, the exhaustion caught up with me — not the tiredness of muscles but the deeper kind, the kind that sits behind the eyes and whispers. Maren put a blanket around my shoulders and said I could sleep in the loft, and I was asleep before I finished climbing to it.

* * *

I dreamed of the Corvane. Not the wreck — the deck, the night of the raid. Lantern light. Boots on planking. And Cyrus's face doing that complicated, terrible thing, and my own voice in the dream saying *you could have thrown the lantern instead*, which is true, he could have, there was time, there's always time until there isn't. In the dream I went over the rail again and again, and each time the water was colder, and each time I surfaced, the deck was still there above me with him standing at it, watching, not reaching down.

I woke in the dark with my heart going like a pump handle and the papers crackling faintly against my ribs when I breathed. Below, Maren's fire had burned to embers. I lay in the loft and stared at the rafters and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the beach.

Here's the sum. I am the last person alive who knows what those papers prove. The crew is in Halloway's cells — Bram, Tally, young Fen with the bad knee, all of them, locked up because of a job I said yes to. Cyrus is — wherever Cyrus is, doing whatever Cyrus does now, which is survive, which he's very good at. The priest whose name was in the letters is presumably dead or bought. Every officer who signed those orders at Marrow Bay is alive, promoted, and braided. And the evidence that could bury them all is currently sleeping in a widow's loft inside a coat with one sleeve cut off.

All I have to do is carry it six hundred miles to the capital, to a magistrate or an editor who won't simply hand me back to the navy for the reward. Through country where every garrison flies the same flag as the men hunting me. With a shoulder that doesn't work and a face that's probably been described in every port office from here to the Straits.

Or.

Or I could stop.

That's the whisper, and I won't pretend I didn't hear it. There's a version of the story where Astrid Vane drowns tonight, officially. A body never recovered, a name struck from the port registries, grief filed and closed. Maren takes in a quiet cousin from down the coast who mends nets and asks no questions. The papers stay sewn in a coat hanging on a peg, and in ten years the thread rots and the truth rots with it, and Marrow Bay stays a footnote nobody reads, and the dead stay unnamed, and I stay *alive*. Warm. Fed. Still.

I have been running since I was seventeen years old. Port to port, season to season, never a lease, never a grave, never anything you couldn't walk away from in one tide. People think divers love the sea. Mostly we love leaving it. And here the sea had finally offered me the one thing I'd always been fleeing toward: permission to stop. No horizon needed. Just a village, a fire, a woman who hauls strangers out of the surf and doesn't ask about stairs.

I could let the truth drown. God knows it's had practice; the navy held it under for fifteen years.

I must have made some sound, because Maren's voice came up through the floorboards, unhurried: "You'll be deciding whether to live or vanish, are you? Take till morning. Decisions keep better after sleep, like fish."

I didn't answer. After a while I heard her bank the fire, and the house settled around me, creaking its old creaks, and I thought about my mentor — old Sela, who taught me to read water and read men and trust neither — and how the last thing she ever said to me at Kestrel Shoals was *bring it up, Astrid, whatever it costs.* She'd meant the salvage. She'd been wrong about a lot of things, but she'd never been wrong about bringing things up. Things sunk stay sunk only if nobody dives.

And I am, whatever else is true of me, a diver.

Morning came gray again, with wind this time, the kind that means weather with ambitions. I came down the ladder one-armed and found Maren at the table with bread, cheese, and a look on her face that meant she'd already decided something on my behalf.

"There's a cart goes inland Thursday," she said. "Miller's boy. He's deaf as a post and loyal as one too. He'll take you as far as the crossroads at Thistlefen without remembering he did."

"That's—"

"And this." She pushed a bundle across the table: a plain wool coat, brown, unremarkable, the kind of coat that gets forgotten in taverns on purpose. "Your other one's got a hole in the sleeve and a story in the lining. Wear the new one. Keep the old one close."

I looked at her. "Maren. If anyone finds out you helped me—"

"Forty years, dear." She sliced bread with that razor knife, unbothered. "I buried a husband the sea kept, and a son the *navy* kept — conscripted at Marrow Bay, they told me. 'Lost with all hands,' they wrote. He was nineteen." The knife paused, just for a breath. "So don't you dare drown that coat in my ocean. Whatever's in it — you bring it up. Whatever it costs. That's what we do with our drowned on this coast. We bring them up."

My throat did something I chose not to examine. I took the coat.

"Where's the nearest road north?" I asked.

Maren sat down across from me and folded her hands, and for the first time since the beach, she looked older than her forearms suggested. "North," she said slowly, "there's only one way anybody goes. The Grayfell road. Six days' climb through the pass, if the snows hold off." She glanced out the window, at the wind bending the smoke sideways now, at the gulls flying low and hard. "And they won't hold off. Snows are early this year. Everyone says so."

Of course they are, I thought. Nothing in my life has ever held off.

I ate my bread, and outside the wind rose, and somewhere over the mountains the first flakes were already falling on a road I'd have to walk, hunted, one-armed, carrying the dead in my lining — and the terrible, treacherous thought crossed my mind that I still couldn't tell whether I was running toward something, or just running.

Then Maren said, "There's a man in the village sold mules," and I knew which one it was.
