## 9. The Mountain Road

Maren's kitchen smelled of fish oil and rosemary, which sounds like a mistake until you've eaten her chowder and understood that the woman simply refuses to let anything in her house be simple. I sat at her table with my ribs strapped, my shoulder stitched, and the documents sewn into the lining of my coat like a second spine, and I watched her knead bread with the violence of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons to the sea and decided long ago that grief was best worked through dough.

"You're leaving," she said. Not a question. She'd known since the second morning, when she caught me standing at her window looking north instead of out at the water.

"I'm resting."

"You're resting *badly*. You sleep like a cat on a ship's deck — one ear open, ready to bolt." She punched the dough flat. "I've sheltered smugglers, deserters, one bishop who'd stolen church silver, and a man who claimed to be a prince of somewhere landlocked. They all looked at my window eventually. You're the first one who looked north."

I didn't answer, because there wasn't an answer that didn't end with me lying to a woman who'd pulled me out of the surf, stitched me shut, and fed me for four days without once asking what I'd done to earn three marines' bullets.

Maren wiped her hands and sat across from me. Up close, her eyes were the pale gray of weather coming.

"Let me save us both some time," she said. "You're going to the capital."

"Maren—"

"Don't insult me. Nobody washes ashore with naval musket balls in their wake and a face like yours unless they're carrying something worth killing for. And nobody carries something worth killing for toward the capital unless they mean to give it to someone who'll use it." She leaned back. "So. The Grayfells."

"The Grayfells," I said, because denying it now would have been like denying the tide.

"There's exactly one road over them that doesn't require wings or a death wish. The Shepherd's Stair, up the Vael valley, over Cold Saddle, down the far side to the post road. Six days if the weather holds. Seven if you're hurt. Eight if you're stupid, which you look like you might be."

"And if the weather doesn't hold?"

She was quiet for a moment, and that quiet told me more than anything she'd said yet.

"It's the ninth day of Frostmoon," she said finally. "The snows came early this year. My sister writes from the Vael side — they had white on the high passes a week ago. A dusting. But a week ago it was a dusting, and now—" she shrugged, "—now it's anyone's guess. The mule drivers have stopped taking contracts. That should tell you something. Mule drivers are not, as a profession, famous for their caution."

"What else is there? Coast road?"

"Coast road is three weeks and every port between here and the capital has garrison troops in it by now. They're looking for a woman. Dark hair, injured shoulder, traveling alone." She said it flatly, without accusation. "The description's been read from every customs house porch from here to Saltmere. Whoever you are, Astrid — and no, don't tell me, I don't want your name, names are how people get hanged — whoever you are, the sea has closed behind you. There's only forward."

* * *

I spent that afternoon doing the thing I do worst: sitting still.

Maren's cottage sat above the village on a shelf of rock, and from her doorstep I could see the whole gray curve of the bay, the fishing boats drawn up for the season, and beyond them, rising wall upon wall into the clouds, the Grayfell Range. From below it didn't look like mountains. It looked like a verdict. The peaks were already white, and the white crept downward as I watched, or seemed to, the way trouble does when you stare at it too long.

Six days' climb. I'd crossed worse ground — I'd crossed the Ashen Spur in summer with a broken oar for a walking stick and Cyrus complaining about his boots the entire way. But summer was summer, and Cyrus had been—

I stopped that thought where it stood. Thinking about Cyrus was like probing a tooth. You knew what you'd find. You did it anyway, and hated yourself after.

He'd pointed at me across the deck while the marines took the crew. He'd said my name like an accusation. Whatever Halloway held over him — debts, threats, his sister, God only knew — it had been enough. Again. It had always been enough, with Cyrus. Survival first, conscience later, if the conscience could catch him.

And still. Still, some stubborn drowned part of me kept saying: he hesitated. Half a heartbeat before he gave the order, he hesitated, and half a heartbeats have started wars and ended marriages and turned ships around mid-ocean.

I made myself stop. There were papers in my coat lining with forty-one names on them, and none of those names were Cyrus Amberdale, and they deserved better than my sentimentality.

Forty-one names. Witnesses to Marrow Bay, carried aboard the *Corvane* under navy seal, sent to a watery grave so that a commodore's record would stay clean. I'd read them a hundred times in my head since the night I'd gone overboard. I could recite them like a litany. Somewhere in the capital there were families who'd been told their kin died of fever, of piracy, of nothing at all — and the proof that they'd been murdered by their own government was currently keeping my left ribs warm.

That was the arithmetic of it, and the arithmetic didn't care about snow.

* * *

The village had one trader, a low-beamed shop that smelled of tar and dried cod, run by a man named Osk with hands like anchor chain and the conversational range of one. I bought rope, a wool blanket, hardtack, a spirit lamp, and asked him, casually, whether he had any stock animals for sale.

Osk looked at me for a long time. Then he led me out back to a lean-to where a single mule stood regarding the world with the deep, unimpressed patience of her kind.

"Bess," he said. "Eleven years old. Sure-footed, stubborn, smarter than most men I've served with. She bit the last tax collector. That's a recommendation, not a warning."

"How much?"

He named a figure that would have bought a decent dinghy. I paid it without blinking, because the alternative was dying on a mountain with full pockets, and I have never seen the romance in that.

"She'll need shoes checked before the pass," Osk said, saddling her himself. "Where are you headed?"

"South," I said. "Down the coast. My sister's in Saltmere."

It came out smooth. That's the ugly truth about lying — eight years of salvage work teaches you to say false things in a level voice, because half the trade is convincing harbormasters you found things where you didn't. I lied to Osk the way I breathed, and I hated myself for it, because Maren had fed me chowder and Osk had sold me a mule that bites tax collectors, and neither of them deserved to be lied to by a woman with stolen government papers sewn into her coat.

But if I told the truth and the wrong ears heard it, they wouldn't just hang me. They'd hang everyone who'd helped me. Maren. Osk. Half the village. Halloway hadn't struck me as a man who distinguished between the guilty and the convenient.

"Saltmere," Osk repeated. His eyes flicked north, to the pass, then back to me. Something moved behind them — not suspicion exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has lived under mountains his whole life and knows exactly why travelers lie about their destinations, and has decided which lies are worth respecting.

"Safe roads," he said, and handed me Bess's lead.

* * *

Maren walked me to the edge of the village at first light. Fog lay on the water like something poured, and the mountains above us had put on fresh white overnight — I could see the line of it, maybe six hundred feet lower than the day before. The snows weren't coming. They were already here, and we were all just waiting for them to finish arriving.

Maren pressed a bundle into my arms: bread, cheese wrapped in waxed cloth, a flask of something that smelled strong enough to strip varnish.

"For the cold," she said. "Or the fear. Whichever gets you first."

"Maren—"

"If you thank me I'll throw this flask in the harbor." She adjusted the strap on my satchel with brisk, rough tenderness, the way you'd rig a sail. "Listen to me. I lost a husband to the sea and a son to a war nobody could explain, and I have spent thirty years learning the difference between the things you can survive and the things you can't. Do you know what it is?"

"Tell me."

"The things you can't survive are the ones you walk away from and spend the rest of your life carrying anyway. Running isn't lighter than fighting. It never is. It just spreads the weight over more years." She stepped back and looked at me, and for a moment the flint went out of her face and something older and kinder looked through. "Whatever's in that coat of yours — and don't tell me, I said — it's already killed you once. Finish the job or come back and let it go. Those are the only two mercies left."

I swung up onto Bess, who expressed an opinion about my riding ability by sidestepping into a fence.

"One more thing," Maren called as I turned the mule north. "Cold Saddle. If the cloud comes down over the saddle, you get below the tree line and you wait it out, no matter what the schedule in your head says. Dead women keep no appointments."

I raised a hand without looking back, because looking back is how you talk yourself into staying, and staying was the one thing I couldn't afford. Behind me I heard her boots on the gravel, unhurried, going home. Ahead of me the valley opened like a gray throat and swallowed the road whole.

* * *

The first day was easy, and I distrusted it completely.

The Vael valley ran north between shoulders of dark pine, following a river the color of gunmetal. Farmsteads thinned out as I climbed; by afternoon there was nothing but forest, the mule's steady clop, and the enormous indifferent silence of country that has never needed anything from anybody. I passed one shepherd driving his flock down-valley ahead of the weather, and we exchanged the nod that strangers on bad roads give each other: *I see you, I want nothing, good luck.*

Bess, it turned out, was everything Osk promised. She picked her way over scree like a dancer and balked at precisely the things worth balking at — twice she refused a stretch of trail I'd have sworn was fine, and both times, dismounting to check, I found ice glazing the stone beneath the dirt. I began talking to her. By evening I was telling her things I hadn't told Maren. She listened with one ear swiveled back and the profound moral indifference of a creature who has heard men lie to each other for eleven years and expects nothing better.

That night I camped in the lee of a fallen spruce and built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. I ate Maren's bread and drank a finger of her flask and laid the map out on my knees — a proper survey map, bought in the village from a boy who didn't ask questions, because asking questions in a navy town is a habit that shortens lives.

The Shepherd's Stair wound up the valley wall in switchbacks. Above it, Cold Saddle — a notch in the ridge like a bite taken out of the sky. Beyond the saddle, the far slope dropped to the post road, and the post road ran to the capital, and the capital held magistrates and editors and, if Cyrus's sister was half the woman he claimed, one safe lodging among ten thousand hostile windows.

Six days. Five, if the weather held. Zero, if it didn't.

Somewhere south of me, Corven — the lieutenant whose face I'd last seen through freezing spray as I went over the rail — was presumably explaining to his commodore how a dead woman had gotten deader. Somewhere behind me, Halloway was discovering that the papers hadn't gone to the bottom of the bay with me, and recalculating. Men like Halloway always recalculate fast. It's what makes them dangerous. They treat murder as logistics.

I folded the map and looked up. Through a tear in the clouds, the saddle gleamed white under a fat moon, close enough to touch and a thousand feet of climbing away.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about running toward something instead of away from it: it feels almost identical from the inside. Same burning legs. Same counting of miles. Same voice at three in the morning whispering that you could stop, you could stop right here, find a valley and a new name and let the world sort itself out. For eight years I'd told myself I was free because I kept moving — port to port, wreck to wreck, never anchored long enough for anything to hold me. Sitting in Maren's kitchen, I'd finally understood the truth: I hadn't been free. I'd been fleeing in a circle. The horizon I chased was just the far side of the thing I was running from.

But this — this was different. This was motion with a destination. Forty-one names at the end of it. A hearing, a reckoning, whatever shape justice took when it finally crawled out of the sea. After that, I could stop. After that, stopping would be a choice instead of a surrender.

Assuming the mountain let me.

Bess snorted and leaned her warm weight against my shoulder, which is mule for *stop brooding and sleep.* I banked the fire, wrapped myself in Osk's blanket, and lay watching the moonlight crawl off the saddle as the cloud closed over it again, soft and total, like a lid coming down on a box.

In the morning I would climb. In six days, if the mountain permitted, I would be in the capital with the truth in my coat and the law, for once, on my side.

I was nearly asleep when I heard it — faint, down the valley, carried on the wind off the snowfields: hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast, moving north, moving the way men move when they're being paid by the mile.

I lay very still in the dark and counted.

Four horses. Coming up the Vael road behind me, into the teeth of the coming storm, at a pace no traveler keeps and no honest man needs.

The snows had come early. So, it seemed, had everything else.
