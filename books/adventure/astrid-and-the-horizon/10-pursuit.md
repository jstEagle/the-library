## 10. Pursuit

The orders came to me at dawn, which should have been my first warning. In eleven years of service I had learned that nothing good is ever issued before breakfast.

Lieutenant Aldous Corven, it read, you are hereby detached from His Majesty's frigate *Vigilant* and assigned to recover certain naval property stolen from the wreck site of the HMS *Corvane*. You will be provided a tracker squad of six men. Use whatever force the situation requires.

*Property.* That was the word they'd chosen. Not documents. Not papers. Property — as though Astrid Vane had made off with a crate of ship's biscuit and a bell rope.

I stood in Commodore Halloway's office while he signed the second copy without looking at it, his pen moving with the smooth economy of a man who had signed away worse things than a lieutenant's morning. The office smelled of ink and bay rum and something underneath both that I couldn't name at the time and have never been able to name since, though I have smelled it again since — on battlefields, in courtrooms. It is the smell of a decision already made, waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"The diver," Halloway said, "is to be treated as a common thief."

"And if she resists?"

"Then she's a common thief who resisted." He blotted the page and slid it across the desk. "You have a reputation for thoroughness, Corven. I trust you won't develop a reputation for anything else."

There it was. The compliment shaped like a threat, or the threat shaped like a compliment — with Halloway the two had worn down into the same coin long ago, and he spent them interchangeably.

I saluted. He did not return it. He was already reading the next paper in the stack, and I understood that I had ceased to exist in that room the moment my signature dried.

Outside, the morning was cold and clean and entirely indifferent to what I was about to do with it.

* * *

Sergeant Dree was waiting in the yard with the squad, and I want to describe them honestly, because history — if it ever bothers to record any of this, which I doubt — will call us Halloway's hunters, and that word does too much work too cheaply.

Dree himself was a tracker of genuine talent, a lean grey man who could read ground the way priests read scripture, and who had once tracked a deserter forty miles through the Kestrel highlands in February and brought him back alive when every order said dead. He'd been reprimanded for it. He said he'd do it again. I believed him.

Then Pell and Oakes, marines both, solid as fence posts. Harrow, young, freckled, nineteen if he was a day, who had joined the navy because his village had one road out of it and the road went through a recruiting tent. The Voss brothers, twins, who finished each other's sentences and each other's rations. And last, a civilian contracted under some clause I didn't ask about: a man called Quill, small and quiet, with eyes like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like the answer to.

Quill was the tracker's tracker. Dree found tracks; Quill found intentions. I'd seen him work once before, in a smuggling case up the coast, and the thing I remembered most was that he apologized to the people he caught. Quietly. Every time. Like a man settling accounts he hadn't opened.

"Where are we going, sir?" Dree asked, falling in beside me at the gate.

"Grayfell country. A fishing village on the cold coast first, then the mountain road."

"And we're after?"

"A thief."

He looked at me sideways. Dree had been a soldier long enough to hear the quotation marks around a word even when nobody spoke them aloud. "What did she steal?"

"Papers," I said, because lying badly to a good man seemed better than lying well.

"Papers," he repeated. We walked another fifty paces. "And how many men does it take to recover papers?"

"As many as the Commodore sends," I said, "and not one fewer."

He grunted, which from Dree was a whole sermon, and we rode out.

* * *

I'll say plainly what I only admitted to myself somewhere around the second day, because a man's honesty with himself is the last thing he spends and he spends it slowly:

I didn't believe the official story. Not from the beginning. Perhaps not even from the moment I read the orders.

Here is what troubled me. Navies do not dispatch seven men and a civilian tracker to recover salvage from a wreck that appears on no survey chart I've ever seen. Navies do not care about wrecks. Wrecks are, by definition, finished business. A frigate goes down, the Admiralty writes its line — lost with all hands, cause unknown, God rest them — and the sea keeps the receipt. Nobody sends lieutenants after drowned ships unless there is something aboard the drowned ship that frightens living men.

And there was the matter of the name. *Corvane.* I'd heard it before, years ago, in the mess at Marrow Bay station, before Marrow Bay became a place officers stopped mentioning. A supply frigate, someone said, that carried witnesses instead of cargo. I'd laughed along with the table because that is what junior officers do; we laugh at the jokes that keep our careers afloat. But the phrase had lodged in me like a splinter, working deeper whenever the weather turned.

Witnesses. To what?

I asked Quill, on the second night, sitting across a fire neither of us needed for warmth but both of us wanted for company. He was cleaning his boots with a concentration that suggested he'd rather be doing anything else, including fighting.

"You've read the file," I said. "What's in these papers we're chasing?"

Quill considered the question the way he considered everything — as though the answer might cost him money. "Names," he said finally. "Dates. The kind of writing that gets kept in triplicate by careful men."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He set down the boot. "Lieutenant, I've done eleven contracts for the Navy. Recoveries, mostly. Ledgers, correspondence, one time a strongbox of letters some admiral wrote to a woman who wasn't his wife. This is the first time the orders came with a list of ports where we're forbidden to stop for supplies." He looked at me across the fire. "You don't forbid ports to a man chasing a thief. You forbid ports to a man chasing a story."

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "And if the story is true?"

Quill picked the boot back up. "Then we're the last page of it," he said, "and I'd rather not be."

That was the whole of our conversation, and it was enough. I lay awake most of that night listening to the horses shift and the sea work the shingle below the bluff, and I composed, in my head, three different reports I might write if things went badly, and discarded all three, because all of them began with the sentence *I should have refused this assignment*, and no report in the history of the Navy has ever begun well with that sentence.

* * *

The fishing village was called Salt Hollow, and it received us the way coastal villages receive the Crown: with bread, beer, and lies.

We arrived on the fourth day, riding in mud-spattered and unimpressive, and I gave the innkeeper my best courteous-officer voice and asked whether a woman had come ashore recently, injured perhaps, alone certainly.

"Fishing's poor this season," he said, which answered nothing, which was the point.

"We're looking for a diver. Dark hair. She'd have come off the water."

"Everyone here comes off the water, Lieutenant. It's a village."

Harrow, bless his guileless heart, nearly laughed, and turned it into a cough. I thanked the innkeeper, paid for rooms we would not sleep in, and took the squad out along the shingle where the tide kept its records.

It was Dree who found her, or rather found where she had been. A stretch of sand above the tideline, pressed by a body that had crawled, not walked — the drag-marks of someone hauling herself with her arms while her legs argued. Blood in the old dried way, days old. And leading up from it, fainter, the print of borrowed boots, two sizes wrong, toward the houses on the hill.

"She's hurt," Dree said quietly. "Badly, when she came ashore. Someone's taken her in."

"Someone always takes her in," I said. "That's the trouble with thieves. They look like people."

We knocked on doors through the afternoon. The village performed ignorance with a unanimity that was almost artistic — forty souls, and not one of them had seen so much as a gull land. Old women told us the weather was turning. Young women told us the weather was turning. A child told us the weather was turning and then asked Harrow if his sword was real, and Harrow, who cannot lie to children, showed him the blunt edge and ruined whatever menace we had left.

By evening I knew two things. First, that Astrid Vane was within a mile of us, behind one of these doors, being fed soup by someone who had decided the Navy was not on her side. Second, that I did not particularly want to find her.

I want to be careful with that second thing, because it sounds like weakness and it wasn't. It was arithmetic. If the papers proved what the shape of this errand suggested they proved — if a commodore had sent seven armed men after a wounded woman to bury the account of a massacre — then every hour I delayed was an hour the truth stayed above water. And if I found her and arrested her, the papers went into an evidence locker with a lock Halloway held the key to, and the massacre went back to being a joke officers told in the mess, and I went back to being the man who delivered it there.

So I slowed us down. Professionally. A door-to-door search done properly takes two days in a village this size, and I did it properly, which is to say thoroughly and at the pace of a funeral procession. Dree watched me do it and said nothing, and his saying nothing was the loudest conversation I'd had in weeks.

On the second evening, Quill found what the village had hidden so carefully: boot prints on the back road north, toward Grayfell, a day old at least, and beside them the round iron shoes of a mule.

"She bought a mule," Quill reported. "From the farrier. Paid too much, in a hurry. Farrier says she told him she was bound for the coast road south."

"South," I said.

"Yes, sir. Which is why the prints go north."

I looked at the mountains. The Grayfells stood up against the sky like a wall somebody had built and then abandoned, their shoulders already white. Too early in the year for snow, everyone said. Everyone had been saying it for a week, in the tone of people trying to convince themselves.

"Six days' climb if the pass holds open," Dree said, following my gaze. "Less if it closes and we turn back. More if it closes and we don't."

"It'll close," Quill said.

"Then we'd best not be under it when it does." I turned to the squad. "We ride at first light. Hard as the horses can bear."

They looked at me — six soldiers and a contractor, tired, muddy, and owed an explanation I couldn't give them. Harrow, of all of them, found his voice. "Sir? The woman. When we catch her." He hesitated. "Is it true what they're saying in the port taverns? About the *Corvane*? About Marrow Bay?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the valley a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"When we catch her," I said, "she'll get exactly what the law provides. No more." I paused, and heard myself add, before I could stop it: "No less."

Harrow nodded, satisfied, and went to see to the horses. Dree lingered a moment longer, studying me with those grey tracker's eyes that read ground and men by the same method.

"And if the law and the Commodore disagree?" he asked, very quietly.

"Then the law wins," I said, "and I lose my commission, and we'll both pretend this conversation happened in a dream."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he touched his cap and went, and I sat alone with the fire and the mountains and the growing certainty that I was riding toward a choice I had already made and simply hadn't informed myself of yet.

Above the tree line, the first flakes were coming down. Early, everyone said. Unseasonable.

The snow did not care what season it was. It fell the way truth falls — slowly, softly, and covering everything — and by morning the road north would be a white page with one set of boot prints and one mule's track written across it, going up.

I went to bed and dreamed, for the first time in years, of Marrow Bay.
