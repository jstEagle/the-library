## 11. What the Papers Say

The shepherd's hut sat in a fold of the hills like something the mountain had coughed up and then regretted — dry-stone walls, a roof of turf gone to moss, and a door that had not been closed properly since before I was born. It smelled of old smoke and older sheep. There was a hearth, half a cord of peat, a stool with three legs where four were advertised, and a view of nothing but gray sky pressing down on gray slopes.

It was, in other words, perfect. Nobody builds a hut like that because they want company.

I'd been walking two days since leaving Maren's village, lying about my destination with the ease of long practice. *North, to my sister's.* I don't have a sister. I have a crew in a naval jail, a coat full of stolen empire, and a face that three separate people on the road had looked at a beat too long. The mule was tethered outside under an overhang, eating what passed for grass at this altitude and judging me for it. Mules are honest that way. They never pretend to like you.

I got a fire going, boiled snowmelt in the dented tin pot from my pack, ate hard bread and harder cheese, and then — because there was nothing left between me and the thing I'd been carrying for nine days but firelight and silence — I took out the papers.

I had touched them every day since I went over the side of the *Kestrel's* sister ship with Halloway's marines shouting behind me. Touched them through the coat lining while I floated in black water. Touched them while I lay in Maren's bed with a fever, checking, the way you check a wound to see if it's still yours. But I hadn't read them. Not past the first page, that first night in her kitchen, when I'd seen enough to know what I was carrying and had put the rest away like a man refusing to look down.

You can't climb a mountain looking down. But huts are for looking down. That's what huts are for.

* * *

There were forty-one documents. I counted twice, because counting is a thing hands do when the rest of you wants to shake.

The first was a manifest, dated the seventeenth of March, eleven years past. HMS *Corvane*, outward bound from Marrow Bay. I expected cargo: guns, brandy, uncut stone, the usual quiet profits navies ship under flags of convenience. Instead the manifest listed, in a clerk's careful hand:

*Passengers, special conveyance, per Admiralty order: fourteen souls.*

Fourteen souls. Not sailors. Not marines. Passengers. The kind of phrase a bureaucracy reaches for when it needs to move people the way it moves crates, without anyone asking whether the crates breathe.

The second document was the order itself. Signed. I read the signature three times, and each time my jaw tightened a little more, until by the third pass I was surprised my teeth hadn't cracked.

*By order of Commodore E. R. Halloway, commanding, Southern Station.*

Halloway. Of course it was Halloway. The universe is lazy; it recasts the same actor in every villainy. The man who held Cyrus's sister's debts like a leash, who sent marines to board us at anchor, who wanted this crate recovered and burned so badly he was willing to drown a crew over it — his name was already wet ink on the oldest page. He hadn't stumbled into this. He'd built it. Eleven years ago, when he was younger and bolder and hadn't yet learned to let lieutenants sign the ugly things, he'd signed it himself.

The next pages told me why.

They were depositions. Sworn statements, taken by a naval inquiry that officially — I checked, later, against the survey charts I knew by heart — officially never happened. Witnesses to Marrow Bay. Fishermen. A harbor pilot. Two soldiers who'd been there and couldn't stomach it. A woman whose name was written as *Mara Quill, widow,* and whose statement ran longer than all the others combined.

I read Mara Quill's statement by firelight, and I will tell you plainly: I have held drowned men in my arms, and I have watched a mentor go down into green dark, and neither of those prepared me for a widow describing, in the flat voice of someone dictated to by a clerk, how soldiers came to Marrow Bay on a market day and made an example of it. How many. How young some of them were. How the harbor burned and the navy stood offshore and watched, and how afterward the reports said *tragic accident, munitions fire, regrettable loss of life among persons illegally sheltering smugglers.*

Regrettable loss of life. Fourteen witnesses, loaded onto a frigate to be carried somewhere they could never testify from, and the sea did the rest. The *Corvane* didn't sink. She was sunk. And the charts that showed her position were quietly wrong by three miles, and the salvage rights were quietly buried, and everyone who might have asked questions was promoted or transferred or dead.

Except one.

* * *

Because the fifteenth document in the stack was not a deposition, or an order, or a clerk's manifest.

It was a letter. Folded small, tucked inside the inquiry records like a knife slipped into a belt, addressed in handwriting I would have known anywhere — handwriting I had seen on tide tables and dive plans and once, folded into my pocket on my nineteenth birthday, on a note that said *you dive like you argue, too deep and too stubborn.*

*T. Merrick.*

Torvald Merrick. My mentor. The man who taught me to read water, who taught me that the sea keeps every promise except the ones you want kept, who went down at Kestrel Shoals eight years ago with a line around his ankle and Cyrus surfacing alone above him with the salvage log clutched to his chest.

He had been investigating Marrow Bay.

I read the letter standing up, because sitting felt like agreeing to something.

*M—* (the initial was cut off, or torn; I have wondered since who M was, and I suspect now it was me, and I suspect he tore it off deliberately, because Torvald never wrote anything down he wouldn't mind a magistrate reading) —

*I've found the Corvane's true position. The charts are false by three miles, which no chart-waster makes by accident. I've also found who signed the passenger manifest, and I'll say it plain because if this letter survives me, someone should say it plain: Commodore Halloway. The same Halloway who has lately taken an interest in our little operation, which interest I do not find flattering.*

*The inquiry records exist. I have copies. Fourteen witnesses, murdered by paperwork, and the paperwork survived them. There's a justice in that, of a sour kind.*

*I mean to bring it to the Admiralty board myself. If I don't come back from the Kestrel job, look in the strongbox.*

If I don't come back from the Kestrel job.

I set the letter down on my knee. The fire popped. Outside, the mule stamped once, indifferent to the rearrangement of my entire understanding of the last eight years.

Look in the strongbox.

The strongbox. The one we dove for at Kestrel Shoals. The job that killed him — the job Cyrus and I swore off forever, the coordinates I recognized the moment Cyrus slid that contract across the tavern table like he was offering me poison in a pretty glass. We thought we were diving for salvage. For whatever prize Torvald had lined up to fund his retirement. That's what Cyrus was told. That's what *I* was told, eight years ago, standing on a deck watching the water close over the place where my teacher had been.

He wasn't diving for prize money. He was diving for evidence. He'd hidden the inquiry copies in the strongbox and stashed it at Kestrel Shoals, and he'd arranged the whole job as cover — and either he meant to retrieve it himself and take it north, or he meant for us to find it if he didn't. Either way, the box we opened — the box Cyrus surfaced with, alone, while Torvald drowned below — contained the proof that could hang a commodore.

And then Cyrus sold it. Or hid it. Or gave it back. Eight years of silence, and the box vanished, and I hated him for cowardice and greed, and both those hatreds were aimed at the wrong target, because—

Because Halloway had taken an interest in their operation. Torvald wrote it himself. Which meant Halloway knew about the Kestrel job. Which meant the "accident" — the fouled line, the shifting current, the dive plan that went wrong in ways Torvald Merrick did not make mistakes — which meant I had spent eight years grieving an accident that may not have been one.

I am not a woman who breaks things when she's angry. I break things when I'm angry at objects. With people, I go very still and very clear, and I make lists.

So I sat down on the three-legged stool, and I fed the fire, and I made lists.

* * *

List the first: what the papers prove. Fourteen witnesses, conveyed under Halloway's own signature. An inquiry that officially never occurred, recorded in his own hand. A massacre described by six sworn voices, including a soldier who named his sergeant and his orders. A chart falsified. A wreck's salvage record buried. Any one of these, before the right magistrate, is a thread. Together they're a noose.

List the second: what the papers need. To matter, they can't die in a shepherd's hut with me. They need eyes that outrank intimidation — a magistrate, a newspaper, a public room full of people whose deaths would be inconvenient. Maren had mentioned names, back in the village, in the low voice coastal people use for dangerous hope: a reformist magistrate in the capital, an editor who'd been gutting the Admiralty in print for years and surviving on spite. Names were a start. Names plus documents plus a living witness — and somewhere out there, maybe, Mara Quill's kin, the soldier who testified — that was a case.

List the third, and I wrote this one slowest: who else knows. Halloway knew the crate existed, or he'd never have coerced Cyrus to raise it. Which meant Halloway had known about the strongbox for eight years and had only now moved to recover it. Why now? Because Cyrus had finally run out of ways to stall. Because someone in the capital had started asking about Marrow Bay. Because paper, unlike people, doesn't rot on a schedule — though salt water argues otherwise, and eight years at thirty fathoms should have ruined ink.

Unless the box had never stayed at thirty fathoms. Unless Cyrus had raised it that day, all those years ago, and kept it, and the job he'd offered me was theater — a way to hand it over to Halloway without admitting he'd had it all along.

I turned that over for a long time. It fit. It fit the way a bad knot fits, snug and wrong. Cyrus Amberdale, surfacing alone with the salvage log. Surfacing alone, always. And yet — and this is the flaw in me, the flaw Torvald used to thump my shoulder about — some part of me kept insisting that Cyrus hiding evidence for eight years to protect a commodore made no sense, while Cyrus hiding evidence for eight years because he was too afraid to move it and too ashamed to destroy it made terrible, human sense. Men drown in shallower water than that.

Either way, the conclusion was the same, and it settled over me not like grief, which is heavy and wet, but like ballast, which is heavy and useful.

This was no longer about clearing my crew, though I would clear them or die trying. This was no longer even about Halloway, though I intended to feed him his own signature until he choked. Somewhere at the bottom of Kestrel Shoals, or in the bottom of this satchel, Torvald Merrick had left me work. He'd been dead eight years, and he'd handed me a job across the grave, and I had never once in my life left a dive unfinished.

*Fury* is a word for people who shout. What I felt was quieter and more durable. Call it heading. Call it bearing. Call it the thing a diver feels when she finds the wreck she's been searching for and stops hunting and starts working — methodical, breath by breath, tie-off by tie-off, because the job is real now and real jobs get done properly or not at all.

I fed the last of the peat to the fire and read everything again, start to finish, until I could have recited Mara Quill's statement in my sleep. Then I sewed the papers back into my coat lining with waxed thread from my kit, double-stitched, the way Maren had shown me, and I lay down on the straw pallet with my boots on.

Sleep came slow. When it came, I dreamed of green water and a lantern swinging, and Torvald's voice saying, amused, *took you long enough, girl.*

* * *

I woke to the mule screaming.

Not stamping — screaming, that saw-edged bray mules save for genuine grievance, and I was up with the knife out of my boot before my eyes finished opening. Gray pre-dawn light through the doorless doorway. Frost on the stones. And down the slope, where the track bent around the shoulder of the hill, movement: riders. Three of them, maybe four, dark against the paler dark, moving at the unhurried pace of men following a trail rather than chasing one.

They weren't on me yet. But they were on the road I'd come by, and they were reading the ground the way trackers read it, and there was exactly one direction from this hut that wasn't back the way I'd come.

Up. Into the Grayfells, ahead of the snows, with whatever daylight the sky decided to grant.

I saddled the mule in ninety seconds flat, apologizing to her with my hands because she deserved better than my hurry, and led her out the back of the hut onto the scree. At the ridgeline I stopped once and looked down. One of the riders had dismounted at the hut door. Even at this distance I could see him crouch, and touch the cold ashes of my fire, and turn his head slowly toward the high country.

Toward me.

So much for resting. So much for lists. The mountain had heard my plans and, in its usual manner, had decided to charge admission.

I clicked my tongue, and the mule — honest creature, honest judgment in her long ears — put her head down and climbed.
