## 12. Cyrus Breaks

There is a particular kind of silence a man makes when he is lying to himself. It is not quiet. It is the noise of a hundred small justifications, all talking at once, none of them listening to each other.

I had been making that noise for eleven days.

The naval encampment at Coldharbor sat in a bowl of gray hills like a fistful of dropped coins — tents, cookfires, picket lines, and Lieutenant Corven's tracker squad drilling in the mud with the joyless precision of men who had been told this was important and did not believe it. I had a tent of my own. A cot. A ration chit. A rank of sorts — "civilian consultant," which is what the navy calls a man it intends to use and deny ever knowing.

I lay on that cot at night and listened to my justifications argue.

*She escaped,* they said. *Astrid Vane has slipped out of tighter knots than a marine cordon. She'll make the pass or she won't, but either way she made her own choice when she went over the rail.*

*You identified her crew,* said another voice, quieter and much older. *You stood on that deck with a lantern in your hand and you pointed at Bram Hale and told the marines his name.*

*I was forced,* the first voice replied, smooth as tar. *Halloway has Sera's debts. Halloway has worse than debts. What would you have done?*

And then the old voice would say nothing at all, which was the worst thing it could say, because the old voice sounded exactly like Astrid, and Astrid had never once in her life accepted an excuse from me without first making me stand in it until it rotted.

I got up. I dressed. I walked out into the cold because lying still in a warm cot felt too much like drowning, and I have some experience with drowning, and I can tell you the worst part isn't the water. It's how calm you get before it takes you.

* * *

Corven found me at the mess fire two mornings later, which surprised me, because up to that point the lieutenant had treated me the way a careful man treats something he stepped in.

He was younger than I'd expected from his reputation. Hard around the eyes, soft around the mouth, the look of a man who'd joined the service for reasons the service had spent years sanding off him. He sat down across the fire without asking, poured himself tea from my kettle without apologizing, and studied me for a long moment.

"You were her partner," he said. "Vane."

"Once."

"And Amberdale. The salvage man." He turned his cup slowly. "Halloway says you know the coast better than any chart. Says you can read where a woman will run the way other men read weather."

"Halloway flatters me."

"Halloway doesn't flatter anyone." Corven drank. "So let's not pretend this is conversation. You want to know if your friend is going to die in the mountains, and I want to know why a man who sold her out keeps looking at the commissary tent like it owes him money."

I should have lied. I was good at lying; I'd had eight years of practice, most of them aimed at myself. But there was something in the way he said *your friend* — no contempt in it, just fact — that loosened something in my chest I'd been keeping cinched since the boarding at anchor.

"I didn't sell her out," I said, and heard immediately how it sounded, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "No. That's not true. I did. I stood there and I named names because a commodore told me to, and I told myself I had no choice, and the truly remarkable thing—" I looked at the fire instead of him— "the truly remarkable thing is that I almost believed it by morning."

Corven didn't move. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked a question nobody else here asks." I finally looked at him. "You don't believe this errand either. I've watched you drill your trackers. Men hunting stolen property don't look like mourners, Lieutenant. Your men look like mourners."

For a moment I thought he'd arrest me for insolence, or worse, report me to Halloway, and honestly part of me wanted him to — wanted the decision taken out of my hands, wanted someone else to make me the coward I already was. Instead he set down his cup and said, very quietly:

"The orders changed yesterday. You haven't seen them?"

"No."

"They're not posted to consultants." He stood, brushing off his coat, and paused beside me long enough to say the rest low, under the crackle of the fire: "Recovery of property. That's what we signed for. Read your own conscience carefully, Mr. Amberdale. It may be the only honest document left in this camp."

Then he walked away, and I sat there with my heart going like a pump in a flooding hold.

*Read your own conscience carefully.* As if I had one left to read. As if it weren't waterlogged past recovery.

But I couldn't sleep after that. And a man who can't sleep starts walking, and a man who walks at night in a military camp eventually walks past places he shouldn't, and that is how, three nights later, I came to be crouched behind the commissary stores at the hour of the dog watch, close enough to the command tent to hear Commodore Halloway's voice through canvas thin as paper.

* * *

"—no witnesses, Captain Rell. I want that understood at every level below this one."

A second voice, drier: "Including the crew, sir?"

"The crew especially." Halloway's voice I knew — I'd heard it through Cyrus's own memory often enough these past months, smooth and patient as tide over a drowned man. Hearing it in the flesh was somehow worse. It was a reasonable voice. It was the voice of a man explaining arithmetic. "Vane is the priority while the documents are loose. Once the documents are recovered — or once we've confirmed their destruction — the survivors become a liability. Hale, the navigator, the boy who works the winch. All of them signed onto an illegal salvage. Their deaths will be attributed to Vane herself. Desperate criminal, cornered in the high country, takes her own crew down with her. Tragic. The newspapers will write it for us."

"And Amberdale?"

A pause. Long enough that I stopped breathing.

"Amberdale believes he's essential. Let him. He'll carry the map knowledge up the mountain himself and save my trackers the trouble. After the pass—" the sound Halloway made might have been a shrug, rendered in voice— "mountains are dangerous. Ask anyone."

Captain Rell said something I didn't catch. Halloway answered, and the words came out with the terrible gentleness of a father tucking in a child:

"You're squeamish, Captain. That's fine. Squeamish officers make excellent witnesses — they remember everything and suspect nothing. Dismissed."

I stayed crouched behind the stores until my knees screamed, until the sentry changed, until the camp settled into its night noises. Then I stood up, and I want to be honest about what happened inside me, because a man ought to at least witness his own trial even if he plans to acquit himself wrongly.

What happened was: nothing dramatic. No thunderclap of conversion. Just a very quiet arithmetic, done at last with honest numbers.

Sera's debts. Sera's safety. Eight years of telling myself that everything I'd done — Kestrel Shoals, the log book, the silence, and now this — all of it had been *for her*, for family, for the people who depended on me. And standing there in the dark with frost forming on my collar, I finally asked the question I'd been dodging since the day I surfaced alone off Kestrel Shoals with Merrick's salvage log zipped inside my coat:

If I betrayed everyone I loved to protect one person I loved, what exactly had I protected?

Because Halloway hadn't promised Sera safety. Halloway had promised Sera *leverage*. And leverage doesn't keep. Leverage spends down. The day I stopped being useful — the day after the pass, per the commodore's own arithmetic — Sera became just another name on another list, and there would be no one left who even knew to grieve her loudly enough to matter.

I had built my whole rotten cathedral on the idea that survival was a thing you could secure for the people you loved by feeding them other people. And the architect of that cathedral had just heard, through a canvas wall, the sound of his own name being added to the demolition schedule.

* * *

The route map lived in Corven's command wagon, in a locked dispatch case, under guard.

I want that recorded plainly, because the version of events that will circulate later — the version Astrid will hear, if she ever hears it — will no doubt make it sound cleaner. It wasn't clean. I am not a hero in this chapter of anything. Here is what actually happened:

I got drunk.

Deliberately, publicly, expensively drunk, on navy rum bought with my consultant's chit, and I made sure the sentries saw it. I sang two songs badly outside the officers' mess. I wept a little about a woman, which required no acting whatsoever. I let three separate men help me toward my tent and shook all of them off within sight of my own flap, so that the last image anyone retained was Cyrus Amberdale, maudlin and harmless, staggering home alone.

Then I lay down on my cot, waited out two hours of the coldest patience I have ever practiced, got up sober as judgment, and went to work.

The sentry on the wagon line was the young one, the one whose boots I'd noticed were cracked, the one who'd mentioned to his mate — men talk near me; I'm furniture, always have been — that his mother was ill in Coldharbor village and he couldn't afford the leave. I didn't bribe him. I want that clear too. I simply walked up to him, sober-eyed, and said, "Your mother. Is it the lungs?" and watched his face do everything a face can do, and then I said, "There's a woman in the village called Mrs. Tarnow who nurses lung-rot for what the army pays a corporal's batman. Tell her Amberdale sent you. Tell her the fee's covered."

And while he stood there trying to decide whether to salute me or hug me, I lifted the dispatch key off the hook inside the guard post, because I'd watched Corven hang it there four nights running, because I am, whatever else is true, very good at watching.

The lock opened like it had been waiting for permission.

Inside: dispatches, requisitions, and the map. A proper surveyor's map of the Grayfell Range, annotated in Corven's tight hand — the passes, the water sources, the shepherd huts marked and dated, and there, in red ink, a projected intercept line running up the western slope toward a place called the Hollow Stair, with a note: *Vane likely route. Blizzard probable. Move before snows close pass or lose her entirely.*

Lose *her*. Not the documents. Her.

I took the map. I took, after a moment's thought, two of the dispatches as well — one bearing Halloway's seal concerning "disposition of detained salvage personnel," which was the polite phrase for the order I'd heard through the canvas, and one thinner letter I hadn't gone looking for and wished afterward I hadn't found: a private note in Halloway's hand to a creditor house in the capital, referencing "the Amberdale obligation," and mentioning, in passing, that the sister's debts had been purchased specifically "as assurance of the brother's cooperation."

Purchased. My sister's ruin hadn't even been incidental. It had been *procured*. An investment. And investments, the commodore's arithmetic ran, get collected.

I put the key back on its hook. I walked out past the young sentry, who touched his cap to me with wet eyes, and God forgive me, I touched mine back.

* * *

The horse was the hard part, and then it wasn't, because it turned out the hard part was never the horse. The hard part was standing at the picket line in the blue hour before dawn with the map inside my coat against my chest, and understanding that the moment I swung up into that saddle, I was done. Finished. Every bridge behind me burned, and the one ahead led up a frozen mountain toward a woman who had every right to open my throat, and I was going anyway.

Eight years ago, off Kestrel Shoals, I had come to a moment like this one. Merrick was down in the dark with his leg caught in the wreck, and the air in my tanks was going, and there was a choice hanging in the water between us — go back down, or go up. I told myself for eight years that there had been no choice, that the air math was final, that any diver would have done what I did.

It was a lie. There was a choice. There is always a choice. That's the whole horror of being a man — you can always choose, and then you have to *be* what you chose, forever, and I had chosen to be the man who surfaces alone.

I put my foot in the stirrup.

"You'll freeze above the tree line," said a voice behind me, and I nearly died of fright on the spot, which would at least have saved everyone trouble.

Corven stood between the tents with his hands in his pockets, no sidearm visible, no escort, breath steaming. How long he'd been there I couldn't say. Long enough, from the look of him. Long enough to have seen the key returned and counted the seconds it was missing.

"Lieutenant," I managed.

"I did wonder," he said, "why a man steals a look at the disposition orders and then gets spectacularly drunk instead of running. Drunk men run *immediately*. Sober men plan." He tilted his head. "You planned."

"Are you going to raise the alarm?"

He considered this with what looked like genuine professional interest. "Raise the alarm, and I report that a civilian consultant stole a map I was responsible for securing, which ends my career and changes nothing — Halloway sends riders, they run you down, and Vane dies in a blizzard with the truth sewn in her coat. Don't raise the alarm, and maybe—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes that I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors for eight years— "maybe one man in this whole rotten operation does one thing that isn't filth."

"Why?" I said. "Why would you let me?"

"Because I read the Marrow Bay dispatches last winter, Mr. Amberdale. All of them. Before they were sealed." His jaw worked. "I know what your commodore buried. So ride. Ride fast, ride high, and if you see my trackers on the mountain—" he turned away, then stopped, and finished over his shoulder— "look for the ones dragging their feet. I've been training them to march badly for a week. They think I don't notice. I notice everything. It's my curse."

He walked back into the dark between the tents, and I stood there a moment longer, holding a stolen map against my heart like the world's coldest love letter.

Then I swung up into the saddle, and I did not look back at the camp, because I have learned that much about myself at least: I am a man who cannot afford to look back, not because of what's chasing me, but because of what isn't. Because of everyone I left standing on decks and docks and doorsteps, watching me go, learning the shape of my leaving.

Not this time. This time I was riding *toward*. Toward the Grayfells. Toward the Hollow Stair. Toward a woman with a knife and a grievance and eight years of receipts.

Redemption, I thought, as the horse picked its way up the frozen track and the camp shrank behind me to a scatter of dying fires. Everyone talks about redemption like it's a destination. Like you arrive somewhere and a bell rings and the ledger clears.

But sitting there in the freezing dark with Halloway's orders against my chest and Astrid's name in my mouth like a prayer I had no right to, I understood it differently. Redemption wasn't a place. It was a direction. And I had spent eight years walking the wrong one, and the correcting of it was going to cost exactly what it had cost to walk wrong — everything, paid out slow, in cold, in fear, in the long climb —

The horse snorted, ears forward, and I followed her gaze up the trail.

Far above, at the ragged edge of the tree line where the pines gave out and the scree began, a thread of smoke rose thin and gray against the dawn. One fire. Small. Careful. The fire of someone who knew how to build a fire that couldn't be seen from a valley — and had built it anyway, because even the careful freeze, eventually, if the cold is honest enough.

Someone was up there. Someone was ahead of me on the mountain, and the snows were coming down behind us both, and the only way through was through each other.

I clicked my tongue, and rode up into the light.
