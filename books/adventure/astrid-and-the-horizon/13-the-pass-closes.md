## 13. The Pass Closes

The mountain told me it was going to kill me three days before it tried.

Mountains are honest that way, if you know how to listen. The wind changed its accent on the second morning — went from a low murmur to something with teeth in it. The birds left in a hurry, all at once, like a tavern emptying when the press gang walks in. And the light went flat and white and strange, the way it does when the sky is deciding whether to be weather or a verdict.

I'd been on the Grayfell road four days by then. Four days of switchbacks and scree and the mule — I'd named him Bishop, for no reason except that he had a stubborn, self-important face — plodding along with my satchel strapped under his pack blankets like a second heart.

Because that's what it was. Twenty-two sheets of paper proving that the HMS Corvane hadn't been carrying contraband when she went down. She'd been carrying witnesses. Sailors and civilians who'd seen what happened at Marrow Bay, hauled aboard under orders signed by Commodore Halloway himself, and sent to the bottom of the Kestrel Shoals so their testimony would drown with them. I'd read them twice more on the trail, by firelight, until I could recite the names in my sleep. Which, as it turned out, I did.

The plan was simple, the way all plans are simple right up until they aren't. Cross the Grayfell Pass before the snows. Reach the capital. Put the papers in front of someone with enough spine and enough enemies to make them matter. Maren had said six days' climb in good weather.

The weather stopped being good on the morning of the fifth day.

* * *

It started as a smudge on the western peaks — a gray bruise where there should have been blue. By noon it had eaten half the sky. By two o'clock the wind came down off the ridge like something chased, and the temperature dropped so fast my breath froze on the wool of my collar before it finished leaving me.

Bishop knew before I did. He stopped dead in the middle of the trail, ears flat, and refused to go another step forward. I hauled the lead rope. He planted his hooves and gave me a look of profound mule-ish contempt, the look of a creature saying: *you may be able to read, madam, but I can smell.*

"You're being ridiculous," I told him.

He was not being ridiculous. An hour later the blizzard hit us like a broadside.

I've been in storms at sea. You learn things in them — how to shorten sail blind, how to lash yourself to something true while everything else lies. But a mountain storm is worse, because the sea at least has the decency to stay horizontal. This came from every direction at once, snow moving sideways, then upward, then in spirals that made no sense at all, the world reduced to a whiteness so total I couldn't see my own boots. The temperature fell through cold into something past cold, a kind of burning. My fingers stopped reporting in. The trail vanished. The cairns that marked it — I'd counted eleven since the tree line — were swallowed whole.

I heard Bishop scream once, a horrible sound, and felt the lead rope tear out of my frozen hands, and then he was gone. Just gone, a shadow swallowed by white, taking the pack blankets and the spare food and most of my sanity with him.

"Bishop!" I shouted, and the wind took the name and shredded it.

I crawled. There's no dignity in the telling and there was none in the doing. I got down on hands and knees because standing upright in that wind was a way of volunteering to die, and I followed the slope downward on instinct, feeling for the fall of the land through my palms. Somewhere behind me, or maybe beside me, maybe everywhere, the satchel strap caught on rock and wrenched at my shoulder, and I had a moment — one clear, crystalline moment — where I understood that I could let go. That letting go would be easy. That the storm would take the papers and bury them and no one would ever know, and I could stop. Stop running, stop carrying, stop being the last person alive who knew what Marrow Bay meant.

My mentor would have let go. No — that's a lie, and an insult. He died holding on. That was rather the point of him.

I didn't let go. I wish I could tell you it was courage. Mostly it was spite. Halloway wanted those papers at the bottom of a shoal; I'd be damned if I delivered them to the bottom of a snowfield instead. Spite has carried me further than faith ever did.

And then the ground opened under my hands and there was stone overhead instead of sky, and I was in a cave, on my belly in the dark, breathing air that didn't try to kill me, and I lay there shaking and laughing at nothing until the shaking won.

* * *

"You're bleeding on my floor."

The voice came out of the cave's depths, low and dry, and I confess I screamed — a short, undignified sound that the storm outside mercifully covered. My hand found the knife at my belt before my eyes found anything at all.

Then a match flared, and I saw him: an old man sitting against the far wall with his back to the stone, wrapped in a threadbare blanket, a small oil lamp guttering beside him. He was gaunt, white-bearded, with the kind of face that had once been handsome and had since been used hard. His eyes were pale and steady and very tired.

"Are you armed?" I managed.

"No," he said. "God saw to that years ago." He nodded at my shoulder. "You're bleeding."

I touched my shoulder and my hand came away red. The satchel strap had torn the skin when the rock grabbed it. I hadn't noticed. Cold is generous that way — it takes your pain along with everything else, and gives you the bill later.

"It's shallow," I said.

"Most wounds are," he said. "At the start."

He moved slowly, joints cracking, and dug a strip of clean linen from his pack without being asked. I let him bind it, mostly because my hands had stopped obeying orders, and partly because there was something in his manner — unhurried, unafraid, faintly amused — that made alarm feel gauche.

"Astrid Vane," I said finally, because silence between strangers in a hole in a mountain is its own kind of madness.

"Father Anselm Crane," he said. Then, with a dry twist of the mouth: "No. Not father. Not anymore. Just Crane. The Church and I have come to a mutual understanding that we prefer each other at a distance."

"Defrocked?"

"Defrocked, unfrocked, stripped, dismissed, and formally anathematized, which I'm told is quite old-fashioned these days." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't. "You're the woman from the coast. Maren's guest. The fisherman's daughter carried word up the valley about you — the stranger who wouldn't say where she was going."

"Maren talks too much."

"Maren grieves too much," Crane corrected gently. "Talking is how she does it. She lost her sons to a press gang eight years ago. They were taken off a merchantman out of Marrow Bay." He watched me very carefully as he said it. Watching to see what I'd do with the words.

I kept my face still. It cost me.

"The storm will run two days," he went on, mercifully changing course. "Maybe three. We're above the tree line and below the summit shoulder, which means we're in the throat of it. Nobody crosses in this. Nobody rescues anybody in this. We wait, we burn what wood I've cached, and we pray the roof holds."

"Do you pray?" I asked. "Still? After everything?"

"I argue with Him," Crane said. "It's a form of prayer. The only kind I trust."

* * *

We burned his cached wood sparingly and talked less sparingly, because talk was the only warmth that didn't run out.

He asked nothing about my business, which told me he already suspected it. Instead he talked about the mountains, about the pass, about the shepherd whose hut I'd slept in two nights back ("Old Tomas — did he feed you the cheese? Don't answer. If you say yes I'll know you lied politely"). He talked the way lonely men talk when they've been alone long enough to forget the habit of listening to themselves.

And somewhere past midnight, with the storm screaming at the cave mouth like a creditor, he said:

"You carry a satchel like a priest carries a reliquary. One hand on it even in sleep. I noticed when you came in, bleeding and half-frozen. You checked it before you checked your own pulse."

I looked at him across the small fire.

"You were at Marrow Bay," I said.

His face did something complicated — grief and relief fighting over the same ground.

"Yes," he said.

"Say what you know."

"Why?"

"Because I have papers in this satchel with names on them," I said, "and I have spent five days wondering if I'm carrying proof or just paper. Because everyone who could confirm them is dead or drowned or paid to lie. Because tomorrow I might die on this mountain and the truth dies with me, and I want—" My voice cracked, damn it, cracked right down the middle. "I want to not be the only one who knows. Just for one night. I am so tired of being the only one who knows."

Crane was quiet a long time. The fire popped. Outside, the wind found some new register of fury.

"There were two hundred and forty-one people in Marrow Bay when the navy came," he said at last, in the flat voice men use for things they've rehearsed against their will. "I baptized forty of the children myself. I buried nineteen of them over the years — good deaths, honest deaths, the kind God signs for. And then one night in the autumn of the year, soldiers came with orders nobody would show me, and by morning there were sixty-one graves I dug with my own hands, and the official record says a fever. A fever." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard in weeks. "I stood in the pulpit the following Sunday and I called it murder, from the altar, with the garrison commander sitting in the third pew. Within the month I was defrocked for 'incitement and falsehood.' The falsehood being, apparently, arithmetic."

"The Corvane," I said. "Did you know about the Corvane?"

"I knew some of the witnesses were taken north. I wrote letters for two years asking after them. I received no answers." He turned those pale eyes on me. "What do your papers say?"

I opened the satchel. My hands were steadier now — anger does that, when it arrives on schedule. I laid the documents out on the flat rock between us, page by page, and watched an old man read his own congregation's death warrant by lamplight.

He read for a long time. He touched none of the pages, as if they were relics, which in fairness they were.

"These names," he whispered. "Sela Voss. The Odry brothers. Captain Merrin's wife — she stayed behind to testify, she *insisted*, and I told her—"

"She's listed here," I said quietly. "'Embarked under escort, orders of Commodore R. Halloway, destination undisclosed.'"

Crane closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, and he made no apology for it, and I respected him enormously for that.

"Every name," he said. "Every date. I can confirm every line of this. I was there. I dug the graves. I counted." He looked up at me. "Where did you get this?"

"From the Corvane herself. From the strongbox the survey charts pretend isn't there."

"And where are you taking it?"

"The capital. To anyone who'll listen."

"They'll call it forgery."

"I know."

"They'll call you a thief, a lunatic, a foreign agent. They'll produce three clerks with sworn affidavits and a doctor to testify to your hysteria."

"I know that too."

"They killed two hundred and forty-one people, Miss Vane, and they will spend a great deal of money to avoid admitting it, and money in the capital is a kind of gravity. Everything falls toward it."

"Then I'll need to be heavier than money," I said. "Or find someone who is. Either way, the papers cross this pass. That part isn't negotiable."

Crane studied me for a long moment, the way you'd study a knot to see whether it would hold weight.

"You understand," he said slowly, "that if you reach the capital, and if you speak, they will need to discredit every witness. Starting with whoever confirms the documents." He tapped his own chest, once. "A defrocked priest with a grudge is exactly the witness they'll want. Unreliable. Bitter. Dismissed by his own Church. I am, professionally speaking, a gift to them."

"So don't come."

"I didn't say I wasn't coming." Something had changed in his face — the tiredness burning off like fog. "I've spent eight years being the man who shouted at a garrison and lost. Eight years digging graves and writing letters into silence. I am old, Miss Vane, and slow, and my knees are a scandal. But I counted those graves. And I find that I would very much like to count them aloud, in a room full of admirals, before I die."

The wind hurled itself against the cave mouth. The lamp flame bent and straightened.

"You realize," I said, "that the storm's buried the trail. We may be stuck here days. And there are men behind us — Halloway's men — and they don't stop for weather."

"Then we'd best hope their convictions are shallower than our frostbite," Crane said, and pulled the blanket around his shoulders, and settled against the stone with the serenity of a man who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to him. "Sleep, Miss Vane. I'll watch. Old men sleep badly anyway — consider it efficient use of a defective resource."

I lay down with the satchel strap looped around my wrist, as I had every night since the anchorage, and stared at the fire, and listened to the storm trying to get in.

For the first time since I'd swum away from the Corvane's shadow with twenty-two sheets of paper sewn against my ribs, I was not the only person alive who knew. The thought should have comforted me. It did, a little. But it also meant there were now two of us to hunt, and one of us was seventy years old with bad knees, and I had just acquired something I'd spent a decade avoiding at all costs.

Someone to lose.

Somewhere far below us, muffled by a hundred fathoms of snow, I thought I heard a sound on the trail — voices, or wind wearing voices' clothes. Crane's eyes met mine across the fire, and neither of us said anything, and the storm went on shouting.
