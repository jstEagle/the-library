## 14. Corven's Choice

Lieutenant Aldous Corven had been a soldier for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned exactly three things worth teaching.

First: never trust a clear morning after a storm. The storm leaves gifts, and the gifts are always evidence.

Second: the navy promotes the men who ask the fewest questions, which means the higher you climb, the more company you keep among people who have made ignorance a career.

Third — and this was the one he'd learned last, and at cost — a man can obey every order he is ever given and still end up on the wrong side of a thing.

He stood at the tree line below Grayfell's shoulder, watching his breath hang in the air like a small ghost that had given up on haunting anything important, and thought about all three lessons at once.

The tracker, Voss, crouched over a patch of scuffed snow near a dead campfire. "She sheltered here. Two of them now." He held up a scrap of grey wool. "Priest's habit, by the weave. There's a defrocked one lives up this way somewhere. The villagers wouldn't say."

"The villagers wouldn't say," Corven repeated. "How surprising. A fishing village protecting a woman from six armed men with a warrant. Whatever could have inspired such loyalty."

Voss grinned. He was a good tracker and a decent man, which in Corven's experience were qualities that rarely survived promotion. "Orders are orders, sir."

"Orders are orders," Corven agreed, and did not tell him what the orders actually said when you read them twice.

He had read them twice. That was the problem. Commodore Halloway's dispatch described the fugitive as a thief in possession of stolen naval property, to be recovered intact, and the thief herself to be brought back alive — *alive* underlined twice, which in Halloway's hand was not mercy but bookkeeping. Dead women couldn't be questioned later, but neither could they be quietly disappeared once the paperwork was tidied. Corven had spent enough years around the commodore to know that when a man underlined something twice, it meant the opposite of what it appeared to mean.

And then there was the other document, the one that had no business existing, the one Sergeant Hale had passed him on the second night out with a look on his face like a man handing off something hot. A copy of a supply requisition from Marrow Bay, eleven years old. Grain, medical stores, and — the line that had kept Corven awake since — *prisoner transport, forty souls, return manifest unfiled.*

Forty souls. No return manifest.

Corven had spent two nights telling himself it was nothing. Clerical error. Fog of war. The sort of loose thread that hung off every campaign like a pulled stitch. And then he had spent the third night remembering that he'd been a junior lieutenant at Marrow Bay himself, standing on a quay while smoke went up from a building the official reports called a granary, and that he had asked his captain what was burning, and his captain had looked at him with something close to pity and said, *Nothing that concerns you, Mr. Corven.*

Nineteen years, and the sentence had finally finished arriving.

* * *

They climbed through the morning. The storm had broken in the night, leaving behind a world so white and silent it felt less like weather than like erasure. The mountain had swallowed the trail; Voss found it anyway, because Voss could read snow the way scholars read dead languages, and by midday they stood before a cave mouth half-hidden behind a curtain of ice, smoke-stained rock above it, and Corven felt his stomach drop through the mountain into some colder country beneath.

Because there was a horse tethered outside.

His own horse. Or rather — he knew the brand, the white sock on the near hind leg — the horse that had gone missing from the corral at Coldharbour ferry two nights ago, along with a stable boy who swore a woman with a knife had persuaded him that silence was the healthier profession. Astrid Vane had stolen his horse. Which meant she was close, and rested, and mounted if she chose to be, and none of those facts improved his day.

"Two sets of tracks going in," Voss murmured. "None coming out."

Corven drew his pistol, checked it, and hated himself a little for how smoothly his hands did it. Nineteen years of muscle memory, all of it pointed at a cave where a diver and an old priest were warming themselves over a fire, and somewhere behind him, a hundred miles down the mountain, a commodore sat in a warm office waiting for his property back.

"Hale, left flank. Voss with me. The rest hold here." He paused. "Nobody fires unless someone fires first. Am I understood?"

Hale's eyes met his, just for a moment, and there was something in them — a question, or an answer to a question Corven hadn't asked aloud. "Understood, sir."

* * *

The cave opened inward like a throat. Firelight reached them before they reached the fire, orange light shivering on wet stone, and then the light reached *them*, and a voice out of the dark said:

"That's far enough, Lieutenant."

Not the woman. An old man's voice, cracked but steady, the voice of someone who had spent decades talking over storms and congregations and had lost all fear of interruption. Corven came around the bend with his pistol low and found the scene he would remember for the rest of his life, though he didn't know it yet.

A fire. A mule blanket. A woman sitting against the far wall with a knife resting open on her knee — not raised, not hidden, just *present*, the way honest things are present. She was younger than the wanted sketch suggested, or the sketch had flattered her, hard-eyed and sea-browned, with the particular stillness of someone who had already decided what she would do and was waiting politely for everyone else to catch up.

And beside the fire, hands folded in his lap, sat an old man in a grey coat that had once been a cassock and had been practical about it.

"You're the priest," Corven said. "Crane. Defrocked, I'm told."

"I prefer *retired*," the old man said. "God retired me. The Church simply handled the paperwork." His eyes went to the pistol, then to Corven's face, and stayed there. "You have questions, Lieutenant. I can see them queuing up behind your eyes like passengers at a dock. Ask the first one."

Corven should have ordered the woman to her feet. Should have announced the warrant, taken possession of the satchel, bound her wrists, begun the long ugly business of getting a prisoner down a mountain. Every regulation he had sworn to was lined up behind his teeth waiting for the word.

Instead he heard himself say: "Marrow Bay. What happened there?"

The silence lasted four heartbeats. He counted them, because counting was what he did instead of praying.

"You've seen the papers," Crane said gently.

"I've seen a requisition."

"Then you know about the forty souls." The old man leaned toward the fire and fed it a stick, unhurried, a man tending something small and alive. "I was chaplain at the garrison chapel. Forty prisoners came off a navy transport in the autumn of the year — dissidents, they were called. Fishermen, mostly. A schoolteacher. Two midwives who'd treated the wrong wounded men." He said it the way a man reads a ledger, flat and precise, and somehow that was worse than weeping. "The order came down that they were to be transferred inland. Instead they were put in the east granary, and the granary was burned, and the report filed said grain stores lost to accident. Forty souls, Lieutenant. I wrote their names afterward, from the parish rolls, all forty, and I carried those names for eleven years like stones in my boots, and when the Church asked me to stop asking questions, I retired."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow slid off a ledge with a sound like a long sigh.

"They were witnesses," the woman said. Her voice was rougher than the priest's, wind-cured. "That's what the papers prove. The Corvane wasn't carrying contraband. It was carrying the last people alive who saw what Halloway did, being moved somewhere they'd never be found. The frigate sank, and the navy called it providence." She tilted her head at him. "You're the one hunting me. Why are you asking questions you already know the answers to?"

"Because knowing and *knowing* are different countries," Corven said, "and I only just crossed the border."

* * *

It was Hale who broke the stalemate, though not in the way any manual would advise.

The sergeant had come around the flank as ordered and now stood at the cave mouth, and Corven watched him look — at the woman, at the priest, at the fire — and watched something happen in the young man's face that Corven recognized because it had happened to his own face three nights ago in lamplight. The moment when a soldier discovers that the uniform fits differently depending on what's written on the orders inside it.

"Sergeant," Corven said carefully, "your orders."

"My orders," Hale said slowly, "are to arrest a thief." He looked at the priest. "Sir. Those names you carry. My grandmother was born at Marrow Bay. Her family —" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Her family stopped writing. After that autumn."

Nobody moved. The fire crackled. And then Voss appeared behind Hale, snow on his shoulders, and looked at the scene, and looked at Corven, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of testimony.

"We're six men," Corven said, mostly to himself. "There's a blizzard coming back tonight, the pass closes behind us tomorrow regardless, and the commodore wants his property." He turned to the woman. "Give me the satchel and come quietly, and maybe —"

"No," she said.

It wasn't defiant. That was the terrible thing. It was simply factual, the way she'd probably answer a question about tide tables. *No.* He believed her instantly and completely, and some part of him — the part that had spent nineteen years doing arithmetic with other people's lives — respected it more than any oath he'd ever administered.

"Ma'am," Voss said, and even the tracker's voice had changed, "those papers. You've read them?"

"Every sheet. Twice."

"And they name Halloway?"

"They don't just name him. They're signed by him."

The wind picked up outside the cave, moaning across the ice like something grieving. Corven holstered his pistol. It felt less like a decision than like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, and then immediately picking up a heavier one.

* * *

Here is what Corven did next, and he would spend the rest of his life deciding whether it was the first honest act of his career or the first cowardly one dressed in honest clothes:

He dismissed his squad to the cave mouth. All but Hale, whom he kept for witness, because a thing done alone can be denied, and he did not want this deniable.

"Lieutenant," the woman said, watching him, knife still open on her knee. "Whatever you're about to do —"

"I'm about to commit treason," Corven said. "Or justice. The admiralty will decide which, and I'll be hanged or decorated accordingly, and either way I'd rather it happened warm." He unbuckled the satchel strap at his hip — his rations, his maps — and set them by the fire. Then he did the harder thing. He walked to the cave mouth, whistled up the white mare grazing on lichen-crusted scrub, and led her inside.

The woman stared at the horse. Then at him. It was the first expression he'd seen on her face that wasn't armor.

"She's yours," Corven said. "Best animal in the squad. She'll get you over the pass before the storm does, if you leave within the hour."

"Why?" It came out of her almost angrily, as if mercy were an insult she hadn't trained for.

"Because eleven years ago I stood on a quay at Marrow Bay and watched smoke go up, and I asked one question, and a captain told me it didn't concern me, and I *believed him*. For nineteen years I believed him." He heard his own voice go flat, ledger-flat, the priest's flat. "Justice outside the law has to begin somewhere, ma'am. Apparently it begins inside a man, and apparently it begins late, and apparently it begins in a freezing cave with a stolen horse. I won't insult either of us by calling it virtue. Call it arrears."

The priest made a sound that might have been a laugh or a prayer. With Crane it seemed to be the same activity.

"And your men?" the woman asked.

"Will remember a cave they never entered." He looked at Hale. "Sergeant?"

Hale straightened. "Never entered, sir. We searched the ridge. Found nothing but weather." A pause. "Though if anyone asks me privately, ever, whether I believe the woman — I'll say I believe her."

"That'll do." Corven dug into his coat and produced the last thing, the thing he'd been carrying since Coldharbour without quite admitting why: a folded paper. The pursuit schedule. The relay stations, the road checkpoints, the names of the hired men Halloway had engaged when the army proved insufficiently flexible. He held it out. "This is every trap between here and the capital. Memorize it and burn it. The bridge at Stonewatch will be watched — don't take the bridge."

She took it. Their fingers didn't touch; she was careful, and he was grateful for it.

"For what it's worth," she said, swinging the satchel up, "you're the first navy man who's ever given me something besides trouble."

"For what it's worth, ma'am, you're the first thief I've ever arrested who stole something that belonged to the dead." He stepped back. "Go. And if you make the capital — make it loud. Quiet truths are how we got here."

* * *

They watched her go from the cave mouth, the white mare picking sure-footed down the frozen switchbacks with the woman bent low over her neck, until the snow-haze took them both. The priest stood beside Corven, uninvited, unafraid.

"You'll lose your commission," Crane said. "Possibly more."

"Yes."

"And you did it anyway."

"It appears so." Corven watched the empty slope. "Eleven years ago I asked what was burning. Nobody answered. This morning I finally got my answer, and it turns out the answer was *everything*." He turned back into the cave to collect his men and his lie. "Come along, Father. You'll walk down with us. If the commodore asks, we found a hermit and a very convincing snowdrift."

Behind them, high on the white shoulder of Grayfell, a rider the six of them had officially never seen crested the ridge and vanished northward, toward the capital, toward the hearing, toward the loud truth —

— and toward the figure waiting on the high pass, half-buried in snow, who had been following her since the ferry: a man with a route map in his coat and a debt eight years overdue.
