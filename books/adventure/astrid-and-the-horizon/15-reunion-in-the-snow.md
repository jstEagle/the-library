## 15. Reunion in the Snow

I heard him before I saw him, which was the only reason he lived to see the dawn.

The Grayfell pass at night makes its own music — wind worrying the scree, ice ticking as it grows on the rocks, the occasional groan of a glacier shifting in its sleep like an old man who resents his mattress. Anyone moving through that has to move *around* the mountain's noise, and most people can't. Most people are the noise.

But I'd spent eight years listening for one particular set of footsteps, and there he was: half a mile back, maybe less, boots finding the rhythm of my own pace the way he always did. Cyrus Amberdale had a gift for matching other people's strides. It made him a good partner. It also made him a very good liar, because a man who can walk like you can talk like you, and a man who can talk like you can make you believe almost anything.

I climbed another quarter mile, found a shelf of granite where the wind had carved out a hollow deep enough to sit in, and waited. I took out my knife. I did not sharpen it, because it was already sharp, because I am not a sentimental person, but I held it the way you hold a thing you've been holding for eight years and only just realized it.

He came up the trail at a careful walk, hood back, breath smoking, and he was thinner than I remembered and grayer and he had a rope coiled over one shoulder like a man who'd brought a gift to a funeral. He didn't see me. That was the insult of it. Eight years, and he walked past the woman he'd ruined without so much as checking the shadows.

"Cyrus," I said.

He froze. The mountain went on making its noise around us, indifferent.

"Astrid." He turned slowly, hands well away from his belt, and I'll grant him this — he didn't look surprised. He looked like a man who'd been walking toward exactly this moment and had simply arrived early.

"Keep your hands where they are," I said, "and come here."

He came. He always came. That was the whole tragedy of Cyrus Amberdale in a single sentence, and I hated that I knew it.

When he was close enough, I put the knife against his throat. Not hard. Hard enough.

"Say your piece," I told him. "Quickly. The cold's bad and my patience is worse."

* * *

He had a route map. Of course he had a route map — Corven's pursuit plan, stolen, he said, from the lieutenant's own satchel, which meant he'd deserted, which meant as far as Halloway was concerned Cyrus Amberdale was now worth exactly as much dead as I was. He laid it out on the stone between us with his hands shaking, and I chose to believe the shaking was the cold, because the alternative was that it was guilt, and I didn't want to feel anything about that.

"Two squads," he said. "Corven's, and a second one out of Fort Marren, hired men, not soldiers. Halloway stopped pretending after the bridge went wrong. They're a day behind me. Maybe less. They have a tracker named Ossler who's better than anyone I've ever seen, and I've seen you."

"Flattery," I said. "At knifepoint. You haven't changed at all."

"Astrid—"

"The map's worth something. The rest of your mouth isn't. Sit down."

He sat. We were in a shallow overhang below the saddle of the pass, out of the worst of the wind, and the snow had begun again — that fine, dry, malicious snow that doesn't fall so much as it *arrives*, sideways, into everything. I built a small fire because I'm not stupid, and because if we were going to have this out, I wanted to see his face while I did it.

"You followed me up the mountain," I said. "Through a blizzard. With Halloway's killers behind you. Why?"

"You know why."

"I want to hear you say it. I've spent eight years imagining what you'd say. Consider it a professional interest. I'd hate for the reality to disappoint."

He looked at the fire for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and even, the voice he used for numbers and hard truths.

"I chose wrong at Kestrel Shoals. I chose wrong at anchor. I've been choosing wrong my whole life because choosing wrong was always faster and it always kept me breathing, and I told myself that staying alive was a kind of loyalty — to you, to the crew, to Hal—" He stopped. He never said my mentor's name. In eight years I don't think I'd heard him manage it once. "I told myself a lot of things. And then Halloway told me the crew dies either way, job done or not, and I sat in that room and did the arithmetic I've always done — how do I live — and for the first time in my life the answer came back *wrong*. So I stole the map, and I walked out, and I've been climbing after you ever since, because you're the only person alive who's carrying the truth up this mountain, and because—"

"Because," I said.

"Because it's you, Astrid. It was always going to be you."

The wind took his words and scattered them. I fed the fire a piece of scrub pine and watched it flare, and I thought about all the things I could say, and how few of them were true, and how the true ones would take all night.

"All right," I said. "We've got all night."

* * *

Here is what the night gave us, in no particular order, because that's how arguments go when both people have been rehearsing them for years.

He said he'd surfaced alone at Kestrel Shoals because the line had parted, because Hal was already gone when he came back down, because he'd spent four minutes — he counted them, he'd counted them every day since — at the anchor point with his lungs burning, looking into water so full of silt it was like looking into milk, and there was nothing. Nothing. And then his air ran low and he made the choice every diver makes and no diver ever forgives: he went up.

"And the log?" I said. "You surfaced with Hal's salvage log. You sold the survey data within the month. You didn't even wait for his funeral."

"No," he said. "I didn't."

"You could have brought it to me. You could have brought it to his daughter."

"I could have done a great many things." He said it without flinching, which was worse than flinching. "I was twenty-six years old and I'd just watched the only father I had drown and I did what I do, Astrid. I survived, and I took the salvage, and I told myself the money would keep the crew fed, and then I kept telling myself things until the telling was all there was."

"You let me hate you."

"Yes."

"For eight years."

"Yes."

"Why?" And here my voice did something I didn't authorize. It cracked. "Why would you let me hate you, Cyrus? Do you know what that cost me? Do you know what I built out of that? I built my whole *life* out of hating you. It was load-bearing."

He was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere above us, snow slid off a rock face with a sound like a long exhale.

"Because if you'd known the truth," he said, "you'd have known it was an accident. And then you'd have had to grieve him clean, without anyone to blame. And I thought — I told myself — that you'd need the blame more than you'd need the truth. That hate would keep you moving and grief would stop you dead. And I was a coward, so I let you have the version of the story that kept you walking." He looked up, and his eyes were wet, or the firelight was playing tricks, and I decided not to investigate which. "I was wrong. I've had a lot of practice being wrong. But that one I'd take back if the mountain would let me."

I wanted to throw the knife at him. I wanted to throw the whole fire at him. I wanted, most of all, to tell him he was right — that the hate had kept me moving, that every port I'd left, every season I'd refused to stay, every crew I'd never let myself belong to, had been built on the foundation of *Cyrus Amberdale chose the salvage and let Hal drown*, and that if I took that away I didn't know what was underneath. Maybe nothing. Maybe just a woman who'd been running so long she'd mistaken the running for a spine.

I said none of that. I said: "You don't get to decide what I needed."

"No," he agreed. "I don't."

* * *

Toward the smallest hours the argument stopped being about the past, because the past is a country you can argue about forever and never settle, and the snow was coming down harder, and the practical questions started shouldering their way in.

"You can't go on alone," he said.

"I've been alone the whole way up."

"You've been *unaccompanied* the whole way up. It's different. The pass narrows above the saddle — you know that. Ice over the cornice, a hundred-foot drop, and the drifts this year are chest-deep in the narrows. You need a second rope. You need someone on the anchor end." He patted the coil on his shoulder like a man introducing a relative. "I've been climbing since before I could swim. Let me do the one useful thing I've ever done."

"And if Ossler's tracker finds us?"

"Then there are two of us to fight, or two of us to run, or two of us to bury in the snow, and Halloway's men will have to guess which body is carrying the papers." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the tavern smile, and I wanted to break his teeth with the fire iron. "Besides. I know you, Astrid. You've already sewn those documents into your coat. You've been sitting on the left side of the fire all night so your knife hand stays free, and you checked the satchel four times, and you haven't slept, because you don't sleep when you're carrying something that matters. Which means by tomorrow night you'll be making diving-grade mistakes on a mountain, and I will be here to make them slightly less fatal."

"I could tie you and leave you for Halloway's men."

"You could."

"I could take the map and the rope and leave you nothing."

"You could," he said again, mildly. "You won't."

"How do you know?"

"Because I wouldn't." He said it simply, without drama, and that was the thing that got under my ribs like a splinter. "I've been the man who takes everything and leaves nothing, Astrid. I know exactly what it costs. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to take the rope."

The snow hissed against the fire. I looked at him — at this man I had hated so long and so well that hating him had worn a groove in me, the way water wears stone, and I could no longer tell where the groove ended and I began.

Here is the truth, since this is my account and I may as well be honest in it: I did not forgive him. Forgiveness is a door, and I wasn't ready to open it, and maybe the door doesn't even open from the inside, maybe it has to be opened from the other side by years I haven't lived yet. But the mountain didn't care about my grievances. The mountain cared about rope.

"Give me the map," I said.

He gave it. I studied it by firelight — Corven's neat military hand, the second squad's route marked in red, Ossler's projected line up the pass like a slow arrow pointed at my spine. They were closer than I'd hoped. A day, maybe less, and the storm would slow us both, but it would slow us more than it slowed them, because they had dogs.

"Corven's squad," I said. "The one that had you at the bridge."

"Deserted them two nights back. Left them my tent and a forged note saying I'd gone to ground in the valley. It'll buy a day, maybe two, once they sort it out." He hesitated. "Corven let you go at the cave. You know that."

"I know he gave me his horse and stood there looking like a man swallowing a fishbone."

"He's not Halloway's. Not in the place that counts. When this is over — if there is an over — he's a name worth remembering."

"Noted," I said, which was my way of agreeing, and he knew it, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and blind, the storm easing just enough to show us the pass: a white throat of rock narrowing ahead, the cornice hanging over it like the lip of a wave about to break. It was beautiful the way the sea is beautiful, which is to say it would kill you without malice and without memory.

I coiled my own rope and his together. I divided the food — I took the larger share, because I was carrying the papers, and he didn't argue, which was another small miracle I chose not to examine. At the edge of the overhang I stopped.

"Cyrus."

He looked up. Snow in his hair, gray in his beard that hadn't been there eight years ago. My oldest wound, standing in the weather.

"I don't forgive you," I said. "I want that on the record, carved in the rock, witnessed by the mountain. Whatever happens on this pass, whatever you do today — it doesn't settle anything. Do you understand?"

"I understand," he said.

"And if you die up here, I'm not carrying any last words of yours to anyone. Your sister will get nothing from me but your body's location and my condolences, and my condolences are worth exactly what you'd expect."

"Understood," he said, and there was the ghost of the old smile, the one I'd spent eight years learning to despise, and I found — this is the honest part, the part I'll never say aloud — that despising it took effort now. That the groove of hate had worn thin in places, and underneath it there was something old and stubborn and inconvenient that had never stopped believing him.

I turned and started up the pass. Behind me, I heard him check his knots, stamp feeling back into his boots, and follow — matching my stride, the way he always did, the way I'd once thought was the finest thing in the world and had lately come to think was the most dangerous.

We were two hundred yards into the narrows when the dogs started baying behind us.
