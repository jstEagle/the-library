## 16. Ambush at Stonewatch Bridge

The mountains had a way of making you honest. There was nowhere to hide from yourself up there — no tavern noise to drown in, no tide schedule to blame your moods on. Just rock, wind, and the sound of a man limping behind you because you'd finally decided to let him follow.

Cyrus had caught up two days past the pass, half-frozen and carrying a stolen route map like it was a love letter. I'd put a knife to his throat for the privilege of hearing him out, and when he was done talking I still hadn't forgiven him. But I'd taken his rope. In the language of people like us, that was practically a marriage vow.

Now we were three days down the eastern slope, and Stonewatch Bridge was ahead of us — an old stone span across a gorge so deep the river at the bottom sounded less like water and more like weather. The guidebooks — if anyone had been fool enough to write guidebooks for the Grayfells — would have called it picturesque. I called it the only way across for forty miles, which meant it was also the one place on this entire mountain where someone who wanted us dead wouldn't have to work very hard.

"You're thinking about the bridge," Cyrus said.

"I'm thinking about how much I hate bridges."

"You hate open water too."

"Open water doesn't have two sides and a choke point."

He grunted, which was his way of conceding the point. He'd been doing a lot of that lately — conceding, deferring, watching me with that careful expression of a man trying to pay off a debt in installments. It should have annoyed me more than it did. The trouble with Cyrus Amberdale was that eight years of hating him had worn grooves in me, and now that I'd stopped hating him, all those grooves were just empty, and something kept threatening to pour back into them.

We came over the last shoulder of scree around midday and stopped behind a wind-bent pine to look.

Stonewatch Bridge was maybe sixty feet of old imperial masonry, arched once in the middle, wide enough for a cart and narrow enough that six men standing abreast would own it completely. On our side, the road climbed out of a bowl of boulders. On the far side, it switchbacked down toward the valley and, eventually, the capital. The gorge walls dropped sheer on both approaches. No cover on the span itself. No cover worth the name on ours.

And there were men on it.

Four of them, dressed as drovers, which fooled nobody because real drovers don't stand perfectly still in the wind watching a road. Two at the near end, two at the far. One of them had a long gun slung under his coat, and the coat hung wrong in a way I recognized from a lifetime of reading men's clothes for weapons.

"Well," I said. "Halloway sends his regards."

"Corven let us go," Cyrus said quietly. "That means Halloway's stopped trusting his own uniforms. Those aren't soldiers."

"No. Soldiers hesitate." I counted again, out of habit, knowing the count wouldn't change. "Those are the kind that don't."

"We could go around. Forty miles north there's—"

"—a crossing that takes five days we don't have, through country where the snow's already waist-deep, carrying you, who can barely walk downhill without swearing in three languages." I looked at him. "Or we cross here, today, and deal with four men instead of the mountain."

"There's a third option," he said. "You give me the papers and I walk out there and negotiate."

"That's not an option, that's a eulogy."

"Astrid."

"Cyrus." I said his name flat, the way you drop an anchor. "You've spent eight years paying for Kestrel Shoals in guilt, which is the cheapest currency there is because you can mint it yourself. You don't get to spend your life today. That account closes on my terms or not at all."

Something moved through his face — hurt, and underneath the hurt, that stubborn amber-dale steadiness that had made him the best partner I ever had and the worst man I ever loved. "Then what are my terms?"

I studied the bridge while I thought. The wind was coming up the gorge, steady from the west, pushing at anything that crossed. The two men at the far end were positioned to stop retreat, not advance — they expected their quarry to come from the east side, from the pass, and to try to run *back*. Nobody expects the quarry to come straight at them.

"They want the papers," I said. "They think the papers are on me. So we make them right about that, and wrong about everything else."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took shape over the next hour while we sat among the boulders sharing hardtack and the last of Maren's smoked fish, which tasted like salt and forgiveness, which is to say better than it deserved.

Cyrus would come out of the rocks first, alone, hands visible, walking like a man who'd been chased too long and wanted to buy his life with information. He'd tell them the woman was dead — lost in the blizzard above the tree line, and here, look, I've got her satchel, empty, but I know where the papers are buried, and I'll show you for safe passage down. Every lie in that story was built on a truth: he *had* carried my satchel part of the way, and he *did* look like a man who'd trade anything to survive. Men like the ones on that bridge read desperation professionally. They'd believe it because believing it was easy.

While they crowded him — and they would crowd him, because greed makes men stupid about distance — I'd come along the gorge rim below the road, where a ledge ran out of the boulder field to within twenty feet of the bridge's near footing. Old goat track, barely a hand's width in places, with a drop that would make the fall from the Corvane's rigging look like stepping off a curb. I'd spent my life on ropes over black water. This was the same work with worse air.

From the ledge, I could reach the footing before they knew I was off the road. Then it was arithmetic: four men, one long gun, and whatever happened next would happen fast.

"And if it goes wrong?" Cyrus asked.

"Then it goes wrong the way things went wrong at Kestrel Shoals, except this time nobody leaves anybody on the bottom."

He was quiet a moment. "You know that's not what happened."

"I know what I saw."

"You saw me surface. You didn't see—" He stopped himself, shook his head. "Not here. Not with that drop listening." He nodded toward the gorge. "When we're across. I'll tell you everything, and you can hate me with the facts in hand."

"I'm looking forward to it," I said, and found, to my considerable irritation, that I meant it in more ways than one.

* * *

The ledge was every bit as friendly as it looked, which is to say it tried to kill me twice in the first fifty feet.

The first time, a hold I'd picked from above turned out to be frost-shattered flake that came away in my hand like stale bread. The second time, the wind gusted up the gorge just as I swung around a rib of rock, and for one long moment I hung by my fingertips over a hundred feet of roaring nothing, listening to the river chew the canyon walls, thinking — absurdly — of the seabed, of how quiet it was down there, of how I'd sworn after Kestrel Shoals never to hang over another drop trusting my grip more than my judgment.

But my grip held. My grip always holds. It's my judgment that gets me into these situations.

I came up onto the footing behind a buttress of the bridge itself, breathing hard, fingers bleeding where the rock had taken its toll, and pressed myself against cold stone older than any nation I had opinions about. Above me, voices.

"—swear it, she's dead. Blizzard took her two nights back. I buried the satchel where she fell because I couldn't carry it and climb." Cyrus's voice, cracked and ragged, pitched beautifully. Eight years ago I'd have believed every word. "There's a cairn. Papers wrapped in oilcloth. Worth more than you're being paid, I'd wager, and I'll draw you a map for passage down. That's fair dealing."

A pause. Then a voice I hadn't heard before, smooth and unhurried, the voice of a man who enjoyed his work: "Fair dealing. From a deserter." Footsteps on stone. "Show me your hands again."

I risked a look around the buttress.

Four of them on the span, as counted. Cyrus stood at the bridge's midpoint, hands raised, satchel-less, looking exactly as broken as he needed to. The talker — a lean man with a dueling scar and the patient eyes of a butcher — stood ten feet from him. The long gun was in the hands of a big fellow near the far rail. The other two had drifted close to Cyrus, closer than they should have, drawn in by the smell of money the way gulls draw in by the smell of blood.

Ten feet between the butcher and Cyrus. Fifteen between the others. The far pair still at their post.

It would do.

I came over the parapet low and fast, knife already moving, and put the nearest man down with a slash across the thigh that dropped him screaming sideways into the rail. The world went loud. The butcher turned with a speed that told me he'd earned that scar honestly, and the big man fumbled the long gun up off his hip—

—and Cyrus moved.

I will see it the rest of my life, I think, in the particular slow clarity that violence sometimes grants, the way the sea goes glass-still before a storm breaks. The butcher's arm coming up with a short blade. Cyrus stepping *into* it, not away — stepping between the blade and me, arms wide, the way you'd shield a lantern from the wind. The butcher lunging. Cyrus twisting, taking the cut along the ribs instead of the throat, grabbing the man's wrist, hauling him off balance toward the rail—

—and then the long gun spoke.

The sound went up the gorge and came back down again, doubled, tripled, the whole canyon suddenly full of thunder. Cyrus jerked. Once. His grip on the butcher tore loose and the butcher staggered back, and Cyrus turned — slowly, terribly — and looked at me across twelve feet of ancient stone with an expression I'd only ever seen on his face once before, at Kestrel Shoals, looking down into black water.

Then his knees went, and he folded onto the bridge like a dropped sail.

Everything after that happened the way dives happen: technically, furiously, without room for thought. I killed the butcher — I won't dress it up; my knife found him before his did, and he went over the rail into the gorge and the river took him the way the sea takes everything, without comment. The thigh-cut man was crawling and no threat. The big man with the gun was working the action with shaking hands, and shaking hands are slow hands, and mine weren't. I put a thrown stone into his face — the Grayfells provide ammunition generously — and followed him down when he staggered, and after that it was short and ugly and over.

The two at the far end ran. Wise of them. I let them.

* * *

The wind came up the gorge and moved Cyrus's hair, and for three heartbeats he didn't move at all, and those three heartbeats were the longest of a life that has contained shipwrecks, drownings, betrayals, and one admiralty hearing still to come.

Then he coughed, and said, with a wet rasp that was trying very hard to be his usual dry tone, "Told you. Not a eulogy."

"You absolute—" I was on my knees beside him before I'd decided to move, hands pressing into his side where the blood welled hot between my fingers. High in the ribs, left side, punched through and out the back by the feel of it. Bad. Very bad. Not immediately fatal bad, if the mountain and I had anything to say about it, and the mountain and I had history. "You stepped in front of me."

"Seemed efficient." His eyes were gray-lidded, unfocused, fighting to stay on my face. "One bullet. Saves Halloway hiring two."

"Don't you dare be funny. Don't you *dare*."

"Wouldn't dream of it." A bubble of red at his lip. His hand found my wrist, gripped with a strength that surprised me. "Astrid. Listen. The ledger — Kestrel Shoals — I've owed you since—"

"You owe me nothing dead. That's the whole point of owing." I ripped the sash from my coat, then his, wadding them against the wound. He hissed. "You owe me a descent, a valley, a capital, a hearing, and a very long conversation about what actually happened at Kestrel Shoals, and you will pay every installment, do you hear me? You don't get to default now. Not after I finally started letting you follow me."

His mouth curved. "Bossy."

"Salvage captain. Same thing."

I got him up. It took everything — his arm over my shoulders, my legs doing the work of two, the two of us staggering across the span together while the river roared below like it disapproved of the whole enterprise. At the midpoint I made the mistake of looking down at the stone where he'd fallen, at the dark stain already going tacky in the cold, and I understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with documents or commodores, that he'd known. Standing out there alone on that bridge, telling his beautiful lies, he'd known exactly what he was offering. And he'd offered it anyway. Eight years of guilt, and in the end he'd paid the debt the only way that counted — not with words, not with maps, but with his body placed between me and the bullet, the oldest payment there is.

"You could have stayed behind the rocks," I said as we came off the far end. "The plan was mine. Your job was to talk."

"The plan," he breathed, "was terrible."

"It worked."

"One of us had to be the backup plan."

* * *

We made perhaps two miles before the blood loss and the cold forced the issue, and we sheltered in the hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite where the wind couldn't find us and the road below was invisible. I packed the wound with snow to slow the bleeding, bound it tight with strips of my shirt, force-fed him water and the last of the fish. He shivered in waves, each one worse than the last, and his skin had gone the color of the granite overhead.

"Fever's coming," he said, in the tone of a man reporting the tide.

"Fever's welcome to try. I've drowned worse things than fever."

"That doesn't make sense."

"Neither does taking a bullet for a woman who hasn't forgiven you."

He was quiet a while. The light was failing, going blue and then violet in the notch of the valley below, and somewhere down there lights would be coming on in villages where people had never heard of Marrow Bay or Commodore Halloway or the price of paper.

"It wasn't the rope," he said suddenly.

"What?"

"At Kestrel Shoals. Torv's line. Everyone thinks the rope parted. It didn't." His eyes found mine in the dusk, fever-bright and steady. "I cut it. Not on purpose — God help me, not on purpose — my knife was out for the snag on the wreck, and the current took me, and the blade caught the line, and I panicked, and I surfaced, and by the time I understood what I'd done it was too late to go back down. That's what I couldn't say for eight years. That's what I've been drowning on ever since." His breath hitched. "I didn't kill him, Astrid. But I ran. I ran, and I let everyone believe the rope broke, because the truth made me a coward and the lie only made me unlucky."

The wind moved through the pines above us. Down in the gorge, the river kept up its argument with the mountain, patient as gravity.

I thought of Torv — my mentor, my anchor, the man whose grave I still owed a visit — and I thought of the letter waiting in the oilcloth against my ribs, his handwriting, his investigation, his name on a list of the silenced. And I found that the fury I'd carried for eight years, the fury I'd aimed at Cyrus like a loaded gun, didn't vanish. It just changed targets. It turned east, toward the capital, toward a man with admiral's braid who had buried witnesses and bought silence and sent killers to a bridge in the mountains.

"Save your strength," I said at last, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "Tomorrow we descend. You're going to live, Cyrus. That's not forgiveness. But it's a start."

He smiled — small, pained, real — and closed his eyes.

I sat watch through the dark hours with my knife across my knees and the papers warm against my ribs, listening to him breathe, counting the breaths the way I used to count the pulls of a dive line. Somewhere behind us, above the closed pass, Corven was choosing what kind of man he'd be. Somewhere ahead, in the winter city, a magistrate and an editor were waiting for what I carried. And somewhere between here and there lay thirty miles of descending road with a wounded man, a stolen map, and whatever else Halloway could afford to hire.

The stars wheeled over the Grayfells, indifferent and bright.

I counted his breaths, and I watched the road, and I waited for morning.
