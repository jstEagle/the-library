## 17. Descent

The pass closed behind us with a sound like a door slamming in the house of God.

I didn't look back at first. I heard it — the wind rising through Grayfell's throat, the snow coming down sideways, the whole white weight of the mountain deciding, all at once, that nothing else was getting over it this year. Corven and his squad, Halloway's hired knives, every man with a warrant and a grudge: sealed on the other side until spring.

No pursuit. Also no road back. That's the thing about doors slamming — you're always on one side of them, and nobody asks which side you'd have preferred.

"Still with me?" I said.

Cyrus answered with something between a groan and a word. I took it as encouragement. He was slung across my back, arms looped over my collarbone, his blood soaking through his shirt into my coat in a warmth that was already going cold. The bullet had gone through his shoulder — clean through, the priest had said, which was the only good news anyone had produced in two days. Clean meant it might not kill him. It also meant he'd lost enough blood to make him heavier than any man had a right to be, and Cyrus Amberdale had always been built like a mast stepped in a wine barrel.

"You know," I said, adjusting my grip on the rope harness I'd rigged from his belt to mine, "when I said I'd take your rope, I didn't mean carry you down a mountain on it."

He mumbled something. Delirium, probably. Or agreement. With Cyrus it had never been easy to tell the difference.

The far slope of the Grayfell was kinder than the near one, if you could call anything kind that dropped four thousand feet through scree and pine and cloud. Kinder mostly because it faced away from the storm. The snow still fell, but it fell politely here, straight down, gathering on our shoulders like it wanted us to stop and admire it. Below us, somewhere under the white, the trail wound toward the valley towns, the river, the coach roads, and eventually — if the papers held, if the magistrate held, if my luck held for once in its miserable life — the capital.

Six days, Maren had said, coming up. Going down with a wounded man on my back, I figured on eight. I figured wrong, as it turned out. It took nine. But I'm getting ahead of myself, and a diver learns early: you don't count the fathoms left. You count the ones behind you.

* * *

The first day I talked to keep him awake.

This is a thing they teach you nowhere and everyone learns anyway: a man bleeding out goes quiet, and quiet men die. So I talked. I told him about the worst storms I'd ever worked through. I told him about Bram's theory that gulls were the souls of debt collectors. I told him about the time in Port Callow when I'd won a fistfight against a customs officer twice my size by biting him, which is not a story I tell sober people, but Cyrus was well past sober.

"Remember Kestrel Shoals?" he said suddenly, on the second morning.

My hands went still on the rope. We were stopped in a stand of black pines, snow hissing down through the branches, and I was feeding him melted snow from a tin cup, and he looked up at me with eyes that were too clear for a fevered man. Too clear and too old.

"We're not doing this," I said.

"Astrid—"

"You've got a hole in your shoulder and a fever cooking your brains. Whatever you think you remember, it can wait until—"

"He didn't drown fighting me." His voice cracked on it. Not from the fever. "That's what you think. All these years. That we fought over the salvage log, that I cut loose, that I let him go down."

I set the cup down very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it off the mountain.

"I saw you surface," I said. "Alone. With the log in your teeth like a dog with a bone. I saw what was left of the line. I did the arithmetic, Cyrus. I've had eight years to do the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic's wrong." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and thin. "God, the arithmetic's been wrong the whole time. Come here. Sit down. My hands don't work and I need—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I need you to hear it while I've got the nerve."

I sat. Because I'm weak, or because I'm curious, or because some part of me had been waiting eight years at the bottom of Kestrel Shoals for exactly this, holding its breath.

* * *

Here is what he told me, there in the pines, with the snow falling soft as an apology:

The line hadn't failed. That was the first thing. The line had been cut — by him, deliberately, cleanly, with the knife he carried for cutting kelp — but not while Tam was on it.

They'd found the crate. The Kestrel Shoals job, the one we swore off after, the one that killed our mentor — the crate had come up easy, easier than it should have, and Tam had been laughing about it, actually laughing, saying the shoals owed them one after all these years. And then the current shifted. Anyone who's worked water knows the moment. The sea changes its mind about you.

Tam had gone back down for the second run — the logbook chest, the small stuff, the gravy on top of the meal. Cyrus was topside, tending the line. And the swell came up out of nowhere, the kind of swell that turns a dive boat into a drum, and the boat started dragging anchor toward the shoals themselves, toward the teeth.

"I had maybe ten seconds," Cyrus said. "Ten seconds to choose. Cut the line and save the boat, or hold and lose everything — Tam, me, the boat, all of it. And I did the sums, Astrid. I did them fast, the way you do them, and I chose the boat." His breath caught. "And then the line went slack anyway. Before I even finished cutting. He'd already — the chest had wedged him, or the current took him, I don't know, I'll never know, because I cut and I pulled and I ran the boat out of there and I didn't go back."

"You should have gone back," I said. Flat. Not cruel. Just true.

"Yes." No argument. Eight years of argument, and now, with a bullet hole in him and death close enough to smell, no argument at all. "I should have gone back. Instead I grabbed the salvage log off the deck — I don't even know why, some animal instinct, evidence, payment, I don't know — and I rowed ashore, and by the time anyone asked questions I'd built the story where the line failed and I did everything right and Tam Vane died because the sea is the sea."

"And you let me believe—"

"I let you believe whatever made you hate me least," he said. "Or most. I honestly couldn't tell which. You needed someone to blame, Astrid. Blaming the ocean doesn't work. Believe me, I tried. So I stood there and let you blame me, because the alternative was telling you your mentor died doing a greedy second run, and that the last thing he said on this earth was a joke about the shoals owing him one."

The snow came down through the pines. Somewhere below us a crow said something rude and flew off.

"He raised you," Cyrus said quietly. "After your father. He taught you to read water before he taught you to read letters. And I couldn't hand you that. That he died for a chest of paperwork. So I handed you a villain instead. Me. It was the closest thing to a kindness I had."

"It wasn't a kindness," I said. "It was theft. You stole eight years from me."

"I know." He closed his eyes. "Add it to the ledger. I've been keeping it a long time."

* * *

I didn't forgive him that day. I want to be honest about that, because stories like to make forgiveness a door that swings open, bright and complete, and in my experience it's more like a leak — slow, finding its own way down, pooling where it pools.

What I did that day was sit with it. I sat in the snow with a man who had finally told me the truth, and I turned it over the way I turn over anything I pull from deep water: looking for the damage, checking whether it's worth the carrying.

And here's the strange thing, the thing I still don't fully understand. The truth hurt less than the lie. Not because it was gentler — it wasn't; Tam dying for a greedy second run was uglier than Tam dying betrayed — but because it was *finished*. A lie keeps moving. A lie makes you chase it for years, diving again and again into the same dark water, looking for the thing you're sure is down there. The truth just lies still on the bottom and lets you look at it.

Tam had gotten greedy. Tam had joked at the wrong moment. Tam had died the way divers die, which is quickly and stupidly and mid-laugh, and no amount of villainy — Cyrus's or anyone's — would change the shape of that.

"You could have told me at any point in eight years," I said, when I finally spoke again.

"Could I have?"

And the infuriating thing — the genuinely infuriating thing — was that he was right. I wouldn't have listened. Grief had built me a story and I'd moved into it and furnished it and paid rent on it for nearly a decade. He'd have knocked on that door with the truth in his hands and I'd have put a knife in him before he got three words out.

Maybe that's forgiveness. Maybe it's just bookkeeping. Either way, I fed him more melted snow, repacked his bandage with the priest's herbs, and got us both back on the mountain.

* * *

The third day the fever peaked.

I knew it was bad when he started talking to Tam. Not about him — to him. Arguing with him, actually, in the low stubborn tone he used for negotiations with harbormasters. *The line's good, Tam, I checked the line twice.* Then later, softer: *I'm sorry about the chest. I'm sorry about all of it.* Then, worst of all, my name, over and over, like a man calling across open water: *Astrid. Astrid. Astrid.*

I held his hands and gave him water and lied to him the way you lie to the drowning — *almost there, nearly down, I've got you* — and I will admit to no one, ever, that I cried. There was snow in my eyes. That's my story and I'm keeping it.

On the fourth day the fever broke, and he woke up hungry, which the priest had told me was the sign to watch for, and I fed him broth from the last of the dried fish Maren had pressed on us, and he ate it like a man who'd been offered a knighthood.

"How long was I—" he started.

"Two days of nonsense. You argued with Tam about anchor lines. You apologized to a chest."

"That tracks," he said, and then, watching my face carefully, the way you watch ice before stepping on it: "You heard the rest, then. Before."

"I heard the rest."

"And?"

I looked out down the slope, at the valley coming up to meet us — green thread of river, smoke from chimneys, the world resuming below the snowline like a play after intermission.

"And I'm still angry," I said. "But it's a different anger. Smaller. More portable." I handed him the cup. "You cut the line to save the boat. Stupid, brave, wrong, right — all of it at once. That's not a villain, Cyrus. That's just a diver. I've made the same sum ten times since. Every one of us has. You just never let me see you do it."

"I let you see me do worse."

"You did." I stood, and started coiling the rope. "But you also came over that pass half-dead with a stolen map and a bullet in you, so the ledger's got entries on both sides now. We'll see how it balances."

He smiled — carefully, because smiling pulled the wound — and lay back in the snow, and for a while neither of us said anything, and it was the first silence in eight years that didn't feel like a held breath.

* * *

We came down out of the high country on the ninth day, into a valley that smelled of woodsmoke and dung and spring-that-was-still-months-away, and I have never been so glad to smell anything in my life, including air.

There was a coaching inn at the crossroads. There was, more importantly, a notice board outside the coaching inn, and I made Cyrus wait with the mule — yes, we still had the mule, that animal was immortal and I intend to say so in print someday — while I read the notices nailed up in the cold.

Mostly it was the usual: lost dogs, auctioned boats, a sermon schedule. But pinned dead center, fresh paper, navy seal at the bottom, was a broadsheet.

**By order of the Admiralty Court.** A hearing, in the capital, eleven days hence. An inquiry into *irregularities in naval operations, Marrow Bay district.* The reformist magistrate had done it, then. Had forced it into the open, made it public, made it a hearing instead of a quiet room and a quiet grave. Names were requested to come forward. Witnesses would be protected.

Eleven days. Four more on the road, if the weather held and Cyrus held and the mule held.

And beneath the official text, in smaller type, a line that stopped my breath:

*A warrant is outstanding for one Astrid Vane, salvager, wanted for questioning regarding stolen property.*

"They named you," Cyrus said, reading over my shoulder. His voice was steady but his hand on my arm wasn't.

"They named me." I stared at the warrant, at my own name sitting there in navy ink like a fish on a slab. Eleven days to walk into the capital, into an admiralty court, with stolen documents sewn into my coat and a warrant out for my arrest — and stand up in front of the whole rotten institution and speak.

Old Astrid would have run. Old Astrid had a whole philosophy about it: never stay in one port longer than a season, never let the shore get hooks in you, keep moving until moving is all you are.

But Tam had a grave in a harbor town with no name on it yet. And somewhere in those papers was a list of names from Marrow Bay whose graves had never existed at all.

"Cyrus," I said, turning away from the board, "how do you feel about arriving in the capital a day early?"

He grinned, wincing. "Badly. Let's do it."
