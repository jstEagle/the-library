## 18. The Capital in Winter

The capital announced itself the way a debt collector does — not with menace, exactly, but with the certainty that you'd arrived somewhere you couldn't leave without paying.

We came down off the Grayfell road on the ninth morning, Cyrus half-carried between my shoulder and a farmer's cart he'd charmed out of its owner with a story about pirates that was, for once, an understatement. His wound had gone from angry to sullen. He complained about it constantly, which I took as a good sign. Men who are dying don't have the energy to be tedious about it.

And then the city rose up ahead of us through the falling snow — spires and slate roofs and chimney smoke standing straight up in the still air like a forest of grey trees, and the harbor beyond it, frozen at the edges, crowded with hulls in the middle where the water still moved. Somewhere under all that ice and stone was a man named Halloway who thought we were dead in a mountain pass. Somewhere in it too were two people whose names Maren had scratched onto a scrap of paper back in the fishing village: Magistrate Elowen Hart, Fourth District; and Tam Voss, editor of *The Meridian Ledger*.

"Two names," I'd said to her. "That's your plan? Two names on a scrap?"

"It only ever takes one person willing to listen," she'd said, "and one more willing to print it."

I'd spent eight years avoiding this city. It had too many walls and too few exits, and everyone in it walked like they owned the ground under their feet. But the papers against my ribs had stopped feeling like salvage somewhere on the mountain. They felt like cargo now. And cargo has to be delivered.

* * *

Cyrus's sister lived above a chandler's shop on Rope Walk, in rooms that smelled of tar and boiled wool. Her name was Sera, and when she opened the door and saw her brother grey-faced and leaning on a stranger, she didn't cry or fuss. She looked him over the way you look over a hull after a storm — cataloguing damage, deciding what could wait.

"You absolute idiot," she said.

"Hello to you too."

"They told me you were working for the navy." She said the word *navy* the way other people said *plague ship*. "Get inside before someone sees you. Both of you."

The rooms were small and warm and full of the clutter of a woman who mended sails for a living — canvas everywhere, needles like fishhooks stuck into a pincushion shaped like a pig. Sera put Cyrus on the bed, cut his coat off him with a sailmaker's efficiency that made him yelp, and cleaned the bullet wound while interrogating me without once looking at me.

"So. My brother. Working for Commodore Halloway. Shot on Stonewatch Bridge by men who weren't navy, which means hired, which means Halloway wanted no record. And you're carrying something worth killing over." She tied off the last bandage and finally turned. "I'm going to make tea, and then you're going to tell me everything, because if the navy comes through that door I need to know how much of my life I'm about to lose."

I liked her immediately. There was a family resemblance around the eyes — that flat, unblinking assessment — but where Cyrus wore his cleverness like a stolen coat, Sera wore hers like skin.

I told her most of it. The Corvane. The strongbox. Marrow Bay. The massacre, the witnesses loaded aboard as prisoners, the frigate sent to the bottom with them and the proof, and Halloway's signature at the bottom of the order. I left out Kestrel Shoals, mostly because Cyrus had gone quiet and pale on the bed and some debts are private.

When I finished, Sera sat very still with her tea going cold.

"Marrow Bay," she said. "My husband's cousin lost three brothers there. The navy said they were smugglers killed resisting arrest. She believed it for eleven years." She set the cup down with a click. "You have the orders. Signed."

"Signed, dated, countersigned by his aide. Plus the prisoner manifest, plus letters."

"Then you can't just hand them to a magistrate and hope. Half the bench owes their appointments to men like Halloway." She was already moving, pulling a clean shirt from a chest, throwing it at her brother. "You need Hart. Elowen Hart. She's been fighting the Admiralty's charter courts for six years and she hasn't been bought yet, which means she's either incorruptible or waiting for a better offer, and I've drunk wine with her and I'm fairly sure it's the first one. And you need Voss. If it isn't in print, it never happened."

"And if Hart reports us the moment we walk in?" I asked. "If Voss sells us to Halloway for a pension?"

Sera smiled, and it was Cyrus's smile, and for the first time I understood why I'd never quite been able to hate him properly. It ran in the blood.

"Then we'll know," she said, "and we'll run. That's the whole art of it, diver. You find out."

* * *

Magistrate Elowen Hart received petitioners in a narrow office behind the Fourth District court, between the hours of seven and nine in the morning, alone, without clerks, because — as she explained when we were shown in — clerks had families and families could be leveraged.

She was smaller than I expected, grey-haired, with ink stains on three fingers and a face like a cliff that had decided, after long consideration, to stop eroding. She listened to me for forty minutes without interrupting once. I laid the documents on her desk one at a time, and watched her read each one twice, and when I got to the order signed by Halloway her hands did not shake, but they went very careful, the way mine go careful handling something that might still be live.

"Where were these recovered?" she asked.

"The wreck of the HMS Corvane. Kestrel Shoals."

"Which the Admiralty surveyed in—" she consulted a ledger without looking away from me, a trick I intended to steal— "the year after the sinking, and declared a hazard to navigation with nothing of value aboard. Nothing of value." She touched the edge of the order. "Do you understand what you've brought me, Miss Vane?"

"Papers."

"I understand what you've brought me." She stood and went to the window. Snow was still falling, fat and slow, erasing the street below. "Six years I have fought charter fraud and press-gang abuses and prize-court theft, and every time I got close to anything that mattered, the file went missing or the witness emigrated or the admiralty judge recused himself for reasons of health. Health." She laughed, short and joyless. "Do you know why I'm still alive, Miss Vane? Because I have never been dangerous. A nuisance, yes. Dangerous, no. What you are holding makes me dangerous. Do you know what happens to dangerous magistrates?"

"They get promoted," Cyrus said from the corner, "or buried. Usually both — posthumously."

Hart looked at him. "You'd be Amberdale. The salvage man." Something moved across her face — recognition, distaste, then a kind of weary arithmetic. "Your name is in three separate fraud proceedings."

"Alleged fraud."

"All true, I'd wager." She came back to the desk. "Here is my problem. If I take these documents into open court tomorrow, Halloway will have me removed from the bench by noon on procedural grounds and the documents sealed as evidence in an admiralty matter, which is to say, disappeared. If I take them to the First Lord directly, I am trusting the chain of command that produced them." She tapped the signature. "If I do nothing, I keep my career and lose whatever remains of my soul, which is a bargain I've made too often already."

"So what do you do?" I asked.

"What I should have done years ago." She drew a sheet of paper toward her. "I convene a hearing. Public. Under the district's inquiry statute — old, obscure, and annoyingly binding: any magistrate may summon public testimony on matters touching the safety of subjects of the Crown, and no admiralty writ stays it. Halloway's lawyers will scream. Let them scream. Screaming in public is the one thing he cannot afford." She began writing. "But testimony alone won't hold him. Documents can be called forgeries. Witnesses can be discredited. I need the story in the streets before I gavel in, so that when they come for me, the whole city already knows why."

"That's Voss," Sera had said, and so that afternoon, we went to see the newspaperman.

* * *

The offices of *The Meridian Ledger* occupied the top floor of a building on Ink Row, and you smelled the place before you reached it — hot lead and lamp oil and the particular sourness of men who hadn't slept in service of a deadline. Tam Voss turned out to be a broad, rumpled man with spectacles mended with wire and sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the wrist, who heard the word *Marrow Bay* in his own doorway and pulled us inside and bolted the door behind us with the speed of long practice.

He read everything. He read it the way a starving man eats, fast and then again slower, and halfway through the second pass he took off his spectacles and polished them on his shirt for a long time without cleaning anything.

"Eleven years ago," he said, "I ran a piece on Marrow Bay. Three hundred words, page six. Survivors' accounts, a customs clerk who swore the tolls never showed contraband, questions about where the dead were buried. The next week I lost my lead advertiser, a naval contract printer. The week after that, the proprietor called me in and explained the economics of the situation. I rewrote the piece myself. Made it an apology for speculation. I've regretted it every day since, in a low-grade way, the way you regret a tooth." He put the spectacles back on. "This is the tooth coming out."

"You understand what printing this costs you," I said.

"Oh, I've done the sums." He gestured at the office — the presses below, the boys setting type, the whole rickety machine of it. "The Ledger dies. Maybe I die, if Halloway's feeling energetic. That's the price column." He gathered the papers with both hands, carefully, like lifting something wounded. "Here's the asset column. Tomorrow night, Hart convenes her hearing. Friday morning, ten thousand copies of this on every breakfast table in the capital, with the order reproduced in facsimile and Halloway's name in the headline in type big enough to read from a horse. By the time he gets his lawyers moving, the story belongs to ten thousand breakfast tables. You can't disappear a thing once everyone's chewed it." He grinned, and it was not entirely a nice grin. "He should have drowned you in the mountains, Miss Vane. Sloppy of him."

"He didn't drown me in the mountains," I said, "because the mountains tried first and lost."

Voss laughed. Then he sobered, and looked at me over the wire-mended spectacles with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors on bad nights.

"One thing," he said. "You should know what you're walking into. A hearing isn't justice. It's a room. In that room, for one hour, the truth gets to stand up and speak with the law's permission. Whether anyone listens — whether it sticks — that's decided afterward, by people, in the cold, over months. I can give you the hour. I can't give you the sticking."

"I've spent my whole life," I said, "learning the difference between getting ashore and staying there. Give me the hour. I'll manage the sticking."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which surprised me, because I'd been running on four hours a night since the pass and my body had opinions. So I sat by the window of Sera's back room with my coat around my shoulders and watched the snow come down on the capital, and for the first time since the Corvane, I let myself think instead of move.

Snow does something to a city. It takes the noise away. All those streets and wheels and shouting voices, muffled under a white hush, until what's left is just the shapes of things — rooftops, spires, the dark line of the harbor wall. I used to think silence meant danger. On a dive, silence means your air's gone wrong or something big is near. But this silence was different. This was the silence of a place holding its breath.

Tomorrow night, in a courtroom, I would stand up in front of braid and brass and say the names out loud. All of them. The names from the manifest, the names from my mentor's letter, the names of the Marrow Bay dead that the priest in the cave had confirmed one by one with his ruined voice while the blizzard screamed outside. Names are strange cargo. You can carry them across a mountain range sewn into your coat, and they weigh nothing, and they weigh everything.

There was a knock, soft, and Cyrus eased himself into the room and sat across from me without asking, which was either intimacy or presumption, and with him it had always been both.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're staring at snow."

"I'm multitasking."

He was quiet a moment. The bandage at his shoulder showed at his collar, white against the dark shirt Sera had found him. "Voss showed me the proofs. Front page. Your name's going to be in it. *Astrid Vane, salvage diver.*" He said it like a toast.

"Don't romanticize me. By Friday half the city will be calling me a thief and the other half a liar."

"Probably both at once. It's a talented city." He leaned his head back against the wall. "Astrid. If it goes wrong tomorrow — if Hart gets shut down, if Halloway walks — I want you to know I've arranged passage. A boat out of the outer harbor, a friend of Sera's, ready at dawn after. For both of us. Whatever happens in that room, there's a door out."

I looked at him for a long time. Eight years ago he'd surfaced from Kestrel Shoals alone, and I had built my whole life on the shape of that absence — a woman who kept moving because stopping meant remembering. And here he was, offering me a door out, and the strange thing, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed coal, was that I didn't want it.

"No," I said.

"No?"

"If it goes wrong, I'll deal with wrong. But I'm done leaving ports in the night, Cy. I've been doing it since I was nineteen years old and it turns out it wasn't freedom, it was just running with better scenery." I turned back to the window. Below, a lamplighter was making his rounds, and the snow caught each new flame and softened it. "Whatever happens in that room, I'll be in it when it happens. All the way through."

He was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "When did that happen? When did you become someone who stays?"

"Somewhere above the tree line," I said. "It's cold up there. Changes a person."

He laughed — quietly, so as not to wake Sera — and after a while he went back to bed, and I stayed at the window until the lamps burned out and the snow kept falling, patient as tide.

* * *

The summons came at midmorning: Magistrate Hart's inquiry, convened for seven o'clock that evening, Fourth District courthouse, public gallery open. Voss's boys had worked through the night; I saw the early edition on a newsboy's tray as we crossed the square, the headline black and enormous, and people standing in the snow actually reading, actually pointing, a knot of them growing around every copy like frost blooming on glass.

We dressed for it as best we could — Cyrus in Sera's late husband's good coat, me in the same salt-stiff clothes I'd crossed a mountain in, because I decided the court could look at exactly what the truth cost to deliver. Sera walked us to the courthouse steps through a crowd already gathering, and at the bottom of the stairs she gripped my arm hard, once, sailor-style, and said, "Find out," and went to stand with the printers at the edge of the square.

Inside, the corridor was lined with uniforms. Not marines — court officers, technically — but braid has a way of multiplying, and I counted more of it than a district inquiry should draw. At the doors to the chamber, a young lieutenant stepped into our path with a paper in his hand, and his face had the particular misery of a man doing a job he's been ordered to do quickly, before anyone thinks about it.

"Astrid Vane?" he said. "You're served. Writ of seizure, Admiralty jurisdiction — the documents on your person are claimed as stolen naval property, and your arrest is authorized pending—" He faltered, because down the corridor a door had opened and Magistrate Hart stood there, small and grey and immovable as a channel marker, and the look she gave the lieutenant could have keelhauled him.

"Lieutenant," she said pleasantly, "you may inform the Commodore that admiralty writs do not stay a statutory inquiry, that the gallery opens in twenty minutes, and that he is welcome to attend. There's seating." She turned to me. "Miss Vane. Mr. Amberdale. Come in. It's time the truth stood up."

Beyond her, through the chamber doors, I could hear the crowd finding its seats — a rustle and murmur like the sea deciding something — and I put my hand flat against my coat, against the papers that had cost a frigate and a mountain range and a bridge and almost everything else, and I walked in.
