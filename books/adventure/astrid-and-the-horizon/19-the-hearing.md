## 19. The Hearing

The magistrate's clerk told me to dress plainly. I own nothing but plain, so that was easy. What he meant was: don't look like a thief, a smuggler, or a woman who has spent the last month sleeping in shepherds' huts and eating snow. I did my best with what remained — Maren's borrowed shawl, my one clean shirt, and Cyrus's coat brushed free of mountain grit until it only looked moderately criminal.

"You're fidgeting," Cyrus said.

"I'm adjusting my collar."

"Your collar's been adjusted eleven times."

"It's a complicated collar." I let my hands drop anyway. "How do you look so calm?"

"I'm not calm. I'm empty. It looks similar from the outside." He was pale under his tan, his shoulder still bound where the bullet had gone through at Stonewatch Bridge, and every few minutes his eyes went to the door of the safehouse as if Halloway's men might come through it even now, three days after we'd reached the capital, two days after Magistrate Ellery Voss had agreed — over her own career, possibly her own neck — to hear us.

"They'll try to stop it," I said.

"Of course they'll try to stop it. That's what power does when it's cornered. It doesn't argue. It bites." He rolled his bad shoulder and winced. "Question is whether it bites before you finish talking."

I had thought about that question a great deal in the night, lying on a stranger's floor in a city where I knew no one, listening to snow silence the streets outside. I had carried those papers across the Grayfell Range with a blizzard chewing at my heels. I had watched a priest stand up to soldiers in a cave. I had watched Corven — Halloway's own lieutenant — hand me his horse and his silence. All of that was behind me. The last mile was a room with admirals in it.

The sea had never frightened me. Rooms full of powerful men did. The sea, at least, didn't pretend to be on your side.

* * *

The hearing chamber of the Admiralty Court was not built for comfort; it was built for intimidation, and it was very good at its job. Vaulted ceiling, gray stone, a bench for the panel raised high enough that whoever sat beneath it developed a permanent crick in their neck looking up. Public galleries ran along both walls, and this morning they were packed — word had traveled the way word does in winter, slow through the streets and then all at once, like ice forming. Fishwives next to clerks next to newspapermen scribbling already. Somewhere among them, I knew, were the families from Marrow Bay. Old Halvard's daughter had come down with the coastal coach. I hadn't met her yet. I wasn't sure I could bear to before it was done.

Magistrate Voss sat at the center of the panel — a spare woman with iron-gray hair and the expression of someone who had been disappointed by institutions for thirty years and had stopped taking it personally. To her left, two naval officers whose faces suggested they'd rather be anywhere else. To her right, an elderly civilian jurist who kept polishing his spectacles as though the truth might smudge them.

And across the floor, Commodore Aldous Halloway.

He'd dressed for it. Full dress uniform, braid enough to outfit a small navy, medals arranged with mathematical precision. He stood talking quietly with his advocate, and when his eyes found mine across the chamber, he did not glare. That was the worst part. He smiled — faintly, courteously, the smile of a man greeting a minor inconvenience he fully intended to have removed by lunch.

"There he is," Cyrus murmured. "The face that wears the braid."

"Don't," I said. "Not yet. Keep your temper till it's useful."

"My temper is a professional instrument, Astrid. I keep it holstered."

The crier called the session. We rose. Snow ticked softly against the high windows, the only gentle thing in the room.

Voss opened without ceremony. "This is an extraordinary session convened at the request of Magistrate's Office, concerning documents alleged to evidence crimes committed at Marrow Bay in the year of the Corvane's loss, and alleged obstruction of inquiry into the same. Before we begin—" she looked down at a paper in front of her, and something in her jaw tightened "—the Commodore's advocate has moved that the principal witness, one Astrid Vane, be remanded into naval custody on charges of theft of naval property, pending resolution of this matter."

The gallery stirred like water before wind.

"Theft," I said, before I could stop myself.

"Miss Vane will have opportunity to speak," Voss said dryly, "in approximately one minute, if the court permits. Commodore, does your motion include a request that the witness be held somewhere other than this chamber?"

Halloway's advocate, a sleek man named Pellwith, stepped forward. "It does, Your Honor. The property in question is sensitive naval material. Its custodian should be the Navy itself. Further, the woman Vane is a known salvage diver of no fixed address, previously chartered under review for license irregularities—"

"She means I never stayed anywhere long enough to pay my taxes," I said.

"Astrid," Cyrus breathed.

"—and has a documented history of disputes with naval authority. Remanding her to civilian custody risks flight. Remanding her to naval custody ensures the integrity of the evidence."

"Ensures its integrity," I repeated. "That's one way to describe a furnace."

Pellwith turned to me with the patience of a man swatting a fly slowly. "The witness will refrain—"

"Oh, I'll refrain. I've refrained across six hundred miles of mountains while your client's hired men shot at me. I can refrain another minute. But let the record show that the first act of this hearing was an attempt to put the witness in the pocket of the man she's here to accuse."

Voss's gavel came down once, not hard. "The record shows whatever I say it shows, Miss Vane, and I say it shows a motion which I am inclined to deny — provisionally. The witness remains in this chamber, in the custody of this court, for the duration. If the documents are genuine, they will be transferred to neutral hands at the close of proceedings. If they are not genuine, Miss Vane will wish she were merely a thief." She looked at me over her spectacles. "Do you understand the position you've put yourself in?"

"Your Honor, I've been in worse positions. But fewer of them had witnesses."

A ripple of laughter went through the gallery and died fast. Voss almost smiled. Almost.

"Proceed."

* * *

I had rehearsed this a hundred times — in the cave with Father Anselm, on the descent, in the safehouse at three in the morning with Cyrus asleep against the wall and the papers spread out under a candle stub. Rehearsal, it turns out, prepares you for the words and not for the weight of them. When the satchel came off my shoulder and onto the table, my hands shook. Not fear. Something older than fear. My mentor Tam used to say that grief and stage fright feel identical in the fingers.

"Your Honor. Members of the panel." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Eight years ago I worked salvage with a man named Tam Corrick. In the spring of that year he told me he'd found something in the wreck of the HMS Corvane that he couldn't sell and couldn't ignore, and that he was going to take it to someone who'd listen. Three weeks later he drowned at Kestrel Shoals. The official finding was diver error. I believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative, and because the alternative had no name I could point at."

Halloway had gone very still. Stillness, I was learning, was his version of a flinch.

"Last autumn I dove the Corvane again. In a sealed compartment the survey charts conspicuously omit, I recovered a strongbox. Inside were these." I lifted the oilcloth packet. "Ship's manifest amendments. Orders. Correspondence. And testimony — sworn statements from survivors of Marrow Bay, taken aboard the Corvane by her chaplain, witnesses the Navy recorded as lost at sea."

"Alleged testimony," Pellwith said smoothly. "Unverifiable documents, recovered by an unlicensed diver with an evident grudge, from a wreck anyone might have planted materials in. With respect, Your Honor, we could produce forgeries of equal quality by Thursday."

"You could," I agreed. "Yours would be better. You've had eight years of practice hiding the originals." I turned to the panel. "So don't take my word for anything. Take his." I nodded toward the doors. "Father Anselm Rooke. Formerly chaplain of His Majesty's frigate Corvane. Defrocked, I'm told, for asking too many questions about Marrow Bay. He took those statements himself. He signed each page. And he spent the blizzard in a cave above the Grayfell Pass confirming every name to me, one by one, because he has carried them in his memory for eight years like stones in his boots."

The doors opened.

Anselm had refused a coat for the occasion — said he'd worn his best cassock to worse places than a warm courtroom. He walked the length of the chamber slowly, favoring the knee the cold had ruined, and the gallery went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with respect for the court. People recognized something in him. Grief recognizes grief.

He reached the table, bowed to the panel, and laid his hand flat on the documents.

"These are mine," he said simply. "My hand wrote them. My seal closed them. I took the statements of eleven survivors of Marrow Bay aboard the Corvane in the week following the burning of that village. Eleven. There were more, but eleven lived long enough to speak." His voice caught once, and he mastered it. "They described soldiers under orders — orders bearing the signature of Commodore Halloway — firing on fishermen and their families at dawn, and then the burning of the boats so the accounts could not sail anywhere. The Corvane was carrying them to the capital to testify. The Corvane sank. The Navy's records list them as drowned with all hands. They were not drowned with all hands, Your Honor. They were drowned with a cover story."

"Father," Pellwith began, "you were defrocked for—"

"For insubordination. For refusing to surrender my copies of these statements to Commodore Halloway's aide. Yes." Anselm smiled, and there was no warmth in it at all. "I have been waiting eight years for someone to bring up my character. Ask me about Marrow Bay instead. I remember it perfectly."

Voss leaned forward. "Father Rooke, do you swear before this court that these documents are authentic, that you witnessed their creation, and that the orders therein bear the true signature of Commodore Halloway?"

"On my life, Your Honor. Which, given recent events, is not the idle phrase it once was."

* * *

Then it was Corven.

He came in uniform, which took a kind of courage I hadn't expected — walking into an admiralty hearing in the braid of the man you're about to bury. Lieutenant Dain Corven had tracked me across half the Grayfell Range on official orders, and had ended it by giving me his horse in a snowfield. I still didn't entirely understand him. I suspected he didn't either.

He swore in. Pellwith rose immediately. "Lieutenant, you serve at the Commodore's pleasure—"

"I served at his pleasure," Corven said. "As of four days ago I serve at the court's."

"Lieutenant—"

"He's right, Mr. Pellwith," Voss said mildly. "Continue, Lieutenant."

Corven testified the way he did everything — precisely, without decoration, each sentence set down like a stone in a wall. He described the order he'd received: recover stolen naval property from the woman Vane, by force if necessary, and ensure the documents were destroyed upon recovery. Not returned. Destroyed. He produced his written orders — he had kept them, God help him, he'd kept everything — and read the relevant line aloud in a voice that carried to the back of the gallery:

"'Recovery of materials to be followed by immediate and total destruction, per Commodore's standing instruction. No report of contents to be filed.'"

"Why would an instruction to destroy evidence accompany a lawful recovery?" Voss asked.

"With respect, Your Honor, I asked myself that question for two hundred miles." Corven's eyes flicked to Halloway — just once, just briefly — and came back. "Lawful property is returned. Only guilty property needs burning. By the time I understood which kind this was, I'd also read enough between the lines of my own orders to suspect the truth about Marrow Bay. I chose not to complete my assignment. I'll accept whatever follows from that."

"You'll accept a commendation if I have any say in it," Voss said, and made a note.

Pellwith tried twice more — Corven was disgruntled, Corven was insubordinate, Corven had conspired with the thief — and each time Corven answered with the flat patience of a man reading aloud from a ledger. Finally Pellwith sat down, and the silence that followed had a new texture. The gallery had stopped being an audience. It had become a jury.

Halloway's advocate called no witnesses. I remember thinking, as the afternoon light crawled across the stone floor, how strange it was that the most powerful man in the room had nothing to say. Power talks loudest when it's winning. Cornered, it goes quiet and counts its exits.

Voss gave closing statements short shrift. Pellwith spoke for ten minutes about forgery and grievance and the dangers of salvage divers with literary ambitions. Then she turned to me.

"Miss Vane. Anything further?"

I had prepared a speech. Standing there, with the papers on the table and Anselm at my side and Corven's testimony still hanging in the cold air, I threw it away.

"Your Honor. Eight years ago a man died trying to bring these pages to a room like this one. I spent eight years running from ports because staying anywhere felt like admitting he was gone. I crossed a mountain range with these papers sewn into my coat because I promised him — late, and badly, but I promised." I touched the oilcloth packet once, lightly, the way you touch a grave. "Read the names. That's all I ask. Read the names of Marrow Bay into the record of this court, out loud, where they can't be burned or sunk or buried again. Whatever else you decide, decide that."

Voss looked at me for a long moment. Then she drew the top document toward her, unfolded it, and began to read.

"Harrow, Edric, fisherman. Harrow, Sela, his wife. Harrow, Tam, aged six..."

She read all forty-one names. The chamber was silent except for her voice and the snow against the glass. Somewhere in the gallery a woman was crying without any effort to hide it, and nobody asked her to stop. Old Halvard's daughter, maybe. Or somebody's somebody. In a city of a hundred thousand people, every name found its listener eventually.

When she finished, Voss set down the page and said, in a voice like a verdict already written: "The panel will deliberate. Commodore Halloway, you will remain within the city and within reach of this court. Bailiff — the documents go to the secure archive, under joint custody of this office and the Crown prosecutor. Nobody burns anything tonight."

There was laughter in the gallery then, real laughter, tired and fierce, and Halloway's smile had finally cracked. He gathered his braid around him like a man gathering a cloak against weather, and as the bailiffs approached, he looked past them — past Voss, past me — toward the doors, calculating.

I knew that look. I'd seen it on sailors abandoning ship.

"Cyrus," I said quietly. "The quay."

He was already reaching for his coat.
