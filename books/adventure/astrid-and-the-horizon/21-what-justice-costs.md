## 21. What Justice Costs

The admiralty paid out in coin the way a wounded animal bleeds — slowly, grudgingly, and only after someone made it look at what it had done.

Three weeks after the hearing, a clerk with ink-stained cuffs and an expression of permanent apology met us in a government office that smelled of cold tea and older lies. He read out the terms from a paper he clearly hadn't written: the crew of the *Kestrel's Daughter* — because that was what we'd renamed her, and no one in that room had the nerve to say otherwise — was exonerated of all charges. Compensation for unlawful detention. Restoration of charter, with back-pay for the season Halloway's review had cost us. A formal apology, delivered by the clerk in a voice so flat it could have been used to calibrate instruments.

Bram stood beside me with his arms crossed, listening to the word *exonerated* like a man tasting wine he suspected was vinegar.

"And the commodore?" he asked.

"Commodore Halloway is remanded to custody pending court-martial," the clerk said. "The families' testimony has been—" he paused, searching for a word that wouldn't crack anything open, "—received."

*Received.* As if grief were a parcel you signed for.

We walked out into the street together, all six of us, blinking in the winter light like men released from a cellar. Sela counted the compensation figure twice on her fingers and then said a number out loud that made old Tam sit down on a bollard.

"That's enough," she said. "That's debts cleared, hull re-coppered, and a year's wages besides."

"It's blood money," Bram said.

"It's both," I said. "Most money is."

Tam looked up at me from the bollard, his weathered face working through something. "The captain's name," he said. "They put it in the gazette. Posthumous commendation. Distinguished service." He said it carefully, the way you'd handle something sharp. "Eight years late."

"He'd have hated it," Bram said.

"He'd have hated it *loudly*," Sela agreed, "and then he'd have framed it."

And that was true, and we all knew it was true, and we stood there in the cold laughing until it turned into the other thing, and nobody minded, because that's what crews are for.

* * *

I went south alone.

Maren met me at her door before I'd finished knocking — she'd seen me coming up the lane, she said later, by the way I walked. "Like a woman going to a funeral she's already been to once," was how she put it.

"You kept the coat?"

"In a chest, under wool, away from the damp." She fetched it and set it in my hands, and I sat down at her table and unpicked the seam along the lining with one of her sewing needles while she watched without asking questions, which was the finest quality she had.

The papers came out stiff with salt and cold but whole. Names. Dates. Orders in Halloway's own hand. And the letter — my mentor's letter, the one I'd read a hundred times now, in shepherd's huts and mountain caves and once, memorably, aloud to a defrocked priest who'd wept onto his own boots.

I folded them back into oilcloth. I didn't need to hide them anymore. But some things you keep close out of habit, and some things you keep close because they're yours.

"You'll stay the night," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"I'll stay the night."

She fed me fish stew and told me about the village — who'd married, who'd died, whose boat had gone aground on the shoals in January. Ordinary news. The news of people who had never once been blackmailed or shot at or declared enemies of the Crown. I listened the way a thirsty woman drinks, and somewhere past midnight I realized my shoulders had come down from around my ears for the first time since autumn.

"You could stop here," Maren said, banking the stove. "Plenty of wrecks off this coast. Good diving, if you don't mind the cold."

"I mind the cold."

"Mm." She blew out the lamp. "So did I, once. Then I stopped minding, and then I stopped leaving. Funny how that works."

In the dark, I thought about it honestly, which is more than I can usually say. I thought about a cottage, and a stove, and mornings where the most dangerous thing I did was argue with a fishmonger. It sounded — and this frightened me more than Corven's trackers ever had — *possible*. Not just possible. Easy. Like a berth I could slip into and never be missed.

I fell asleep before I decided anything, which is how I make most of my important decisions.

* * *

The grave was on a headland above a fishing churchyard two days' walk north, because sailors get buried where their people can see the water, and his sister had chosen well. You could hear the sea from the stone. He'd have approved — he always said a man should be able to check the weather from wherever he ended up.

The stone was new granite, gray as the sky behind it, and the lettering was still crisp:

**CAPTAIN ALDERIC VOSS**
*Sailor. Teacher. Troublemaker.*
*Distinguished Service, Posthumous*
*"He asked too many questions, and we are poorer for having answered so few."*

His sister had written the epitaph herself. She'd shown me the draft in the capital, hands shaking, and asked if it was too much. I'd told her it wasn't nearly enough, and she'd laughed and cried at the same time, which is a skill I hope never to need.

I stood there a long while with the wind coming off the water and my breath making little ghosts in front of me.

"Hello, old man," I said finally.

There's a particular foolishness in talking to graves, and I've never been able to talk myself out of it. Maybe that's the point. Maybe the talking isn't for them.

"They gave you a medal," I went on. "A real one, struck in silver, with your name spelled correctly, which given the navy's record with names is probably the greater honor. They read it out in a hall full of officers who'd have thrown you out of the service twenty years ago for saying half of what you actually said." I crouched down and brushed some lichen off the base of the stone, though it didn't need brushing. "You'd have loved every minute of it and told us all afterward what rubbish it was. I know. I'm counting on it."

I took out the oilcloth packet and unwrapped it, kneeling in the wet grass.

"I brought the names," I said. "You spent eight years chasing this. Chasing Marrow Bay, before any of us knew there was anything to chase. You thought I didn't notice. You thought I was just a girl learning knots." My voice did something unsteady and I let it. "I noticed."

And I read them.

All forty-one. Out loud, into the wind, one by one, the way the old woman at the hearing had said *it happened*, the way the priest in the cave had confirmed each one with his eyes closed. Fishermen and their sons. A naval surgeon who'd refused an order. Two women who'd rowed out to warn the village and been turned back. Children — three children, and I had to stop after the third and press my forehead against the cold stone and breathe until the world was a place I could speak in again.

When I got to the last name I sat back on my heels.

"There," I said. "That's all of them. Every one. Nobody gets to be a number anymore."

The sea kept doing what the sea does. Somewhere below the headland a gull argued with another gull about something neither would remember in an hour. It was not peace, exactly. Peace is a word for people who haven't buried anyone. But it was quiet in a way that didn't feel like waiting, and I found I could sit in it, and that sitting in it didn't make me want to run.

That was new.

* * *

He came up the path while I was repacking the oilcloth, and I heard him before I saw him because Cyrus Amberdale has walked like a man trying not to wake a house since the day I met him, and eight years and one bullet wound hadn't cured it.

"The sister said you'd be here," he said, stopping a respectful ten feet off. His arm was still in a sling, and he held himself like a man who'd recently learned exactly how much of him was held together by habit. "I told her I'd wait in the village. She said — and I quote — 'don't be a coward, boy.'"

"She sounds wise."

"She's terrifying. It's the same thing."

He came closer and stood looking at the stone, hat in his hand, and I watched him read the epitaph twice. His jaw worked.

"Troublemaker," he said. "Yes."

"He'd have liked that best of all of it."

"He'd have hated that I'm standing here." Cyrus said it quietly, without flinching, which was new for him. New since the bridge, maybe. Since the pass. Since the night on the high slope when fever stripped him down to the truth and he finally told me what really happened at Kestrel Shoals — that the line parted, that Alderic went under in seconds, that Cyrus surfaced alone because there was nothing else to surface *with*, and that he'd carried the salvage log ashore like a thief because grief makes cowards of even honest men and he'd never learned to tell the difference between guilt and crime.

I hadn't forgiven him. I want to be clear about that, in case anyone reading this expects the snow to melt and everything to be tidy. Forgiveness is not a door you walk through; it's a tide, and it comes in slow, and some days it goes back out further than it came. But I had stopped keeping the ledger. That was something. That might have been everything.

"They're refitting the ship," he said. "Sela's got her eye on a brig out of the yard — fast, ugly, exactly our taste. Bram's already arguing with the rigger about sails. Tam's teaching the new boy knots." A pause. "Your knots. He teaches them wrong on purpose so they'll ask questions and he can tell stories about you."

"That absolute villain."

"He misses you. We all—" He stopped. Started again, which he'd gotten better at. "There's a berth. There's always been a berth. But that's not why I came."

"No?"

"No." He crouched down beside me, awkward with the sling, and looked at the grave instead of at me, which I appreciated. "I came to say thank you. To him. For teaching me enough to eventually do one right thing, late, badly, at great expense." He touched the stone with two fingers, the way you'd touch a shoulder. "And to ask you a question I've been carrying since Stonewatch Bridge."

"Ask it."

"What will you do now?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since Maren's stove, since the hearing, since the moment on the quay when Halloway went into irons and the crowd made a sound I'd never heard a crowd make — not cheering, exactly. Something quieter and heavier. Relief with weight to it.

What would I do now?

The old answer was easy: sail. Keep moving. Stay ahead of whatever comes next, because staying still is how you get caught, and getting caught is how you die, and I had built a whole life on that arithmetic. Eight years of ports and seasons and never unpacking anything fully. Eight years of running from a wreck I couldn't name and a night I couldn't remember straight and a man I couldn't stop trusting.

But the arithmetic had changed. The wreck was named. The night was remembered. The man was sitting in the wet grass beside me with his hat ruined and his arm in a sling, waiting for my answer like it was the verdict at a trial.

"I don't know yet," I said, and it felt enormous to say it. "That's the honest answer. I've never once not known. There was always a next port, a next job, a next thing to outrun. Now—" I looked out at the horizon, flat and gray and patient. "Now the horizon's just the horizon. It's not going anywhere. It'll wait."

Cyrus nodded slowly. "And if it waits long enough?"

"Then maybe I'll stop somewhere. Properly stop. Unpack things. Learn what my own kitchen looks like." I glanced at him. "Maybe I'll stop somewhere and find out it doesn't suit me, and leave again, and that'll be fine too. Because it'll be a choice, not a habit. That's the difference. Running looks the same as sailing from the outside. Only the sailor knows which one she's doing."

"And which one are you doing?"

I stood up, brushed the grass from my knees, and looked at Alderic Voss's stone one last time. *Troublemaker.* The wind came off the water and moved my hair and smelled of salt and cold and the rest of my life.

"Not today," I said. "Today I'm going back to the village to drink something warm and terrible with his sister. Tomorrow I'm walking north. After that—"

"After that?"

"After that, Captain Amberdale, you can tell Sela I'll want first watch."

He smiled — the real one, the rare one, the one that had survived Kestrel Shoals and Halloway and a bullet at Stonewatch Bridge and was, against all odds, still pointed at me. He put his ruined hat back on and offered me his good arm, and we walked down the headland path together toward the lights of the village, leaving the old man to his weather report.

Behind us the sea kept its counsel. Ahead of us, somewhere north, a brig waited in a yard with her ribs bare and her future unwritten — ours, if we wanted her, and we did.

I had carried forty-one names over a mountain. I had set them down where they belonged. And for the first time since I was a girl learning knots from a troublemaker, the road ahead wasn't an escape.

It was just a road. And I was free to take it or not.

I took it.
