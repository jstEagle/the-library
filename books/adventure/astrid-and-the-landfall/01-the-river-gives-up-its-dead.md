## 1. The River Gives Up Its Dead

The Vellin has three moods, and I have made my living off all of them: the flat grey patience of high summer, the brown fury of the monsoon, and the sullen, hungover quiet that follows a night storm. It was that third mood we woke to on the morning everything started — or continued, depending on how much you believe rivers plan ahead.

I was on deck before dawn because that's when a captain earns his pay. Not steering, not shouting, just walking the length of the *Amberdale* with a cup of chicory going cold in my fist, checking lines, checking the bilge, checking the sky for what the storm had left behind. Forty years on this river teaches you that the water is a ledger too. Everything that goes in gets written down somewhere, and after a hard night it likes to read its accounts back to you.

That morning it read me a chest.

Tamir spotted it first, which was fitting, since Tamir spots everything first and pretends afterward he didn't. He was coiling the stern line when he went still in that particular way of his, like a heron deciding whether the fish is worth the strike.

"Captain," he said. "There's a thing."

"There's always a thing," I said. "The river's full of things."

"This thing's got a lid."

Well. That changed the arithmetic. A lid means intention. A lid means somebody, at some point, wanted what was inside kept in and the river kept out, and the river had disagreed.

It came down to us slow, turning, riding about half-submerged the way drowned things do — not floating, exactly, more like loitering. The storm had swollen the channel overnight and the current was running lazy and confused, shoving debris along in clots: branches, a fence rail, half a henhouse roof with the shingles still on. The chest moved among them with the unhurried confidence of something that had been waiting a long time and didn't mind waiting a little longer.

"Log," I said, mostly to myself.

"It's got hinges, Captain."

"A hinged log."

"You want to look or don't you?"

Here is where I should tell you about my feelings regarding drowned things, because Tamir already knew them and used them against me, as he does everything. My mother ran a ferry crossing upriver at Sorrel Bend, and she raised me on two commandments: never take a fare you can't carry, and never bring up what the river puts down. The second one wasn't superstition dressed as religion, or not only that. She'd seen men pull bodies from the current and never quite put them back down themselves. The river gives things up, but it doesn't give them up clean. Whatever they were carrying when they sank, they're still carrying.

So no, I did not want to look. But I was curious, and curiosity on a boat is like water in the bilge — ignore it long enough and it finds the seams.

"Grapple hook," I said, and hated myself for saying it.

* * *

We fished it aboard with the cargo net, which took some doing, because whatever was inside weighed like sin. Lead, we'd learn later. Lead lining, lead in the corners, someone had wanted that box heavy enough to stay down forever, and here it was on my deck dripping river water into the caulking seams, which felt like an omen with poor manners.

Up close it was worse than a log. It was a good chest — iron-banded, oak, the kind of work nobody does anymore because nobody expects anything to last anymore — and stamped into the lid was a seal.

I knew the seal. That's the part I keep coming back to. Every man my age on the Vellin knows that seal, the way every man my age knows the shape of a scar he got before he understood what scars meant. A bridge over water, crossed by a single star. Ostrava.

Twenty years gone. Twenty years since the maps stopped printing it, since the schoolbooks got their new editions with the borderlands drawn smooth and empty, since the word itself became the kind of word you say quietly, if at all, in the towns along the banks. Ostrava hadn't been a country so much as a stretch of river and the towns on it, and then it had been a trouble, and then it had been nothing at all. The governors who run the borderlands now are very respectable men. They have marble residences above the flood line and portraits painted of them looking thoughtful.

And here was their old seal, coughed up out of the mud at my feet like a bone from a dog's throat.

"Roll it back in," I said.

Tamir looked at me. Tamir is twenty-three and has the face of a man perpetually being told something interesting, which is either wisdom or insolence, and I've stopped trying to determine which.

"Captain—"

"It's drowned goods. It's bad luck. You know the rule."

"I know the rule. I also know it's got a lock on it, and locks mean somebody wanted it found eventually, or they'd have sunk it open." He crouched beside it, hands hovering, not touching. Even he wasn't eager to touch it. "Somebody sealed this on purpose, Captain. Sealed it heavy. That's not a man throwing away his own secrets. That's a man hiding somebody else's."

"And that's our business how?"

"It's not." He said it easily, which somehow made it worse. "But it's sitting on your deck, and it's not going to roll itself back overboard, and if we put it back, we'll spend the rest of the season wondering. You'll wonder loudest. You'll pretend you're not wondering, but you'll wonder at four in the morning when the engine's running sweet and there's nothing else to think about."

The boy knew me too well. That's the danger of hiring honest deckhands; they get familiar.

I stood there with my cold chicory and looked at the seal, and the seal looked back, the way seals do. And I'll admit something I didn't admit to Tamir: it wasn't only superstition holding me back. It was the other thing, the older thing, the thing my mother actually meant under the superstition. Drowned things come up attached to their histories, and I have spent forty years keeping my boat free of other people's histories. Passengers bring luggage; I prefer cargo. Cargo doesn't cry at night, doesn't ask questions at the wrong bends in the river, doesn't get people shot at checkpoints. A man who hauls freight on the Vellin learns early that the safest hold is an empty one.

But the chest was on my deck. And the river, having gone to the trouble of delivering it past every snag and sandbar between wherever-it-sank and my hull, seemed unlikely to accept a refund.

"We keep it until we find its owner," I said finally. "That's all. We ask around at Corben's Landing. Somebody upstream lost this, or somebody downstream wants it, and either way it stops being ours. Then it goes, and we go back to hauling timber and pretending the world's simple."

"That's the spirit," Tamir said, grinning.

"Don't grin at me. And don't touch it without gloves. Lead's poison."

"So's the river."

"The river I can steer around."

* * *

We stowed it in the forward hold, under the tarpaulin, behind a stack of salt barrels that I arranged with more care than the salt deserved. I told myself the arrangement was practical — ballast, balance, nothing to do with wanting the thing where I couldn't see it — and I almost believed myself, which is the trick I've built a whole life on.

By midmorning the storm's leavings had cleared and the Vellin had gone back to its grey patience, and the *Amberdale* was running south toward Corben's Landing with a hold full of salt, a deck full of drying nets, and one item of cargo that weighed nothing at all and dragged on everything.

Tamir spent the day finding reasons to walk past the hold. I spent the day finding reasons to stop him. Around noon he asked me what I remembered about the Hollowing, and I told him I remembered freight rates, and he gave me that heron look and let it lie, which was tactful of him, and which I appreciated, and which I will deny appreciating.

Because here's the truth, and I might as well say it once, quietly, to a page nobody may ever read: I remember more than freight rates. I remember the year the ferries stopped running north of Weir Nine. I remember the towns that emptied so fast the laundry stayed on the lines. I remember that my mother's rule about drowned things came from a specific winter, though she never said which, and I remember that respectable men signed papers that made all of it legal, and I have spent twenty years arranging my life so that none of those memories required anything of me. Distance is a skill. I'm very good at it. Ask anyone; they'll have to shout, I'll be so far away.

The chest sat in my hold anyway.

Toward evening the light went copper on the water and Corben's Landing showed itself around the bend — a straggle of wharves, a customs house with peeling paint, the usual congregation of boats and rumors. I brought us in easy, thinking about caulking money and nothing else.

Tamir appeared at my shoulder as we made the line fast.

"Captain," he said. "There's a woman on the dock who hasn't taken her eyes off the boat since we rounded the bend. Pale as a drowned candle. Been standing there an hour, by the look of her, and the wind's coming straight off the water and she hasn't once turned her collar to it."

"People wait for boats."

"Not like this. This one waits like the boat owes her something."

I looked down the gangway at the figure on the dock, and she looked up at me, and I felt the chest in my hold the way you feel weather coming — pressure behind the ears, a change you can't name yet.

"Find out what she wants," I said. "Carefully."

"She's already walking up the gangplank, Captain."

Of course she was. The river gives things up, and it doesn't give them up clean, and it seems it doesn't give them up alone, either.
