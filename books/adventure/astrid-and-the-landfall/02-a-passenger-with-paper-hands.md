## 2. A Passenger with Paper Hands

Corben's Landing smelled the way it always did — tar, frying fish, and the particular desperation of a town that existed only because the river happened to slow down there. I'd been tying up at Corben's for eleven years and had never once seen anyone arrive who wasn't already leaving.

The Amberdale needed caulking. That was the whole of my ambition that morning: forty feet of seam work, two barrels of oakum, and enough pitch to make my fingernails black until midsummer. Caulking is honest misery. You drive the fibers into the gaps between planks and you hammer them home, and when you're done the boat holds water out instead of in, which is the entire contract between a captain and his vessel. No history involved. No drowned nations. Just wood, fiber, pitch, and the river's opinion of all three.

I'd been at it since dawn, kneeling on the dock with a mallet and an iron, when the woman came down the gangplank road carrying a carpet bag and looking like she'd been assembled from spare parts by someone in a hurry.

That's unkind. Let me be fairer: she was pale the way paper is pale, thin the way a ledger page is thin, and dressed in the kind of grey traveling coat that says *I have money but resent spending it on clothes*. Her hair was dark, pinned up with what looked like two pencils, and her eyes went everywhere at once — to the wharf office, to the grey-uniformed toll clerk dozing over his tea, to me, back to the office — with the restless arithmetic of a person counting exits.

I kept hammering. In my experience, people who look like that either want something or are running from something, and both categories pay better than the ones who just want directions to the privy.

"Captain?" she said.

"Depends on the question."

"I'm told you run cargo downriver. Corben to Marrowgate."

"I run whatever fits in my hold and doesn't bite. Marrowgate's a long way."

"That's why I'm asking." She stopped a polite distance off, close enough to talk, far enough to bolt. "I need passage. For myself and one item of freight."

"What freight?"

"A chest. It came ashore here yesterday."

My mallet stopped mid-swing, which I regretted immediately, because her eyes fixed on it the way a hawk fixes on anything that moves.

"Came ashore," I said. "You mean it was fished out."

"The lock keeper said a riverboat pulled it up after the storm. He said—" she hesitated, choosing words like a woman crossing ice, "—he said it might have been yours."

"It was mine," I admitted. "For one night. It's sitting in my hold right now because Tamir talked me out of giving it back to the river, and because I'm too old to argue with deckhands before breakfast."

Something went through her face then. Not relief exactly. Relief relaxes a person. This tightened her, like a knot drawn snugger. "Then we can settle this quickly," she said, and reached into the carpet bag.

What she brought out was a document folder, oilcloth-wrapped, and inside it papers that were meant to impress me: letters of introduction, a shipping agent's stamp from somewhere upcountry, a bill of lading made out in careful copperplate. The name on them was Vant. Ilse Vant, archivist, attached to some institute whose letterhead I didn't recognize and whose seal had been stamped slightly crooked, as if whoever applied it had been in a hurry or had never used a seal before.

I read them twice. Not because they were complicated. Because they were too clean.

"You're an archivist," I said.

"I am."

"And these records belong to your family."

"They do." She said it steadily. Steadily is the tell, sometimes. People rehearse the steady answers.

"And your family's records were in a lead-lined chest at the bottom of the Vellin River."

"Families lose things in floods."

"Floods don't usually line their losses with lead."

Her hands, which had been folded neatly around the folder, shifted. And there it was — the thing I'd remember later, the detail that should have warned me properly instead of just amusing me. When she mentioned the chest again, her fingers trembled. Not much. A fine shiver, like a tuning fork touched lightly. She pressed them flat against the oilcloth to stop it, and they stopped, obediently, the way hands do when their owner has spent years making them obey.

"Captain Amberdale," she said, reading my boat's name off the transom rather than off any paper, "I'm not going to insult you by pretending this is ordinary freight. But I am going to ask you to treat it as such, and I'm prepared to pay for the privilege."

"How much?"

"Forty crowns to Marrowgate. Fifty if you leave tomorrow."

The number did what numbers do. Forty crowns was most of what the caulking would cost, plus coal, plus Tamir's wages, plus the margin I pretended to keep for emergencies. The Amberdale had been leaking at the stern seam since Weir Seven, a slow insistent seep that Maren called "the river paying rent" and I called "the river making threats."

"Sixty," I said, "and you sleep aboard, eat what we eat, and stay out of the engine room."

"Sixty is robbery."

"Sixty is caulking, coal, and wages. Robbery would be seventy."

She looked at me for a long moment, doing sums of her own, and I watched her decide that being robbed was cheaper than being caught. "Fifty-five," she said, "and I want it understood that no one opens the chest. Not you, not your crew, not a toll officer, not anyone."

"No one opens the chest," I agreed, "because no one needs to. Freight is freight. What's in it is between you and whoever you're delivering it to."

"And if someone asks?"

"Someone always asks. Then I lie, and you let me do the lying, because I've been lying to toll officers since before you could read a catalogue card."

That almost got a smile out of her. Almost. It got as far as the corners of her mouth and then thought better of it, which told me she hadn't smiled at much lately, and that she knew it.

We shook on it. Her hand was cold and dry and light as a bird's foot, and it shook again — once, briefly — before she mastered it. Paper hands, Tamir would call her later, and the name stuck, though he meant it more kindly than it sounds.

* * *

Tamir appeared an hour later with the caulking supplies and an expression I'd learned to distrust, because it preceded every idea of his that had ever cost me money or dignity.

"We're taking a passenger," he said, seeing the carpet bag already stowed in the cabin I'd grudgingly vacated.

"Passenger and freight. Sixty crowns."

"Fifty-five."

"Fifty-five," I conceded, because lying to your own crew about margins is a habit that ends with you believing your own books. "And before you ask — yes, that's the chest's owner. An archivist. Family records."

Tamir looked at the chest, which sat in the hold where we'd wedged it, lead-lined and patient, its Ostravan seal catching the lamplight. Then he looked at me. Tamir is twenty-three and has the gift of saying nothing loudly.

"Family records," he repeated.

"So she says."

"And you believe her."

"I believe she paid fifty-five crowns, which buys belief at any rate the market will bear."

He crouched by the chest and ran a thumb near the seal without touching it, the way you'd test a stove. "Edmund. This thing was nailed shut, wrapped in sailcloth, and weighted to sink. Families don't archive like that. Governments do. Or people hiding from governments."

"People hiding from governments also pay cash," I said. "Hand me the oakum."

He handed me the oakum, but he kept looking at the chest, and I kept not looking at it, and between the two of us we maintained a respectable fiction that the largest object in my hold was none of our business.

* * *

Maren came aboard at dusk, smelling of the Pilot's Rest and whatever the Pilot's Rest had been pouring, and made straight for the hold, because Maren treats locked doors as personal invitations.

I heard her stop. Heard the particular silence of a woman reading a seal she recognizes.

"Amberdale," she called, conversationally, "come look at this."

"I've looked at it."

"Look again."

I went down. She was standing with her arms crossed, and she tapped the seal with one blunt finger. "Ostrava. You know what that is."

"I know it's a country that isn't anymore."

"I know more than that." She turned, and the lamplight did her no favors; she looked every one of her sixty-odd years and then some. "I had a cousin married into an Ostravan family. Before the Hollowing. Before the maps changed. There were magistrates there, real courts, law that meant something, and then one autumn it all went quiet, and the quiet had signatures behind it." She looked at me hard. "This chest didn't drown by accident. Things get sunk so they'll stay lost. And things that get sunk so they'll stay lost come back up for reasons."

"Maren—"

"Don't 'Maren' me. Who's the woman?"

"An archivist. Family records."

Maren laughed, short and joyless. "Family records. In lead. At the bottom of a river." She shook her head and headed for the ladder, pausing at the top. "I'll pilot us downriver same as ever. But captain — when the questions start, and they will start, remember that I asked you first, and you answered me with a lie you borrowed from her."

She left me alone with the chest and the lamp and the slow seep of the stern seam, ticking into the bilge like a patient clock.

Here's the truth I didn't say to any of them, and barely said to myself: I didn't care what was in the chest. That's not quite right either. I cared very much, in the way you care about a sound in the night — not wanting to investigate, wanting only for it to stop. My whole life on the river had been built on a simple architecture: other people's histories on one side, my miles on the other, and a hull between them. Cargo comes aboard, cargo goes off, and nothing that rides the Amberdale gets to follow me home, because home is the wheelhouse and the wheelhouse goes forward, always forward, and forward has no past in it.

But the chest had come up out of the water on my watch. Superstition or sense, that made it mine in some way I couldn't invoice. Drowned things give themselves to whoever pulls them out. Every riverman knows it. The river doesn't release what it holds for free — it trades. And I'd stood on my own deck and taken the trade without knowing the price.

* * *

We cast off the next morning on the morning tide, which at Corben's is less a tide than a suggestion the river makes twice a day. Ilse Vant stood at the rail with her carpet bag clutched to her chest like a child, watching the town shrink, and I noticed she didn't relax until the last rooftop went under the horizon. Then her shoulders came down maybe half an inch, which for her was evidently a full surrender.

I put her to work by noon, partly because idle passengers brood, and mostly because Tamir had sprained something heroic hauling lines and needed the help. She took the direction awkwardly — archivists are not natural rope-handlers — but she took it, and she didn't complain, and when a coil slipped and caught her across the knuckles she bit down on the sound and coiled it again.

"You've handled freight before," I said, watching her.

"Catalogued it. Different muscles."

"Where's your institute, exactly? I didn't recognize the letterhead."

A pause. Half a second too long. "It's small. Private collection. We don't advertise."

"Private collections don't usually send people downriver after family chests."

"This one makes exceptions." She said it evenly, and her hands, busy with the rope, stayed steady, and that steadiness itself was wrong — too practiced, like a note held a beat past its natural end. She was better at it now than she'd been on the dock. She'd had a day to practice.

I let it go. Fifty-five crowns. Caulking, coal, wages. A man can afford a great deal of not-asking at those rates, and I intended to afford it all the way to Marrowgate.

But that evening, checking the hold before turning in, I found her there. She'd come down with a lamp, thinking herself alone, and she was standing in front of the chest with one hand raised — not touching, hovering a finger's width above the seal, the way you'd hover a hand above a grave marker or a sleeping animal. Her lips were moving. Not words I could catch. A prayer, or a list of names, or both; with the drowned, it's often both.

She heard me on the stairs and spun, lamp swinging, and for one unguarded instant her face was open as a wound. Then it closed, plank by plank, and became the archivist's face again, mild and reasonable and false.

"I was checking on my freight," she said.

"At midnight."

"I'm an early riser."

"It's past midnight. That makes you a late riser."

She set the lamp down carefully, as if the chest might be listening. "Captain. I'll ask you plainly, since plainness seems to be your currency. Does it matter to you what's in the chest?"

I considered my answer, and found, to my irritation, that I had three of them stacked up: the captain's (*no*), the businessman's (*not at fifty-five crowns*), and a third one, newer, rougher, that had no good reason for existing.

"No," I said, using the first one, and hating how easily it came.

"Good," she said. But she picked up her lamp and climbed past me on the stairs, and at the top she paused and said, without turning around, "Thank you for pulling it out of the water. Whoever did that — the river chose well."

She went up. I stood in the hold a while longer, listening to the stern seam tick, looking at the seal that some dead republic had pressed into lead twenty years ago, back when pressing seals into lead was still worth doing.

Two days later, in the narrows below Halloway Bend, we'd see the first of the grey boats — low, unmarked, running without lights at dusk, holding our wake a half-mile back like a debt waiting to be collected. But that evening I knew nothing yet except that my passenger lied badly, my pilot disapproved, my deckhand brooded, and my boat leaked.

Of all of them, the leak was the only problem I knew how to fix.
