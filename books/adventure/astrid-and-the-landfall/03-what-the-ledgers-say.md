## 3. What the Ledgers Say

Captain Amberdale gave me the cabin under the wheelhouse because it was the only one with a door that latched, and I think he believed the latch was a courtesy. It was not. A latch is an invitation to be alone with yourself, and I had spent eleven years avoiding exactly that.

The cabin smelled of engine oil and old tea and the particular dampness of a boat that has never once been dry. There was a bunk with a blanket the color of regret, a shelf of charts curling at their edges like leaves in autumn, and a lamp bolted to the wall so it could not be stolen — or perhaps so it could not escape. I set the chest on the bunk, sat on the chart stool, and did not touch it for a long while.

Above me, the Amberdale's deck crew went about their evening in the language of thumps and rope. Tamir's laugh came through the planking like weather. The captain's boots crossed overhead at intervals, unhurried, a man pacing his own small kingdom. Somewhere aft, Maren was smoking something that made the whole river smell of cloves.

I had told them I was an archivist's daughter retrieving family records. This was true in the way that a match is a fire. My grandfather had been an archivist of nothing; he had been the last Chief Magistrate of Ostrava, and the records in this chest were not family papers. They were the state's confession, and I was its granddaughter twice over — by blood and by cowardice, because my family had run when the republic fell, and running had become our only hereditary trade.

But I am getting ahead of myself, which is what I do instead of sleeping. Open the chest, Ilse. You have been rehearsing this moment since you were nine years old.

* * *

The lock was Ostravan work, which is to say it was elegant and slightly impractical, and it opened for a key I had worn on a cord around my neck since before I understood what it opened. Inside, the lead lining had done what lead does: kept everything cold and secret and faintly funereal. Three ledgers, bound in grey leather gone green at the corners, each stamped with the seal — the bridge over the water, the seven stars above it, the motto that schoolchildren used to sing without hearing: *The River Remembers Its Course.*

I want to tell you that my hands were steady. They were not. They shook the way they had shaken on the dock at Corben's Landing when Edmund Amberdale watched me touch the seal and politely pretended not to notice. He notices everything, that man. He simply files it all under *not my business*, which is either wisdom or a wound; I hadn't decided yet, and wouldn't for some time.

I opened the first ledger.

The early pages were almost boring, which surprised me. Requisitions. Docket schedules. A magistrate's complaint about the price of lamp oil in the winter of the last year of the republic. My grandfather's handwriting appears there — I would know it anywhere, that careful slanted script, the way he crossed his sevens because his own teacher had done it — signing off on grain shipments, on minor disputes, on a fine levied against a fishmonger who had shorted the market weights. A man governing a country one herring at a time. I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, and I pressed my hand over my mouth as if the river outside might hear.

Then the dates turned. Then the ink changed.

It changed in a way I had read about but never seen: the official ledgers of Ostrava's final months were kept in duplicate, and the second set — the set that was never supposed to survive — recorded not what the government did but what it was ordered to do, and by whom. The magistrates who kept these pages knew they were writing evidence. You can feel it in the hand. The letters get smaller. The entries get shorter. Nobody wastes ink on a confession they expect to die for.

*14th of the Reed Month.* Order issued from the Governor's Residence at Vellinholm: districts four through nine of the capital to be "cleared of seditious elements." Signed: **C. Bray, Secretary of Interior Security.**

Cassel Bray. Who now held the governorship of the entire borderlands, who had been photographed last spring opening a hospital wing, whose portrait hung in every customs house between here and the delta with the caption *A Steward of Order.*

I turned the page because stopping was impossible, the way you can't stop watching a flood come up your street.

*17th of the Reed Month.* Directive concerning the disposal of persons detained at the Hollowing. The word *disposal*. As though they were lamp oil. As though they were herring. Signed: **C. Bray.** Countersigned by two men whose names meant nothing to me until I read the marginal note in my grandfather's hand: *Both now sit on the Border Commission.*

There were numbers. That was the worst part, worse than the orders themselves. The magistrates had counted. Someone had stood in the aftermath with a tally-stick and a face like stone and written down how many. District four: two hundred and six. District five: three hundred and forty. The Hollowing itself — the quarry pits outside the capital where the "disposal" happened — the figure ran past four thousand and then stopped being itemized and became an estimate, and somewhere in that transition between exact and approximate, the arithmetic of murder revealed itself as obscene no matter how carefully you did it.

My grandfather's marginalia threaded through all of it. *Photographed where possible. Names taken from detainees' effects. Copies sent to Rook.* Anselm Rook. The name landed in me like a dropped anchor. Judge Anselm Rook, the last man empowered under Ostravan law to convene a tribunal — the man my grandfather had trusted with the location of the duplicates, the man who had escaped into exile in Marrowgate when everyone else who might have spoken was silenced or sunk.

And the chest. The chest had been sent downriver on the last night, aboard a mail packet that never reached the delta. Twenty years it had lain in the Vellin's brown dark while the men who signed those orders grew old and respectable and rich. Twenty years, and then a storm, and then a superstitious captain with a hook and a line, and the river — which remembers its course, which apparently remembers everything — gave it back.

I closed the ledger and discovered I was crying, which annoyed me deeply. I had budgeted for grief later. Grief later was scheduled. But the body keeps its own docket, and mine had ruled against me.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being the granddaughter of a dead republic: you inherit the debts without the land.

My father never spoke of Ostrava. He spoke around it, the way you speak around a burn scar. We lived in Corben's Landing under a name that wasn't ours, and he worked as a clerk in a shipping office, filing other people's cargo manifests with a diligence that looked, to anyone who didn't know, like temperament. It was penance. He had been nineteen when the republic fell, and he had gotten on a boat, and he had spent the rest of his life not drowning in the fact of it. When he died — quietly, of his heart, in a room that smelled of ink — the only thing he left me besides the key was a sentence, delivered once, when I was grown and pressing him: *"Your grandfather stayed. Remember that someone stayed."*

Someone stayed, and wrote it all down, and put it in a box, and sent it to the water for safekeeping. And the water kept it. And now it was mine, sitting on a bunk in a stranger's boat, smelling of lead and river mud, heavier than any object I had ever lifted.

I thought about the captain. I thought about how he'd said, when I hired him, *I haul cargo, not causes,* and how his eyes had flicked to the seal and away again, quick as a card sharp. I thought about Tamir, who had argued to keep the chest until its owner was found — *you don't just throw a drowned thing back, Captain, that's asking for trouble* — and about Maren, who had looked at the seal for one long moment and said nothing at all, which from a woman of her age on this river meant she remembered something.

I thought about the grey boats I'd seen holding station two miles back since noon, patient as gulls behind a fishing net. Edmund had seen them too. He'd said nothing, but he'd altered course by two points without announcing it, and he'd checked the fuel gauge twice.

They knew. Of course they knew. Bray's people had known about the chest before I did, probably — some clerk in some archive had finally connected a twenty-year-old manifest to a missing magistrate's granddaughter, and now there were men on the water with rifles and a mandate that left no paperwork. I had led them straight to it. I had walked onto the most conspicuous boat on the river and paid cash and lied badly, because I am an archivist's granddaughter and not, whatever else I am, a spy.

So the choice arranged itself with terrible clarity, the way choices do at two in the morning when the lamp is low and the truth is out of its box and cannot be persuaded back in.

Option one: give the chest to the river. Roll it overboard somewhere deep, let the Vellin keep its secrets another twenty years, go back to Corben's Landing and live out my father's life after him, filing other people's paper. Safe. Cowardly. The kind of choice that looks like peace and is actually just a slower form of drowning.

Option two: keep running downstream with it, alone, badly, until the grey boats caught up, and die in some reed bed with the evidence, which would accomplish nothing except adding my name to the ledger's margins in someone else's hand.

Option three.

Option three was the one where I stopped being a courier and started being what my grandfather had been: a magistrate. Not by appointment — the appointments had all drowned — but by the only mechanism left, which is faith. Somebody keeps the law alive by keeping faith with it, or it dies, and dead law is just ink, and ink is just soot and oil, and the men who signed those orders know that better than anyone. That is the whole architecture of their power. Not guns — guns are the furniture. The architecture is the belief, spread thin and quiet across twenty years, that Ostrava is over, that its law died with it, that the past is a country that no longer issues passports.

Well. I had a passport. It was stamped with a bridge and seven stars, and it was three hundred pages long, and it was sitting on my bunk.

I took out the notebook I always carried — archivist's habit, my father's habit, probably my grandfather's before us, we are a dynasty of people who write things down — and I began to copy. Names, dates, signatures, numbers. Page references. If the chest went into the river or into a fire, there would be at least one more copy in the world, in a hand that shook and then, as the night went on, gradually stopped shaking.

Somewhere past midnight I heard the captain's boots stop directly overhead and stay there. A long pause. Then they moved on. I don't know what he heard through the planking — the scratch of my pen, or nothing, or the sound a person makes when they stop crying and start working — but I remember thinking: he knows too. He's known since Corben's Landing. He's just waiting to see whether I'm worth the trouble.

Fair enough, Captain. So am I.

* * *

Near dawn the river fog came up thick enough to drink, and with it came the sound I had been dreading and half-expecting: an engine, somewhere upstream, running at the low steady thrum of a boat holding position rather than going anywhere. Then a second. Then a whistle — two short blasts, the signal for *heave to, prepare for inspection* — hanging in the fog like a question nobody wanted answered.

I blew out the lamp and sat in the dark with my arms around the chest, listening to the boots cross overhead, faster now. Below me, in the hold, three hundred pages of dead men's testimony slept in lead. Above me, a man who hauled cargo and not causes was climbing to his wheelhouse to decide what we all were now.

Two short blasts again, closer. Patient. Certain.

Edmund Amberdale rang the engine bell — slow ahead — and I felt the boat lean into the current, away from the whistle, away from the channel, toward the shallow water where honest boats don't go.

I put my hand flat on the chest's lid, on the seal, and whispered the only prayer my family had ever taught me, the one my father said at night like a password: *The river remembers its course.*

Apparently, so did the captain.
