## 4. Grey Boats at Dusk

I have a rule about boats that follow you. It's the same rule I have about dogs that follow you, and women who follow you, and debts that follow you: sooner or later they want something, and by then it's too late to pretend you didn't notice them.

The grey boat appeared an hour past Corben's Landing, just as the sun was going down behind the western bluffs and turning the Vellin into a long ribbon of hammered copper. I spotted it in my stern glass while pretending to check my wake, which is what captains do when they don't want their passengers asking questions. A low, flat-hulled thing with no name on its bow and no flag on its staff and no business being on this stretch of river at all. The Vellin above Weir Nine belongs to loggers, fish traps, and fools like me. Government traffic keeps to the channel below.

"Tamir," I said. "Company."

He took the glass without being told — that's why he's my deckhand and not somebody else's — and studied the shape for a long moment. "Grey."

"I see it's grey."

"No markings. No running lights, and it'll be dark inside the hour." He lowered the glass. "That's not a fishing boat, Captain."

"Fishing boats don't usually come in pairs." Because there was the second one now, sliding out from behind a willow island half a mile back, matching our speed like it had been doing it all day. Maybe it had. Maybe I'd been so pleased with myself about the chest and the caulking money and the general state of my affairs that I'd missed it entirely. That was possible. That was, if I'm honest, probable.

Ilse Vant came up from below decks with a book she wasn't reading. She'd been down there most of the afternoon with the ledgers, though she thought I didn't know that. A woman who handles paper the way she does — like it might bite her, or save her — doesn't spend four hours alone with a locked chest out of scholarly devotion.

"Trouble?" she asked, and her voice did a fine job of sounding merely curious. Fine enough that I almost believed her. Almost.

"Two boats back there," Tamir said, because Tamir has never learned the value of a comforting lie. "Unmarked. They've been shadowing us since the narrows opened."

She went very still. People think stillness means calm. It doesn't. Calm people keep moving; scared ones freeze, like rabbits deciding whether the fox has seen them. Ilse Vant froze.

"They're government?" she asked.

"Nowhere on this river is government," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

Let me tell you something about the narrows above Weir Nine, since we were making for them and since it matters. The Vellin comes down off the high country through a cut of slate and old flood gravel where the channel pinches to less than forty yards in places, and the water runs fast and mean over a bed of snags and drowned fence posts from farms nobody remembers. The charted channel hugs the eastern bank. Every pilot on the river runs it, because every pilot on the river learned from the same three dog-eared charts, and because the western side looks like death: a shelf of water barely four feet deep over gravel bars and one submerged stone wall that some farmer built two hundred years ago to spite his neighbor, or God, or both.

Every pilot except me. My father ran the shallows once, in '09, carrying grain he hadn't paid for, and he showed me the line before he died: hard past the leaning alder, then a lazy S through the gap in the stone wall where the spring floods knocked a hole, then straight down the throat of the gravel shelf with your heart in your teeth and your hull singing. You lose paint. You maybe lose a plank if you flinch. But no one follows you, because no one else knows the way, and the ones who try learn the river's opinion of trespassers the hard way.

I'd run it twice in ten years. Both times sober. Both times swearing I wouldn't again.

The light was failing. The grey boats were closing. And I had a hold full of drowned history and a passenger whose hands shook around seals, and I found myself doing arithmetic I didn't like: whatever those boats wanted, they wanted it badly enough to work the narrows at dusk, and men who want things that badly at dusk are not planning to ask politely.

"Heave to signal," Tamir said. "The lead boat. They're showing a lantern. Two up, one down."

That's river police grammar. Stop your engine. Prepare for boarding. I'd seen it used on smugglers, on debt-runners, once on a floating chapel whose congregation got rowdy. Never on an honest cargo boat hauling timber and one nervous archivist.

Except I wasn't honest cargo anymore, was I? I was the man who'd hauled an Ostravan chest out of the river and then taken silver from a woman who lied about it with all the grace of a dropped plate.

"Captain," Ilse said quietly. "If they board us—"

"If they board us, they take the chest, and probably you along with it, and me for good measure, because dead witnesses float and live ones talk." I said it flatly, watching the lantern signals repeat. Two up, one down. Getting impatient. "So before I decide anything, madam, you're going to tell me the truth. Not all of it. I haven't got fuel for all of it. Just this much: who wants your family records badly enough to send gunboats?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Dusk was coming down between us like a curtain, and I could see her deciding — I've watched a lot of people decide things on my deck, whether to jump, whether to confess, whether to lie to me one more time — and I watched her choose.

"Not my family's records," she said. "Not exactly. They're the republic's. Ostrava's. Magistrates' ledgers, sealed the night before the Hollowing. There are men downstream — respected men, governors, men with statues — who signed orders in those books. Orders that would hang them if anyone read them aloud in front of the right people." Her chin came up. "Someone wants them drowned again. Permanently."

"And you're the archivist's daughter."

"I'm nobody's daughter," she said. "I'm the last magistrate's granddaughter, and if those boats catch us, everything my grandfather bled for ends in this river a second time."

Well. There it was. I stood at my own wheel with the copper light dying off the water and felt the exact weight of the moment settle onto my shoulders, which is a feeling I know well and have never once enjoyed. Every man gets a moment like this, maybe two or three in a whole life, where the road forks and both branches are bad and you pick one mostly on instinct and spend the rest of your days explaining the choice to yourself at night.

I could heave to. Let them board. Hand over the chest, hand over the woman, collect my story afterward about how I was just a simple captain minding my own business. That was the sensible branch. That was the branch my wife — never mind. That was the branch that kept a man alive and small.

Or I could run the shallows.

"Tamir," I said. "Get forward and watch for the alder. Ilse — below decks, strap yourself to something, and if you hear grinding, pray to whatever magistrate you like."

"You're not going to stop," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Madam, I've been stopping for other people's troubles my whole life and it's cost me everything I ever loved. Tonight I appear to be starting the opposite habit." I rang the engine bell twice. "Hold on to your ledgers."

* * *

The narrows swallowed us at last light. The channel proper glowed faintly ahead — other pilots' water, safe water, watched water — and I swung the Amberdale hard to port instead, straight at the black mass of the western bank, and heard Ilse make a small sound below like a woman reading a very bad ledger entry.

"There's nothing over there but gravel," Tamir called from the bow.

"There's a channel over there. It's mine. Mind the alder!"

The leaning alder rose up white and skeletal on the left, exactly where my father said it would be, twenty years of floods having failed to finish the job the lightning started. I took the boat past it close enough to slap the branches, and then the bottom came up under us the way it always does — sudden, personal, offended — and the Amberdale began to sing.

That's the only word for it. Gravel under a flat hull makes a sound like a choir of dragged furniture, a long shuddering groan that goes through your boots and your teeth and your opinion of yourself. Paint first. Then pride. I held the wheel in the lazy S through the gap in the old stone wall — there, the hole, wider than I remembered, thank the river for its small mercies — and then straightened her out down the throat of the shelf with the depth gauge pegged at nothing useful and my whole body leaned toward the bow like I could pull us through by wanting it.

Behind us, a lantern swung wildly. Voices carried across the water, sharp and angry. The lead grey boat had followed us toward the bank — of course it had; they had orders, and men with orders don't think — and then stopped thinking about it real fast when its keel found the same gravel we were riding. I heard the impact carry across the narrows, a crack and a grind and then a great deal of profanity in an official accent.

The second boat held back. Smarter crew, or a smarter captain, or just slower. Its lantern swept the shallows, hunting our line, and found nothing but white water and dark, because the line isn't visible unless you already know it, and knowing it had cost my father a season in jail and cost me a childhood of listening.

We came off the shelf with a lurch that threw Tamir against the rail and sent something below decks crashing — hopefully crockery — and then the deep water of the lower narrows took us and the Amberdale ran clean and fast and free, and I let out a breath I felt I'd been holding since Corben's Landing.

"Paint," Tamir reported, coming aft. He held up a sliver of green planking, our starboard quarter, sacrificed to the stone wall. "And pride."

"Pride grows back." I checked astern. One grey boat aground and blinking its lantern in fury, one grey boat standing off in the main channel, unwilling to commit. By morning they'd have skiffs and local guides and a warrant with my name spelled wrong on it. By morning we needed to be fifty miles gone. "But she doesn't. Get the patch kit. And Tamir — find out what that crash below was before the lady files a complaint."

"It was the chest," Ilse's voice floated up the companionway, shaky but alive. "It fell against the bulkhead. Everything's... everything's fine."

Everything was not fine. Everything was a woman with a false name and a true inheritance, two government boats with no government markings, a holed quarter-plank, and a hold full of evidence that could topple governors — or get a riverboat captain and his crew fed to the fish. I knew all that. I catalogued it, the way I catalogue everything, miles and costs and tonnage.

And underneath the catalogue, quiet as silt, sat another fact I refused to enter into any ledger: when the fork in the road came, I hadn't even hesitated. I'd run the shallows for a stranger and a drowned chest, and part of me — the part I keep below the waterline, where I don't have to look at it — had been glad.

"Captain," Tamir said softly, beside me at the wheel. "Those boats. They'll come again."

"Yes."

"And next time they won't signal."

"No," I agreed. "Next time they'll shoot." I put the wheel over for the dark stretch below the narrows, where the current runs strong and the banks offer nothing but reeds and shadows, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that I was smiling. "Best make miles, then. While miles are still ours to make."

We ran black water all night. And somewhere behind us, patient as the river itself, the grey boats regrouped in the dark.
