## 5. The Toll at Weir Nine

My name is Tamir Osk, and before this summer I had told exactly three lies in my life, all of them small, all of them to my mother, and all of them about whether I had eaten. She always knew. Mothers know. The river knows too, I used to think — the river sees everything that floats on it and remembers everything it swallows — so a man who works the water ought to keep his accounts clean, because one day the water calls in its debts.

That was my philosophy, such as it was, right up until the morning a toll officer at Weir Nine looked me in the eye and asked me what we were carrying.

* * *

We came down out of the narrows with our paint scraped to bare wood along the port side and Captain Amberdale sulking like a man who'd been insulted by geography. He doesn't sulk out loud. He sulks the way stone sulks: silently, over years. But I'd worked for him two seasons and I could read him the way Maren read clouds, and everything about him that morning said *we are not done running yet.*

Ilse Vant stayed below decks. She'd been staying below decks since the grey boats, coming up only for meals she barely touched, her paper hands wrapped around tin cups of tea gone cold because she forgot to drink them. I brought her bread once and found her asleep over an open ledger, cheek on the page like a child at a school desk, and I didn't wake her, but I did look.

I shouldn't have looked.

Columns of names. Dates going back twenty years and more. And beside some of the names, in a clerk's careful hand, a single word in old Ostravan script that even I recognized, because my grandmother used to say it when she swept the floor of our house in Corben's Landing, sweeping toward the door as if pushing something out of it: *hollowed.* Emptied out. My grandmother said it about houses where everyone had died or left. In those ledgers it was written next to whole villages.

I closed the book and put the bread down quietly and went back up on deck and thought very hard about nothing for a while.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about complicity: it doesn't arrive like a storm. It arrives like damp. You don't notice it until your bones ache with it.

* * *

Weir Nine sits where the Vellin drops through a cut limestone sill, and the lock there is older than any of the nations that claim the river now. Two gates, a stone chamber big enough for four boats, and a tollhouse built of the same grey rock, squatting on the bank like a toad that has decided to stay. The toll officers wear the borderlands' green-and-cream and carry stamped ledgers of their own, and mostly they want their fees and a look at your manifest and then they wave you through, because the river moves too much commerce for anyone to inspect it properly.

Mostly.

The captain sent me ashore with the fee money, which was itself unusual — he handles the tolls himself as a rule, because he trusts no one else's arithmetic and no one else's mouth. But his face was known now, he said. Grey boats meant reports, reports meant descriptions, and Edmund Amberdale's face is not the kind you forget: carved out of dark oak, with eyes that make customs men feel audited. Mine, apparently, was forgettable. I chose to take this as a professional compliment rather than a personal one.

"Vessel?" said the officer at the desk. Young. New boots. A ledger open in front of him and a stamp lying beside it like a sleeping animal.

"*Amberdale,* out of Corben's Landing."

"Cargo?"

"General freight." This was true. We had bales of cotton cloth, crates of lamp oil, sacks of rice, a consignment of plough blades. "Passenger, one."

He wrote it down. I watched the stamp. It did not move.

Then he looked up, and I saw that behind him, half-hidden by a shelf of manifests, stood another officer — older, grey-whiskered, wearing the same uniform but wearing it differently, the way a man wears a coat he's grown into rather than one he's been issued. The young one glanced at him, got whatever answer passed between them without words, and turned back to me.

"We'll need to inspect," he said. "Standard hold inspection. All cargo."

"That's not standard," I said, because it wasn't, and because honesty was still, at that moment, my native language. "I've run this lock six times a season for two years."

"New regulations." He said it flatly, the way you say something you've been told to say. Behind him the older officer came forward, unhurried, and set both hands flat on the counter.

"Ostrava," the older man said.

I blinked. "Sir?"

"The seal. On the chest your passenger brought aboard at Corben's Landing. Lead-lined, brass-bound, stamped with the double-tower seal of the Republic of Ostrava." He recited it like a laundry list, which somehow made it worse. "A fisherman upstream reported it. A drowned chest comes up after a storm, and within a week a pale woman with soft hands pays gold for downstream passage. Gold, mind you, not silver. That's a story with legs, boy. Stories like that walk all the way to the capital if nobody stops them."

I want to tell you that my heart hammered and my palms went slick and I felt the terrible vertigo of a good man about to do wrong. Some of that happened. But underneath it, quieter and stranger, was something else — the memory of my grandmother sweeping toward the door, saying *hollowed*, and the look on Ilse Vant's face when she slept on names of the dead like a child sleeps on a schoolbook, learning them into her skin.

The river sees everything, I had always believed. Well. Let it see this.

"Hold inspection," I said. "Of course. Come aboard whenever you like. Though I'll warn you, our bilge smells like a crime against nature, and the captain will bill you for the boots."

* * *

Here is what I knew, walking back down the gangplank faster than dignity allows: the ledgers were in the aft hold, wrapped in oilcloth, under a false floor panel that Captain Amberdale had installed years ago for smuggling tobacco during a brief and unhappy period when he tried to make money instead of just enough. And here is what I also knew: the hold inspection would find them, because the false floor was the second place anyone would look, and these were not men looking casually.

And here is what I saw, halfway down the plank, that saved us or damned us, depending on how the accounting comes out in the end: the funeral barge.

It lay moored below the lock, waiting its turn, draped in white mourning cloth with garlands of marigold going brown at the edges. Delta custom — the dead travel home to the water that made them, and a coffin bound for Marrowgate rides with honors no officer wants to delay. There's a superstition, even among the borderland men who pretend otherwise, that hindering a corpse's journey home brings the hindrance back on your own house. Every boatman knows it. Every toll officer was a boatman once.

I stood on the plank with the sun hot on my neck and lied for the fourth time in my life, and this time to myself: *this is not really stealing, this is not really treason, this is just moving a box from one hiding place to a better one.*

Then I went and did it.

* * *

The coffin was in the barge's stern cabin, attended by a professional mourner — a delta trade, women paid to weep convincingly, though this one sat eating rice with great composure and nodded to me like a colleague. I explained, in the fluent nonsense of a man improvising, that our vessel carried a passenger's family effects that needed secure stowage, and might we rent space in the mourning compartment, and here was silver, and here was more silver.

She looked at the silver. She looked at me. Professional mourners, I have since learned, miss nothing; grief is their business and they know every shape a lie can wear.

"You're hiding something from the tollmen," she said, not unkindly.

"I'm hiding something *in* a coffin from the tollmen," I corrected. "Which is different."

"It is worse," she said. "But it is also traditional." And she took the silver, and helped me shift the lid, and asked no questions beyond one: "Is it yours?"

"No," I said. "But it should be somewhere safe, and I don't think anywhere is safe anymore except with people who've decided to make it safe."

She considered this while I wedged the oilcloth bundle down beside the body — an old man, dried flowers on his chest, a face at peace in a way I envied. "You're a strange one," she said. "Honest hands doing dishonest work. It shows, you know. When you lie, your ears go red."

"Noted," I said. "I'll work on it."

"Don't," she said. "Better they know you're new at it. Men who lie easily are the ones who'll sell you." She put the lid back herself, tucking the marigolds straight, and patted the wood twice, gently, as if apologizing to the occupant. "Travel well, grandfather," she murmured. "You're carrying passengers now."

* * *

They searched the *Amberdale* for two hours.

I served them tea on deck and answered questions with the truth wherever the truth was harmless, which is, I have decided, the entire art of lying well: build the wall out of true bricks, and only the mortar is false. Yes, we took on a passenger at Corben's Landing. Yes, she brought luggage. No, I couldn't say what was in it — I'm deck crew, not a customs service. Yes, the captain ran the shallows above the narrows, because the captain runs the shallows whenever he feels like it, ask anyone, he's famous for it, it's practically a religious practice with him.

The young officer wrote everything down. The old one wandered the deck touching things — the rail, the winch, the awning canvas — the way a blind man reads. Twice he stopped near the aft hatch and I felt my ears go hot and hated them for it, and poured tea, and talked about caulking compound until he moved on.

In the end they found the false floor panel, because of course they did, and pried it up, and found beneath it exactly what the captain had left there for them: forty pounds of contraband tobacco, three seasons stale, wrapped in oilcloth with a bribe already tucked into the wrapping, because Edmund Amberdale plans for inspections the way other men plan for weather.

The old officer held the tobacco up to the light, and then held the bribe up to the light, and looked at me for a long time.

"Confiscated," he said finally. "And a fine. Your captain can pay it at the office." He dropped the tobacco back into the hole as if it were something dead. Then, quietly, so the young one couldn't hear: "There was a chest. Lead-lined. Where is it?"

"On the bottom of the Vellin," I said, "where drowned things belong."

Something crossed his face — not belief, not quite, but a kind of tired recognition, the look of a man hearing an answer he'd expected and didn't know whether to be relieved or disappointed by. He closed the panel with the toe of his boot.

"My father came out of Ostrava in '09," he said, almost to himself. "Never talked about it. Died talking about it, actually, at the end, when the filter of a lifetime wore through." He straightened his uniform. "Lock opens in twenty minutes. Tell your captain the fine is payable in silver, and that Weir Nine gets very busy around inspection season, and busy men miss things."

He walked away down the gangplank. The young officer followed, glancing back once at me, at my ears, which I could feel burning like signal lamps.

* * *

The lock chamber filled, the gates groaned open, and we dropped through the sill into the lower river with the funeral barge a cable's length ahead of us, white cloth bright against the brown water, riding slow and dignified toward the delta.

Captain Amberdale stood at the wheel and didn't speak for a long time. When he did, it was one word: "Well?"

"They took the tobacco. Fine's payable in silver. And the ledgers are traveling first class in a coffin, escorted by a professional mourner who thinks I have honest hands and bad technique."

He looked at me then — that long, stone-slow look, the audit look — and whatever he found in my face seemed to satisfy him, or at least to be filed for later review. "You lied to a toll officer," he said.

"Yes, Captain."

"And to a mourner."

"Negotiated with a mourner. Lied to the officer."

"Mm." He turned back to the river. "Maren says four days before the monsoon closes the gorges. We make the gorge mouth by tomorrow night or we wait out the flood in the reeds like muskrats." A pause. The wheel ticked under his hands. "Tamir. What you did back there — I'm not thanking you for it. Because if I thank you for it, then it becomes the kind of thing we do. Understand?"

I understood. I understood perfectly, and I understood also that he was thanking me, in the only dialect he spoke fluently, and that from this day forward it *was* the kind of thing we did, all of us aboard, the captain included, whatever he called it.

"Aye, Captain," I said.

Below decks, faintly, I heard Ilse Vant turning pages.

Ahead, the funeral barge rounded the bend, and the white cloth caught the light, and for one strange moment the whole river seemed arranged around it — the water, the banks, the brown hills, the small stubborn boat following in its wake — as if the dead man in the coffin were piloting us all somewhere, and we had only agreed to come along.

Then the bend took them, and the gorges opened ahead, black and narrow, and the first far-off thunder of the monsoon rolled down out of the mountains like a door being locked behind us.
