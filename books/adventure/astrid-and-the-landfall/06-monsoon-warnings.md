## 6. Monsoon Warnings

Maren read the sky the way other people read letters from estranged relatives — with a kind of grim satisfaction, like she'd known all along it would end badly.

We were tied up at a fueling dock below Kessel Bend, the kind of place that sold diesel, rope, and opinions in roughly equal quantities, when she climbed out of the pilothouse, squinted east for a long moment, and spat over the rail.

"Four days," she said.

"Four days until what?" I asked, though I already knew. You always know. That's the trick of living on a river — you spend half your life knowing things you'd rather not.

"Until the monsoon's leading edge comes down off the highlands and closes the gorges." She pointed her chin at a band of cloud sitting on the eastern horizon like a debt collector. "That's not weather yet. That's weather *thinking about it*. When it makes up its mind, the Vellin rises six feet in a day and the gorges turn into a meat grinder with water in it. No pilot runs them after that. Not me, not you, not any fool who's ever told his crew he knows a shortcut."

I looked at the cloud. It looked back.

"Four days to Marrowgate," I said. "Through the gorges."

"With a patched hull, a passenger who lies like a child with jam on her face, and a deckhand who still says good morning to the river like it's going to say it back." Maren shrugged. "I've run worse odds. But I wanted you to hear it from me before you heard it from the sky."

That was Maren all over. She never gave you bad news so much as she gave you the courtesy of being present when it arrived.

* * *

Ilse Vant came up on deck an hour later looking like she hadn't slept, which was fair, because neither had I. The chest sat in my cabin under a tarp, and every time I walked past it I felt the way you feel walking past a dog that's watching you eat — not threatened exactly, but *noted*.

She stood at the rail beside me without speaking for a while. Downriver, the grey water ran flat and patient. Somewhere behind us, presumably, were more unmarked boats. Somewhere ahead of us was a gorge full of drowned rocks and rising water. And somewhere in between was whatever it was about this woman and this chest that made armed men chase riverboats through the narrows.

"You said your family's records," I said. "Back at Corben's Landing."

"Yes."

"That was a lie."

"It was—" She stopped. Her hands did their shaking thing, the paper-hands tremor I'd noticed the first day, and she pressed them flat against the rail as if pinning them down. "It was mostly a lie. The records are what I said. They're just not my family's records in the way I let you believe."

"I'm listening," I said, "but I'll be honest, my listening rates went up after the grey boats."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and died of exposure. "My name isn't Ilse Vant."

"Of course it isn't."

"My name is Ilse Vant Ostrava. Vant is my family name. Ostrava is—" She exhaled. "Ostrava is everything else. My grandfather was Aldous Vant. He was the last chief magistrate of the republic. When they dissolved the government twenty years ago, he was one of the men who carried out the archives. The ledgers in your cabin are part of what he saved. I'm his granddaughter."

I let that sit. A riverboat captain learns patience the way a stone learns smoothness — slowly, and by having a lot of things rub against him.

"The magistrates hid evidence," I said. "Of what?"

"Of the Hollowing." She said the word carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "The purge. The one they don't teach in schools anymore because the men who ordered it wrote the new curriculum. Three towns along the upper Vellin emptied in a single season. Officially, resettlement. Unofficially—" She turned and looked at me directly, and I'll give her this: she didn't flinch. "There are mass graves above Weir Nine. The ledgers have the orders. Signed. Dated. Counted."

"Counted?"

"They counted, Captain. That's the worst of it. They kept books. Grandfather used to say tyrants' greatest gift to history is their own bookkeeping."

I thought about the chest coming up out of the river after a night storm, lead-lined, seal-stamped, sitting on my deck like a question I hadn't asked for. I'd nearly rolled it back overboard. Superstition, I'd told Tamir. Drowned things want to stay drowned. And here was the woman who'd paid me good money to haul the drowned thing to safety telling me it wasn't cargo at all. It was testimony.

"And where does it go?" I asked. "This testimony."

"Marrowgate. There's a man there. Judge Anselm Rook." She watched my face when she said the name, and I suppose she saw something, because she pressed on. "He was the third magistrate on the high bench. The only one who escaped. He's been in exile in the delta for twenty years, old now, ill, I'm told. But under the old Ostravan statutes he is still — technically, legally, if anyone cares to honor it — empowered to convene a tribunal. To hear evidence. To issue warrants."

"Warrants against whom?"

"Against the men who signed." She said it quietly. "One of them is Governor Cassel Bray. Of the borderlands. The man whose river police tried to board us in the narrows."

Well. There it was. I've spent thirty years on the Vellin perfecting the art of not knowing things — not knowing which crates hold contraband, not knowing which passengers are running from husbands or creditors or worse, not knowing why a fare pays cash and doesn't haggle. Not-knowing is a skill, and I was a master of it, and this pale archivist with her paper hands had just taken a crowbar to the whole discipline in under five minutes.

"You understand," I said, "that what you're describing is suicide with extra steps."

"I understand that if we don't reach Marrowgate before the monsoon, the gorges close, the season turns, and Governor Bray has months to find us. And that if the ledgers die with us, then the Hollowing worked. The massacre becomes resettlement forever. The dead stay nameless because the only names left are in a chest on a broken boat, and the boat sank." She looked downriver, toward the delta she'd never seen and I'd seen too many times. "Judge Rook is seventy-eight years old, Captain. Whatever tribunal he convenes, it has to be while he's alive to convene it. There is no second chance. There isn't even a second witness. Just me, and the books, and four days."

* * *

I called the crew together that evening on the aft deck. All three of us, which tells you something about the scale of the conspiracy — a captain, a pilot, and a deckhand, versus a governor, his police, and the entire legal architecture of two nations.

Tamir sat on a coil of line with his knees up, nineteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who'd been consulted on matters of consequence before. Maren leaned against the rail with a tin cup of something she claimed was tea. Ilse stood apart, holding herself like a woman awaiting sentencing.

I laid it out plain. The chest, the ledgers, the Hollowing, Bray, Rook, the gorges, the four days. I didn't dress it up. Dressing things up is for funerals and invoices.

When I finished, nobody spoke for a moment. Then Tamir said, "So the coffin at Weir Nine—"

"Had ledgers in it instead of a body, yes."

"Huh." He nodded slowly, absorbing this. "I lied to a toll officer."

"You did."

"For this."

"For this."

Another nod. "Good," he said, with the fierce simplicity of the young, and I felt something in my chest I didn't have a name for and didn't go looking for one.

Maren drank her tea. "Rook," she said at last. "Anselm Rook. I knew a deckhand served on his barge once, back when he still held court on the water. Said the man fined his own clerk for spitting off the bench. Fair, but he'd fine God if God came before him without an appointment." She set the cup down. "If there's one man alive who'd take those books seriously, it's him. If there's one man alive who'd take *us* seriously for bringing them — well. That's a longer conversation."

"So we're agreed?" I said. "Marrowgate. Through the gorges. Ahead of the monsoon."

"I didn't say agreed," Maren said. "I said the sky agrees, and the sky doesn't negotiate. Four days. We leave at first light and we don't stop except to fuel, and we pray the hull holds, and if it doesn't hold, we drown with our principles intact, which is more than most people get."

"That's the most encouraging thing you've said all year."

"I know. I've been saving it."

* * *

I couldn't sleep, which surprised no one, least of all me. Around midnight I found myself in the pilothouse with a lamp turned low, and ten minutes after that Ilse appeared in the doorway with two cups of coffee that tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said.

"I sleep fine. I just do it standing up, in the dark, holding a wheel." I took the coffee anyway. "You should rest. Tomorrow starts four days of the worst water on the river."

"Captain. May I ask you something honest? Since we're apparently done lying to each other."

"One honest question per customer. House rule."

"Why did you keep the chest? That first morning. Tamir told me you almost put it back in the river. What stopped you?"

I looked out the window at the black water sliding past the hull. The truth was I'd asked myself the same question maybe forty times since Corben's Landing, and I'd come up with forty answers and discarded thirty-nine.

"A drowned thing came up out of the Vellin carrying a seal from a country that doesn't exist anymore," I said finally. "And every instinct I have said roll it back, Edmund, some doors open onto rooms you can't walk out of. But I kept thinking — somebody sealed that chest hoping it would be found. Not by anybody. By *somebody*. Somebody specific, someday, who'd know what it meant. Twenty years it waited down there. And then it comes up, out of all the rivers and all the mud, into the path of my boat." I shrugged, feeling foolish. "Seemed rude to send it back down. Like refusing a letter because you don't like the handwriting."

Ilse was quiet for a while. "My grandfather used to say something like that. That evidence is a letter the dead write to the living, and most of them go undelivered."

"Your grandfather sounds like he'd have made a decent river captain."

"He'd have been terrible at it. He couldn't swim." She smiled, small and real, the first one I'd seen from her that wasn't running for its life. "Thank you, Captain Amberdale. For the delivery."

"Don't thank me yet. Thank me in Marrowgate, if we make it, and if Judge Rook doesn't fine us for arriving without an appointment."

Downriver, the eastern horizon had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Four days. One gorge. One old judge with a dead nation's law in his pocket and, if Maren's stories were true, a fine schedule for everything including miracles.

I turned the wheel a degree I didn't need to turn it, just to feel it answer, and thought: *some doors open onto rooms you can't walk out of.*

Then I rang for Maren, and we ran for the delta ahead of the rain.
