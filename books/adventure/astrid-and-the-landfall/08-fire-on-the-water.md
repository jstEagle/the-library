## 8. Fire on the Water

There is a particular quality to dusk on the Vellin near the charcoal wharves — a brown-gold light that comes off the kilns and lies on the water like something spilled. The river folk call it the burning hour, and they mean it as a warning: everything you can see clearly at that hour can also see you.

I was thinking about caulking when Maren said her one word of the day.

"Smoke."

Maren rationed words the way a miser rations coin, so when she spent one, I paid attention. I looked upstream past the last of the wharves, where the kiln smoke hung in its usual grey shroud, and found what she'd already seen: a column that didn't belong. Kiln smoke stands still and sulks. This column moved. It moved the way ambition moves — fast, straight, and with no interest in anything in its path.

"Grey boat?" Tamir asked from the bow, where he'd been mending a net he had no intention of ever using.

"Grey boat," I said.

The *Amberdale* was running light, which was the only mercy left to us. We'd taken on coal and water at the charcoal settlement an hour before, and Ilse Vant had spent the whole stop below decks with her ledgers, emerging once to ask me — in the careful voice of a woman asking about weather — how long until Marrowgate. I'd told her three days if the river behaved. Neither of us believed the river would behave. We were past pretending otherwise; we were just two people on a boat, each carrying something the other needed, sailing toward the same storm for different reasons.

Now the storm had grown an engine.

"How far?" I asked Maren.

She held up four fingers, then folded one down. Three miles. Maybe less. A patrol launch could do eight knots against our five and a half loaded, which meant it would have us inside twenty minutes, and the narrows above the charcoal flats were too open to lose anyone in. I'd used my one trick already, back at the shallows. Rivers have long memories, but so do the men who police them.

"Edmund." Ilse stood at the cabin door, and I noticed she'd stopped bothering to hide the ledgers under her coat. That was new. That was either surrender or resolve, and with her it was hard to tell the difference. "Is it them?"

"It's whoever Bray sent," I said. "Same thing."

"You could give me up." She said it flatly, the way you'd read out a figure from a ledger. "You could put me in the skiff with the chest and let them take us. You'd be clean. You'd keep your boat."

I looked at her for longer than the situation allowed. Twenty minutes, maybe less, and I was spending ten seconds of it studying a woman who offered herself up like ballast to be jettisoned.

"First," I said, "they don't want you. They want what's in the box, and then they want nobody left who's seen it. Second, I don't run a ferry service for people I'm planning to sell. Third—" I turned back to the wheel. "Third, get below and tell me where the marsh channels start."

"The what?"

"She means the reed beds," Tamir said, grinning through the netting. "Captain's got a map in his head. Scary place. Full of things that bite."

"Below, both of you," I said. "Tamir, bring the skiff's cover canvas forward. If we're going to be shot at, I'd rather not make it easy."

Nobody argued, which told me how scared they were. A crew argues when it still believes the captain might be wrong. Silence means they've done the arithmetic themselves.

* * *

The grey launch came on exactly as I expected it to, which is the most frightening thing a pursuer can do. No feints, no games. Captain Ferro — and I knew by then it would be Ferro, because Bray doesn't send anyone else twice — ran her straight up the channel with her gun mount manned and a loudhailer crackling.

"*Steamer Amberdale. Heave to or we fire.*"

The voice was a woman's, level as a surveyor's line. I'd heard about Ferro for years — the Governor's own, they called her, the only officer on the Vellin who'd never been caught taking a bribe, which on this river made her either incorruptible or the best-paid crook afloat. From the sound of her, I suspected the first, and that was worse. A crook you can buy. A believer you can only outrun.

"Heave to, Edmund!" she called again, and there it was — my name, my boat's name, my whole unremarkable history recited back at me across two hundred yards of brown water. Somebody had briefed her thoroughly. That frightened me more than the gun.

"Maren," I said quietly, "the reed cut. Past the third kiln. Is it still there?"

Her eyes did something I'd never seen them do — they widened, a full millimeter. "That's smugglers' water. Nobody runs it since the flood year."

"I'm nobody," I said. "Take the tiller."

She took it without another word, and I went forward to the engine while Tamir crouched behind the canvas with his eyes enormous. Below, I could hear Ilse moving — not hiding, I realized. Stacking. Putting the ledgers into oilcloth, into the bilge space under the floor plates, doing in thirty seconds what I should have ordered days ago. Whatever else she was, whatever lie she'd boarded under, the girl worked like someone who'd already decided how this ended.

The first shell hit the water fifty yards astern and threw up a column of spray that came aboard warm as bathwater. The second walked closer. Ferro wasn't trying to kill us yet — she was measuring, letting me know the ladder had more rungs.

Then Maren put the wheel over and the *Amberdale* turned toward the kilns, and I understood we were no longer being chased. We were being hunted, and the difference is that hunted things are allowed to run anywhere.

"Fire at will," Ferro's voice said behind us, almost regretful. Almost.

* * *

I will not pretend the next ten minutes were navigation. They were violence conducted with a helm. The reed cut opened off the main channel just past the third kiln — a slot in the wall of rushes barely wider than my beam, invisible unless you knew the drowned fence post that marked it, which Maren evidently did, because she took us in without slowing and the world closed around us like a fist.

Reeds six feet over our funnel. Water the color of steeped tea. The channel bent every forty yards, and half those bends were lies — dead ends that opened onto nothing but more reeds, sumps where boats went to die quietly and be eaten. Somewhere behind us the grey launch's engine note changed, dropped, hesitated. Ferro had reached the mouth of the cut and discovered what I'd known: she could follow, but she couldn't follow fast, and a gunboat in a reed channel is a whale in a creek.

A rifle cracked from her foredeck anyway. The bullet went through the canvas beside Tamir's head and out the other side, and Tamir — bless him, curse him — popped up like a gopher to look at the hole.

"Down," I roared, and he went down, laughing, the little idiot, laughing because he was nineteen and bullets were still a story other people told.

The second rifleman was better. Or luckier. The shot came through the reeds at the second bend, low, and I heard it hit wood — real wood, structural wood — and felt it in my boots before I heard Tamir cry out.

He was sitting against the deckhouse with his hand clamped high on his left shoulder, and between his fingers the blood came bright and shocking against all that brown water and green reed, the first red thing I'd seen all day.

"Tamir—"

"I'm fine," he said, in the voice people use when they are discovering whether it's true. "It went through. Edmund, it went *through*, I can feel the air on both sides—"

"That's not the comfort you think it is." I was already beside him, tearing the sleeve off my shirt, wadding it against the entry wound while he hissed. Behind us, faintly, the grey launch grounded somewhere with a crunch and a shout of voices — Ferro's boat, wedged in the soft bottom, buying us the hour we needed and hating every minute of it.

Ilse came up the companionway with the medical kit I hadn't known we had and knelt in the blood without a flicker. She bound him with quick, competent hands — field dressings, proper ones, the kind you learn to use somewhere specific — and I filed that away with everything else I wasn't saying out loud.

"Hold this," she told Tamir, pressing his own hand over the knot. "Press. Hard. Yes. You'll live. It missed the bone and it missed the—" she paused, "—everything important."

"You've done this before," I said.

"Read about it," she said, not looking at me, and lied beautifully for the first time since I'd known her.

* * *

We ran the cut until dark, Maren steering by feel and memory through water that had no right to be remembered at all, and when the reeds finally thinned into open marsh we dropped anchor in a pool no chart has ever admitted to and let the engines die.

The silence afterward was enormous. River silence always is — it isn't absence of sound, it's the sound of everything holding its breath.

Tamir sat propped against the deckhouse with whiskey inside him and morphine from Ilse's kit threatening to join it, insisting loudly that he could still work a winch one-armed. His shoulder was dressed in white that was already going pink at the center. Nineteen years old, and I'd brought him to this.

I sat down across from him in the dark and said the thing captains say.

"I'm sorry."

He looked at me like I'd spoken in tongues. "For what?"

"For the bullet."

"Edmund." He said my name the way he said it when he wanted to be the adult in the conversation, which was increasingly often and increasingly annoying. "You didn't shoot me. The lady with the gun shot me. And she shot me because we're carrying something worth shooting at, and I knew that when I hid the ledgers in the coffin at Weir Nine, and I'd do it again tomorrow." He shifted, winced, grinned. "Besides. Chicks dig scars."

"What in God's name is a chick?"

"Dunno. Heard a trader say it. Sounded good."

I laughed — actually laughed, out here in the dark with my boat holed below the waterline, my deckhand shot, my hull patched with hope and pine board, my passenger a liar with excellent field-dressing technique, and a police captain named Ferro presumably extracting her launch from the mud with murder in her brief. Laughing felt like theft. I did it anyway.

Maren appeared at my elbow with the lamp turned low and pointed at the hull. "Holed," she said. Two words. Generous night for her. "Above waterline. Patch holds if we don't fight the river."

"And if we do?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and in the lamplight her face was older than old, older than the river, some kind of patience carved into it that I didn't understand yet and would pay dearly to understand later.

"River wins," she said. "River always wins. Question is what it lets you carry across first."

* * *

I found Ilse at the stern rail near midnight, standing watch over nothing, wrapped in a blanket against a heat that didn't warrant it. The marsh breathed around us — frogs, wind in the reeds, the far-off mutter of the main channel where, come morning, Ferro would be waiting at every sensible exit with every sensible plan accounted for.

"She'll know these waters eventually," Ilse said, without turning. "Won't she."

"Sooner than I'd like. There aren't many ways south from here, and she knows I know them."

"Then why did she stop chasing tonight?"

I thought about it honestly, which is a luxury I rarely afford myself. "Because she lost us fair, and because she's the kind of officer who'd rather catch you whole than catch you sorry. And because—" I stopped.

"Because?"

"Because she asked for me by name, and she hasn't fired on the boat itself. The hull shots were warnings. She wants the chest and the witnesses gone, but she wants the *Amberdale* intact, and I've been wondering all evening why a man like Bray would care about my boat."

Ilse was quiet a while. Out in the black water something large moved and subsided.

"When I hired you," she said, "at Corben's Landing. I told you the chest was my family's records."

"You did."

"That part was true. Nearly all of it was true. Just not the parts that mattered." She turned then, and in the low lamp her face had none of the tremble I'd first taken for weakness. It had been fear, back then. It wasn't anymore. Fear burns off, and what's left underneath is something you build fires with. "Ask me tomorrow, Captain. Tomorrow I'll tell you where we're really going, and who's really waiting, and why four days matters more than you know. Tonight I'd rather you slept angry at me than awake hating me."

"I don't hate you, Miss Vant."

"No," she said softly. "But you're starting to believe in this. I can hear it. And believing is worse for men like you — it costs more." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Get some sleep, Edmund. Tomorrow the river gets narrower."

I stayed at the rail after she went below, listening to the marsh count its frogs, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the first shell fell astern. One deckhand shot. One hull holed. One police captain between us and the delta, four days of monsoon closing over the gorges like a book shutting, and somewhere behind all of it a governor in marble who had signed his name in my river's blood and wanted the page back.

Every mile south, the price had gone up. I'd spent my whole life believing that was the trade — distance bought with danger, paid in installments, settled at journey's end.

But lying in my bunk that night, listening to Tamir breathe slow and even through the bulkhead, I caught myself thinking in a currency I didn't recognize. Not miles. Not fares. Something smaller and heavier, like coins counted out one at a time in the dark.

In the morning, Maren woke me before dawn with her hand on my shoulder and one word, and this time it wasn't smoke.

"Boats," she said. "Plural."
