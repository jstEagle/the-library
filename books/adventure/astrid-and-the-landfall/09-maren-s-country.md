## 9. Maren's Country

I have been running the Vellin for fifty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching: the river always wins, the river always lies, and the river keeps everything you throw into it. People think that last one is a comfort. It isn't. The Vellin is not a keeper of treasures. She is a keeper of debts.

So when the Amberdale limped out of the reed marshes with a hole below her waterline and Tamir bleeding through a wadded shirt, I did what I had been doing since the grey boats first showed up at dusk: I stopped thinking like a pilot and started thinking like a woman with somewhere to go that nobody else knew about.

"Take the next cut," I told Edmund.

He looked at me over the wheel. The man has a face like a locked ledger — everything entered, nothing readable — but even he was doing arithmetic. "The next cut isn't on any chart."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

"You know it how?"

"Fifty-one years," I said, "and a reason."

He didn't ask what the reason was. That's the thing about Edmund Amberdale that people get wrong when they call him cold. He doesn't ask because he knows every answer costs something, and he's too careful a man to spend other people's money. I respected it. I also wasn't going to let it kill us, so I took the wheel myself and put her nose into the cut.

* * *

The channel behind the reed islands is not hidden so much as it is uninteresting, which is better. Charts show open water and a note about unstable bottom, and no chart-reader alive has ever volunteered to verify an unstable bottom. We went in at half throttle with Tamir grey as ash on the deck boards and Ilse Vant — or whatever her name truly was; I'd been watching her hands, and archivists don't hold their fingers like they're holding a knife — crouched over him, keeping pressure on the shoulder wound and talking to him in a low stream of words that might have been Ostravan. Old Ostravan. The kind they don't teach anymore except in kitchens.

That's how I knew, before we ever reached the settlement, before the elder ever saw the seal. You can burn a country off a map. You cannot burn it out of a grandmother's mouth.

The hull groaned the whole way in. Edmund had stuffed the breach with a mattress, two life vests, and — I am not inventing this — a crate of pickled eels, and the patch held the way patches hold, which is to say out of spite. Every hundred yards I expected the river to take her from under us. Every hundred yards she didn't, and I found myself almost fond of the stubborn old tub, which is a dangerous way to feel about a boat. Boats are like men that way. The ones you love are the ones that nearly drown you.

"Smoke," Ilse said suddenly, pointing east where there shouldn't have been smoke.

"Cook fires," I said.

"There's nothing out here."

"There's everything out here," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

You have to understand what this country was before you can understand what it is. Twenty years ago, when Ostrava went under — and I use *went under* deliberately, because countries drown the same way people do, slowly and then all at once — the borderlands filled up with the runoff. Soldiers who wouldn't sign the new oaths. Clerks with the wrong grandfathers. Fishermen whose villages got renamed by men in clean coats. Some fled downriver. Most couldn't afford to flee anywhere, so they did the next best thing: they disappeared into the marshes, married each other, built houses on stilts above the flood line, and raised children who spoke two languages, one for the market and one for the kitchen.

And in the kitchens, they kept telling stories. About the republic. About the magistrates. About the night the Hollowing came to the river towns and the water ran strange colors for three days. The governors' histories say those stories are sentimental nonsense. The governors' histories were written by the men who gave the orders, which is a detail I recommend checking whenever anyone offers you a history.

My own mother was born in Ostrava. My father ferried refugees out of the delta during the last bad winter, and when the river police caught him, they didn't shoot him — shooting makes martyrs, and martyrs make trouble. They made him an example instead, quietly, the way respectable men do things, and my mother took me into the marshes and never once said his name aloud again until the day she died, and then she said it eleven times.

So yes. I knew the cut. I'd known it since I was nine years old, paddling behind my mother's skiff while she taught me which reeds marked deep water and which ones meant a sandbar that would eat your keel. Everyone in that settlement knew my mother's name. Nobody in the world outside had heard it in twenty years.

That is Maren's country. Not mine by ownership. Mine by burial.

* * *

We came around the last bend at dusk, and there it was: forty-odd houses on stilts, lantern-lit, strung along a horseshoe of high ground the charts insist does not exist. A boatyard. A drying shed full of fish. Children on walkways overhead, staring down at us with the frank rudeness of children everywhere.

Edmund brought the Amberdale alongside the yard's cradle ramp like a man setting down something fragile, and I'll give him this — he looked at the settlement for a long moment before he said anything, taking in the stilt houses and the Ostravan script painted over the boatshed door, faded but legible, and I watched him do the same arithmetic I'd done at his age.

"This whole place," he finally said. "Off every map."

"Maps cost money," I said. "Staying forgotten is free."

A crowd gathered the way crowds do in small places — not rushing, just accreting, one neighbor telling another, until there were thirty people on the walkways and a big woman with forearms like dock rope pushing to the front. Sera Voss. Boatwright, midwife, arbiter of disputes, and the closest thing my country has to a government, which suits us fine because governments are how the Hollowing happened.

"Maren Kestrel," she said, and my mother's surname in her mouth hit me harder than I expected. "Fifty years, and you come back with a hole in your boat and a hole in a boy."

"And a chest," I said.

Her face changed. Not much. But Sera Voss reads water for a living, and she read mine.

"Bring the boy first," she said.

* * *

They carried Tamir up to the surgery house — a clean room over the drying shed, run by a former army medic named Pell who'd deserted during the second purge year and had been stitching smugglers ever since. I sat outside on the stairs because old pilots know when they're furniture, and through the window I watched them work on him: Pell cutting away the shirt, Sera boiling thread, Ilse holding the boy's hand and murmuring in that kitchen-Ostravan, steady as a metronome.

Edmund stood apart, arms crossed, watching the settlement the way he watches weather. Cataloguing exits, I expect. Counting boats. The man trusts nothing that hasn't been weighed, measured, and given a receipt.

"You could rest," I told him.

"I could," he agreed, and didn't move.

After a while he said, quietly, without looking at me: "How many places like this are there?"

"On this river? More than the governors would sleep well knowing." I shrugged. "Nobody counts us. That's the arrangement."

"The chest," he said. "The seal. They'll know what it means here."

"They'll know more than you've guessed, Captain." I looked at him straight. "You've been hauling other people's history around for a week now and treating it like cargo. It isn't cargo. It never was. Down here, that chest is a grave marker somebody finally dug up."

He absorbed that the way he absorbs everything — silently, at interest. Then Tamir cried out inside, once, and Edmund forgot philosophy entirely and was through the door before I could stand up. Cold man, Edmund Amberdale. Cold as river ice, and about as easy to see through if you know where the current runs.

* * *

They patched the hull over two days. I won't bore you with caulk and canvas, except to say that Sera's crew worked like they were repairing a relative — which, in a sense, they were, because the Amberdale was carrying something that belonged to them, though none of us yet knew how much. Tamir mended faster than any shot boy has a right to, being nineteen and stubborn, and by the second evening he was sitting on the walkway eating fish stew and letting the settlement girls fuss over him, which he bore with the heroism of the very young.

It was the second night that the elder came.

Old Dossan. Ninety-one years, blind in one eye, deaf in both directions when it suited him, and sharp as a fishhook in between. He'd been a court clerk in the capital before the end — the last generation that understood what Ostrava's law actually was, not the flag-and-anthem version but the machinery underneath. He came down to the boatshed slow, leaning on a stick, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts for a piling.

"That's it?" he said. "That's the chest?"

Ilse opened it. I'd seen the ledgers before — leather-bound, lead-lined case, the twin-tower seal of Ostrava stamped in wax gone dark as dried blood. I'd felt their weight. But I'd felt it as weight. Dossan felt it as something else entirely.

He put his hand flat on the top ledger and stood there a long moment, and the whole boatshed went quiet enough to hear the river against the pilings. Then the old man began to weep. Not delicately. Great, honking, undignified sobs, the kind you'd be embarrassed by in anyone under eighty, and nobody laughed, because everyone in that shed understood they were looking at twenty years of grief finding its receipt.

"They told us it was lost," he managed. "The magistrates' records. They burned the archive — I saw the smoke from the bridge — and after that we told our children stories and called it memory, and memory is nothing, memory convicts no one—" His hand pressed flatter on the cover. "What is written is written. What is written can be *read*."

Ilse knelt beside him. And there, in front of the whole settlement, she said: "My grandfather was Magistrate Corven Hale. He signed the order hiding these. I'm bringing them to Judge Rook in Marrowgate."

Dossan turned his good eye on her, and I swear the old man grew two inches.

"Then you're not an archivist," he said. "You're an heir."

* * *

Later, Edmund found me on the walkway outside the surgery house, smoking my pipe, watching the lanterns double themselves in the black water.

"She lied to us," he said. "From Corben's Landing. The whole time."

"Yes."

"You knew."

"I suspected. Her hands told on her the day she boarded." I knocked out my pipe against the rail. "Question is what you do about it."

He was quiet long enough that a night heron went past, low over the water, trailing its reflection.

"When Ferro's boats came at the narrows," he said at last, "she could have told me to surrender the chest. Saved us the shallows, the scrape, the bullet in my deckhand. Instead she paid for passage and kept lying, and when the shooting started she worked the pumps till her fingers bled." He shook his head slowly. "I keep trying to file her under *deceiver*, and the file won't close."

"Maybe it's the wrong drawer," I said.

He looked at me.

"Captain," I said, "you've spent your whole life believing the past is something that happens to other people, downstream, behind you. Well. Look around." I gestured at the stilt houses, the lanterns, the boatshed with the faded script over its door, the sound of old Dossan still reading ledger entries aloud to a crowd of sixty inside, his cracked voice reciting names that hadn't been spoken on this river in twenty years. "This is your past too. Everybody's. It's not dead. It's just submerged — and the Vellin, Captain, the Vellin gives up everything eventually. You of all people should know. You're the one who pulled the chest out of her."

Down the walkway, a door opened, and light spilled out. One of Sera's runners, a girl of maybe twelve, breathless.

"Captain Amberdale? The elder wants you. All of you." She swallowed. "There's a fuel barge coming up the back channel tomorrow morning. Marsh traders. And they're saying—" she glanced at the grown-ups' faces around her, lowered her voice, "—they're saying there's a police launch anchored at the mouth of the main channel. Grey hull. Been sitting there since sundown. Not moving. Just waiting."

Edmund closed his eyes for one breath. When he opened them, the ledger-face was shut again, and the captain was back.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd best decide what this country is willing to spend on us."

Inside the boatshed, old Dossan's voice rose over the crowd, reading another name into the lamplight, and the river carried it out over the water like a debt coming due.
