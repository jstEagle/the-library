## 10. The Price of Passage

The marsh people did not negotiate the way rivermen negotiate. Rivermen negotiate with their hands — a slap on a gunwale, a coin spun and caught, a look that says *we both know what this costs and neither of us will say it aloud*. The marsh elders negotiated like lawyers who had buried their law books in peat and remembered every clause anyway.

They convened us at dusk in the long boatshed where Dossan had been reading names since sundown. The hull of some half-dismantled barge formed one wall, tarpaulins and netting the others, and the lamplight came from oil lamps hung on fishhooks so they could be lowered when the water rose, which, Maren's cousin Odile told me cheerfully, it always did.

There were seven elders. I counted twice because I didn't trust my eyes the first time: three men, four women, ages running from a wiry fellow who couldn't have seen fifty winters to a woman so old her hands looked like driftwood that had given up pretending to be anything else. Her name was Sera Voss, and she sat at the center the way an anchor sits at the bottom of a channel — not moving, everything else arranging itself around her.

Ilse sat across from them with the ledgers stacked in front of her like a wall she'd built out of paper. Tamir stood behind her chair with his arm bound and his jaw set in a way I recognized. It was the jaw of a man who has decided he is willing to be unreasonable, and is rather looking forward to it.

I stood by the door, because someone had to look like he could still leave.

"You want passage," Sera Voss said. Not a question. She'd been told our request before we were invited in, which told me the marsh had more ears than the Vellin had tributaries.

"Through the back channels," I said. "Past the flood line, down to where the channels meet the Marrowgate roadstead. Your pilots know water no chart knows."

"Our pilots know water no chart *wants* to know," said the wiry one, whose name was Pell. "That's why they're alive."

"And you'll take us."

Sera Voss let the silence sit for a while. She was good at silence. I respected that professionally; a captain who can't sit through silence ends up paying for whatever fills it.

"We'll take you," she said at last. "For nothing."

Tamir shifted. Ilse went very still. I kept my face doing nothing, because I've learned that when a price sounds too low, it isn't low. It's just been written somewhere you haven't read yet.

"For nothing," I repeated.

"The river brought you here," Sera Voss said. "The river brought *that*—" she nodded at the ledgers— "up out of its own throat after twenty years. We don't charge tolls on what the river intends." She folded her driftwood hands. "But we have a condition. And it isn't small."

Here it was. There's always a here-it-was.

"If you fail," she said, "the ledgers stay with us."

* * *

I should explain what "if you fail" meant, sitting in that boatshed with the lamps swaying on their fishhooks and Dossan's voice still carrying faintly from the other end of the shed, reading names into the dark like a man paying off a mortgage one syllable at a time.

It meant: if Ferro caught us. If the grey boats ran us down in the back channels. If Edmund Amberdale, his broken boat, his shot deckhand, and his lying archivist ended up at the bottom of a slough or in irons on a patrol deck — then before any of that happened, the marsh would take the chest. Copy it, hide it, seed it through a hundred stilt-houses and smokehouses until no search could ever find all of it again.

And if we *succeeded* — if we reached Judge Rook and the tribunal convened — then presumably the ledgers would go into evidence, into history, into whatever vault Marrowgate kept for things too dangerous to leave lying around. Which meant the marsh people, who had kept Ostrava alive for twenty years with nothing but stories and spite, would get back exactly what they'd started with: stories.

"They'd be copies," Pell said, seeing my face. "You keep your originals. We keep ours. Fair division."

"It's not a division," Ilse said quietly. "It's a fracture."

She hadn't raised her voice. She almost never did. But the whole shed heard her anyway, the way you hear a hairline crack in a hull if you're listening for it.

"Madam," said Sera Voss, with the patience of a woman who had outlived everyone who'd ever tried to hurry her, "twenty years ago, men with signatures hid the truth in one place. One archive. One city. One set of doors. And when the soldiers came, the doors opened, and everything inside burned, and the only reason anyone remembers the Hollowing at all is that my mother sewed the names into a fishing net and taught them to children who thought they were learning a song." She leaned forward. "You want to carry the whole truth in one boat, down one river, past one governor's police, to one old judge. That isn't justice, girl. That's a single point of failure with a seal on it."

I watched Ilse take that. I watched her hands — those paper hands, shaking the first day aboard and steadier now than mine — flatten against the ledger covers as if she could hold the argument shut the way she held the books.

"The chain of evidence matters," she said. "If copies circulate, the Governor's lawyers will say they're forgeries. Fabrications. Marsh propaganda. The originals have to arrive intact, uncorrupted, provably continuous from the moment they left the riverbed. That's how a court works. That's how Rook's court *has* to work, or Bray walks."

"And if the boat sinks?" Pell asked. "Does the continuity matter then? Does the chain?"

Silence. The lamps swung. Somewhere outside, a heron made its sound like a saw going through wet wood.

"I've spent twenty years keeping faith with dead people," Sera Voss said, softer now, which was somehow worse. "Don't tell me how the living should honor them."

* * *

We broke for the night without agreeing on anything, which in my experience is what happens when both sides are right. Rightness doesn't compromise well. It's got no give in it.

Odile put us up in a stilt-house at the edge of the settlement, a room above the water with a floor of split bamboo that flexed when you walked on it like the deck of a ship that had learned to breathe. Tamir collapsed onto a pallet with his bad shoulder propped on a rolled net and was asleep between one breath and the next, the way young men sleep when they've bled enough for one week.

Ilse sat by the window with a lamp turned low, and I stood out on the little platform walkway smoking, because I don't smoke, but I needed something to do with my hands that wasn't talking, and eventually the cigarette won and I went in.

"You should sleep," I said.

"So should you."

"I'm the captain. Sleeping's for passengers." I lowered myself onto the bench across from her, bamboo creaking its objections. Below us, the black water moved without hurry. Somewhere under it lay the channel we'd follow tomorrow, and somewhere beyond that, a governor with a signature and a captain named Ferro who hadn't answered my offer yet, and I found I was thinking about Ferro's face when I'd shown him page one — the hunger and the horror fighting each other like two cats in a sack — and I made myself stop.

"She's right, you know," Ilse said.

"Sera Voss? About the single point of failure?"

"Yes."

"And you're right about the chain of evidence."

"Yes."

"Well." I tapped ash into the water. "Then we're rich in correctness and poor in options. Story of my life."

Ilse almost smiled. Almost. "What would you do, Captain? If it were yours to decide?"

That was the question, wasn't it. I'd been asking myself a version of it since the night the river coughed up the chest and I nearly rolled it back overboard. *Not my cargo. Not my history. Not my fight.* I'd built a whole life out of that sentence, plank by plank, and here I was sitting in a stranger's house over black water, holding the plans to tear it down.

"When I was younger," I said, "I ran cargo for a factor in Corben's Landing. Grain, mostly. Once, salted pork during a shortage, and I knew — knew — that half of it was stolen off relief barges headed upriver to a fever town. I asked him about it. He paid me double and told me the river doesn't ask questions." I looked at my hands. "So I stopped asking them. Twenty years of not asking. You get very good at it. It's a skill, like reading water." I flicked the cigarette butt out into the dark. "The thing nobody tells you about that skill is what it costs. You save up all the questions you didn't ask, and one day they come due. All at once. With interest."

Ilse waited. She was good at waiting too. Everyone in this story was better at patience than me.

"What I'd do," I said finally, "is what I'm going to do. Which is find the option where everybody loses a little and nobody loses everything, and then live with the fact that it satisfied nobody, including me. That's what command is, most days. Choosing which wrong shape fits the hole best."

"That's a terrible philosophy of justice."

"It's not a philosophy of justice. It's a philosophy of rivers. Justice is above my draft."

This time she did smile, briefly, tiredly, and then it went away and she looked out the window at the water, and I could see her doing arithmetic with the future the way Maren used to do it with the sky.

* * *

Morning came grey and smelling of rain that hadn't committed yet. The elders reconvened in the boatshed, and this time there was a table — actually a hatch cover laid across two barrels, but ceremony counts for something — and the ledgers sat on it between us like a hostage.

Pell opened with the same terms. Ilse refused with the same reasons. Sera Voss watched us both with the expression of a woman watching weather she'd already predicted.

It might have gone around again all day, except Tamir spoke up.

He was standing by the door with his arm in its sling, and when he talked, his voice cracked in the middle the way it does at seventeen when you haven't slept.

"Why does it have to be copies or nothing?" he said. "Why not — pages?"

Everyone looked at him. He flushed but held his ground, which I noted for later, because first mates are made out of moments like that one.

"There's — what, nine ledgers?" He nodded at the stack. "Split them. Some go with us, some stay here. If we make it, you send yours on to the judge. If we don't, we still lost only part, and you've still got part, and the Governor can't burn what he can't find."

"No," Ilse said immediately. "Dividing the record divides the proof. Any single ledger alone shows orders but not the system. The system is the crime. That's the whole point — it wasn't one massacre, it was a *policy*, and the policy only exists across all the volumes together—"

"Then split the volumes' *contents*," Tamir said, stubborn now. "Copy half the pages into each set. Mix them up. Every copy's incomplete, but together—"

"Together they're scattered across a hundred miles of marsh and a sinking boat," Ilse said, "and reassembly takes months, and the trial can't wait months, because Ferro's report reached Bray days ago and Bray does not wait—"

"He's right about the principle, though," I said.

Everyone turned to me. Even Sera Voss raised an eyebrow, which I gathered was roughly equivalent to another woman standing on a chair and shouting.

"Go on," she said dryly.

"The principle is redundancy without surrender." I walked to the table. My knee complained; it had opinions about bamboo floors. "Nobody gets the whole record. Nobody gets nothing. Here's how I see it." I put my hand flat on the top ledger. "One volume comes with us. The rest stay here — originals, not copies, sealed in your keeping. If we reach Rook, you send the rest down under marsh pilot, quiet, after us. If we fail, you've got eight-ninths of the truth hidden where it's been hiding for twenty years anyway, plus a witness—" I tipped my head at Ilse— "who has all nine volumes in her skull, if half of what she's shown me below decks is true."

Ilse stared at me. "You want to *split the archive*."

"I want to stop arguing about it. Bray doesn't argue. He sends boats."

"And if your one volume is captured? Then the Governor holds a piece of the original, and he'll claim the rest are forgeries built around it—"

"Then he admits the piece is real," I said. "Which is one signature more than he's admitted in twenty years. And signatures, madam, are the one thing in this whole sorry business that can't be forged into existence. They can only be explained away. And explanations wear thin." I looked around the shed, at the elders, at the nets and the lamps and the names Dossan had read into the dark. "Look. None of you are getting what you want. She keeps her chain of custody bent but unbroken. You keep most of the truth under your own roof instead of trusting it to my luck. I lose the comfort of carrying cargo I can account for. Everybody bleeds a little. That's the deal. Take it or leave it, but decide fast, because the sky's been threatening rain since dawn and Maren's cousins tell me that means the monsoon's leading edge is closer than comfortable."

The elders conferred. In marsh fashion, that meant they argued in low voices while Sera Voss said nothing at all, and everyone understood that nothing was the vote that mattered.

Finally she looked at Ilse. "It's your grandfather's seal, girl. Not mine. Say it."

Ilse's hands were flat on the ledgers again. I watched her wrestle it — the archivist in her wanting the whole record whole, the granddaughter wanting it safe, and underneath both, I suspected, the magistrate's heir understanding, in her bones, that law is just a promise, and promises survive by being kept in more than one place.

"One volume travels," she said at last. "The seventh. It has Bray's signature on the Hollowing order itself — the primary instrument. If nothing else arrives, that one convicts him." She drew it out and set it apart from the stack, and her fingers lingered on the cover like a woman leaving a child at a school gate. "The rest stay here. Sealed. Witnessed. And if we reach Marrowgate, your pilots bring them down within the month, or the bargain's void and I will say so publicly, in Rook's court, on the record."

"Done," said Sera Voss, and spat in her palm, and held it out.

Ilse looked at the hand. Then, to her credit, she spat in her own and shook it, and the old woman's mouth twitched with something that in a younger face would have been a grin.

"And you, Captain," Sera Voss said, turning to me. "You brokered this. What do *you* get out of it?"

I considered lying. I even had one ready — something about fair payment for passage, professional pride, the usual ballast. But the boatshed smelled of lamp oil and old wood and twenty years of names, and I was tired of ballast.

"A clear conscience," I said. "Never owned one before. Curious to see how it handles."

Pell snorted. Sera Voss studied me for a long moment with those driftwood eyes, and then she nodded once, slow, like a lock turning.

"They all say that," she said. "The ones who mean it, anyway."

* * *

We spent the rest of the day preparing, and I'll say this for the marsh: they worked like they argued, thoroughly and without wasted motion. Odile's people caulked the seams the settlement's boatwrights had patched, re-stepped the jury mast proper, and loaded us with smoked fish, rice, and a jar of something Pell called "medicine" that Tamir eyed with the reverence of a boy meeting a legend.

The seventh ledger rode in a oilskin wrap against my own chest, under my coat, tied with cord. Ilse had insisted I carry it, not her. "Ferro searches me first," she'd said simply. "He knows I'm the archivist. He doesn't know what you are yet."

"What am I?" I'd asked.

"Honest," she'd said, "in a way nobody's thought to plan around."

At dusk, two marsh pilots came aboard — a lean brother and sister named Corvo and Ash, faces brown as teak, eyes that read water the way I read ledgers of debt. Ash took the wheel from me without asking, tested it with two small movements, and gave a grunt that I chose to interpret as approval.

"Back channels run narrow and shallow," Corvo told me, spreading a hand-drawn chart on the deck that wasn't on any chart house's list. "Three days to the delta roadstead if the rain holds off. Less if it doesn't, but less also means drowned markers and new snags, so let's hope for slow."

From the west, over the drowned forest, the sky had gone the color of a bruise going green at the edges. The air had that pressed, held-breath weight it gets before a monsoon commits. Old Maren would have read it in one glance and started counting hours.

Maren. I made myself not think it, which is to say I thought it thoroughly, and put it away where I put the questions I used to not ask.

Ash rang the shallow bell twice. The settlement's boats slid aside. On the stilt-walkways, dozens of faces — smugglers, deserters, grandchildren of a drowned country — watched us pull away, and somebody began to sing, low, one of those songs Dossan's names had been sewn into, and I didn't know the words but I knew the tune's purpose. It was a receipt. Proof of passage. Something to hold against the day the debt came due.

"Captain," Ash said at the wheel, nodding west at the bruised sky. "Rain by midnight. Gorge water by the second dawn. You want to pray, pray for boring."

I looked back once at the marsh, already folding into the reeds and the failing light, eight ledgers heavier in its keeping and lighter in its heart, and then I faced downstream, toward Marrowgate, toward Rook, toward whatever Ferro had decided while I slept on a breathing floor.

Three days. One signature. A sky full of borrowed time.

"Give me the channel," I said, and the Amberdale slid into the dark water like a secret going home.
