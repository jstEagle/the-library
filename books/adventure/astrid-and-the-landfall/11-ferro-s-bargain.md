## 11. Ferro's Bargain

There are two kinds of men who work for the Governor's river police. The first kind believe in the work — order on the water, tolls collected, smugglers drowned or jailed, a clean ledger from the highlands to the delta. The second kind know what the work is and do it anyway because the pension is real and conscience is not.

I used to be the first kind.

I want that written down somewhere, in case this account survives me: Captain Dario Ferro, eleven years on the Vellin, commendations for the Kessel Narrows rescue and the suppression of the Saltmarsh piracy ring, was once the first kind of man. Whatever I became at the fuel depot below Halfway Light, I did not start there.

* * *

The Amberdale showed up on my charts three days out of Weir Nine, which meant someone upstream had sold her. That didn't surprise me. Everything on the river is for sale; the only variable is the price and whether the seller knows he's set one. A lock officer here, a fuel clerk there, a marsh guide with a sick daughter — the Governor's money moves through them like water through gravel, and what comes out the other end is a position fix and a name.

The name was Edmund Amberdale, same as his boat, which told you everything about how much imagination the man allowed himself. Thirty years running cargo on the middle Vellin. No debts worth collecting, no vices worth exploiting, no family worth threatening. My file on him was thin as communion paper, and thin files worry me more than thick ones. Thick files mean a man has appetites. Thin files mean a man has already decided what he'll never want, and those are harder to buy.

The woman was another matter. Ilse Vant, traveling under papers that had been very good six weeks ago and were now merely expensive. My people in Corben's Landing said she'd paid cash, asked strange questions about delta shipping schedules, and kept a leather case chained to her wrist even while sleeping. People who chain things to their wrists are either carrying treasure or expecting thieves. On this river, it amounts to the same thing.

The chest was the third matter, and the one that kept me up at night, if I'm honest, which I am trying to be, since honesty appears to be the currency of whatever this is turning into.

A lead-lined chest bearing the seal of Ostrava comes up out of the river after twenty years, and within a week it's running south ahead of my boats. The Governor wanted it recovered quietly. Quietly, in his vocabulary, has always been an elastic word. It stretches to cover bribery, and it stretches to cover arson, and in the past month I'd watched it stretch all the way to a standing instruction that left no room in it for prisoners.

"Recovery of the materials is the objective," his secretary had said, in a marble office that smelled of lilies and money laundering. "Personnel incidental to recovery are to be handled at the captain's discretion."

Handled. At my discretion. Which is the Governor's way of handing you a knife and telling you the cutting is your idea.

* * *

I found them at the Corvel & Sons fuel depot, forty miles above the gorges, tied up at the downstream berth where the depot's tin roof throws a shadow deep enough to hide a boat from casual eyes but not from mine. I'd been sitting across the channel in a moored barge for six hours, drinking coffee that tasted like boiled rope, watching the Amberdale's crew work.

Three of them now. The boy — Tamir, dark-skinned, maybe nineteen, arm in a sling from where my marines had put a bullet through his shoulder at the charcoal wharves. I'd signed off on that engagement. I'd stood on the wheelhouse deck of the *Vigilant* and given the order to fire when they ran, and I'd watched the boy go down, and I'd told myself it was lawful because they were fleeing inspection. Fleeing inspection. As if bullets were a reasonable answer to a question about cargo.

The old pilot, Maren, moved around the deck like weather itself, reading the sky, spitting over the rail. And Amberdale himself, coiling line with the economy of a man who'd done nothing else for half his life.

And the woman, on the roof of the cabin, watching the water. Watching for me, probably. Good. I liked being expected. It saved us both the pretense that what happened next was anything other than what it was.

I waited until the depot attendant went inside for the evening ledger. Then I brought the launch alongside, alone, hands visible, sidearm left aboard the *Vigilant* anchored out in the channel where its silhouette could be seen by anyone who cared to look. That was deliberate. A man comes to a negotiation unarmed, he's saying something. A man comes with a gunboat behind him, he's saying something else. I wanted to say both things at once, because I genuinely did not know yet which man I was.

"Captain Amberdale," I called up. "Dario Ferro. River police."

He came to the rail without hurry, which annoyed me. Men usually hurry at my approach. "Captain Ferro. You're a long way from anywhere you can arrest anybody."

"I'm exactly where I need to be." I looked up at the woman on the cabin roof. She hadn't moved, but she'd gone still in a way that meant she'd heard every word. "May I come aboard? I promise I'm more dangerous talking than shooting."

"That's a low bar for a man whose people shot my deckhand."

"He'll live."

"He'd better," Amberdale said, and dropped the ladder.

* * *

His wheelhouse smelled of diesel, tobacco, and old varnish. He poured coffee without asking, which I took as either hospitality or contempt, and handed me a cup chipped along one side. I sat on the bench beneath the rear window. Maren drifted into the doorway behind him and stood there with her arms folded, and the boy appeared at the forward hatch, pale and stubborn, sling and all. Nobody dismissed them. Nobody could have.

"I'll keep this short," I said, "because we both have better uses for the dark hours."

"You'll keep it however long it needs to be," Amberdale said. "You're the one rowing upstream."

Fair. I set down the cup. "Here's the shape of it. The chest you're carrying contains documents belonging to the Ostravan state archive. Those documents are the property of the successor administration. Their removal is theft of state property. Returning them is not a crime; it's a correction. If you surrender the chest and the woman — who, I should tell you, is traveling under false papers and is wanted in connection with the theft — you walk away. Full amnesty for the narrows incident, the wharves incident, the lock at Weir Nine, all of it. Clean slate. And compensation."

"How much?"

"Five thousand crowns."

The boy made a sound like he'd been struck. Five thousand crowns was ten years of a riverman's wages. It was a new hull, a new engine, a house on dry ground. It was everything the Amberdale had been scraping toward for thirty years, offered in a single envelope.

Amberdale didn't blink. "And the woman?"

"Goes south with me. She'll be questioned and released."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," I said, because lying to him seemed suddenly pointless, and because I was curious what he'd do with the truth. "But that's not your problem. That's mine. Your problem is five thousand crowns and a clean record versus a flooded gorge in monsoon season with a holed boat and a crew of four."

Maren snorted from the doorway. "Crew of four and a half," she said. "The half being you, if you count yourself useful."

I ignored her, mostly because she was right.

Amberdale turned his cup slowly on its saucer. When he spoke, it wasn't to me. "Ilse. You hearing this?"

From outside, through the window, her voice came flat and clear. "Every word. Tell him the chest isn't mine to sell and neither am I."

"She's right," Amberdale said. "It's not mine either. I fished it out of the river. By rights it belongs to whoever it drowned with." He looked at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't prepared for, because my file on him was thin and files don't carry faces. "You said questioned and released. Let me ask you something instead, Captain. What's in the chest?"

"You know what's in it."

"Ledgers. I know that much. Ledgers from the last magistrates of Ostrava. So let me ask it plainer." He leaned forward. "Did you ever read page one?"

The wheelhouse got very quiet. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"Page one," I repeated.

"The first entry. The first order. The one with the youngest signature on it." He tapped the chart table with one finger, slow, like a man counting. "Twenty-two years ago, a provincial administrator named Cassel Bray countersigned Directive Eleven. You know what Directive Eleven authorized? Neither did I, until I read it. It authorized the Hollowing. Not the famine — the famine was weather. The directive ordered that relief supplies be withheld from the Ostrava districts until the unrest burned itself out. Withheld. From districts that were already starving. Fourteen thousand people, give or take, and the man who signed the order now lives above the flood line in a marble house and sends boats like yours after the paperwork."

I knew. God help me, I knew. Not the details — I'd never seen the ledgers — but the shape of it. Every policeman on the river knows the shape of it. You grow up hearing the stories from the old refugees, the ones who talk too much when they drink, and you learn to stop hearing them, the way you learn to stop hearing the church bells. Twenty years of practice.

"That's a story," I said.

"It's a signature," Amberdale said. "Stories you can argue with. Signatures are just ink and a date."

"And you're offering to show me."

"I'm offering you the thing nobody else will offer you, Captain Ferro. Not amnesty. Not gold. A look. One page. Then you go back to your gunboat and decide which kind of man you are, and I won't think worse of you either way, because I've spent thirty years learning that most men aren't choosing evil, they're just not looking."

* * *

I want to record accurately what happened inside me at that moment, because later — if there is a later — I'll want to know whether it was principle or vanity or simple exhaustion.

He opened the case. The ledgers were wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was stained brown in places, and the smell that came off them was the river's own smell, silt and rot and time. The top volume fell open — deliberately, I understood later; he'd marked it — to a page dense with faded magistrates' script, and at the bottom of the page, a signature.

*Cassel Bray, Administrator Plenipotentiary.*

Below it, in a different hand, a distribution schedule. Grain shipments rerouted. Medical stores impounded. Telegraph lines to the Ostrava districts cut, quote, *to prevent the spread of alarm*, unquote. Dates. Countersignatures. A column of figures that might have been inventory except that the units were human beings processed through a camp at a place called Vesseny, and beside several entries, small annotations in a clerk's careful hand: *deceased*, *deceased*, *unaccounted*.

I read it twice. I'm a fast reader and a slow forgetter, and I want it known that I read it twice, and that the second time was not for comprehension. It was because the first time, some part of me was still looking for the trick — the forgery, the misdating, the innocent explanation. There wasn't one. There was just the ink, and the date, and the name of the man whose portrait hung in every government office I'd ever worked in.

"You could burn it," Amberdale said quietly. "Right now. Take it off the boat, deliver it south, collect whatever they pay you, and sleep the way you slept before. Some men can. I'm not mocking them. Sleep is worth a lot."

"Is that what you'd do?"

"I lost people to a fever town once because I sailed away instead of staying. So no. I'd look at the page every night for the rest of my life." He closed the ledger, gently, the way you close a coffin. "That's the bargain, Captain. Not the one you came with. Mine."

* * *

I stood on his deck afterward in the last grey light, and the woman Ilse Vant came down off the cabin roof and stood near me, close enough to speak without carrying.

"You were going to shoot us at the wharves," she said. Not accusing. Just establishing the geography.

"Yes."

"And the boy."

"Yes."

"He's nineteen. He lied to a toll officer for the first time in his life two weeks ago, to protect these books, and he was so frightened he shook, and he did it anyway." She looked at me with eyes that had clearly done their own arithmetic about what people are worth. "What did Bray offer you for us, Captain? Not the number. The other thing. Permission."

I didn't answer. That was answer enough, and she nodded like a woman confirming a delivery.

I went down the ladder. At the bottom I stopped, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning around: "The gorges close in three days. After that, the monsoon owns the river, and even Bray's boats stay home. If you run the gorges, you might live. If you make Marrowgate, you might even matter."

"And if you catch us before then?"

I climbed into the launch. The boy Tamir watched me from the rail with his arm in its sling, and I found I couldn't meet his eyes, which was a new experience and not a pleasant one.

"Captain Ferro," Amberdale called after me. "Page one. Think about it."

I thought about nothing else.

* * *

The *Vigilant* rode at anchor in the channel, her running lights doubled against the dark, and I sat in her wheelhouse until midnight with the logbook open in front of me and wrote nothing. Behind me, downriver, the fuel depot lamps went out one by one. Somewhere in that darkness the Amberdale was taking on fuel, or casting off, or waiting to see what I would do, and the truth — the honest truth, which I promised at the beginning of this account — is that I did not know myself.

My orders were explicit. Intercept, recover, handle personnel at discretion. My career was built on orders like those, executed cleanly, questions unasked. Eleven years of unasked questions, stacked like sandbags, holding back exactly the kind of water that was now coming through.

Bray would want confirmation by morning wire. *Contact made. Subject uncooperative. Pursuit continuing.* Or else the other message, the one with no words in it, the one that gets sent when discretion does its full work.

I picked up the wireless handset twice. Both times I put it down.

Then, near one in the morning, the depot watchman flagged me down from the dock — the Amberdale had cast off an hour ago, running dark, headed straight for the gorge gates with the monsoon's leading edge already stacking black over the highlands like a debt coming due.

They'd gone. While I sat reading signatures and arguing with myself, they'd gone.

And I stood on the deck of the *Vigilant* with the handset in my hand and the storm light rising in the east, and I gave the order — but not the order Bray had written for me.

"Get underway," I told my helmsman. "Follow the Amberdale into the gorges. And signal the other boats to hold at the mouth until I call for them."

He looked at me. "Until you call, sir?"

"Until I call," I said, and turned my face downriver so he couldn't see whatever it was I was deciding, because I hadn't decided it yet — but I knew, the way you know a flood is coming, that by the time the gorges spat us all out the other end, one of us would not be the man who entered them.
