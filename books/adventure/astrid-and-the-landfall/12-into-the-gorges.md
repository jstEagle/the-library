## 12. Into the Gorges

The sky went the color of wet slate an hour before we reached the gorge mouth, and Maren stood at the bow rail with her hands behind her back like a woman appraising a horse she intended to buy.

"That's not weather," she said. "That's a schedule."

I had learned, over thirty years on the Vellin and its bad-tempered tributaries, that when Maren used the word *schedule* about the sky, you did not argue. You checked your bilge pumps and said thank you.

"Four days," I said.

"Three now." She turned and looked at me with those pale river eyes of hers, the ones that had seen more drownings than funerals. "The leading edge comes down the valley ahead of the rain. Wind first, then water, then everything else. If we're inside the gorges when it lands, we'll wish we were anywhere else. If we wait outside them, Ferro will find us sitting in open water like ducks at a fair."

"So we go in."

"So we go in."

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a crew when the captain says something stupid with confidence. Tamir, who was supposed to be resting his bullet-torn shoulder but had instead appointed himself supervisor of everything, looked up from coiling line and gave me the flat stare he usually reserved for my cooking. Ilse Vant — or Ilse Rook-Vant, or whatever name she'd finally confessed to owning — closed her ledger book and said nothing at all, which from her was practically applause.

* * *

Let me tell you about the gorges, since you weren't there and are therefore luckier than any of us.

The Vellin runs fat and slow through most of its length, a brown working river that carries charcoal and grain and the small ambitions of small towns. But between the marsh country and the delta it drops through a fold in the earth where the old maps simply give up and write *falls* in letters too large to be useful. In dry years it's a canyon run — fast water, rock gates, two stretches where the whole river squeezes through gaps you could spit across. In flood years, the canyon fills to the brim and becomes something else entirely: a hundred feet of green water moving through stone with the patience of a landslide.

We were going in on the leading edge, which meant we'd have current enough to make good time and just enough chaos to keep things interesting. The marsh elders had given me a chart — hand-drawn, grease-stained, annotated in three generations of handwriting — showing the safe lines through each gate and, crucially, the location of the winch passage at Halfway Light, where the river narrows around a spur of black rock and no boat larger than a skiff goes through under its own power. You warp through. Rope and winch and muscle, hauling yourself along the cliff face while the water tries to peel you off the wall.

"Halfway Light is the crux," Maren told us that morning, spreading the chart on the wheelhouse table and weighting the corners with coffee cups. "Everything before it is skill. Everything after it is luck. At Halfway Light you need both, plus rope that isn't rotten and people who do what they're told the first time."

"I always do what I'm told," Tamir said.

"You argue first," Maren said. "That's different."

* * *

We entered the gorge mouth at midday, if you can call it daylight when the sky has been replaced by a low grey ceiling pressing down like a lid. The wind came ahead of us, funneled up the canyon against our direction of travel, cold and smelling of iron and far-off rain. The Amberdale's patched hull — planked, caulked, and blessed by a marsh elder who'd spat rum over the transom for reasons nobody explained — groaned once as the current took hold, a long creaking complaint from amidships that sounded like an old man being asked to dance.

"She's talking to us," Tamir said.

"She's complaining," I said. "There's a difference. Complaints you can work with."

The first gate came up faster than I liked. Two walls of granite leaning together, maybe forty feet apart at the waterline, the river pouring through in a single brown muscle. The chart showed the line: hard to starboard off the entry, ride the eddy behind the downstream fang, then punch across the main flow into the slack water beyond. On paper it looked simple. Paper has never been hit in the face by forty feet of flood-stage river.

"Tamir, bow line ready. Ilse — below, hold onto something and stay out of the way."

Ilse looked at me. It was becoming a habit of hers, this looking, this quiet refusal to be dismissed. "Where's the most useful place to stand?" she asked.

"The shore," I said. "Preferably a high one."

She stayed on deck. I didn't have time to fight about it, which I suspect she knew.

The gate took us the way a door takes a draft. One moment we were steering; the next, the river was doing the steering and I was making suggestions. The fang rock slid past close enough on the port side that I could have touched it, white water standing off it in a permanent fluted crest, and then we were through, wallowing in the eddy below while spray rained down on the deck and everyone started breathing again at different rates.

"Again," Maren said, pointing upstream. There were four gates. We had done one.

* * *

The second gate nearly killed us, and I say that with the calm of a man describing something that happened to other people.

The chart's line was wrong — or the river had moved, which amounts to the same thing — and the eddy I wanted wasn't where the grease-pencil said it would be. Instead there was a boil, a column of water coming up from the depths like something exhaling, and the Amberdale's stern swung toward it before I could correct. For about six seconds we went sideways down the face of the gorge, the patched hull groaning that dying-animal groan again, and I watched Tamir throw the bow line around the forward bollard purely out of instinct, and I watched Ilse — pale, paper-handed Ilse, the archivist who'd never hauled anything heavier than a book — plant both hands on the rail and hold on with her whole body while the deck tilted forty degrees and the river tried to roll us.

Then the boil passed under us, spat us out, and the current forgave us all at once, and we were through, listing slightly and considerably humbler.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. Then Tamir, holding his bandaged shoulder with his good hand, said, "Captain, I've decided I don't like the gorges."

"Noted," I said. "Two more gates and a winch passage to go."

"I'm noting harder."

* * *

Between the third gate and Halfway Light there's a stretch where the canyon widens into a drowned valley, and we took an hour there to breathe, eat, and lash down everything that wasn't already lashed. The wind had risen steadily, funnelling downriver now instead of up, carrying the first spits of actual rain — big, warm, deliberate drops, like the sky clearing its throat.

Maren tasted the air and frowned. "It's early," she said. "The edge is running ahead of itself. We'll have the monsoon proper before we clear the last gate, if the last gate doesn't clear us first."

"Cheerful," said Tamir.

"I'm old," Maren said. "Cheerfulness is for people with time."

I found Ilse aft, sitting on the hatch cover with her back against the wheelhouse wall, watching the canyon walls slide past. Her knuckles were raw from the rail. She saw me looking and tucked them into her sleeves, an archivist's reflex even now.

"You held on well," I said.

"I held on," she corrected. "Well is generous."

"It counts double the first time." I sat down beside her, which surprised us both. "You could have gone below. I told you to."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

She considered the question honestly, which is more than most people do. "Because every time I've stayed out of the way in my life, things happened without me. Ostrava happened without me. My grandfather died in a cellar and I read about it in a newspaper. I'm finished being cargo, Captain. The chest was cargo. I'm crew now, or I'm nothing."

I thought about telling her that crews follow orders, and orders exist so that people don't get killed doing brave stupid things. I thought about it and decided she'd only point out that I'd run the shallows at the narrows against direct instructions from the river police, and that my entire career was a series of brave stupid things executed with excellent boat-handling.

"Winch passage next," I said instead. "When I put you on a winch handle, you turn until someone tells you to stop. Not until it hurts. Until someone tells you to stop. Those are different points, and the difference is fingers."

"Understood," she said, and for the first time since Corben's Landing she smiled, small and crooked and real. "You know, Edmund, you give orders like a man who expects to be disobeyed."

"I'm a man with experience," I said.

* * *

Halfway Light earns its name from the way the sun comes down the canyon at mid-afternoon and catches the spray above the narrows, turning the whole gap into a column of gold light with a black rock spur standing in the middle of it like a knife. It's beautiful. Everything on this river that can kill you is beautiful; I think that's the Vellin's idea of humor.

The approach was straightforward, which lulled us. The spur splits the channel: main flow to port, boiling and impossible; a slot to starboard, twenty feet wide, through which you warp the boat hand over hand along a fixed chain-and-bollard system the old timber companies drove into the rock a century ago. The marsh elders' chart marked three sound chains and two winch points. What it couldn't mark was how much water was coming down.

We warped in through the slot under half power, Maren calling the line from the bow, Tamir on the starboard winch with his good arm, me at the helm holding us against the current with the engine growling like a thing possessed. And Ilse took the port winch, because there was no one else, and because she had asked to be crew.

The first hundred feet went fine. The second hundred feet, the monsoon arrived.

I don't mean rain began. I mean the sky opened along a seam, all at once, and the gorge filled with water falling sideways, and the river beneath us rose — visibly, within minutes, inches crawling up the rock walls — and the current in the slot doubled its opinion of our presence. The patched hull groaned, long and continuous now, a sound like a ship deciding whether to die. Somewhere below decks, something shifted and settled and did not shift back.

"Hold!" Maren screamed, though the wind tore the word apart. "Hold the line! Don't let her swing!"

The Amberdale's stern swung anyway. The current got its teeth into the quarter and pivoted us, and suddenly we were broadside in a twenty-foot slot with a rising river trying to fold us in half around the spur. The port winch line went bar-tight with a sound like a struck harp string, and I saw Ilse thrown against the housing, saw her wrap both arms around the winch handle and hang on rather than let the line pay out, saw her boots skid on the wet deck —

— and then Tamir was beside her, one-handed, throwing his weight on the same handle, and the two of them held it, held *it*, the whole river's argument, while I gave the engine everything the old girl had left and Maren clawed forward to pass a snubbing line around the forward bollard.

"Turn her! Turn her NOW!"

We turned. I will never know entirely how. The engine, the ropes, the two people on the winch, the river briefly reconsidering — some conspiracy of all of them swung the Amberdale's bow back into the slot, and we warped the last fifty feet through water falling from heaven and rising from hell, and came out the far side into the long pool below Halfway Light with the rain hammering the deck flat and everyone on their knees and breathing like landed fish.

The hull groaned one final time and went quiet, sulking.

I got up. My legs worked, which seemed ungrateful of them after the performance they'd given. I made my way aft to the winch, where Ilse sat in the rain with her arms still wrapped around the handle, white-knuckled, shaking — not with fear anymore, I think, but with the aftermath of effort, the body's honest accounting.

"Let go," I said gently. "We're through. Let go of the handle."

Her fingers wouldn't open at first. Tamir pried them loose one at a time, grinning through the rain, and when her hands came free they left the shape of the handle printed in white on her palms.

"Well," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle. "That counted double."

"It counted triple," I said. "Welcome to the crew, Ilse."

Maren appeared at my elbow, streaming water, wearing the expression she saved for miracles she didn't trust. "That was the crux," she shouted over the rain. "But look astern, Captain. Look at the water level on the walls."

I looked. The flood marks — old ones, cut into the rock by generations of high water — were disappearing beneath the current as we watched. The river was still climbing, and the last gate lay ahead of us, and behind that, the delta, and somewhere in the delta, Ferro's grey boats waiting at every berth they thought we might reach.

"We rest here one hour," Maren said. "No more. Then we run the last gate in the middle of the storm, because it'll be the only chance the water gives us."

"One hour," I agreed.

And that was when Tamir, who had gone forward to check the bow line, called out — not alarmed, exactly, but in the careful voice he used for things he wanted me to see before anyone panicked.

"Captain. There's a lantern on the bank. Somebody's signaling. Three flashes, pause, three flashes." He turned, rain pouring off him. "That's a marsh signal, isn't it? That's *their* code?"

Maren went very still beside me.

"No," she said quietly. "That's not ours. Ours is two and two."
