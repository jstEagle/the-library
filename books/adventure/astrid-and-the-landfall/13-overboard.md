## 13. Overboard

I want it on the record, whoever ends up reading this, that I said the lashings were wrong. I said it twice. The first time Edmund grunted, which from Edmund is a full paragraph of agreement, and the second time he said, "It'll hold," which is what he says about caulking, about engines, about the Amberdale herself, and which has been wrong at least four times that I personally witnessed.

So when the wave came, I was not surprised. Grieved, yes. Drenched, certainly. But not surprised.

We were two hours into the gorges by then, and the river had stopped being a river and become an argument between mountains. The monsoon's leading edge had caught us at the mouth, exactly as Maren promised it would, because Maren reads sky the way Ilse reads paper and Edmund reads water, and none of them, apparently, read lashings. Rain came down in ropes. The gorge walls rose on either side of us, grey and dripping, close enough some places that I could have touched them with a boathook, and the water between them was moving like it had somewhere important to be and a grudge against everything in its way.

I was aft, minding the chest.

You should understand what the chest meant to me by then. A week earlier it had been a drowned thing, a bad-luck thing, a thing my captain had nearly rolled back into the Vellin out of pure superstition. But I had carried it past a toll officer hidden in a coffin, lying with a straight face for the first time in my twenty-two honest years, and something about a lie commits you. You can't unlie. Once you've told a lie to protect a thing, the thing is yours in a way ownership doesn't cover. It was ours. All of ours. The marsh folk had copied pages by lamplight and wept over them, and Ilse had slept with her hand on the lid like it was a sick relative, and Maren had died — no. Maren's death was two days later. Let me keep the order straight. At this point in the story Maren was still alive at the tiller, jaw set, eyes on the water, and I still believed old pilots were a permanent feature of the world, like rocks.

The chest was lashed to the deck ring just forward of the wheelhouse, under a tarp, because Ilse had argued — successfully, God help us — that below decks was no good if we holed, and Edmund had agreed, because Edmund agreed with Ilse approximately always, a development I found interesting and said nothing about, being a man who values his skin.

The wave came out of a bend where the gorge narrowed and the river dropped, and it was not a wave so much as the river standing up to look at us. I heard Maren shout something — her voice had a register for emergencies that skipped language entirely — and then the world tilted, and the lashings, which I had said were wrong, parted like they'd been waiting for an excuse.

The chest went over the side the way a stone goes into a well. No hesitation. The river took it the way it takes everything, immediately and without gratitude.

And here is the part I've turned over a hundred times since, lying in bunks in three different towns: I didn't think. That's the truth and it's the only defense I have. I didn't think about my mother, or the money I'd saved, or the fact that I swim like a man being electrocuted. The chest went over and I went over after it, and the last thing I saw from the deck was Edmund's face, and it wasn't horror. It was recognition. Like he'd been waiting for one of us to do something stupid and loyal and was only surprised it was me and not him.

Then the water closed over my head and the Vellin introduced itself.

* * *

People think drowning is quiet. It isn't. The gorges in flood were a cathedral with every organ playing at once and the congregation screaming. The water had me the way a dog has a rat, and it turned me over and over, and I got one breath that was half river, and then I was down where the world was brown and roaring and there was no up.

But my hands knew what they were doing even if the rest of me had resigned. I'd grown up on this river, hauling nets for a father who believed children were cheap labor with opinions. My hands found the chest — it was tumbling below me, lead-lined, sinking like its own guilt — and I got both arms around it and hugged it, and the chest and I went down together like a couple of drowned magistrates.

Which is when I understood I had made an error.

The chest did not want to float. The chest had never wanted to float. It was lined with lead because someone, twenty years ago, had wanted its contents to survive fire and flood, and they had succeeded so well that its contents were now going to survive at the bottom of the Vellin until the end of the world. And I, in my infinite wisdom, had attached myself to it.

I kicked. I kicked with everything my father's nets had built into my legs, and the river laughed, and we sank, and the light above went from grey to brown to a rumor. And I remember thinking, with a clarity that still embarrasses me: *Tamir, you have drowned yourself for a box of paper. Edmund will be furious. He'll say something about miles.* Because Edmund measured everything in miles, back then. Miles to Marrowgate, miles of fuel, miles between a man and his grief. I thought: he'll count this too. Two miles down. That's what I thought, going under, and if you want to know what a man is, look at what he thinks about when he's dying. I thought about my captain being annoyed at me.

Then something grabbed my collar and hauled, and the brown world tore open, and there was air, half of it rain, all of it welcome.

Edmund had come in after us. Of course he had. He was beside me in the flood, one hand fisted in my jacket, the other hooked under the chest's rope handle, and his face was the face he wore at the tiller in bad water — not calm, exactly, but *decided*, which is better. Calm is what you feel. Decided is what you do.

"Let go of the chest," he shouted, and the river ate half the words.

"Can't," I shouted back, which was true in every direction. My arms were locked. My fingers had made a decision my brain hadn't authorized and were refusing to revisit it.

"Tamir—"

"It's the *evidence*," I said, and I don't know where the words came from, because I am not a man for speeches, but the river had knocked something loose in me. "It's the only one. You let go of it, there's no trial, there's no nothing, there's just Bray and his grey boats forever."

Something moved across Edmund's face that I'd never seen there, and I've spent enough years reading that face to know its whole alphabet. It was the look of a man being argued out of a position by the least likely lawyer available, in the worst courtroom in the world, and losing.

So he didn't argue. He shifted his grip, got his shoulder under my arm, and started swimming, and we went up the river together — the three of us, a captain, a deckhand, and a box of dead men's handwriting — fighting for a gravel bar that Maren was shouting about from a deck that was shrinking above us like a coin dropped in deep water.

* * *

I will tell you what the gravel bar was, because the river people have a word for it and I've adopted the word: a *spine*. A ridge of stones the flood hadn't swallowed, maybe forty feet long, maybe three feet wide at the crown, and the water broke over it in white sheets on both sides. Getting onto it was not landing. It was being deposited, the way a flood deposits driftwood, and that is exactly the dignity we arrived with.

Edmund got me onto the stones first. I want that in the record too, because later, in the marshes, people told it the other way — the loyal deckhand and the noble captain, diving together, brotherhood of the river, all of that. What actually happened was Edmund put my half-drowned body on the bar like a sack of mail, then went back into the flood after the chest alone, because I was useless, curled around my own coughing, and he was not. He went out and the river took him to its middle and stood him up and tried to fold him, and for about ten seconds I watched the water where he'd been and understood, in my whole body, what Maren had tried to tell me once about the Vellin: *it doesn't hate you, boy, it just doesn't know you're there.*

Then his hand came up out of the brown, and then his head, and then the chest's rope, and then it was a matter of the two of us on our knees on a spine of stones in the middle of a flooded gorge, dragging a lead-lined box up the shingle while the river pulled at it like a child refusing to give back a toy.

We got it to the crown. We got the tarp — half-torn, still hooked at one corner — dragged over it. And then we just knelt there, the two of us, breathing like bellows, while the rain hammered the tarp and the gorge roared on both sides of us and the Amberdale came wallowing down the channel with Maren holding her on a line as close to the bar as she'd ever let anyone put her, which was close enough to throw a line and not close enough to touch.

"Tamir." Edmund's voice. I'd never heard it like that. Not angry, which I'd braced for. Something underneath angry, or maybe underneath everything else he kept down there.

"I know," I said. "The lashings. I said—"

"Not the lashings." He was looking at the chest, at his own hands on it, white-knuckled. "You went in after a box."

"You went in after a deckhand."

"That's different."

"It's not, though." I was shivering too hard for this conversation and had it anyway. "That's the whole point of the box, Captain. It's not different. That's what's *in* the box — names of people who went in after other people, or didn't, and got paid either way." I didn't know if that was true. I hadn't read the ledgers. But I'd seen Ilse's face over them, and I'd seen the marsh elder weep, and I knew the shape of it the way you know the shape of a thing under a tarp. "Bray signed a paper and men drowned and nobody went in after them. Somebody has to be the one who goes in."

Edmund looked at me for a long time. Rain ran off his nose. Somewhere upstream the thunder rolled along the gorge like a barrel.

"You nearly died," he said finally, "for other people's history."

"Seems only fair," I said. "Other people's history nearly died for mine. Those names in there — some of them have to be river names. Ostrava sat on this water same as us." I'd worked that out in the marsh settlement, listening to the old ones talk. The past wasn't dead. It was just submerged, and I had a professional relationship with submerged things now.

He did something then that I have told exactly one person about, and I'm telling it now because the story should be straight somewhere. He put his hand on the back of my neck, the way my father never did, and he held it there for maybe three seconds, and he said, "Never again without a rope on you," and that was the whole speech, and it was enough. From Edmund it was a eulogy and a medal and a promise all at once.

Then Maren's throw-line smacked me in the face, because tenderness has a schedule on a riverboat and ours was over.

* * *

Getting the chest aboard was the worst work I have ever done, and I once re-caulked a seam in a hailstorm. The line went around the chest, the chest went over the side of the bar, and for one long, screaming moment the river had it again — had all of it, the box and the rope and nearly me, because I would not let go, because my hands had by now developed opinions of their own and all of them were *never again* — and then the Amberdale groaned, the winch Ilse was cranking took the strain, and the river gave up its dead a second time.

Ilse hauled me over the gunwale like a net full of fish and then, to my lasting surprise, hugged me, hard and quick, paper hands and all, and said nothing, which from Ilse was a paragraph.

The coffin-stowage trick was gone. That was the arithmetic Edmund did out loud, standing dripping at the wheel while Maren took us on down the gorge: the tarp was torn, the deck ring was bent, the chest had been seen by the river and the river tells things. We couldn't hide it in a coffin again. There would be no more coffins, no more quiet toll houses, no more passing. From here to Marrowgate, anyone who looked at the Amberdale would see what she carried.

"Then we stop hiding it," Ilse said.

Everyone turned. She was soaked to the skin, hair flat to her skull, standing over the chest with the tarp half off it like she was considering uncovering it entirely.

"Everyone on this river already knows something's in it," she said. "The grey boats know. The marsh knows. Ferro knows. The only people who don't know are the people it belongs to." She put her palm flat on the wet lid, over the seal. "Let them see it. Let every lock keeper and fuel dock man and ferry pilot see us carrying it, open, in daylight. Make it so famous that killing us for it becomes the confession."

Nobody said anything for a while. The gorge roared. Maren steered. And Edmund, who measures everything in miles, looked at the chest and then at me, and I watched him do a sum he'd been refusing to do since Corben's Landing.

"Rope on you," he said to me, which was not agreement, but was adjacent to it, and on the Amberdale, adjacent is how agreement arrives.

We ran the rest of the gorge that day, and I won't pretend I wasn't afraid — I was afraid in a new way now, the way you're afraid after the river has held you and given you back. Everything looked different from the deck of a boat you've been drowned out of. The walls were higher. The water was faster. And the chest sat on the deck under its torn tarp, and I found I couldn't look away from it, because it wasn't a box of paper anymore. It was the weight of a man on a rope, mine, and I'd chosen it, the way you choose a thing you'd dive into a flood for, and there is no unchoosing.

Near dusk the gorge opened its jaws and spat us into the lower channel, and the rain eased just enough to show us the sky, bruise-colored and low. Maren studied it, and I studied Maren, and what I saw on her face I didn't like.

"Back channels from here," she said. "Main river's watched. There's a marsh guide who knows a cut through Halfway Light — saves us a day and a half, and puts us where the grey boats can't follow."

"Can he be trusted?" Edmund asked.

Maren was quiet a moment too long, and Ilse, standing at the rail with her hand still on the chest, went very still, and I — honest Tamir, who had lied exactly once in his life and found it fit like a borrowed coat — felt the first cold thread of it go down my back, though I couldn't have said yet why.

"He's river folk," Maren said. "That used to be enough."
