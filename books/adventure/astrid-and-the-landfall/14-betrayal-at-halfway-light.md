## 14. Betrayal at Halfway Light

I have spent my life among documents, and documents have taught me one thing above all others: every betrayal is written down somewhere, in some ledger, with a date and a sum beside it. Treason is never an act of passion, whatever the ballads claim. It is bookkeeping. Someone adds a column, carries a figure, balances a life against a boat passage out of the flood zone, and finds the arithmetic acceptable.

His name was Doss. I should write that down first, the way the old magistrates would have — *Doss, marsh guide, sold the Amberdale for two berths on a fuel barge and dry boots in Marrowgate.* There. Now it exists outside my memory, where memory can be argued with.

But let me start earlier, because starting at the end is a magistrate's habit and I was trying, in those days, to be anything but a magistrate.

* * *

We left Maren's country on a grey morning with the river already swollen brown and impatient. The marsh folk had done what they could for the Amberdale — new planking over the bullet wound in her flank, seams packed with pitch and hope — and they had done more for Tamir, who came up from the sick bunk grey as ash with his arm bound tight against his chest, insisting he could stand a watch. He could not stand a watch. He stood one anyway, because Tamir's pride is a load-bearing structure and nobody wanted to find out what would collapse without it.

Maren took us into the back channels herself. She knew them the way I know catalogue systems — not as geography but as grammar, every slough and cutoff a sentence in a language only she was fluent in. For three days she read that language aloud to us: *hard to port past the dead cypress, the current lies there; take the left of the two channels though the right looks wider, the right floods first and drowns last.*

Doss was one of four guides the elders sent along, because the channels braided worse as we neared the gorges' mouth and no single pilot could hold all of it in their head. He was young, quick, funny in a way that made Edmund's jaw unclench by degrees. He had a scar through one eyebrow and a story about it that changed every time he told it. I liked him. That is the part I keep returning to, the part that sits in my chest like a swallowed stone. I liked him, and liking him was exactly what he was counting on.

On the second night, moored against a willow bank with rain ticking on the awning, I found him looking at my ledgers.

I had them out because I am who I am — I cannot stop reading them, cannot stop checking the chain of names against the chain of dates, as if repetition might wear the horror smooth. It does not. The Hollowing does not dull. You read *village of Serev, population entered at 1,140, remainder entered at 61*, and the numbers stay sharp as broken glass no matter how many times you pick them up.

"You read that every night," Doss said, settling across from me. "Same pages."

"Different pages. Same night, though." I closed the ledger. "It doesn't get easier."

"What happens if you don't make Marrowgate?"

"Then the men who signed those orders die in their beds, respected, and everyone who remembers Ostrava keeps remembering it alone until they're dead too, and then it never happened at all."

He nodded slowly, and I saw something move behind his eyes that I mistook for resolve. That is my confession, and I make it here so the record is complete: I mistook calculation for resolve, because I wanted the company. Three days on a boat teaches you to want your companions to be good. It is a weakness of small crews and I suspect it is a weakness of small nations too.

* * *

Halfway Light was the name the river people gave a leaning beacon tower on a gravel point where the marsh channels narrowed toward the gorge approach. Once, Maren told us, its lamp had guided grain barges through the narrows; now it listed like a drunk uncle and housed nothing but herons and old nests. We would pass it at dawn on the fourth day, she said, and after it the water would own us entirely — no banks wide enough to land, no channel shallow enough to stop, forty miles of white commitment before Marrowgate's delta.

Edmund stood beside me at the rail as the tower came up out of the morning fog, and I remember thinking how calm he looked. He had that quality, the captain — a stillness that wasn't peace but discipline, a man holding himself level the way you'd hold a full cup on a rough deck. Later I would learn what that stillness cost him and what it was hiding, but that morning I only envied it.

"Maren says the current swings hard around the point," he said. "We'll hug the far bank and—"

The sound reached us before the sight did: engines, more than one, running low and throttled, the way boats run when they don't want to be heard until it's too late.

Grey hulls slid out of the fog below the tower. Two of them, then a third behind, fanning across the channel with the crisp geometry of men who had drilled this exact maneuver. Ferro's river police. And Ferro himself stood in the bow of the lead boat — I could see him clearly even at that distance, a compact figure with his hands folded behind him, patient as a bailiff.

"They can't know our course," Edmund said. "Nobody knows our course except—" He stopped. I watched him arrive at it, watched the sentence die in his mouth, and I arrived there a half-second later and felt the stone in my chest turn over.

Doss was not aboard. Doss had gone ashore the previous evening at a fuel depot stop, to "scout the next junction." He had taken his pack. He had smiled at Tamir and said he'd see us at Halfway Light. He had asked me, casually, over cards, exactly what time Maren planned to run the point.

"He sold us," I said. It didn't sound like my voice.

Edmund was already moving. "MAREN!"

She was there before he finished the name — seventy years old and moving like the deck owed her money. One look at the grey boats and she understood everything, the way she understood everything about water: instantly, completely, without negotiation.

"Ferro's got the point blocked," she said. "But he's committed to the inside line. Current'll swing us wide if we take it full." Her hands were already on the wheel. "Hard aport, Edmund. Give me the engine and stay off my tiller."

"The shallows will tear the patch open—"

"The shallows will tear the *patch* open," she agreed. "The deep line tears the *boat* open. Choose your hole." And she grinned at him, that gap-toothed marsh grin, and something in her face made it impossible to argue, which I suspect was a skill she'd been practicing since before either of us was born.

The Amberdale swung. The patched hull groaned — a long, fibrous complaint, like an old man being asked to dance. Behind us the grey boats corrected, engines rising to a snarl, and the first rifle shots came across the water flat and ugly, slapping the river into white divots around our stern.

Tamir was at the stern rail with his one good arm and Edmund's spare pistol, returning fire with no accuracy whatsoever and complete moral commitment. "Get down!" Edmund roared at him, and Tamir shouted back something in his mother's tongue that I chose, generously, to interpret as compliance.

I did not get down. I want that recorded too. I crouched behind the deckhouse with the ledgers strapped against my chest under my coat — Ilse Vant, who had spent her whole life learning that paper must be protected, doing the one thing her training had actually prepared her for. My grandfather had died carrying less than this. I could at least be shot carrying it properly.

* * *

Here is what happened next, and I set it down slowly because it happened fast.

The current at Halfway Light did exactly what Maren said it would do. It took the Amberdale's stern and swung it wide, and for one glorious, impossible moment we were sliding past the grey boats with twenty yards of churning water between us and gaining — the whole plan working, the marsh grammar reading true, Maren's hands feathering the wheel in small corrections while bullets stitched the fog above her head.

Then the third boat, the one that had hung back, cut the corner.

Later I learned its pilot was a local man too — Ferro recruited river-born pilots precisely for moments like this, knowing the channels the way we did. He came out of the fog on our starboard bow with his rail already lined with marines, close enough that I could see the wet brass of their buttons, and he put himself squarely across the deep line. Not across our line. Across the *only other* line. The message was simple: go shallow and bleed, or come about and be taken.

Maren looked at the blocking boat. Then she looked at Edmund. And I swear on every ledger I have ever catalogued, she *smiled*.

"There's a third way," she said.

"No," Edmund said. He knew. I didn't know yet, but he knew, and the word came out of him cracked down the middle.

"The weir cut," she said. "Off the point, where the old millrace used to run. It's narrow. It's fast. It drops two feet in fifty yards and it's got rocks in it like teeth in a poor man's mouth." She was already turning the wheel. "But it comes out below them all, and no grey boat in the Governor's fleet will follow us into it, because their captains value their hulls and their careers."

"And you?" Edmund's voice. Just that. *And you.*

"I'm too old to value either." She glanced back at him, and her grin softened into something else, something I recognized from my grandmother's face in the last photograph I have of her — the look of a person handing over a burden and glad of the weight leaving. "Hold her steady after I'm gone, Captain. She'll want to broach in the drop. Counter-steer left, then ride it."

"*Maren.*"

"Boy," she said — and she was older than his father could have been, and he was fifty if he was a day, and she called him *boy*, and he obeyed — "you're carrying something worth more than me. That's just arithmetic. I've always trusted arithmetic."

Then she stepped away from the wheel, deliberately, fully, planting both hands on the spokes like a woman taking possession of land, and drove the Amberdale at the millrace gap with the grey boats converging and the marines firing and the fog swallowing everything but the white line of water ahead.

* * *

I will tell you what the millrace was like, because someone should, and Edmund never will, and Tamir tells it wrong.

It was like being swallowed by a country that hated us personally. The gap was barely wider than the boat. Rocks rose on both sides close enough to touch — Tamir touched one, with his shoulder, and spun across the deck cursing magnificently. The drop hit us like a verdict. The whole world went green and roaring, and the patched hull screamed along its entire length, and I lay on top of the ledgers on the deck planks and thought, with a clarity that still shames me: *if I die here, the signatures die with me, and Bray wins by default, and default is how he has won everything.*

Then we were through. Below the race the river widened, calmed, went brown and businesslike again, and the grey boats were nowhere — Ferro's pilots, as Maren promised, refusing the gap, circling above like dogs at a fence.

And Maren —

Maren was at the wheel. She had held it through the whole of it, through the gap and the drop and the rocks, holding the tiller-line true exactly as long as the boat needed her, and the moment the boat didn't need her anymore — the moment we were through and safe and *done* — a marine's bullet from the blocking boat, fired in the last second before the rocks took us from their view, found her through the base of her throat.

She died standing up. Both hands on the wheel. Steering us into the rest of our lives.

Edmund got to her first. He lowered her to the deck with a care that broke my heart more than the blood did — laid her down against the deckhouse, arranged her arms, closed her eyes, all of it methodical, all of it the motions of a man who had done this before and knew there was no other way to survive doing it. Then he knelt there with his forehead nearly touching hers and did not move, and did not make a sound, and the silence coming off him was worse than any grief I have ever heard expressed.

Tamir sat down hard on the deck and cried like a boy, openly, horribly, his bandaged arm forgotten. I stood at the rail and watched the Halfway Light tower recede into the fog behind us, leaning, blind, its lamp dark for twenty years, and I understood that it would be dark forever now, because the last person alive who remembered why it mattered had just steered out of the world.

* * *

We buried her at noon according to marsh custom, which meant we didn't bury her at all. The river takes its own; the marsh folk say the Vellin is a better graveyard than any hillside because it moves, and the dead who move keep traveling, and travelers are never really gone. Edmund sewed her into canvas — his own awning, the good piece — with a coil of her own tarred line at her feet, and Tamir spoke the words he knew from his mother's people, and I spoke the ones from mine, the old Ostravan committal, the one that begins *we return you to the current from which all records flow*. It is a documentarian's prayer. It assumes everything ends up filed somewhere.

Edmund said nothing at the graveside. But afterward, standing at the wheel in the position she had died in, he said, quietly, to me or to the river or to her:

"She called me boy."

"Yes," I said.

"Nobody's called me that in thirty years." His hands were steady on the spokes. Everything about him was steady; that was the terrible thing, watching steadiness perform itself at that cost. "I keep thinking I should have argued harder. Taken the deep line. Fought."

"She wouldn't have let you."

"No." Almost a laugh. "No, she'd have tied me to the mast." He was quiet a while. The river ran. Somewhere ahead, unseen, the gorges waited with their rock gates and their rope-and-winch passages, and beyond them Marrowgate, and beyond that a trial that now had a name to speak first in its opening silence.

"Vant," he said finally. "When we get there — when Rook convenes his court — read her into the record. Whatever the procedure is. However it's done. She bought this with more than any of us."

"I will," I said. And then, because I am my grandfather's granddaughter and precision is the only inheritance I trust: "Full name, date, and manner of death. Entered first."

He nodded once, and turned the boat downstream.

* * *

That night, anchored in a willow brake with the fog closing in like a lid, I opened the ledgers one more time. Not to the massacre pages. To the front — the flyleaf of the first volume, where the last Chief Magistrate of Ostrava had written, in ink gone brown as old blood:

*A law is not a building. Buildings burn. A law is a debt the living owe the dead, and debts survive the bankruptcy of nations.*

Underneath, in a child's careful hand — my hand, aged eight, visiting his chambers for the last time — someone had copied the sentence out as practice. My grandfather had graded it. In the margin, in his tidy clerk's script: *Good. Now keep it.*

I had thought, for twenty years, that I kept it by remembering. Sitting in the fog with Maren's blood still on the deck boards beneath my feet, I finally understood that remembering is only the interest. The principal is paid forward. Somebody has to carry the debt into the rooms where it can be collected.

Two days to the gorges. The monsoon's leading edge was a bruise on the southern horizon, and Ferro's boats were somewhere behind us in the dark, and the man who had sold us was sleeping dry tonight on a fuel barge with his two clean berths and his clean conscience, and none of it — none of it — changed what was written in these pages.

I closed the ledger, strapped it to my chest, and went to tell Edmund that whatever the gorges cost, we were going to pay it.

Someone had signed those orders. Someone was going to hear them read aloud.
