## 15. The Broken Boat

The engine died an hour after Halfway Light, and I can't say I blamed it. Twenty years she'd turned over for me without complaint, and in the last week alone she'd taken a bullet hole below the waterline, a marsh patch job held together with resin and prayer, and a monsoon's opening argument. When the water climbed over her manifolds and she coughed twice and quit, it felt less like mechanical failure and more like a crew member finally sitting down on the deck and refusing to get up.

"Ed," Tamir said from the engine hatch, his voice coming up through the floorboards like a confession through a grate. His shoulder was still bound, his arm useless, so he'd been supervising rather than fixing, which he hated worse than the wound itself. "She's flooded. Not fouled — flooded. River's inside everything."

"How long?"

"If I had two days, dry weather, and a machine shop? Maybe never."

"That's three answers."

"Yeah." A pause, and then, quieter: "I'm sorry, Captain."

"You want to apologize to something, apologize to the river. She's the one who beat us."

I said it wry because that's what I do when the alternative is saying something true. It's a habit older than my marriage had been.

Maren was dead. I hadn't said that sentence out loud yet either. We'd wrapped her in sailcloth at dawn and slid her over the side where the current bent south toward the delta, because Maren had told me once — laughing, drunk on plum brandy, steering with two fingers — that if she ever went into the Vellin, she wanted to go the whole way, all the miles, no locks, no tolls, no stopping. So we gave her that. The river took her the way it takes everything: without comment.

Which left three of us on a broken boat drifting toward Marrowgate with a chest full of dead republic and a police captain somewhere behind us who did not, as far as I knew, accept surrender gracefully.

* * *

Ilse came up on deck carrying the awning canvas like a woman carrying a flag she wasn't sure belonged to her country anymore. She'd been below with the ledgers since the ambush, and she had that look people get after they've read their family's name in a massacre order — dry-eyed, precise, dangerous.

"The awning," she said. "You were looking at it."

"I was looking at it."

"You're thinking sails."

"I'm thinking that this boat has no engine, a cracked rudder, and a current doing most of our work for us. What she doesn't have is steerage. Sails would give us that. Barely."

"Barely is a word you've been using a lot lately."

"It's the only honest word left on this boat."

We rigged them by afternoon. Two masts lashed from the cargo booms, the awning canvas cut and grommeted with whatever Tamir could punch holes through one-handed while swearing in three languages. The result was not sailing rigging. It was a suggestion of sailing rigging, a rumor of canvas, two grey wings that caught the monsoon wind the way a drunk catches a doorframe — eventually, and leaning hard.

But she moved. That was the miracle nobody aboard was rude enough to say out loud. The *Amberdale*, holed and patched, rudder groaning like a bad knee, under scraps of her own awning, crept down the flooded channel at maybe three knots, and every knot of it was borrowed from the storm that was trying to kill us.

Tamir slept below, feverish, his wound hot to the touch. Ilse took the tiller. I sat amidships with my back against the chest — old habit now; I'd stopped being able to sleep unless I could feel the lead through my shirt — and watched the drowned country go by.

That's what it was, the flood plain. Drowned country. Fence posts standing to their shoulders in brown water. A church roof with the bell still hanging in it, ringing occasionally when the wind found it, a sound like a slow alarm nobody was left to answer. Cattle on levee islands, huddled and stupid with fear. And everywhere, everywhere, the smell of wet earth and diesel and something floral underneath it that I couldn't name and didn't want to.

Ilse steered us past a farmhouse with its second story above water and laundry still strung between the windows, sheets snapping in the wind like signal flags from a country that had already surrendered.

"Do you see much of this?" she asked. "The floods?"

"Every monsoon. The Vellin doesn't flood. It repossesses."

"And people just come back afterward?"

"They rebuild on the same footings. Every year. My father called it optimism. I call it stubbornness, but I've never been able to tell those apart at this latitude."

She laughed — short, surprised at herself, guilty. First laugh since Maren went into the water. I counted it as cargo.

* * *

Night came down grey on grey, and the wind steadied enough that Ilse could leave the tiller lashed. We ate cold rice out of the same pot and didn't talk about Ferro or Bray or the trial, because there are conversations a boat can't hold and survive, and we'd had our ration.

It was Ilse who broke the rule, because she breaks rules professionally.

"You knew her thirty years," she said. "Maren."

"Thirty-one. She signed on before I owned the boat. I bought the *Amberdale* off a man named Corben — yes, that Corben, the landing's named for his grandfather — and Maren came with the purchase, like a lien. Best lien I ever carried."

"What was she running from?"

"Nothing. That was the thing about Maren. Everyone on this river is running from something or toward something, and she was just... here. She used to say the river was the only place that never asked her a question." I turned the tin cup in my hands. "I asked her once, late, after brandy, whether she'd ever been married. She said, 'Once, to a man who measured everything.' Then she laughed and wouldn't say more. You didn't push Maren. Pushing Maren was how you learned to swim unexpectedly."

"She sounds like my grandfather."

"Your grandfather hid a republic in a chest. Different skill set."

"The same one, maybe. Keeping things safe by keeping them quiet."

We let that sit. The canvas overhead snapped and filled and slacked. Somewhere ahead, far off, a delta foghorn moaned — Marrowgate announcing itself to ships that couldn't see it yet.

Then Ilse asked the question I'd been sailing away from for fifteen years.

"Why miles, Edmund?"

I looked at her.

"You count," she said. "Out loud, sometimes. Distances. Corben's Landing to Weir Nine, forty-one miles. Weir Nine to the narrows, sixty-three. You told me the length of the gorge run to the half-mile. You know the whole river like a ledger. But you never talk about before. About anywhere that isn't a distance. Why does a man measure his life in miles?"

There it was, laid open on the deck between us like a fish gutted at market. I could have done what I always did — made a joke, checked a line, found work for my hands. Fifteen years of practice. But the boat was broken, the night was black, Maren was in the river, and a man can only run so many miles before even he notices the road.

So I told her.

* * *

"There was a town," I said. "Upstream. Kessel's Bend. You won't find it on charts anymore either — different reason than Ostrava. Fever, not politics. Sweating sickness, the kind that comes up out of the marshes in a bad summer and walks through a town house to house like a landlord collecting rent.

"I was twenty-six. I ran cargo there regular — timber down, salt and lamp oil up. And there was a woman. Ana. Schoolteacher. She had a way of looking at you when you lied that made lying feel like a physical ailment, so mostly I didn't. Married her in the spring. Had a daughter by the next spring. Lira. Six pounds, four ounces, born during high water, and I remember thinking — I remember this exactly, Ilse, because it's the last clean thought I had for a decade — I remember thinking, *now I measure in years.* Her years. All of them. I'd take the boat up and down the river and every mile I ran, I'd think, that's one mile closer to coming home, and home had a face and a weight and a laugh like a door hinge that needed oiling and I loved that sound, God help me, I loved that creaking door-hinge laugh.

"Then the fever came."

The canvas cracked overhead. I waited it out.

"It started at the far end of the bend and worked its way along the street like it was reading. The doctor died third day. The priest died fifth. By the seventh day the town council put a chain across the bridge road and posted men with shotguns, and the word went out to the river traffic: no landings. Nobody in, nobody out. Quarantine. Which was correct. Which was, I want you to understand, medically correct, and I have spent fifteen years knowing that and it has never once helped.

"My boat was at the dock. Half loaded. Ana sent Lira's wet nurse down with a note — she wrote notes, even then, even dying, she wrote in that schoolteacher hand — and the note said stay away. Said don't come up. Said the baby needs you well more than she needs you near.

"And I did what the note said."

I heard my own voice go flat, the way it goes when I read distances off a chart.

"I cast off. I took the *Amberdale* downstream, and I told myself I'd wait at Corben's Landing, two days, until it passed, and then come back. Two days became four because a lock jammed. Four became nine because I let a lock jam become an excuse become another excuse. Eleven days, Ilse. I sat eleven days downstream of my own wife and daughter, measuring the miles back up to them — forty-one, then fifty-two when I drifted further, then seventy — and telling myself that miles were the same as time, that I could make them up later, that distance was just time wearing a disguise.

"On the twelfth day a timber raft came down with two men on it, the only ones left of a crew of nine, and I asked about Kessel's Bend, and one of them looked at me the way you look at a man you're deciding whether to hit, and he said, 'Bend's done.'

"Ana lasted nine days. Lira lasted six. I wasn't there for either. I was seventy miles downstream being careful."

Ilse didn't say anything. That was right. There's nothing to say to that part, and she was smart enough to know it, and kind enough to let the silence do the work.

"I sold nothing, changed nothing, kept the boat," I went on, because now the door was open the whole flood came through. "Kept running the river. Kept counting miles. Because miles are honest, Ilse. A mile is a mile. It doesn't die. It doesn't blame you. You cover it or you don't, and when you've covered it, it's behind you, finished, done, paid. Miles settle their accounts. Years don't. Years just accrue. Fifteen years of them, accruing interest, and every year I thought, next year I'll go back to the bend, next year I'll stand at the gate of that churchyard, and every year the miles got longer instead of shorter, which is impossible, which is the one thing I know about rivers that isn't true of anything else in this world.

"Maren knew. Figure she always knew. Never said a word, thirty-one years, except once — last month, at Weir Nine, before any of this, before the chest, when it was just cargo and weather. I was counting off the reach to the narrows and she said, 'Ed. You know what a mile is?' And I said, 'Five thousand two hundred eighty feet,' because I'm insufferable. And she said, 'No. A mile is a year you're not living.'"

The foghorn sounded again, closer. Or I imagined it closer. Hope does that with distances.

"So that's why miles," I said. "Not because I love them. Because they're the only measurement that never asked me to stay."

* * *

Ilse was quiet a long moment. Then she stood, crossed the tilting deck, and sat down beside me against the chest, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, which neither of us acknowledged, because some cargoes you carry without marking the manifest.

"My grandfather used to say," she offered, "that Ostrava didn't fall the day the maps changed. It fell the day the last magistrate decided keeping faith was someone else's job. He said evil doesn't need your agreement. It just needs your mileage. Your distance. Everybody staying exactly where they are, doing exactly what they're paid to do, seventy miles downstream of everyone who needs them."

"Your grandfather sounds like he'd have made a fine insufferable river captain."

"He'd have hated boats. He got seasick in rowboats." A pause. "Edmund. You couldn't have saved them."

"No."

"You know that."

"I've known it for fifteen years. Knowing it and believing it live in different towns and don't write."

"But?"

"But." I looked downriver, into the dark where Marrowgate was supposed to be. "But I've been casting off from things ever since. People, mostly. Crews that got too familiar. Ports that got too comfortable. Anywhere a life might start accruing. I figured if I never stayed, I'd never again be the man leaving. Didn't occur to me — took me fifteen years and a chest of dead men's paper to figure it out — that a man who never stays is always leaving. There's no version of it where you're not the one walking away. You just spread it thin. A hundred small departures instead of one big one. Cheaper per unit. Same total cost."

"And now?"

"Now I'm delivering a dead republic to a dying judge so that a living governor answers for a massacre, on a boat held together with awning canvas and spite, and every person aboard has bled for it, and one of them is in the river." I laughed, and it came out rougher than I intended. "Somewhere in the last three weeks I stopped being able to cast off. They got lines on me. Tamir. You. Even her, God rest her, the old pirate. I'm moored, Ilse. Against my will and better judgment. And the strange thing — the genuinely absurd thing — is that I keep checking the lines like a man afraid they'll slip."

The wind shifted then, backing around to the southeast, filling both our ragged sails at once, and the *Amberdale* leaned and gathered herself and went a little faster, as if the river had heard all that and decided to stop mocking us for five minutes.

"Years, then," Ilse said quietly. "We'll try measuring in years."

"We'll try."

"One year from tonight. Wherever we are. We compare counts."

"That's not how years work."

"Neither are miles, apparently. You taught me that."

* * *

Toward morning the rain thinned and the fog came up off the delta in long white banks, and out of those banks, dead ahead, low and dark, rose a shape that shouldn't have been there: a headland where no chart showed one, green and black with mangrove, and beyond it — faint, intermittent, unmistakable — the sound of Marrowgate's flood sirens, rising and falling like something breathing.

And under the sirens, threading between the banks where the channel narrowed, moving fast with the flood tide, came the wake of a boat. Grey hull. No lights. Running quiet.

Headed the same direction we were.

"Ferro," Ilse said.

"Or someone who wants us to think Ferro," I said, and put my hand on the tiller, and steered the broken *Amberdale* toward the sirens.
