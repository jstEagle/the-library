## 16. Marrowgate Rising

### Edmund

The Amberdale came up the last reach of the Vellin like an old man climbing stairs — one groan at a time, with frequent rests and no dignity whatsoever.

We had two sails made of awning canvas, a rudder held together with wire and prayer, and an engine that had drowned so thoroughly it might as well have been baptized. The current did most of the work. I did the steering. Ilse and Tamir took turns bailing, which was less a job than a philosophy: you threw water out of the boat because refusing to was a kind of surrender, and we had all decided somewhere back in the gorges that we were not going to be surrendered to anything, least of all a river.

Ahead, Marrowgate announced itself the way it always had — not by skyline but by sound. The flood sirens. Three long notes rising over the water, repeated, passed from one district tower to the next until the whole delta seemed to be moaning in its sleep. Every soul on that river knew what they meant. High water coming. Get your boats up on the blocks, get your goods off the low shelves, get your children counted.

"Sounds almost friendly," Tamir said. His arm was still strapped to his chest, the shoulder wound three weeks healing and angry about every mile of it. "Like the city's singing us in."

"It's warning us," I said. "There's a difference."

"There usually is with you."

I didn't answer, because he was right, and because just then I saw what was waiting for us where the river widened into the harbor roads.

Boats. Grey ones. Ferro's river police, laid across every legal berth like a hand across a doorway. Two at the customs wharf. One at the ferry landing. Another idling off the coal quay with its searchlight already sweeping the water, though the light was grey daylight and there was nothing to find but rain.

"They know we're coming," Ilse said quietly. She'd come up without my hearing her, which she'd gotten good at. "The marsh network. Someone sold us once; someone else is buying us now."

"Or they're waiting for every boat on the river," Maren would have said, if Maren were alive to say it, "because half of them are running something." But Maren wasn't alive, and the boats weren't waiting for smugglers. I could feel it in my teeth, the way you feel weather. They were waiting for us.

I looked at my ship. My beautiful, ruined ship. Canvas sails, cracked rudder, a hull that had been patched by marsh folk with reed-baskets' worth of pitch and stubbornness. There was no way through those berths. There was no way past those searchlights. And behind us the monsoon was coming down the gorges like a debt collector.

"Well, Captain," Tamir said. "What's the maneuver?"

And that was when the docks answered for me.

* * *

### Ilse

They came out of the stilt districts like the tide deciding to come in early.

I had seen crowds before. I had grown up in Ostrava before the Hollowing, in a city of processions and queues and the particular grey obedience of people who have learned that gathering is dangerous. What came down Marrowgate's ramps and gangplanks and rope-ladders that morning was nothing like obedience. It was hundreds of river people — fisherfolk, coal-heavers, net-menders, women with market baskets, boys who should have been in school — and they moved with the unhurried certainty of people doing something they had already agreed to do.

They swarmed the legal berths. Not violently. Worse than violently: usefully. A customs launch trying to hold position found itself surrounded by a dozen punts whose owners needed, urgently, to discuss mooring fees. A police cutter at the ferry landing was hemmed in by barges unloading cargo that appeared to be mostly other barges. Somewhere a man began loudly auctioning eels, and the auction was either genuine or the most committed piece of theater I have ever witnessed, and either way two constables disappeared into it like stones into a pond.

"They're hiding us," I said. "The marsh network — the elders sent word ahead."

Edmund stood at the tiller with his jaw set in the shape of a man refusing to be moved by anything, including gratitude. "They're making trouble," he corrected. "Hiding implies we asked."

"You didn't have to ask. That's what it means, when people decide you're theirs."

He glanced at me then — quickly, the way he looked at things he didn't want to examine too closely — and turned back to the water. "Where do we go? We can't make any berth. They'll have our description at every office on the delta."

It was Tamir who answered, pointing with his good arm toward the eastern flats, beyond the last of the working wharves, where the hulks went to die.

"The graveyard," he said. "Look."

### Tamir

I want it written down somewhere, since apparently I'm the one who notices things: the gunship *Vigilant* had been beached on the Marrowgate mudflats for eleven years, scheduled for scrap by four successive administrations, each of which had scheduled it more firmly than the last. She sat there on her keel like a whale nobody wanted to claim, rust streaking her flanks, her guns long gone, her name half-eaten by salt. Every kid in the stilt district played on her. Every adult pretended not to see them.

She was also, I realized as we limped past the coal quay, hollow. And dark. And sitting directly between us and the open sea road, which meant she was also cover.

"Take us behind her," I told Edmund.

"Behind her is mud."

"Behind her is *not being seen*. Mud dries. Being seen doesn't."

He gave me the look he gives me when I've said something sensible and he resents it. Then he put the tiller over, and the Amberdale — bless her rotten heart — leaned into the turn like she understood exactly what we were asking.

The problem was the crowd. Word travels fast on a waterfront, faster than police, faster than sirens, and by the time we rounded the breakwater there were people on the mudflats. Dozens of them. Wading out toward us through the shallows in their rolled-up trousers, arms out, and my first stupid thought was *they're going to take the chest*, and my second thought was shame, because look at them. Look at their faces. Nobody comes wading through flood-mud grinning like that to rob you.

A woman with forearms like dock rope caught our bowline before I could throw it, as if she'd been catching boats her whole life, which she probably had. Behind her, men were already wading in under our hull.

"She'll ground here," Edmund shouted down. "She draws too much—"

"She'll ground where we tell her to ground!" the woman shouted back, laughing. "Captain, we've been moving wrecks on this flat since before you were born. Let go your lines and let the river folk work!"

And they worked. I have never seen anything like it, and I grew up on this river. Thirty, forty people in the shallows, taking the Amberdale's weight on their shoulders and their rollers and their long practice, walking her up the flat inch by inch while the tide ran out beneath her, calling numbers in a dialect I half knew. They walked her into the shadow of the *Vigilant*'s flank, into the black gap between the hulk's stern and a rotting coal barge, and they wedged her there with spars and barrels until from the water you'd have seen nothing but rust and garbage and the ordinary wreckage of a city that throws away its ships and forgets them.

Then they threw tarpaulins over our masts, shook hands all around as if we'd concluded a business arrangement, and waded back ashore to become, instantly, ordinary people again.

The woman with the rope forearms climbed aboard last. She looked around at our broken rigging, our bloodstained deck planking — Maren's blood, though nobody said so — and her grin faded into something older and sadder.

"You're the ones from the marshes," she said. "With the papers." It wasn't a question. "Elder Sefa sent word by eel-boat yesterday. She said: when they come, hide them like you'd hide your own children."

"And do you?" Ilse asked quietly. From the rail. Always from the rail, that one, watching everything.

The woman looked at her for a long moment. At the canvas bundle under her arm that held the ledgers. At the seal stamped into the lead chest, which we'd never managed to keep covered, and which she clearly recognized, because her mouth went tight the way mouths go tight around grief that has waited a long time.

"We're scrap people," she said. "Nobody's ever wanted what we keep. Now somebody finally does." She spat over the side, ceremonially, in the direction of the harbor police. "Welcome to Marrowgate. Keep your heads down till dark. And Captain — your rudder's worse than you think. My brother's a shipwright. He owes me."

### Ferro

By evening I had forty reports and no boat.

Every berth on the delta reported the same thing: crowds, confusion, eels. My constables came back with wet boots and empty hands and stories about auctioneers and mooring disputes and one old woman who had lectured a marine for twenty minutes on the proper handling of a punt pole and would not be interrupted. I have policed this river for nine years. I know what a coordinated crowd looks like. This was a coordinated crowd wearing the costume of chaos, and the costume fit perfectly, because it wasn't entirely a costume. These people genuinely did need to discuss mooring fees.

"They're in the city," Sergeant Doss said, dripping on my deck plates. "Or they never arrived. Can't prove either."

"They arrived. The marsh network doesn't move that chest two hundred miles and then lose it in a crowd." I stood at the rail and looked east, toward the flats, where the scrap hulks sat in the failing light. The *Vigilant* was a black shape against a blacker sky. I'd served a year aboard her as a junior officer, before they beached her. I knew how much shadow a ship that size could hold.

"Search the flats?" Doss asked.

"With what? I've got six boats and a city full of flood refugees and a governor who wants results by morning." I watched the water. Rain starting again, fine and needling. "Post watches on the sea road. Nothing leaves the delta without inspection. And Doss —"

"Sir?"

"If anyone asks, we're stretched thin on flood duty. Which we are." I hadn't decided yet why I was saying that. I told myself it was tactics. A search of the flats would fail publicly, and public failure feeds the very story Bray is drowning in. That was the reason. That was the whole reason.

Doss believed me. Doss believes everything I say; it's one of the reasons I can't stand him some days.

I stayed at the rail after he'd gone, and I thought about page one. About a signature I'd been ordered to protect for years without ever reading the document it sat on. About a woman on a fuel depot deck who had looked at me like I was a man being offered a door, and about whether I'd walked through it or just stood in the frame.

The rain got heavier. Out on the flats, the *Vigilant* held its darkness, and whatever it held inside it held silently, the way rivers hold drowned things until they're ready.

* * *

### Edmund

Dark came down on Marrowgate like a lid.

From the deck of the Amberdale, wedged in her coffin of rust and tarps, I could hear the city breathing — sirens, generators, the endless argument of a waterfront at high water. The shipwright came at dusk with four helpers and a lantern shielded behind canvas, and they did things to our rudder that involved swearing in three languages and a fitting cut from the *Vigilant*'s own bones, which struck me as a kind of poetry. By midnight she steered again. Badly. But badly is a direction.

Ilse sat beside me on the hatch coaming, the ledger-bundle in her lap, watching the lights of the patrol boats crawl along the sea road like patient beetles.

"They'll find us eventually," she said. "This city isn't big enough to hide in forever."

"No," I agreed. "But we don't need forever. We need one man."

"Rook." She said the name carefully, the way you carry something fragile in your mouth. "If he's even alive. If he's well enough. If he'll do it."

"He'll do it." I surprised myself with how sure I sounded. "Men who spend thirty years keeping a law nobody enforces don't stop because the room gets cold."

She was quiet a while. Down in the stilt district, a child laughed somewhere, absurdly, in the middle of all that rain.

"Edmund. Those people today. On the flats. They knew the seal. The woman — she looked at it like she was looking at a grave." Her voice dropped. "Twenty years I thought I was carrying these papers alone. Carrying them for ghosts. Today I found out the ghosts have descendants."

"That's usually how it works," I said. "Ghosts are just people nobody wrote down."

Tamir stuck his head up from below, one-armed, grinning. "Shipwright says come below. Says there's a man here to see the captain. Came by punt, wouldn't give a name, paid a boy a coin to sit in the rain and whistle if police came close."

Ilse's hand closed on the bundle. Mine went to the boathook, out of habit older than sense.

"A man," I said. "Old or young?"

"Old," Tamir said. "Coughs like a bellows. And Captain — he's wearing a judge's coat. Or what's left of one."

We went down the ladder into the lamplit dark of my broken ship, and there, folded onto my own cabin bench like a letter that had taken twenty years to arrive, sat a gaunt old man in a threadbare black coat, with the Ostravan scales embroidered at the collar in faded silver, looking around at my charts and my clutter with the expression of a man reading a familiar book.

"Captain Amberdale," he said, in a voice worn smooth as river stone. "My name is Anselm Rook. I'm told you've brought my court some business."

I looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at the ledgers. Outside, the rain kept falling on the hull of the dead gunship, steady as applause.

"Judge," I said. "You'd better sit comfortably. It's a long record."

"I imagine," Anselm Rook said, and opened his hands, "that it begins with a massacre."

It did. And for the first time since the Vellin coughed that chest into my life, I felt the miles stop counting — because ahead of us, at last, there was something better than distance.
