## 17. Judge Rook

The gunship smelled of rust and old smoke and, beneath that, something almost like incense — lamp oil burning in a place where no one had bothered to sweep in years. The river folk who'd smuggled us aboard called her the *Vigilant*, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer. She lay beached on the mudflats south of the scrap berths, listing to port like a drunk leaning on a friend, scheduled for the cutting torches come autumn. Her guns had been stripped decades ago. What remained was iron bones and shadowed corridors, and somewhere inside her, if the marsh network's message could be believed, an old man who used to be the law.

Edmund went first. Of course he did. He'd been going first since Corben's Landing, though he'd have denied under oath that he'd chosen any side at all. Tamir came behind me with his arm still bound from the bullet, holding a lantern low, and I carried the chest.

I had carried it for eleven days now. My arms knew its weight better than they knew their own.

"Deck plate," Edmund murmured, and we stepped over a gap where corrosion had eaten through to the river below. Black water moved down there, patient, breathing with the tide. The whole hulk seemed to breathe. Somewhere above us a loose cable clanked against a mast with a rhythm like a slow clock.

"You know," I whispered, "in Ostrava, entering a courthouse armed was a contempt offense."

"We're not armed."

"I'm carrying eleven days of other people's deaths. That counts."

Tamir made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been his shoulder. Edmund held up a hand and we stopped at a door — a real door, oak, salvaged from some drowned building and fitted into the gunship's steel frame by someone with more reverence than sense. A brass plate had been screwed above the lintel, green with age but legible:

**COURT OF THE THIRD CIRCUIT — IN RECESS**

Not *closed*. Not *abolished*. *In recess*, as though the judge had merely stepped out for a pipe and might return any moment, and woe betide the litigant who talked too loudly while waiting.

Edmund knocked. Three times, evenly, the way you knock on a door you intend to be answered.

"Enter," said a voice like dry paper folding.

* * *

The cabin beyond had been a wardroom once, I think — long table, high portholes, benches bolted to the deck. Now it was a library drowning slowly. Books everywhere: stacked on the table, wedged along the bulkheads, piled in towers that leaned at angles engineers would call impossible and librarians would call Tuesday. Candles burned in jars on every surface, and the light they threw was warm and unsteady, making the whole room flicker like a memory of itself.

And at the far end of the table sat an old man so thin his dressing gown seemed to hang on a coat rack, with a blanket over his knees and a pistol lying on the table beside an inkwell, close enough to reach, far enough away to be a statement rather than a threat.

He looked at us over spectacles mended with wire. His eyes were yellowed at the edges — illness, age, or both — but they were not confused eyes. They were eyes that had spent forty years deciding things, and were prepared to decide about us.

"You're not scrap thieves," he said. "Scrap thieves knock worse and apologize less."

"No, sir," Edmund said. "We were told there was a judge aboard."

"There is a man aboard." The old man's mouth did something that had once been a smile. "Whether there is still a judge is a question of metaphysics. State your business. Quickly. I'm told my heart keeps irregular hours and I've stopped arguing with it."

I set the chest on the table. The sound it made — lead-lined, waterlogged, twenty years drowned and three weeks hunted — was heavier than wood has any right to sound. The old man looked at it. Then at the seal stamped into its lid. Then at me.

Something moved behind his face. It wasn't surprise exactly. It was more like what happens when you hear your own name spoken in a language everyone else has forgotten.

"Where," he said quietly, "did you get that?"

"The Vellin River gave it up," I said. "After a storm, below the old Ostravan floodgates. The captain here nearly rolled it back overboard — he's superstitious about drowned things."

"Drowned things," the old man repeated. "Yes. We are all drowned things, girl. Some of us just haven't finished sinking." He drew the pistol toward himself, and for one absurd heartbeat I thought he meant to use it — and then he simply turned it around and laid it on the bench beside him, out of his own reach. "There. Now I can't do anything foolish. Sit. Both of you. The boy can stand; standing boys make me feel supervised."

Tamir stayed by the door, grinning faintly. Edmund sat the way he did everything — carefully, as if the chair might invoice him later.

"My name," the old man said, "was Anselm Rook. Third Circuit of the Republic of Ostrava. You'll notice I say *was*. The republic is gone. Its courts are gone. Its law—" he gestured at the books around him, hundreds of them, rescued volume by volume from the Hollowing "—is a rumor kept alive by an old man with bad lungs and a candle budget. So before you open that chest, young woman, tell me why I should look. Because I will tell you now, freely: whatever is in there, I cannot try it. There is no court. There is no writ. There is no Ostrava. There is only this ship, dying on schedule, and me dying slightly ahead of it."

"That's not true," I said.

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "You'll want to be more specific. Judges respect specificity."

"The law isn't gone. The *state* is gone. Those aren't the same thing." I heard my own voice come out harder than I'd planned, and I didn't soften it. I'd rehearsed this argument for eleven days — against Ferro's patrol boats, against the gorges, against the rope winches, against the dark after Maren died — and I was not going to lose it now to an old man's modesty. "A law survives as long as someone keeps faith with it. That's all law ever was. Not the marble buildings — those are just where faith gets stored when people get tired of carrying it."

"Poetry," Rook said. "Admissible in some jurisdictions."

"You kept the books." I swept a hand at the cabin. "Twenty years. On a scrap hulk. With candles. If the law died with Ostrava, what is all this?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, the cable clanked. Water breathed beneath the deck plates.

"Sentiment," Rook said at last, but the word came out cracked down the middle.

"Habit," Edmund offered unexpectedly. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged. "Some habits are worth keeping. I keep a boat that should've sunk twice. Doesn't mean she's dead. Means nobody's let her finish."

Rook studied him for a long moment. "And you are?"

"Edmund Amberdale. Riverboat captain. I haul cargo."

"He hauled us," I said. "Past grey boats, past a toll officer, through the gorges in the leading edge of the monsoon. He lost a crew member. Maren. She held the tiller at Halfway Light so the rest of us could live." My voice caught on the name and I let it. "She's part of why that chest is on your table, Judge. So don't tell me there's no court. There's already been testimony. There's already been a price paid. All that's missing is someone with the authority to write it down."

Rook took off his spectacles. His hands shook — not the nervous shake Ilse Vant the archivist had faked at Corben's Landing, but the deep tremor of a body negotiating its own surrender — and he polished the lenses on his sleeve for longer than lenses require.

"Open it," he said finally.

* * *

I unlatched the chest and lifted out the ledgers, and the smell of them filled the cabin — river mud and mildew and ink, the particular smell of paper that has been to the bottom and come back angry. I opened the first volume to the page I had read so many times I could recite it, and turned it toward him.

Rook read. I watched his lips move slightly, the way old men read, the way they read when the words are names they know.

"Magistrate Delvine's hand," he murmured. "I'd know it anywhere. She wrote her *f*s like anchors." He turned a page. Another. The candle flames bent in a draft from the porthole and straightened again. "Requisition orders. Troop dispositions. Grain seizures in the Hollow districts — dated, countersigned..." He stopped. His finger rested on the bottom of a page, on a signature, and did not move for a long time.

"Cassel Bray," he said.

"Yes."

"He signed it himself. Not a deputy. Not a stamp. His own hand." Rook looked up at me, and his yellowed eyes were wet, and furious, and terribly clear. "I knew him. He sat in my courtroom when he was thirty years old — some land dispute, nothing — and he thanked me afterward for the fairness of the proceedings. He *thanked* me. And then he grew ambitious, and the republic got inconvenient, and he signed an order to erase four hundred thousand people and their courts and their graves, and then he became a governor, respectable, with a marble house above the flood line." He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "How many volumes?"

"Seven. Complete. Dates, orders, requisitions, and the after-reports — the ones that admit what the orders accomplished. There are witness statements too, from the marsh settlements. Descendants. Survivors. One woman recognized the seal and wept; her grandmother came out of the Hollowing with nothing but a story, and the story matches the ledgers line for line."

"And you brought them to me. To a dead court on a dying ship." Rook laughed — a small, rusty, astonished sound. "Why? There are delta authorities. Newspapers. The borderlands commission. Any of them would print this."

"Any of them would *lose* this," I said. "Bray owns the commissions. He owns the newspapers' advertisers. He owns the delta magistrates' appointments. The moment these ledgers enter a lawful system, they enter *his* system, and lawful systems are very good at misplacing inconvenient documents — I know, I was trained to archive them." I leaned forward. "But you're not in his system, Judge. You're outside it. That's not your weakness. That's the only thing left that makes you dangerous."

"Outside the law, I'm not a judge. I'm an old man playing dress-up with a brass plate."

"Then convene something else." The argument I'd been building for eleven days came out of me all at once, the way floodwater comes through a broken gate. "Don't call it a court if the word frightens you. Call it a tribunal. Call it a hearing. Call it Tuesday. But the river people are here, Judge — the marsh settlements, the smugglers, the dockworkers of Marrowgate, the descendants of everyone Bray tried to drown. They hid our boat inside this hulk today because they decided, without anyone ordering them, that this mattered. They're delegates waiting to be asked. Every community on this river kept the stories alive for twenty years. Kept faith, whether they knew that's what it was called. All they lack is a form. Give them the form. You're the last living magistrate empowered under Ostravan law to convene one. Empowered by whom? By the same thing that empowers anything — by people agreeing you are."

Rook sat back. The candles guttered and recovered. For a long moment he looked not at me but at the brass plate above the door — *IN RECESS* — and I watched him wrestle with it the way Edmund wrestled with the chest the night the river gave it up. Superstition. Fear. The comfort of staying sunk.

"If I convene this," he said slowly, "and Bray ignores it — and he will, he has marines, he has the legal fiction of the borderlands charter — then we will have proven nothing except our own powerlessness. Worse than nothing. We'll have taught the river that hope is a thing that gets shot."

"Or," said Edmund, in the flat voice he used for river conditions, "you run the trial anyway, and what happens after belongs to the current. I've spent my life learning you don't control the river. You just decide whether you're on it."

Rook blinked at him. "Did you just quote jurisprudence at me, Captain?"

"No, ma'am— sir. That's just how boats work."

That startled a real laugh out of the old man — brief, painful-sounding, genuine. He pressed a hand to his chest as if holding something in place, and when he spoke again his voice had changed. The paper-dryness was still there, but underneath it something had caught fire, banked and old and stubborn as coal in a flooded hold.

"Very well," Anselm Rook said. "Here is what we shall do, and mark me, because I will only have the strength to say it once. I will convene a tribunal of the river — delegates chosen by the communities of the Vellin, the marshes, and the delta waterfronts, as many as can be gathered and sworn within — " he glanced at the porthole, at the grey churn of the rising monsoon flood "— three days. We will sit aboard this vessel, which is, I note with some satisfaction, technically still commissioned property of a republic that no one has ever formally dissolved. Nobody thought to abolish us properly, you see. They erased the maps and the ministries and the graves, but the *Vigilant*'s decommission papers were lost in the confusion, and so, in the eyes of a law that survives as long as someone keeps faith with it—" his eyes found mine, and there was a glint in them that was almost wicked "—this ship remains a courtroom of the Third Circuit, and I remain its judge, and everything done upon this deck is done in law, however inconvenient that may prove for the Governor."

Tamir, by the door, whistled softly. "The scrapyard's gonna want her back."

"The scrapyard," Rook said, "can file a motion."

He reached for the inkwell, and his shaking hand steadied — I saw it happen, saw the tremor simply stop, as though the body understood that this was not the hour for it — and he pulled a fresh sheet toward him and began to write in a hand that was surprisingly strong, anchor-*f*s and all.

"Captain Amberdale. Your boat — the one hidden in my hull. How many river folk does she hold?"

"Depends how friendly they're feeling."

"Find out. I need runners to every settlement in the marsh network tonight, before the flood closes the channels. The girl—" he looked at me, and for a moment the judge fell away entirely and there was only a very old, very tired man looking at a very young one across a table of drowned books "—the girl will stay. If I am to resurrect a republic's law in three days, I will need someone who knows where the bodies are filed."

"I'm not leaving the ledgers," I said.

"I should hope not. They're exhibits now. Exhibits don't wander." He signed the summons — the first lawful document written in the Republic of Ostrava in twenty years, drafted by candlelight on a beached gunship with a monsoon screaming closer by the hour — and blotted it, and looked up at the sound of boots on the deck plates above.

Fast boots. Many of them.

Edmund was already moving for the door, and Tamir had the lantern snuffed between one breath and the next, and Judge Anselm Rook laid his hand flat upon the closed chest as though taking an oath upon it.

"Well," he said into the sudden dark. "It appears the court is back in session."
