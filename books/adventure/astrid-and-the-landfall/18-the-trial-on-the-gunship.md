## 18. The Trial on the Gunship

I have run cargo through weather that would make a churchman swear, and I have watched men drown in water shallow enough to stand up in. But I had never until that morning seen a courtroom built out of a thing meant to kill.

The gunship was called the *Vigilant* before they beached her, and she still wore her name in flaking gold above the stern windows, which struck me as either a joke or a prayer. Her hull sat canted in the mud of the scrap flats like an old drunk leaning on a wall, and her main deck — where the guns used to live, where men once stood behind iron shields and did arithmetic with other people's lives — had been swept clean and lined with benches. The benches were river benches: planks on barrels, borrowed from chapels, fish houses, one funeral parlor whose owner insisted we return them by Friday because he had three clients coming.

Judge Rook presided from a chair lashed to the deck because the ship listed four degrees to port and the old man's balance was not what it had been. He was wrapped in two blankets and a judicial robe that had gone grey as river fog, and he looked, frankly, like a man who ought to be dead already and was simply refusing on principle. But when he lifted his gavel — a length of oak dowel, salvaged, someone said, from the old Ostravan customs house — his voice carried the way a bell carries, clear over everything.

"This court is not lawful," he announced to the assembled delegates. "Let no one here labor under a delusion. The state that chartered it was drowned twenty years ago. What convenes today convenes only because you have chosen to believe in it." He paused, and coughed, and then added, dry as August: "Which is more than can be said for most lawful courts."

There was laughter among the delegates. Not much. Enough.

Twelve communities had sent people. Marsh folk in oilskin coats still stiff with salt. Delta fishermen with rope burns on their palms. Two women from the charcoal wharves who'd closed their kilns for the day, which for them was roughly equivalent to declaring a national holiday. A boy of maybe fifteen who claimed to represent the reed-cutters upstream and whom nobody challenged, because he'd walked four days through flood country to sit on a barrel and listen.

And outside, ringing the hulk in the grey morning light, Captain Ferro's marines.

I stood at the rail near the gangway and counted them. Forty rifles, give or take. They had come at dawn in two patrol boats and taken up positions on the mud flats without a word to anyone, and nobody inside had said a word back. It was understood by all parties that this was a held breath. Somewhere upstream, Governor Cassel Bray was deciding whether forty rifles were enough, or whether he needed fire instead. And somewhere below decks, in a cabin lit by candles because the hulk's wiring had been stripped for copper years ago, Ilse Vant — Ilse Aldmere, last magistrate's granddaughter — was sitting with the ledgers open in front of her, reading them one final time the way you read a letter you're about to send.

Tamir stood beside me at the rail with his arm still bound from the bullet, though he'd traded the sling for a jacket sleeve pinned shut so he could look presentable. Presentable, for Tamir, meant he'd washed behind his ears.

"You think they'll fire?" he asked quietly.

"I think," I said, "that if Bray wanted us burned, we'd be smoke already. He wants something else."

"What?"

"An audience." I nodded at the marines on the flats. "You don't surround a courtroom with soldiers unless you're worried about what the jury decides."

* * *

Rook opened with procedure, because old judges are creatures of procedure the way rivers are creatures of gravity, and even a court that doesn't exist must do things in order. He named himself. He named the twelve delegates and asked each to speak their community aloud, and when the boy from the reed-cutters said his village's name — a place I won't write down here, because half those places are still hunted — Rook repeated it back to him formally, entered it into the record, and the boy sat down looking like he'd been handed a medal.

Then Rook said: "The charge. Read the charge."

Ilse came up the companionway into the daylight, and I confess my chest went tight seeing her. She'd dressed in her archivist's plain clothes, deliberately, I think — the same woman who'd lied her way aboard my boat at Corben's Landing with paper hands and a bad story. She looked different now. Not steadier, exactly. Committed. There's a difference between a person holding a line and a person who has decided where the line runs.

She opened the ledger to its first page and read:

"On the third of Frostmonth, in the final year of the Republic of Ostrava, order issued under seal of the Provisional Security Directorate, authorizing the action designated *the Hollowing*: the removal of the population of the Vellin valley districts, and the disposition of persons therein as security requirements dictate."

*As security requirements dictate.* Twenty years I'd lived on this river, and I'd heard the word *Hollowing* maybe twice, always whispered, always followed by a change of subject. Now it was being read aloud on a gunship's deck to twelve communities and forty rifles, and the sky didn't fall. That was the whole miracle of it, right there. You say the forbidden thing in public and the sky does not fall. It just keeps going, blue and indifferent, while people finally breathe.

"Read the signatures," Rook said.

Ilse turned pages. "Deputy Director for Internal Order, M. Havel. Countersigned, Director of Provincial Security, L. Ostrer." Page turn. "Countersigned, Special Commissioner for the Valley Districts—" and here her voice did something I'll remember the rest of my life. It didn't break. It slowed. The way a pilot slows entering rapids, choosing every rock. "—Cassel Bray."

The name went across the deck like a current. Nobody shouted. That was the strange part. I'd expected shouting, maybe weeping, maybe the marines moving at some signal. Instead there was silence — the particular silence of several hundred people deciding, all at once, not to interrupt.

Down on the flats, one of Ferro's marines shifted his weight. Just that. A man uncomfortable on his feet.

* * *

The witnesses came from the marsh settlement, Maren's country, and they testified in the afternoon after the tide turned, because the marsh folk had asked Rook to wait for the tide — "so our words come in on the same water that carried us," their elder said, and Rook, who understood ceremony better than any living man I've met, agreed.

The elder went first. She was small and brown and her hands were ruined from sixty years of nets, and she stood before the bench without any fear at all, which told me she'd already survived the worst thing that could happen to her and was operating on house money.

"I was nine years old," she said. "My mother put me in a grain barrel and rolled me into the reeds. I heard the valley that night. Not gunfire — gunfire you understand, gunfire has a shape. This was quieter. Wagons. A great many wagons, going downriver, loaded, and coming back empty. And singing. Some of the soldiers sang while they worked." She folded her hands. "I have waited fifty years for somebody to ask me what I heard. Everybody always wanted to tell me what happened instead. So I will say only what I heard, and let the ledgers say the rest, because the ledgers have names in them and I was in a barrel."

Rook wrote nothing — a clerk delegate took the record — but he leaned forward on his lashed chair and said, "What else did you hear, madam? Take your time. The tide is with us."

So she gave it to them. Names of neighbors. The smell of the smoke, which she said was wrong for wood, too sweet. The soldier who found the barrel and looked at her through the staves and walked away, and how she had spent her whole life trying to decide whether that man was merciful or merely tired, and how she'd finally concluded it didn't matter, because either way he had known, and knowing and walking away was the crime the ledgers described in ink.

A second witness spoke of survivors who'd drifted into the marshes over the years, the ones who never gave their real names, who flinched at whistles. A third brought a thing wrapped in oilcloth and unwrapped it on the bench: a child's shoe, river-stained, kept fifty years. "Found it snagged in my father's nets," she said. "We kept it because nobody would claim it and throwing it away felt like agreeing to something."

By then there wasn't a dry face on the deck, including mine, and I include myself grudgingly because I've spent thirty years cultivating a captain's face and it failed me completely around mid-afternoon.

I looked down at the marines during the shoe testimony. They hadn't moved. But their officer — a young lieutenant, river-born if his accent was anything — had taken off his cap, and he hadn't put it back on. Small thing. Rivers are made of small things.

* * *

It was near dusk when the defense arrived, and by *defense* I mean a lawyer.

Bray had sent one — a delta barrister named Quill, silk-waistcoated, smelling faintly of the governor's cologne, who came up the gangway with papers under his arm and a smile like a fixed rudder. He addressed Rook with elaborate courtesy and informed the bench that these proceedings were unlawful, that the documents were unauthenticated, that the witnesses were anonymous agitators, and that any verdict rendered would be, in law, void.

Rook listened to all of it with his chin on his hand, and then said: "Counselor, you are entirely correct. Every word. This court is unlawful, the documents are unauthenticated, and any verdict will be void." He let that hang. "Now sit down, sir, and hear the evidence anyway. Because your principal sent you here to hear it — otherwise why pay your fee? — and I promise you, whatever happens on this deck tonight will matter considerably more than what happens in any courtroom he owns."

Quill sat. To his credit — or perhaps his terror — he took notes.

The cross-examination, when it came, was brief and brutal in the way that honest things are brutal. Quill asked the marsh elder whether she could identify Cassel Bray personally as author of any document. She could not. Quill asked whether the signature might be forged. Ilse produced the ledger's chain of custody — magistrates' seals, transfer records, the archive trail she'd spent her life learning to read — and Quill, who was many things but not stupid, recognized a sealed archive when it was laid out in front of him and stopped asking about forgery. His last question, to Ilse, was whether she hated the governor.

"No," she said. "Hate is a shortcut. I want the long way. I want it done properly."

Even Quill wrote that down.

* * *

They read the signatures aloud at full dark, by lamplight, all of them, page after page, because Rook ruled that the record required it — that the Hollowing had been signed into being by ordinary men adding their names to other men's orders, and it would be un-signed the same way, name by name, in public. It took two hours. The delegates took turns reading so no single voice would wear out. Tamir read a passage; his voice cracked on the second name and he steadied and finished. Even the boy from the reed-cutters read, standing on a barrel to reach the lamp, pronouncing each syllable like a man defusing something.

And through all of it, the marines stood on the flats in the dark with their rifles, waiting for orders that did not come.

I watched Ferro during the reading. He'd come ashore at some point — I don't know when — and stood apart from his men at the edge of the lamplight, close enough to hear. He never looked at the bench. He looked at his boots, mostly, and once, briefly, at the child's shoe when it was passed along the delegates' row and held up to the light again for the record. I thought about the fuel depot, and the bargain he'd offered me, and the page I'd offered him back. I don't know what he'd read since. But I know what a man looks like doing arithmetic he doesn't like, and Ferro was doing it.

Near midnight, Rook called for the verdict.

He instructed the delegates first, and his instruction is the part I want carved somewhere permanent: "You are not a jury of law. You are a jury of memory. Weigh what you heard against what you know, and render judgment in the only currency this court holds — the truth, spoken plainly, before witnesses."

The delegates conferred for less than an hour. Then their spokesman — the charcoal-wharf woman, as it happened — stood and read it out:

"We, delegates of the river communities, having heard testimony and record, find that the action called the Hollowing was a massacre ordered against the people of Ostrava; that the order was authored and countersigned by named officers of the Provisional Directorate; and that Cassel Bray, signing as Special Commissioner, bears responsibility as principal. We pronounce him guilty before the river and its people, and we call upon all persons of authority to deliver him to answer."

No legal force. No cell, no gallows, no writ. Just words, in public, on a dead warship, witnessed by forty rifles and the moon.

And the sky, I am pleased to report, did not fall.

* * *

We were still sitting with it — that stunned, unfinished quiet after a thing long dreaded finally happens — when a marine came up the gangway alone, unarmed, cap in hand. The young lieutenant. He crossed the deck past all of us, ignored Quill entirely, and bent to speak to Judge Rook.

I was close enough to catch most of it.

"Sir," the lieutenant said, low. "Governor Bray's barge just cleared the city moorings. Running lights lit. He's coming down to the water himself."

Rook looked up at him for a long moment. Then at the rest of us. And the old man smiled — a thin, tired, genuinely dangerous smile, the kind you see on pilots entering water everyone else calls impossible.

"Well," said Anselm Rook, settling back into his lashed chair and pulling his blanket tighter. "Then we had better stay in session. It seems the defendant wishes to address the court."
