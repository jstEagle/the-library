## 21. High Water

The monsoon peaked on a Tuesday, though nobody in Marrowgate was keeping track of days by then. The river rose eleven feet in a night and swallowed the lower wharves whole — the fish market, the coal stairs, the tavern called the Drowned Rat, which had finally earned its name after forty years of trying. From the deck of the beached gunship we watched brown water climb the streets like something patient coming to collect a debt.

"Should we be worried?" Ilse asked.

"We're sitting inside a rusted hulk on a mudflat," I said. "Worry implies there's somewhere better to be."

She laughed, which surprised me. She'd been doing more of that since the trial — small laughs, like coins found in an old coat. Tamir, propped against the rail with his arm still slung in canvas, snorted and immediately regretted it. The shoulder was knitting, but it complained about laughing the way old men complain about stairs.

We stayed on the gunship through the worst of it. The river folk brought food by skiff when the water allowed and by rope-line across the flooded lanes when it didn't. A woman named Odile who'd testified about her father's disappearance at the Hollowing brought us eel stew every evening and refused payment with such ferocity that I stopped offering. The delegates came and went in twos and threes, holding their little tribunal sessions below decks while rain hammered the hull like a creditor at the door.

And Judge Rook held court through all of it, coughing between rulings, wrapped in three blankets and a dignity that no weather could touch. When the flood sirens screamed and half of Marrowgate ran for high ground, Rook sat in his candlelit cabin and read depositions aloud in a voice like dry paper. "The law," he told me once, catching me watching him, "is not a building, Captain. Buildings wash away. It's a habit. And habits survive floods better than most things."

I didn't argue. I'd spent twenty years treating the river as a habit myself — same route, same locks, same tolls — so I knew what he meant. You keep doing the thing until the thing is what holds you up.

* * *

The water began to fall the way it rises: all at once and then slowly, which is a contradiction only rivers can manage. By the fourth day the fish market reappeared, dripping and embarrassed. By the sixth, the Drowned Rat's owner was bailing out his tavern and charging double for the privilege of drinking in it.

That was also the day they started rebuilding the Amberdale.

I hadn't asked anyone to. I want that on the record, because later accounts made it sound like some grand gesture of gratitude, and the truth was messier and better than that. A dozen men simply showed up on the mudflat one morning with tools and timber, led by a shipwright named Corvo who'd lost two brothers to Ferro's river police back when the grey boats were just rumors. He looked at the Amberdale's holed flank, cracked rudder, and drowned engine the way a doctor looks at a patient he intends to save whether the patient consents or not.

"She's sound underneath," Corvo said, thumping the hull. "Flooded, fouled, and ugly. But sound."

"That's how I'd describe most of my crew," I said.

He grinned and put a saw into my hands before I could object further.

They worked for nine days. River folk, scrapyard men, two of Ferro's former marines who showed up sheepish and useful, a boy of maybe fourteen who claimed to be a caulker's apprentice and turned out to be a caulker's apprentice in the way that a lie repeated often enough becomes a trade. They patched the hole where Ferro's patrol boat had punched us near the charcoal wharves. They steamed and bent new planking for the cracked rudder. They winched the engine apart, dried it piece by piece in the sun like laundry, and put it back together with a prayer to whatever saint governs machinery, or possibly spite.

Tamir worked one-armed for six of those days until I ordered him to stop and he ignored me, which is precisely the quality that made promoting him to first mate such an obvious decision. I did it formally on the last day, standing on the foredeck while Corvo's crew pretended not to listen.

"First mate," Tamir said, testing the word. "Does that come with more pay?"

"It comes with more responsibility."

"So less pay, effectively."

"It comes with a share," I said, "and you stop arguing with the captain in front of the crew."

"I'll take the share." He shook my hand with his good arm and gripped harder than necessary, which from Tamir was a speech.

Maren should have seen it. That thought arrived without permission, the way those thoughts do, and I let it sit a moment before putting it down again. Grief doesn't drown; it learns to swim alongside you. I've had enough passengers of that kind to know.

* * *

The Amberdale floated on a grey morning, riding light and eager, and I will admit — since this account has otherwise been nothing but admissions — that I stood at the tiller position and felt something I hadn't felt since before the fever town. Not joy exactly. Readiness. The difference matters. Joy is a gift. Readiness is a choice.

Ilse came down the gangplank with a carpetbag and a stack of documents tied in oilcloth, and I knew before she spoke.

"You're staying," I said.

"I'm staying." She looked at the boat with an expression I recognized — the look of someone saying goodbye to a place that saved them without meaning to. "Rook's court needs clerks. Real ones. The tribunal proved the law can live out here, but a tribunal isn't a court. Someone has to build the bones into a body. Records, precedents, warrants — proper warrants, the kind Ferro can arrest people with." She smiled thinly. "It turns out I'm good at paper. Who knew."

"Everyone who met your hands," I said. "They shook around anything else."

She laughed — a real one this time — and then grew serious in the way she did, all at once, like weather. "Edmund. The verdict won't hold everywhere. Bray's allies in the interior are already calling the trial a mob action. There will be appeals, reprisals, maybe worse. Marrowgate is going to need friends on the river. Boats that move evidence, witnesses, judges' letters. Boats whose captains know every back channel from here to the gorges."

"You're offering me employment as a floating courthouse clerk."

"I'm offering you a purpose with a salary."

"I had a purpose. It was miles."

"And how did that work out?"

I looked at the river. High water still, but falling, the current running strong and clean over the drowned wharves. Somewhere upstream was the sandbar where a lead-lined chest had come up out of the dark and ruined my comfortable life. I found I couldn't muster any resentment about it. Comfortable, it turns out, is not the same as alive.

"I'll run your letters," I said. "But I'm not joining anything. No commission, no oath, no medal. The last man who gave me a medal wanted me to forget what it was for."

"There's no medal," Ilse said. "There's a retainer and a lot of wet paper."

"Then we understand each other."

She hugged me, briefly and briskly, the way she did everything — as if affection were a task on a schedule. Then she walked up the gangplank toward the gunship without looking back, which is either bad manners or very good manners depending on the goodbye, and I decided it was the second.

* * *

The commission attempt came three days later, and the medal attempt four days after that, and I declined both with what I consider admirable economy.

The commission came from the delta authorities themselves — a delegation in clean uniforms who boarded at the customs berth and offered me a warrant as an official river marshal, with pay, authority, and a hat. I'm not inventing the hat. There was a hat, in a box, with a braided band, and I believe the hat alone would have been reason enough to refuse.

"The river already polices itself," I told them. "Badly, sometimes. But it does it honestly, and it knows things your marshals don't. If you want the Amberdale carrying warrants, fine. If you want me wearing that hat, you'll need to find a bigger man with smaller standards."

The delegation left the hat anyway. Tamir wears it sometimes when he thinks I'm not looking. I let him. A first mate should have one humiliating possession.

The medal came from Judge Rook personally, which made refusing it considerably harder. He sent for me aboard the gunship — now officially designated the Tribunal Hall of the Free Court of Marrowgate, per a sign Odile had painted and hung with great satisfaction — and received me in his candlelit cabin with the medal lying on the table between us like a small accusing sun.

It was cast from salvaged bronze, river-made, with Ostrava's seal on one face and a date on the other. The date of the verdict.

"Captain Amberdale," Rook said, "the delegates voted unanimously."

"Then the delegates can keep it in trust. Hang it in this cabin. Let everyone who testifies here see it and remember that ordinary people did that."

"You're a modest man."

"I'm a superstitious man, Your Honor. Medals are for dead men and liars, and I intend to remain neither."

Rook studied me for a long moment. He was thinner than during the trial; the illness was winning its slow argument, and everyone aboard pretended otherwise with the disciplined unanimity of people who love someone. "You know," he said finally, "twenty years ago I would have insisted. I would have told you that honor must be accepted publicly or it protects no one." He pushed the medal aside. "Twenty years in exile taught me that honors are heavy, and the living carry enough. Keep your refusal, Captain. Consider it noted in the record."

"Thank you, Your Honor."

"Don't thank me. Bring me coffee. The clerks make it like bilge water, and I'm dying too slowly to drink it."

I brought him coffee. We talked for an hour about river routes and safe houses and which lock-keepers could be trusted with sealed letters — Maren's country, mostly, the marsh folk who'd hidden us and mourned her and carved her name where names get carved. Before I left, Rook said, quietly, without ceremony: "You carried the law upriver when it weighed more than your boat. Whatever you refuse, refuse knowing that."

I nodded and got out before he could see what that did to my face.

* * *

We took the Amberdale out on a morning of washed light, the river high and fast and full of itself, debris streaming past the docks like a parade of everything the flood had taken and was reluctantly returning. Corvo's repairs held. The engine caught on the third try, which Corvo swore was normal and I swore was luck, and we slid out into the current with a load of freight bound upriver — honest cargo, boring cargo, the kind I used to think was the point.

Tamir stood at the bow with the wind in his face and the marshal's hat jammed down over his eyes, one arm still stiff at his side, grinning like a man who'd never been shot, which is the correct way to stand at a bow.

"Course, Captain?" he called.

"Upstream. Weir Nine, then the narrows." I paused. "Slow, through the narrows. There's a gravel bar past the second rock gate. I want to look at it."

He didn't ask why. First mates who don't ask why — that's worth every extra share.

We passed the charcoal wharves, rebuilt and smelling of smoke again. We passed the spot where Maren died, and I rang the bell once, low, because she'd have hated a speech and loved being acknowledged. We passed the shallows where I'd run Ferro aground in another life, two months and one entire person ago.

At the gravel bar, I slowed to a crawl. The water had fallen enough to show it — a long pale tongue of stone and silt, unremarkable, identical to ten thousand other bars on the Vellin. Somewhere under that flat grey surface, a chest had waited twenty years for a storm strong enough to give it up. Somewhere near it, Tamir and I had nearly drowned hauling it ashore while the river tried to take it back.

"Looks like nothing," Tamir said beside me.

"Most important things do. That's how they stay important."

He considered this, then said, "That's either wisdom or exhaustion."

"With me it's usually both."

I opened the throttle and the Amberdale climbed the current, patched hull humming, new rudder biting clean, the whole boat running like something that had been broken on purpose and put back together better. Behind us Marrowgate shrank into the haze, its new court a candle in a rusted hulk, its judge dying slowly and usefully, its archivist building laws out of paper and stubbornness.

Ahead lay the gorges, the locks, the narrows, the whole long road of the Vellin — and somewhere on it, sooner or later, another chest, or another passenger with shaking hands, or another grey boat at dusk. The river gives up its dead when it's ready, and it's always ready for something.

I used to measure my life in miles behind me. Standing at the tiller with my first mate wearing a ridiculous hat, I caught myself doing arithmetic of a different kind entirely — years ahead, seasons ahead, springs when the high water would carry pilgrims up to a memorial in the marshes, and my name wouldn't be on it, and that was right, because the names on it belonged to people who'd stayed.

The bar fell astern. I didn't look back.
