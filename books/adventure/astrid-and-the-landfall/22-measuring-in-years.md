## 22. Measuring in Years

Spring high water on the Vellin is a liar's season. The river runs fat and golden-brown and looks for all the world like a gentleman — smooth as poured tea from Corben's Landing all the way down to the narrows — and every year it drowns somebody who believed that face. I've pulled enough bodies out of April water to know better. But there's a difference between respecting a river and fearing it, and it took me fifty years and one broken boat to learn where the line sits.

The *Amberdale* ran the Vellin again that spring, and I'll tell you plainly: she ran better than she had any right to.

You'd never guess her history looking at her now. The hull Tamir and I scraped off a gravel bar with our fingernails, the rudder I cracked against rock gates while the monsoon tried to fold us in half like a letter — all of it rebuilt by strangers. River folk from the marsh settlement, dockworkers out of Marrowgate, two deserters from Ferro's own river police who showed up with tools and wouldn't give their names. They worked eleven days on the mudflat below the scrapyard where we'd hidden inside that beached gunship, and when they finished they refused payment, and when I insisted, the eldest of them — a woman named Odile with hands like tree roots — took my coin and pressed it back into my palm and said, "Buy paint. Make her pretty. That's payment enough."

So I bought paint. She's grey at the waterline and a deep river-green above it, and if you look close on the port bow you can still see where the old name shows through like a scar under new skin. Tamir wanted to sand it away entirely. I told him no. A boat ought to remember what happened to her. It's how she knows what she's worth.

* * *

We run pilgrims now. That was Ilse's idea, though she'd deny it — she'd say the idea came out of the marsh council, and technically she'd be right, but I watched her sit up half a night with Odile sketching routes on the back of a ledger page, so I know whose hand held the pen.

Here's how it works. Twice a month during high water, the *Amberdale* takes on forty souls at Marrowgate — old men, young mothers, children too young to know why they're crying when they see the memorial — and carries them up through the back channels Maren used to run blindfolded, into the heart of the marsh country, to the place the river folk call the Standing Names.

It isn't much to look at, if you're the sort who needs marble. A gravel rise above the flood line, planted with white cypress, and a long wall of dark river stone with names cut into it. Thousands of them. All the dead of the Hollowing, so far as anyone could reckon them, gathered over three years by Ilse and Rook's people from parish books, family Bibles, and the memories of the very old. And near the top of the newest section, cut deep because the mason said deep cuts weather best:

**MAREN VOSK, PILOT OF THE VELLIN. SHE HELD THE TILLER.**

Four words and a title. Maren would have hated it and loved it in equal measure. She'd have hated the ceremony — she once threw a customs inspector off her boat for humming a funeral hymn within earshot — but she'd have loved being carved beside people she considered worth dying among. Which, knowing Maren, was everybody except customs inspectors.

Tamir runs the gangplank on pilgrimage days. He's grown into his shoulders since the bullet went through the old ones, and he's grown into something else besides: he counts passengers aboard and counts them again ashore, every trip, without being asked. First mates do that. He signed on as mine the week after the verdict, shook my hand like it was a business arrangement, and then ruined the effect by grinning like a boy at a fair. He still lies badly, which I count as a virtue in a crew. But he doesn't flinch anymore when the grey boats pass on the river. None of us do. They're just boats now, with men in them doing a job, and most of those men were born on this water and know exactly what the stone wall says.

* * *

On the third pilgrimage of the season, coming downriver heavy with quiet passengers and lighter than usual in the purse — Odile's council fixed the fare at whatever a family could spare, which meant some trips we made money and some trips we made soup — we passed the sandbar.

You know the one. Every captain has one: the patch of gravel where his life divided into before and after. Mine sits just below the mouth of the Charcoal Reach, a low tongue of shingle that shows its teeth only when the water drops, and on that morning it showed barely an inch of itself, glistening, unremarkable, exactly the kind of place you'd spit over the side without a second thought.

Two years ago I nearly rolled a lead-lined chest off my deck right there, because drowned things are bad luck and I have always been a careful man. Careful men keep their distance from other people's histories. Careful men measure their lives in miles — miles behind, miles ahead, miles of brown water between themselves and anything that might ask something of them.

I stood at the rail and looked at that sandbar for a long time, longer than a working captain can afford on a schedule, and Tamir noticed, because Tamir notices everything now.

"Thinking about rolling something else overboard?" he asked.

"Thinking," I said, "that I don't remember what I was so afraid of."

"You were afraid of caring," he said. "Ilse says—"

"Ilse says a great many things," I said. "That's what judges' clerks are for."

"She's not your clerk, Captain. She's your—" and here he stopped himself, which showed growth, because two years ago he'd have finished the sentence and spent a week repairing the rigging as penance.

But he wasn't wrong, and neither of us needed to say it. Ilse Vant came up from the passenger deck then, grey at the temples now and no paler than she ever was — the woman simply does not tan, it's a medical marvel — and stood beside me at the rail and looked at the sandbar too, because she knew what it was. She always knew what things were. It was her one flaw and her one gift, in roughly equal proportion.

"Odile wants to put a marker on it," she said. "A small one. *Here the river gave up its dead.*"

"The river gave up a chest," I said. "The dead it kept."

"That's what makes it a marker and not a monument." She smiled, that dry half-smile that had gotten us through winch-work and ambushes and one truly catastrophic dinner with the Marrowgate harbor board. "Rook approved the wording. He says markers should be honest or not exist."

"How is the old vulture?"

"Writing. He calls it a history; Odile calls it a grievance with chapters. His cough is worse in the mornings." She said it evenly, but her hand found the rail a little tighter than it needed to, and I covered it with mine, because some things you learn to do without deciding to. "He wants to see the trial records bound properly before the wet season. He says a law survives as long as someone keeps faith with it, and paper is how faith travels."

"He stole that from you," I said.

"He stole it from me and improved it. That's what judges do."

* * *

We tied up at the marsh settlement that evening, as we always do on the return run, and the pilgrims who wished to stay the night were fed fish stew in Odile's long hall while the rest slept aboard. I walked out to the wall after dark, alone, which is also becoming a habit, and I'll tell you what I did there because it matters more than anything else in this account.

I read names.

Not all of them — nobody could, not in a night, not in a year — but a page of them, running my thumb along the cut letters the way you'd read braille, the way Maren used to read water by putting her bare hand flat on the tiller. And then I stood in front of her name for a while, and I did not make a speech, either aloud or in my head, because Maren would have risen from whatever afterlife pilots go to and boxed my ears for sentimentality.

What I did instead was take stock. It's a captain's disease; you can't cure it, you can only aim it at better things.

The stock ran thus: one boat, sound. One first mate, sounder than the boat. One woman waiting on the deck who had chosen, out of all the rivers and all the captains in the world, this one — not because it was safe, God knows, but because it went somewhere. One judge, failing, writing. One governor, formerly of these parts, serving twenty years in a delta prison where the walls sweat and the guards were mostly hired from the marsh country, which means he eats what they eat and answers to their cousins, and I am told he has finally learned to say please. One enforcer, Ferro, who commands the river police now under a charter Rook drafted, and who sends me a bottle of terrible brandy every midwinter with a card that says *For the sandbar*, and whom I have decided to forgive on the installment plan.

And one hole, the size of a wife and a daughter, that does not fill. I want to be honest about that, because this whole business started with ledgers and honesty, and it would be a poor ending that got sentimental about everything except the true cost.

There is no version of this where the fever town gives them back. There is no verdict, no tribunal, no wall of names that touches it. For years I ran upriver and down because motion felt like penance and distance felt like safety, and I counted miles the way misers count coins — not because the coins buy anything, but because counting keeps you from thinking about what you've lost. Miles behind. Never miles ahead. Ahead was where the losing lived.

But standing at that wall, with the marsh frogs going like a jury being sworn in, I understood something that would have embarrassed me to say out loud even then. The miles were never the point. The miles were the anesthesia. What I'd been running from wasn't grief. It was the possibility that something could matter again, and cost again, and that I could survive the paying.

I had survived the paying. We all had. That was the whole discovery, really — the one the ledgers carried, the one the gunship trial proved, the one the marines on the hulk deck learned when they lowered their rifles: that the price gets paid, and the payers live, and living turns out to be the point after all.

* * *

We cast off from the marsh at dawn, with the mist standing on the water in columns like a congregation, and forty-odd pilgrims sleeping off fish stew, and Tamir at the wheel because I've taken to letting him have the dawn watch — his hands are steadier in the cold than mine, and a captain who won't admit that is a captain the river will eventually correct.

The Vellin ran high and slow and gold in the early light. Somewhere ahead, past the Charcoal Reach, past the narrows, past all the water I once measured in miles, Marrowgate was waking up — sirens tested, markets opening, a courthouse rising on the old customhouse foundations with a scale carved over the door that Rook personally inspected and pronounced *adequately smug*. Ilse would be there by week's end; she splits her time now between the city and the marsh, carrying papers and faith in equal tonnage. She'd waved from the passenger deck as we cleared the moorings, and she hadn't said goodbye, because we stopped saying goodbye somewhere in the gorges and never picked the habit back up. Goodbyes are for endings. We're not doing endings anymore.

I passed the sandbar a last time as the sun came clear of the eastern timber. Barely a hand's width of gravel showing. In a month the flood would take it entirely, and in the autumn it would surface again, indifferent, patient, keeping its own counsel the way rivers do. Odile's marker would stand on the bank above it — small, honest, easy to miss unless you knew to look.

I didn't think about what the river had given up there. I thought about what it was carrying downstream right now: my boat, my crew, my passengers, my share of a spring that would come again next year and the year after, each one bringing high water and pilgrims and fish stew and terrible brandy and a woman who tans not at all.

Miles behind? A great many. I've stopped counting them.

Ahead — well. Ahead there's a dawn watch to hand over, a kettle to put on, a schedule to keep, a wall of names to visit twice a month, and years. Years ahead, stretching out brown and gold and patient as the Vellin itself, and for the first time in a long life on this water, Captain Edmund Amberdale intends to spend every single one of them.

Tamir glanced back from the wheel. "Course, Captain?"

I looked downriver, where the light was coming up on the water like a promise kept.

"Straight on," I said. "Straight on for home."
