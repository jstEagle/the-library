## 1. Weather Coming In

The tide was out so far that morning that the sea seemed to have lost interest in the land altogether.

Orsen Eriksen noticed it from the air, the way they noticed everything—sideways, without seeming to look, the way a person learns to watch the world when they'd rather not be watched back. The flats below stretched gray-green and glistening all the way to a horizon where the water had retreated into a thin silver line, like a hem coming undone. Tide pools caught the early light and held it in small burning coins. Somewhere under all that exposed mud lay things the sea usually kept to itself: old anchors, drowned fence posts, the ribs of boats nobody had missed in fifty years.

Orsen didn't think about what else might be down there. That was the trick of it. You learned not to think about things.

The Cessna 180 bucked once—a soft warning, not yet a threat—and Orsen corrected with two fingers on the yoke, lazy and precise, the way a surgeon moves or a liar does. The plane was twenty-six years old, patched in six places, and smelled of avgas, salt rust, and the dried ginger root Marta Voss kept pressing on them every time they passed through Salt Hollow, convinced that flying made a person sick in ways doctors hadn't named yet. The ginger did nothing. Orsen kept taking it.

Below, the coast unspooled the way it always did this time of year: black rock, green scrub, the scattered white teeth of houses. Nine settlements between here and the northern lights station, none bigger than two hundred souls, most smaller than a church congregation. The map called this stretch the Hollow Coast because the mountains came down to the water like cupped hands, and everything human lived in the creases. Orsen called it home, though never out loud, and never in a way that would hold up if anyone asked them to define the word.

*Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing.* The rule lived somewhere behind their sternum, worn smooth as a river stone from handling. It wasn't written anywhere. Rules like that never are.

The first stop was Gullwrack, forty minutes north, where old Sef Lundgren's heart pills had run out three days early because Sef, characteristically, had waited until the bottle was empty before telling anyone there was a problem. Orsen had been flying this route for six years and could have predicted the request before the radio call came. They could have predicted the weather too, and had—the barometer in the office at Merrow Field had been falling since Tuesday, slow and steady, like something sinking.

"Merrow Base to Seven-Three-Bravo." The voice crackled through the headset, young and bored. "Ceiling's dropping fast over the pass. You sure you want to go up today?"

"I'm already up," Orsen said.

There was a pause, the particular pause of a dispatcher deciding whether to argue with someone he couldn't see. "Copy that. Squall line building off the western shelf. Nothing on the scope yet, but the fishing fleet's coming in early. Take that for what it's worth."

Fishing fleets coming in early. Orsen filed it away with the falling barometer and the way the light had gone strange overnight—not storm-light exactly, but the memory of it, a rehearsal. The monsoon season was still weeks off by the almanac. But almanacs were written by optimists, and the sea had never once consulted one.

"Thanks, Merrow. Seven-Three-Bravo out."

They flew on into the thickening morning.

* * *

Gullwrack materialized out of the haze the way it always did—all at once, as if the village had been holding its breath. A crescent of gray shingle beach, eleven houses, a drying rack hung with fish that turned slowly in the wind like a mobile made by someone with dark intentions. The strip was grass, mowed by sheep, sloping gently toward the water. Orsen brought the plane down crosswind, one wheel, then the other, the tail swinging wide, and rolled to a stop precisely where a flat stone marked the halfway point. Six years of practice. Nobody clapped. That was fine. Applause was a debt, and Orsen had spent a lifetime avoiding debts.

Sef Lundgren was waiting at the edge of the strip, which meant he'd been watching the sky for an hour. He was seventy-eight and built like driftwood, all angles and weathering, with hands that shook only when he wanted them to be still.

"You're late," Sef said.

"The wind's from the east."

"The wind's always from the east when you're late."

Orsen handed over the paper bag of pill bottles without comment. Sef peered inside, counted by weight rather than number, and grunted, which from Sef was gratitude. He lingered, though, shifting his weight, looking past Orsen at the plane, then at the sea, then finally—at last, reluctantly—at Orsen's face.

"Tide's going to be the lowest anyone's seen in sixty years," he said. "Tomorrow night. Maybe the night after. Old men are talking."

"Old men are always talking."

"Not like this." Sef's eyes were pale as shallow water. "My grandfather talked about a tide like this. Said it pulled the skirt off the bay. Said you could walk out to places that weren't places anymore." He paused. "You'll be grounded somewhere when it comes, I expect. Weather's turning filthy behind you."

"I'll be wherever I am," Orsen said, which was the closest thing to a philosophy they had.

Sef looked at them a moment longer, the way people sometimes did—like he was trying to read a label that had been soaked and sun-bleached until the print gave up. Then he tucked the bag inside his coat and shuffled back toward his house, and Orsen was alone again with the wind and the engine ticking as it cooled.

It was better this way. They'd told themselves that for so long it had stopped being a sentence and become a fact, like gravity, like the color of the sea. People who knew nothing about you couldn't use anything against you. People who expected nothing from you couldn't be disappointed, and neither could you. Invisibility wasn't loneliness. It was armor. It was—

It was a story, and Orsen knew it was a story, and told it anyway, the way Sef's grandfather must have told his stories about tides that pulled the skirts off bays. Some lies you keep because the truth has too many rooms, and you've only ever lived in one of them.

They preflighted, took off, and climbed south through air that had begun to feel like soup.

* * *

The second delivery was Skarnvik, a fuel stop really, and by the time Orsen tied down there the squall line had come visible on the horizon—a bruise-colored wall sitting on the western sea, organized and patient, the way bad weather is when it knows it's won. The harbor master, a woman named Ilsa with forearms like dock rope, stood outside her hut watching it with the professional detachment of someone who had survived forty years of storms by respecting every single one.

"That's not a squall," she said, when Orsen came in for coffee they didn't drink. "That's the leading edge. Radio says the system's tracking northeast, faster than they forecast. Could be here in two days. Could be eighteen hours." She shrugged. "Could be tonight. The sea's lying low right now. See how flat it is? That's not calm. That's a held breath."

Orsen looked out at the water. Ilsa was right—it lay strangely smooth, oily almost, reflecting the darkening sky with an obedience that felt rehearsed. The whole coast had the quality of a stage just before the curtain.

"They're calling it Cyclone Vessa," Ilsa added. "Named already. That's never good. When they name them early, it means the models agree, and when the models agree, somebody's going to have a very bad week."

"I'll be done with the run by tomorrow noon."

"Where's your last stop?"

"Salt Hollow."

Ilsa snorted softly. Salt Hollow was the end of the line in every sense—the last settlement before the coast bent west into cliffs no pilot argued with, a village of maybe ninety people who lived by fish and stubbornness and a church whose bell had cracked in some ancestor's memory. "If the front comes early, you'll be stuck there. There's worse places to be stuck. Marta Voss will feed you until you beg for mercy."

"Marta feeds everyone until they beg for mercy."

"It's how she loves." Ilsa said it lightly, but her eyes stayed on the horizon. "You know, Eriksen, six years you've been flying this route, and I don't know a thing about you except your signature's legible. Most people, that would bother me." She turned and looked at Orsen directly, and there was nothing unkind in it, which somehow made it worse. "It doesn't bother me. I figure everybody's got a reason. But whatever yours is—" she nodded at the western wall of cloud "—weather like that doesn't care about reasons. Storms make neighbors of everybody. Might as well practice."

Orsen smiled the smile they used for exactly these moments—warm enough to pass, cool enough to close the door—and finished the coffee they hadn't wanted, and went back out to the plane.

* * *

Between Skarnvik and Salt Hollow the weather began its argument in earnest.

It came in gusts first, testing the aircraft like a hand testing a door handle—push, release, push harder. Then the rain arrived, not falling but traveling sideways, silver ropes of it that appeared and vanished in the broken light. Orsen flew instruments more than eyes, the coast reduced to glimpses: a headland, a flash of white water where the swell was starting to stand up against the rocks, the brief ghost of a fishing boat running for harbor with its wake curled high and anxious.

This was the part of flying Orsen loved, if love was the right word for it—and they had never found a better one, though they'd tried several. Up here there was no past. The air didn't care where you'd come from. Every second demanded only the next second: the gust, the correction, the trim, the glance at the gauges, the horizon appearing and dissolving like a thought you couldn't finish. Flying was the only place Orsen Eriksen had ever felt entirely present, entirely necessary, entirely *now*. Down there, on the ground, among people, the past had weight. Up here it had none. Up here you could be no one in particular, which was the same as being free.

The radio crackled. "All stations, all stations, this is Coastal Weather Service, advisory update." The voice was clipped, professional, and underneath the professionalism ran something that hadn't been there that morning. "Cyclone Vessa has intensified. Track has shifted northeast. Landfall now projected within thirty-six hours along the Hollow Coast. All vessels seek shelter. All coastal communities should secure loose materials and prepare for sustained winds exceeding one hundred kilometers per hour with a significant storm surge. Repeat: significant storm surge. This system is larger and slower than initially modeled. It will be here a while. Next advisory in six hours."

*A while.* The words sat in the cockpit with Orsen like a third passenger.

Thirty-six hours. The run was nearly done—one stop left. They could deliver Marta's insulin and Dr. Nair's medical resupply, wait out the front at Salt Hollow as everyone assumed they would, and fly out after. It was simple. It was routine. Routine was the whole architecture of their life, load-bearing and invisible, and Orsen trusted it the way other people trusted God.

And yet.

They thought of Sef's grandfather and his tide that pulled the skirt off the bay. They thought of the flats that morning, stripped bare, gleaming, keeping their secrets in plain sight. Something about the combination—the record tide, the great storm arriving like a hand reaching into a drawer—stirred a feeling Orsen couldn't name and didn't try to. It was the feeling of a locked room in a house you'd lived in all your life, one you'd walked past ten thousand times without ever once putting your hand on the handle.

Don't, they told themselves. Whatever it is, don't.

*Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing.*

Salt Hollow appeared through the rain like something remembered rather than seen: the crooked harbor wall, the cluster of roofs hunched against the hillside, the church with its blind white tower. The wind was quartering across the strip now, gusting to thirty and rising, and Orsen brought the plane down through turbulence that rattled the cargo netting, touched firm, and taxied to the lean-to shed that passed for a hangar, wrestling the tie-downs on while the rain tried to take them apart.

Marta Voss was waiting under the eaves of the supply hut, seventy years old and immovable as the headland, wearing a yellow oilskin two sizes too big and an expression that had ended arguments before they started.

"You're flying in *this*," she said.

"I'm landing in this. There's a difference."

"Don't get clever with me, boy." Marta had decided decades ago—or perhaps upon first sight—that Orsen was a boy, and no correction had ever dislodged it. Orsen had stopped offering corrections around the second year. Some misidentifications were cheaper to live inside than to fight. "Come in. There's bread. There's soup. There's a bed at my sister's if the weather keeps you."

"The weather will keep me."

"Then it's settled." She took the medical crate from Orsen's hands as if accepting a newborn, checked the insulin vials immediately with the fierce competence of a woman who had buried a husband to diabetes complications and sworn never again to lose anyone to something as stupid as a delayed delivery. "Priya will want these tonight. She's been asking after you."

"Asking after the medicine."

"Asking after *you*, boy. There's a difference." Marta's eyes glinted, sharp as fish hooks. "You'd know about differences, if you ever let anybody close enough to explain them to you."

Orsen said nothing, and the silence was fluent, practiced, a language they spoke better than any other. Marta sighed, herded them inside, fed them soup and bread and ginger root, and talked enough for both of them—about the storm, about the boats coming in, about her sister's goat, about the tide.

Because Marta talked about the tide too.

"My grandmother saw one like it, the year she was a girl," Marta said, in the lull between topics, in the tone people use for things they've been saving. "Nineteen forty-something. The water went out and out and out, farther than anyone had ever known, and the whole bay lay naked, and the old people wouldn't go down to look at it. Wouldn't even stand at the seawall. They said the sea only gives back what it wants to give, and when it gives too much—" she broke the bread, deliberately, a small crisp violence "—it's because it wants something in return."

Outside, the wind rose another notch and set the supply hut's tin roof whispering. Down the coast, invisible in the rain, the leading edge of Cyclone Vessa ground steadily closer, vast and unhurried, carrying its surge and its hundred-kilometer winds and its thirty-six hours toward a shore that had no idea what was about to be uncovered.

Orsen ate the bread, thanked Marta, and said they'd help secure the plane in the morning.

They slept badly that night in Marta's sister's spare room, listening to the wind learn its new strength, and dreamed—not for the first time, but for the first time in years—of a woman's hands fastening a locket, and of water rising over a name they had spent their whole life refusing to say.

In the morning, the bay at Salt Hollow would begin to empty.
