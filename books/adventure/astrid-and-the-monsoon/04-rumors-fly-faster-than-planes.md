## 4. Rumors Fly Faster Than Planes

Marta Solvang had lived in Salt Hollow long enough to know that the village had two hearts: one that beat slow and steady through the tides, and one that raced whenever something new arrived. The first heart was the true one. The second was the dangerous one.

She was mending a net on her porch when she heard it—the particular quality of silence that meant every radio on the coast was talking at once. Old sets crackling in kitchens. Handhelds buzzing in pockets. The fishing skiffs out past the point all suddenly chattering like gulls over a fish kill.

She'd said too much herself, she supposed. She'd gone pale in front of Halvard's boy and his cousin when they'd come asking why old Marta stood staring at the bay like she'd seen a ghost. And Marta Solvang, who had kept her mouth shut through sixty years of storms, funerals, and feuds, had opened it just wide enough.

"The cave," she'd told them. "The one from the stories. The Meridian Star."

That was all it took. Salt Hollow ran on gossip the way the lighthouse ran on diesel—constant, reliable, and impossible to shut off once started.

* * *

By evening, the net was alive with it.

Marta sat in her kitchen with the radio tuned to the village channel, listening to the voices layer over each other like weather fronts colliding.

"—tide's not due that low again for weeks, so there's time—"

"—time for who? Time for *them*, you mean—"

"—my grandfather said his father helped bury bodies off that wreck. That's grave ground, that is. You don't dig grave ground—"

"—grave ground don't pay for a new engine, Soren—"

"—and what happens when the survey boats come? When the government men come asking questions about who found what, and where, and when? We've got catches nobody asks too many questions about, friend. We've got fuel nobody counts twice. You want lights on this coast?"

Marta turned the volume down until the voices became a murmur, like surf heard through a wall. She looked out her window at the bay, which lay flat and pewter-colored under the last of the storm light, hiding everything as bays do.

At the far end of the shingle, near the fuel shed, she could see Orsen Eriksen's plane sitting on its floats, tied down against the wind. A small shape moved around it—Orsen, checking the tie-downs, checking them again, doing what pilots did when their hands needed work and their heads needed quiet.

Marta had known Orsen for eleven years. Long enough to know the pilot landed, delivered, drank one cup of coffee standing up, and left. Long enough to know that if you asked Orsen Eriksen anything real—where from, family, before—you got a smile like a locked door and a change of subject smoother than glass. The village had long ago decided that whatever Orsen was hiding wasn't worth the effort of finding out. People need their bush pilot more than they need their curiosity satisfied.

But now Marta thought about the way Orsen had gone still, standing at the cave mouth. Not surprised. *Recognizing.* Like someone hearing an old song they'd sworn they'd forgotten.

And she thought: oh, you poor fool. Whatever you buried, the sea has just dug up.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble always did in Salt Hollow, with money—or rather, with the idea of money, which was more corrosive than the real thing ever managed to be.

Bram Oduya ran the general store and the fuel dock, and by ten the next morning he had already sold three cases of lamp oil, two coils of rope, and a diving mask to people who had no business diving anywhere. He'd also, Marta learned via the radio grapevine, made a call on the long-range set to a cousin in Port Andersson who "knew people" who "dealt in maritime salvage interests."

"You can't sell what isn't yours," Soren Vik told him on the open channel, and his voice carried the particular tremor of a man who was frightened rather than angry.

"Nobody's selling anything, Soren. I'm making inquiries. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference, right up until there isn't."

Marta listened and knitted and thought about 1947. She'd been a girl then, but she remembered the winter the Meridian Star went missing—remembered the women standing on the seawall in their black coats, watching a sea that gave nothing back. Eleven souls, the papers said. Some said twelve. Her own grandmother had sworn till her dying day that the manifest was wrong, that there had been a woman aboard traveling under a name that wasn't hers, but grandmothers swore many things and the sea kept its counsel, and eventually everyone let it go.

Everyone except the storytellers. And the storytellers had kept the cave alive the way coals keep heat under ash—dim, patient, ready.

Now the ash was blown off, and the whole coast could see the glow.

* * *

It was Priya Nair who came to Marta's door that afternoon, rain beading on her jacket, her face wearing the expression of a doctor delivering news she'd rehearsed.

"Marta. Have you seen Orsen today?"

"Not since yesterday evening, down by the plane. Why?"

Priya hesitated on the step, and Marta, who had raised four children and buried one husband and outlasted two priests, recognized hesitation when she saw it. She stepped back and let the door swing wide.

Inside, with tea poured and the radio turned off—a small ceremony of privacy that both of them understood—Priya said, "Something's wrong with them. I've known Orsen three years. They're steady. Annoyingly steady. Yesterday at the cave, and again this morning when they came in for their blood pressure check—" she paused, choosing words like a surgeon choosing instruments, "—their hands were shaking. They wouldn't say why. But they asked me something strange. They asked me how long a person's records stay sealed. Medical records, death certificates. Whether names can be... removed from things."

Marta set down her cup. Outside, the wind had begun its evening argument with the eaves.

"And what did you tell them?"

"I told them records don't disappear unless somebody makes them disappear." Priya wrapped her hands around her cup. "Marta, I don't know what's going on. Half the village thinks Orsen found treasure. The other half thinks Orsen is going to bring the government down on us. Nobody's asking the obvious question."

"Which is?"

"Which is what the wreck has to do with *Orsen*." Priya's eyes were sharp and kind at once, the combination that made her good at her job. "I watched their face at that cave mouth. That wasn't discovery. That was grief."

Marta looked at the young doctor for a long moment. Then she got up, went to the dresser, and took down a tin box that hadn't been opened since her husband's funeral. Inside, under a layer of receipt stubs and a dead man's watch, was a photograph: a group of women on the Salt Hollow seawall, 1947, faces grim as slate. She turned it over. On the back, in her grandmother's handwriting, a list of names—and beside one name, underlined twice, a single word: *Ingrid?*

"I never knew any Ingrid," Marta said. "My grandmother wrote that question mark the year the Star went down and never explained it. Said the manifest was a lie, or a mercy, or both." She slid the photograph across the table. "You're right that nobody's asking the right question. But I'll tell you something else, Doctor. Around here, the right question doesn't stay unasked for long. By tomorrow, half the coast will have connected the pilot to the wreck somehow, rightly or wrongly. Rumors fly faster than planes. Always have."

Priya stared at the faded face in the photograph—a young woman, dark-haired, looking slightly away from the camera as though she'd heard something behind her.

"That's not Orsen," Priya said slowly. "But it could be."

"It could be anyone. It could be no one. That's the trouble with the dead—they leave their faces to the living." Marta took the photograph back, then, after a moment, pressed it into Priya's hand instead. "Keep it. You've got steadier hands than mine, and I think you're going to need to show it to someone before long."

* * *

That night the village held no meeting, because no meeting had been called—but everyone knew where everyone stood anyway, because in Salt Hollow positions were broadcast the way weather was: constantly, redundantly, and with much exaggeration of severity.

The fortune party gathered at Bram's store after closing, and their voices carried across the water to Marta's porch. Bram himself, laying out figures like a man dealing cards: salvage percentages, finder's fees, the price of gold per ounce repeated with the reverence of scripture. Young Halvard, who owed the bank two seasons' worth of debt. The Lindqvist brothers, who between them owned one working boat and half an argument.

Against them, the old guard: Soren Vik invoking graves and grandfathers. Elder Ines quoting the covenant the founding families had signed about shared waters and shared silences. And old Gunnar, who rarely spoke at all, saying only, once, in a voice like a keel grinding sand: "I fished those waters forty years. My father before me. We always knew something was down there. We left it alone because leaving it alone was the price of sleeping well. Now you want to wake it up and charge admission."

Marta listened to it all from her porch with her knitting idle in her lap, and she understood something that neither faction seemed to grasp: it didn't matter who won the argument. The argument itself was the end of something. For seventy years the cave had been a story, and stories belonged to everyone and cost nothing. Tomorrow it would be a site, and sites belonged to whoever had the permits, the lawyers, the money. The outside world had been given coordinates—not on any chart, but in the air, in the radio waves, in the eager mouths of her neighbors—and the outside world always came when called.

And when it came, it would come asking questions. About the wreck. About the cargo. About the passengers. About who found it, and who was standing at the cave mouth with shaking hands.

* * *

Near midnight, Marta saw a light moving along the shingle toward the plane. She knew the walk before she knew the figure—that deliberate, contained stride, shoulders squared against the wind out of habit even when the wind was gentle.

Orsen reached the aircraft, untied one float line, checked it, retied it. Untied it again. Stood there a long moment with the rope in both hands like a man holding onto something that wasn't a rope at all.

Then Orsen looked up—at the black water, at the place beyond the point where the bay deepened toward the cave—and even at this distance, even in the dark, Marta felt the weight of that look land on the water like a stone.

She rose quietly, took her coat from its hook, and walked down to the shingle. Gravel announced her, because gravel announces everyone, and she saw Orsen's spine stiffen before she spoke.

"Can't sleep either?" she said, keeping her voice easy. "Storm's coming back. Barometer's falling. My knee says so, and my knee hasn't lied to me since '89."

"Marta." Orsen's voice was level, pleasant, closed. The door, locked again. "Shouldn't you be asleep?"

"At my age, sleep and I are only casual acquaintances." She came to stand beside them, both of them facing the water, which was easier than facing each other. For a while neither spoke. Then Marta said, "Word's all over the net. About the cave."

"I figured."

"Bram's talking to people in Port Andersson. Salvage people." She watched Orsen's profile in the darkness. Nothing moved in it. Nothing ever did. "There'll be others. Once word's out, it's out. You know how it goes."

"I know how it goes."

"They'll come with paper, Orsen. Permits, lawyers, men in good boots who've never been cold in their lives. Paper's stronger than rock, eventually. It just takes longer." She paused. "And when they come, they'll ask who found it. And the answer will be your name. Because it will be your name whether you want it or not. Names get attached to things. That's what names are *for*."

The rope creaked in Orsen's grip. When they finally spoke, the voice was still level, but Marta heard the strain beneath it, the way you hear a hull straining in heavy seas—nothing visible, everything audible, if you'd spent enough years listening.

"I didn't ask for any of this, Marta."

"No," she agreed. "Nobody ever does. The sea doesn't ask permission either." She turned to go, then stopped, and gave them the only thing she had that was worth giving: the truth, plain. "Listen to me, child. I've watched you land and deliver and leave for eleven years, and I always figured you had your reasons, and I always figured they were good ones, and I never pried. But borrowed quiet is still borrowed. Sooner or later somebody comes collecting. Looks like the sea sent a bill."

She walked back up the shingle without waiting for an answer, because she knew there wouldn't be one. Behind her, the rope creaked once more, and then the light went out, and the plane sat dark against the darker water, holding its secrets the way the bay held the cave—barely, temporarily, and only until the next tide.

* * *

Marta slept badly and woke before dawn to the sound she'd been dreading: an engine, but not Orsen's. Deeper. Diesel-heavy. The kind of engine that belonged to a vessel built for purpose rather than weather.

She stood at her window with her tea going cold and watched the gray light resolve a shape at the mouth of the bay: a survey ship, white and purposeful, running lights still burning against the early gloom, a name on her bow that Marta couldn't read yet but somehow already dreaded.

On the village channel, Bram Oduya's voice came through, bright as brass:

"She's here! Corvus, out of Port Andersson—salvage registry, official. Says they've got a permit. Says they've got *rights*."

And beneath Bram's excitement, underneath the channel chatter blooming like fire, Marta heard another sound: the low, patient horn of the Corvus announcing itself to the bay, three notes, unhurried, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use every second of it.

Down on the shingle, a figure was running for the plane—running, for the first time in eleven years, as if invisibility were something that could be outrun.
