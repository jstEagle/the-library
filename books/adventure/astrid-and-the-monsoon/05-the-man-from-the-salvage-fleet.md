## 5. The Man from the Salvage Fleet

Anton Reyes had learned long ago that the sea told you everything you needed to know about a place, if you were willing to listen. The Corvus came up the coast through a morning of bruised light, and the sea here was talking in a language he knew well: sullen, secretive, holding its breath. Storm water still hung in the air like a debt unpaid. Gulls wheeled over the fishing grounds with the particular aggression of birds that had been hungry for days. And beneath the hull, the bottom shelved away into deep water far too quickly, as if the coast itself were reluctant to be touched.

He stood at the bow rail with his coffee going cold in his hand and read the coastline the way other men read ledgers. A scatter of rooftops behind a shingle beach. Drying racks leaning at tired angles. A breakwater built by people who had run out of money halfway through and finished it anyway. Smoke from chimneys standing straight up in the dead air—no wind yet, but the barometer on the bridge had been falling all night, and Reyes trusted a falling glass more than he trusted most men.

"Twelve fathoms," said Voss beside him, her binoculars fixed on the headland to the north. "Shoaling fast past the point. There's your cave country."

"I can see it." He could: a seam of darker rock where the cliffs met the tide line, half-hidden even now, though the charts showed nothing there but coast. The survey drones had found it two days ago, before the storm shut everything down—a cavity in the limestone, flooded at all but the lowest tides, its mouth choked with sediment that sonar could not quite resolve. Something was in there. The sonar returns had been strange, layered, man-made-shaped.

Something worth six tides of his life.

He drank the cold coffee anyway. Ritual mattered. His father had taught him that much before the sea took him—never board a vessel, never begin a negotiation, never start anything you intended to finish, without finishing something small first. A cup of coffee. A knot tied properly. A promise kept to yourself.

The Corvus slowed. Her engines dropped to an idle grumble, and the sound carried across the flat water to the village, where he could see figures gathering on the beach. Word traveled faster than any vessel; it always had. Somewhere in that cluster of weathered houses, someone had already worked a radio key, and somewhere beyond the headlands, other someones were listening.

Good. Let them watch him come. Certainty was half his cargo, and he delivered it free of charge.

* * *

They sent Marta to meet him. Of course they did. There was always a Marta—in every coastal settlement from here to the ice, there was a woman of a certain age who had buried a husband or outlived a brother, who ran the radio net and the gossip and half the actual economy, and who looked at outsiders the way a lighthouse looks at rocks. She stood on the shingle with her arms folded and her weight settled low, gray hair escaping a wool cap, eyes the color of the water on a bad day.

Reyes came ashore in the Corvus's tender alone, which was deliberate. One man in a small boat looked like a visitor. Three men in a small boat looked like an occupation.

"Captain Reyes." He stepped onto the shingle and offered his credentials folder open, permit on top, laminated and official and stamped in three colors of ink. "Salvage survey, under maritime statute fourteen. I have a legal window of six tides, beginning—" he checked his watch, a gesture he'd rehearsed until it looked casual, "—from tomorrow's low water."

Marta did not take the folder. She looked at it the way she might look at a dead fish left on her doorstep.

"We heard you coming," she said. "Half the coast heard you coming. That engine's got a voice like a sick walrus."

"She does. I keep meaning to have her throat looked at." He smiled—the smile that had opened harbors in eleven countries—and let it sit there in the cold air between them, doing its quiet work. "I'm told there's a wreck in the sea cave north of your point. The Meridian Star. Lost in 'forty-seven."

"Told by who?"

"Told by sonar," he said pleasantly. "Told by history. The Meridian Star went down off this coast with forty-one souls aboard, and the sea has been keeping her for seventy-odd years. The law says she belongs to whoever recovers her within the statutory window once she's found. The window opens tomorrow." He closed the folder gently, as if tucking in a child. "I'm not here to take anything from Salt Hollow, Mrs.—"

"Marta. Just Marta."

"Marta. I'm here to do a job, cleanly and legally, and then to leave. Six tides. You can count them on your fingers."

"And after six tides?"

"After six tides, if the work isn't done, the window closes and the wreck reverts to protected status. Everyone goes home." He spread his hands, showing them empty. It was a good trick, empty hands. He'd practiced it in mirrors. "I've salvaged eleven wrecks in my career. Not one of them made anyone poorer."

"That you know of," said Marta.

He laughed, genuinely, because it was a good line and because laughing cost nothing and bought goodwill at a fair price. Behind her, more villagers had gathered at the edge of the shingle—a knot of faces, young and old, watching him with expressions ranging from hunger to hostility. He catalogued them automatically. The hungry ones would talk to him by tonight. The hostile ones would take longer, but hostility was just fear wearing work clothes, and fear could be negotiated with.

There was one face he didn't see, and its absence interested him more than all the others combined. The pilot. The bush pilot who flew medicine up this coast, who had been grounded in the village when the storm hit, who had—according to the harbor master at the last fuel stop, a man who sold information along with avgas—walked out onto the tidal flats at extreme low water and gone into the cave alone, and come back carrying something small and closed in his fist.

Reyes wanted very much to know what was in the fist.

* * *

They gave him the room above the fish shed because no one would say no outright and no one would say yes warmly. It smelled of salt and diesel and old rope, and through its single window he could see the tidal flats emerging as the afternoon ebb began, vast and glistening and treacherous, the water retreating like something being pulled backward against its will.

Voss came in at dusk with the tablet and the tide tables, and they sat on the narrow bed side by side like schoolchildren with a shared book.

"Six lows in the window," she said. "But look." Her finger traced the predicted heights. "Only two of them are extreme lows. Deep enough to work the inner chamber. The rest barely drop below the cave sill—you'd get a diver into the outer passage at best, in black water, against the drain current. Unworkable."

"When?"

"First extreme low, day four. Second, day six—the last tide of the window, and it falls at dusk." She looked up at him. "Two chances, Anton. In six days. With a chamber full of uncharted debris and a current that runs like a river at the turn."

Reyes turned the tablet toward himself and studied the curve of the tide graph, rising and falling like a slow heartbeat. Two heartbeats left, in effect. Two doors opening briefly in a wall of water, and behind them whatever the sonar had seen—or hadn't seen.

"Then we don't dive blind," he said. "We survey. Every hour of every moderate low, we put cameras into the outer passage. We map what we can reach. When the big water drops, we go straight to the target. No hesitation, no wasted bottom time." He tapped the screen. "And we file for blasting permits today. Sedimentation over seventy years doesn't shift for divers with hand tools. If the entrance to the inner chamber is choked, we clear it mechanically."

"Blasting permits need regional sign-off. That's a week of paperwork if it's a day."

"It's three days if the right people want it to be three days." He said it lightly, and watched Voss decide not to ask. She was good that way. She understood that a ship ran on some things that were never written in the log.

Because there was a backer, of course. There was always a backer. Herr Falk, whose letters arrived encrypted and whose money arrived instantly and whose patience, Reyes suspected, was a finite resource dressed in expensive tailoring. Falk believed there was gold in the Meridian Star—bullion from a wartime shipment that had never been accounted for, riding down with the ship in sealed cases. The historical record said maybe. Falk's researchers said yes. And Falk paid enough that maybe had become yes in every way that mattered.

Reyes himself didn't care about gold. Gold was heavy and loud and attracted exactly the wrong kind of attention. What Reyes cared about was the finish. Eleven wrecks, eleven clean recoveries, every document filed, every claim honored, every i dotted. He was a man who completed things. The world was full of people who began; he had built his whole life on being one of the vanishingly few who ended.

Six tides. He could hear them already, if he listened—each one a tick of a clock the size of the ocean.

* * *

He walked the village that evening, hands in pockets, hatless in the wind that had finally arrived ahead of the next system, and let himself be seen. This was the other half of the trade: you couldn't buy a coastal town, but you could be patient with one. Patience was currency here, rarer than money. He bought bread at the bakery and overpaid without appearing to. He admired a child's drawing of a boat taped inside a window. He asked an old mender of nets about knots, and listened to twenty minutes of answer, and learned three things about the village's mood and one thing about the cave.

"The cave breathes," the net-mender said, squinting at his work. "My grandfather said so. Sucks the water out of the bay like a lung sometimes, when the tide's right. Fishermen used to give it a wide berth. Now—" he shrugged, thread snapping between his teeth, "—now everyone's down on the flats poking at it like it owes them something."

"Does it?" Reyes asked. "Owe them something?"

The old man looked up then, and his eyes were shrewd and tired. "Everything on this coast owes somebody something, captain. Question is who's collecting."

Reyes walked on. The wind was strengthening, carrying the smell of the coming monsoon—wet stone, kelp, distance. On the flats, exposed now in the failing light, he could see figures again, small against the enormous gleam of the drained bay. Villagers, wandering the tide line, looking at the dark seam in the cliff where the sea kept its secret.

And one figure apart from the others, standing where the wet sand reflected the last light like hammered pewter. Tall, lean, wearing a flight jacket with the collar up. Not moving. Just looking at the cave mouth with the absolute stillness of a person listening to something no one else could hear.

The pilot.

Reyes stopped and watched, and felt the particular quickening he always felt at the start of a job—the moment when the abstract became specific, when a wreck became a rival, when a clock began. The pilot stood there in the dying light like a man guarding a grave, or mourning one, or both.

Interesting, Reyes thought. Everything interesting about this job was standing on that tidal flat, keeping a secret with its whole body.

Tomorrow the window opened. Tomorrow the first of six tides would fall, and the Corvus would lower her lights and her lines into the breathing dark, and the law—his law, his oldest ally—would walk into the cave ahead of him.

He turned back toward the fish shed, and behind him the wind rose off the water, and somewhere out in the darkening bay the tide turned and began, patiently, to fill.

* * *

That night he wrote his daily letter to Falk—two paragraphs, factual, unhurried: arrival confirmed, site assessed, window opens tomorrow, permits in motion. He sealed it and set it by the door for the morning launch, then stood a while at the window with the lamp off, watching the water.

The bay lay black and quiet under a sky the color of iron filings. Far out, the Corvus rode at anchor with her deck lights burning, a constellation pinned low over the water. And beneath all that stillness, beneath sixty feet of cold green dark, the thing he had crossed an ocean to finish waited with the patience of the drowned.

Everyone on this coast thought the clock started tomorrow, with the first legal tide. But clocks, Reyes knew, started the moment someone decided to win. His had started the day the sonar return came back strange and layered and shaped like a ship.

Somewhere in the village, a light burned late—he could see it, a yellow smudge behind salt-fogged glass. The pilot's room, perhaps. Or the radio hut, where Marta would be keying warnings up and down the coast, telling everyone who would listen that the wolves had arrived.

Let her tell them. Wolves at least were honest about what they were.

Reyes lay down on the narrow bed, folded his hands on his chest, and slept the sleep of a man with a plan, while outside the tide crept up the flats in the dark, covering the cave mouth inch by inch, sealing it, keeping it, counting.
