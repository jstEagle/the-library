## 6. Six Tides

The tide tables came with the mail run on Thursday, folded into a bundle of newspapers and a crate of insulin that had to stay cold. Orsen carried them up the stairs of Marta's boarding house two at a time, out of habit, though there was no one waiting and nothing burning. The paper was cheap newsprint, the kind that left gray ghosts on your fingers, and the columns were printed in a font so small it seemed designed to discourage anyone from ever knowing exactly when the sea would come for them.

Orsen spread them across the kitchen table under the lamp, weighted the corners with a salt cellar, a tin cup, a coil of spare radio wire, and the locket.

The locket had not left their sight since the cave. It sat now where it always seemed to end up—near the center of whatever surface they worked at, close enough to touch without reaching, far enough away that touching it would be a decision. The corrosion had eaten one hinge entirely. Through the gap, when the light caught it right, you could see the ghost of a photograph: a woman's jawline, a sweep of dark hair, nothing more. Orsen had not opened it. Opening it felt like something that should be done in front of witnesses, or never.

They pulled the pencil from behind their ear and began.

* * *

The arithmetic was simple, which made it cruel.

The Meridian Star lay in the second chamber of the cave, behind the first bend, and the cave mouth only cleared at extreme low tides—the kind that came twice a month at most, when the moon pulled with its whole shoulder and the bay emptied itself like a lung. Ordinary spring tides left three feet of water over the sill. Neap tides left six. Only the lowest lows, the ones the almanac marked with a second asterisk, dropped enough to let a person walk in dry-shod, or nearly, with a lantern held high.

Reyes had arrived on the Corvus nine days ago, waving his permit like a marriage license. Six tides, maritime law said. Six tidal cycles from the filing of salvage notice, and whatever he could lawfully claim within them was his.

Orsen counted backward through the tables, lips moving, pencil scratching, checking each figure twice because the stakes had stopped being abstract somewhere around the moment Marta had gone white as gull bone on the tideline.

Tide one: passed. Tide two: passed. Those had been Reyes's survey days, his divers in the black water, his sonar pinging the chambers like a blind man tapping a cane. Tides three and four fell in the coming week—but here the pencil slowed. Three feet over the sill. Four and a half. Workable, maybe, for divers with lights and nerve, but not for heavy equipment, not for the crane barge Orsen had watched him anchor off the point, and not for anything approaching the inner chamber, which even at the great lows kept a foot of churning water over its threshold.

Then tide five.

Orsen found it near the bottom of the page, printed small and unadorned, and felt the floor of their stomach tilt. The fifth tide fell eleven days out—a minus 2.3, the deepest low of the season, deeper than the one that had exposed the cave in the first place. A door thrown wide open. And it fell one day *after* Reyes's window closed.

One day. Twenty-four hours. The sea would offer everything, and the law would have already looked away.

The sixth tide was a week after that, another deep low, but by then it wouldn't matter what the water did. Whatever Reyes wanted, he'd have taken—or failed to take—and either way the story would be finished.

Orsen sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, Marta's radio murmured the evening shipping forecast, a voice reciting wind speeds like psalms.

So that was the shape of it. The wreck was safe from Reyes only by the width of a single day, and safe from everyone else only so long as no one else read the same tables Orsen was reading. Any diver with a lantern and a strong back could reach the outer chamber on tides three and four. The letters, the effects, whatever else the second chamber held—all of it sat behind a foot of water that any competent free-diver could hold their breath through.

Invisibility, Orsen thought, had been a luxury purchased with other people's ignorance. And the purchase price had just come due.

* * *

They took the tables down to the harbor office the next morning, because a calculation you keep to yourself is just a fear with numbers attached, and fears multiply in the dark.

Halvard Rask ran the harbor office the way he ran everything—slowly, thoroughly, and with the air of a man who had already considered your problem before you brought it in. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a bollard, with hands so scarred by rope and fish spine they looked braided. He read the tide tables in silence while Orsen stood at the counter dripping rain, and when he finished he laid one thick finger on the fifth tide and tapped it twice.

"One day late," he said. "That's what you're telling me."

"That's what I'm telling you."

"And Reyes knows this?"

"He has the same almanac."

Halvard grunted, which from Halvard was a paragraph. He turned to the shelf behind him, ran a finger along a row of binders, and pulled down one labeled in fading ink: *Coastal Works — Permits — Current Year.* He flipped it open, licked his thumb, turned pages until he found what he was looking for, and then went very still.

"Orsen," he said, in a voice Orsen had never heard him use—not in storms, not in the year the fish plant burned. "You'd best sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Dynamite." He turned the binder around. "Blasting permit application, filed four days ago, jurisdictional routing through the regional works office because the cave sits below the mean high-water line and outside village boundaries. Applicant: Corvus Marine Salvage, Ltd. Status—" his finger moved down the form, and Orsen watched him read the word before Orsen could read it themselves, "—*pending approval*, with an expedited review requested on grounds of navigational hazard remediation."

"Navigational hazard," Orsen repeated. The words tasted like engine oil. "It's been sitting there since 1947."

"It's been sitting there since 1947 *unknown*. Now it's charted. And a charted obstruction in a channel approach is a hazard, and a hazard can be removed, and removal doesn't require the owner's consent because there is no owner." Halvard closed the binder with the gentleness of a man closing a coffin. "He doesn't need the low tides, Orsen. Not really. The tides are for careful work. Dynamite doesn't care about the water over the sill. He can blast at high water if the permit comes through."

"When does it come through?"

"Expedited review." Halvard's eyes were old and tired and honest. "Could be a week. Could be less. There's money moving behind this—I've seen the signature block on the supporting documents. Some outfit from the southern provinces. They don't file paperwork like that unless someone important wants the answer fast."

Orsen stood very still. Rain ticked against the harbor office window. Out in the roadstead, the Corvus rode at anchor, her deck lights burning through the gray morning like a row of patient yellow eyes.

For years—for all the years since they'd first lifted a plane off this coast—Orsen's entire strategy for living had fit in four words: land, deliver, leave, forget. Don't be interesting. Don't be owed. Don't owe. The sky didn't ask who you were; it only asked if you could fly. And Orsen had answered yes, ten thousand times, and let the question of everything else drown quietly somewhere beneath the propwash.

But the dynamite didn't care about invisibility. The dynamite would crack the cave open like an egg, and inside the egg was a mother whose face Orsen had spent thirty years refusing to finish remembering, and a hundred strangers whose descendants sold fish in the market and sang in the church loft and buried their own dead in the hillside cemetery above Salt Hollow. The dynamite cared about none of it. The dynamite was just a tool in the hands of a man who had done nothing illegal yet, and might never need to, because the law itself was the lever and he'd simply leaned on it.

Fear came first. Orsen let it come, let it move through them like weather, cold front and squall line, hands steady on the counter because hands that shook couldn't do the next thing. But underneath the fear, in the place where the fear ended, something else was setting like concrete.

It had been so long since Orsen had fought for anything that they'd almost forgotten the feeling. The last time had been a different life, a different name, a courtroom hallway and a lost cause and a door closing. After that they had learned the truer lesson: that fighting cost more than fleeing, that the fleeing life was lighter, that a person could live decades in the space between other people's wars if they kept their head down and their tanks full.

The feeling now was not nostalgia. It was recognition. Like finding an old tool in the bottom of a kit, still sharp.

"Halvard," Orsen said. "Who countersigns a blasting permit?"

"Regional works officer. Fellow named Oduya, up in Torvik."

"How long does it take to fly to Torvik?"

Halvard looked at them for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and the Corvus's lights blurred into smears of gold.

"In that weather?" he said. "In that plane? Two hours, if the engine loves you. Three if it doesn't."

"Then I'd best go while it still loves me."

* * *

Marta caught them at the airstrip, of course. Marta caught everyone at the airstrip; it was the front door of the village whether anyone admitted it or not. She came down the shingle path with her collar up and a loaf of bread wrapped in cloth under one arm, and she pressed the bread into the cargo hold without asking, the way she'd done a hundred times, and then she stood in the rain looking up at Orsen with those pale seaglass eyes that missed nothing and forgave most of it.

"You've got the look," she said.

"What look?"

"The look my husband had the day he went out after the storm that killed the *Solveig*. Calm as milk. That's how I knew." She wiped rain from her face with the heel of her hand. "Whatever you're flying into, Orsen Eriksen—you come back from it. You hear me? This village has lost enough people to the water. We're not losing our pilot to a piece of paper."

*A piece of paper.* Orsen almost laughed. The permit, the manifest, the tide tables, the letter they hadn't written and the name they hadn't spoken in thirty years—it was all paper, every bit of it, and paper had turned out to be the heaviest cargo on the coast.

"Marta," Orsen said, and heard their own voice come out lower than intended, rougher, like an engine turning over after a long winter. "If Reyes gets that blasting permit, there won't be a wreck to argue about. There'll be gravel and a legend. Everything in that cave—everything those families are owed—goes to powder."

Marta was quiet. Rain ran off the brim of her hood. Down the beach, the tide was coming in over the flats where the cave mouth hid, covering it inch by inch, patient as a secret being kept.

"Then don't let him," she said simply. As if it were that easy. As if, for her, it had always been that easy—standing on ground you claimed, saying *mine, ours, no.*

Orsen climbed into the cockpit. The seat knew their shape. The controls came to their hands like a language never forgotten. Priya's clinic roof showed above the tree line at the edge of the field, a scrap of red tin, and Orsen made themselves look at it, made themselves remember there was a doctor down there who had started asking questions lately in a voice that wasn't clinical at all, and that some promises ought to be kept.

Then the engine caught, and the rain became a silver wall, and the wheels left the shingle, and Orsen pointed the nose north into the weather toward Torvik, toward a man named Oduya and a stamp that hadn't fallen yet, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that the years of being no one were over—not because they'd chosen to end them, but because the world had finally come looking.

Fine, Orsen thought, banking into the cloud, the whole gray coast falling away beneath them. Let it look.

Let it find someone ready to fight.
