## 7. Manifest of the Missing

The village archive lived in the back of Salt Hollow's community hall, behind a door that stuck in damp weather and a smell that was equal parts salt, mildew, and old paper. Marta kept the key on a nail by her stove, and when Orsen came to ask for it, she looked at them for a long moment before taking it down.

"You'll want the shipping ledgers," she said. "Bottom shelf. And Orsen—" She hesitated, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been pity, or its close cousin. "Whatever you find in there, it's been waiting seventy years. It can wait until you've eaten."

"I'm not hungry."

"No," Marta said. "You're not. But you will be, after. Grief is hungry work." She pressed the cold iron key into their palm and closed their fingers over it. "I'll bring coffee. You don't get to be alone with this all day."

Orsen wanted to argue. They had spent thirty years being alone with everything—alone in the cockpit at four in the morning, alone with the engine noise and the fog and the small hard knot of secrets they carried like ballast. Being alone with this should have been easy. Instead they found themselves nodding, once, and walking out into the grey morning with the key biting into their fist.

* * *

The hall smelled of dust and lamp oil and decades of rain coming through the roof in slow, apologetic leaks. Orsen lit the two lamps on the reading table because the single window gave more gloom than light, then knelt at the bottom shelf and began pulling ledgers.

They were heavier than they looked, bound in cracked leather gone soft as skin, their spines labeled in faded copperplate: *Shipping Registers, Coastal District, 1940–1952*. Orsen set them on the table one by one, and the sound each made landing was like a small coffin lid settling.

The Meridian Star would be in 1947. Of course it would. The plaque in the cave had said so, the letters still legible beneath the coral crust: MERIDIAN STAR, 1947. Orsen had run their thumb over those letters three days ago and felt nothing but cold metal, and had told themselves that was all it was—metal, cold, a coincidence of names. Ships sank all the time. Names repeated. The sea was full of Meridians.

But they were here now, weren't they. Kneeling in a village archive with wet knees and dry mouth, opening a ledger to a page they somehow already knew.

1947 began with routine entries: herring quotas, mail contracts, a coastal steamer refitted in March. Orsen turned pages with fingers that trembled only slightly, and hated them for it. April. May. June.

And there it was.

*MV Meridian Star — Passenger & Crew Manifest — Final Sailing, 14 August 1947.*

The entry was written in a clerk's careful hand, ink gone brown with age. Orsen read down the list of names without breathing. Crew first—the master, the mate, the engineer, deckhands with surnames that still filled the phone books of half the coast. Then passengers, eleven of them, listed with ports of embarkation and, where known, purpose of travel. A fish buyer from the mainland. A schoolteacher going home for the winter term. A couple recorded only as *Mr. & Mrs. Halvorsen, returning north*.

And then, fourth from the bottom:

*Eriksen, Ingrid M., 26 — female — passenger — embarked Salt Hollow — destination: Northern Reach.*

Orsen read it five times. The lamps hissed. Rain started against the window, soft as fingers.

Ingrid M. Eriksen. Twenty-six years old. Embarked Salt Hollow.

Their mother's name, sitting in a dead man's ledger like it had always been there, patient as stone. Not a legend. Not a story Marta half-remembered from her grandmother. A line of ink. A fact. Their mother had stood on the dock at Salt Hollow on the fourteenth of August, 1947, and walked up a gangplank onto a ship that never reached the Northern Reach, and somewhere in the dark water between here and there, the sea had taken her—or the ship had failed her, or the fire had, or whatever it was that had happened in that cave—and she had been twenty-six years old, which meant she had been younger than Orsen was now.

Younger than Orsen was now. That was the thought that broke something loose. All their life, *mother* had been an enormous word, a mountain word, a woman grown and gone and impossibly far away. But she had been twenty-six. She had been young enough to be scared. Young enough to make mistakes. Young enough to die with her whole life unspent.

Orsen sat down slowly on the archive's one straight-backed chair and put their head in their hands.

* * *

There was a second ledger. Orsen hadn't noticed it at first—a thinner volume, its spine unlabeled, tucked behind the shipping registers as if someone had shelved it there hoping it would be forgotten. Inside, the handwriting changed: less clerical, more human, entries added in different inks across different years. A record of losses. The coast kept its own count of its dead.

*Meridian Star, lost 15 August 1947, vicinity of the Hollows.* Eleven passengers, nine crew. Twenty souls, and the sea had given back none of them—not bodies, not wreckage, not so much as a life ring. The ship had simply ceased to exist, and the coast had spent a generation telling stories about why.

Orsen ran their finger down the list of the lost until it stopped at the name they knew was coming.

*Eriksen, Ingrid M. — passenger.*

Listed among the dead. Officially. In ink, in a ledger, in a room that smelled of rain and lamp oil, their mother was dead. Had been dead since before Orsen learned to walk. Dead in every record, every register, every mind on this coast for seventy years.

And yet.

Orsen sat very still and let the memory come, because it was going to come anyway and it was better to meet it standing.

A kitchen. Not this coast—somewhere inland, somewhere with real winters. A woman at the stove with her back turned, dark hair pinned up carelessly, a strand escaping down her neck. Singing something low and foreign under her breath. The smell of cardamom, which Orsen would spend forty years unable to smell without their chest tightening. The woman turning, laughing at something—a child's question, maybe, or nothing at all—and her face doing that thing it did, that quick bright crumpling into joy—

Then nothing. The memory ended there, the way it always ended, cut off clean as a film snapped in the projector. After that: the aunt who didn't talk about her. The neighbors who whispered. The word *abandoned*, heard through a wall when Orsen was nine, spoken by a woman whose voice dripped with the pleasure of having something juicy to say. *She left the child. Ran off, they say. Some women aren't made for it.*

Some women aren't made for it.

Orsen had built a life on that sentence. On the certainty of it, ugly as it was, because certainty—even terrible certainty—was load-bearing. Their mother had left. Their mother had chosen the horizon over the child. Everything Orsen had become grew out of that bedrock: the locked heart, the rule of land-and-leave, the invisibility worn like armor. If you expected nothing from anyone, no one could leave you. If you were no one in particular, no one could take your name away twice.

And now a ledger said otherwise. A ledger said the sea took her, and the coast mourned her, and some clerk with brown ink had written *lost* beside her name while a child inland ate supper without knowing their mother existed anywhere but in memory.

Unless the ledger was wrong.

The thought arrived quietly, the way dangerous thoughts do, and Orsen pushed it away, and it came back, and they pushed again, and it stayed. Because the ledger could be wrong. Records could be wrong. People could be wrong. And Orsen had seen, three days ago, in a cave that shouldn't exist, a locket they had last seen hanging around a young woman's neck in a photograph taken years before any ship called Meridian Star went down.

The locket had been in the wreck. The wreck was real. The manifest was real.

So either their mother had died in that cave at twenty-six, leaving behind a locket and a child and a lifetime of grief Orsen hadn't known they were carrying—

—or she had walked out of that cave alive, and let the world write her name among the dead, and never once, in all the years after, come back for the child she'd left behind.

Orsen didn't know which possibility was worse. That was the thing nobody told you about grief reopened: it wasn't just the old pain flooding back. It was discovering the pain had been load-bearing too. Pull it out and you had to ask what else was holding the house up.

* * *

Marta found them like that, an hour later—still in the chair, the ledgers open, the lamps burned low. She set a thermos of coffee on the table without a word, poured two cups, and sat down across from Orsen the way people sit down when they intend to stay.

"Found her?" she asked gently.

"You knew." Orsen's voice came out rougher than intended. "You knew she was on the manifest."

"I knew there was an Eriksen on the Meridian Star. Everyone my age knows the names; we grew up on them." Marta wrapped both hands around her cup. "I didn't know she was *your* Eriksen until you told me about the locket. And I'll tell you something else I know, since you're going to sit there looking at me like that. I know what the coast says about the Meridian Star, and I know what the coast doesn't say, and the difference between those two things is bigger than the bay."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Marta said, "that in 1947, things went on in these waters that decent folk agreed not to look at too closely. And ships that got mixed up in such things didn't always sink the way the papers said." She met Orsen's eyes. "Your mother boarded at this dock, Orsen. Somebody on this coast put her on that boat. Somebody here said goodbye to her, or loaded her trunk, or sold her a ticket. Seventy years is long enough for most memories to rot, but not all of them. You want to know why your mother was on that ship—you don't stop at the manifest. Manifests tell you *that*. They don't tell you *why*."

Orsen looked down at the open ledger, at the brown-ink name, at the word *lost* written beside it in a hand that had been steady and sure and certain of its facts.

"Marta," they said slowly, "what if she wasn't lost?"

Marta went very still. Outside, the rain thickened against the windows, and somewhere down in the emptied bay, the tide was already beginning its slow, patient turn.

"Then somebody on this coast helped her disappear," Marta said at last, quietly. "And somebody on this coast has been keeping that secret for seventy years. And if Captain Reyes and his dynamite get to that chamber before you do—" She stopped herself, and Orsen watched her decide, deliberately, to finish the sentence. "Before *we* do. Then whatever truth is down there gets blown apart for salvage weight, and your mother becomes cargo."

Orsen closed the ledger. Their hands had stopped shaking. Something else had replaced the shaking—something harder, older than fear, and stranger. For thirty years they had lived by one rule: land, deliver, leave, remember nothing. Remember nothing, need nothing, be no one.

But their mother's name was in a book in this room, in this village, on this coast. Whatever Ingrid Eriksen had been—coward or survivor or something with no name yet—she had begun her ending here. And Orsen understood, sitting in the lamplight with the rain coming down, that they were not going to leave. Not this time. Not until they knew.

"Marta," Orsen said, "who keeps the old records? Shipping agents, harbor masters, anyone from before?"

Marta smiled, slow and thin, and rose to refill the cups. "Old Henrik Aune. Harbor master here for forty years, God rest him—but his daughter kept all his papers, and she's still living, up past the church. Sharp as a gull and twice as mean." She set the pot down. "Fair warning, though. If your mother faked her death, Orsen, there's a reason. And reasons like that don't stay buried just because we want them to."

"I know," Orsen said. "Believe me. I'm the proof."

They gathered the ledgers, returned them to the shelf with a care that surprised them, and stepped out into the rain. Down in the bay, the water was creeping back over the flats, swallowing the sandbar, sliding toward the black mouth of the cave—as if the sea itself were counting tides, keeping its own ledger, patient as stone.

Six tides, Reyes had said. Orsen stood at the hall steps and watched the cave disappear beneath the green water, and thought: *Hold on, Mother. Whoever you were. Hold on six more tides.*

Behind them, in the archive, the manifest lay closed on the table, and the name *Eriksen, Ingrid M.* waited in the dark among the other names of the dead—no longer alone, and no longer, perhaps, among the dead at all.
