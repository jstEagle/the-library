## 8. The Doctor Who Stayed

Priya Nair had learned to read the weather in people before she learned to read it in the sky.

It was a skill her grandmother had drilled into her over monsoon seasons in Kerala—*the body tells you first, child, long before the mouth admits anything*—and it was a skill that had served her well through medical school, through residency, through the slow unraveling of her own life that had eventually deposited her, of all places on earth, in Salt Hollow: forty-one houses, one clinic, a radio mast that crackled like frying oil, and a bush pilot who arrived every second Thursday with insulin and penicillin and gossip measured out in careful, rationed spoonfuls.

Three years of Thursdays. Priya could chart Orsen Eriksen's moods the way she charted blood pressure.

She knew the Thursday face: tired but level, eyes doing their quick inventory of the clinic's shelves before Orsen would let themselves look at anything else, as if cargo came before courtesy, as if being useful were the only door they trusted. She knew the storm-delayed face, when the plane sat grounded on the tidal flats and Orsen had to stay the night in the clinic's spare room—wary, polite, sleeping with their boots by the door and their jacket folded over the chair back like armor laid within reach. She knew the rare, fleeting Thursday-better face, when something small went right—a kid's fever broken, a delivery early—and Orsen's mouth almost remembered how to smile.

What she did not know, until this particular gray afternoon, was the face that came after all of those. The face underneath them.

Orsen landed late, in weather that should have kept any sane pilot on the ground at Kestrel Bay, and walked into the clinic without knocking, which they never did. Their flight jacket was soaked through at the shoulders. Their hands, when they set the medicine crate down on Priya's examining table, were not quite steady—not shaking, exactly, but carrying a tremor the way a rope carries a vibration from somewhere far off, some shock transmitted down its whole length.

"Insulin, twelve vials," Orsen said. "Amoxicillin, the wide-spectrum. And the morphine for old Halvard—you'll want to sign for that one."

"I'll want you to sit down," Priya said.

That stopped them. For a moment they stood there with the rain dripping off their collar onto her clean floor, and Priya watched the machinery behind their eyes grind through options—deflect, joke, refuse, leave—and then, astonishingly, none of the above. Orsen looked at the chair by the window as though it were an unfamiliar piece of equipment whose purpose they'd forgotten.

"The tide," Orsen said. "I have to be gone before the tide."

"The tide will wait an hour for me. It won't wait for you if you fall over." She kept her voice light, clinical, the voice she used for stubborn fishermen with burst eardrums who insisted they were fine. "Sit down, Eriksen. That's not a request, it's a differential diagnosis."

They sat. It cost them something visible; Priya saw the effort of it move through their shoulders like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The coffee in Salt Hollow was famously terrible. Marta said it was because the water came up through peat; Priya suspected it was because the clinic's ancient percolator had been manufactured sometime during the last ice age and had opinions about heat. Either way, it produced a liquid the color of creek water and the flavor of burnt driftwood, and she poured two cups of it anyway because ritual mattered more than taste, and carried them to the window where Orsen sat looking out at the bay.

The bay was wrong. That was the thing Priya couldn't stop noticing, even before Orsen spoke. She'd lived here three years; she knew the bay the way she knew her own pulse. And today it lay emptied out beyond anything she'd ever seen—the tide withdrawn so far that the sandbars stood up like the spines of buried animals, and the water had pulled back past the black rocks at the northern end, past everything, leaving exposed a stretch of seabed no living person in Salt Hollow had ever looked upon.

And at the far edge of that naked seabed, half-veiled in sea fog, a dark opening in the cliff line where no opening should be.

"You've seen it," Orsen said. Not a question.

"The whole village has seen it. Marta's been down there twice with a lantern and came back both times looking like she'd met a ghost." Priya handed over the coffee. "Half the village thinks there's treasure in it. The other half thinks there's a curse. I think there's probably rusted metal and cold water, which is its own kind of curse."

Orsen wrapped both hands around the cup and didn't drink. The tremor was worse now that they were still; Priya could see it in the surface of the coffee, fine concentric rings spreading from the center, a seismograph of something happening deep underground.

"When did you last sleep?" she asked.

"Tuesday."

"It's Friday."

"I slept Tuesday."

Priya let the silence do its work. This was another thing her grandmother had taught her: silence is a scalpel too, and sometimes the kindest cut. Outside, the fog moved against the cliff face, and the cave mouth seemed to breathe with it—dark, then darker, then dark again.

"There's a ship in there," Orsen said finally, so quietly that Priya had to lean forward to catch it. "A wreck. The Meridian Star. 1947. There's a plaque on the hull, or what's left of one." A pause, long enough that the rain filled it. "My mother's name is on the passenger manifest."

Priya had treated enough grief to know its many dialects. There was the raw kind, the screaming kind, the kind that came in the middle of the night with a hand clutching hers. And there was this other kind—the kind that arrived decades late, that had been packed away so long it had fossilized, and that now, exposed to air again, was proving more dangerous than it had ever been fresh. She'd seen it in soldiers, in parents who'd lost children years ago and had never once said the name aloud. The body keeps the score, her teachers used to say. So does the heart, her grandmother had answered. But the heart cheats. It hides the ledger.

"Ingrid," Priya said.

Orsen's head came up sharply. "I never told you that name."

"No. You told me your mother left when you were small, and you told me you didn't talk about it, and I respected that." Priya turned her cup slowly in her hands. "But three years ago, my first week here, you fell asleep in that chair waiting out a squall, and you talked in your sleep. You said *Ingrid* four times. Once you said *come back*. And once—" she hesitated, wondering whether mercy or honesty served better, and chose honesty, because this patient had spent a lifetime being handled with mercy and it hadn't healed anything— "once you said *you said you'd come back for me*."

The rings in Orsen's coffee stilled. Everything else about them stilled too, the way water goes glassy before a squall line hits.

"I don't remember saying that."

"People rarely do. That's rather the point of sleep."

Orsen set the cup down on the windowsill with exaggerated care, the way a person does when they're afraid of what their hands might do otherwise. When they spoke again, their voice had gone flat and controlled, a pilot reading instruments in turbulence.

"She abandoned me. That's the whole story. That's the beginning, middle, and end. She put me on a train to my aunt's with a paper tag tied to my coat, and she kissed my forehead, and she said she'd be back before the blackberries ripened, and she wasn't. Blackberries have come and gone thirty times since." They laughed, and it was the worst sound Priya had heard in three years of hearing people receive bad news, because it was so practiced, so worn smooth with use. "And now there's a wreck and a manifest and apparently she died in 1947, except I was born after that, so either the manifest is wrong or—" The sentence hit a wall. Orsen stared at it.

"Or the official record is wrong," Priya finished gently. "Records are written by people. People lie, or guess, or hope."

"Or she lied." The flatness cracked, just at one seam. "Thirty years, Priya. I built thirty years on top of that story. You know what it's like to build on a story? Every year you add another floor. You tell yourself, *she chose the road over me, she chose whatever she wanted over me, and therefore I will choose nothing, I will need nothing, I will be no one in particular and owe no one anything.* And it holds. God help me, it holds. It holds up a whole life." Orsen's hands had closed into fists on their knees. "And then the tide goes out further than it's ever gone, and the foundation turns out to be a wreck, and every floor comes down at once."

Outside, as if summoned, the fog thickened. The cave mouth vanished entirely, and Priya found herself absurdly grateful for it—that at least one secret on this coast could still keep itself.

"Tell me what you found down there," she said. "All of it. Not the version you gave Marta."

* * *

It took a while. It came out in pieces, the way wreckage comes ashore—some of it smooth with handling, some of it sharp and new. The ribs of the ship rising out of the silt like the skeleton of a leviathan. The plaque. The manifest, copied down in Orsen's own handwriting, folded now in the inner pocket of the wet flight jacket, kept against the chest like a wound dressing. And then, haltingly, the locket—how Orsen had known it instantly, had known it the way you know your own front door in the dark, because it hung around a young woman's neck in the single photograph Orsen owned, the photograph that had survived every purge of the past, hidden inside the cover of a flight manual for twenty years.

"I held it," Orsen said. "In the water. In the dark. And I thought—I thought, *this is the closest I've been to her since I was six years old*, and it was a piece of metal in a drowned room, and I wanted to scream and I wanted to laugh and I wanted to dive down and never come up." They looked at Priya then, directly, and their eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified. "Do you understand? I spent my whole life making sure I felt nothing about her. Nothing was safe. Nothing was *manageable*. And in ten minutes in that cave, the ocean reached up and undid all of it."

"Yes," Priya said. "I understand."

And the strange thing—the thing that made her heart turn over like a boat in a swell—was that she did. Not the specifics. But the architecture of it. The way a person builds a sealed compartment around a wound and calls the seal a personality. She had done it herself. She was doing it right now, sitting in this chair, in this village at the edge of the map, wearing her competence like Orsen wore theirs. *Doctor Nair, unflappable, tireless, always fine.* Nobody in Salt Hollow knew why a physician with her credentials had chosen a posting so remote that the nearest hospital was a floatplane away. Nobody knew about the case, the inquiry, the newspaper headline she kept folded in the same drawer as her passport, taken out perhaps twice a year, looked at, and put away again. *Fleeing* was such an ugly word. She preferred *relocated*. She preferred *the coast needed doctors*.

Two people in this room, she thought, each holding a sealed compartment, each pretending the other's was the only one.

"Here's what I know as your doctor," she said, standing, because if she stayed seated much longer she was going to say things she hadn't authorized herself to say. "You haven't slept in three days. Your hands are trembling. You flew through weather that could have killed you to deliver medicine I could have waited two days for. And tomorrow you're going to want to get back in that plane and fly straight at the thing that's hurting you, because that's what you do—you outrun it. You've been outrunning it since you were six years old, and it has finally, finally caught up, and you cannot outrun it anymore, Orsen. Nobody can outrun grief. It has better endurance than we do. All it needs is for us to get tired."

"And what's the alternative?" Orsen's voice rose, and something raw showed through the crack. "Stop? Sit still and feel it? Feel *all of it*?"

"Yes." Priya crouched down in front of the chair, so their eyes were level, so there was nowhere to look but at her. "That's exactly the alternative. You sit still and you feel all of it, and it is the worst thing in the world, and it is also the only door out. I know because I've sat in that chair myself. Different chair. Same fire."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The percolator ticked as it cooled. Rain ran down the window in silver threads. And Orsen Eriksen—who had landed, delivered, and left on schedule for three years, who had made an art form of departure—looked at Priya Nair with an expression she had never seen on them and could not immediately name, and understood, in the same instant she named it for herself, that it was recognition. Two castaways sighting each other across the same water.

"Why are you here?" Orsen asked hoarsely. "Really. In Salt Hollow. Why does a doctor like you end up in a place like this?"

Ah, Priya thought. There it is. The honest question, asked honestly, at last.

"Bad coffee," she said, and heard her own voice wobble, and decided she didn't care. "Terrible coffee, actually. The worst on the coast. Someone had to drink it." She took a breath, and the sealed compartment groaned open one inch, one seam, enough. "Ask me again someday when you're not bleeding out, and I'll answer it properly. That's a promise. But it goes both ways, Eriksen. You don't get to be the only one in this village with a locked room."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Orsen's face—tired, disbelieving, real. "Someday," they repeated, testing the word like a plank that might hold.

"Someday," Priya confirmed. "Now. You're staying the night. The spare bed, the blanket that doesn't smell of aviation fuel, and at least eight hours. The tide can have you in the morning."

She expected the argument. She had the argument already drafted, the clinical counterpoints arranged in formation. But Orsen only nodded, once, and looked back out the window at the fog-swallowed bay, and said, so quietly it was nearly lost under the rain:

"Priya. If she's alive—if she faked it, if she *chose* that wreck, chose everyone thinking she was dead—" The fists tightened again. "Then I don't know who I am. Any of it. My name, my whole—" They stopped. Started again. "Who am I, if the story was never true?"

And Priya, who had asked herself the same question every morning for four years while looking in a clinic mirror above a sink in a village at the edge of the world, reached out and covered one of those trembling fists with her hand, and did not let go.

"You're the person who flew medicine through a monsoon for strangers," she said. "Start there. We'll build the rest together."

Outside, far off across the emptied bay, a sound rolled in from the water—low, mechanical, wrong. Not thunder. Not surf. An engine, deep-throated and deliberate, moving through the fog toward Salt Hollow where no engine had any business being.

Orsen's head lifted. Priya felt the fist beneath her hand go rigid as iron.

"That," Orsen said, standing, all the softness draining out of their face like tide off a sandbar, "will be the salvage fleet."
