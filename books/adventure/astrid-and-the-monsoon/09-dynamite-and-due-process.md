## 9. Dynamite and Due Process

The permit office in Port Calloway smelled of mildew and old ink, the way all government buildings smell when they are built on stilts over water that wants them gone. Anton Reyes stood at the counter with his hat in his hands and let the clerk—a young man with the complexion of someone who had never been cold in his life—read the same line for the fourth time.

"It says here," the clerk said, tapping the page, "that your salvage claim is registered under coastal jurisdiction."

"That's correct."

"But the wreck itself—" The clerk looked up, and Reyes watched him do the arithmetic of ambition, weighing what was easier: to refuse a man with a fleet at anchor, or to pass the problem upward to someone who would only blame him for the delay. "The wreck is inside a sea cave. On land that falls under the Territorial Shelf Act. That's provincial authority. I can't sign off on blasting under coastal jurisdiction."

Reyes smiled. It was a good smile; he had practiced it in mirrors until it stopped feeling like anything at all. "You're absolutely right," he said. "Which is why I'm not asking you to sign off on blasting. I'm asking you to acknowledge receipt of my amended filing." He slid a second folder across the counter, thin as a hymnal. "The Meridian Star is a vessel. Vessels fall under admiralty law regardless of where they come to rest. The cave is incidental. The ship is the subject. Your own statute book says so—Section 14, paragraph three. A wreck does not become geology simply because the sea chose to bury it in stone."

The clerk read Section 14, paragraph three. His lips moved.

"Of course," Reyes went on, gently now, the way a man speaks to a horse he doesn't want spooked, "if you'd rather escalate this to the provincial office, we can wait. Six weeks, perhaps eight, for the review cycle. Though I should mention"—and here he let the smile soften into something almost apologetic—"the monsoon window closes in eleven days. After that, the tides won't expose the cave again until next season, and the site will re-flood, and any evidence of structural instability will be underwater again along with it. Structural instability," he repeated, letting the phrase sit there like bait in clear water. "You've seen the survey photographs. That chamber roof is limestone riddled with fissures. If it collapses during the spring storms with one of your fishing boats anchored above it—and they will anchor above it, because it's sheltered water—that's a tragedy with your signature at the bottom of the file."

He had learned long ago that you rarely have to threaten people. You only have to hand them a version of events in which doing what you want is also doing their duty. Men almost never forgive you for threatening them. They thank you for absolving them.

The clerk stamped the amended filing. Then, after a pause in which his conscience made one small wet sound and drowned, he initialed the blasting authorization pending environmental waiver—the waiver being another form, another counter, another clerk, but a form Reyes had already paid a man in the capital to expedite. The whole apparatus would grind through its motions within forty-eight hours. Due process was a machine, and machines could be oiled.

"Thank you for your thoroughness," Reyes said, and meant it, in the way he meant most courtesies—as tactics first and truths second, though sometimes, if you said a thing often enough, it grew a thin root of sincerity.

Outside, Port Calloway was drowning in light. The monsoon hadn't arrived yet, but you could feel it massing beyond the horizon like a debt coming due; the air had that bruised, pressurized quality, gulls flying low and silent, the harbor water lying flat and green as bottle glass. His crew were aboard the *Corvus*, running the dive checks. He could hear the compressor cycling from two hundred meters away, a sound like slow breathing.

His satellite phone buzzed against his hip. He knew who it was before he looked. There was only one man whose calls arrived with the weight of weather.

* * *

Edmund Voss did not use video. He said once that faces were for people who needed to be believed, and Reyes had never decided whether it was an insult or a compliment. The voice came through crisp and dry, transatlantic, the voice of a man who owned things so far apart they required their own time zones.

"Anton. Tell me it's done."

"The environmental waiver clears tomorrow morning. Blasting authorization by evening. We begin cutting the outer shelf on the third tide."

A silence, filled with the small sounds of Voss's elsewhere—ice in glass, a clock somewhere with a deep voice. "And the villagers?"

"Fishing community. Two hundred souls, maybe fewer. They'll make noise. Noise is free. I'll buy the ones worth buying and wait out the rest."

"You said there was a pilot." Voss's voice changed by a degree, the way water changes just before it freezes. "The one who found the cave. My sources say this person has been asking questions about the passenger manifest."

Reyes stood very still. The harbor lay flat before him, and somewhere out past the breakwater the horizon had begun to organize itself into a single dark line. "Everyone asks about the manifest. It's a famous wreck. Forty-seven dead, gold bullion in the hold—or so the legend goes. Curiosity is cheap out here."

"This isn't curiosity." Ice shifted in the glass. "Ingrid Eriksen was on that manifest. I want to know why a bush pilot with no surname history in the region cares about her specifically. Find out. Quietly."

There it was. The thing beneath the thing. Reyes had carried cargo for Voss for nine years—salvage contracts, mostly legitimate, occasionally gray—and in all that time he had understood that the gold was real to Voss, but it was not the point. Somewhere behind the fortune and the foundations and the philanthropic galas there was a locked room, and Ingrid Eriksen's name was a key Voss had spent decades trying to duplicate.

"And if the pilot is a relative?" Reyes asked. He kept his tone flat, professional, a man confirming inventory.

"Then the situation changes." A pause long enough to hold an entire unspoken instruction. "Relatives complicate provenance. Provenance is everything, Anton. You know that better than anyone. A clean chain of ownership, or nothing at all. Make sure whatever comes out of that cave comes out clean."

The call ended. Reyes lowered the phone and looked at his reflection in the darkened screen—a square face going silver at the temples, eyes the color of the harbor before a storm. *Make sure it comes out clean.* As if truth were a hull you could scrape and repaint.

He thought about the manifest himself, honestly, in the privacy of his own skull where no one could audit him. He had read it twice. Forty-one names, six unnamed crew, and Ingrid Eriksen, age thirty-four, listed among the dead. Officially drowned in 1947 when the *Meridian Star* went down running the storm with more in her hold than the customs manifests admitted. And yet Voss spoke her name like a man speaking of a debt still owed.

Reyes had a rule: never learn more than the contract requires. Knowledge was ballast. It kept you stable right up until it dragged you under.

But rules, he was beginning to suspect, were like permits. They held only as long as no one powerful enough wanted them broken.

* * *

That evening he walked the tidal flats below Salt Hollow while his chief engineer recalibrated the cutting charges aboard the *Corvus*. He told himself it was reconnaissance. It was partly reconnaissance.

The flats at low tide were a country of their own—mudflats ribbed like a plowed field, pools holding the sky upside down, the smell of iodine and rot and salt so thick it felt edible. Far out, where the water gave up entirely, the black seam of the cave mouth showed at the base of the headland, breathing with the residual swell. A dark slot in living rock. Forty-seven people had gone into water near that seam, and the sea had swallowed the evidence whole for seventy years, and now the tides were running low enough to exhume them.

He understood obsession. He had married one once—a woman named Celia who chased a missing brother across three continents until the chasing ate the marriage and then, eventually, Celia herself. Reyes had learned from watching her that some people cannot leave a closed door alone, and that the door always costs more than whatever's behind it. He had promised himself he would never be that man.

And yet here he was, walking a mudflat at dusk for a wreck he didn't even want, for gold that would pass through his hands to a man he didn't respect, carrying out instructions he hadn't fully examined. Duty, he thought. That was the word he used. Contractual obligation, professional reputation, the clean machinery of a career built without scandal. But standing there with the cave breathing in the distance, he felt the old question stir, the one Celia had asked him once with her shoes off and her eyes wild on a hotel balcony in Lisbon: *And when the contract and the conscience disagree, Anton—which one do you follow?*

He had answered, then: the contract. Contracts keep you fed. Conscience keeps you poor.

She had looked at him with something between pity and love and said, *One of those things will outlive you.*

A light moved on the headland above the cave. Small, handheld, quickly shuttered. Someone else was out here in the dark, watching the water go down. The pilot, probably. Orsen Eriksen—no first-name history, no family registered in any parish roll Voss's investigators could find, which was itself a kind of history. People who erased themselves always had a reason, and reasons were leverage, and leverage was how Reyes had stayed solvent for twenty years.

But watching that shuttered light blink out against the vast indifferent dark, Reyes felt something he refused to name. Not guilt—he was too well-built for guilt—but a hairline fracture in the certainty, the way good limestone looks solid until you core it.

He turned back toward the harbor lights. Behind him the tide had begun its long return, sliding up the flats in silence, patient as paperwork, erasing his footprints one by one.

By morning the waiver would be signed. By evening, the dynamite would be legal.

And somewhere out in that black water, forty-seven dead waited to find out whether the living would let them rest—or sell them by the pound.
