## 10. Low Water, High Stakes

The tide table lied by four minutes.

Orsen had learned to read the sea the way other people read faces—by its small betrayals, the way a swell hesitated before committing, the way foam dragged backward against its own direction when something beneath was pulling. This morning the bay at Salt Hollow was pulling hard enough to make their teeth ache. The monsoon's leading edge had arrived overnight not as rain but as pressure, a low gray ceiling that sat on the water like a hand pressing down, and every wave that came into the shallows came in swollen, overfull, carrying more of the open ocean than it should have.

They stood at the cave mouth with the dive bag slung across their shoulder and let the cold water climb their shins, and they told themselves the same lie they'd been telling themselves since dawn: *just a look. Just the second chamber. In and out before the turn.*

The first chamber had given them the ribs of the Meridian Star and the locket—Ingrid's locket, the one from the photograph on the dresser in a house Orsen hadn't entered in twenty years, silver gone black as a drowned man's fingernail, the hinge still working when you thumbed it open like it had been waiting. The first chamber had been generous. The second chamber was where the ship had actually died, Marta said, where the passengers had been carried when the hull broke, and the old woman had said it in the tone people used for graves.

"You shouldn't go today," she'd said, too. She'd come down to the flats with her lantern still lit from the night, absurd against the gray morning, and held it up like evidence. "Current's wrong. I've watched this bay for seventy years and the current is *wrong*, Orsen."

"The window closes," Orsen said. "Reyes gets his blasting permit signed by Thursday. After that there won't be a cave to look at, just a hole in a hillside and lawyers."

Marta had looked at them for a long time. Lantern light moved on her face like water. "You're not going for the village," she said finally. "You're going for her."

Orsen hadn't answered, because there was no answer that wasn't yes.

* * *

The water took them the way cold always takes a body—all at once, a fist closing around the chest—and then released them into a green murk where the storm-light came down in slanted, trembling columns. Orsen checked the gauge, checked the knife at the calf, checked the line they'd run from the anchor point outside the cave mouth, because the one thing a bush pilot knows about any kind of flying is that you always keep a line back to something solid.

Inside, the first chamber was as they'd left it: the great arched ribs of the hull furred with anemones, the plaque with its corroded letters, MERIDIAN STAR 1947, the debris field scattered like the contents of a house after a long argument. But the current was here too, even this deep, even at slack—or what should have been slack—a slow, muscular surging that pushed Orsen sideways off the line twice in the first thirty meters, a hand on the shoulder saying *not yet, not yet*.

The passage to the second chamber opened low in the rock, half-choked with collapsed stone, exactly where Marta's grandfather's story said it would be—the crew's corridor, the one the fishermen's legend called *the throat*. Orsen had measured it yesterday at high water from the outside, swimming the cliff face with a torch, counting body-lengths. Four meters of crawl. Doable. Stupid, but doable.

They went through with the tank scraping rock and the world reduced to a tunnel of gray-green and the sound of their own breathing amplified into something animal. Halfway through, the current found the throat and shoved. Orsen felt the whole ocean lean on them, felt the regulator buck, felt a moment of pure pilot's panic—the engine coughing at altitude, the ground coming up patient and enormous—and then did what they'd trained themselves to do in twenty years of weather: stopped fighting, went loose, let the water spend itself, and pulled through on the exhale.

And then the second chamber opened around them like a cathedral.

* * *

It was bigger than the first. That was the shock of it—that the sea had kept a room this size hidden inside the coast all these years, a secret held so long it had become geology. Orsen's torch beam swept across a sloping floor of silt and stone and caught shapes that resolved slowly, reluctantly, into human order.

Luggage. Rows of it, or what remained—leather rotted to lace, brass corners green with age, a child's trunk with stenciled letters still legible: E. VASS. A pram frame, folded flat, its wheels fused to the rock with corrosion. And along the far wall, where the hull of the ship had come to rest against the cave's inner curve, the passenger compartment itself—a section of the Meridian Star torn away almost whole, doorways still framed, a staircase rising into darkness toward a deck that no longer existed, going up and up into nothing, and Orsen's mind supplied the rest: people climbing those stairs in the dark in 1947, climbing toward a deck that wasn't there anymore.

The beam found a shoe. Then another, paired neatly, as if set beside a bed.

Orsen hung in the water and made themself look. Made themself see. This was the deal they'd struck with themself at the cave mouth—*you wanted the truth, here is the invoice*—and the truth was that forty-one people had climbed out of the cold North Atlantic into this throat of rock, and some of them had made it this far, into this airless dark, and none of them had made it out. The legend said the sea gave some back. The legend was kind. The sea had kept most of them, and the ones it returned were the ones whose names were on the memorial stones in seven villages along this coast, the ones whose grandchildren now argued on the beach about salvage rights.

Orsen's eyes burned behind the mask and they couldn't tell if it was salt or grief and decided it didn't matter.

They swam the compartment slowly, methodically, because method was the only thing holding them together—same discipline as a preflight check, item by item, hands moving, mind quiet. A writing desk bolted to the deck, its drawer swollen shut forever. A mirror, silvered with rot, reflecting the torch back as a smear of light like a soul trying to remember its shape. A life vest, still white, still buckled, never used, hanging on a hook as though someone had meant to come back for it.

And then, wedged beneath the writing desk, in the angle where the deck met the torn bulkhead, a strongbox.

Not ship's issue. Orsen knew ships' fittings the way they knew engines, and this wasn't one—it was civilian, private, the kind of case a woman might buy to carry papers she didn't trust to luggage. It was latched, not locked, the hasp bent by decades of pressure but not sealed. Orsen worked it free of the silt with fingers gone clumsy inside the gloves, and the case came up trailing gray water like smoke, and inside—

Papers. Bundles of them, wax-sealed once, the wax cracked, the paper within turned the color of tea and soft as skin. And on top, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully across seventy-eight years, a leather folio with initials stamped in gold that had not entirely faded:

*I.E.*

Orsen's heart stopped—not metaphorically; they felt it stutter, an engine missing—and the torch beam wavered across the compartment walls and came back.

Ingrid Eriksen.

Their mother's initials, in their mother's handwriting on the label beneath, in ink that had survived: *documents — do not surrender*. And below that, in smaller script, added later, hurriedly, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss: *if I don't come back for these, I was never here.*

Orsen's hands shook so badly they nearly dropped the folio.

* * *

They surfaced into the pocket of trapped air beneath the chamber's ceiling without deciding to—the body choosing survival while the mind was elsewhere, another thing twenty years of flying teaches you. They spat the regulator and hung in the black stillness under the rock, torch clipped to a strut, breathing air that tasted of stone and old salt, and unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that would not obey.

The folio held three things.

The first was a birth record. Not Orsen's—an older one, brittle, official, stamped by a registry office two hundred miles south. A name Orsen had never heard: *Ingrid Solvang*. Born 1911. And beside it, folded into the same page, a second document, newer, crisper despite the years, a certificate of registration under the name *Ingrid Eriksen*, dated 1946—one year before the wreck. One year before the Meridian Star sailed. One year before she died, officially, with forty other souls, in a cave that had kept her secrets better than she ever had.

She'd already been someone else before the ship went down. The name Orsen had grown up inside—the name on the photographs, the name sung half-remembered in a kitchen in another life—was itself a mask, worn barely a year.

The second thing was a letter, unfinished, undelivered, addressed to no one. The ink had feathered but held. Orsen read it three times in the torchlight, lips moving behind the mask, reading aloud to the dead air because silence had become unbearable.

*"...the inquiry will want someone to blame and Halvard cannot hang for what he only carried, he never knew what was in the crates, I made sure of that much. If I am listed among the lost then I am lost, and a lost woman can go anywhere and be no one, which is the only safety left to give. Let them think what is convenient. Let my child think—"*

The sentence stopped. There was no more. Either the pen failed or the courage did.

*Let my child think—*

Think what? Orsen hung in the dark with the unfinished sentence burning a hole through seventy-eight years and landed on the word their whole life had already chosen: *think I abandoned them.* Think I chose the sea over you. Think you weren't worth staying for.

And the third thing—the smallest, the one that broke them open—was a photograph. Creased once, worn soft at the corners from handling, many years of handling before it ever came to rest in this box. A woman on a dock, squinting into sun, laughing at whoever held the camera. Dark hair, a coat too warm for the season, a suitcase at her feet.

Ingrid. Younger than Orsen had ever known her, younger than the photographs at home—but unmistakably, devastatingly her.

And on the back, in pencil, in a different hand, a date.

*June 1953.*

Six years after the Meridian Star went down. Six years after Ingrid Eriksen drowned. Someone had taken this photograph of her six years after her death, and she was laughing, and she was alive, and she had handled this picture often enough to wear its corners round before sealing it in oilcloth and hiding it in a dead ship's desk, in a drowned chamber, in a cave the legends said no living person could find.

She had survived. She had walked out of the wreck's shadow, shed her name like a coat, and built a life somewhere on the far side of the water—and at some point she had come back, or sent something back, or arranged for this box to wait here like a letter posted to the future. To Orsen. To whoever her child became.

*Coward,* said one voice in Orsen's skull, cold and certain, the voice of a child left standing at a window watching a road. *She could have come back. She could have written. She let you grieve her your whole life.*

*Survivor,* said another voice, quieter, older, the voice of someone who had spent twenty years doing exactly the same thing—cutting ties, changing registers, becoming no one in particular, land deliver leave remember nothing. *She did for you what you do for yourself every single day. She just did it harder.*

Both voices were right. That was the unbearable part. Their mother was a coward and a survivor, a liar and a protector, a woman who drowned herself on paper to keep her child safe from whatever Halvard had carried in the crates—and the proof of both truths lay in Orsen's shaking hands at the bottom of the sea, in a pocket of borrowed air, while above them the monsoon leaned on the water and the tide table's four-minute lie began coming due.

* * *

The current turned while Orsen was still reading.

They felt it before they understood it—a vibration in the rock, a change in the pressure against their ears, the trapped-air pocket beginning to shrink as the rising water compressed it. The throat they'd crawled through was flooding. The line back to the anchor point ran through four meters of passage that was about to become a washing machine.

Orsen moved.

No more method, no more inventory—just the raw arithmetic of survival, gauge needle dropping, torch beam swinging wild, kicking hard for the throat as the water in it began to move the wrong way, inward, a river running uphill into the cave. They got to the entrance as the flow grabbed them and for three endless seconds the ocean simply took them, tumbled them against stone, tore the torch from their hand and sent it spinning away like a dying star—and then the surge spent itself, as surges do, and Orsen hauled themself through on the slack between pulses, elbow bleeding freely into the green water, folio jammed inside the dive suit against their chest where it beat against their heart like a second pulse.

Outside, the bay had changed its mind entirely. What had been slack was now a brown-green torrent, the storm pushing the flood tide in early and angry, and Orsen surfaced fifty meters from the cave mouth in waves that hit like doors, gasping, clutching the bag, being carried sideways along the cliff face toward the rocks at a speed no swimmer could argue with.

The line saved them. Of course the line saved them—they'd tied it to something solid, because that was the whole of everything Orsen Eriksen had ever learned about staying alive. Hand over hand, wave over wave, they dragged themself back to the anchor, then along the shallowing flats as the water fell off them, until they staggered up onto the shingle below Marta's cottage, streaming, bleeding, half-drowned, alive.

Marta was already there. Of course she was. She took one look—at the blood, at the bulge of the oilcloth package inside the torn dive suit, at Orsen's face, which Orsen knew must have shown everything, because hiding it was suddenly beyond them—and she didn't ask what they'd found.

"Inside," she said. "Hot water. Now."

But Orsen stood on the shingle with the storm coming in over the bay and the folio pressed against their chest, and heard again the unfinished sentence, the pen pressed hard into the paper: *let my child think—*

"Marta," they said, and their voice came out scraped raw, a stranger's voice, "she didn't die in the wreck. She lived. There's a photograph—1953—she was *laughing*—"

The old woman went very still. Rain began to fall, fat and sparse, the monsoon's scouts. Somewhere down the coast, faint under the wind, Orsen thought they could hear it—engines, deep-water engines, a survey vessel making steady way toward Salt Hollow with a blasting permit in its captain's pocket and six tides left on his clock.

"She lived," Orsen said again, and it wasn't grief anymore, and it wasn't relief either, it was something larger than both, something with edges. "Which means she's been *somewhere*. Which means there's a trail."

Marta looked at them the way you look at a pilot who's just decided to fly into weather.

"Orsen," she said. "Whatever you're thinking—"

But Orsen was already looking south, past the rain, past the headland, toward the regional records office and the name *Solvang* and the six days remaining—and the tide came in over the flats behind them, erasing the cave, erasing the footprints, keeping, as it always had, its own counsel.
