## 11. The Village Decides

Marta had lived through forty-one monsoons in Salt Hollow, and she could read a storm the way other women read letters—by weight, by smell, by the way the light went strange an hour before anything happened. The storm that came in on the evening of the lantern meeting was not the worst she'd seen. But it arrived carrying something worse than wind.

It carried the whole village down to the beach.

She watched them come from her doorway above the fish sheds, wrapped in oilskins and blankets, children half-asleep against their fathers' backs, and she thought: *this is what it looks like when a place makes up its mind.* Forty-one years, and Salt Hollow had never once gathered like this—not for the herring collapse, not for the chapel fire, not even for the drowning of the Brandt brothers in '89. Grief they could do alone. This was different. This was a question with teeth in it, and everyone knew it, and no one would say it aloud until they stood together where the tide could hear.

The storm-lanterns were old Halvard's idea, or old Halvard's memory—he'd dug them out of the chapel loft, twelve glass-and-brass things his grandfather had used for night fishing before the radio came, and now they hung from driftwood posts driven into the wet sand in a rough crescent, each one burning with a stubborn yellow flame that guttered and steadied and guttered again. The rain hadn't started yet. It hung over the water like a held breath, and beneath it the bay lay exposed and gleaming, the tide out so far that the sand smelled of things that should have stayed drowned: kelp rot, old iron, the mineral ghost of whatever the sea kept in its pockets.

And far out, at the edge of the dark, the cave mouth. Black on black. Breathing.

Marta pulled her shawl tighter and went down.

* * *

They arranged themselves without arranging—the way villages do, the way families do. The fishermen and their wives near the waterline, closest to the thing itself. The shopkeepers and the schoolteacher set back where the sand was drier. Dr. Nair stood slightly apart, arms crossed, watching Orsen Eriksen the way Marta had watched her own daughter watch a pot about to boil over.

And Orsen stood at the edge of everything, just past the last lantern, half in shadow, hands jammed in the pockets of that battered flight jacket. Present but not there. Marta had known them six years—had fed them bread, had mended their coat seam with thread too fine for their face—and in six years she had never once seen Orsen stand in the middle of anything. Always the edge. Always the door within reach.

Tonight the door was a plane on the ridge, and everyone knew that too.

Halvard opened it, because Halvard always opened things; he'd been harbormaster back when the harbor had anything left to master. He was ninety-one and walked like a man carrying his own coffin upstairs, and he planted himself before the lanterns and said, in a voice like gravel poured slow:

"You all know why we're here. The Meridian Star." He let the name sit in the salt air. "My grandfather helped build her hull. My mother's cousin went down with her. And for seventy-eight years we told our children she was lost off the deep water, beyond the shelf, where drowned ships go to be forgotten." He turned, slowly, toward the black line of the cave. "She wasn't. She was here. Under our feet. Under our boats. All this time."

Nobody spoke. A lantern flame bent sideways in a gust and righted itself.

"So now the question," Halvard said. "The salvage captain—Reyes—has paper. Legal paper, from people who've never smelled this water. Six tides, he says, and then the wreck is his by law. Unless." He held up one gnarled finger. "Unless the wreck is proven to be something else first. A grave. A communal grave, declared and registered, and then no permit in the world can touch her without going through us."

"And who pays for a declaration?" That was Soren Voss, the fuel depot man, and Marta felt the meeting tilt the moment he spoke, the way a boat tilts when cargo shifts. "Lawyers? Surveyors? Fees? With what money, Halvard? We're a village of two hundred souls and a chapel with a leaking roof."

"Then there's the other road," said Petra Lund, and her voice was quiet and reasonable and made Marta's stomach drop, because Petra was never quiet unless she'd already counted the cost. "Reyes offered compensation. For information. Cooperation. Access across our land to the flats." She spread her hands. "I have three children. One of them needs surgery the regional hospital keeps postponing. If there's money in that cave—"

"If there's *bones* in that cave," said old Inga Brandt, and her voice cracked across the beach like a whip, "then every krone Reyes pays you is paid over my family's dead."

"They wouldn't be disturbed—"

"They were *people*, Petra. They had names. My great-uncle had a laugh you could hear across three fields. And you want to sell the door to his coffin because your boy needs—"

"Don't you dare—" Petra's voice rose, and then it broke, which was worse. "Don't you dare make me choose between a stranger's bones and my son's spine. Don't you *dare*."

The silence after that was the worst kind—the kind full of understanding. Marta looked around the crescent of lanterns and saw it plainly: not greed against grief, nothing so clean. Just need against need, the oldest war there was, fought tonight on wet sand under borrowed light.

Halvard raised a hand. "There's a third matter," he said, and Marta saw him turn, deliberately, toward the edge of the light. "Before we decide anything, we should hear from the one who found her."

Every head turned.

Orsen didn't move. For a long moment they stood in the shadow past the last lantern, and Marta watched them the way you watch a bird deciding whether the window is sky or glass. Then they stepped forward—one step, no more—into the ring of yellow light, and the flames caught the sharp bones of their face and the exhaustion in their eyes, and Marta thought: *they haven't slept since the locket. Maybe longer.*

"I found the cave," Orsen said. Their voice was flat, careful, a pilot reading instruments. "That's all I can tell you. I was grounded by weather. I walked the flats. The tide was lower than I've ever seen it."

"That's not what we're asking," said Soren Voss.

"No," Orsen said. "I know."

"The question," Halvard said gently, "is whose side you're on, Orsen Eriksen. You found her. You've been inside her. You know more than any soul on this beach about what's down there. So we're asking you straight, in front of God and the tide: when the time comes—when Reyes comes for her—where will you stand?"

The rain chose that moment to arrive. Not hard—a fine cold sift, hissing softly on the lantern glass, beading on oilskins, running silver down Orsen's jaw. Marta waited. Everyone waited. It was the kind of waiting that has weight, that presses on the chest, and she had lived long enough to know that a person's whole self lives in the pause between a question and its answer.

Orsen opened their mouth.

And hesitated.

It was not a long hesitation. Two seconds, maybe three. But Marta saw it happen—saw the eyes flick, once, toward the ridge where the plane sat, saw the calculation move behind them like a shadow under ice, saw the old reflex reach for the door. *Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing.* Six years of watching this person keep themselves folded small as a map, and now, asked for one plain word, the folding showed.

"I—" Orsen began. Stopped. "This isn't—it's not simple. There are things I—"

"There it is," said Soren Voss, not loudly, but clearly, the way you say a thing so the whole crescent hears it. "There it is, plain as day."

"We don't need speeches," Petra said, and there was no kindness in it now, only tiredness. "We need to know if the person who found our dead is with us or watching us."

"I have never watched—" Orsen's voice sharpened, then caught. "I flew medicine to this coast through weather that grounded every other pilot for six years. I have never once—"

"Flying medicine isn't standing with us," Inga Brandt said. "Flying medicine is passing through. There's a difference, child, and you've always known it, and so have we."

Marta closed her eyes. *Oh, Inga.* True, every word, and cruel as a filleting knife for being true.

When Marta opened them again, Orsen had gone very still—the stillness of a person who has been hit somewhere no one can see. The rain ticked on the lanterns. Out past the darkening flats, the cave breathed its slow black breath, and Marta thought absurdly: *even the wreck gets to be honest. Even the drowned get a plaque with their name on it.*

"I need time," Orsen said finally, and the words came out stripped and small. "There are things about the Meridian Star that—I'm not ready to say them here. But I'm not against you. Whatever you decide, I'm not against you."

"Not against us," Halvard repeated. He said it without anger, which somehow made it heavier. "Sixty years I've watched pilots come and go, and they all talk like that. *Not against.* Like standing beside a person is a favor you might grant." He shook his head slowly. "Go home, Orsen. Or go wherever it is you go. We'll decide this ourselves. We always have."

Orsen stood there one more breath—Marta saw their hands flex at their sides, saw the exact moment the retreat was chosen—then turned and walked up the beach, out of the lantern light, boots silent on the wet sand, and did not look back. The plane's engine coughed awake on the ridge a few minutes later, and the sound moved away south into the rain, and the crescent of villagers watched it go the way you watch a debt leave town.

* * *

The meeting ran another hour. Marta barely heard it. She stood by the last lantern, watching the dark where the plane had gone, feeling an old grief she couldn't rightly name—grief for the girl Orsen must have been, the one who learned somewhere, young and hard, that being nobody was safer than being someone. Marta had buried a husband and a son; she knew what hiding cost. She'd just never seen it worn so completely, or so long, that the wearer forgot it was a coat and not skin.

In the end they voted nothing. Halvard called it true: a village split down its middle doesn't vote, it bleeds. They agreed to send word to the magistrate requesting the grave-site survey, and they agreed that no one would speak to Reyes until the council met again—but Marta, walking home through the fine cold rain with her shawl soaked through, knew both agreements were rope bridges over a gorge.

Because she had seen Petra Lund's face when Orsen hesitated. And she had seen Soren count heads.

And she had seen, in the third row, half-hidden behind Inga Brandt's broad shoulder, a young deckhand from the Corvus who had no business being on this beach at all—standing in the rain with his hood up, listening, and slipping away before the lanterns were doused.

Marta stopped at her door and looked back once at the bay. The tide was turning; she could feel it in her knees, the old ache that meant the water was coming home. By morning the cave mouth would be swallowed again, sealed, patient.

But the village had cracked open tonight, same as the hull, same as Orsen's careful face. And things that crack open don't wait politely for the people inside to finish deciding who they are.

The rain thickened. Somewhere south, down the coast, an engine she knew better than most was flying into the dark with no destination she could guess—and Marta Almeida, who had survived forty-one monsoons by knowing exactly which silences meant danger, stood on her step and understood that the next tide would bring more than water.

It would bring Reyes. And by then, whatever Orsen had gone to find or flee, the story would no longer belong to any of them.
