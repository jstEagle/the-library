## 12. Flight into the Monsoon

The sky over Salt Hollow had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong—yellow-green at the horizon, iron above—and every pilot on the coast knew what that meant. The monsoon's leading edge was coming down like a lid.

Orsen stood on the tidal flats at dawn with a wrench in one hand and a decision in the other, looking up at the little blue-and-white Stinson that had carried them through eleven years of weather no sane person would argue with. The plane was fueled. The oil was fresh. The spark plugs had been cleaned twice because Orsen's hands needed something to do that wasn't thinking.

The regional records office was in Port Callum. Four hundred kilometers south. A six-hour flight in good conditions, and these were not good conditions, and they were about to get worse.

*You could wait,* said the reasonable voice, the one that sounded like Marta, like Priya, like the whole coast that had spent two weeks telling Orsen to slow down, sleep, eat, grieve properly instead of tearing through it like a man digging for something buried under his own floorboards.

But waiting meant tides. Waiting meant Reyes's dynamite permits moving through channels Orsen couldn't see. Waiting meant another night lying awake in the loft above Marta's kitchen, staring at the ceiling beams, doing arithmetic with a dead woman's life: *if she left the wreck alive, if she faked her death, if she walked away from me—then where did she go?*

The manifest gave a name. The letters in the second chamber gave fragments—a boarding pass stub from a coastal steamer, dated three weeks after the Meridian Star went down; a receipt from a tailor in a town whose name had corroded away to two letters. Fragments weren't enough. Fragments were how you went mad.

Port Callum kept the civil registries. Births, deaths, marriages, name changes. If Ingrid Eriksen had become someone else—if she'd stepped out of her own drowning the way she seemed to have stepped out of everything else—there would be paper. Paper didn't rot. Paper didn't lie, though it could be made to hold its tongue.

Orsen wiped the wrench, stowed it, and climbed into the cockpit.

The radio crackled as they ran the checklist. "Salt Hollow traffic, Stinson seven-four-quebec, departing the flats, heading south." A pause. Then Marta's voice, dry as driftwood: "Seven-four-quebec, be advised there's a wall of weather between here and Port Callum that would make God nervous. Confirm your intentions."

"Intentions confirmed," Orsen said.

A longer pause. "Then fly the airplane, girl, and not the sky."

*I'm not a girl,* Orsen almost said, the old reflex, the old flinch at being named at all. But Marta had earned the word, and besides—the monsoon was coming, and there wasn't time to be anything but what people already believed you were.

They took off into the low yellow light, wheels leaving the wet sand with a sound like a kiss pulled away too soon.

* * *

For the first hour, the coast unspooled beneath them in its familiar grammar: black rock, white foam, the scattered villages strung along it like beads on a wire, each one a strip of gravel or a shelf of water where Orsen had landed medicine and mail and gossip for a decade. Kettle Cove. Grey Narrows. The mission at Ansel Point with its tin roof flashing in the sick light. Places that knew the sound of the Stinson's engine the way they knew the tide tables—something regular, something that came and went without asking anything of anyone.

That was the contract Orsen had lived by. Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing. Be the weather, not the news.

Now the whole coast knew their name, and the contract was torn, and the only thing left to do with the wreckage of invisibility was to fly straight through the storm it had been hiding from.

The cloud bank showed itself forty minutes out: a front stacked from sea level to beyond the Stinson's ceiling, green-black and boiling, dragging curtains of rain behind it like the train of some enormous dress. Lightning walked inside it, silent at this distance, patient. The air ahead of it was strangely still, strangely warm—the held breath before the blow.

Orsen checked the charts again, though they'd memorized them. There was a gap. There was always a gap, if you read the terrain right—a river valley cutting through the coastal range, a corridor of lower ground where the front thinned against the mountains' spine. Bush pilots had been threading such needles since before Orsen was born. It was dangerous. It was not impossible.

It was also, Orsen admitted somewhere over Grey Narrows, exactly the kind of flying that happened when a person stopped caring whether they arrived.

They cared. That was the thing Priya had said, two nights ago, standing in the rain outside the clinic with her arms crossed against the cold and against Orsen: *"You keep acting like you don't care if you come back, and I can't—I can't stand next to someone who's halfway gone already."*

Orsen hadn't answered. Hadn't been able to. Because the truth was that caring had become the most frightening thing of all—caring about Priya's stubborn face, about Marta's bread, about the village that had watched them hesitate on the beach and drawn its own conclusions. Caring meant surface area. Surface area meant things could strike you.

Ingrid Eriksen had cared about something enough to vanish for thirty years. Orsen was beginning to understand that this was not cowardice. It was the opposite. It was the kind of love that amputates itself to save the body.

The gap in the front was where the charts promised, a smoky corridor above the Verrin River valley. Orsen turned south into it and the world closed around them like a fist.

* * *

Flying inside the monsoon's edge was like flying inside a throat.

Rain hit the windshield in ropes, not drops. The visibility dropped to nothing, then less than nothing, then the strange grey luminance where you flew entirely by instrument and memory and the animal knowledge of where the ground wanted to be. Turbulence took the Stinson in its teeth and shook it—not violently, not yet, but with the casual strength of something that hadn't decided whether to swallow.

Orsen flew. Hands light on the yoke, eyes moving in the old rhythm—airspeed, attitude, altitude, heading, again, again—while the storm leaned its shoulder against the fuselage and the radio spat static and fragments of other voices fighting the same weather.

"Seven-four-quebec, Port Callum approach—" a voice said once, shredded by interference. "—severe activity along the front—recommend you divert—"

"Negative, Callum," Orsen said. "Continuing."

"You're the only idiot airborne, quebec. Make it quick."

The valley narrowed. The walls of cloud narrowed with it. And then, twenty minutes from the gap's far end, the temperature fell off a cliff.

Orsen felt it first as a change in the engine's voice—a roughness, a hesitation, the Lycoming clearing its throat. Then the carburetor ice warning, that small amber eye, glowing steady in the gloom.

Carb heat. Full. The engine coughed, stumbled, caught, coughed again. The propeller's rhythm went ragged, and the Stinson began to sink, and outside the windshield the rain turned suddenly, horribly white.

Ice.

Not on the carburetor—or not only there. Structural ice, forming on the wings in the freezing layer that the charts had sworn sat higher, that the warm day below had lied about, because fronts like this one dragged their own atmosphere with them, their own private winter. Ice built fast in this kind of moisture. Ice added weight and stole lift, and the Stinson was carrying both too much of the first and not enough of the second, and the valley walls were closing in like hands.

"Come on," Orsen whispered. "Come on, come on—"

Full carb heat. Mixture rich. Everything forward. The engine surged, died, surged—a heartbeat skipping, finding itself, losing itself again. The altimeter unwound. Eight thousand feet. Seven-six. The white rain became white hail, ticking against the airframe like fingernails, and through a momentary tear in the cloud Orsen saw the river below, a brown ribbon maybe four hundred feet down, and a bridge, and—

Seven-two.

The engine caught. Held. Roared back to full song with a violence that shook the whole aircraft, and Orsen hauled it out of the descent with the stall horn singing its thin warning note, the wings shuddering, the ice on the leading edges shedding in chunks that banged away into the dark like thrown crockery.

For a long time afterward there was only the flying. The mechanical, merciful, mindless flying. Hands and eyes and the engine's recovered pulse. The gap widened. The hail became rain again, then just heavy air, and then Orsen broke out of the front's trailing edge into a sky of torn silver clouds and hard clean light, and Port Callum lay ahead on its harbor, ordinary and oblivious, gulls wheeling over fish sheds.

Orsen's hands were shaking so badly they had to circle the field twice before the flare came out right.

On the ground, sitting in the parked plane with the engine ticking as it cooled, they discovered they were crying—had probably been crying since seven thousand feet, though they had no memory of it. Not from fear, exactly. From the sudden, appalled clarity of knowing how close the arithmetic had come to finishing itself. *If she left the wreck alive.* If. All the ifs balanced now against one stalled engine and four hundred feet of brown river.

Priya's voice in the rain: *I can't stand next to someone who's halfway gone.*

"I'm here," Orsen said aloud, to the empty cockpit, to themselves. It didn't feel entirely true. But they wiped their face on their sleeve, checked the ice scars on the wing leading edges with a bare hand—scalloped dents, ugly, survivable—and walked toward the records office with their legs feeling borrowed.

* * *

The regional registry occupied a granite building two streets up from the harbor, a nineteenth-century customs house with a dome that had gone green with salt air and a reading room that smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular melancholy of paper that no one had asked about in decades.

The clerk was a narrow man named Fenn with spectacles thick as portholes and the unhurried manner of someone who had outlived every deadline ever imposed on him. He listened to Orsen's request—a search of name-change registrations, coastal districts, 1947 through present—with the expression of a man watching weather come in.

"Family history?" he asked.

"Something like that."

He brought the ledgers himself, wheeled them on a cart with complaining casters, and left Orsen alone with a reading lamp and forty years of recorded reinventions.

It was slow work. Name changes in those years had been common enough—war brides, debt-fleers, men escaping wives and wives escaping men, and always the smugglers and wreckers of the coast laundering themselves into respectability. The ledgers recorded old name, new name, district, date, and a stated reason, and the reasons were a novel in themselves. *Dissatisfaction with former identity.* *Marriage dissolved.* *Wished to begin again.*

Orsen worked backward from the Meridian Star. Checked the death registers first—Ingrid Eriksen, listed dead, March 1947, cause: lost at sea, vessel Meridian Star. The ink was faded brown. Someone had entered her death with the same pen, the same pressure, the same bored efficiency as the hundreds of names around it. One line. That was all a person got.

Then the name changes, district by district, year by year. Orsen searched for *Eriksen* and found nothing. Searched for *Ingrid* and found nothing. The hours passed. The light through the tall windows moved and dimmed. Rain began on the dome, a soft drumming, the monsoon's rear guard catching up with its fugitive.

It was nearly closing time when Orsen stopped searching for the name and started searching for the shape of the story instead. A woman, late twenties in 1947, Scandinavian-born, traveling alone with a child she would never reclaim. She wouldn't change her name immediately—too hot, too watched. She'd go inland. She'd wait years. And when she finally did it, the reason given would be careful, bland, rehearsed.

Orsen flipped to the inland districts. 1951. 1952. 1953.

And there it was.

**Eriksen, Ingrid Astrid — to — Lindqvist, Anna Maria. District of Vhalen. Registered 1954. Reason: Widow remarrying; wishes to begin again.**

Orsen stared at it for a long time. *Ingrid Astrid.* They had never known their mother's middle name. It had never come up. It had been waiting in a ledger for seventy years, and it was the name Orsen carried in secret and had told almost no one—had told Priya, once, drunk on bad coffee at midnight, and immediately regretted.

She had hidden herself inside her own child's name. Or the child had been hiding inside hers all along.

"Widow remarrying," Orsen murmured. Married whom? To whom? The marriage registers for Vhalen district were in the next cart. Fenn, locking up the outer office, glanced at the clock, sighed the sigh of a man defeated by his own decency, and brought them.

Anna Maria Lindqvist had married a merchant named Tomas Brandt in 1955, in Vhalen, an inland river town in the hill country eight hundred kilometers south. The Brandts appeared in the commercial registries after that—timber, shipping interests, a modest, respectable firm. And then, in the probate index, eight years ago:

**Brandt, Anna Maria (née Lindqvist) — deceased. Estate administered by surviving spouse, T. Brandt.**

Eight years ago. Orsen had been twenty-three, flying mail runs, alive in the same world as their mother for eight more years without knowing it. Eight years of Sundays. Eight years of a woman buying bread and arguing with merchants and growing old in a house with a husband, while her real daughter delivered penicillin to villages that didn't know her name.

Orsen sat very still while the reading room emptied and Fenn turned down the lamps one by one, politely pretending not to notice the person at the table who had stopped moving.

Vhalen. The name snagged on something. Orsen dug out the crumpled notes from the cave—the fragments transcribed from Ingrid's effects, the tailor's receipt with its corroded place-name, the steamer ticket. And something else, something from the last week: Marta's gossip, the radio net's endless intelligence, the talk about Anton Reyes and the money behind him. The salvage fleet's permits had moved fast—too fast—because a wealthy man wanted the Meridian Star's gold, a man from the southern hill country, a timber-and-shipping fortune, a name that had been said twice on the radio net with a tone of lowered voices.

Brandt.

Tomas Brandt's money was trying to buy its way to the wreck. And Anna Maria Brandt—Ingrid Eriksen—had died eight years ago in his household, in his town, married to him.

Which meant either the coincidence of the century, or Ingrid had spent her second life married to the family that had sunk the first one. The smuggling ring the letters hinted at. The lover she'd stayed behind to shield. The fallout she'd fled. *What if the fallout had a name, and a fortune, and a son or nephew or heir who now wanted the last evidence off the seabed?*

The rain on the dome grew heavier. Somewhere out in the harbour, the Corvus's sister ship was surely riding at anchor, and somewhere south of that, in the hill country, an old man sat on a fortune built on drowned people.

Orsen copied the ledger entries by hand, in full, in the careful script of someone building a case they didn't yet know how to bring. Fenn stamped the copies without being asked. At the door he paused.

"Find what you were after?"

"No," Orsen said. "Found worse."

Outside, the storm had followed them all the way here, and the walk back to the airfield was through horizontal rain and the smell of salt and diesel, and Orsen barely noticed any of it. The engine would need inspection before the return leg—the ice, the coughing, the plugs. There was a mechanic two fields over who owed Orsen a favor from the flu season. It would take until morning.

Morning. One more night in this town, alone with the name.

At the airfield fence Orsen stopped, rain running off their chin, and looked south toward the dark hills where Vhalen was, where a woman named Anna Maria had lived and died and perhaps, in her last years, had thought about a child she'd left on a northern doorstep. Had she known where Orsen was? Had she watched from a distance? Was there a letter in a lawyer's office in Vhalen with Orsen's name on it, sealed, waiting, eight years stale?

The obsession had its hooks in deep now. Orsen could feel it the way you feel a barbed lure—every movement drove it further in. Priya's face surfaced, unbidden, furious and frightened in the rain. *You're choosing a dead woman's secrets over living people.*

"Not yet," Orsen said to the storm, and didn't know which of them they were arguing with.

Tomorrow they'd fly home through whatever was left of the weather, carrying a name like contraband. And Priya would be waiting at the airstrip—Orsen knew it the way you know a tide—and this time there would be no bad coffee, no midnight, no easy dark to hide the conversation in.

This time she would ask. And Orsen, holding a dead woman's alias in one pocket and a living woman's patience in the other, had no idea anymore which one they were betraying.
