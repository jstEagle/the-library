## 13. What Priya Saw

Priya Nair had learned to read people the way other doctors learned to read charts—quickly, quietly, and with a certain professional detachment that she had once believed was her greatest strength. Three years in Salt Hollow had taught her otherwise. Out here, detachment was a luxury the coast did not permit. You knew your patients' grandmothers. You knew which houses smelled of kerosene and which of salt-rotted wood. You knew who drank, who prayed, who beat their children and who fed strays. The sea stripped away the pretense of distance until all you had left was the raw, unfiltered fact of other people's lives pressed against your own.

So it was not surprise she felt when Orsen Eriksen walked into the clinic on a Tuesday evening looking like a man who had been drowning for weeks and had finally stopped struggling. It was grief—not her own, but a sympathetic ache, the kind she felt when a wound she'd been watching finally went septic.

Orsen stood in the doorway with rain streaming off their oilskins, puddling on the floorboards Priya had mopped an hour ago, and said nothing at all. That was the thing about Orsen. They could fill a room with silence the way other people filled it with noise.

"You're bleeding," Priya said.

Orsen looked down at their left hand as though discovering it belonged to someone else. A gash across the heel of the palm, shallow but ugly, crusted with salt and old blood. "Caught it on the wreck," they said. "The metal out there. It's like teeth."

"The wreck." Priya set down the chart she wasn't reading. "Of course. The wreck."

She pointed to the examination table without another word, and Orsen came, obedient in the way of people too exhausted to resist, and sat where she told them to sit while she gathered saline, forceps, sutures. Outside, the monsoon worked at the village like a patient animal worrying a bone. Rain hammered the tin roof in long rolling sheets. The lamp above the table flickered twice and steadied.

"Tell me if I hurt you," Priya said, though she knew Orsen wouldn't. Orsen never did. That was part of the problem.

* * *

She cleaned the wound in silence first, because silence was a language Orsen understood, and because Priya needed the time to arrange what she had to say into some order that would survive contact. She had watched this person for three years. She had catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: competent beyond reason, solitary by design, kind in small unwatched ways—leaving firewood on Marta's porch, flying medicine through weather that grounded every other pilot on the coast, never once asking for thanks or expecting it. She had told herself for three years that what she felt was professional interest. Respect. The natural warmth one pilot's patient develops toward the only lifeline between herself and the regional hospital four hundred kilometers south.

She had told herself that lie right up until the night Orsen landed through the leading edge of the storm to reach the records office, and Priya had stood on the flats watching the plane shrink into that grey violence, and had understood with terrible clarity that if that plane went down, something in her would go down with it.

Now she tied off the last suture, snipped the thread, and did not let go of Orsen's hand.

"When did you last sleep?" she asked.

"I sleep."

"That's not an answer."

Orsen's mouth twitched—the ghost of the deflection Priya knew so well—but it didn't land. Something in them was too tired tonight to perform. "Two days ago," they admitted. "Maybe three. It's hard to keep track. The tides don't follow a clock."

"No. But your body does." Priya released the hand at last and pulled her stool around so she was facing them directly, knee to knee, close enough to see the exhaustion mapped in the fine skin under Orsen's eyes. "I've been your doctor for three years, Orsen. I know your resting heart rate better than you do. And for three years you have been the most controlled person I have ever met. Careful. Contained. You fly like a surgeon works—every movement deliberate, nothing wasted. And then three weeks ago something changed." She held up her hand before Orsen could speak. "Let me finish. Because I've been letting you finish for three years, and tonight it's my turn."

Rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked at the thunder and gave up.

"You flew into the monsoon's leading edge," Priya said. "Alone. In a plane with no de-icing equipment worth the name, to chase a paper trail about a woman who has been dead—or gone—for longer than I've been alive. You came back with ice in the carburetor and a look on your face I see on patients who've survived things they shouldn't have. And since then you've been diving a cave alone, in current, in cold water, with no dive buddy, no safety line, no surface support. Do you know what happens to solo divers in tidal caves? I do. I've read the incident reports. They don't come to my clinic, Orsen. There's nobody left to bring them."

"Priya—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice cracked on the words, and she hated that, hated the tremor, hated how much this cost her—and then decided, with a strange calm, to stop hating it. To stop managing herself. "You want to know what I saw? You asked me once, months ago, why I stayed in Salt Hollow when I could be in a city hospital making real money. I told you it was the work. It was partly true. But here is the rest of it, and you can do whatever you want with it after I say it."

She made herself breathe. Made herself hold Orsen's gaze, those grey eyes that gave away nothing except, tonight, everything—fear and longing and a lifetime of practiced concealment all fighting for the same narrow ground.

"I stayed because of you," Priya said. "Because three years ago a bush pilot landed on the flats in fog to bring me antibiotics and a bag of oranges, and looked at me like I was a person and not a resource, and I thought: oh. Oh no. Not this. Not here. Not with someone who treats their own life like cargo to be delivered and forgotten." She laughed, short and unsteady. "I have run from exactly one thing in my life, Orsen, and I ran here, and I built a whole quiet careful existence, and then you flew into it and I understood that running doesn't work. It just relocates the thing you're running from. So I'm not running anymore. And I'm asking you to stop."

Orsen had gone very still. Stillness, Priya had learned, was Orsen's native language—the dialect of people who grew up learning that being noticed was dangerous. Their hands lay open on their knees, the bandaged one and the whole one, and for a long moment they stared at her as though she were speaking a language they recognized but hadn't heard spoken aloud since childhood.

"You don't know what you're asking," Orsen said finally. Their voice was rough, scraped thin.

"Then tell me. That's the entire point. Tell me."

"It's not—" Orsen looked away, toward the window, where the rain turned the world to smoke. "It's not a story with a clean ending, Priya. My mother didn't die on that ship. Or she did, officially, and then she didn't. She walked away from a wreck that killed forty-one people and she let everyone—including me—believe she was dead. She let *me* believe she abandoned me. I spent thirty years building a life on top of that grave, and now the tide's gone out and the grave's empty and I don't—" The breath caught. Priya watched them fight it, watched the old discipline clamp down, and watched, this once, lose. "I don't know who I am if she chose to leave. Everything I am is built on hiding from that. And now Reyes has dynamite permits and the villagers are ready to sell each other out over salvage rights and Marta won't meet my eyes since the meeting on the beach, and you're standing here telling me to—to what? Stop? Let it go? Let them blast the only truth I will ever get?"

"No," Priya said softly. "I'm not telling you to let it go. I'm telling you that you can't carry it the way you're carrying it. Alone. Half-dead. Diving caves at midnight because sleep feels like surrender." She reached out and took Orsen's whole hand this time, deliberately, unmistakably, and did not let go when they tensed. "There's a difference between pursuing the truth and punishing yourself with it. You crossed that line somewhere around the second dive, and nobody's called it, because you're very good at making sure there's nobody close enough to call it. Well. I'm calling it."

The lamp flickered again. Thunder rolled in off the water, long and low, a sound more felt than heard.

"Why?" Orsen asked, and it came out almost angry, almost desperate. "Why are you doing this? Why now? You've had three years to say any of this. Why does it have to be now, when I'm—" They gestured vaguely at themselves, at the wreckage of composure, and couldn't finish.

"Because now I'm afraid," Priya said simply. "For three years I've been fond of you from a safe distance, and safe distances are comfortable, and I told myself the distance was respect. And then you started dying by inches in front of me and I discovered that the distance was cowardice. Mine." She tightened her grip on Orsen's hand. "I fled a scandal, Orsen. A malpractice suit that was mostly politics and partly my fault, and I came here to disappear, and disappearing worked beautifully until I met someone who had made an art form of it. Two cowards teaching each other to hide. That's not love. That's just loneliness with company."

The word hung in the wet air between them. Love. Priya had said it plainly, without ceremony, the way she did everything that mattered—because she had learned on the coast that hesitation cost lives, that the moment you softened a hard thing to make it easier to hear was the moment it failed to land.

Orsen's eyes were bright. Not crying—they were far too disciplined for crying, or perhaps too frightened—but bright the way the sea goes bright before a squall, all that pressure with nowhere to go.

"And what do you want me to say?" Orsen whispered.

"I want you to tell me the truth. Any truth. One true thing, said out loud, to another living person, on purpose." Priya leaned forward. "You've spent your whole life being no one in particular. Fine. I'm not asking you to become someone else. I'm asking you to be someone *to* someone. To me. Right now. That's all."

Outside, the storm intensified, as if the sky itself were leaning in to listen. Rain drove sideways against the glass. The clinic felt suddenly very small, two people and a lamp and forty years of separate silences converging on a single point.

Orsen looked down at their joined hands. Priya watched them the way she watched a patient teetering on the edge of confession in her examining room—knowing that what happened next depended less on courage than on whether the body decided, in its animal wisdom, that it was finally safe.

"I—" Orsen began.

And stopped. Priya saw the door close behind their eyes, saw the old architecture reassert itself, brick by invisible brick. When Orsen spoke again, the voice was level and distant and heartbreakingly polite.

"I need to check the barometer," they said. "If this front holds, the fifth tide comes early, and Reyes will move the Corvus tonight. If he blasts the inner chamber before I—" They rose, gently disengaging their hand, and Priya let it go because she was a doctor and she knew you could not hold a patient down who was determined to bleed out standing up. "Thank you. For the stitches. For... saying it. I heard you. I just can't—"

"Not yet," Priya said. "I know. Not yet."

Orsen paused at the door, oilskins dripping, half-turned back toward her, and for one suspended second Priya thought the wall might come down after all—that they might turn around and cross the room and let themselves be known. The lamp threw their shadow long across the floorboards, reaching toward her even as they didn't.

"There's something in the chamber," Orsen said quietly. "Something I found on the last dive. I haven't told anyone. Not Marta. Not anyone." A breath. "When this is over—if I'm still—when it's over, I'll tell you. I promise."

"Promises require surviving," Priya said. "Come back alive and we'll call it kept."

The ghost of a smile touched Orsen's mouth—there and gone, like light on moving water—and then the door opened onto the roaring dark and they were gone, boots splashing down the flooded lane toward the airstrip, toward the plane, toward the cave and the tides and the mother-shaped hole at the center of everything.

Priya stood alone in the lamplight with the suture scissors still in her hand and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she understood, with the clarity that always arrived a moment too late, that she had just done the bravest and possibly the most foolish thing of her life: she had told the truth to someone who wasn't yet free to answer it.

She began cleaning up, winding the tape, capping the saline, giving her hands work because her thoughts refused to be quieted. And it was in the middle of this—wiping down the table, the rain still hammering, the village dark beyond the windows—that she noticed it.

A scrap of oilcloth on the floor by the examination table, fallen from Orsen's coat when they'd shrugged it off. Small, folded tight, stained with salt. She should have left it. She knew she should have left it; privacy was the one currency she had never stolen.

But she was a doctor, and doctors were trained to notice what patients hid, and her hands were opening the fold before her conscience caught up.

Inside was a photograph, water-swollen and silvered with age: a young woman with Orsen's grey eyes and a stranger's smile, standing on the deck of a ship, her arm linked through the arm of a man whose face had been scratched out—deliberately, furiously, with something sharp. On the back, in faded pencil, in handwriting Priya had seen a hundred times on Orsen's flight manifests:

*Mama — before the Star. He knows.*

Priya stood holding it as the storm shook the little clinic, and outside, faint beneath the rain, she heard the sound she had been dreading all evening: the engine of Orsen's plane turning over on the darkened flats, coughing, catching, rising—carrying the person she loved straight back out into the teeth of the monsoon, toward the fifth tide, toward whatever waited in the drowned chamber with its scratched-out faces and its unfinished sentences.

She grabbed her kit and ran for the door.
