## 14. The Corvus Moves

The Corvus came up the coast at dawn like a rumor with an engine, her hull the color of wet slate, her crane arm folded against the superstructure like a sleeping bird of prey. Anton Reyes stood on the flying bridge with his coffee going cold in his hand and watched Salt Hollow resolve out of the morning haze—first the church spire, then the scatter of tin roofs, then the long gray sweep of tidal flats where, he was told, a bush pilot had landed a floatplane two days ago and stood in front of a cave like a man defending a cathedral.

Reyes had been doing this work for twenty-two years. He had pulled wrecks out of the Java Sea and the Coral Triangle and the cold green graveyards off the Hebrides, and he had learned early that every salvage operation was really two operations: the one you ran with cranes and permits, and the one you ran inside people. The second one always decided the first.

He finished the coffee. It tasted like the paper cup.

"Position's marked," said Okonkwo from the helm, her voice flat as chart paper. "Two hundred meters off the cave mouth. We'll hold with the bow thrusters when the tide turns."

"Good." He set the cup down. "Nobody touches the water until I say."

Below him, the deck was already alive—deckhands coiling line, the compressor coughing to life, the smell of diesel and salt and the particular metallic anticipation that settled over a ship when a deadline got close. Three tides left on his permit. Two usable lows after that, both outside the legal window, which meant the law had given him exactly enough rope to hang himself with if the weather turned mean. And the weather, this coast being what it was, intended to turn mean.

His satellite phone buzzed against his hip. He didn't have to look at the caller ID. There was only one person who called at this hour, and only one tone she used: the patient, silken patience of a woman who had never once in her life been told no by anything smaller than a government.

"Captain Reyes." Voss's voice came through crisp, unbothered by the eleven thousand kilometers between them. "I see you've arrived. Excellent. And the permit?"

"Signed yesterday. Blasting authorization is in my safe."

"And the obstruction? The pilot?"

"The pilot," Reyes said carefully, "is a local fixture. Delivers medicine. Half the coast would take a bullet for them."

"A bullet," Voss repeated, amused. "Charming. Captain, I've read your reports. You're a thorough man, so let me be thorough back. That wreck contains cargo my family has spent forty years looking for, and I do not mean sentimentally. If the local population becomes an obstacle, buy them. If they can't be bought, wait them out—the tide doesn't care whose grandmother drowned down there. And if the pilot becomes a legal problem—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel "—there are ways to make a person's history inconvenient. People who live under quiet names usually have quiet reasons. Find theirs."

The line went dead before he could answer, which was her way of not asking a question she already knew he'd obey.

Reyes stood a moment longer at the rail. Below, the flats were emptying as the tide began its long withdrawal, exposing ribbons of dark sand and tide pools that caught the light like spilled mercury. Somewhere under all that retreating water was a cave, and in the cave a ship, and in the ship—according to forty years of freight manifests Voss's lawyers had reconstructed—eleven crates of contraband gold that had never been reported stolen because it had never been legally anyone's.

He had told himself, signing the blasting permit, that this was ordinary work. Wrecks were wrecks. Dead men owned nothing; the sea kept their accounts. But there was a splinter in that reasoning, and he could feel it working deeper every day: the dead aboard the Meridian Star hadn't just drowned. According to the old articles he'd pulled from the regional archive—articles the villagers had photocopied and nailed to the church door like Luther—they had drowned running from a smuggling ring that some of their own number had built. Victims, the village said. Accomplices, Voss's historians implied. The truth, as always, was probably wearing both faces.

"Skipper." Okonkwo again, at his elbow now, her tablet held out. "Tide table. Low water at 14:40, minus one-point-three. We'll have maybe ninety minutes of workable slack in the cave throat. After that the surge comes back through like a freight train."

"Ninety minutes." He nodded. "Get Vasquez suited up."

* * *

Vasquez was already at the dive station when Reyes came down, sitting on the bench with his fins in his lap in the unhurried way of a man who had dressed for deep water more times than he'd eaten breakfast. He was fifty-one, built like driftwood, with a face the sun had cured brown and a scar through one eyebrow from a reef in the Sulu Sea that he refused to discuss. He'd been diving wrecks longer than Reyes had been captaining them. He was also, Reyes had discovered three days ago while reviewing crew files for the hundredth time, the reason this whole operation had begun to feel less like salvage and more like fate playing a very slow joke.

Because Vasquez had known her.

Not Astrid—not the name on the manifest, not the dead girl from 1947. That would have been impossible. He had known Ingrid Eriksen under the name she'd worn afterward: Lena Marsh, dive tender, Saba and Curaçao, late seventies. Reyes had found the overlap by accident—a name in Vasquez's service record, a port, a decade—and had said nothing, waiting to see if the old diver would say something first.

He hadn't. Which meant either he didn't know what Reyes knew, or he knew exactly, and was keeping it in the airless dark where divers keep the things that scare them.

"Water's cold today," Vasquez said, not looking up. "Thermocline's shallow. You'll want me on the second chamber, not the outer throat."

"You've seen the survey charts."

"I've seen them." He pulled a fin strap tight. "Charts are what the sea tells you it is. The cave is what it actually is. Different documents."

Reyes leaned against the rail beside him. Out across the flats, the tide was peeling back from the land in long silver sheets, and the cave mouth was beginning to show itself—a black seam in the headland, low and breathing, exactly as hostile as advertised.

"How long since you worked these waters?" Reyes asked, casual as a man discussing weather.

"Never worked these waters." Vasquez spat over the side, an old habit. "Worked the Caribbean mostly. Red Sea once. Bad year."

"What year?"

A pause. The compressor throbbed somewhere behind them. When Vasquez finally looked up, his eyes were the flat gray-green of deep water, and Reyes understood that the old man had known this conversation was coming since the day he signed aboard.

"Eighty-one," Vasquez said. "Through eighty-six. Tender and safety diver, Windward Islands. Worked with a woman named Lena Marsh who could read a wreck like a priest reads scripture. She taught half the salvage divers in the Antilles how to survive a collapsing chamber." He tied off the fin strap with unnecessary precision. "She disappeared in eighty-seven. Left a boat, left gear, left a life, gone between one tide and the next. People said smugglers got her. People said she got smart and left first. Both stories had believers."

"And you believed which one?"

Vasquez stood, slow, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and looked out at the cave.

"I believed she was running from something that finally found her address." He turned. His voice dropped, not for drama but because some things shouldn't be said above a murmur on a working deck. "You've done your homework, skipper. I saw you reading my file. So let's save us both the dance. Lena Marsh. Ingrid Eriksen. Same woman. I did the math years ago when I heard about a Meridian Star coming up off this coast—I keep track of ghosts, it's a bad habit of the trade. She had a kid. Never talked about it, but there was a photograph in her kit, laminated against the salt. A child on a dock. She'd look at it sometimes when she thought nobody was watching, the way you check a wound to see if it's healing. It wasn't."

The wind moved along the deck, carrying the smell of exposed weed from the flats.

"She faked her death in '47," Reyes said quietly. "Lived thirty years under another name. Then vanished again in eighty-seven."

"People don't vanish twice by accident, captain." Vasquez shouldered his tank. "First time, she was hiding from something. Second time, she was hiding from someone finding the first thing. You put those together with a salvage permit and a rich backer, and you get a question I'd want answered before anybody lights a fuse down there."

"Which is?"

The old diver looked at him for a long moment, and Reyes saw something in his face he had almost never seen there in six years of working together: doubt. Not fear—Vasquez didn't scare—but the particular unease of a man who has realized the water he's about to enter belongs to somebody else's grief.

"That everything in that cave," Vasquez said, "she spent her whole second life making sure stayed buried. And now we're bringing dynamite." He clipped his mask to his harness. "I'll dive your chamber, skipper. I always dive the chamber. But you should know what I know, because you're the one who has to live with the order."

He walked to the dive ladder without waiting for an answer.

* * *

Low water came at 14:40 exactly, the sea drawing back from the headland like a held breath released. From the bridge, Reyes watched the cave throat emerge fully for the first time—a jagged arch of black basalt, twenty feet high at its crown, dripping, exhaling a fog of cold air that smelled of stone and old iron and something else, something organic and sweetish, like the inside of a long-sealed room.

Vasquez went down at 14:52. Reyes listened to the whole dive on the comm, standing at the sonar display with his arms crossed, watching the diver's trace move through the outer throat into the corridor beyond.

"Visibility's better than expected," Vasquez's voice crackled, filtered through the rebreather scrubbers. "Four meters, maybe five. Following the port side. Hull plating's intact here—she went in bow-first, must have been riding a flood tide. Coming up on the bulkhead breach now."

Silence, except for breathing.

"There's scarring on the rock," Vasquez said at last. "Old tool marks. Somebody's been in this chamber before—with equipment. Recent-ish. Decades, not centuries." A pause. "And there's a sealed compartment aft. Riveted plate over what looks like a passenger section. No corrosion breach. Whatever's in there has been dry since '47."

"Can you open it?"

"Not with my hands. Not in ninety minutes." Another pause, longer. "Skipper. There's writing on the plate. Scratched into the metal, inside surface of the compartment door—someone scratched it from within, or wrote it before sealing. I can't read it all. Silt. But I can read part of it."

Reyes closed his eyes briefly. "Read it."

"'I. E.,'" Vasquez said slowly, "'for A. Forgive me. Live far. Live loud.'" A breath through the regulator, steady as machinery. "There's a date under it. August '47. Three weeks after the sinking."

The bridge was very quiet. On the sonar display, Vasquez's trace hung motionless in the black water of the inner chamber, a small bright soul suspended over a ship full of the dead.

Ingrid Eriksen. For Astrid. The child on the dock, laminated against the salt.

She hadn't drowned. She hadn't even simply survived—she had stood in that compartment, in the weeks after the wreck, and carved a message to the child she was about to abandon to the world's mercy, and then she had walked out of the sea and out of history and lived another forty years under a borrowed name, and somewhere out on that coast right now was a bush pilot named Astrid who delivered medicine and gossip and remembered nothing, and who had stood on the tidal flats two days ago defending this cave as though the bones in it were kin.

Which, Reyes thought, they probably were.

"Pull him up," he said. His voice came out level, professional, and he hated how easy the professionalism still was, how many years it took to build a voice that could say pull him up over a message from the dead and sound like a man discussing ballast.

Vasquez surfaced at 16:10, climbing the ladder with water sheeting off him, and stood dripping on the deck looking at Reyes with that flat gray-green gaze.

"Well, skipper," he said. "Now you know what I know."

"Yes."

"So what's the order tomorrow?"

Reyes looked past him, toward the headland, where the tide was already sliding back in to cover the cave, patient as a creditor. Tomorrow the blasting permit became active. Tomorrow, if he gave the word, the Corvus would set charges in the outer throat and blow open a passage that had been sealed by grief and geology for seventy-five years, and whatever Voss wanted out of that compartment would come up into the light, and so would everything else—the letters, the effects, the scratched plea of a mother to a child, the whole drowned archive of a lie that a whole coast had been living inside of.

He thought about Voss's voice on the phone, silk over steel. Find theirs.

He thought about the magistrate's warning, the villagers' lantern-lit meetings, the pilot standing alone on the flats.

"Tomorrow," Reyes said, "we position the charges."

Vasquez held his stare a moment longer, then nodded once, unreadable, and went below to wash the sea off.

That night Reyes did not sleep. He sat in his cabin with the permit in front of him and the sonar log open on his desk, and he read the transcribed words again and again—I.E., for A. Forgive me. Live far. Live loud.—until the letters stopped meaning language and started meaning weight. At 0300 he got up and stood at the porthole, watching the Corvus ride her anchor chain in the rising swell, and admitted to himself, in the privacy of the dark where captains keep the things that scare them, that for the first time in twenty-two years he did not know whether the next order he gave would be the right one—or merely the profitable one.

Out in the black water, the tide turned, and began, tide by patient tide, to give the cave back its silence.

And forty miles up the coast, unseen, a single aircraft light blinked low over the monsoon clouds, heading for the records office—someone else, it seemed, was racing him to the truth, and they had a head start he wouldn't discover until it was nearly too late.
