## 15. Sabotage

The night the Corvus broke loose, Orsen slept badly, and that was the only alibi they had.

They'd lain awake in Marta's spare room listening to the monsoon's outer bands drag across the roof like a hand testing a latch, thinking about tides, about permits, about the way Reyes had smiled when he said *six tides* as though time itself were on his payroll. Around three in the morning the wind shifted and the rain came sideways, and Orsen finally drifted into something that wasn't quite sleep—half-dreams of black water and a locket turning over and over in their palm like a coin that would never spend.

So they heard nothing. That was the thing everyone would come back to, later. They heard nothing.

Dawn came gray and bruised, the kind of light that made the harbor look like old pewter. Orsen was down at the water before six, checking the tie-downs on the plane out of habit, when the first shout went up from the quay. Then another. By the time Orsen reached the seawall, half of Salt Hollow was already there, standing in the drizzle with their collars up, looking at the survey vessel sitting wrong against her moorings—listing slightly toward the channel, swinging free, four cut lines trailing in the water like severed tendons.

Not dragged. Not slipped. Cut. The frayed ends were clean, deliberate, each one sliced above the chafe gear where no storm would have bitten. Whoever had done it had come alongside in the dark, in weather that would have swamped any skiff less than seaworthy, and gone to work with a blade and patience.

Reyes stood on his own deck in an oilskin, arms folded, watching the crowd gather the way a man watches weather he predicted. He didn't shout. He didn't have to. When enough of the village had assembled along the seawall, he raised his voice just enough to carry over the slap of water against hulls.

"Someone in this town knows something," he said. "And I'd take it as a courtesy if they told me before the magistrate does."

Nobody answered. But heads turned. Orsen felt them turn—the particular weight of a dozen glances arriving at once, the way a crowd's suspicion has mass and direction, like current. Marta stood beside them, and her shoulder was there, solid, but her face was carefully blank in a way it hadn't been since the lantern meeting.

Orsen looked at the cut lines and thought: *I was asleep.* And then thought: *I was asleep alone, in a house full of people who know I hate the Corvus being here.* And understood, with a cold settling in the chest, that those two facts were the same fact now.

* * *

The magistrate arrived by noon, ferried over from the mainland seat on a government launch, a narrow man named Odell with rain-beaded spectacles and a voice like dry paper. He set up in the fish hall because it was the only building big enough, and he conducted the inquiry the way a man drains a pond—to see what's in it.

Orsen sat through two hours of testimony on a bench that smelled of brine and diesel. Fishermen described the weather. The harbormaster produced the mooring logs. A boy swore he'd seen a light moving near the water around midnight—"a torch, maybe, or a lamp"—and couldn't say more than that. And then Odell took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, and looked directly at Orsen for the first time all day.

"Ms. Eriksen." The title landed strangely; most of Salt Hollow still called them by first name, or nothing at all. "You've made no secret of your opposition to the salvage operation."

"I've made no secret of anything," Orsen said. "That's rather new for me."

A ripple of something—not quite laughter—went through the benches. Odell didn't smile.

"You were seen arguing with Captain Reyes on the quay Tuesday. You flew over the Corvus at low altitude last week—a maneuver several witnesses describe as intimidating. You have a pilot's access to boats, fuel, night hours unaccounted for. And you have a personal interest in the wreck that you have never fully explained to this community."

The hall went very quiet. Rain ticked against the tin roof. Orsen was aware of Priya three rows back, very still, and of Marta beside them, and of the whole warm crowded fact of Salt Hollow suddenly arranged into an audience.

"My interest is my family's dead," Orsen said. "That's the explanation. I haven't hidden it. I've just stopped offering it to people who ask questions with verdicts already written."

"Then answer this one plainly. Where were you between midnight and four?"

"Asleep. In Marta Voss's house. Alone in a room, which is to say nowhere anyone can prove."

Odell put his spectacles back on. "Convenient."

"It's the truth," Orsen said. "It's only convenient if you've already decided otherwise."

He let that sit. Then he delivered the sentence he'd clearly come to deliver, wrapping it in procedure so it would go down easier: the sabotage investigation would be referred to the mainland constabulary; the village's provisional claim to the wreck site—filed collectively after the lantern meeting, the one scrap of legal standing Salt Hollow had managed to scrape together—would be suspended pending review. "If your community cannot maintain order," Odell said, addressing the room now, not Orsen, "it cannot hold rights. That is how the law sees it. I'd suggest you see it that way too."

The benches emptied slowly, in silence, the way water goes out of a tide pool.

* * *

Afterward, on the quay, the village did what villages do, which is not what towns do. No one accused Orsen to their face. They simply stopped talking when Orsen walked past. The baker sold them bread without meeting their eyes. Two fishermen who'd waved every morning for three years found urgent business with their nets. It wasn't hostility. It was worse—it was arithmetic. Orsen could almost see the sums running behind their neighbors' faces: *one stranger, however useful, against the whole village's claim.*

Only Marta spoke plainly, and she did it on her own doorstep, in the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool.

"I don't believe you did it," she said. "But I'll tell you what I believe that matters. Somebody wanted it done, and somebody wanted it blamed on you, and those might be the same somebody or two different somebodies, and either way you're the knife they're using." She pressed a heel of dark bread into Orsen's hands, an old gesture, automatic. "Question is whether you let yourself be the knife or start being the hand."

"Whose hand? Doing what?"

"That," Marta said, "is what you're supposed to figure out before the constables arrive. Because they won't ask who benefits. They'll ask who's strange. And love, you've spent years making sure that's you."

* * *

Orsen walked the tideline until dark, past the cave mouth breathing its slow breath under the last of the ebb, and tried to think like someone who cut mooring lines. Who benefited? Reyes, obviously—he got sympathy, jurisdiction, a pretext to demand armed protection for his operation. But Reyes hadn't needed sabotage; the law was already his. Unless the law was starting to feel less certain than he liked. Unless the letters, the effects, the truth leaking out of that chamber had begun to frighten him.

Or the backer. The name had surfaced twice now, always secondhand, always in Reyes's careful phrasing: *my client's interests.* A man rich enough to buy blasting permits and jurisdictional loopholes was rich enough to buy a midnight boat crew too—and rich enough to prefer that the blame land on the bush pilot who kept finding things in the cave before the professionals did.

Orsen stood in the wind and admitted the shape of it: whoever did this had known exactly which thread to pull. Not the plane—too obvious. Not a person—too costly. Just the village's fragile legal standing, snapped like a mooring line, with Orsen tied to the other end.

It was nearly full dark when Priya found them, walking out along the shingle with a lantern held low, her coat streaming.

"I've been looking everywhere," she said. "You can't stand out here cataloguing enemies by yourself."

"I'm not cataloguing. I'm counting."

"Same thing, with you." She stopped beside them, close enough that the lantern lit them both. "Listen to me. Odell's report goes to the mainland tomorrow. If the constables come asking, you need witnesses, an account, something better than 'I was asleep.' So tell me everything you did yesterday, hour by hour, and don't leave anything out because it seems small. Small is what saves people."

Orsen told her. The morning run to the lighthouse station. Fuel. The archive, again, cross-checking passenger names against coastal parish records. Coffee at Priya's own table at four o'clock—she confirmed it, frowning, adding it to the list. Supper at Marta's. Bed. Nothing. Hours of nothing.

Priya wrote it all in a little waterproof notebook, her handwriting slanting hard in the wind. When she finished she looked up, and in the lantern light her expression was doing something complicated—anger and fear and something underneath both that she was keeping, as always, carefully triaged.

"There's one more thing," she said quietly. "I checked the infirmary log. The night the lines were cut, someone came to the dispensary door around eleven—knocked, and left before I answered. I assumed a fisherman too proud to be seen sick. But this morning the latch was smeared with tar. Boat tar. Someone had come off the water."

Orsen stared at her. "You waited until now to say that?"

"I waited until it was safe to say it where no one else could hear," Priya said. "Because if I announce it in the fish hall, the next smear of tar lands on me, and then neither of us can help you." She closed the notebook. "Whoever cut those lines stopped at my door first, Orsen. Maybe hiding. Maybe checking whether I was awake. Maybe—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was louder than the wind, "—maybe wanting me to see them. Wanting a witness who'd stay quiet."

"Why would I stay quiet?"

"Because I'm the one person on this coast whose word for you is worth anything right now," Priya said. "And whoever planned this knew that too."

The lantern guttered. Out past the point, invisible in the dark, the Corvus rode at anchor on fresh lines, and somewhere aboard her a man named Reyes was almost certainly awake, doing his own arithmetic. Orsen looked at Priya—at the only person who had ever chosen their side without being asked—and felt the trap close another notch, and understood at last that this was never going to be solved by staying invisible.

Tomorrow the constables would come. Tonight, there was a dispensary door with tar on the latch, and a name Orsen had sworn off using, and one tide left before the law arrived to do everyone's thinking for them.

"Priya," Orsen said. "How fast can you get me that log?"
