## 16. Confessions at Slack Tide

The cave had a way of breathing.

Orsen had noticed it on the first visit, and again on the second, and now, trapped inside it with Dr. Priya Nair while the sea climbed the walls like something hungry, they noticed it most of all. The water came in through the lower throat of the passage in long, slow surges, and each surge pushed air out through cracks in the limestone above, so that the whole chamber exhaled—a low, wet sigh, salt-smelling, patient. The tide was not supposed to be this high for another four hours. The almanac said so. The almanac had been wrong before; the monsoon was rearranging everything, shoving water where it didn't belong, and now the two of them stood ankle-deep in a rising pool beneath the ribs of the Meridian Star, watching their exit disappear.

"We have time," Priya said, though her voice did the thing voices do when they're trying to convince themselves. She held her phone up toward the crack of grey light at the cave mouth. "No signal. Of course no signal." She lowered it. "Marta knows we came down here?"

"Marta knows I came down here."

"And you told her you were bringing me because—"

"Because you followed me," Orsen said. "I didn't bring you anywhere. You saw me take the trail from the clinic and you followed, and I was too tired to argue."

"I wasn't following you. I was walking."

"You were wearing clinic shoes."

Priya looked down at her white sneakers, already soaked to the laces, and made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been rationed between them for three years. "Fine. I was following you. You've been flying like a maniac and sleeping like a corpse and looking through me like I'm a window, and I wanted to know what was on the other side of the glass." She gestured at the dark around them—at the curved timbers emerging from the black water like the skeleton of some drowned animal, at the rusted plaque Orsen had pried loose weeks ago, at the scatter of corroded metal and cloth that the fifth tide's blast had shaken loose from the inner chamber. "So this is it. This is what's been eating you."

The water rose past their ankles. Somewhere above them, in the cracked ceiling, a drip found a rhythm.

Orsen should have lied. That was the old reflex, the deep one—the one that had kept them alive since they were nine years old and learning that names were doors and doors could be kicked in. Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing. But the cave had taken the choice away. There was nowhere to leave to. The tide had sealed them in with the wreck and with each other, and the truth sat in Orsen's chest like ballast, too heavy to carry and impossible to jettison.

"Her name was Ingrid Eriksen," Orsen said.

Priya went still. Even the water seemed to hush.

"My mother. She's on the manifest. Passenger list, Meridian Star, 1947. Officially dead. Drowned with forty-one others when the ship came apart on the outer shoal." Orsen's voice sounded strange in the cave, flattened by stone, stripped of all its usual careful weather. "Except she didn't drown. I found the passenger compartment sealed behind the collapse. Her effects were there—but packed. Folded clothes. A bag stowed neat as Sunday. Nobody packs a bag when they're drowning, Priya. She walked out of this wreck. She let the sea keep her name, and she took mine away with her, and I spent thirty years thinking I was the kind of person a mother leaves."

The exhale came again through the ceiling, longer this time. Orsen pressed a palm against one of the ribs of the hull, cold and furred with barnacle, and told the rest of it—the childhood of borrowed surnames, the aunt who spoke in whispers about scandal, the first forged document at fifteen, the bush pilot's license earned under a name that fit like a coat two sizes wrong. The rule. Land, deliver, leave, remember nothing. The years of being no one in particular, which had felt like safety right up until a record-low tide pulled the sea back and showed them the one thing they'd been running from, sitting in the dark the whole time, patient as bone.

"She didn't abandon me," Orsen said, and heard their own voice crack on it, ugly and human. "Or maybe she did. I don't know yet. There's a letter somewhere in that chamber—I've seen the tin, I haven't been able to open it—and I'm terrified of what it says. Because if she left to protect me, then my whole life of hiding was a gift I never knew I was given. And if she left to protect herself—" They stopped. Swallowed salt air. "Then I inherited the running. Either way, I never got to choose anything except how well I disappeared."

The water was at mid-calf now. Priya hadn't moved closer, but she hadn't moved away either, and in the thin light from the cave mouth her face was very steady, the way it got in the clinic when someone was bleeding and panicking and needed a calm to borrow.

"Say the name again," she said quietly.

"Ingrid."

"No. Yours. The real one."

Orsen stared at her. Thirty years, and no one had asked—not Marta, not the magistrate, not Reyes with all his permits and patience. The name lived in a locked drawer of them, folded like Ingrid's clothes, stowed neat as Sunday.

"Eirik," Orsen said. "Eirik Eriksen. She named me after her father. I dropped it when I dropped everything else." Saying it aloud in the cave felt like opening a valve. Something rushed out; something else rushed in. "It doesn't feel like mine anymore. But it's hers. It's the only thing she gave me that I know is true."

"It's true," Priya said. "And so are you. Whatever name you fly under."

* * *

The water reached their knees and stopped climbing—or slowed, anyway, the surge finding its slack, the sea pausing at the top of its breath. They climbed onto a shelf of rock above the waterline, shoulder to shoulder, sharing the last dry square meter of the cave, and Priya dug a foil-wrapped packet of biscuits out of her jacket pocket because she was, apparently, the kind of doctor who carried emergency rations for other people's emergencies.

"Eat," she said. "Hypoglycemia makes confessions melodramatic."

"You're one to talk."

"I haven't confessed anything."

"You will," Orsen said. "Everyone in this cave confesses eventually. It's in the lease."

Priya laughed—really laughed, once, brief and startled out of her—and then the laugh died and she looked at the black water for a long time, turning the biscuit wrapper over in her fingers. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed register. Lower. Careful. A voice coming off a high shelf of its own.

"You remember my file," she said. "The one the health board has on me. Third-year residency, St. Brendan's Regional. You've seen the way I flinch when anyone mentions audits."

"I've seen you flinch."

"There was a patient. Devan Menon, fifty-four, chest pain, atypical presentation. I was exhausted, sixteenth hour of a double shift, and I read his ECG as gastritis and sent him home with antacids." Her thumb creased the foil, back and forth, back and forth. "He arrested in his car in the parking garage. By the time anyone reached him, there was nothing left to restart. His daughter was in the passenger seat. She was nineteen."

The drip above them kept its rhythm. Orsen didn't say *it happens*, or *you were exhausted*, or any of the absolutions that would have been true and useless. They waited, the way you wait at a bedside.

"The board cleared me. Procedural error under fatigue, no negligence, case closed, record sealed." Priya's laugh came out flat. "Sealed. Do you hear it? We speak the same language, you and I. Sealed records. Assumed names. Clean slates that aren't clean, just closed. I chose Salt Hollow because it was the farthest posting from anyone who knew, and I told myself I was serving an underserved coast, and some days that was even true. But mostly I was hiding, Orsen. Three years of hiding in plain sight, patching fishing cuts and delivering babies and being very, very good, because being good was the penance and the camouflage both." She turned to look at them, and her eyes were wet and furious and unflinching. "And then you flew in with your medicine crates and your thousand-yard stare, and I thought: there. Someone else who's sealed. I could breathe near that. I could be quiet near that. I built three years of friendship out of our mutual silence, and I told myself it was enough."

"Was it?"

"No." The word came out without armor. "It was never enough. I fell in love with you somewhere around the second winter, and I couldn't even say it, because saying it meant being known, and being known meant—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Devan Menon's daughter knows my face. If she ever walks into my clinic, I don't know what I'll do. That's the tide I live with. That's my cave. So when I say I understand why you ran your whole life, understand that I mean it from the waterline up."

The sea breathed out below them. Orsen found Priya's hand in the dark—not reaching so much as colliding, two hands arriving at the same cold rock at the same moment—and held it. Priya's fingers were freezing and gripped back like a lifeline.

"I can't answer you yet," Orsen said. "Not the way you deserve. I'm still full of Ingrid. There's no room in me for anything else until I know what's in that tin."

"I'm not asking for an answer. I'm asking you to stop disappearing." Priya turned her hand over and laced their fingers together, deliberate, witnessed by no one but the dead. "Fly reckless one more time and I'll ground you myself. Come back every time. That's all. Come back, and let me watch you come back, and we'll call that a beginning."

Above them, faint through the stone, came a sound that did not belong to the cave or the sea: an engine, deep-throated and rhythmic, out on the water beyond the headland. Orsen knew every engine on this coast by its heartbeat, and this one they knew best of all—the Corvus, moving in the dark hours ahead of the sixth tide, positioning itself over the cave like a held breath.

"They're here early," Orsen said.

Priya followed their gaze up through the dripping dark toward the sound. "How early?"

"Early enough that the law won't matter by morning. Only the water will." Orsen squeezed her hand once, then let go, and stood on the rock shelf with the wreck at their back and the letter waiting in its tin somewhere below the black water, and the engine beating outside like a countdown. "When this tide turns, we swim for it. And Priya—when we surface, whatever happens next, I need you to remember one thing."

"What thing?"

"My name is Eirik Eriksen. Say it with me, so somebody in this world knows."

Outside, the Corvus dropped anchor over the grave of the Meridian Star, and the sound rolled into the cave like thunder deciding whether to become rain.
