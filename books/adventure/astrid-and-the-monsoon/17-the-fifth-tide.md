## 17. The Fifth Tide

Anton Reyes had blown up ships before. He had never hated it before.

He stood on the deck of the *Corvus* at four in the morning, watching the monsoon come down the coast like a slow grey animal, and told himself—for the eleventh time since midnight—that the charges were placed correctly, that the timing was sound, that the rock above the inner chamber would part along the fault line his surveyor had mapped with sonar and patience. He told himself these things the way a man tells himself a ladder is sound while standing on a rung he can hear creaking.

The problem was not the engineering. The engineering was beautiful. The problem was Voss.

* * *

Edmund Voss had arrived two days early, unannounced, on a white-hulled yacht that looked less like a vessel than an argument about money. It came around the headland at dusk with its lights already burning, absurd against the fishing boats hauling in under storm-dark skies, and Reyes had watched it from the bridge with the particular dread of a man who recognizes, in the silhouette of another man's boat, the shape of his own mistake.

Voss came aboard in a cashmere coat dry as paper despite the rain, shook Reyes's hand with fingers that were cold for reasons no weather could explain, and said, "Show me where my gold is sleeping."

"Your gold," Reyes said, "isn't asleep yet."

"Everything sleeps eventually." Voss smiled. "That's why we wake things."

They stood at the rail while the yacht anchored offshore, a second light now, redundant and watchful. Below them the bay lay emptied by the tide, black mud and standing water, the cave mouth visible as a seam of deeper darkness in the cliff. Somewhere down there, behind forty feet of basalt, the Meridian Star was holding her secrets the way she'd held them for seventy years—patiently, and without any intention of giving them up to men like him.

Reyes knew how men like him worked because he was one. That had always been the arrangement: he supplied the competence, someone else supplied the conscience, and between the two of them they got the job done. For thirty years this division of labor had served him well. He had stripped wrecks from the Java Sea to the Hebrides, and if he sometimes dreamed of hulls groaning in the dark, of brass fittings coming loose in his hands like teeth, well—he'd learned long ago that dreams were just invoices the mind sent to the wrong department.

But Voss didn't want the wreck opened. Voss wanted it *breached*. There was a difference, and the difference was dynamite.

"The permit specifies controlled demolition," Reyes had said, the first night, over charts spread across the Corvus's mess table. "Sequential micro-charges along the fault plane. The chamber opens clean. No damage to contents."

"Contents," Voss repeated, tasting the word. "You talk about gold bullion like it's your grandmother's china."

"I talk about it like it's evidence. Provenance matters. Condition matters. You blow that chamber apart and half what's in there is scrap."

"And half is still worth more than you'll earn in ten years." Voss had leaned across the chart, and his shadow fell over the Meridian Star's penciled outline the way night falls over a harbor. "The window closes in two tides, Captain. The magistrate will not extend it. Your blasting authorization expires with it. So we do it now, tonight, or we do it never—and I did not sail eleven thousand miles to do it never."

Reyes had looked at the chart instead of the man. At the little annotations in his own handwriting: *fault line*, *charge points 1–6*, *slack water 0440*. Everything correct. Everything ready. And underneath it all, faint as a watermark, the thing he'd been refusing to read since the diver Kwan had come to him three nights ago with salt-damp eyes and a story about a woman named Ingrid who hadn't drowned in 1947 after all.

"Tonight," Reyes said finally. Because what else was there? A man doesn't spend twenty years building a reputation and then hand it back over a feeling.

* * *

Now it was four in the morning, and the feeling had grown teeth.

The demolition crew worked by floodlight on the tidal flats, small figures in yellow slickers moving between charge points with the unhurried precision of surgeons. Rain came down in fine, needling sheets. The generator coughed. Somewhere inland a dog was barking at nothing, had been barking for an hour, and the sound carried across the emptied bay like a warning nobody had asked for.

Kwan stood beside him at the rail, silent. The diver had barely spoken since telling him about Ingrid Eriksen—or whatever name she'd died under—since describing, in the flat voice of a man emptying out a room he'd lived in too long, how she'd run supply runs for a smuggling ring out of the same archipelago Voss's family money had touched, decades ago, before the ring broke apart and the survivors scattered like spilled ball bearings. How she'd been good at it. How she'd disappeared one season and stayed disappeared.

"You think she's down there," Reyes said. Not a question.

"I think something is down there that isn't gold." Kwan pulled his hood tighter. "I've dived wrecks for nineteen years, Captain. Gold wrecks have a smell. Greed has a smell. This—" he nodded at the cave mouth, at the water sliding darkly in and out of it like breath, "—this smells like a door somebody locked on purpose."

"Then why are you here?"

Kwan considered the question honestly, which Reyes found worse than evasion. "Because I wanted to see if I was right. And because a man who walks off a job mid-blast still gets paid for the blast." He paused. "One of those reasons is the true one. I'll know which when the smoke clears."

Below, the crew chief raised an orange flag. The floodlights swung, caught the wet rock of the cliff face, held it there—ancient, indifferent, streaked with seventy years of salt.

"Charges armed," crackled the radio on Reyes's hip. "Awaiting your word, Captain."

Reyes looked at the cave. Looked at the yacht riding its anchor lights offshore, patient as a spider. He thought about the manifest in his cabin, the list of names he'd read so many times they'd begun to feel like passengers of his own—a ship within his ship, sailing wherever he sailed. Forty-one souls. He thought about the woman in Salt Hollow with the pilot's face and the locked jaw, the one whose mother's name sat on that list in faded ink, and how she had looked at him across the magistrate's table not like an opponent but like a person deciding whether he was worth hating.

He had told himself she was an obstacle. Obstacles were simpler.

"Captain?" the radio said.

"Fire on my count." He raised his hand, and the rain ran down his wrist into his sleeve, cold as a confession. "Three. Two. One."

* * *

The sea announced the explosion before the sound arrived.

A swell rose out of the cave mouth—not large, but wrong, moving against every current the bay owned—and then the boom reached them, felt through the deck plates and up through boot soles and spine, a single deep concussion like the world clearing its throat. Gulls exploded off the cliffs in a ragged white sheet. The dog inland stopped barking.

Smoke rolled from the seam in the rock, grey-white against the grey morning, smelling of cordite and crushed stone and something older beneath it—the mineral breath of a place that had been sealed since before anyone alive was born.

For a long moment, nothing. The crew on the flats stood motionless, faces turned upward. Even the rain seemed to pause, gathering itself.

Then the rock face shivered, sighed, and slumped inward along the fault line with a grinding roar, six tons of basalt folding away like a curtain being drawn by a tired hand—and the inner chamber of the Meridian Star stood open to the sky for the first time since 1947.

"Camera," Reyes said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Lights on the approach. Let's go look."

* * *

He went in himself. He always went in himself; that was the rule, the penance, whatever it was. The new breach was stable enough—his surveyor confirmed it twice, scowling at the fresh fracture maps—but the floor was ankle-deep in seawater and shifting rubble, and the air inside tasted of iron and time.

His headlamp swept the chamber.

And stopped.

Not gold.

The far wall—forty feet of it, curving with the old hull—was covered. Floor to overhead, in rows, in hundreds, in thousands: personal effects. Suitcases stacked like masonry. Coats hung on lines strung between stanchions, rotted now to lace. Shoes, pairs and singles, arranged with terrible neatness. Photographs in oilcloth packets, hundreds of them, their corners curled. And letters—bundles of letters, tied with string gone soft as thread, labeled in faded ink in a dozen hands: names, dates, destinations. *To be delivered.* One bundle near the center bore a label in careful block capitals: **IF THE SHIP IS LOST — FOR THE FAMILIES.**

It was not a treasure vault. It was a post office. A dead-letter office for the drowned.

Reyes stood in the water among the shoes and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a hull taking water below the waterline, exactly what he was looking at. The Meridian Star hadn't been carrying gold. She'd been carrying the smuggling ring's paper—letters, ledgers, identities, the whole circulatory system of an operation that moved people and contraband and money up and down this coast. The "treasure" legend had been built deliberately, a decoy story to explain why the ship mattered, to draw salvage hunters toward a fantasy of bullion and away from the truth of documents. Documents that would have named names. Names that included, very possibly, the ancestors of Edmund Voss.

And when the ship went down, someone—several someones—had done the last thing such people ever do. They had organized. They had bundled the letters. They had written instructions for delivery to the families, knowing they would drown, knowing the letters would never arrive, doing it anyway, because order in the face of death was the only dignity left to them.

"Captain." Kwan's voice behind him, hollow in the stone. His lamp found the wall of effects and held there. "Nineteen years," he said quietly. "I've never seen anything like this."

"No," Reyes said. "Neither have I."

He picked up one of the oilcloth packets. Inside, a photograph: a young woman on a dock, squinting into sun, holding a child on her hip. On the back, in pencil: *Marta's boy, summer '46.* Marta. The old woman in Salt Hollow. The boy would be sixty now, if he'd lived, which the manifest said he hadn't.

Reyes put the photograph back exactly where it had lain, aligned to the millimeter, though no one would ever know or care. It mattered anyway. It mattered the way a man's signature matters on a document he knows will ruin him.

Behind him, boots splashed. The crew chief, breathless, holding a portable lamp high. "Captain—we've checked the forward section. No bullion. No strongroom. There's a ledger case, sealed, stamped with shipping marks—and papers, hundreds of pages. Sir, some of these names—" He hesitated, and Reyes watched a man realize, in real time, that he was holding something heavier than gold. "Some of these are still known names. Big ones."

Of course they were. Voss's family fortune had a smell too, and Reyes had caught it only now, too late, standing waist-deep in other people's mail.

"Seal the chamber," Reyes said. "Nothing leaves. Nothing gets photographed for distribution. Post two men at the breach."

"Sir?"

"You heard me."

The crew chief looked at him, then at the wall of suitcases and letters and photographs, then back. Whatever he saw in Reyes's face made him nod and turn and splash away, calling orders in a voice carefully kept level.

Kwan lingered. "So," he said. "Which reason was the true one?"

Reyes didn't answer. Above, through the new wound in the cliff, the monsoon light came down grey and thin, falling across the shoes, the coats, the bundles marked *for the families*, and it looked almost like weather inside a church.

His radio crackled. The mate on the Corvus, apologetic, urgent: "Captain, Mr. Voss's launch is crossing the bay. He's coming ashore. He says—" a pause, the sound of a man relaying words he disliked, "—he says he wants to inspect his property."

Reyes closed his eyes. In the dark behind them he saw the photograph again: the woman on the dock, the child on her hip, the pencil note naming a boy who had drowned. He thought of the pilot, Orsen Eriksen, and understood suddenly, completely, why she had stood in front of this cave like a wall herself.

Because some things weren't cargo. Some things were the dead, still trying to finish their deliveries.

"He can inspect," Reyes said into the radio, opening his eyes. "But he comes through me. And someone get me the magistrate's frequency. Now."

Kwan raised an eyebrow. "Changing sides, Captain?"

"No," Reyes said, turning toward the light, toward the launch cutting white across the black bay, toward the man in the cashmere coat waiting to claim a fortune made of other people's last words. "Finally picking one."
