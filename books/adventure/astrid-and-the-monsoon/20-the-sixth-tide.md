## 20. The Sixth Tide

Anton Reyes had signed away wrecks before. He had signed away ships, contracts, crews, a marriage, and once—briefly, in a hotel bar in Panama—a version of himself he'd spent the next decade trying to buy back. But he had never signed away a fortune. Not until the sixth tide.

He stood at the rail of the *Corvus* at dawn and watched the water fall away from the coast like a curtain being drawn by a careful hand. The monsoon had spent itself overnight; what remained was a washed sky, rinsed pale, and a sea lying flat and exhausted in its own aftermath. Below him, exposed in the trough of the extreme low tide, the mouth of the cave showed black and wet, breathing its slow brackish breath. Inside it lay the *Meridian Star*, cracked open now along her starboard flank where his own charges had done their work, her ribs exposed to the light for the first time in seventy years.

And inside her, stacked in the flooded chamber like the inventory of a life no one had claimed, were the letters.

Not gold. Not bullion. Not the rumored cargo that had made a wealthy man in the north willing to fund a salvage operation with permits stitched together from jurisdictional loopholes and other people's signatures. Letters. Personal effects. A wall of them, he'd seen the footage from the dive cameras—bundles wrapped in oilcloth, tin boxes fused shut with corrosion, a child's shoe. The kind of cargo that had no market value and every kind of value, depending on who you were and what you'd lost.

Reyes had spent thirty years in this business, and he knew the difference between a wreck and a grave. He had always known it. He had simply never before been in a position where knowing it cost him anything.

Behind him, on the aft deck, his crew worked in the tight economical silence of professionals who understood that something had gone wrong with the morning. The dive gear was stowed. The crane was locked down. Nobody had been told anything officially, and everybody knew anyway. Crews always knew. A ship is a small country and its government has no secrets worth keeping.

"Captain." It was Vasquez, his salvage master, a woman who had pulled bodies and bullion out of water from the Java Sea to the Hebrides and who could read a tide chart the way priests read scripture. She came to the rail beside him and did not look at him. That was her courtesy. "The backer's launch is coming out from the harbor. Fifteen minutes, maybe less."

"I know."

"He's brought a lawyer."

"I know that too."

Vasquez was quiet for a moment. The launch was a white smudge against the grey water, running fast, throwing a fan of spray. "You want us to stay on deck for this?"

"I want you to do exactly what I say and nothing else. When I give the word, the *Corvus* weighs anchor and we stand off. Full stand-off. No diver touches the water. No line goes over the side."

"And if he countermands you?"

Reyes watched the launch come on. "He can't. The contract is void. I voided it at oh-six-hundred, in writing, filed with the harbormaster and transmitted to maritime registry before he woke up. As of this morning, the *Corvus* is not salvage vessel of record on this site. We are a survey ship with no permit and no client. We're nobody."

Vasquez finally turned her head. Her face was weathered down to essentials, all bone and salt, and her eyes were the flat grey of deep water. "You voided the richest contract of your career."

"Yes."

"Because of letters."

"Because of falsified provenance documents," Reyes said. "Because the man who hired us presented a chain of title to the *Meridian Star*'s cargo that was forged—badly, arrogantly, the way rich men forge things, assuming no one would ever look. Because the cargo manifest I pulled from the regional registry two nights ago shows that everything in that hold was contraband being smuggled, and the passengers who died around it were victims of the men who owned it. Because one of those victims was a woman named Ingrid Eriksen, and her child is standing on that tidal flat right now with the whole of Salt Hollow behind them, and I have spent six tides trying to dynamite their mother's grave."

He heard his own voice climb and made himself stop. The launch was closer now. He could see the backer standing in the bow—Herrmann, his name was, Aldous Herrmann, of a shipping fortune old enough to have forgotten its own crimes—wearing a deck coat worth more than the *Corvus*'s engine and an expression of patient, proprietary irritation, as though the sea itself were running late for an appointment with him.

"Vasquez," Reyes said, quieter. "When this is over, everyone on this crew gets paid. Full contract value, out of my own share if it comes to that. I'm not making my people pay for my conscience."

"I know," she said. "That's why nobody's arguing with you." She paused. "That, and the letters. I read one of them through the camera feed before you locked the chamber. A man writing to his wife about the crossing. He knew the ship was carrying something wrong. You could tell he knew. He wrote that he'd asked the purser to move his family to the other side." She looked back out at the water. "We don't get to blow that open for a man like Herrmann."

No, Reyes thought. We don't.

* * *

The launch came alongside with the practiced arrogance of money, and Aldous Herrmann came up the pilot ladder without waiting for a hand, which told Reyes everything about how the man had lived his life. He was seventy, maybe older, silver-haired, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had been athletic once and had replaced the habit with authority. His lawyer followed—a narrow, grey, apologetic-looking person carrying a document case the way a soldier carries a sidearm, close and ready.

"Anton." Herrmann's voice was warm. It was always warm. Warmth was a tool he kept oiled. "You'll forgive the early start. I don't like receiving news secondhand, and the harbormaster's office was very forthcoming this morning."

"Then you know where I stand."

"I know you've done something extraordinary," Herrmann said. He walked to the rail and looked down at the exposed cave with the expression of a man surveying property. "Do you know what's down there, Anton? Do you have any idea what the hold of a 1947 coastal freighter, running contraband for three governments, might be worth? Not just the cargo. The provenance. The story. Museums pay for stories. Private collectors pay more. I have buyers lined up who would—"

"The provenance documents you gave me are forged," Reyes said.

The warmth did not leave Herrmann's face, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts over something large passing beneath. "Is that an accusation?"

"It's a fact. I had the chain of title authenticated in the regional registry. The signatures don't match. The dates don't hold. And the cargo manifest from the wreck itself—" he nodded at the cave "—matches the smuggling manifests in the registry archives. Your documents describe a legitimate commercial cargo legally owned by your grandfather's company. The wreck describes contraband. Both cannot be true, and the wreck was there first."

The lawyer had gone very still. Reyes noticed that he had not opened his document case. Interesting, Reyes thought. Even the hired paper knows.

Herrmann turned from the rail. When he spoke again, the warmth was still there, but it had thinned, stretched over something colder, the way ice skins over moving water. "Anton. I've known men like you for forty years. Competent men. Proud men. Men who discover, somewhere in the middle of a job, that they have principles, and who are always astonished to learn how expensive principles are." He smiled. "You have a crew of eleven. You have a vessel on a charter that you personally guaranteed. You have a reputation that I can make or unmake with three phone calls, and you have just voided a contract worth more than you will earn in your lifetime. Walk this back. Void the void. I'll even increase your share. Call it an apology, for both of us."

Reyes looked at him for a long moment. He thought about the number. That was the honest thing to admit—he thought about the number. Thirty years of lean seasons and rusted hulls and clients who paid late and divers who died young, and here was the number, the number that would have ended the lean seasons forever, and it was standing in front of him wearing a deck coat and offering to double itself.

And underneath the number, quieter, the image he could not put down: a wall of oilcloth bundles in a flooded chamber, and a man's handwriting asking the purser to move his family to the other side of the ship.

"I can't," Reyes said. "You should have forged better documents. Or better, you should have told me the truth and let me refuse you honestly. I'd have respected that. What I can't respect is being made a tool, and what I won't do is hand a grave to a collector."

Herrmann studied him the way he might study a survey report with an error in it—looking for the mistake, the place where the numbers could be corrected. Then he sighed, and the warmth left his face entirely, and what remained was not rage but something more efficient.

"All right," Herrmann said. "You understand that there will be consequences."

"I assumed."

"You'll be blacklisted from every registry my family touches. That's most of them. Your certification will face review—I have standing to request it. The charter company that owns the *Corvus* will hear from my lawyers about breach, and they will not defend you, because they cannot afford to." He said it all mildly, the way a man reads a tide table. "You are fifty-eight years old, Anton. You have perhaps ten working years left, and I am going to make sure you spend them watching other men dive."

Behind him, Reyes heard the crew go very still. Vasquez had not moved. The lawyer had opened his document case at last and was not looking inside it.

Reyes found that he was not afraid. That surprised him. He had expected fear the way a man expects cold water, and instead what he felt was something lighter, almost buoyant, as if a ballast he had been carrying for thirty years had finally been cut loose. He had spent his whole career on the right side of the law and the wrong side of the question—taking wrecks that were technically his to take, from seas that had never agreed to give them up. He had told himself for decades that legality was the same as rightness. It had taken a bush pilot with a locked face and a village full of fishermen, standing on a tidal flat between his crane and their dead, to show him the difference.

"I'd like you off my ship," Reyes said. "Both of you. The *Corvus* weighs anchor within the hour."

"You're dismissing me," Herrmann said, "from a ship I'm paying for."

"You're not paying for it anymore. And it was never yours." Reyes gestured toward the ladder. "The tide's coming in, Herrmann. In this bay, that happens faster than men expect."

Herrmann held his gaze for a long moment. Then he smiled—thinly, without any warmth left in it at all—and turned. At the ladder he paused, one hand on the rail, and did not look back.

"Your father salvaged for forty years," he said. "Everyone said he was the best in the fleet. And everyone also said he left money in the water, year after year, because he was too careful, too slow, too scrupulous. I bought his silence once, did you know that? A site in the Sulu Sea. He found what I wanted and he sat on it, and I paid him to forget it, and he took the money and forgot it, and died comfortable." Now he did look back, and his eyes were old and hard and satisfied. "I always wondered which of you was the better man. I suppose today I found out. It cost you more, though. It always costs the better man more."

He went down the ladder. The lawyer followed, and at the top rung the lawyer turned and said, very quietly, "For what it's worth, Captain—the authentication you filed. I would have found the same thing. He never checks whether we check." Then he, too, was gone.

* * *

The launch receded toward the harbor, shrinking to a white fleck, then to a suggestion, then to nothing. Reyes stood at the rail until Vasquez came to stand beside him again.

"Eleven of us," she said. "You just made eleven enemies of the Herrmann shipping interest."

"I just made eleven people who can say they were there when a man walked away from it," Reyes said. "There's a difference. Small. But there."

"And you? What are you now?"

Reyes looked down at the water. The tide had turned; he could see it at the cave mouth, a thin lip of green sliding over the black rock, the sea beginning to take its wreck back. By the next low tide the chamber would be sealed again, and this time no one with a permit would come to open it, because the man with the permits had torn them up himself. The letters would stay where they were, in the dark, in the cold, with the man who had written to his wife about the crossing. That felt right. Some things were cargo. Some things were never cargo at all.

He thought about the pilot—Eriksen—standing on the flats two tides ago, arms at their sides, not moving, not shouting, just present, the way a lighthouse is present, and behind them a village that had every reason to distrust the man with the dynamite and had chosen to stand there anyway. He had faced down pirates in the Sulu Sea and inspectors in three ports and one hurricane with a name, and he had never once been as afraid as he had been looking at that line of people on the sand. Because they had not been defending property. They had been defending each other. He had built a career on the assumption that nobody did that anymore.

"I'm unemployed," Reyes said aloud. "Blacklisted, probably sued, and fifty-eight years old." He turned and found that his crew had gathered at the aft rail—not summoned, just gathered, the way crews do when the shape of the day has changed. "So. Here's what happens now. We weigh anchor. We run the coast survey we were originally chartered for—the honest one, the hydrographic work, the charts. There are eleven harbors on this coast that haven't been properly sounded since the war, and every one of them has a community that could use accurate water. It's not treasure. It pays less than treasure. But nobody's grave is in the way of it."

He looked at their faces. Skepticism, mostly, and underneath it something else, something he recognized because he'd seen it that morning on a tidal flat: people deciding, together, what they were willing to stand for.

"Anyone who wants off at the next port gets a full payout and my recommendation, and I'll mean the recommendation. Anyone who stays—" He shrugged. "Anyone who stays gets to chart a coast that nobody's bothered to chart, and I can't promise it will be exciting. Mostly it will be soundings. Thousands of them."

Vasquez was the first to move. She reached past him and pulled the anchor release lever herself, and the *Corvus* shuddered as the chain ran out and the old ship came free of the bay.

"Soundings," she said, in the tone of a woman agreeing to a long and slightly boring marriage. "God help us all."

There was laughter, brief and surprised, the kind that comes after a storm. Reyes took the wheel himself and brought the *Corvus* around, slow and heavy, her bow swinging toward the open coast and the washed morning light.

As the cave mouth slid astern, he saw them on the flats—Eriksen, the old woman Marta, the doctor, the magistrate, a scatter of lantern-lit figures from the night meeting still not quite gone home, standing in the early sun beside a wreck that was, as of this morning, nobody's treasure and everybody's grave. One of them raised an arm. He couldn't tell who. He raised his own in answer, and held it up longer than he needed to, and then the point of land took them all away.

He had spent six tides trying to take something from this coast. It had taken one morning for the coast to give him something instead—a thing he hadn't known he was missing, a thing with no market value at all.

The *Corvus* ran north into the light, and behind her the sixth tide came in over the cave, soft and complete, closing the sea's hand gently around what it had finally been allowed to keep.
