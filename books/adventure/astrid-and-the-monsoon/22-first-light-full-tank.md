## 22. First Light, Full Tank

The hangar smelled of kerosene and salt and, faintly, of bread.

Orsen noticed the bread first. It was impossible not to—Marta's loaves had a presence about them, a warm yeast-and-woodsmoke authority that announced itself before the eyes confirmed it. Two of them sat wrapped in cloth on the workbench beside the oil can, with a note in her looping hand: *For the run. Don't argue. The Kestrels' boy has a fever coming on and Mrs. Aldous will want her paper.* Below that, smaller: *You look better when you eat.*

Three weeks ago, Orsen would have read that last line as surveillance. Kindness with a hook in it. Now they read it the way you'd read weather off the water—a fact of the coast, neither threat nor miracle, just the shape of things.

They pulled the chocks from the Cessna's wheels and ran their palm along the strut, feeling for ice damage that wasn't there anymore. The engine had been rebuilt after the monsoon flight—the icing event that had nearly ended everything over the ridge line—and the mechanic at Tern Bay had done honest work, though he'd charged like a man who knew his customer had no choice. The propeller caught the first light through the hangar doors and threw it back in a slow silver arc when Orsen tested the blades by hand.

Clear post-monsoon light. That was the thing people didn't tell you about the season's ending: how clean the world became afterward, scoured and rinsed, every color turned up a notch, as if the storm had been polishing the coast all along instead of trying to break it.

On the passenger seat, someone had left a stethoscope.

Not Priya's good one—that lived around her neck like a talisman, dented chestpiece and all. This was the spare, the one she kept in the clinic drawer for emergencies, and Orsen knew exactly what its presence meant because she'd told them the night before, deadpan, while stitching a gash on old Halvard's forearm: *"Kestrel boy's fever. If I'm not back from the Aldous farm by dawn, you're my courier. Take it to Marta so she can listen to his lungs. And Orsen—"*

*"What."*

*"Fly like a person, not like a ghost."*

Orsen set the stethoscope carefully in the seat's pocket, where it wouldn't slide, and found themselves smiling at it. A ridiculous little object. A tube and two metal cups. And yet it sat there like a promise, or a dare, or both.

* * *

The preflight was ritual, and ritual was the one language Orsen had never stopped speaking fluently. Fuel sample drained and held to the light—clear, no water, no sediment. Control surfaces moved through their arcs, cables singing their familiar small songs. Pitot heat checked even though there wasn't a cloud within fifty miles; habits were what kept you alive on the days the sky changed its mind. Tire pressure. Oil. The tie-downs coiled and hung on their pegs.

But this morning the ritual kept snagging on new things.

The cargo hold, for instance. It used to be anonymous weight—crates, cans, envelopes sealed against curiosity. Now Orsen loaded it like a person carrying names. Marta's bread, wrapped twice against the damp. Mrs. Aldous's newspaper, three days stale but treasured anyway, because out here news arrived however it could. Antibiotics for the Kestrel child, packed by Priya's own hands with dosing instructions written in her tidy, impatient script. A parcel of letters, bound in twine—copies, certified by the magistrate, destined for descendants in three different settlements whose grandparents had sailed on the Meridian Star and never been believed.

That last bundle was the heaviest thing aboard, and it weighed almost nothing.

Orsen stood at the open hold door a moment longer than the checklist required. Three weeks since the sixth tide. Since Reyes had torn up his contract on the flats with the whole coast watching, citing falsified provenance papers, walking away from the richest find of his career with his jaw set like a man swallowing glass. Since the memorial dedication, when the effects of the dead had gone home one by one—watches, hair combs, a child's tin whistle that made an old woman in Gullskerry weep into her sleeve—and then, last of all, Ingrid Eriksen's letter, placed formally into Orsen's hands before two hundred witnesses, read aloud by Marta in a voice that cracked only once.

*She stayed behind to shield a lover accused in the smuggling ring. She fled to protect her child from the fallout. Her disappearance was sacrifice, not abandonment.*

Orsen had known those words by heart before Marta finished reading them. Had read them forty times in the cave light, in the hangar, in bed at three in the morning with Priya asleep beside them. But hearing them in Marta's voice, in front of everyone—*everyone* knowing, everyone seeing Orsen stand there with wet eyes and not look away—that had been the moment the ground shifted under thirty years of careful footing.

And the coast hadn't punished them for it. That was the part Orsen still couldn't quite make peace with. They had spent a lifetime believing that being known meant being cornered, that every secret surrendered was a knife handed to whoever held it next. Instead, the villages had simply... adjusted. Made room. Started saying their name differently—not *the pilot*, not *that Eriksen*, but *Orsen*, plain and warm, the way you'd say the name of a lighthouse or a harbor. Like it belonged there.

Like it had always belonged there, waiting.

* * *

The radio crackled to life as Orsen settled into the cockpit and ran through the startup checks.

*"Salt Hollow to Meridian One, Salt Hollow to Meridian One, you up yet, flyboy?"*

It was Marta, on the village net, using the call sign the fishermen had invented for the plane weeks ago and refused to retire. Orsen keyed the mic.

"Meridian One, reading you loud and clear."

*"Weather's clean as a whistle to the north. Kestrel boy slept some. Priya says lungs are clear if you get that scope to me today."*

"Scope's aboard. Bread's aboard. Tell Mrs. Aldous her paper's coming."

A pause on the net, filled with the particular static of a woman deciding whether to say something. Then: *"Your mother would've liked this morning."*

"Marta—"

*"I'm just saying. Ingrid flew too, you know. Before your time. Little floatplane out of Gullskerry, mail runs mostly. Nobody remembers that anymore except me."*

Orsen stared at the instrument panel. Thirty years of believing their mother had been a passenger, a victim, a ghost—and here was Marta casually handing them one more piece of her, one more ordinary Tuesday of a life: *she flew too*. Mail runs. Probably complained about the same fuel prices. Probably did the same walk-around, touched the same struts.

"She never told me," Orsen said finally. "Any of it."

*"People carry what they carry," Marta said. "Doesn't mean they weren't headed somewhere good. Go on now, before the tide turns and I have to listen to you complain about it."*

Orsen laughed—an actual laugh, unguarded, loud enough to startle a gull off the hangar roof—and keyed off. The whole coast was awake on the net behind them: Gullskerry reporting swells down to a meter, Tern Bay asking after diesel filters, someone's kid reciting the tide table wrong and getting corrected by three voices at once. For years Orsen had listened to that chatter like a man outside a lit window. This morning they were inside it. Their name—*their actual name*—was already moving along the frequency like weather, like tide, like something the coast simply had.

* * *

Engine start. The Cessna coughed, caught, settled into its idle with the contented rattle of a machine that knew its job. Oil pressure green. Volts green. Orsen taxied out onto the strip—the long gravel flat above the tidal meadow, still dark-wet with dew—and paused at the threshold the way they always did, running the final mental check.

But this time, at the pause, they let themselves look.

Behind the strip: the hills, rinsed green, streaming mist off their shoulders. Ahead: the bay, lying calm and enormous, holding the cave mouth somewhere beneath it like a closed eye. The memorial buoy floated over it now, painted white and red by the schoolchildren, visible from the air as a small stubborn dot. Every pilot on this coast would know what it marked from now on. Every diver. Every fisherman who worked these waters. The Meridian Star would never be a legend again—it would be a grave, a story, a fact, tended by the living.

And beyond the bay, the whole scattered coastline of Orsen's route: Salt Hollow, Gullskerry, the Aldous farm, the Kestrel homestead, Tern Bay, all the small stubborn lights of human habitation strung along the edge of the sea like beads on a wire. Medicine and gossip. Bread and newspapers. A stethoscope riding shotgun.

It used to be that takeoff felt like escape. Wheels leaving earth, the coast shrinking below, the world reduced to instruments and horizon—Orsen had loved that moment precisely because nothing could follow them into it. Up there, they were no one's child, no one's suspect, no one's grief. Just a machine and a sky and a rule: *land, deliver, leave, remember nothing.*

Orsen advanced the throttle.

The engine climbed through its notes, the tail came up, the gravel rattled away beneath the wheels, and then—the old sweet unweighting, the earth letting go—and the bay swung into view through the windscreen, wide and shining, the memorial buoy a white fleck on blue-green water, and Orsen banked gently south toward the first stop and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that nothing about the flying had changed at all.

Only the direction of it.

Every departure was still a departure. But now each one bent back on itself, a curve drawn toward home no matter which way the compass pointed. Land, deliver, stay a while. Remember everything. Come back to the hangar that smelled of bread, to the radio that said their name, to the woman with the spare stethoscope who had dared them to fly like a person.

The coast unrolled below, post-monsoon bright, every inlet and skerry washed clean. Orsen leveled off at a thousand feet, trimmed for cruise, and reached over to touch the stethoscope in its pocket once—lightly, the way you'd touch a charm—before settling both hands on the yoke.

Ahead, the morning opened like a page.

And far to the south, barely visible at the edge of sight, a single boat was working its way up the coastline—too far off to make out her name, too slow to be fishing this early, riding a wake that pointed straight toward Salt Hollow.

Orsen watched it for a long moment, thumb resting on the radio.

Then the Kestrel boy's antibiotics needed delivering, and the tide table needed correcting, and the day, mercifully and entirely, belonged to the living.
