## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Air

The trick to breathing borrowed air, Mirabel's father used to say, is to never forget whose air it is. Every breath you take below the waterline belongs to someone, and the moment you believe otherwise, that's the moment you drown.

Mirabel thought about this with great affection and greater resentment, forty feet down in the green dark off Gullwrack Harbor, chipping copper sheathing off a derelict that had been dead longer than they'd been alive. The air in the tanks was syndicate air. Bought on credit. The lantern hanging from the dive line swayed with the current, throwing wobbling light across the hull like a drunk reading a letter.

*Fourteen pounds of copper to go,* Mirabel calculated, working the pry bar into a seam. *At harbor rates, that's maybe six crowns. Six crowns against a debt of two hundred and eleven.*

The arithmetic was insulting. It had been insulting for three years.

They worked anyway, because the alternative to working was thinking, and the alternative to thinking was remembering, and Mirabel had built an entire life on the sturdy foundation of not doing either. Chip. Pry. Breathe. The derelict groaned under their hands like an old man being turned in bed, and somewhere above, gulls argued over something dead.

That was when the bell rang.

Not the harbor bell — the syndicate's bell, the one mounted on the *Charity's Interest*, Brannock Hale's collection barge, rung twice for "inspection" and three times for "you're done running." It rang twice. Then, after a pause long enough to be insulting on its own terms, it rang three times.

Mirabel went very still in the water, which is a skill divers develop and poets do not understand. Below, everything is patience. Below, you can wait out almost anything except your own lungs.

Almost anything.

The inspection skiff would be lowering its grapple lines by now. Standard procedure: hook the wreck, post a watcher at the surface, let the diver finish so the syndicate could take its percentage without the bother of chasing anyone. Hale's men were not cruel, exactly. They were worse than cruel — they were *thorough*, and thoroughness has no moods to play upon.

But there was a storm coming. Mirabel had felt it all morning in the water, a low grumble of pressure, the fish gone quiet, the light going the color of old pewter. Storms made Hale's people cautious and careless in equal measure: cautious about their boats, careless about their divers. A diver caught out in open water during a squall was, officially, the diver's own problem.

Unofficially, it was an opportunity.

Mirabel slung the copper bag across their back, unclipped from the wreck, and did not swim up toward the inspection skiff. They swam sideways, along the seabed shelf, into the tangle of old pilings north of the harbor mouth where the syndicate's charts went vague and their interest went vaguer. It was against every rule Mirabel had. Rule one: be nowhere when looked for. Rule two: owe nothing you can't pay in copper. Rule three, learned at fourteen and never once broken since: *never be seen.*

The storm found them before the surface did.

It came down in sheets, turning the world above into a gray roar, and Mirabel surfaced in the lee of a broken breakwater with rain hammering the sea flat and their borrowed air spent down to fumes. They hauled themselves onto the stones, coughing, alive, invisible — and then heard, over the wind, a sound that did not belong to the storm.

A voice. Thin, cracked, calling words that the wind kept stealing.

Old Tam's skiff was wedged in the rocks like a walnut someone had tried to crack and given up on. The prospector himself was wedged more thoroughly still, half in the boat and half out of it, one hand pressed to his side where a wound had soaked his coat black. He was perhaps seventy, perhaps ninety; prospectors age like driftwood, all weather and no calendar. His eyes found Mirabel the moment they clambered aboard, and he laughed — actually laughed, a wet, delighted sound.

"There you are," he said. "Knew you'd come."

"You knew nothing of the kind," Mirabel said, already tearing strips from the man's spare shirt, already pressing them to the wound because hands know what to do before pride can argue. "I'm nobody. I was sheltering."

"Nobody." Tam's grin showed gaps like a fence missing pickets. "Nobody swims the pilings in a storm with a bag of stolen copper on their back. Nobody dives derelicts alone without a tender. Nobody—" he coughed, and the cough had blood in it, and the grin faltered just slightly before recovering, "—nobody holds their breath like they're apologizing to the water. I've watched you three weeks, girl. Boy. Whoever you are today."

"Today I'm leaving," Mirabel said, but they didn't leave. The rain was a wall now, the harbor a rumor, and the old man's grip on their wrist was stronger than a dying man's had any right to be.

"Provision," Tam said.

The word landed like a dropped anchor. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

Every child on the coast grew up on the *Provision*. Lost thirty years ago with all hands, twenty-two souls, somewhere past the Teeth reef. A treasure story, a ghost story, a cautionary tale told to keep young divers off the far currents. Salvage rights had been fought over, lapsed, forgotten. The sea had kept her, and everyone had agreed to let her keep her dead.

"What about it," Mirabel said carefully.

"I found her." Tam's eyes were fever-bright and absolutely steady, which is a combination Mirabel would learn to dread and trust in equal measure. "Past the Teeth, in the deep channel where the charts lie. Three months I've sat on it. Three months I couldn't go down myself — too old, too slow, and my heart's a coward." He patted his coat, and paper crackled. "Map's here. Bearings, depths, the safe window through the Teeth. Everything."

"I don't want it."

"Course you don't. You want to be nobody, and nobodies don't want things." He pulled Mirabel closer, and his breath was iron and salt. "Here's what I want, and mind you listen, because I've only got so many words left in me and I intend to spend them well. When I was younger — younger than you — I dove her first. Before the syndicate ever came sniffing. And I took something off her. Not gold. Not plate. Papers. Identity papers, letters of record, the names of every soul aboard. Sold them to a broker in Saltmarsh who paid better than gold, because dead men with names are worth more than dead men without them, if you're the sort who profits by erasure."

Mirabel went cold in a way the storm couldn't account for. Erasure. They knew about erasure. Their father's name had been scraped off the rolls of the Archaeological Society like barnacles off a hull, and Mirabel had spent a lifetime learning how much easier it was to sink when no one was watching.

"I sold the dead," Tam said simply. "Twenty-two names, and I sold them, and I've been rich and haunted ever since. Now the papers have come back around — brokers die, ledgers circulate — and I bought them back at a price that ate everything I had. And I'm going to die tonight with them in my coat unless—"

"Unless," Mirabel said.

"Unless you take them back to her. Give the dead their names. That's the price of the map. Not coin. A promise." His hand tightened. "And I'll tell you the worst part, the part that should make you sail away fast as your little crew can row. The syndicate knows she's been found. Hale's fleet outfits for the Teeth even now — I saw the manifests when I was still able to walk into places I shouldn't. You've got six tides, maybe. After that, whatever's on that wreck belongs to Brannock Hale, and the names of twenty-two drowned folk get erased a second time, permanent, this time with lawyers."

Lightning walked across the sky. In its glare, Tam looked small and finished, a candle burned to the wick.

"Why me?" Mirabel asked. "You've known me three weeks. You know nothing about me except that I steal copper and hold my breath apologetically."

"That's exactly why," Tam said, and laughed again, softer. "Because you want nothing, and a person who wants nothing can't be bought. Because you're the better digger — oh, don't look at me like that, I knew your father, we drank together in better years, and he always said his child could read a wreck like scripture." He pressed the oilskin packet into Mirabel's hands, folding their fingers closed over it with both of his own. "Take it. Take the promise. And when you stand on that deck and give those poor souls back their names, tell them Tam Voss sent his apologies, thirty years late."

His hands slipped away. The rain filled the silence where his voice had been, and Mirabel knelt in a stranger's blood in a stranger's boat, holding a map to a grave, while somewhere beyond the storm the syndicate's fleet lay at anchor, counting down tides they didn't yet know were counting.

Rule three said: *never be seen.* Never take sides, never take chances, never take promises from dying men.

Mirabel looked down at the oilskin packet, warm from a dead man's hands, and heard themselves say — quietly, hopelessly, already committed —

"I'm going to need a crew."
