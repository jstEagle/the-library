## 2. Six Tides

Mirabel buried Old Tam at dawn, in the shingle above the tideline where Gullwrack's dead who couldn't afford the churchyard went. No marker. A pile of gray stones, a bottle of the cheap plum brandy from Tam's own kit poured into the gravel, and a promise that was already starting to feel like a millstone.

*Return what was stolen from the dead.*

The map sat against Mirabel's ribs, wrapped in oilskin, warm from their own body heat. They had spent the night with it spread on the floor of their rented bunkroom, holding a candle low and their pride lower, and the truth was simple: Mirabel Sandoval could read a wreck like a love letter, but this map was written in a cipher of tides and bearings that made no sense alone. *Six tides*, Tam had rasped, before the blood finished its argument. *Six tides before the Provision's window closes, and six before the syndicate's fleet sails with the same rumor I sold them.*

Because Tam had sold them the rumor too. A dying man hedging his bets. Mirabel couldn't even be angry about it.

What Mirabel needed was a navigator. What Mirabel had was eleven coins, a debt, and a face the harbor syndicate's enforcers were starting to remember.

* * *

Vess Quill was drinking breakfast at the Drowned Lantern when Mirabel found her, which was fitting, because Vess Quill had been drinking breakfast, lunch, and supper in that order for three years — ever since she'd run the *Kindly Fortune* onto the Teeth of Marrow and drowned nine sailors in the dark.

"They say it was fog," Mirabel said, taking the stool beside her. "Charts said clear."

Vess didn't look over. Her eyes were the color of a harbor in January. "Charts lie. I know. I wrote some of them."

"I need someone who can read a map that's half cipher and half apology."

"And I need to not be having this conversation." She turned her glass. "Go away, salvage rat."

"The Provision," Mirabel said, and watched the glass stop turning.

Silence stretched. Around them the Lantern clinked and lied to itself, morning drinkers rehearsing their alibis.

"Everyone knows the Provision," Vess said at last, quietly. "Went down off the Serpent's Teeth with a full hold and a full crew, and the insurance was paid before the bodies were cold. My grandmother crewed on a sister ship. She used to say the Provision was cursed." Her laugh was dry as old rope. "Not cursed. Just *erased*. You know what that does to a navigator? Knowing the sea keeps honest records and men don't?"

"I'm beginning to."

Vess looked at Mirabel then, properly, and Mirabel watched her do the arithmetic — the debt, the fleet, the odds. "I'll read your map," she said. "But the price is this: when it's done, whatever we find out about who sold those sailors to the deep, it goes in the open. No burying it to keep us safe. I spent three years buried. I'm done being comfortable."

Mirabel thought of their own rule — *stay invisible, stay alive* — and felt it crack down the middle like river ice.

"Done," they said, and paid her three coins, and hated how light the purse felt after.

* * *

The map made more sense with Vess's fingers on it, that night, in the bunkroom. She read it the way other people read handwriting — fast, frowning, muttering.

"Tam was a tide-mathematician. Look — these aren't depths, they're *hours*. He's charted the Provision by the moon's pull. The wreck only opens at slack water on the sixth tide. Before that, the currents around the Teeth will chew a hull to splinters." She tapped the parchment. "He's given us the door and the schedule."

"And the syndicate?"

"Sailed this morning, or close to it. But they'll take the wide channel, the safe route. They think they have days." She glanced up. "We take the Narrows. We have hours."

* * *

The rigging-master Mirabel needed was deaf, which was why the syndicate had let her go, and why Mirabel wanted her.

Corrin Brask worked the high spars of the harbor cranes, hauling salvage cable in weather that sent other riggers home. She read rope and wind and weight through her hands and the soles of her feet, and when Mirabel found her at the top of Crane Four, she looked down at them with an expression that said, in no language at all, *this had better be worth the climb down.*

Mirabel shouted up. Corrin cupped her ear, theatrically, and Mirabel gave up and climbed.

At the top, wind screaming, the whole gray harbor turning below, Mirabel pulled out the last of their coins — five — and held them up. Then mimed, badly, a ship, a storm, a dive.

Corrin watched the whole pantomime with the flat patience of someone watching a child drown a kitten. Then she held out one hand: *pay*. Mirabel put the coins in it. She weighed them, bit one, and then — with her free hand — pointed at Mirabel, then at her own eyes, then made a slow circle over the water.

*Watch me work. That's the price. Don't look away.*

Mirabel nodded. Corrin grinned, sudden and white, and went down the crane cable hand over hand like it was a staircase, and Mirabel understood they had just spent their entire fortune on the best rigger in Gullwrack Harbor.

* * *

The cook was the hardest, because the cook was fourteen.

Pim Onda had stowed away in Mirabel's skiff two nights ago, curled among the salvage tarps, and had refused with tremendous dignity to explain the bruise on their cheek or the name of the ship they'd run from. They could, however, make a stew out of kelp, salt fish, and despair that tasted like someone's grandmother loved them.

"You can't come," Mirabel said, for the fourth time. "It's a wreck dive. It's dangerous."

"So's your cooking." Pim stirred the pot with the calm of a cardinal. "You'd all starve or poison yourselves by the third tide. I'm not asking to join. I'm informing you."

"Pim—"

"I know what the Provision is." The spoon stopped. Pim's chin came up, and for a second the fourteen-year-old bravado fell away and something older and sadder looked out. "The cook on it was named Aldous Ferrow. My gran's uncle. There's a plaque in the Seamen's Chapel with no names on it — just *the crew of the Provision, remembered*. Remembered *how*, if nobody knows who they were?" The spoon resumed. "I'm coming. And you're having seconds."

Mirabel looked at Vess, who was pretending to study the map. Vess looked at Corrin, who was signing something that Mirabel was fairly sure meant *feed the child, you coward.*

Mirabel sat down and had seconds. It tasted like a promise.

* * *

On the fourth tide, Mirabel told them the secret. It came out over the chart table, in the small hours, because Vess had asked the price of trust be paid in kind, and Mirabel's father's name sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

"Sandoval," Vess repeated. "The Sandoval. The one who—"

"Who found the Provision's papers in a syndicate strongroom twenty years ago and was ruined for saying so. They called him a forger. Called him a thief. He drank himself into the harbor by the time I was twelve, and I've spent my whole life since making sure nobody connects the name." Mirabel turned the map. "Tam's plea isn't just about the dead. It's about everyone the syndicate erased. Him. My father. Me, if I let them."

Corrin, who had been reading lips across the lamplight the whole time, reached over and put one rope-hardened hand flat on the table. *Enough. We sail.*

Pim, who had been awake the entire time in the corner, pretending to sleep, said nothing at all. But in the morning there was a fourth bowl set out at breakfast without being asked, and Mirabel understood that the crew had counted them in.

* * *

They slipped the Narrows at slack water on the fifth tide, in a borrowed ketch with a patched sail and a name scraped off her stern — the *Magpie*, because Vess said a ship with no name needed a bird's at least. Behind them, far out in the wide channel, the syndicate's fleet was a smudge of smoke on the horizon, three hulls' worth of money and muscle, confident and slow.

"Sixth tide opens the wreck at dawn," Vess said, eyes on the Teeth of Marrow rising black ahead. "The fleet reaches the outer reef by mid-morning. We have hours, not days."

"And if the currents don't open for us?"

"Then the sea keeps its schedule and we keep our promise, and both of us are crueler than the syndicate." She glanced at Mirabel. "There's something else. On the map — the cipher I couldn't break until last night. Tam didn't just chart the door. He charted what's *inside* it. And Mirabel — there's no gold marked. Not one coin. Only a strongbox, and a word."

Mirabel's mouth had gone dry. "What word?"

"*Names*," Vess said. "He wrote *names*."

Ahead, the Teeth of Marrow parted around them like a jaw, and the sixth tide began to turn.
