## 3. What the Wreck Keeps

**MIRABEL**

The reef announced itself the way grief does—suddenly, and all at once, from every direction.

One moment the *Kestrel's Rest* was sliding over water the color of bottle glass, and the next the whole sea stood up in white teeth around us. Vess had called it an hour before, standing at the wheel with her ruined charts and her ruined reputation and that look she got when the world agreed with her.

"Reef break, two points off the bow," she'd said. "The Provision's under it. If Tam's map is honest."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we've sailed three days for a very scenic drowning." She'd almost smiled. Vess's smiles were like coins at the bottom of a harbor—rare, and you had to dive for them.

We beat the fleet. That was the miracle nobody said out loud, because saying it felt like tempting the sea to take it back. Six tides, and we'd spent five of them running dark, sails patched with cookpot copper, Juniper feeding us salt cod and optimism in roughly equal measure. Old Hollis—deaf as a barnacle and twice as stubborn—had rigged us through two squalls by feel alone, reading the shudder of the lines through his bare feet on the deck.

Now the reef curled around us like a cupped hand, and beneath it, forty feet down in the green gloom, I saw it.

The *Provision*.

She lay on her side like a sleeping animal, masts snapped, hull furred with coral, her name still legible along the stern in flaking gilt. My chest did something complicated. Tam had described her to me with his last breath—*she was a mail ship, girl, not a treasure ship, whatever the syndicate thinks*—and here she was, exactly as promised, holding her dead the way wrecks do: patiently, and forever if nobody came.

"Current's wrong," Hollis signed to me, his hands quick in the gray morning light. He couldn't hear the reef roaring, but he could read it better than any of us. "Water runs through there like a river. Dive now, you come back in pieces or not at all."

"So we wait," Juniper said, hugging the galley pot to his chest like a talisman.

"We wait," I agreed, "and we watch the channel."

Which is when Vess, glass raised, said quietly: "We're not the only ones who found her early."

There—a longboat, tucked into the lee of the reef's far arm, painted harbor-gray, flying no colors at all. No colors meant someone who didn't want to be asked questions. Someone was already down there, chipping at my wreck.

At *Tam's* wreck. At the promise I'd made a dying man with blood on his lips.

"Mirabel," Vess said carefully, "whatever's down there—it isn't worth dying for. You told us that yourself when you hired us. Salvage first, sentiment never."

She was right. I'd said exactly that. I'd even believed it.

I started rigging the dive line anyway.

* * *

**VESS**

Here is what they don't put in navigation manuals: you can chart a lie perfectly. Every bearing true, every current accounted for, and the whole thing still rotten at the center, because the person who drew it wanted something other than what they wrote.

I knew about lying with maps. I'd done it professionally, once, for men who paid better than the harbor office and forgave less. It cost me my certification, my berth, and eleven years of believing I'd never trust another chart—or another captain—again.

And then Mirabel Sandoval had shown up at my table in the Drowned Rat with Tam's map spread flat, and instead of asking *can you get us there*, they'd asked, *what did the mapmaker want?*

Nobody asks that. Captains ask where and how fast. Mirabel had looked at the faded ink and seen a man's intentions, and I had thought, unwisely, dangerously: *oh no.*

So now I watched them coil the dive line with shaking hands, about to slip into a current that would fold them in half against the coral, all because a stranger's promise weighed more to them than their own skin. And I watched the gray longboat, and I did arithmetic.

Three divers from the longboat already down. The syndicate fleet maybe four hours out—we could see their smoke past the point, a smudge like a dirty thumbprint on the horizon. The current would slacken at slack tide, one window, perhaps twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes to beat three armed strangers to whatever the Provision kept, and then outrun a salvage fleet.

"You know this ends badly," I told Mirabel.

"Most things do," they said, settling the mask over their face. "Doesn't mean you skip the middle part."

"That's not—" I exhaled. "That's not how endings work, Mirabel."

But they were already at the rail, and Juniper was gripping their sleeve, and Hollis was signing something fast and fierce that I translated aloud because somebody had to: *"He says come back up or he's coming down after you, and he sinks like an anvil."*

Mirabel grinned—that crooked, infuriating grin—and went over the side without a splash, which should have been impossible for a person carrying that much stubbornness.

I turned to the wheel and did the only useful thing left. I plotted our escape before we needed one, because I have learned that much about endings: you can't choose whether they come. Only whether you've left yourself a door.

* * *

**MIRABEL**

Down was quiet. Down was always quiet—the syndicate could keep its ledgers; give me the ledger of the sea, where everything owed is written in silt and nothing compounds.

The current grabbed me at the mast line and shook me like a dog shakes a rat, but Tam had drawn the slack-water path on his map in a diver's careful hand, and I rode it, hand over hand along the fallen mainyard, into the shadow of the Provision's broken stern.

The rival divers had been busy. They'd torn open the captain's cabin like gulls on a fish carcass—chests split, floorboards pried, silver candlesticks already bagged and drifting on their tether lines above. Treasure. Of course. The syndicate's smoke on the horizon and these three in the water ahead of it: everyone in the world certain the Provision was a purse waiting to be cut.

I swam through the cabin's dead mouth and found what Tam had tried to tell me about.

A strongbox, bolted under the bunk frame, so ordinary the looters had stepped over it a dozen times. Inside—not coin. Not plate. Papers. Hundreds of them, oil-wrapped, sealed in wax by some quartermaster who'd understood, even dying, that paper outlasts flesh.

Crew manifests. Identity papers. Letters of passage. Forty-one names, each one written out in full, each one belonging to a sailor the harbor court had recorded as *lost, identity unverified*—which is court language for *worth no compensation, mourned by no record.* The syndicate hadn't just let the Provision sink. They'd sold the crew's names first, erased them on paper so the insurance claim would come clean, and Tam—young Tam, deckhand Tam, the only man who crawled onto the reef alive—had stolen the box back and spent fifty years running from the men who sold forty-one souls for salvage rights.

I held the box against my chest and the current pulled and somewhere above me the gray longboat's divers noticed their mistake too late, and I thought: *this is the treasure. This worthless, priceless paper. This is worth more than my invisibility, more than my debt, more than—*

The first knife caught my shoulder line, not my shoulder, which meant the diver behind me wanted me alive, or wanted me slow.

* * *

**VESS**

They surfaced fighting.

I saw it from the wheel—Mirabel breaking the water inside the reef pool with the strongbox strapped to their back and a harbor-gray diver on them like a burr, and then the second one rising over the rail of the longboat with a gaff hook, and Juniper screaming and hurling a cookpot with genuinely impressive accuracy, and Hollis already moving, deaf to all of it, reading the chaos through the deck and cutting the longboat's anchor line with two strokes of his knife because Hollis had decided, in his wordless way, whose side he was on.

The longboat swung loose. Its crew scrambled. And past the point, the syndicate's smoke thickened into sails.

"Vess!" Mirabel, hauling themselves aboard, bleeding, laughing—*laughing*—"the window! Slack tide's closing!"

"I know what slack tide does, I'm the navigator!"

"Then navigate!"

So I did. We came about through the reef gap with the Provision's coral crown scraping our keel and the strongbox dripping on our deck and the syndicate's lead ship standing in past the point with her salvage crane already swinging free, her rails lined with enforcers, and at her bow a figure I recognized from bad years—Captain Ede Rask, who signs his erasures with a smile.

Rask's voice carried across the water, pleasant as a shopkeeper's. "Sandoval! Hand up the box and walk away owing nothing! Your debt dies today!"

And there it was. Everything I'd watched Mirabel refuse to want, offered on a plate. Freedom, bought with forty-one names handed back to the man who'd erased them.

Mirabel stood at the rail, blood thinning in the spray, and looked at the box, and looked at Rask, and I saw them arrive at the choice the way you arrive at a reef—suddenly, and all at once.

"No," Mirabel called back. "These names are going to the harbor court. All forty-one. And yours is going in the file with them."

Rask's smile went away. The crane began to swing.

"Hollis," Mirabel said, "hard turn. Vess—get us out."

"I'm already getting us out," I said, and put the Kestrel's Rest straight at the one channel Rask's fat-hulled crane ship could never follow, and prayed to every chart I'd ever betrayed that I still remembered how to be worth trusting.

Behind us, the first harpoon line whistled past the mast.

Ahead, the channel narrowed, and narrowed, and I had not yet told anyone that I'd never actually sailed it.
