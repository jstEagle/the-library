## 4. Names Returned to the Sea

The harbor court smelled of old rope and older lies, and Mirabel Sandoval had spent a lifetime avoiding rooms that smelled like both.

They stood now in the gallery of the Gullwrack Admiralty, damp from the morning tide, watching Captain Ede Varrow of the salvage fleet sit with the easy posture of a man who had never once been caught. His coat was pressed. His boots gleamed. Behind him, two syndicate lawyers arranged their papers like men setting a table for a meal they expected to be served.

Mirabel's own coat was salt-stiff at the cuffs. Their hands still bore the white creases of six tides' diving. They had not slept properly in three days. And in the satchel at their feet sat the only thing in the room worth anything: a oilcloth packet of papers, thirty-one names written in fading ink, recovered from the captain's strongbox aboard the Provision.

Vess sat beside them, straight-backed as a mast. The disgraced navigator had argued all the way up the harbor steps that this was a mistake. *Courts take and give nothing back,* she'd said. But she'd come anyway. That was the thing about crews—they showed up even when they thought you were a fool.

The hearing was about insurance fraud, technically. The Provision had gone down eleven years ago with all hands, and the syndicate had collected on her loss—twice, it turned out, once under her own name and once under a ghost ship invented from her spare parts. For that trick to work, the dead crew had to stay dead on paper. Their identity papers—their proof of ever having existed—had been pulled from the wreck by a young prospector named Tam Wicker, sold to the syndicate's captain, and used to erase thirty-one people so thoroughly that their families stopped receiving letters, their wages stopped being owed, and their names stopped appearing anywhere at all.

Tam had spent the rest of his life trying to buy them back. He'd run out of time in a leaking skoff during a storm, bleeding from a wound he never explained, trading his map to a stranger for a promise.

*I'm sorry,* Mirabel had told him. *I can't promise you'll be remembered.*

*Don't remember me,* Tam had said, gripping their wrist with a strength that surprised them both. *Remember them.*

So here they were. Being remembered at, in front of a magistrate with a voice like gravel in a bucket.

"State your name and trade," said the clerk.

"Mirabel Sandoval. Salvage."

The word landed in the room like a dropped plate. Salvage. Not archaeologist—not the respectable title their father had worn into disgrace, and then out of it, and then into an early grave. Just salvage. The harbor syndicate's lawyers smiled at each other like men watching weather come in that would ruin someone else's picnic.

"And how did you come to possess documents belonging to the estate of the vessel Provision?"

"I dove for them." Mirabel kept their voice flat. "From the wreck. Which is where they belonged, because that's where the dead left them."

A murmur went through the gallery. The magistrate rapped his gavel without much conviction—he seemed, Mirabel thought, like a man who enjoyed murmurs.

Captain Varrow's lead lawyer rose. "The court should note that these documents were stolen property, taken from my client's strongbox aboard the wreck, which my client holds salvage rights to."

"They weren't in your client's strongbox," Mirabel said. "They were in *my* hand, forty feet down, in a compartment behind the galley, sealed in wax. Your client's divers have been picking that wreck for a year and never found them, because your client wasn't looking for papers. He was looking for coin. The papers were worthless to him."

"Worthless," the lawyer repeated, delicately. "And yet you risked your life for them."

"Not for them. For what they are." Mirabel looked at the magistrate. "Thirty-one people. The court has their death registered. It doesn't have their lives. These do."

Silence, of the particular quality that happens in courtrooms when someone says something true and everyone wishes they hadn't.

The magistrate turned over one of the pages with a forefinger, careful as a man handling a bird. He read for a long moment. Then he looked up over his spectacles.

"These names. Do you know them?"

"No," Mirabel said. "But I know a man who did. Tam Wicker. Prospector. He took these papers off the wreck eleven years ago and spent every coin he earned afterward trying to undo what he'd done. He died before he could. I made him a promise."

"You understand," the magistrate said slowly, "that returning these could reopen wage claims. Pension claims. There are families in this harbor who were told their kin drowned penniless. If these papers authenticate—"

"That's rather the point, sir."

Another silence. This one longer.

It was Vess who moved next, which surprised no one who knew her and everyone who didn't. She stood, produced her own papers—her reinstatement petition, stamped and rejected twice—and laid it on the rail.

"If the court wants authentication," she said, "the navigator who charted the Provision's last voyage is standing right here. I trained two of the crew whose names are on those pages. I can vouch for the handwriting, the seals, and the route. And while I'm at it—" her voice went dry and precise, "—I'd like the record to show exactly which salvage fleet captain paid me to falsify that route, eleven years ago, and which one threatened to drown my career if I mentioned it."

Captain Varrow stopped looking comfortable.

* * *

It took four hours. Mirabel counted them by the water stains creeping up the courtroom wall.

Lawyers spoke. Ledgers were produced. A syndicate clerk cracked under questioning like an egg and gave up everything except his mother's maiden name, which nobody asked about. By mid-afternoon, Captain Ede Varrow stood alone at the rail, stripped of his salvage charter, facing charges that would take years to finish ruining him—which, everyone agreed, was only fair, since he'd taken years to finish ruining everyone else.

The magistrate, before adjourning, looked down at Mirabel.

"The court acknowledges the recovery of these documents. There may be a finder's claim. Salvage law provides—"

"No."

The single syllable dropped into the room and rolled around.

"No claim?" The magistrate blinked. "You're owed—"

"I'm owed a debt," Mirabel said. "To a dying man. He asked me to give these back. You don't collect a fee for keeping a promise. You just keep it." They hesitated. Then, because Tam deserved better than a half-truth: "There is one thing. Tam Wicker's name. It's in none of your records except as a thief. He wasn't. He was a coward for one bad hour and honest for eleven years after. Put that in the record. All of it. The bad hour too. He'd have wanted the whole story told—that was the kind of stubborn fool he was."

The magistrate wrote it down himself.

"And the Sandoval name?" Mirabel added, quieter. "My father's record. He was called a grave-robber for cataloguing wrecks the syndicate wanted stripped clean. The court's own archives hold his surveys. Check them. Then decide what to call him."

The magistrate's pen paused. "That request is... irregular."

"So am I, sir. Ask anyone."

* * *

Outside, the evening had gone gold and the harbor smelled of frying fish and tar, which Mirabel had always considered the two best smells in the world, though never at once until now.

The crew was waiting on the quay. Juniper the cook had somehow acquired a barrel of candied ginger from somewhere she refused to name, and Brannick the rigging-master—who was deaf and read lips with unnerving accuracy—was signing something at her that made her laugh so hard she had to sit down on a bollard. When Mirabel approached, Brannick's hands stilled, and he studied their face with the frank attention of a man who missed nothing spoken or otherwise.

*Well?* he signed.

"It's done. The names go home. Families get notified by week's end."

Juniper stopped laughing. She looked out at the water. "My brother was merchant marine," she said, to no one in particular. "Lost with a coaster eight years back. No paper ever came. Ma lit a candle for a man the harbor said never existed." She wiped her eyes briskly, angrily. "Maybe he wasn't on the Provision. Maybe he was. But somebody's ma gets a letter now. That's enough."

Nobody said anything for a while. It was that kind of quiet—the good kind, the kind a crew earns.

Then Vess cleared her throat and unrolled a chart on a crate with the flourish of a woman announcing a crime. "The *Kestrel's* refit finishes Thursday. New rigging, patched hull, and I've charted a run north—three wrecks off the Skerry Shelf that nobody's touched because everybody thinks the currents there are impossible."

"Are they?" Mirabel asked.

"Almost entirely."

Brannick grinned and signed something fast. Juniper translated, still sniffling: "He says impossible currents mean no competition, and no competition means first pick of the good stuff."

"We'd need a diver," Vess said, looking at Mirabel.

"You have one."

"For shares. Equal shares. No debts, no syndicate, no hiding." Vess's eyes narrowed slightly. "Which means you'd have to be seen, Sandoval. Regularly. In daylight. Possibly even *spoken to.*"

Mirabel considered the harbor—the syndicate ships at anchor, the court windows going dark, the whole glittering apparatus of a town that had never once noticed them and now, thanks to an afternoon of testimony, never would again forget their name.

It sounded, they decided, survivable.

"Thursday," they said, and shook on it, and the crew of the *Kestrel*—navigator, cook, rigging-master, and one reformed ghost—went to find dinner, unmoored by choice, anchored to each other, and already arguing about who got the bunk by the stove.
