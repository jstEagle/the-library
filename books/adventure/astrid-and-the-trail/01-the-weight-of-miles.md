## 1. The Weight of Miles

Marguerite Coldharbour measured his life in kilometers now, because kilometers could not lie to him the way calendars did.

A calendar said five years had passed since Karst Ridge. A calendar was a thing with teeth. It counted down toward anniversaries he refused to observe, toward inquiries that had closed without ever opening, toward a name that appeared on two blacklists and no payroll. But a kilometer was honest. It began where it began and ended where it ended, and it asked nothing of him except that he put one boot in front of the other until it was finished.

So he counted.

The Lower Falls circuit: eleven point four kilometers, four hundred meters of gain, three hours at tourist pace, two if he walked alone. The Fern Gully traverse: six point eight, mostly flat, good for honeymooners and men in sandals who insisted they were hikers. The Old Quarry loop, which he saved for overcast days when the light made the flooded pit look like a coin at the bottom of a green glass: nine point two kilometers, and not one of them memorable, which was the entire point.

He had become, by any honest accounting, a keeper of forgettable distances.

The tourists loved him for it, or thought they did. They wrote in the guesthouse ledger things like *steady* and *knows every rock* and *never once felt unsafe*, and Marguerite read these compliments the way a man reads his own obituary—grateful to be remembered, unsettled by what he was remembered for. Steady. He had been steady once in a way that mattered, on a rope bridge above a gorge full of white water, and steadiness had gotten a man killed and a guide ruined, and now steadiness meant keeping the pace slow enough that Mrs. Aldous from the coast could manage the scree without complaint.

He kept the pace slow. He kept his voice level. He kept his face arranged into something pleasant and unremarkable, the face of a man you would struggle to describe to a constable an hour after meeting him. This last skill he had learned the hard way, in border towns, in ferry queues, in the long gray years of being a name that made clerks reach for their files.

On the morning the broker came, Marguerite was counting again—the last two hundred meters of the Quarry loop, the grade easing toward the trailhead, the tourists strung out behind him like beads on a frayed cord—and he felt the particular restlessness that arrived every autumn, when the rains thinned and the air sharpened and something under his sternum began pacing like an animal in too small a pen.

He told himself it was nothing. He told himself it was the season. He had gotten very good at telling himself things.

* * *

The broker's name was Quill, and he found Marguerite at the dockside cantina where the guides drank cheap rum and lied about fish. He was a small man built like a folded letter, all creases and corners, with a canvas satchel he never set down and eyes that moved around a room the way a pickpocket's hands did.

"Coldharbour." He said it quietly, sliding onto the stool two down from Marguerite's, close enough to talk, far enough to be deniable. "Heard you were up this way."

"Heard wrong."

"Maybe." Quill ordered nothing. That was how Marguerite knew this wasn't social; a man who bought no drink wanted no record of sitting here. "I've got a run. Supply work. Upriver."

"I do trails," Marguerite said. "Not rivers."

"You used to do both."

"Used to is a country I don't have papers for."

Quill smiled at that, thin as paper itself. He opened the satchel halfway—not enough to show contents, only enough to show there were contents—and spoke to the middle distance between them. "Coldwater Mining. Survey outfit working the upper reaches of the Vane system. They went quiet six weeks back. Their resupply contract lapsed, and the company wants someone to take a boat up with provisions and radio parts and come back with a report. Simple freight. Nobody's asking you to find anybody."

"Then why does it smell like finding somebody?"

"Because everything smells like something, if your nose is as ruined as yours." Quill finally looked at him directly. "The pay is triple your season rate."

"My season rate is fair."

"The pay is triple it anyway."

Marguerite turned his glass a quarter turn on the wet wood. Outside, the river ran brown-green and patient, carrying its cargo of leaves and secrets toward the delta. He watched a heron stand in the shallows with the stillness of something pretending not to be hunting, and he recognized the posture intimately.

"No," he said.

Quill nodded as though he'd expected exactly that, finished some invisible arithmetic, and left without touching the satchel's contents. Marguerite told himself the relief he felt was the relief of a sensible man declining a bad bargain. He told himself several other things that night, lying awake in his room above the boat shed, listening to the Kestrel knock against her mooring lines like a horse testing a fence.

* * *

Three days later, Quill came back.

This time he found Marguerite at the boat shed itself, which Marguerite took as a message: *I know where you sleep, and I know what you love.* The Kestrel sat on her trailer in the yard, hull open to the sky, and Marguerite had spent the morning with a scraper in his hand removing old caulk from a seam near the bow—a job he'd done four times in five years and could do blind, a job that left his hands busy and his mind free to wander places it shouldn't.

"Twice the offer," Quill said, standing at the edge of the yard with his satchel held against his chest like a shield. "And I'll cover materials for whatever repairs she needs before you cast off."

"She doesn't need repairs."

Every guide within earshot turned to look at the Kestrel, whose hull was patched in three places with fiberglass and stubbornness, whose bilge pump had opinions, whose name had been repainted twice to confuse port authorities in countries where Marguerite's face was worth money to certain people. The Kestrel leaked the way old men coughed—chronically, predictably, survivably.

Quill had the grace not to smile. "Two and a half times, then. And the company throws in a radio set for the boat. New. You'd keep it after."

"I said no."

"You said no to triple. This is different numbers."

"It's the same word."

But his hands had stopped moving on the scraper, and Quill saw it, because Quill was the kind of man who made his living noticing the exact moment resistance became ritual. He didn't push. He unfolded a scrap of paper, set it on the trailer fender, weighted it with a bolt, and walked away down the lane with his creased coat flapping, a folded letter returning to the mail.

The paper had coordinates on it. A rendezvous date ten days out. A figure written in a hand so careful it might have been printed. And beneath it, in smaller script: *Coldwater survey, Elias Vane leading. Quiet for six weeks. Company's nervous.*

Elias Vane. Marguerite knew the name the way he knew most names in the valley trade—from manifests, from gossip at fuel docks, from the particular reverence guides gave men who staked claims in country nobody else would touch. Vane was supposed to be good. Vane was supposed to be thorough. Thorough men did not go quiet for six weeks unless the country had eaten them or the company had buried them, and either way it was none of Marguerite Coldharbour's business.

He scraped caulk until his knuckles ached. He burned the paper in the shop stove, watched the careful figures curl and go black, and told himself that was the end of it.

It was not the end of it. It was never the end of anything; that was the lesson of Karst Ridge, and he had learned it so thoroughly he sometimes forgot he'd learned it at all.

* * *

The restlessness got worse.

That was the truth of it, though he dressed it in pragmatism the way other men dressed wounds in clean bandages. The money was real—triple, then two and a half times, a sum that would buy a new bilge pump, new caulk, a winter of not guiding honeymooners past the flooded quarry while they asked him questions about himself he had answers for but no intention of giving. The radio was real. The work was real: freight upriver, freight back, no heroics.

But underneath the ledger of practical reasons, something else paced and paced. Five years of forgettable kilometers had worn a groove in him, smooth and safe and shallow, and every autumn the groove felt less like a channel and more like a cage. He had survived his ruin by shrinking his world to distances he could count, and now the counting had begun to feel like the punishment rather than the sentence served.

He caught himself, on the eighth day, standing at the rail of the footbridge over the lower rapids, watching the water come through the narrows, and thinking—not deciding, thinking—that the upper Vane was ninety kilometers of river he had never run, through country he had never seen, toward an answer he hadn't been asked to find.

Ninety kilometers. His mind had already measured it. That was the tell. A man truly uninterested does not measure.

* * *

On the ninth day he went to see Quill.

He found the broker at the same cantina, same stool, same untouched drink, as though the intervening week had been a single continuous conversation with a pause in the middle. Quill looked up, and for the first time his eyes stayed still.

"Yes?" he said.

Marguerite sat down. He kept his voice low and his face arranged. "One condition. The report I bring back goes to the family first. Before the company. Whatever's happened up there, whoever's waiting for news—they hear it from me, not from a boardroom."

Quill studied him for a long moment. Something flickered behind the pickpocket eyes—not warmth exactly, but recognition, the look of a man confirming a rumor about another man's character. "Vane has a daughter," he said. "Astrid. She's been asking the company the same question for six weeks and getting the same nothing. You want me to route through her?"

"I want the truth going where it'll hurt least and help most. If that's her, then her."

"That's not in my contract."

"Neither am I. Not yet."

Quill almost smiled. He produced a second sheet of paper, already prepared—which told Marguerite the broker had known how this would end before Marguerite did—and slid it across the table along with a pencil stub. "Rendezvous is the fuel dock below the weir, dawn day after tomorrow. Load list is on the back. And Coldharbour—" Quill tapped the paper once, lightly, before letting it go. "The company chose you because you're invisible. Cheap, deniable, no questions asked if you don't come back. Don't make them regret a decision that cheap."

"And if I do come back?"

"Then maybe you're not invisible anymore." Quill finally bought a drink, which Marguerite understood as a kind of signature. "Maybe you're a problem. Problems get solved, Coldharbour. Invisibles just fade."

Marguerite signed. The pencil scratched the way small irrevocable things scratch. He took the load list, folded it into his breast pocket over the place where his heart conducted its restless animal business, and walked out into the late light.

At the boat shed, he stood a long time looking at the Kestrel on her trailer, patched and leaking and stubborn, the only witness to five years who had never once asked him what happened at Karst Ridge. He laid his palm flat against her hull, feeling the sun-warmed fiberglass, the faint tremor of the yard around them, the whole patient machinery of a life measured in safe distances.

"Ninety kilometers," he said aloud, to the boat, to himself, to the dark coming down over the water. "Up and back. Freight. Nothing else."

The Kestrel knocked against her cradle, noncommittal.

That night he packed by lamplight: line, anchors, the medical kit he hadn't opened in five years and checked anyway, spare parts for a pump with opinions. He worked slowly, deliberately, telling himself each item was pragmatism, each choice was arithmetic, the whole enterprise was a sensible man taking sensible money for sensible work.

He was almost done when he heard footsteps on the dock boards outside—light ones, quick ones, the steps of someone who had learned to walk quietly on boats and had never quite unlearned it—and a shadow crossed the lamplit gap under the shed door, paused, and moved on.

Marguerite stood very still in the yellow light, listening to the footsteps fade along the dock, and felt the first cold thread of certainty that the sensible work had already stopped being sensible.

Someone had been watching the boat.
