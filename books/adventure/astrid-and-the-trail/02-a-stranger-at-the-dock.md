## 2. A Stranger at the Dock

The Kestrel sat low in the brown water like a dog that knew it had done something wrong, and I stood on the dock of Port Anselm with a manifest in my hand and rain starting to needle the surface of the river, counting.

That was the thing about me, if you asked anyone who'd worked with me before Karst Ridge: I counted. Distances, not days. Kilometers to the first bend. Kilometers from the bend to the shallows where the channel braided and you had to read the water like a difficult letter. Kilometers upriver to Coldwater camp, which was—according to the broker's paperwork, which smelled of cigarette smoke and bad faith—ninety-one kilometers of river, give or take whatever the monsoon decided to subtract.

I'd refused the job twice. The first time in the broker's office, where he'd slid a folder across a desk scarred with ring-stains and said *supply run, easy money, nobody asks questions*, and I'd said no because nobody asking questions is exactly what people say when there are questions worth asking. The second time outside, in the street, when he'd named a figure with three zeroes and I'd said no again, more firmly, and then gone back to my room above the chandlery and lain awake listening to the roof drip and thinking about three zeroes.

Restlessness, I told myself, is just pragmatism with nowhere to sit down. My savings were a rumor. The Kestrel needed caulking, paint, a new stern gland, and possibly an exorcism. The tourist season was dying the way it always died in the weeks before the rains—slowly, then all at once—and the safe forgettable trails I guided paid in tips and small talk. *Is that the mountain? How high is it? Is it dangerous?* No, yes, and depends what you mean by dangerous, I'd answer, and count the switchbacks instead of the hours.

So on the third day I went back to the broker and said yes, and hated myself with a clean, efficient hatred, the kind that doesn't waste energy.

The cargo was unglamorous: rice in sealed drums, diesel, medical crates marked with a red cross that someone had already tried to peel off, spare parts for a pump generator, mail. Mail—that was the detail that snagged me. Forty kilos of letters and parcels, tied in oilcloth bundles, addressed to men and women at a mining survey camp that had, per the broker, simply stopped answering its radio. Not unusual, he'd said. Radios out here die like flies. Wet season coming. Probably nothing.

Probably nothing. I'd carried those words up enough mountains to know they weigh the most.

I loaded through the morning, working alone because working alone was a discipline I kept the way other men kept religion. Rice drums first, low and centered. Diesel aft, lashed against the thwarts. The mail last, dry under a tarp I'd bought secondhand and patched with bicycle-tire rubber. Rain came and went in fits, the river rising a finger's width every hour, the color of strong tea left too long. Gulls argued over fish guts two slips down. A customs man watched me from the shade of the shed with the particular boredom of a man being paid whether or not he looks.

By noon the Kestrel rode maybe ten centimeters lower than she should have for the cargo aboard.

I noticed it the way you notice anything wrong with a boat you've owned five years—not by measuring but by feel, some animal arithmetic in the gut. I crouched at the gunwale, checked my lashings, counted drums. Eleven loaded; eleven on the manifest. Diesel: four jerry cans, correct. Crates: six, correct. Then I walked the length of her, hand trailing along the rail, and found the tarp over the mail bulging in a way tarps don't bulge unless something under them breathes.

I pulled it back.

A woman lay curled among the mail bundles like she'd been poured there and set, knees drawn up, one arm folded under her head, eyes closed. She was maybe thirty, dark-haired, cut short in a way that suggested she'd done it herself with a knife and a mirror rather than paid anyone to do it properly. Her clothes were travel clothes gone past travel into endurance—canvas trousers, a shirt that had been white, boots that had been resoled at least twice. There was mud on her to the knee. River mud, not town mud. She'd been walking the banks.

She opened her eyes without any of the fumbling startle most stowaways manage. Just looked up at me, gray-eyed, level, as if I were the interruption.

"That's my space," I said. "You're stealing it."

"I'm borrowing it." She unfolded herself and sat up, and I saw she'd been sleeping sitting upright against the transom before lying down—there was a crease pressed into her cheek, and her hands had the faint tremor of someone running on coffee and will. "You were going to leave at dawn. I saved us both a conversation."

"Us." I looked at her the way I look at a rope bridge with frayed strands: assessing load. "There's no us. This is a contracted run. One man, one boat, one manifest. You're not on it."

"Then add me."

"No."

She stood, which took effort—the Kestrel rolled and she rode it without grabbing anything, which told me she'd spent time on water, though not necessarily this water. Up close she was thinner than she'd looked curled up, with the specific thinness of someone who'd been eating badly on purpose, stretching money. Her jacket had a torn pocket sewn shut with dental floss. Somebody resourceful, somebody broke.

"Name's Vane," she said. "Astrid Vane."

I didn't offer mine. Names are currency and I'd learned to keep my wallet shut.

"Elias Vane," she said, watching my face. "Expedition leader, Coldwater Mining survey. He's my father. He hasn't answered a radio call in six weeks, and the company tells me he's fine, everything's fine, don't worry, miss, and meanwhile they've stopped forwarding his mail." She nodded at the oilcloth bundles. "Which is why I'm standing in your boat. That's his mail. Some of it's mine."

The rain thickened, drumming on the tarp, hissing off the river. Down the dock the customs man had lost interest in us entirely, absorbed in a newspaper. I felt the job shifting under my feet the way a scree slope shifts—one stone at a time until suddenly it's all moving.

"Six weeks," I said.

"Forty-three days." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "But who's counting."

I almost smiled. Almost. "Company says he's fine?"

"The company says a lot of things." Her voice stayed flat, but something moved underneath it, deep down, the way big fish move under calm water. "Meridian Consolidated runs the survey. They've got a liaison office in Anselm with a man named Corben who has a handshake like a wet glove. I've been to that office nine times. Nine. The first three times Elias was 'unreachable due to field conditions.' The next three he was 'expected to make contact shortly.' The last three they wouldn't see me at all. So I stopped asking them and started asking the river."

"And the river sent you to me."

"The river sent me to every boat captain on this dock. You're the fourth. Two said no. One said yes and then wanted payment in a form I wasn't prepared to discuss." Something flickered across her face at that—disgust, quickly banked. "You're the only one loading actual supplies for Coldwater. Which means you're contracted, which means you have a reason to be there besides curiosity, which means when we arrive, you're expected. I'm not. But a woman arriving with the supply runner draws less attention than a woman arriving alone."

"We," I said. "Listen to yourself. You've already made the trip."

"I'm a fast negotiator."

"You haven't negotiated anything. You've committed trespass and theft of deck space, and now you're describing the consequences as a partnership." I picked up the tarp edge. "Off the boat."

"No."

"It wasn't a question."

"Neither was that." She held my gaze, and I'll grant her this: she didn't blink, didn't perform fear, didn't do any of the small theatrical things people do when they want to be seen as brave. She just stood there in the rain getting soaked, arms crossed, a woman who had decided something and was now simply waiting for physics to catch up.

I've put ashore stowaways before. Drunk miners, a seminarian with a fear of boats, once a man carrying a crate of parrots he claimed were his own grief. It never gets pleasant. You lift them over the side, you set them on the dock, you cast off, and you watch them shrink. It's a muscle. Mine was well developed.

So why did I hesitate?

Because of the mail, probably. Because forty-three days was a number I recognized—I'd been out of contact with the world for stretches longer than that, and I knew how silence stops being weather and becomes a wall. Because she'd said *who's counting* with the exact cadence of someone who counted everything, and I trusted counters. Or because the broker's *probably nothing* had been sitting wrong in my chest since Tuesday, and here was a woman whose whole body was evidence that it might be something.

None of which changed the arithmetic. One boat, one contract, one man whose name was on a blacklist in two countries and whose continued freedom depended on being boring. Passengers were complications. Complications were witnesses. Witnesses were risk.

"Last chance," I said. "Dock or over the side. Your choice which is gentler."

"Triple."

The word landed between us and just sat there.

"What?"

"Triple your contract rate. Passage to Coldwater camp and back, plus return passage to Anselm whenever you choose to come. Paid half now, half on arrival." She reached inside her jacket—the sewn-shut pocket, the dental-floss stitching—and came out with a flat oilskin pouch. She didn't open it. Didn't need to. The weight of it said enough. "I sold the flat in the capital. All of it. I'm not rich anymore, but I'm liquid, and I am extremely motivated."

Rain ran off my hat brim and dripped off my nose. I thought about the Kestrel's stern gland, which wept like a widower. I thought about caulk, and paint, and the exorcism. I thought about three zeroes.

And I thought: *she's paying me to walk into whatever swallowed her father.* People pay guides good money to take them away from danger. When someone pays triple to go toward it, either they don't understand the danger or they understand something you don't. Both possibilities deserved respect. Neither made the trip safer.

"Half now," I said, "in my hand, before you sit down anywhere. And conditions."

Her chin lifted a fraction. "Conditions."

"One: you're cargo with opinions. If I tell you to move, you move. If I tell you to get down, you get down. If I tell you we're turning around, we turn around, and you don't argue, you don't sulk, you don't produce more money. Two: you pull your weight. Lines, bailing, cooking if it comes to that. I'm not a ferry, I'm a crew of one, and now I'm a crew of one and a half. Three—" I paused, because condition three was the one that mattered and also the one I couldn't fully explain. "Three: you don't ask me about myself. Where I've been, what I've done, why a man like me takes jobs like this. The river's long enough without biography."

Something sharpened in her eyes at that. She heard it—people always hear it, the shape of the hole where a story should be. For a moment I thought she'd push, and I braced to put her ashore after all, triple or no triple.

Instead she said, "Same goes for me, then. We agree: no biographies."

"Agreed."

She shook my hand. Her grip was dry despite the rain, hard, brief—a climber's grip, or a rower's, callused across the fingers in a pattern I couldn't quite place. Then she counted notes out of the oilskin pouch onto the dry spot under the tarp, and it was triple, and it was real, and I watched my pragmatism and my restlessness shake hands like old enemies calling a truce for the duration of a war neither would admit to.

"Stow your gear," I said. "Small. One bag. Anything that floats badly stays on the dock."

"It all floats badly. It's luggage."

"Then pack like you expect to swim."

She looked at me for a beat, and I watched her decide to believe me, which was smarter than deciding I was joking. She climbed out over the rail with a economy of motion that confirmed the water time, dropped to the dock, and walked off toward the boarding houses with her collar up, already becoming a smaller silhouette in the rain.

I stood alone with the Kestrel and did the math I'd been avoiding.

Ninety-one kilometers upriver. A camp that had gone quiet. A company that lied in threes. A daughter with a dead father's mail and a live father's stubbornness, carrying enough cash to buy her way onto a stranger's boat and enough anger to fuel the whole journey twice over. And me—blacklisted, invisible, five years into a life built on the principle that nothing interesting ever happens to men who guide tourists past waterfalls.

I'd taken the job for money. That was the story I'd tell myself tonight, lying awake above the chandlery, listening to the roof drip. Money for the hull, money for the caulk, money because miles are the only honest currency left to me.

But even then—even there, in the rain, with her footsteps fading down the dock—I knew the truth, the way you know a storm is coming before the clouds stack: I hadn't accepted because of the zeroes.

I'd accepted because forty-three days of silence was a number, and numbers were the only language I still trusted, and somewhere up that river something had stopped counting back.

* * *

She came back ninety minutes later with one canvas duffel, a rifle wrapped in sacking—which I inspected, approved, and made her declare to the customs man, who stamped a form without reading it—and a tin of biscuits she set on the thwart between us like a peace offering or a siege ration.

"Cast-off at dawn?" she asked.

"Dawn," I said. "Sleep aboard. There's a tarp and a space that isn't yours yet."

"Generous."

"Don't mistake it for hospitality. It's so I can leave without you if you change your mind."

She settled into the bow, wrapped herself in the tarp, and within minutes was asleep—truly asleep, the sleep of the exhausted, her breathing slow and even while the rain kept up its commentary on the river. I sat at the tiller under my hat and watched the lights of Port Anselm blur and smear, and I checked the lashings twice, and I listened to the Kestrel's familiar small leaks doing their familiar small work.

At some point in the dark she spoke without waking, one word, muffled:

"Papa."

I looked upriver, where the black water ran toward country I'd never guided anyone through, and began, quietly, to count the kilometers.
