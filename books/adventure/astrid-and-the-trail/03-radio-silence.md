## 3. Radio Silence

**Marguerite**

The river changed its mind twice in the first day.

It began as the brown, unhurried water of the lowlands, wide enough to hold its breath, and by afternoon it had narrowed into something quicker and greener, shouldering between clay banks where kingfishers stitched blue along the current. By evening it was a different animal altogether—deeper, colder, carrying the smell of high country down to us like a message sent ahead: *stone, pine, old snow.* The Kestrel climbed against it patiently, her engine muttering its one long complaint, and I let her.

I had done this run a dozen times in other years, for other outfits, back when my name still opened doors instead of closing them. Two days upriver to Coldwater camp, three if the weather argued. Fuel drums, rice, tinned milk, batteries, the small freight of men pretending they were pioneers. It had been, once, the kind of job nobody wanted and everybody needed, and I had been good at it in the way I was good at most things: quietly, without ceremony, counting kilometers instead of days.

Astrid Vane slept through most of the first morning, curled under the tarp in the bow with her boots drawn up, and when she woke she did not apologize for sleeping or thank me for letting her. She simply sat up, looked at the river as though confirming it was still there, and said, "You steer with your knees."

"Hands are for other things."

"What other things?"

"Charts. Coffee. Throwing stowaways overboard."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was an expression I would learn to watch for over the following weeks—the way you watch a horizon for weather—but that first day I only noted it and turned back to the channel, because there was a snag line ahead where the water folded over itself like cloth, and because looking at her too long felt like reading someone else's mail.

We spoke little. She asked questions the way surveyors ask them: precise, unhurried, expecting answers eventually but not immediately. How far to Coldwater. What the camp was like. Whether the radio at the landing worked better than the one on the boat. I answered what I could and let the rest go past us like driftwood.

The truth about the radio was that it worked exactly as well as radios work in valleys—which is to say, according to mood. The Kestrel's set was a Marconi portable older than either of us, its casing cracked and mended with resin, its antenna a length of wire I'd strung from the mast to the stern cleat. In open water it could raise Port Anselm on a good day. In the narrows, where the hills closed in like hands cupped around a whisper, it heard mostly itself.

By the second morning we were deep in those narrows. The walls rose steep and dripping on both sides, hung with moss that drank the light and gave back green. Mist sat on the water in slow-moving shelves. The engine noise came back off the rock so that it seemed to travel with us from behind, like a follower.

At noon I nosed the boat into an eddy below a shelf of gray stone, killed the engine, and let the silence arrive all at once—the way it does when a motor dies, sudden as a door closing. Water ticked against the hull. Somewhere upstream a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Coldwater keeps scheduled contact," Astrid said. She had the schedule sheet out, Elias's own handwriting on it, copied in her neater hand. "Eleven hundred hours, every second day. Today is a contact day."

"I know."

"You've known since yesterday."

"I like to see if clients do their homework." I pulled the wire antenna taut and checked the connection at the set's brass terminal. "Eleven hundred. We're early. Eat something."

She didn't eat anything. She sat with the schedule sheet in her lap and watched me tune the dial, and the mist moved past us in the eddy like slow traffic, and at four minutes to eleven the valley was so quiet I could hear the moss dripping.

I pressed transmit. "Coldwater camp, Coldwater camp, this is supply launch Kestrel out of Port Anselm, inbound with freight, do you copy? Over."

Static. Not the clean static of empty air—that has a texture, a hiss like sand poured on paper—but the thick, layered static of country interfering with itself, mountains chewing the signal and spitting out pieces.

I tried again. Gave our position, our heading, the weather. Asked for confirmation of landing conditions at the Coldwater jetty, which was half-rotted two years ago and which I'd told the broker I'd want repaired before arrival.

Nothing. The static breathed. Underneath it, once, I thought I caught a rhythm—a pulse, a syllable—and then it was gone, and I couldn't have sworn to it any more than you can swear to a face seen in falling snow.

"They'll answer," Astrid said.

"It's early yet."

"It's eleven-oh-six."

I looked at her. Her hands were flat on the schedule sheet, pressing it smooth against her knees as though the paper might otherwise fly. Her knuckles were white. She noticed me noticing and made herself relax them, finger by finger, deliberately, the way you'd calm a horse.

"Somebody will answer," she said again, quieter, and this time it wasn't a statement. It was a request addressed to the valley.

I transmitted every ten minutes for an hour. I swept the frequencies above and below the camp's assigned band, in case their set had drifted or theirs was broken and they'd fallen back to a secondary channel. I called Port Anselm and got the harbor office, whose operator told me, in the bored voice of a man reading from a form, that Coldwater had missed its last two scheduled contacts and that no inquiry had been lodged. That was standard, he said. Camps went quiet. Batteries died. Men got drunk. He wished me a good run and rang off, and his click of disconnection sounded obscene in all that green silence.

Astrid stood at the bow the whole hour, holding the mast, not speaking. When I finally set the handset down she turned around, and her face had done something I recognized from mirrors in bad years: it had stopped waiting and started deciding.

"He missed two contacts," she said. "Six weeks ago he wrote to me—wrote, an actual letter, which he never did, he thought letters were for lawyers and widows. Six weeks ago, and then nothing. I told myself the mail was slow. I told myself the dry season roads were washed out. I told myself—" She stopped. Breathed. "I've been telling myself things for six weeks."

The mist came through the narrows in long gray banners. I killed the engine fully and let us sit in the eddy, rocking, and I thought about how many times in five years I had watched people receive news on rivers—in the set of shoulders, in the way a person suddenly becomes very interested in rope.

"Elias Vane," I said, "is careful with schedules?"

"Military before he was mining. Twenty-two years in signals, before I was born. He used to wake me for dawn contacts when I was nine because he said a missed check-in was a debt you owed an explanation for." Her mouth twisted. "He'd sooner miss his own funeral than a scheduled call."

"Then somebody owes an explanation."

She looked at me across the length of the boat, and whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly, as if I'd passed some examination she hadn't announced.

* * *

**Astrid**

There is a particular quality to fear that arrives gradually. It doesn't shout. It accumulates, the way silt does, until one day the channel you knew is gone and the map is lying to you.

I had been accumulating for six weeks. On the dock at Port Anselm I had dressed the fear up as stubbornness—*I'm going whether he likes it or not*—and Marguerite Coldharbour had let me wear the costume without comment, because he was the kind of man who understood that people need their disguises until they don't.

But sitting in that eddy with the static still ringing faintly in my ears, I ran out of costume.

"Papa taught me Morse before he taught me cursive," I said. I hadn't meant to say it. It came out because the silence demanded filling and because this man had just spent an hour doing the diligent, unglamorous work of worry, and diligence deserves honesty. "I was seven. He'd tap it out on the dinner table under the noise of conversation, little messages, and I'd tap back. *Pass the salt* in dots and dashes. He said the airwaves were the only honest country left—all signal, no scenery."

Marguerite said nothing, but he took his hat off, which I had not seen him do before, and ran a hand through his hair, and put the hat back on. A small ceremony. I understood it was his version of taking off shoes before entering grief.

"The letter," I went on. "Six weeks ago. It was strange. Businesslike—he listed the survey progress, the core samples, the tonnage estimates—and then at the end, one line that wasn't like him. He wrote: *If anything delays my letters, don't come looking. Sell the house.*" My throat tightened around the words. "Papa never says *don't*. He says *consider*, he says *weigh*, he says *the mountain will still be there in spring*. He doesn't say *don't come looking*."

"And you came looking."

"I sold the house."

For the first time since the dock, Marguerite laughed—not much, a single dry note, but real. "That," he said, "is the first thing you've told me that makes me think you're actually related to him."

"He'd have hated it."

"He'd have understood it." He leaned forward, forearms on his knees, and the boat rocked us both slightly toward each other and apart, toward and apart, like a slow argument. "Listen. I've run supplies to camps going quiet before. Nine times out of ten it's a dead battery and a hangover. The tenth time—" He paused. I watched him decide how much truth to spend. "The tenth time it isn't. But you can't tell which from here. You can only keep moving and stay sharp. So. Options."

"Options," I repeated.

"We turn around. I take you back to Anselm, refund what's left of your fare, and you hire a proper search when the monsoon's done—official channels, constabulary, the whole machinery. Slow, but survivable." He held up a second finger. "Or we go on. Two more days to Coldwater. We look ourselves, uninvited, unarmed, in country I know only from charts, before the rains close everything. And if what's happened at that camp is the tenth kind of quiet, we'll be standing in the middle of it with no one knowing where we are."

The mist moved. A heron lifted off the bank somewhere above us, enormous and prehistoric, and beat away upstream.

"You've already decided," I said. "You're listing options you've already priced."

His jaw worked. "I price everything. It's how I stay alive. Pricing isn't deciding."

"No," I agreed. "Deciding is deciding." And I heard my father's cadence come out of my own mouth, twenty-two years of signals discipline distilled into a girl of thirty-one sitting in a leaking boat. "Go on. I'm not turning around. If he's alive, he needs someone who taps back. If he's not—" I made myself say it. "If he's not, then someone owes an explanation, and I intend to collect."

Something shifted in Marguerite Coldharbour's face then. Not warmth exactly—it was too guarded for warmth, too practiced at being unreadable—but a recalibration, the way a man revises a load estimate when the cargo turns out heavier than manifest. He looked at me the way he must have looked at rivers he'd decided to run anyway.

"All right," he said. "Together, then. But we do it my way on the water. My boat, my rules, my pace. You get a veto on land."

"Why land?"

"Because land is where I stop being useful and start being responsible, and I don't like being responsible for people who buy triple fares to chase ghosts." He said it harshly, but his eyes weren't harsh; they were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "Deal?"

"Deal." I put out my hand. He looked at it for a moment as though hands were complicated instruments he'd stopped trusting years ago, and then he shook it—briefly, firmly, engineer's grip, no flourish.

"One condition of mine," I added, as he turned to restart the engine.

"Of course there is."

"When we make contact with anyone—anyone—about Papa, I talk. You translate if you have to. But I talk."

"That's not a condition, that's a liability."

"It's a fact. Take it or leave it."

He studied me for a long moment, mist beading on his hat brim, and I realized I was holding my breath, and made myself stop. Whatever he was weighing, he weighed it fast.

"Taken," he said, and pulled the starter cord, and the Kestrel coughed, caught, and carried us out of the eddy and back into the current, upstream, toward the quiet.

* * *

**Marguerite**

We ran the narrows until dusk, tied up in a side slough where the water was still and smelled of drowned leaves, and ate rice and tinned fish without ceremony. Astrid cleaned the pot without being asked and stowed it exactly where I'd have stowed it, which annoyed me in a way I chose not to examine.

I took first watch, because I always did, because sleep was a country I crossed only with difficulty and only when necessary. She protested, then didn't. Around midnight I heard her breathing change and knew she was awake, and after a while she said, to the darkness rather than to me:

"You checked the lashings again. Twice. While you thought I was asleep."

"Habit."

"You count things. Kilometers, lashings, minutes past a scheduled contact." Her voice was soft, curious, not accusing. "Why?"

Because miles couldn't leave me. Because numbers kept faith. Because a man who counts distances never has to count losses. I said none of it.

"Keeps the hands busy," I said.

In the dark she made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite acceptance. "My father does that too. Did. Counts everything. I used to think it was a surveyor thing." A pause, long enough for the river to say several sentences. "Now I think it's a grief thing."

I fed a knot of moss to the fire and watched it catch, and said nothing, and she let me, and that—for reasons I didn't yet understand and wouldn't for some weeks—felt less like evasion than like agreement.

Dawn came up gray and smelling of rain that hadn't started yet. We cast off into a river running higher than it had the day before, the water the color of strong tea, and by midmorning we rounded the great bend below the Coldwater valley, and I cut the engine, and we listened.

No generators. No hammering. No voices carrying across water the way camp voices always carry, complaining about porridge and weather. Just the river, and birdsong, and far off a sound I told myself was wind in the pines and knew, even as I told myself, was smoke-colored silence.

Astrid stood at the bow with her father's schedule sheet in her hand.

"Half a day," I said, and opened the throttle, and the Kestrel leaned into the current like a woman leaning into a door she already knows is locked.
