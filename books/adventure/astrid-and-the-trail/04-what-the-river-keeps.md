## 4. What the River Keeps

The river taught me to read before any school did.

Not words—my mother had done that, patiently, with a battered alphabet book and her finger under each letter—but the other kind of reading, the kind that has no alphabet at all. My father used to spread his survey maps on the kitchen table of whatever rented house we'd landed in that year, weight the corners with teacups and river stones, and set me on his knee.

"Tell me what this line is doing," he'd say, tapping a brown curve with one blunt finger.

"It's a hill."

"Wrong. It's a promise. Every contour is the ground making you a promise: climb me and here's what you'll find. Two lines close together—" he'd pinch the air between thumb and forefinger, "—that's a cliff being honest with you. Lines far apart? A gentle slope. The map never lies, Astrid. People lie. Maps just repeat what the earth already said."

I was six when he first put a pencil in my hand and let me trace the contours myself. I was nine when I could look at a valley and see it doubled—the living green of it, and underneath, ghosted in brown ink, the skeleton the contours described. He called it "seeing twice," and he said it was the only inheritance worth leaving anyone, because land could be taken but not the way of seeing it.

He was wrong about that, as it turned out. But I didn't know it yet, sitting on his knee with pencil shavings in my hair, while somewhere beyond the window the concessions were already spreading toward us like weather.

* * *

Now I sat in the stern of a leaking boat called the Kestrel and watched the real thing scroll past—river, bank, ridge—and found I couldn't stop seeing twice. The jungle wasn't jungle to me; it was elevation lines waiting to be drawn. The oxbow we rounded at midmorning was a contour loop closing on itself. My father had given me this way of seeing, and now it was the only part of him I had left, and I carried it upriver like a lantern with a dying wick.

Six weeks. Six weeks since his last letter, which hadn't even been a letter, really—three lines on Coldwater Mining stationery, written in the handwriting that had gone small and cramped over the years, the way handwriting does when a man writes less and less:

*Survey going slower than planned. Ground doesn't match the old maps. Don't worry about me. A.*

*Don't worry about me.* As if those four words had ever once worked, in thirty years, on anyone who loved him.

Marguerite sat forward at the tiller, saying nothing, which was most of what he ever said. He steered the way he seemed to do everything—economically, without flourish, as though motion itself were a budget he refused to overspend. When the current shoved at the hull he corrected without appearing to move. When a deadfall loomed around a bend he was already angled away from it before my eyes finished finding it. I had watched him for two days now, cataloguing him the way I'd been taught to catalogue terrain, and the picture that emerged was of a man built entirely out of deflections.

"You grew up around boats?" I asked, mostly to break the silence, partly because silence and I had an old grudge.

"No."

Just that. The word dropped into the water between us and sank. I waited for more, the way you wait for a fish to rise, and got ripples and nothing.

"But you handle this one like you were born in it."

"Anyone can learn a boat." He adjusted the tiller a degree. "It's learning to leave them that takes practice."

It wasn't wit, exactly, but it was the closest thing to an opening he'd offered, so I pressed it. "Where did you learn, then?"

A long moment. The engine ticked. Somewhere on the left bank a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"Places," Marguerite said.

*Places.* I turned the word over. It had the flat finality of a door closed gently rather than slammed—a courtesy, almost. And I understood, with the particular clarity that comes from having grown up around men who kept things, that I had just been refused. Politely. Completely.

Most people, refused like that, would have taken offense or taken the hint. I took notes instead. My father's other lesson, unspoken but absorbed: *when the ground won't tell you what it's hiding, mark where it refuses.*

* * *

We tied up at midafternoon below a shelf of red clay where the bank firmed enough to take stakes. Marguerite cooked—rice, tinned fish, chili oil from a jar he guarded like contraband—and ate looking at the fire instead of at me, which I was beginning to understand was his version of conversation.

"My father taught me maps," I said, because apparently I was incapable of taking a hint. "Contour maps. Before I could ride a bike."

Marguerite spooned rice into his mouth. Said nothing. But he didn't get up and check the mooring lines either, which from him was practically an invitation.

"He was a surveyor. Not mining—not then. He mapped watersheds for the government, back when the government still pretended to care about watersheds. We'd go out together in the dry season, him with his theodolite and me with a notebook too big for my pack, and he'd make me stand on hills and guess elevations. If I got within ten meters he'd buy me a lemonade." I turned my tin cup in my hands. "I got very good at guessing elevations. I drank a lot of lemonade."

The fire popped. Marguerite's face was half-lit, all planes and shadow, and I couldn't read it—which bothered me more than it should have, because reading faces was supposed to be another thing I was good at.

"When did it change?" he asked.

I looked up. It was the first question he'd asked me that wasn't logistical. *Do you get seasick. Can you swim. How much do you weigh.* This one was a door, cracked.

"The concessions," I said. "When I was about twelve. He stopped mapping watersheds and started staking claims. Or trying to. He'd disappear for months—Kalahari, the Cordillera, places I couldn't find on our school atlas. Letters would come. Then fewer letters. Then letters that sounded like they'd been written by someone wearing my father's hands." I laughed, and hated how it came out. "My mother lasted eight years of it. Then she stayed behind for good, in the last house, and it was just me and the postmarks."

"And you followed the postmarks."

"I followed the postmarks." I wrapped both hands around the cup. "You learn to live in the gaps. That's the thing nobody tells you about loving someone who's always leaving—you don't miss them all at once, you miss them in installments. Birthdays. First snows. The day you pass their height. You keep a ledger of absences, and after a while the ledger is fuller than the person."

Marguerite set down his spoon. For a moment I thought he was going to say something—something real, something that would cost him—and I braced for it the way you brace for cold water.

Instead he stood, collected both our tins, and walked down to wash them at the waterline.

I sat by the fire and listened to the river and felt something in my chest do a slow, furious folding, like a map being put away.

* * *

But I'm not easily discouraged. It's a family trait, possibly a disease.

That night, with the fire burned to coals and the mosquitoes drawing their fine gray curtain outside the net, I tried again. Not with questions—with payment. That was the trick I'd learned, watching my father work rooms full of hard men: nobody gives you information, but nearly everyone will trade it.

"Karst Ridge," I said into the dark.

The silence across the fire changed quality. It's hard to describe how a silence can change, but this one went from empty to occupied, like something had stepped into it.

"There was an inquiry," I went on, before he could shut the door. "Five years ago. A guide named Marguerite Coldharbour. A crossing collapsed. A client died. They blacklisted him in two countries without ever holding a proper trial—I looked it up, on the boat, the first night, while you slept. The file's thinner than it should be. Inquiry reports run two hundred pages minimum. His runs forty, and eleven of those are appendices."

"You've been busy." His voice was level. Too level. The voice of a man standing very still on ground he suspects is hollow.

"I have a habit of reading things people don't want read. My father says—" I caught myself. "Said it would get me in trouble someday."

The coals ticked. Down at the waterline, the Kestrel knocked softly against her mooring stake, patient as a dog.

"Why are you telling me this?" Marguerite asked finally.

"Because you know why. Because you've spent two days not asking me anything personal, which means you know exactly how much it costs to be asked. Because—" I pulled my knees up inside the net, "—because I'm about to walk into whatever happened at that camp, and I need to know if the man beside me is someone whose disasters follow him, or someone the disasters find anyway. There's a difference. I've traveled with both kinds."

Another silence. Longer. I watched his shape against the dying fire, and I saw him do something I recognized from the mirror: the small settling of the shoulders, the gathering of a story behind the teeth, the breath before a confession or its refusal. I had seen my father do it a hundred times across a hundred kitchen tables, deciding how much truth the room could hold.

"Sleep," Marguerite said. "We make the confluence by noon tomorrow. After that the water gets honest."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It isn't."

And that was all. He rolled into his blanket with his back to the fire, and the dark took him the way it takes things that have practiced being taken.

* * *

I didn't sleep well. I lay under the net listening to the river run its endless sentence and thought about my father teaching me to read contours, and how he'd never once, in all those years, let me read *him*. A man who gave his daughter the whole grammar of the earth and withheld every word of himself. Maybe that was the family trade: we learned to see twice, and we spent the skill on everything except each other.

Somewhere past midnight I heard Marguerite stir. I kept my breathing slow and even, the way you do, and through the mesh of the net I watched him sit up and look—at me first, briefly, checking, the way you check a sleeping stranger you're responsible for—and then out at the black water, for a long time, with an expression I couldn't parse in the dark except to name it grief and immediately doubt the naming.

Then he lay back down, and the night resumed.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of weak tea. We broke camp in the efficient silence of two people who have negotiated a truce without signing it, and by seven we were back on the water, the Kestrel complaining in her patched seams, the banks narrowing as the river gathered itself for the high country.

I took the bow seat, where I could watch the water ahead, and I found I was reading it without meaning to—reading the surface the way my father taught me to read paper. The smooth glassy tongue down the center meant depth. The scattered boil to the left meant rock under pressure. Where the current bent a standing tree's reflection into a quiver, the channel ran fast and shallow, and the V of darker water threading through it was the passage, the promise, the line the river was honest enough to draw for anyone who knew its language.

"Rapids today," I said. Not a question. I'd seen the contours in my head all night, the brown lines pinching together above the confluence, cliffs being honest.

Marguerite glanced at the sky, then upstream, and I saw him do the same arithmetic I'd been doing—the distance, the light, the water level, the boat's wounded hull.

"Yeah," he said. "Today."

There was something in the way he said it. Not fear, exactly. Recognition. Like a man hearing, far off, a piece of music he'd sworn never to listen to again.

I gripped the gunwale and watched the river gather speed beneath us, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets toward the white water, and I thought: *the map never lies, Astrid. People lie. Maps just repeat what the earth already said.*

The earth, just then, was saying *rapids*.

I only hoped we were both listening.
