## 5. The First Rapids

The river told you things before it showed them. That was the first lesson of the Coldwater, and I had learned it the way most men learn anything worth knowing — by paying for the mistake. A change in the smell of the water, green and mineral where it had been warm and silted. A new note in its voice, lower, like a man clearing his throat before he says something you won't like. The birds lifting off the water in a single sheet instead of drifting up in twos and threes.

By midmorning of the third day, the river was telling me everything at once.

I stood at the tiller with my boots braced against the wet planking and watched the valley narrow ahead of us into a throat of grey granite. The jungle pressed down to the banks on both sides, dripping from the night's condensation, and through the gap in the hills came a sound I didn't need a map to interpret: white water, a long stretch of it, the river dropping through rock shelves like a staircase built by something that hated boats.

"Marguerite." Astrid was crouched amidships, retying the tarp over the cargo. She'd learned to do it my way by now — diagonal lashings, never square knots, everything cinched twice. "What's that noise?"

"That," I said, "is why I wanted to leave yesterday."

She looked up. She had river mud dried along one forearm and her hair tied back with a length of paracord she'd found in my kit without asking, which should have annoyed me and somehow hadn't. "Can we run it?"

I let the question sit while I read the water. The entry channel was visible from here — a tongue of smooth green between two boulders, maybe four meters wide, narrowing fast. Below it the river vanished into spray and standing waves. There might be an eddy partway down where a boat could pull out and line through the worst of it. Might be. The last survey chart I'd seen of this reach was eleven years old, drawn by a government hydrologist who had probably never run it in anything heavier than a folding canoe.

"There's a line," I said. "Maybe."

"Maybe is better than no."

"Maybe is how people drown." I said it flatly, not unkindly, and she took it the way she'd learned to take most of what I said — as information rather than argument. That was the thing about Astrid Vane I was still refusing to admire: she treated fear like weather. You noted it, you dressed for it, you went out anyway.

We ate cold rice and smoked fish with our eyes on the water, because there was no sense pretending we weren't going to run it. The Kestrel sat low in the current, riding heavy with the supplies — diesel, rice, medical crates, radio batteries, the whole brittle economy of a camp that had gone silent. Half of it belonged to the broker in Port Meridian; half of it, technically, was now ballast for a woman who had paid triple passage and never once explained why triple was necessary. I'd stopped asking. Some questions you save for calmer water.

Afterward I talked her through the plan while we stripped the boat to essentials. Everything not lashed went ashore — my duffel, the spare fuel, the tool roll, wrapped in the tarp and cached above the high-water mark under a cairn of stones. We'd come back for it if the boat lived. If the boat died, we'd want our legs free anyway.

"You'll take the bow line," I said, coiling rope and pressing it into her hands. "When I shout *line*, you get out on the left bank, fast, and you hold this. Don't wrap it around your wrist. Don't wrap it around anything. If the boat comes at you, drop it and step back. The boat's replaceable."

"And you're not?"

"I'm insured," I said, and she almost smiled, and I turned away before either of us could make more of it than that.

* * *

The entry channel took us clean. For about ninety seconds I let myself believe the rest would go the same way — the bow rising, the green water peeling away beneath us, the first standing wave hitting the hull with a slap like a door slammed by wind. Then the second wave hit differently. Harder, and from underneath, and I heard the sound every boatman knows and prays never to hear again: not wood flexing, but wood giving.

The patch. The seam I'd caulked at the dock with resin and canvas and the particular optimism of a man who couldn't afford a proper yard. It opened along a hand's width, then a forearm's width, and the Coldwater came in like it had been waiting five years for the invitation.

"Line!" I shouted. "*Line!*"

Astrid moved. I'll say that for her until the day I die — she moved. No hesitation, no looking back at me for permission, no wasted motion. She had the bow line in her fist and she was over the side into thigh-deep water before the Kestrel finished its slow, sickening wallow toward the left bank, hauling against the current with her whole body angled low, boots finding purchase on wet stone the way I'd seen porters find it and tourists never do.

I fought the tiller. The river fought me harder. Water to my knees inside the hull already, cargo shifting, a crate of radio batteries grinding against the gunwale like a tooth working loose. The current wanted us broadside, and broadside meant swamping, and swamping in this reach meant the rocks downstream would have us in pieces before we finished drowning.

The Kestrel swung. I felt the stern come round, felt the river take the boat the way a strong man takes a child's wrist — not cruelly, just completely — and then the line went taut and the world changed.

Astrid held.

Not gracefully. Not the way they draw it in the illustrations, feet planted, rope singing. She held the ugly way, the real way — dropped to one knee on the bank, rope across her shoulder, taking the load on her skeleton instead of her arms, walking backward step by step against two tons of boat and current, mud sliding under her boots, breath coming in ragged shouts I couldn't parse over the water. And the Kestrel, feeling that anchor, swung back. Bow downstream. Into the current's good graces.

"Eddy!" I roared, pointing left, where behind a granite fin the water curled back on itself in a slack pocket no wider than the boat. "Left! Hold it — *hold*—"

She held. God help me, she held. I threw my weight on the tiller, engine screaming in that thin choked note engines make when they're asked for everything, and the Kestrel slid sideways into the eddy like a drunk finding a doorway, and the roar of the main channel fell away behind us as if someone had shut a door on it.

Silence, after. The strange, ringing silence of a place where the river has been shouting for hours. Water sloshed at my shins. The crate of batteries floated past my elbow, serene as a swan.

I looked at the bank. Astrid was still on one knee in the shallows, the bow line slack in her hands now, chest heaving, mud to her waist, a red scrape running down one shin that she plainly hadn't noticed. She looked up at me, and her face did something complicated — relief and fury and disbelief all trying to use the same muscles at once.

"The seam," she said, when she had breath for it. "It opened."

"It opened."

"How bad?"

I lifted the floorboard and looked into the bilge with the honesty the moment deserved. The split ran along three planks, the caulking shredded, one frame cracked where the hull had kissed a submerged rock somewhere in that first chaos. Bad. Not sinking-in-open-water bad. Sinking-anywhere-else bad.

"We beach her," I said. "Here. We camp, we strip the hull, we patch it properly. Two days, if the weather holds."

"The weather isn't going to hold." She said it quietly, looking upstream at the sky, and I followed her gaze and hated what I saw. Clouds stacking over the western ridge in towers, blue-grey at the base, white and burning at the tops. Monsoon clouds. They didn't mean rain today or tomorrow. They meant the season turning, the rivers rising, every crossing and rapid and shallow ford between here and Coldwater camp becoming a different animal entirely.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

We beached the Kestrel in the lee of the granite fin, hauled her up on rollers cut from deadfall, and got to work. This was the part I knew how to do with my eyes closed — I'd repaired worse boats in worse places, sometimes alone, sometimes bleeding — and for the first hour it was almost restful, in the way that familiar labor is restful. Strip the caulking. Dry the seams with a spirit flame. Open the crack wide enough to fill it, which feels like destruction and is actually repair. Mix resin. Cut canvas.

Astrid worked beside me without being asked. She handed tools before I reached for them, learned the resin mix on the first batch, and when I sent her to gather dry moss for packing the widest gap she came back with exactly the right kind, having apparently identified it by some private taxonomy of her own.

"You've done this before," I said. It wasn't a question.

"My father kept a boat." She knelt to press canvas into the seam, her fingers quick and sure, resin webbing between them in pale strings. "On Lake Verany, when I was small. He used to say a boat is just a promise you keep making to the water. Every season. Every seam." She paused. "He stopped keeping promises around the time I turned fourteen."

I let that lie on the ground between us. There were things a man could pick up and things he shouldn't, and other people's griefs were mostly the latter unless offered.

"He taught you the rope work," I said instead. "On the bank. Nobody learns to take a load on their shoulder like that from a book."

Something moved through her face — surprise, then something warmer and more guarded. "He taught me that falling in the water is embarrassing but survivable, and letting go of the boat is neither." She wiped her hands on her trousers, leaving grey streaks. "You shouted at me to drop the line if it came at me. He used to say the same thing. Word for word, almost."

"People who work rivers say the same things," I said. "There aren't many ways to stay alive on one."

"No," she said, watching me with those level grey eyes that saw too much and said too little. "I don't suppose there are."

We worked through the afternoon. The resin cured under a tarp while the stacked clouds climbed higher and the light went the color of old brass. We lost the light early under the storm's shadow, and by the time we'd eaten, the first far-off thunder was talking to itself beyond the ridge, a long rolling conversation we weren't invited to join.

* * *

I woke in the dark to the sound of Astrid crying.

Not loudly. Not even audibly, really — just the small involuntary catch of breath that a person makes when they think sleep has made them safe. She was sitting against the overturned hull, knees drawn up, and in the dim glow of the covered lantern I could see her holding something: a photograph, bent soft at the corners from handling. A man with her same level eyes and a younger version of her own stubborn mouth, squinting into sun on some dock or other, one hand raised half-wave, half-salute.

I should have stayed asleep. It would have been kinder, and kinder was a currency I'd been hoarding for five years.

"Elias Vane," I said quietly.

She didn't startle. She folded the photograph away with the composure of someone who had decided, sometime in the last thirty seconds, to stop pretending. "Six weeks," she said. "His letters came every ten days, like clockwork, since the expedition started. Then nothing. The company says he's moved camps. The company always says he's moved camps." She looked at me across the dead fire. "You've heard of Coldwater Mining. Everyone's heard of it. Do you know who owns it?"

"Some consortium," I said. "Port Meridian money."

"Meridian Consolidated," she said. "My father staked that claim twenty years ago. Before they bought him out, before they bought everything. And now he works for them, and now he's silent, and now the only supply boat willing to run the river belongs to a guide whose name makes brokers flinch." Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about it. "I paid you triple because you were the only one who'd go, and I paid it because I thought you might understand what it costs to be erased."

The thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first fat drops began ticking against the tarp over the hull.

"Astrid," I said.

"Don't," she said. "Don't tell me it's nothing. You read that radio static the same as I did. You knew before I said a word that something was wrong upriver, and you kept poling anyway." She stood, mud-stiffened clothes cracking, and looked down at me with an expression I'd spend the next several chapters of my life trying to name. "So whatever you did, whatever they say you did — I don't care tonight. Tonight I care that you know how to fix a hull and I know how to hold a line, and tomorrow we're going to need both."

She lay down on her bedroll with her back to me, and the rain came properly at last, drumming on the tarp, filling the dark with the sound of the season changing.

I lay awake a long time, listening to it, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the dock. Two days' travel to Coldwater camp, if the patched hull held. Rivers rising behind us and ahead. A woman asleep three meters away who had just told me, in the language of people who don't repeat themselves, that she intended to see this through to whatever end the river offered.

And somewhere upstream, in the country the clouds were swallowing, a camp that answered no one, and a ledger of names I wouldn't read for another two days — one of them struck through, though I didn't know it yet, in ink that had dried long before we ever launched.

The rain fell. The river rose, a finger's width, then another. And the Kestrel, patched and repatched, promise renewed to the water, rode her rollers in the dark like a held breath.
