## 6. Camp Ashfall

**Marguerite**

The river told them before they saw it.

He'd learned to read water the way other men read faces, and for the last half mile the Coldwater had been carrying things it shouldn't: a sliver of charred wood, soft as bread; a curl of tarpaulin gone gray and weightless; a smell that didn't belong to any living thing on the river—burnt diesel and burnt plastic and underneath both, faint as a memory, the sweetness of burned canvas.

He cut the engine and let the Kestrel drift.

"What is it?" Astrid asked. She'd been dozing against the gunwale with her father's map folded in her lap, and she came awake the way she did everything—abruptly, entirely, like a match struck.

"Smell," he said.

She lifted her chin. He watched her take it in, watched her understand it, watched her refuse to understand it. Those were three separate moments and he would remember all of them.

"That's not—" she started.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

He restarted the engine and took them around the last bend slow, with the current doing most of the work, and there it was above the cutbank where the survey maps had promised it: Camp Ashfall. That was what the manifest called it. Someone at Meridian Consolidated had named it years ago, back when ash was only something a cookstove made, and now the name had come true like a prophecy nobody wanted.

The camp sat on a gravel terrace maybe forty meters above the waterline—a cluster of canvas wall tents, a corrugated cook shelter, a radio mast leaning at an angle no mast should lean, its guy wires snapped and trailing in the wind like hair in water. Or it had been those things. Now it was their skeletons. The tents stood but the canvas was scorched lace; through the door flaps he could see sky. The cook shelter's roof had collapsed into itself, a black flower pressed flat. Everything wore the same color, that particular gray-brown of things that had been alive with heat and weren't anymore.

No smoke. Whatever had happened here had happened days ago. Maybe weeks.

"Radio mast's down," Marguerite said, because saying something practical was better than saying nothing while his chest did what it was doing. "That explains the static."

Astrid didn't answer. She was standing at the bow with one hand on the rail, and her face had gone very still—not blank, nothing so merciful as blank—and he thought: *she's counting*. He knew the arithmetic. He'd done it himself at Karst Ridge, standing in the rain while men counted heads and came up short. You count the tents. You count the boats at the bank. You count the places where people should be.

There were two boats at the bank, both burned to the waterline, both full of rainwater and ash.

"Nobody left by water," Astrid said quietly.

"Nobody left," Marguerite said, "by anything we can see."

* * *

**Astrid**

She made herself step onto the gravel before she let herself think about it, because thinking would stop her legs and her legs were the only part of her still obeying orders.

The ash came up between her boot treads like dirty snow. It got into everything immediately—instantly, greedily—the creases of her sleeves, the collar of her jacket, the wet corners of her eyes. Six weeks. Elias Vane had been silent for six weeks, and this camp had been dead longer than that, long enough for the rain to wash the ash into gray paste and for green shoots to be coming up through it, absurd and obscene, little flags of life planted in the ruin.

"Dad?" she called, and her voice cracked apart on the second letter and she hated herself for it and called again anyway. "Dad! It's Astrid!"

The camp said nothing. The wind moved through the burned tents and made them whisper.

Marguerite appeared beside her without seeming to have crossed the distance, which was a thing he did, a way he had of arriving already present. He put two fingers to his lips and shook his head—not *don't call*, exactly. More like: *let me listen first.*

They listened. The river below. Wind in a stand of alders, still green, spared by whatever fire had stayed small enough to die. Somewhere upslope, a bird making a sound like a hinge.

"Four tents," Marguerite said finally, low. "Cook shelter. Equipment shed. Latrine trench behind that screen of brush. Crew of six, if the manifest ran standard." He was reading the camp the way she'd seen him read the rapids, top to bottom, fast and total. "Fire started here." He pointed to the cook shelter. "Diesel, by the smell. Poured, not spilled—see how the burn is even along the sill? Then it jumped to the near tent, and someone fought it there—" he indicated a trampled arc in the ash, a blackened blanket half-buried, "—and lost, and after that it went where it went."

"You can tell all that from—"

"I've set fires and I've put them out and I've stood in the middle of ones I couldn't do either to." His voice was flat as the gravel. "Poured diesel means people did this who wanted it thorough. Thorough people usually also want the equipment."

She looked then, really looked, and saw what the ash had been hiding from her. No crates. No core boxes. No drill steel, no sample sacks, no theodolite case, no fuel drums—only the pale rectangles in the ground where drums had sat, printed in dead grass like photographs of themselves. The shed door hung open on empty shelving. Even the nails had been pulled from one wall section, pried out whole, the kind of salvage you only bother with when you're not in a hurry and not worried about being seen.

"They took everything," she said.

"They took everything worth carrying," Marguerite corrected. "And they burned what was worth erasing."

*Erasing.* The word went through her like a fishhook. Because paper burns. Names burn. And her father had been a man made almost entirely of paper—maps, permits, field reports, letters home that arrived late or never.

She turned toward the nearest tent, the one with the fight-marked ground before it, and made herself go in.

Inside, the world was gray. The cot frames remained, steel legs splayed where the canvas had shrunk and twisted them. A boot lay on its side, sole up, unburned, patient. On the floor where a footlocker had been: a rectangle of less-gray, a shadow of itself.

And in the corner, half under the collapsed bunk board, a book.

Not a notebook—those she'd check for later, she'd tear this whole camp apart for later—but a ledger. Hardbound, swollen with rain, its cover blistered. She knelt and worked it free and felt Marguerite come into the tent doorway behind her, blocking the light, then crouching to block it less.

"It's waterlogged," she said. Her hands were shaking. She made them stop. "It might—"

"Open it gently. Let it dry as it opens. Force it and the pages turn to pulp."

So they opened it together, page by page, like something being born, and most of it was mush, ink bloomed into blue clouds, columns dissolved into weather. But the last third of the book had been closed tight when the water hit, and those pages held.

Roster pages. Names in a surveyor's neat hand: crew members, dates, supply tallies. *J. Okonkwo — cook. R. Serrano — driller. M. Adeyemi — chainman.* Six names. Beside each, entries in different ink, later, harder pressure: supply received, supply sent downriver, radio contact logged, radio contact missed.

And the seventh name, added in the same hard hand, out of order, at the bottom of the final roster page:

*E. Vane — expedition lead.*

Beside it, a single line drawn through the name. One stroke, ruled, deliberate, the kind of line you draw with a straightedge when you are closing an account.

Astrid heard her own breathing, harsh and wrong in the small gray space.

"That's not what I think it is," she said.

Marguerite was quiet a moment too long.

"A ledger line struck through means the account's closed," he said. "In supply books it means the item's issued out, written off, removed from inventory." He paused. "It doesn't have to mean what you're afraid of."

"But it could."

"Yes. It could."

She looked at the name. Her father's name, reduced to four characters and a line, filed between a tally of diesel drums and a note about a broken winch cable. Six weeks ago he'd taught her to read contour lines on his knee, twenty-some years ago, the close-spaced ones meaning steep country, the far-apart ones meaning easy walking, *and always, Astrid, the map is not the territory, the map is just what somebody bothered to write down.*

Somebody had bothered to write this down.

"We keep it," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "The ledger comes with us."

* * *

**Marguerite**

He let her carry the ledger. There was no argument worth having about it and every argument worth having about what it meant, and he chose silence, which was its own kind of cowardice, and he knew it, and did it anyway.

What he did instead was work. Work was the shape his fear took; it had been for five years. He walked the camp's perimeter and read it like a page, because that was the only scripture he trusted anymore.

The story the ground told was simple and it was bad. Vehicle tracks—two vehicles, heavy tires, coming in from the old logging spur to the north, standing deep in soft ground, leaving loaded. Boot prints everywhere, too many, overlapping, days old at least, older than the last rain. Drag marks from the cook shelter toward the treeline, shallow ones, the kind a body makes when it isn't walking under its own will—or supplies, he amended, forcing himself, supplies get dragged too—but the drag marks ended at the trees and there was no pile of supplies anywhere in this camp, and no graves, and no bodies, and somehow the absence of bodies was worse than bodies would have been. Bodies you could bury. Bodies you could grieve properly, with a shape and a number. This was grief with the center cut out of it.

Six men. Elias Vane. Seven names, seven struck-through accounts, and not one of them lying in the ash where a fire like this should have left them.

Which meant either everyone had walked out—and then why the poured diesel, why the stripped sheds, why the drag marks—

Or everyone had been carried.

He found Astrid at the edge of the terrace, looking north along the logging spur, where the trees stood aside for a road that hadn't seen honest traffic in a decade. The light was going the color of tin. Rain was coming—he could feel it in his bad knee and in the barometric weight of the sky, the monsoon gathering itself somewhere over the high country like a debt coming due.

"The tracks head inland," he said. "Toward the divide. Toward the old claim staked in your father's filings, if I'm reading the manifest right."

"I know where they head." She didn't look at him. She was holding the ledger against her chest with both arms, the way you hold a thing you're afraid will be taken. "I've been reading his maps since I was six years old, Marguerite. I know exactly where they head."

"Then you know what I'm going to say. Weather's turning. We've lost half our cargo in the rapids, the Kestrel needs her seams looked at, and this camp is a grave with the bodies stolen out of it. The smart move is downriver. Report it. Come back with—"

"With who?" Now she turned, and her eyes had a brightness in them that frightened him more than tears would have. "Report it to whom, Marguerite? Name the office. Name the man whose desk it lands on. My father wrote to every office there is for two years and got form letters. Whoever did this owns the offices. Downriver is where they *want* us to go—back to the towns, back to the paperwork, back to the place where this becomes a file number and closes quietly, like everything else closes."

"And inland is where they want you to follow," he said. "Drag marks can be laid. Tracks can be made. Anyone thorough enough to pour diesel in a cook shelter is thorough enough to leave a trail they'd like walked."

The words came out colder than he intended, and he saw them land, and he didn't take them back, because they were true, and because some stubborn drowned thing in him believed that truth was the only thing he had left to offer anyone.

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. The wind moved the ash at their feet in little gray breaths.

"Maybe," she said. "Maybe the trail is bait. But my father's name is in this book with a line through it, and there are six other names, and none of them are in the ground here, and the monsoon is three days out, maybe four. So here's what I know." She held up the ledger. "Paper survives. That's the whole lesson of my life—people vanish and paper stays. Somebody wrote these names down expecting them to matter. I am going to make them matter."

She turned and walked toward the ruined cook shelter, and after a moment he followed, because he always followed, because following her had become over nine days on the river a habit his body had formed without asking his permission.

* * *

**Astrid**

The cook shelter's floor was a mosaic of ash and broken glass and melted aluminum, things fused into shapes that had never been manufactured. She sifted with a tent stake, gently, the way her father had taught her to excavate a test pit: *layers tell the truth, Astrid. Fire lies, water lies, but layers don't know how.*

Under the collapsed shelf where the radio had lived, she found the survivors.

Paper doesn't all burn equal. Tucked against the cold ground beneath a fallen sheet of roofing iron, shielded by the accident of architecture, a folder had half-made it. Most was carbon. But one page—one, caught at the fold where the metal met the dirt—had kept its ink.

It was a supply ledger page, torn out, the stub still visible in the waterlogged book she carried. Standard columns. But running down the margin, in her father's handwriting—she knew it the way you know a voice, the particular forward lean of his *E*, the way he crossed his sevens—was a list of names with numbers after them. Not crew names. Other names. Company names, concession numbers, parcel references, and beside each, in shorthand she didn't fully understand but understood enough: figures that should have matched and didn't. Boundary coordinates that disagreed with boundary coordinates. Acreages that grew between one filing and the next like things fed in the dark.

At the bottom of the margin, underlined twice:

*Ask Delphine O. re: original Karst Ridge exhibits — E.V.*

Marguerite read it over her shoulder. She felt him go still, the particular stillness of a man hearing his own name spoken in a room he thought was empty.

"Delphine Okafor," he said, very quietly. "Court clerk. Regional assizes." A pause. "She handled the Karst Ridge inquiry."

He said nothing else. But something moved behind his face, some door opening onto a corridor she wasn't shown, and she marked it—filed it away the way he'd taught her without knowing to file things, the way you watch a client on a mountain and learn which silences are tiredness and which are load-bearing.

"My father knew yours," she said. "Or knew *of* him. Enough to trust the name."

"Enough to write it down." Marguerite straightened. He looked north, up the logging spur, where the first true drops of the coming rain were starting to stipple the dust, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—flatter, harder, the guide's voice, the voice that made decisions. "Rain inside the hour. We can't hike that road tonight and we can't stay in these tents. There's an overhang I passed on the approach, half a mile back downriver, limestone, dry floor. We take the ledger, the folder, the boot—"

"The boot?"

"Evidence walks out with us. All of it. If this camp is going to be erased, it gets erased *after* someone official has looked at it, not before." He was already moving, gathering a tarp, tearing a strip of clean-ish canvas to wrap the documents. "We come back with the assizes behind us. With names of our own."

Astrid stood a moment longer in the doorway of the burned shelter, looking at the gray world of it, at the little green weeds pushing up through the ash, at the rectangle shadows where men had slept and weren't sleeping now.

*Dad,* she thought. *Wherever you are—walked out, carried out, hiding, held—I'm coming up that road. The map is not the territory. But I'm coming anyway.*

Then she wrapped the ledger in oilcloth, tucked the folder inside her coat against her heart, and followed Marguerite down through the first fat drops of rain toward the boat, toward the overhang, toward the darkening inland country where the tracks pointed like a compass needle that had finally stopped lying.

Behind them, Camp Ashfall gave up nothing else. It had already given the only things it had left: seven names, one line through them, and a page that refused to burn.

And north, under the stacking clouds, the logging spur climbed toward the divide, empty, waiting, patient as a question that had been asked six weeks ago and was about to be answered.
