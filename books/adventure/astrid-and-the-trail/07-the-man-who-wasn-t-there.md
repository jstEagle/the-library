## 7. The Man Who Wasn't There

The ash had a smell I would carry for the rest of my life.

Not woodsmoke—woodsmoke is honest, domestic, the smell of someone's supper. This was different: diesel and polymer and scorched canvas, the smell of equipment that had been *made* to burn, doused in something to make it burn hotter, faster, more completely. Someone had wanted this camp gone so thoroughly that they'd helped it along.

I crouched at the edge of what had been the mess tent and pressed my palm flat against the ground. Still warm. Not hot—but warm the way a stove is warm an hour after the fire dies, warmth held in the earth itself like a secret it can't quite keep.

"Yesterday," Marguerite said behind me. He'd been reading the ground the way other men read newspapers, walking the perimeter with his hands loose at his sides, his eyes doing all the work. "Maybe eighteen hours. Rain would have washed more of this away if it had been longer."

I didn't answer him. I was looking at a spoon.

It lay half-melted into the ash, its bowl warped into something like a lily, and I knew it—I knew it the way you know your own front door. My father had carried that spoon for twenty years. He'd taken it from a mess hall in Ushuaia on his first survey job, stolen it as a joke, and then kept it out of superstition, because the first job had gone well and he was not a man who argued with luck. He'd eat his breakfast with it and tap it twice against the rim of his cup when the coffee was good. *Twice for yes,* he used to say. *Never once. Once is for no.*

The spoon was here. Elias Vane was not.

That was the arithmetic I couldn't stop running. If the camp had burned with everyone inside it, there would be bodies. Marguerite said so without saying so, in the careful way he moved around the question, giving it room. Six men in the Coldwater survey crew. Six names on the manifest I'd found folded in my father's study six weeks ago, when the silence had finally grown loud enough that I stopped pretending it was just Elias being Elias. No bones in the ash. No teeth, which is what fire leaves even when it takes everything else. The rain barrels were intact. The radio mast was down but the radio itself was gone—not destroyed, *gone*, carried off or hidden, and who burns a camp and then steals the radio?

Unless there was nothing left to say on it.

No. I put the thought down the way you put down something with wet hands, carefully, before it slips and breaks.

*He got out. He always gets out.*

* * *

I searched the way he taught me to search, which is to say I searched badly first and then properly.

When I was nine, he'd lost a brass compass somewhere along a ridge above our house, and instead of letting me sulk about the lost afternoon he'd turned it into a lesson. *You don't look for the thing,* he'd said, squatting in the ferns, his gray eyes bright with the particular joy he took in method. *You look for where the thing isn't. You divide the world into places it could be and places it couldn't, and you kill them one by one.* He'd made me walk a grid over the whole hillside, ten meters at a time, until dusk, until I was crying with frustration, until—just as the light went copper—the compass caught the sun under a huckleberry bush and flashed like a signal.

*There,* he'd said, and hugged me hard enough to crack ribs. *Now you'll never lose anything again.*

I walked a grid through the ashes of his camp.

The sleeping quarters first. Six tent platforms, steel frames mostly unburned, the canvas gone to lace. I turned over each frame. Under the third one, wedged against a cross-brace where a man might shove something in a hurry, I found a tin of tobacco—empty—and beneath the tin, flattened into the mud, a boot print. Large. Left foot. The heel worn on the outside edge, the way a man's wears when he walks with his weight rolled outward, the way my father walked, the way he'd walked since the old knee injury in '09 that he never told my mother the full story of.

I knelt over that print for a long time. The mud had dried around it, holding it the way amber holds a fly. It could have been weeks old. It could have been hours. Mud doesn't testify; it only suggests.

*Twice for yes,* I thought, and hated myself for it, because hope is not evidence and I knew that better than anyone alive. Hope was the thing that had kept me writing letters to a father who answered maybe one in five. Hope was the thing that had let six weeks of silence become seven without my calling anyone, because calling someone would make it real, would turn absence into emergency, emergency into grief. Hope was a tool, and like every tool in that burned camp, someone had gotten to it first and bent it out of true.

I stood up. My knees were black to the thigh. Somewhere behind me Marguerite was still working the perimeter, silent as weather.

* * *

By mid-afternoon I had catalogued the camp the way an archaeologist catalogues a tomb, and the inventory told a story I didn't like.

Gone: the radio. Gone: the survey instruments—all of them, theodolite, levels, the laser rangefinder that cost more than my car. Gone: the sample crates, or rather, the samples; the empty crates themselves were stacked neatly by the riverbank, which was somehow worse than if they'd burned. Gone: the field ledgers, though their shelf remained, and the dust-shadow of their spines was printed on the wood like a photograph of a book.

Left: everything replaceable. The cookware. The cots. A crate of tinned peaches, unopened, labels blistered but legible. A deck of cards scattered across a platform, face-down, mid-game, as if the players had simply stood up and walked into the trees.

Nobody packs cards when they're fleeing. Nobody stacks crates neatly when they're running. And nobody—if they're murderers—leaves a game unfinished unless the men who were playing it left first, alive, under orders, carrying exactly the things worth carrying and nothing else.

"They were evacuated," I said to Marguerite. He'd come to stand at the edge of my grid, arms crossed, watching me work with an expression I was beginning to recognize as respect wearing disguise. "Or relocated. Or—" I stopped, because the next word was *arrested*, and I wasn't ready for it.

"Or marched," he said quietly.

I looked at him.

"It happens," he said. "Camps like this. Companies move crews when a site goes quiet, and sometimes the crew doesn't get a vote." He crouched, picked up one of the face-down cards—a seven of hearts—and set it back exactly where it had lain, which struck me as either tenderness or superstition. "But evacuation doesn't explain the burn."

"No," I agreed. "Evacuation doesn't explain the burn."

We looked at each other across the ruin, and I understood that we were both thinking the same ugly thought and neither of us wanted to say it first: you burn a camp to destroy *evidence*. You don't burn it to hide a kidnapping. Kidnappings want silence, not spectacle. Fire draws eyes. Fire brings rangers, police, questions. Unless—

Unless the fire wasn't meant to hide anything. Unless it was meant to say something. Unless it was a message written in the only alphabet that needs no translation: *this place is closed, this claim is dead, stop looking.*

And if it was a message, then it had been sent by someone who expected people to come looking.

Someone like a daughter.

* * *

Marguerite found the prints at dusk.

I heard him call my name—actually my name, not "Vane" or "the girl" or the various wordless noises he usually substituted for address—and the sound of it pulled me up the slope behind the camp faster than any alarm. He was standing at the treeline where the ground rose toward the valley's throat, and he was crouched again, and beside his own boot, deliberate as a signature, was another print.

Large. Left foot. Heel worn on the outside edge.

"There's a trail," he said. His voice had changed—flattened, professional, the voice of a man reporting facts because feelings would only slow him down. "Faint. They tried to brush it, but whoever did it was in a hurry and the ground's soft after the rain two nights back. Single file. Six, maybe seven sets, heading northeast." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to give me the rest, and watched him decide to give it anyway. "One set drags. Right leg. Consistent drag, same depth, every stride."

My father's right knee. The injury in '09. The limp he hid on video calls by sitting at a desk, the limp my mother used to tease him about, *you walk like a man following his own funeral*, the limp that meant—my chest closed around the thought and wouldn't open—meant he was *alive*. Dead men don't leave dragging prints. Corpses don't walk in single file up a valley. Whatever else had happened at Coldwater camp, Elias Vane had stood up from a card game and walked into the mountains under his own power, limping, alive.

I put my hand against a tree trunk because the world tilted slightly and the tree was kind enough to hold still.

"Northeast," I said, when I could speak. "What's northeast?"

Marguerite was quiet long enough that I knew the answer before he gave it, knew it the way you know thunder before the flash finishes arriving.

"The old claim," he said. "Vane's original stake. What's left of it."

* * *

We stood at the treeline as the light failed, and I made myself do the thing my father taught me, the killing of possibilities one by one, and discovered that hope is the hardest possibility to kill because it keeps resurrecting under different names.

*He escaped.* Then why single file? Escaped men scatter.

*He was taken.* Then why burn the camp afterward, drawing attention to a site you've already emptied?

*He's leading them somewhere.* And there it was, the thought I'd been circling all day, the one with teeth. Northeast. Toward the claim. Toward whatever my father had found out here that was worth burning a camp over, worth silencing a radio, worth marching six men into the high country in the last dry week before the monsoon. If Elias had found proof of something—fraud, forgery, theft dressed as geology—then the claim site was the one place on earth where that proof lived. Original stakes. Original markers. Ground truth, literally.

Which meant my father hadn't been evacuated or arrested or murdered.

He'd been *escorted*. Marched inland to show them where he'd buried the body of their crime—or to dig it up for them, or to watch them dig it up and burn it, and then—

I stopped. I made myself stop. Because past that point the possibilities stopped being possibilities and became a corridor, and every door in it opened onto the same room, and I was not going to stand in that room tonight.

"He's alive," I said aloud, to the darkening trees, to Marguerite, to myself, and it came out not as hope but as a verdict, and I realized as I said it that I had stopped hoping somewhere in the last hour and started *intending*, and that the difference between those two things is the difference between weather and a blade.

Marguerite looked at me sideways. In the last light his face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, a contour map of a man. "Alive," he repeated, testing the word.

"You saw the prints."

"I saw prints."

"You know what a dragged heel means. You've guided long enough to read a story in mud."

"I've also read stories in mud that turned out to be bears," he said. But he didn't look away, and after a moment he added, lower, almost grudging, "It's a good trail. Fresh. Whoever brushed it missed the soft ground below the seep line. Amateurs." He straightened, rolling his shoulders the way he did when a decision was loading behind his eyes. "They're a day ahead of us. Maybe less. The monsoon will close the high passes in three days, four if we're lucky, and after that nobody moves in that valley until November."

"Then we don't wait for November."

"I wasn't suggesting we wait." He turned to look up-valley, where the ridgeline had gone the color of iron and the first stars were pricking through above the pass. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something I hadn't heard from him before—not the brokered calm of the dock, not the flat competence of the rapids, but something older and wearier and more awake. "I'm telling you what it costs. That trail goes to the claim, and the claim is why there's ash down there instead of a camp. Whoever burned it may still be up there. And if your father is up there, he's up there because someone wants something from him, and people who want things from other people in country like this—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening, and finished differently than he'd begun. "We go light. We go quiet. And we go now, while the trail's still talking."

I went back into the camp once, alone, while he broke down our gear by feel in the blue dark. I found the spoon in the ash where I'd left it, wiped it clean against my sleeve, and put it in my breast pocket, over my heart, where it sat small and warm and bent.

*Twice for yes,* I thought, and tapped it twice with my fingernail, and did not let myself hear the answer.

Above us, in the pass, something moved—a shape against the last gray band of sky, brief and upright and gone. Marguerite saw it too. Neither of us said anything. We shouldered our packs and followed the dragged heel into the trees, and the mountain closed behind us like water closing over a stone.
