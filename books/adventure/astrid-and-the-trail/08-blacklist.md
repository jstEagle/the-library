## 8. Blacklist

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Astrid asked the question I'd been waiting five years for someone to ask.

We'd made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder where the river bent north, out of the worst of the wind. The Kestrel sat beached on the shingle above the waterline, hull propped on driftwood chocks while I re-tensioned the patch I'd sewn into her below the waterline after the rapids. My fingers still smelled of pitch and cold rubber. Above us the sky was doing what it had been doing all evening—stacking itself into layers of slate and bruise, the first real argument between the dry season and the monsoon.

Astrid sat cross-legged on her bedroll with the ledger page from Camp Ashfall spread flat on a slab of rock, weighted at the corners with four stones she'd chosen with the same care her father must have taught her for choosing anchors. She'd been staring at it for an hour. Elias Vane's name, struck through in someone's impatient hand, stared back at both of us.

"You knew them," she said. Not a question, really. "The people at Coldwater. When you took the job. You knew something."

I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"I knew the name," I said. "Meridian Consolidated. It was on the broker's paperwork, buried in the middle of a paragraph nobody reads. I read it anyway. People like me always read the fine print."

"People like you." She turned her head, firelight moving across the sharp planes of her face. "Which is what, exactly?"

And there it was. The door, standing open, the way doors do when you've been avoiding them so long that avoidance becomes its own kind of invitation.

I could have lied. I'd gotten good at lying—not the elaborate kind, which takes imagination, but the small kind, the omissions, the half-answers, the practiced shrug that says *nothing interesting here*. For five years I'd been a man made entirely of small lies, a guide who led tourists up forgettable trails and counted distances instead of days because distances couldn't ask questions.

But we were two days from anywhere, committed to a river that was about to turn against us, carrying a dead man's ledger page and following boot prints inland toward a claim that had gotten people killed. If the monsoon caught us, or if Meridian's men found us—and I'd been watching the ridgelines all evening, the way you watch a wound—you would need to know exactly who was standing next to you when things went wrong. Not the version of me that existed on paper. The whole ugly inventory.

"Karst Ridge," I said.

She waited. She was good at waiting. I'd noticed that about her somewhere around the second set of rapids—that she could hold silence the way other people held a rope, with weight on it, trusting it to bear.

"Five years ago I was the senior guide on a high-alpine crossing operation. Karst Ridge, in the northern ranges—a limestone massif, all sinkholes and underground rivers, the kind of country where the ground lies to you. We were moving a client party across a gorge on a fixed-line traverse. Tyrolean crossing over a hundred meters of nothing. There were six clients. One of them—" I stopped. Started again. "One of them was family."

"Your brother-in-law," Astrid said quietly.

My head came up. "How did you—"

"You said 'family,' not 'a client.' Nobody says 'family' about a stranger unless they're about to explain why they have to." She pulled her jacket tighter around her shoulders. "Go on. I won't interrupt again."

The stick in my hand had burned to charcoal. I dropped it into the fire.

"The crossing failed. The main anchor—the one anchored into what every survey said was solid limestone—pulled out. Not frayed, not cut. *Pulled out*, like the mountain itself had decided to let go. Daniel—my brother-in-law—was mid-span when it went. He fell maybe sixty meters before the backup line caught him, except the backup line was rigged off the same anchor system, because that's how the engineering had been approved, because redundancy on paper isn't redundancy in rock. The shock load stripped the secondary anchors one after another. I was at the near station. I had his rope in my hands."

The fire popped. Somewhere downriver, a fish jumped, a small silver slap against the dark.

"There are two versions of what happened next," I said. "The official version and mine. They're almost the same story, which is the cruelest part. In both versions, the anchor fails, Daniel falls, and I'm holding the tail end of his life line trying to arrest a load no human being can arrest. Sixty meters of fall, a man of ninety kilos plus gear—the physics don't care how much you love someone. The rope burns through your hands. You can feel it going, feel the sheath melting through your palms, and you know, you *know*, that in about four seconds he's going to hit the river, and there is nothing in the world you can do except hold on until holding on stops being possible."

Astrid had gone very still. Not the stillness of horror—the stillness of someone listening with their whole body.

"In my version," I said, "I held on until the rope tore through and he fell anyway, and I woke up three days later with third-degree burns and a broken wrist and a grief so large I couldn't find its edges. That's the version I told myself for a long time. It might even be true. But there's a moment—I've gone back to it ten thousand times—there's a moment where the pain became more than I could stand, and my hands opened, and I don't know whether they opened because the rope forced them or because some part of me chose to let go. Half a second. Maybe less. No inquiry in the world can weigh half a second."

"And the official version?"

"The official version didn't care about half a seconds. The official version cared that the crossing failed, that a client died, and that the senior guide on site had signed off on the anchor placement. Which I had. Based on surveys I didn't run myself—subcontracted geotechnical work, stamped and certified, the kind of paperwork you trust because trusting it is the entire architecture of the profession. The inquiry found operator error. My error. They never tested the anchor steel. Never audited the subcontractor. They took testimony for eleven days and issued a finding in nine pages, and my license was revoked administratively—no trial, no appeal hearing I could actually attend, just a letter and a blacklist entry that followed me across two borders like a smell." I looked at my hands, ghost-lit in the firelight. The burn scars had faded to pale ridges, but in certain light they still rose like topography. "You can't cross-examine a document, Astrid. That's the thing nobody tells you. A verdict doesn't have to be true. It only has to be filed."

"Who ran the inquiry?"

"A provincial safety board. Funded, advised, and quietly steered by—" I gestured at the ledger page on the rock, at the struck-through name. "Guess."

"Meridian."

"Meridian's subsidiary held the concession for the ridge development. A dead client and a disgraced guide made excellent cover for the fact that their certified subcontractor had been cutting corners on every anchor installation in the region. I found that out later—from a journalist who dug for six months and then stopped answering my letters. I've spent five years assuming the worst about why he stopped."

Astrid was quiet for a long time. The wind shifted, came down off the ridge carrying the green mineral smell of coming rain, and the fire leaned and recovered.

"Say the rest," she said finally. "The part you're not saying."

I almost laughed. "You don't miss much."

"My father taught me that mountains make people honest eventually. Or they kill them. Either way, the evasions stop." Her eyes were steady on me, dark and unblinking. "Say it."

So I said it. The part I'd never said aloud to anyone, the sentence that had lived in me like a splinter working toward the heart.

"When I picture that moment on the rope—and I picture it every day, every single day since—I can't tell anymore whether I remember my hands being torn open, or opening. And some mornings, the ones with fog in them, I wake up absolutely certain that I let go. That some exhausted, grieving, cowardly fraction of me did the arithmetic—*one man already falling, two hands beyond saving*—and let the rope go. And if that's true, then I didn't fail Daniel. I killed him. And everything since—the blacklist, the exile, the miles—it isn't injustice. It's just the bill arriving late."

The words came out flatter than they deserved, worn smooth by five years of silent repetition, like stones in a riverbed. Saying them aloud felt strangely anticlimactic, the way a wound looks smaller once it's open and bleeding.

Astrid didn't move for a long moment. Then she reached forward, picked up the kettle, and poured hot water over the last of the tea leaves into both our cups. She handed me mine. Our fingers touched on the tin, and neither of us hurried the touch.

"My mother died when I was nine," she said. "Not dramatically. A fever, a bad hospital, a slow month. Afterward my father couldn't look at me for almost a year, because I had her face, and every time he saw me he saw the day she died. I grew up being loved by a man who was also, in some locked room inside himself, blaming me for existing." She sipped her tea. "When I was fourteen I found his field notebooks from that year—all these beautiful contour maps, and in the margins, in tiny handwriting, notes to my mother. *Astrid lost a tooth today. She has your stubbornness.* He'd been writing to her the whole time. He just couldn't say any of it out loud." She looked at me over the rim of her cup. "People carry guilt that doesn't belong to them, Marguerite. It's practically a profession. What matters is what they do with the weight."

"That's very generous," I said. "It's also not an answer to whether I—"

"No. It isn't. Because there isn't an answer, and you know it, and torturing yourself with an unanswerable question for five years isn't penance, it's just a habit." She set her cup down. "But here's what I can tell you, and I can tell it as someone who's spent the last week learning how this company works. You said the anchor pulled out of rock that every survey called solid."

"Yes."

"And you said the subcontractor who certified those surveys was tied to Meridian."

"Yes."

"And tonight we found a burned camp, a stolen ledger, and a struck-through name belonging to a man who staked a claim Meridian wanted, whose survey crew went silent six weeks ago." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to burn, low and blue, like alcohol flame. "Marguerite. Your disaster wasn't five years ago. It's the same disaster. It's been running the whole time, up and down this valley, and you weren't its author—you were its first witness. They didn't blacklist you because you failed. They blacklisted you because you were standing too close to the truth, and a dead man's grieving brother-in-law makes a much tidier villain than a falsified geotechnical report."

The fire cracked. I sat with it—with the idea of it—turning it over the way you turn over a stone you've walked past a thousand times, afraid of what's underneath and more afraid that it's nothing.

"If that's true," I said slowly, "then the inquiry—"

"—wasn't justice. It was landscaping." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. Better. Both.

I looked at my scarred hands again, and for the first time in five years I looked at them without flinching. The ridges of healed burn crossed my palms like contour lines on one of Elias Vane's maps—elevation marks of a single terrible afternoon. I had read those lines as a confession for so long that I'd never thought to read them as evidence.

"You don't flinch," I said. "I just told you I might have killed a man with my own hands, and you're sitting there making theories."

"I told you I wouldn't interrupt." A ghost of a smile moved across her mouth and was gone. "Also, I watched you go back into white water for a crate of medical supplies that belonged to strangers. Whatever lives in the half-second on that rope, it isn't the whole of you. I've seen the rest of you. The rest of you holds on."

I had to look away then, out at the river, because there was a pressure behind my eyes that had no business appearing in front of a woman I'd known for eight days, and the dark water was easier to face than her face. The river ran black and patient under the bruised sky, carrying its cargo of rain-melt and secrets toward the coast, indifferent to everything except downhill.

"Thank you," I said, when I could trust my voice.

"Don't thank me yet." She nodded at the ledger page, at the boot prints we'd photographed at the camp's edge heading inland, at the stacked clouds. "If you're right about any of this—if Meridian burned Coldwater and took my father—then the men who ruined you are the same men who have him. And when we find them, you're going to have to decide what kind of witness you want to be. The invisible kind, or the kind that stands up in a courtroom and lets them try to destroy him twice." She held my gaze. "I'm not asking you to answer tonight. I'm telling you the question exists, so it can't ambush you later."

Fair. More than fair. I banked the fire, added wood for the night watch, and checked the Kestrel's lashings out of pure habit—hands busy while the mind worked. The patch was holding. The boat, like everything else, was a repair pretending to be a vessel.

Astrid rolled into her bedroll facing the fire. "Get some sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we leave the river."

I lay awake long after her breathing slowed, listening to the water and the wind and the first far-off mutter of thunder rolling down from the high valley like something clearing its throat. Boot prints heading inland. Fresh-cut wood. Someone living out there in the country my maps had stopped describing five years ago.

And beneath the thunder, faint but unmistakable, another sound: rain beginning to fall on the upper ridges, hours ahead of schedule, as if the monsoon had heard our plans and meant to race us for them.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. I was tightening the last lashing when Astrid stood abruptly at the treeline, one hand rising to point.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, human smoke—rising from the col where the boot prints had disappeared two days ago. Someone inland had seen our fire last night.

And had answered it.
