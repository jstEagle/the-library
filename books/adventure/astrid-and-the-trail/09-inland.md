## 9. Inland

### Marguerite

The Kestrel sat in the shallows like a thing already dead, and Marguerite could not look at her for long.

He had beached her at a gravel bar where the river bent north and the country folded upward into ridgelines the maps rendered as blank space—unclaimed, unsurveyed, or surveyed and then erased, which by now amounted to the same thing. The hull's wound was worse than he'd let himself admit on the water. He'd patched it twice since the rapids, and the second patch had held through forty kilometers of flat current out of pure stubbornness, but above the bend the river climbed into boulder gardens and standing waves that no caulking job in the world would survive.

"So we walk," Astrid said.

She said it the way she said most things—flatly, as if stating a fact about weather—and Marguerite found he was grateful for it. Gratitude was becoming a habit with her, and habits were dangerous. He crouched at the waterline and ran his thumb along the seam below the gunwale, feeling the give of swollen wood, the slow weep of river through tar.

"She needs two days to dry before I can recaulk properly," he said. "Maybe three."

"We don't have three." She nodded upstream, where the sky had begun to assemble itself. It was still morning—the light lay long and gold across the gravel—but over the western ridge the clouds were stacking, layer on layer, white going gray going the color of wet slate. Monsoon clouds didn't drift; they built. Somewhere behind them, over the coast, the first storms were already eating the horizon.

"Two days," she said again. "You told me yourself. Three days to the claim site on foot if the trail holds. Four before the rivers close."

He looked at her. Rain gear rolled tight, the notebooks from Camp Ashfall sealed in waxed canvas against her chest, boots laced to the ankle, hair tied back so severely it changed the shape of her face. She had stopped being a passenger somewhere around the second set of rapids, and he wasn't sure when exactly it had happened—one moment she'd been cargo he resented carrying, and the next she was reading water ahead of him and calling the lines before he did.

"The trail may not hold," he said. "Nobody's walked it since the camp burned. If they burned the camp, they'll have watched the trails."

"Then we walk fast."

* * *

They stripped the Kestrel to what backs could carry. Marguerite made the arithmetic without sentiment: rope, sixty meters; tarp; stove and one pot; rice, salt, tea; the medical kit; the axe; ammunition for the rifle he'd carried five years without once firing at anything that breathed. Everything else went under the thwarts, wrapped and weighted, because a boat abandoned was a boat robbed, but a boat cached might still be there when you came back down.

Astrid took the food and the notebooks. When he tried to take the notebooks from her pack to redistribute the weight, she put her hand flat on the flap and said, "No," and he heard underneath the word everything the ledger page had cost her—her father's name struck through in someone else's ink—and he let it go.

They climbed away from the river through stands of alder that gave way, after an hour, to old-growth cedar, and the light changed from gold to green to a kind of underwater dusk. The air thickened. Somewhere above them thunder spoke once, experimentally, and the whole forest seemed to lean east to listen.

Marguerite counted paces. He always counted paces. Eleven hundred to the kilometer on this grade, more when the trail switchbacked, and the numbers kept the other counting quiet—the counting of days, of years, of the particular weight of a woman walking three steps behind him who trusted him with her father's name and didn't yet know what he'd done with his brother-in-law's life.

"You're doing it again," Astrid said.

"Doing what?"

"Counting. Your lips move."

"Trail habit."

"What are you counting?"

"Paces."

"Liar," she said, without heat, and lengthened her stride until she drew level with him, and after that she stayed level, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, and the silence between them had changed texture—it was no longer the silence of two strangers sharing a boat but something closer to the silence of people who have agreed not to say a thing they both know.

The trail, when they found it, was barely a trail—a game path worn into the hillside, climbing through fern brakes toward a saddle between two shoulders of the ridge. Marguerite checked it the way he checked everything: slope, footing, sightlines, the age of the cut stumps flanking it. And it was the stumps that stopped him.

He knelt beside one. Cedar, felled recently—not this season, but not long ago either. The sapwood hadn't grayed. The saw marks were clean, machine-cut, a chain saw's even teeth, not an axe. He put his palm against the stump and felt the faint residual slickness of resin not yet cured to crystal.

"Astrid."

She came back down the trail to him. He pointed without touching. Fresh-cut wood, high on a ridge that every map called empty, four hours' hard climb from a burned survey camp.

"Somebody lives up here," he said quietly.

"Loggers?"

"Loggers work roads. There's no road within a day of here." He stood, scanning upslope, reading the forest the way she read contour lines. "This is firewood cutting. Households. Someone's been taking trees one at a time, close to home, for years." He turned a slow circle. "And they've been careful about it. See how the canopy closes over the stumps? They cut what falls in shadow. They don't want to be seen from the air."

"From whose air?"

"That," Marguerite said, "is the question."

They went on, but differently now. He moved ahead with the rifle slung forward across his chest, and he heard Astrid adjust her own gait behind him—lighter, quieter, her breathing through her nose. She'd grown up in expedition camps; whatever else Elias Vane had given his daughter or withheld from her, he'd taught her how a body moves when it doesn't want to be heard.

Near the saddle, in a fold of ground where a stream cut down through mossy shale, Marguerite raised his hand and stopped.

The snare line ran along the stream bank like a sentence written in wire. Seven anchors, seven nooses of braided cable, each set at the height and angle that said *deer, small deer, muntjac*, each anchored to living wood with a tension stick carved by someone who understood animals better than most hunters did. Not snares of desperation. Snares of craft.

He crouched at the nearest one without touching it. "Look at the anchor wrap," he murmured. "Figure-eight with a locking hitch. That's not subsistence. That's somebody who learned this properly, then came out here and got very good at being alone."

"Do you think—" Astrid started, and hope did its ugly work on her voice, the way it had done at the burned camp, turning every sign of human presence into a possible father. "Could it be him? Surviving out here? Hiding?"

Marguerite wanted to lie to her. He examined the impulse honestly, the way you examine a bad knot, and found it wouldn't hold. "Elias Vane was a company man his whole life," he said. "Company men don't learn to snare. This is someone who's been wild a long time." He paused. "But whoever it is knows we're coming. Look."

He pointed downstream, where the stream bent through the ferns. Set into the mud of the game path, half-hidden by a fallen frond, a thin branch had been laid across the track at ankle height. A trip line—or the memory of one, disarmed. The snare wire that should have hung from it had been lifted and coiled neatly around the branch itself.

"They heard us on the trail an hour ago," Marguerite said. "They came down and took their snares off the line so we wouldn't blunder into them. Or so we'd know they knew." He straightened slowly, feeling the forest press in around them, every shadow suddenly furnished with possibility. "We've spent two days thinking of ourselves as the hunters. Following tracks, following smoke, following a dead man's ledger." He met her eyes. "We just crossed onto someone else's ground, and they've been watching us since the gravel bar."

Thunder rolled again, closer now, and the first wind came down off the ridge smelling of iron and rain.

### Astrid

Her father had taught her to read contour lines when she was seven, drawing them in the dust of a survey camp with a stick, teaching her that a map was a promise the land only sometimes kept. *Every line is a place a foot can stand,* he'd said. *Follow the lines and you're never lost.* She had believed him for years—for too many years—and then the concessions had come, and the silences, and the birthdays marked by radio calls cut short by static, and she had learned that the man who taught her never to be lost had gotten very good at losing himself.

Now she climbed through cloud forest with a stranger's secrets sewn into her pack and her father's name crossed out in a dead man's ledger, and the land beneath her feet matched no line on any map she owned.

The snare line had frightened her more than she let show. Not the wire itself—she'd grown up around traplines, around the matter-of-fact economy of camps that lived partly off the country—but the care of it. The tidiness. Someone up here had arranged their solitude the way her father arranged his desk, and for one vertiginous moment on the stream bank she had wanted it to be him so badly that she'd heard herself ask the question aloud, had heard the naked want in her own voice, and hated it.

*Company men don't snare.* Marguerite had said it gently, which was somehow worse than saying it cruelly. Gently meant he'd considered lying and decided against it, and she was beginning to understand that Marguerite Reyes spent his lies carefully, like a man on rations, and that when he gave you the truth instead it was a kind of payment.

The storm was building behind the ridge. She could feel it in her back teeth, in the pressure drop, in the way the birds had gone silent all at once an hour ago—the whole forest holding its breath. Her father used to say monsoon season didn't arrive, it *condemned*: the sky passed sentence and then, after a decent interval, carried it out.

They topped the saddle as the light failed. Below them the valley opened northward—a deep green trough stitched with mist, the old claim country, and beyond it, faint as pencil ghosting, the gray wall of the high peaks where the mine had been staked and the surveys forged and men erased. Somewhere down there, Elias Vane had walked with field notebooks full of proof, and somewhere down there, if he still walked at all—

She shut the thought. Hope was a tool, Marguerite had said once, use it, don't carry it. She was learning to put it down and pick it up, put it down and pick it up, like the heavy things it was.

"We should make camp before dark," Marguerite said. "Not in the open. Against rock, backs covered, one way in and out."

"You think they'll come in the night?"

"I think someone disarmed their own snares rather than let us step in them." He shrugged, and the gesture contained multitudes she couldn't yet read. "That's courtesy, or it's a message, and I don't like messages I can't parse."

They found a shelf of granite below the saddle's eastern face, overhung by a slab that would shed rain, screened by a curtain of hanging moss. Marguerite built the fire small—very small, dead wood only, no flame higher than a hand—and cooked rice and tea with the efficiency of a man performing a ritual he'd performed ten thousand times alone. Watching him, Astrid felt the strangeness of it settle over her again: that she had known this man six days, that he had pulled her from white water and lied to her about nothing that mattered and everything about himself, and that she trusted him more completely than she had trusted anyone since she was a child trusting her father.

It occurred to her, sitting in the small red circle of firelight with the storm gathering its breath overhead, that trust was exactly what the snare line was about. Someone out there in the dark had chosen, today, not to hurt them. Someone had weighed two strangers on their trail and decided their deaths were unnecessary. In a valley where a mining company had burned a camp and struck names through ledgers, that choice was practically a love letter.

"Marguerite." His name still felt foreign in her mouth, too many consonants, a word from another country. "Whoever's out there. If they come—if we meet them—what do we do?"

He fed a twig to the fire. The light moved on his face, and she saw the tiredness in it that he hid during daylight, the tiredness of a man who had been watchful for five unbroken years.

"We do what they did for us," he said. "We make it clear we're not worth killing. Hands visible, voices low, no sudden anything. And we ask about the trail ahead, because whoever lives up here has walked to the claim site more times than we ever will, and knows things about this valley that aren't in any notebook." He glanced at her. "People who get ruined by companies don't usually love companies, Astrid. Whoever our neighbor is, they may be the best ally we haven't met yet."

"Or Meridian's eyes."

"Or that." The twig caught and flared. "Out here everything is both until it isn't."

She took first watch because he'd taken last watch every night since the rapids and hadn't asked, and because she wanted, just once, to be the one awake while he slept—to see what his face did when it wasn't managing itself. What it did was nothing dramatic. The vigilance drained out of it by degrees, like water finding its level, and what remained was younger than she'd expected, and lonelier, and she sat with the rifle across her knees and the storm ticking against the granite overhang and let herself look at him as long as she liked.

The rain began near midnight, a soft preliminary hiss in the canopy, and by two it had become a steady drumming, and by three the drumming had a rhythm underneath it that was not rain.

She froze, hand on the rifle, listening.

Footsteps. Below the shelf, downslope, moving unhurried through the wet dark. More than one set. And threaded among the sounds of boots on stone, unmistakable even through the rain, a sound she knew from a hundred childhood camps and had not heard in fifteen years:

Someone whistling. Low, tuneless, patient. The three-note signal her father's survey crews had used to announce themselves at the edge of a clearing—*friend coming, friend coming, stand easy*—a signal that had no business existing in this valley unless the man who invented it had taught it to someone, or was using it himself.

Astrid put her hand on Marguerite's shoulder, and his eyes opened instantly, fully awake, and she leaned close and put her mouth almost against his ear.

"Someone's coming up the trail," she whispered. "And they're whistling my father's signal."
