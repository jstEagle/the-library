## 10. The Hermit of Grey Fork

The whistling came closer, unhurried, three notes and a rest, three notes and a rest, the way her father had taught his crews to announce themselves decades ago—*friend coming, friend coming, stand easy*—and Astrid crouched in the fern shadow with her heart going like a bird against a window.

Marguerite had already moved. That was the thing about him she was learning: he didn't decide to move, he simply was somewhere else, low behind a fallen trunk with the hatchet drawn from his belt, his body arranged into stillness the way water arranges itself around a stone. He didn't look at her. He didn't need to. She understood that she should stay where she was, downwind, quiet, one hand on the strap of the pack that held everything they owned now that the Kestrel sat wrecked and half-buried in gravel forty miles below.

Three notes. A rest.

Then a voice, cracked and dry as bark peeling off a dead birch: "I can hear you breathing, girl. Both of you. Come out before I decide you're Meridian."

Astrid's breath caught on the name. *Meridian.* Spoken here, in this green cathedral of a valley where no road ran and no wire reached, spoken casually, contemptuously, the way you'd speak of weather that had ruined your harvest.

Marguerite rose slowly from behind the log, hands visible, hatchet still in his grip but pointed at the ground. "We heard your snares," he said. "And your woodpile. We weren't hunting you."

"Everyone's hunting someone." The man stepped out of the treeline.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined—she would admit this later—a feral creature, some castaway gone to moss and madness. Instead he was perhaps sixty, broad through the shoulders even now, with skin the deep brown of river clay and a white beard trimmed with more care than a man living alone had any right to exercise. He wore oilskin patched with fishing-net twine, and over his shoulder hung a surveyor's chain coiled like a sleeping snake, brass links gone green with age but oiled, maintained, kept.

He carried a rifle the way Marguerite carried the hatchet—not aimed, not relaxed, simply present, an extension of a body that had learned long ago what the world required of it.

"Tomas," Astrid said, before she could stop herself.

The old man's head turned toward her sharply. "Now how does a girl who smells of river water and bad decisions know my name?"

"I don't. Not yours specifically." She stood, letting him see her hands too, mirroring Marguerite without having discussed it. "I know the chain over your shoulder. My father ran survey crews for twenty years. Men who left the trade don't carry their chains. They sell them."

For a long moment the old man studied her, and then Marguerite, and then the trees behind them both, checking the empty air for followers out of habit so ingrained it looked like superstition.

"Elias Vane's daughter," he said.

It wasn't a question, and the certainty of it knocked something loose in her chest. "You knew him."

"I knew everyone worth knowing in this valley, back when knowing people was safe." He lowered the rifle finally, slinging it across his back, and jerked his chin up the trail. "Grey Fork's twenty minutes. You're walking into my home either way, because I'm not telling you what I know standing in the wet. Move. And you—" a gray eye flicked to Marguerite, lingering, narrowing, "—you walk behind me where I can hear your feet."

* * *

The cabin sat in a fold of the hill above Grey Fork creek, so deliberately hidden that they'd have walked past it within thirty feet. It was built into the hillside itself, sod-roofed, fronted with split cedar darkened to the color of iron. A garden patch, small and fierce, fought the forest for its square of light. Dried fish hung under the eave. A rain barrel. A second door, she noticed, on the far side—no cabin built by a careful man had only one door.

Inside it smelled of woodsmoke, dried herbs, and lamp oil. One room. A bunk, a table scarred by decades of knives and mugs, shelves of jars, and—one wall, floor to ceiling—paper.

Maps. Dozens of them. Hand-drawn, hand-inked, layered over one another like sediment. Contour lines crawled across every surface in fine, patient script, and pinned among them were survey plats, boundary diagrams, copies of documents whose official letterheads had been carefully blotted out or burned away at the corners.

Astrid stopped in the doorway and forgot, briefly, to breathe.

It was her father's study. It was every map-table she'd grown up under, every night Elias Vane had spent hunched over tracings while she did her homework at his elbow. This man had built himself a shrine out of the same religion.

"You were a surveyor," she said.

"I was *the* surveyor." Tomas Reyes-Idowu set the kettle on the stove without asking if they wanted tea; it wasn't a question in this country, it was a sacrament. "District of Coldwater, eleven years. Every claim staked between the forks, I drew the boundaries. Every mine registered, my signature is on the plat. Including—" he tapped the table once, hard, "—one belonging to a man named Vane, who paid me honestly and argued with me about contour intervals and bought my daughter a geography book when she was seven because he said she asked better questions than his own crew."

Astrid sat down slowly. The chair was warm from the stove's nearness, absurdly domestic. "You knew me."

"I knew a small fierce girl who corrected my bearing readings." Something moved behind his eyes and was put away. "You have her jaw. His stubbornness. Now." He turned, and all the softness went out of him like tide off a flat. "Names. Yours first."

"Astrid Vane."

"And him."

She hesitated. It was Marguerite who answered, his voice level, giving nothing away and therefore giving everything. "Marguerite Coldharbour."

Tomas Reyes-Idowu went very still. Then he laughed.

It was a terrible sound—not amusement but something older and more corrosive, a laugh that had been buried alive for years and had clawed its way out wrong. He laughed until he had to grip the edge of the table, until the jars rattled on their shelf, until Astrid found herself on her feet with her hand halfway to him, not knowing whether she meant to strike him or comfort him.

"Oh, oh, that's—" He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand. "That's magnificent. That's the funniest joke this valley has played on me in nine years. Do you know who walked into my house? Do you know what the river just washed up on my shore?" He pointed at Marguerite, finger trembling, not with fear—with recognition. "Coldharbour. Karst Ridge. The rope man. They blacklisted you so thoroughly they used your name as a *warning*. First thing they told me when they took my license: *remember Coldharbour, Reyes-Idowu. Remember what happens to men who ask questions.*"

"I don't know what you're talking about," Marguerite said, but his voice had changed. Astrid heard it—the hairline fracture running through the flatness.

"Don't you?" Tomas pulled a jar down, poured tea into tin mugs with hands that had steadied themselves by force of will. "Karst Ridge crossing. Five years back. Suspension bridge over the gorge, client party, mid-crossing collapse. Four survivors, one dead, and the guide who swore till they dragged him out of the inquiry room that the anchors were sound. Anchors that somebody else had inspected. Anchors that—" he set Marguerite's mug down with a click, gentle as a landmine being armed, "—were subcontracted. Guess by whom."

The fire ticked. Rain had begun again outside, soft against the sod roof, and Astrid realized she hadn't moved, hadn't drunk, hadn't breathed properly since the word *anchors.*

"Meridian Consolidated," she said quietly.

"Meridian Consolidated." Tomas drank like a man taking medicine. "Say it enough times and it stops sounding like a company and starts sounding like a diagnosis. I audited their boundary surveys in '09. Routine work—except their numbers didn't close. Lines that bent exactly where rich ore showed up in neighboring claims. Boundaries redrawn so the ore fell inside *their* stakes and outside everyone else's. I wrote it up. Polite memo, proper channels, nothing inflammatory." He smiled, and it was the saddest expression Astrid had ever seen on a living face. "Eight months later I'm drunk on duty, per the inquiry. Falsified measurements, says the tribunal. Struck off. Lost my house, my practice, my wife's patience. Came up here because it was the last place their writ didn't run, and I've been mapping a valley nobody will ever pay me for ever since."

He spread one hand at the papered walls. "Every contour on these walls is a correction of theirs. Every line I've drawn for nine years says: *you lied, and here is the truth of the ground.* And there's no one left to show it to."

"There will be," Astrid said.

Both men looked at her. She hadn't planned to speak; the words had come up from somewhere below thought, from the place where six weeks of silence from her father had hardened into something with edges.

"My father found something. Up at the old claim. Something worth burning a camp full of men for." She laid it out plainly, watching Tomas's face change as she spoke—the camp ash, the stripped equipment, the ledger page with Elias's name struck through, the boot prints heading inland. "Whatever he found, it connects to what you found. To what happened at Karst Ridge. To—" she turned to Marguerite, and made herself say it, gently, because gentleness was the only weapon she had left, "—to whatever happened to *him*."

Marguerite stared into his untouched tea. When he finally spoke, his voice was the voice of a man reading coordinates off a chart he'd hoped never to consult again.

"The anchor bolts on the Karst Ridge crossing were replaced three weeks before the accident. Subcontract job. I never saw the work order—I was told it was routine maintenance. Afterward, the inquiry had the paperwork. Perfect paperwork. My signatures on inspection reports I never signed." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "They didn't just blame me. They *became* me. They forged me the way they forge boundaries. I was a line on a plat that got moved."

Tomas set his mug down and covered his face with both hands. For a moment the old man's shoulders shook, and Astrid couldn't tell if it was grief or laughter trying to get out again, and suspected it was both, the two feelings ground together so fine they'd become a single mineral.

"Do you understand," he said through his fingers, "what the three of us are? A guide they erased. A surveyor they erased. And a leader they struck through in a ledger." He dropped his hands. His eyes were wet and blazing. "Vane found the original field notes. He must have—he was always the best of us at reading ground truth against paper lies. And instead of publishing, instead of running, he went inland to pull the whole root up, and they burned his camp and hunted him into the high country, and now his daughter walks into my kitchen carrying the same death warrant he does, escorted by the ghost they invented to hang their crimes on."

"It sounds less mad when you say we're all still alive," Astrid offered.

"It sounds *worse*," Tomas snapped, and then, abruptly, he laughed again—shorter this time, almost real. "Which is how I'll know it's true. Truth always sounds worse first."

He stood, crossed to the papered wall, and began pulling maps down with sudden purpose, laying them across the table, weighting the corners with mugs and stones. Old plats. Boundary lines in red ink overprinted on his own corrections in black. And at the center of the newest sheet, drawn in fresh graphite, a valley upstream of Grey Fork with a single mark at its head and a date beside it—three weeks old.

"That's the old Vane claim," he said. "Your father's original stake, before Meridian's red pen got to it. And that mark is where I watched four armed men make camp on the ridge above it, nineteen days ago. They're still up there, girl. They're sitting on your father's grave—or his prison—and waiting to see who comes looking."

Outside, the rain thickened against the roof, and somewhere up the darkening valley, thunder rolled down from the heights like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Tomas looked from the map to Marguerite, and his voice dropped to almost nothing.

"So. Before I tell you the way in—you'd better tell me what you're carrying that's worth dying for. Because I've seen what Meridian does to people who carry things."
