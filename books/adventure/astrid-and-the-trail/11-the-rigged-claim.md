## 11. The Rigged Claim

Tomas Reyes-Idowu talked the way water moved through his valley—finding every low place, filling it, moving on without apology.

He fed us first. That was the order of things in his camp, and I understood the order: you did not ask a man to spend his words until you had put something warm in his belly. The shelter was a lean-to of split cedar against a rock overhang, half-hidden by fern, with a fire pit ringed in stones worn smooth as river teeth. He set down three tin plates of rice and smoked fish and something bitter-green that grew only above the fog line, and he waited until we had eaten before he said anything that mattered.

Astrid sat cross-legged across the fire from him, her hands wrapped around a mug of pine tea she wasn't drinking. She hadn't spoken since we'd come up out of the trees and seen the snare lines, the drying racks, the neat stacks of cut wood—since Tomas had stepped out of the shadow of the overhang with a machete in one hand and recognition on his face like a scar reopening.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," he'd said, and laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell everyone that eventually," I said.

Now the fire cracked and spat, and rain began somewhere high above us—a sound like sand poured on a drumhead, still far off but coming. Tomas looked at me across the flames with eyes that had gone flat and patient the way eyes do when a man has lived alone too long with a grievance.

"You want to know about Coldwater," he said. "About why the camp burned. About your father." He nodded at Astrid. "I'll tell you. But understand what you're buying first. This isn't a story about one bad camp or one dead survey crew. It's a story about a machine. And machines don't burn."

* * *

He started, as surveyors do, with the ground itself.

"This valley runs forty kilometers from the divide to the sea," he said, drawing in the dirt with a stick—the habit of a man who had spent thirty years making the earth legible to other men. "Coldwater River here, the old claim up the north fork, the timber concessions east. When I came here, nineteen years ago, I came as a boundary man for the provincial land office. Honest work. You walk the lines, you shoot your bearings, you write down what the earth says and not what anyone wishes it said. That's the whole covenant of the job. The map is a promise. You don't get to lie in it."

He drew the river, then the tributaries, then a lattice of straight lines crossing them.

"Meridian Consolidated arrived eleven years ago. Timber first, then mineral leases. And they brought their own surveyors. Fine men, some of them. Good instruments. And every line they shot ran exactly where Meridian wanted it to run." He tapped the lattice. "Boundary disputes are decided by surveys. Leases are granted by surveys. Compensation is calculated by surveys. If you control the survey, you control everything the ground is worth—and everything the people standing on it are worth. Do you see? They didn't steal this valley with guns. They stole it with a transit and a chain and a signature."

"Forged how?" Astrid asked. Her voice was very controlled. I recognized the control; I'd been carrying my own version of it for five years.

"The oldest way there is." Tomas smiled without any warmth at all. "You don't fake the field notes. Faking notes is hard—they have to match the terrain, match the weather logs, match the instrument serial numbers. No. What you do is simpler. You take honest notes, and then you never file them. You file a different set. A transcription, they call it. Clean copies. And who checks a clean copy against the mud and mosquitoes of the original? Nobody. Nobody ever has. The original goes into a box, the box goes into a warehouse, and the copy goes into the registry, and the registry becomes the truth, because paper beats dirt in every courtroom in this country."

"And the men who took the original readings?" I asked. "The ones who knew?"

Tomas looked at me for a long moment over the fire.

"There were four of us in the district office who audited Meridian's filings between the sixth and ninth years," he said. "Four. Delphine Okafor kept the records—she flagged the discrepancies, though she couldn't prove them. A junior clerk named Anselm, who quit and moved to the coast and doesn't answer letters. Myself. And Elias Vane."

The name landed in the clearing like a stone dropped down a well. Astrid didn't move, but I saw her hands tighten around the mug until the knuckles went pale.

"Elias was the best of us," Tomas went on. "Old school. He believed the covenant. He found the first discrepancy by accident—a lease boundary that had migrated two hundred meters into a village's cassava fields between the original 1908 crown grant and Meridian's modern filing. Two hundred meters. Small thing. Except it wasn't small, because if that line could migrate once, it could migrate everywhere, and Elias being Elias, he went looking."

* * *

Tomas rose and went into the back of the overhang, where the rock shelved inward, and came back with an oilcloth bundle. He unwrapped it with the care of a priest handling relics: a handful of papers, soft with age and damp, and a photograph gone silver-gray at the edges.

"Audits," he said. "Mine. Copies of copies—I learned early not to keep originals where they could be found. Look."

He spread them on a flat stone. Even by firelight I could read enough: columns of bearings, coordinates, dates. And in the margins, in a cramped hand, comparisons. Original filed coordinates beside resurveyed ones. Lines that had walked. Not randomly—that was the thing Tomas made me see, tracing one column after another with a blunt finger. The lines always walked the same direction. Toward Meridian.

"Eleven parcels," he said. "That I know of. Probably more now. Every discrepancy favors Meridian. Every single one. You understand what that means? Chance walks both ways. Error scatters. This—" he tapped the page, "—this is intention. This is a machine doing what it was built to do. And when I filed my audit report, I was a licensed surveyor with nineteen years of good standing. Three weeks later I was a licensed surveyor with none. Incompetence, they said. Alcoholism, they hinted. They didn't need to prove anything. A license is a permission, and permission can be withdrawn by whoever holds the ledger. I lost my right to practice in a country where surveying was the only thing I knew how to do. So I came up here, where the ground doesn't care what the registry says, and I've been reading the true map ever since."

"And the others?" Astrid asked quietly. "Anselm. And—"

"Anselm got out while getting out was possible." Tomas's mouth twisted. "And Elias Vane fought. That was his mistake and his glory. He didn't just audit—he collected. For six years he gathered original field notes, chainman tallies, instrument calibration logs, photographs of boundary markers before Meridian's crews 'relocated' them. He built a case. A real one. Complete enough that when it surfaced, no clean copy in any registry could survive it. He showed me pieces of it, up here, the winter before last. He said—" Tomas paused, and his voice roughened. "He said, 'Paper beats dirt, Tomas. But dirt beats paper if you dig deep enough.'"

The fire had burned low. Above us the rain-sound was closer now, a steady whisper through the canopy, and the dark beyond the firelight had gone absolute. Somewhere down the valley, faint as memory, a generator throbbed—Meridian's logging station, keeping its hours.

"He finished it this spring," Tomas said. "The full dossier. Originals, not copies. He told me he had it somewhere no one would think to look, and that when the time came he'd send it downriver to someone who could use it. Then the Coldwater survey camp went quiet, and then it burned, and then riders started walking my ridgelines with Meridian badges on their sleeves, and I understood that the time had come and gone, and that Elias had been stopped."

"But the dossier survived," Astrid said. It wasn't a question. Her whole body had changed—spine straight, head up, the way a compass needle comes alive near iron. "It's still up here. Somewhere no one would think to look."

"I don't know where," Tomas said. "If I knew, girl, I'd have sent it down myself, consequences be damned. But he told me once—if anything happens to me, it's at the claim. The old stake. Where it all started." He shook his head slowly. "I thought it was a figure of speech. Elias was a man of figures of speech. But your Marguerite here found boot prints going inland, toward the north fork, toward the old claim, and those prints were made by men who weren't lost. Men who were searching."

"Meridian's riders are already looking for it," I said.

"Meridian's riders are looking for *him*," Tomas corrected. "For Elias, alive or dead, and for whatever he carried. They burned the camp to erase the expedition. But you don't burn a camp to hide a murder—you burn it to buy time while you search. And they haven't found what they're looking for yet, or they'd have left. Instead they're crawling up the north fork in the rain, going through the dirt centimeter by centimeter." He looked at Astrid, and something almost gentle moved through the flatness of his face. "Your father bought himself time the only way he could. By hiding the truth so well that they can't kill him until they find it. Which means, girl, that as long as that dossier stays buried, your father stays breathing. And as long as it stays buried, so does the valley."

* * *

Astrid stood. She walked to the edge of the firelight and stood looking north, toward the black wall of the ridge, though nothing could be seen—not the trail, not the fork, not the claim stake that had started everything. Rain ticked through the canopy above her. I watched her shoulders, and I saw the grief in them, and under the grief something harder taking shape, the way ice takes shape on still water.

"My father taught me to read contour maps when I was seven," she said, not turning around. "He used to say the map was a promise. That the man who draws it owes the truth to everyone who follows it." She turned, and the firelight caught the wet shine on her cheeks, though her voice was level. "He spent six years building a case because he believed a map should mean something. And they burned a camp full of people to stop him."

"Not full," Tomas said softly. "Some of the crew ran. Some were paid to run. But yes. Enough."

"Then we go to the claim," she said. "Tonight. Now."

"No," I said.

She rounded on me, and I took it. I deserved the look she gave me—the look you give a man who puts his hand on the rope you're pulling.

"The monsoon is stacking over the divide," I said. "Three days, maybe four, before these rivers close and this valley turns into a trap with the exits flooded. The riders are between us and the north fork, they know this country better than we do, and we'd be walking into them in the dark with everything we own on our backs. That's not courage. That's arithmetic done wrong."

"Then when?"

"When we can see. First light. We go fast, we go quiet, and we go ahead of the storm instead of inside it." I held her gaze. "Your father stayed alive this long by being patient. We can give him one night of it."

She held my eyes for a long moment, and I watched the fight go out of her by degrees—not surrender, never that, but the disciplined consent of a woman who understood terrain. She nodded once and turned back to the dark.

Tomas caught my arm as I moved to check the gear. His grip was dry and strong, root-strong.

"You understand what you're walking into," he murmured. "Not the riders. The rest of it. That dossier names names—surveyors, registrars, magistrates. Half the law in this province is built on Meridian's paper. Whoever surfaces that document doesn't get thanked. He gets erased. Like the camp. Like me, except slower." His eyes searched mine. "I know your name, Coldharbour. Karst Ridge. Whatever you're running from, running north will make it worse. There's no anonymity at the end of this road. Only testimony."

I looked at the fire. I thought about five years of forgettable trails, of counting miles because miles couldn't leave me, of a name I'd tried to bury in safe routes and tourist smiles.

"I've been invisible for a long time," I said. "Maybe it's overrated."

Tomas studied me, then grunted—the closest thing to blessing he offered—and went to check his snares in the dark.

Above the ridge, the clouds had stacked themselves into a single mass, moonless and total, and the first fat drops of the coming monsoon struck the canvas of the lean-to like fingers testing a lock. Three days. Maybe four.

In the morning we would walk into the country that had ruined all three of us, looking for a buried truth that was keeping a dying man alive.

And behind us, patient as paper, the machine was already moving.
