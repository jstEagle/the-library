## 12. Monsoon Warning

The clouds came in the night, stacking themselves over the western ridge like quarried stone being loaded onto some vast and patient barge. Marguerite saw them first thing when he crawled out of the shelter Tomas had built against a fallen spruce—saw them and felt the old arithmetic begin turning in his chest, the one that had never stopped running since Karst Ridge, the one that measured everything in margins.

*Three days,* he thought. *Maybe four.*

He'd watched that same sky build itself once before, over a different valley, and he'd stood in it too long because a client wanted photographs. The sky did not care about photographs. The sky cared about nothing at all; that was its whole character. It simply accumulated, and then it spent, and what it spent was water—weeks of it, whole rivers of it, dropped in a single unbroken roar until every gully became a throat and every throat became a flood.

He built the fire small, the way Tomas had shown him, feeding it dry alder bark and keeping the flame low enough to hide under the canopy. The hermit was already gone into the trees with his snare line, moving through the wet fern like a man who had made his peace with solitude the way other men made peace with God. Astrid slept on, curled in her groundsheet with her back to the cold, one hand open on the earth as if she were still holding a rope.

He let himself look at her for exactly as long as it took to fill the kettle. No longer. He'd gotten good at rationing things—fuel, distance, attention. Looking at Astrid Vane was something he rationed the way a man rations water in the high country: carefully, because he could not afford to need it.

* * *

She woke the way she always woke, all at once, no drifting, no soft edges—one breath she was asleep and the next her eyes were open and fixed on him across the fire, and he had the uncomfortable sense she'd been aware of him the entire time.

"You've been staring at the sky," she said.

"I've been reading it."

"And?"

He handed her the tin mug. Tea made from spruce tips, bitter enough to strip paint, but hot. "And it's going to spend itself on us. Three days. Four if we're lucky."

"Then we leave now." She drank without wincing, which he'd learned was a performance. Everything about her ease in this country was a performance laid over something harder, and he was beginning to suspect the hard thing underneath was grief wearing competence as a coat. "Tomas says the claim site is two days' hard walk up the Grey Fork. We can make it there and back before the rivers close."

"Back," Marguerite said. "That's the word you're betting everything on."

"We can make it."

"Can we." He crouched by the fire and turned a stick of alder between his fingers, watching the bark char. "Say we push hard today and camp below the scree. Say the weather holds another half day while we search whatever's left of your father's stake. Then say the front comes early—the way fronts do—and we're standing in a downpour above a valley whose only exit is a river that will be running brown and blind by noon of the second day. What's the plan then? Swim?"

"We go around. Tomas knows the high traverse."

"Tomas," Marguerite said carefully, "knows the high traverse for himself. One man, light pack, nothing to lose. You're asking him to move two people through country that kills solo travelers in good weather."

"He offered."

"He offered because he's lonely and angry and he remembers what Meridian took from him. That's not the same as him believing it's survivable." He looked up at her, and the words came out flatter than he intended, which was how his worst words always came out. "Astrid. I have carried people off mountains. I know exactly how heavy a person is when they stop helping."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, not yet, but the thing that comes before fear, the animal calculation of whether the man in front of you is an obstacle or an ally.

"And I know," she said quietly, "how heavy a father is when you never get to carry him at all."

* * *

The fight, when it properly arrived, came in pieces rather than all at once, the way weather does.

It started again over breakfast, or what passed for it—smoked fish Tomas had left them, hard bread gone soft in the damp. Astrid spread her father's last letter flat on a rock between them, the paper translucent with handling, and put her finger on the line where Elias described the original field notes. *Buried at the claim. Under the marker. If anything happens to me, someone has to dig them up.*

"If anything happens to me," Marguerite read aloud. "You hear that? He wrote that six weeks ago. Six weeks, Astrid. Whatever's buried there has been buried through six weeks of nobody coming. It will keep another four days."

"It won't keep if Meridian gets there first."

"They don't know it exists."

"They burned a survey camp," she said. "They stripped it and burned it and took the men somewhere, and you think they don't know my father found their forged boundary lines? They know. That's why the camp is ash. That's why there are boot prints heading inland. Somebody went up that valley looking for exactly what we're going to look for, and they didn't find it, because if they'd found it my father would be—" She stopped. Her jaw worked once. "Because they wouldn't still be searching."

Marguerite set down his bread. He chose the next words the way he chose footholds, testing each one before trusting it.

"Or they found it," he said, "and they're waiting for anyone who comes digging."

Silence, except the fire ticking and, far off, the first low mutter of thunder rolling along the ridge like furniture being dragged across an upper floor.

"That's fear talking," she said.

"Yes," he said. "It is. Fear is the only voice I trust anymore. Hope lies. Hope told me a crossing was safe once. Fear told me to count the anchors twice. I should have listened to fear both times." He heard his own voice crack on the last of it and hated that she heard it too. "I'm not telling you to turn back because I'm careful. I'm telling you to turn back because I have already buried the consequences of one bad decision, and I will not—"

"Do not," she said, rising, "make this about you."

"It's all about me! Don't you see that?" He was on his feet too now, and the fire spat between them. "Everything I touch on this river goes wrong in exact proportion to how much I want it to go right. I took this job because I wanted one last run, one clean job, and now there's a burned camp and a missing expedition and a woman who looks at me like I'm the kind of man who comes back. I am not that kind of man. I'm the kind who counts exits."

"Then count this one!" She grabbed the letter off the rock and held it up between them, her arm shaking—not with weakness, he realized, but with the effort of holding herself in one piece. "Count the exit where I go up that valley alone and you stay here with your fear and your arithmetic and your five years of safe forgettable trails. Count what that makes you when you're old. Because I'm going, Marguerite. With you or without you. My father taught me contour maps before he taught me to ride a bicycle. I have been reading this country since I was seven years old, and I will read it alone if I have to."

The thunder came again, closer, and beneath their feet the ground seemed to lean toward it.

He looked at her—at the rain-stiffened jacket, the rope burns on her palms from the rapids, the set of her mouth that was pure Elias Vane according to every photograph—and he understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching a bridge load past its limit, that she meant it. Every word. She would walk up that valley alone, into the teeth of the closing weather, and she would die doing it, or worse, and she would do it because love is not a feeling, it is a direction, and hers pointed inland.

And his pointed after her. That was the whole ruinous truth of it. His feet had decided days ago, somewhere on the river, somewhere in the white water when she'd hauled the line without being asked and looked back at him with river-spray in her eyelashes and no fear at all. His feet had already chosen, and the rest of him was just catching up, arguing, filing objections nobody would hear.

"Two conditions," he said.

Her chin came up. "I'm not negotiating."

"Everyone negotiates. It's the ones who say they don't that you have to watch." He crouched and began repacking his kit with sharp, deliberate movements, not looking at her, because looking at her would undo him. "One: we turn at the first sign the front is early. Not when it arrives. When it's early. A wind shift, a pressure drop, anything. We turn and we don't discuss it."

"And two?"

He tied off the last strap and finally raised his eyes to hers.

"Two: if it goes wrong up there—if either of us goes down—you leave me. You don't carry me, you don't wait, you don't bury me. You take whatever we find and you walk out and you testify. That's the whole point of this. Not us. The notebooks. The truth. Promise me."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the silence stretched so thin he could hear the river working in the gorge below, patient as time.

"No," she said.

"Astrid—"

"No. I'll promise to testify. I'll promise to carry the documents out if it costs me everything. But I don't leave people on mountains, Marguerite. That's the difference between us. That's the difference between me and everyone who ever walked away from you." Her voice gentled, which was somehow worse than shouting. "You think your curse is that everything you touch fails. Your curse is that you believe it. And I'm not going to stand here and agree with you just because it would make your leaving easier."

The words went into him like water into a crack, finding the fault line, widening it. He opened his mouth and closed it again. There was nothing in him equal to that—not argument, not agreement, only the raw stunned feeling of a man who has spent five years building a wall and just watched someone walk through it without breaking stride.

"Break camp," he said finally, gruffly, because the alternative was to say something true. "We burn daylight."

* * *

They climbed.

The Grey Fork trail was barely a trail—Tomas's description of it as a path had been generous in the way that lonely men are generous—and by midday they were moving through cloud forest where the moss hung off the branches like wet laundry and every surface dripped. The air had weight to it now, a pressed, expectant quality, as though the whole valley were holding its breath. Somewhere above them, behind the cloud deck, the sun was making its last attempts, and the light that filtered down was the color of old brass.

Astrid walked well. That was the thing he kept having to relearn about her. She placed her feet where he placed his without being told, read the switchbacks before they appeared, and when the trail steepened into root ladders she climbed them with a rhythm that matched his own. Twice she stopped without warning and cocked her head, listening, and both times he heard it a moment later: distant, mechanical, wrong for this country. An engine. Faint, intermittent, somewhere up-valley.

Neither of them said anything about it. Saying it aloud would make it real, and they had enough real things.

By late afternoon they broke out of the trees onto the shoulder below the scree, and Marguerite called the halt not because he wanted to but because the light was going and because stopping was the discipline and discipline was all he had left. They made camp in the lee of a granite fin, and he cooked rice with the last of the dried mushrooms, and they ate watching the west, where the clouds had finished their loading and begun, very slowly, to lower themselves like a lid.

"Tell me about her," Astrid said suddenly. "The client. The one who died."

The spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Not tonight," he managed.

"You always say not tonight." She wasn't looking at him; she was looking at the storm, her profile etched against the last brass light. "I'm not asking for the story. I'm asking you to stop pretending there isn't one. Because tomorrow we walk into a place where one of us might not come back, and I refuse—I *refuse*—to find out afterward that I spent our last clear evening letting you hide behind weather reports."

Thunder rolled again, longer now, and the first drops came down through the canopy fat and slow and warm, striking the granite around them like fingers drumming.

"Tomorrow," he said at last, "we reach the claim site."

"And the night after?"

"The night after," he said, "the sky spends everything it's been carrying. And we'd better be long gone, or we'll learn firsthand what the river keeps."

Above the ridge, the clouds closed ranks, and the first real rain began to fall—not the monsoon yet, only its herald, tapping at the world like a creditor at a door.
