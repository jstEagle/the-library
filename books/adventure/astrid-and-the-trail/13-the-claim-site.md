## 13. The Claim Site

The valley opened like a held breath.

We had climbed since first light, following the boot prints Marguerite had found in the ash of Camp Ashfall—prints that went inland and did not come back. The trail was barely a trail: a game path worn into the shoulder of the ridge, threading through ferns that stood taller than my waist and dripped cold water down my sleeves every time I brushed them. Above us the sky had begun its slow stacking, cloud on cloud, gray on grayer, the monsoon assembling itself somewhere beyond the ridgeline like an army we could hear but not yet see.

Marguerite walked ahead with the economy of a man who had never once wasted a step. He didn't look back to check on me; he listened instead. I'd learned that about him in the six days since the Kestrel lost half her cargo in the rapids—that he navigated by sound as much as sight, by the rhythm of my boots behind his, by the creak of my pack frame, by the small sounds I made when the ground turned treacherous. When those sounds changed, he stopped. He hadn't said a word about it. He just stopped.

"Here," he said now, and the word came out flat, careful, the way he spoke when something mattered.

I came up beside him and saw what he saw: a bend in the game path where the ferns thinned, and beyond them a shelf of ground jutting out over the drop of the valley—a shelf of bare stone and old cut timber, half-swallowed by moss.

A claim marker.

It was nothing like the painted survey stakes I'd grown up around, the crisp white posts with their numbered tags that my father used to bring home photographs of, proud as a man showing off grandchildren. This one was older, rougher—a peeled sapling driven into a cairn of stones, its top charred black as if it had survived a fire that took everything else around it. Someone had carved letters into it long ago, and the weather had eaten most of them, but when I knelt and scraped away the moss with my thumbnail, they surfaced like drowned things coming up for air:

**C.W. No. 7 — E.V.**

Coldwater. Elias Vane.

My hands were shaking before I fully understood why. Not fear. Something older than fear—the tremor you get standing at a door you've knocked on your whole life without ever hearing it open.

"This is it," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, too loud in the hush under the clouds. "This is the claim. The original one. Before Meridian redrew everything."

Marguerite crouched beside me but didn't touch the stake. He was reading the ground the way other men read newspapers—every line of it. His eyes moved from the cairn to the treeline to a scatter of stones near the shelf's edge, and something in his face tightened.

"Someone's been here," he said quietly. "Recently. But not many someones." He nodded toward a patch of earth at the shelf's edge where the moss had been disturbed and pressed flat again, badly. "One man. Careful. He wanted this place to look untouched."

My heart lurched. "Elias."

"Maybe." He said it the way he said everything honest—not cruelly, but without decoration. "Or maybe someone who came looking for what he left here."

* * *

We searched the way he taught me to search, back when I was nine years old and he was still a stranger with a leaking boat and a face like a closed door. Work outward from the anchor point. Touch everything once. Trust the ground more than your eyes, because the ground doesn't lie—it only keeps records nobody bothers to read.

The cairn came apart in my hands stone by stone, and beneath the third layer I found it: an oilcloth bundle, wrapped tight and tied with wire, buried deep enough that the moss had begun to knit itself over the top of it. It was heavier than it looked. When I lifted it out, water beaded off the oilcloth and ran down my wrists, cold as confession.

Marguerite watched the treeline while I worked the wire loose. I remember being grateful for that—for the fact that he gave me this moment with my back protected, so that whatever was inside the wrapping, I would meet it facing forward.

Inside were three things.

Field notebooks—four of them, rubber-banded together, their covers swollen and spotted with damp but intact. Elias's handwriting on the spines, the same slanted, impatient script that had filled the margins of every book in our house: dates going back eleven years. Survey dates. Boundary dates. Dates that should have meant nothing to me and made my chest ache anyway, because I recognized the months. I recognized which ones fell near my birthday, near Christmas, near the summer he missed entirely.

Photographs—two dozen, printed on site from a field camera, their edges curled. I spread them across the flat stone with hands that would not stay steady. Shots of the valley floor. Shots of boundary markers, some standing, some toppled. And then shots that were not landscapes at all: close-ups of documents, of stamped certificates, of a map with two sets of lines drawn on it—one in ink, one in pencil—and the pencil lines sitting fat and comfortable well inside territory the ink lines said belonged to someone else.

And a letter. One page, folded twice, written fast, in the dark probably, in handwriting that started controlled and deteriorated line by line until the last sentences looked like they'd been carved rather than written.

*Astrid—if you are reading this, then either I am dead or I am somewhere I cannot send for you, and either way you deserve better than silence.*

I sat down on the wet stone. I don't remember deciding to sit. My legs simply ended their argument with me.

Marguerite glanced back, read my face, and moved—not toward me, but to the edge of the shelf, putting himself between me and the whole open world. Giving me privacy the only way a man like him knew how: by guarding it.

I read the letter twice. Then a third time, out loud, because some words need the weight of a voice to become real.

*The surveys are forged. All of them, the whole valley, going back past the flood. Meridian Consolidated paid the district land office to move the boundaries, and the man who moved them is Assistant Registrar Corben Hale—he signs the amendments himself, both sides of every transaction, and no one audits him because the magistrate owns shares and looks the other way. I have the original field notes. Tomas Reyes-Idowu kept copies before they stripped his license. If anything happens to me, the notes are worth more than I am. Don't trade yourself for me. Trade the truth for everyone.*

*You always read contour maps like they were stories. Read this valley like one. Every line they drew is a lie, and lies have edges, and edges can be found.*

*I am sorry about the years. I thought I was building you something. I was only ever running from what I found.*

*—Your father*

The rain arrived while I was still holding the page. Not the monsoon—not yet—but a fine cold drizzle that came down through the canopy in threads, beading on the oilcloth, spotting the photographs. I got the notebooks wrapped again before they could drink any of it, and the letter last, folding it along Elias's own creases, tucking it inside my jacket against my chest, where it lay against my sternum like a second heartbeat.

"Astrid." Marguerite's voice, low. "We need to move. Weather's turning."

"I know." I was still kneeling among the photographs. "He named him. Marguerite—he *named* him. Corben Hale, assistant registrar. He wrote it all down. The magistrate, the shares, all of it." I looked up at him, and the drizzle was running down my face and I couldn't have said if any of it came from my eyes. "This is everything. This is what they killed the camp for. This is what they took *him* for."

Marguerite crouched across from me, rain dripping off the brim of his hood. He picked up one of the photographs—the one with the two sets of lines—and studied it for a long moment.

"He was right," he said finally. "About the notes being worth more than him." A pause. "That's not an insult. That's how they'll see it too. Which means whoever has him is keeping him alive exactly as long as these notebooks stay lost."

The logic of it landed in my stomach like a dropped anchor. They hadn't killed my father. They'd done something worse—they'd made him collateral. A hostage held against a document they weren't sure existed, in a place they weren't sure they'd found.

Unless they'd already searched here.

"Marguerite." I stood, wiping my palms on my trousers. "The prints you found at the camp. One man, heading inland. Do they match anything here?"

He rose too, slowly, and turned a full circle, reading the shelf again with that flat, total attention. Then he pointed—downslope, past the claim marker, where the game path continued along the ridge toward the high country.

"One set leaving. Same boots as the camp. Days old, maybe a week." He paused. "No set arriving. He came up from the river side, through the scree, where he wouldn't leave prints."

"So he was here. He buried this. And then he walked on." I stared down the trail, into the gray-green distance where the ridge climbed toward country none of the maps in my father's house had ever bothered to detail. "Toward what?"

"Toward the sluice stations, maybe. Or the logging cuts. Or nowhere." Marguerite's jaw worked. "Or he was walking wherever they told him to walk, so they'd keep letting him breathe."

*Don't trade yourself for me,* Elias had written. *Trade the truth for everyone.*

Easy words to write in the dark with a steady hand. Harder words for a daughter standing in the rain with the truth sewn against her ribs and her father somewhere down that trail, alive or not alive, breathing or not breathing, waiting on a decision she hadn't been asked to make.

* * *

We made camp early, in the lee of a granite overhang a quarter mile below the claim shelf, because Marguerite refused to sleep anywhere the riders could find us silhouetted against the sky. We ate cold rice and the last of the smoked fish, and neither of us talked much. The drizzle thickened toward evening, and the clouds came down off the ridge until the whole world was the size of our overhang and the sound of water.

I took out the letter again, just to feel its weight. Just to confirm it was real.

"He apologized to me," I said into the fireless dark. "In writing. Eleven years of silence and he spends his last paragraph apologizing."

Marguerite was cleaning the grit out of the rope coil, strand by strand, methodical as prayer. "Men apologize best on paper," he said. "Paper doesn't interrupt."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He wound the rope into its loop and set it down. "But it's true. The worst thing I ever did, I've never said out loud. There's a version of it in my head that's cleaner than the truth. Some nights I almost believe the clean version." He looked at me across the small dark space between us, and the rain made a curtain at the mouth of the overhang, and his face was half shadow. "Your father wrote you the true version. That's more than most of us manage."

I wanted to ask him about it—about the worst thing, about Karst Ridge, about the five years he carried like ballast. I could feel the question sitting in my throat like a stone. But he had given me the claim site undisturbed; he had stood guard over my grief without naming it. There would be a time to ask. Tonight wasn't it.

Instead I said, "He's alive. I know you won't say it unless the evidence says it, so I'll say it. The prints leave. They don't end. Nobody buries a cache, writes a letter to his daughter, and then dies peacefully down the trail. He's alive, and they're keeping him that way because they're afraid of paper." I pressed my hand flat against my jacket, against the fold of the letter. "And I'm holding the thing they're afraid of."

Marguerite was quiet a long moment. Rain drummed on the granite above us.

"The assizes close in three weeks," he said at last. "Delphine Okafor is four days' travel downstream, if the rivers hold. And the monsoon—" he tilted his head toward the sky, listening to it the way he listened to everything, "—is two days out. Maybe less. After it breaks, nothing moves on this river for a month."

"Then we go tomorrow. First light."

"We go tomorrow," he said, "and we go fast, and if anyone follows us off this ridge, we do not stop to fight. These notebooks reach that clerk if I have to swim them downriver in my teeth." He said it without drama, the way he said everything, and somehow that made it worse and better at once.

I lay back against my pack and pulled the oilcloth over us both, and in the dark I listened to the rain gather strength, and I thought about edges. *Lies have edges, and edges can be found.* My father had spent eleven years finding them, alone, in country that wanted him silent. And now the edges were folded against my chest, and the man who drew them was somewhere upslope in the hands of the people who'd drawn the lies, and the rain was coming to close every road behind us.

Somewhere far off, thunder rolled along the ridge like a door being shut.

Tomorrow we would run for the capital with the truth in our coats. And every mile of the way, I would feel my father's absence walking beside me—closer than he had ever been in life, and further than I could bear.

I did not sleep. But I held the letter until dawn, and dawn came.
