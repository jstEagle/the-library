## 14. Riders on the Ridge

The morning came in gray and slow, the way mornings do when the sky is already thinking about rain. I woke with my boots half on, which is a habit you pick up when you've slept badly in enough places to know that bad sleep is usually trying to tell you something. Astrid was already up, crouched at the cold fire ring, feeding the last of our dry kindling into a small flame. Tomas stood at the edge of the clearing with his head cocked, listening to the forest the way an old man listens to a room full of children—half fondness, half arithmetic.

I had learned by then that Tomas listened in layers. Birds first. Then wind. Then the absence of both.

"Something's wrong," he said, before I'd finished lacing the second boot.

I came to stand beside him. The clearing sat on a bench of ground above Grey Fork creek, ringed with cedar and hemlock so dense they swallowed sound. Below us, the trail we'd climbed yesterday wound back down toward the river in a series of switchbacks, each one visible for maybe thirty meters before the trees closed over it like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

For a long moment there was nothing. Then there wasn't nothing.

It came up through the soles of my feet before it reached my ears—a vibration, low and rhythmic, the kind of tremor that water makes when it's displaced by something heavy moving through it. Not animals. Animals don't keep time.

"Horses," I said.

Tomas nodded once. "Four. Maybe five. Coming up the lower switchbacks."

Astrid rose from the fire, and I watched her face do what her father's must have done a hundred times in boardrooms: go smooth and still while everything behind it started running. "Meridian?"

"Who else walks this country with horses?" Tomas said. "Nobody local rides. There's no feed up here worth carrying, no trails wide enough, and every man born in this valley knows the ground is against them." He spat, a small dark punctuation mark on the moss. "Company men ride because they think the company owns the ground."

He was right, and being right didn't help us. We were three people with two rifles between us—one mine, one Tomas's ancient bolt-action that he kept wrapped in oilcloth under his lean-to—and a canvas satchel containing Elias Vane's field notebooks, sealed in wax and waterproof cloth, heavier than lead and worth more than everything else we carried combined. Behind us, four days' hard travel away, lay the district town and Delphine Okafor and whatever chance the documents had of meaning anything. Ahead of us, the high valley, the claim site already searched, nothing left there but ghosts.

And coming up the switchbacks, patient as weather: men paid to make sure those notebooks never reached a courtroom.

"How far?" Astrid asked.

"An hour," Tomas said. "Maybe less if they've got a guide who knows the cutoff."

"Do we run for the claim site? Circle around—"

"No." I said it flatly, and she turned to look at me, and I made myself meet her eyes. "They'll have someone watching the high trail. That's the obvious route, and Meridian doesn't hire men who miss the obvious. We go down through the treeline, east along the creek, and take the old logging spur toward the ford. It's rougher, but it's blind."

"It's also flooded," Tomas said. "The Fork's been rising since Tuesday."

"Then we'll get wet."

Astrid looked from me to Tomas and back again, and I saw her do the same arithmetic I was doing—the same grim ledger where every column ended in the same sum. Three of us, slowest as fast as our legs could carry us, through country a man who'd lived here twenty years knew better than his own hands.

"Tomas," she said carefully. "You don't have to come."

The old surveyor smiled, and it was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a man who has been waiting a long time for something and finds the waiting almost pleasant now, the way a debt is almost pleasant when you finally see how it will be paid.

"Child," he said, "they took my license, my name, and eleven years of my life. You're carrying the thing that might give all three back. Where exactly would I go?"

* * *

We moved fast and quiet, which is a contradiction you learn to resolve or die trying. Down through the cedar shadow, boots finding the soft ground between root systems, the notebooks passed between us in shifts because the weight told on the shoulders. Astrid carried them better than I expected—not easily, but stubbornly, the way she did everything, jaw set against the ache. Twice I reached to take them and twice she shook her head.

"They're mine to carry," she said the second time. "My father wrote them."

I let it be. There are arguments that cost more than they're worth, and that one would have cost everything.

Behind us—we couldn't see them, but we could feel them the way you feel a storm front—the riders came on. Sound carries strangely in steep timber; sometimes their horses seemed close enough to touch, sometimes gone entirely, and only the jingle of tack and the occasional flat word of command told us they were real. They weren't hurrying. That frightened me more than speed would have. Men who hurry are men who fear losing you. Men who amble are men who know the country is a net.

Tomas led us off the spur at a place where a deadfall had rotted into a green rampart, and took us down a slope so steep we rode it on our heels, hand over hand through salmonberry brambles that opened the skin on my palms and left them stinging with juice and pitch. At the bottom, Grey Fork Creek ran high and brown and loud, swollen with snowmelt out of the high country, carrying whole branches past us like it was in a hurry to deliver them somewhere.

"The ford's a kilometer down," Tomas said. "But—" He stopped. His head went up.

I heard it too. A whistle, thin and deliberate, from upstream. Then another, downstream. Answering.

"They've split," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "They've got flankers on the creek."

Tomas's face in the gray light was unreadable, which meant it was very readable indeed. He looked downstream, then upstream, then at the wall of timber across the creek—old growth, dense, unbroken for miles if you could cross the water, which at this level you could not, not without losing an hour and probably a rifle and possibly a life.

"There's a place," he said slowly, "two hundred meters up. The creek pinches between rock shelves. Water's fast but it's knee-deep, and there's gravel on the far side. If we cross there and climb, we're in timber they can't ride through. On foot, in this country, nobody catches anybody who doesn't want to be caught."

"Then why do you look like a man reading his own obituary?" Astrid asked.

"Because somebody has to hold the near bank," Tomas said, "while the other two cross. And I'm the only one of us who knows these woods well enough to make holding it mean anything."

"No," Astrid said immediately.

"Yes," Tomas said, with the mild finality of a man stating that water runs downhill. "Listen to me, girl. I'm sixty-three years old, my knees are shot, and I can't climb that far bank at your pace. Every minute you wait for me is a minute closer those riders get. But I can move through this brush like smoke, and I can make four armed men believe there are six of us, and I can buy you the time to disappear." He was already unwrapping his rifle from the oilcloth, checking the action with hands that moved without hurry. "This isn't sacrifice. This is accounting. I've owed Meridian a long time, and today I finally get to pay in a currency they understand."

"You could die," Astrid said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and I understood suddenly that this was not about tactics for her, that somewhere in the last week the old ruined surveyor had become something she hadn't had in years—family, chosen rather than given.

"Everybody dies," Tomas said. "Not everybody gets to choose what for." He looked at me then, and his eyes were steady and dry and absolutely certain. "Marguerite Coldharbour. Take her across. Take her to the capital. Take her to that clerk with the good memory. And when they ask who helped you, tell them my name right—all of it, Reyes-Idowu, both halves, the way my mother insisted. I spent twenty years letting them erase it. Don't you let them finish the job."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad things do, and slow, the way memory insists on replaying them.

We crossed at the pinch. The water hit mid-thigh and shoved like a living thing, cold enough to stop breath, and I went first with the line, then Astrid with the notebooks held high and dry above the spray, her face white with cold and fury and grief all at once. On the far side we climbed, slick granite and wet moss, hands burning, until we reached a shelf of ground where the timber closed around us like a door.

From below, we heard Tomas begin his work.

He fired once—high, wide, harmless, but loud as judgment in that narrow valley—and shouted in a voice pitched to carry, giving orders to men who didn't exist, calling positions on a phantom squad. *Left flank, hold the deadfall! Second team, cut the lower trail!* It was beautiful, honestly. It was the work of a lifetime spent alone in country like this, and for perhaps ninety seconds it worked. The whistles downstream stopped. A horse screamed somewhere out of sight. Boots crashed through brush, going the wrong way.

Then a voice called out, clear and unhurried, a voice used to being obeyed: "Old man. Come out with your hands empty and nobody gets hurt. We know it's one rifle. We counted the shots on the ridge yesterday."

Silence. Then Tomas laughed—that same bitter laugh from the night we'd met him, the laugh of a man who has been ruined by professionals and recognizes the accent.

"One rifle," he called back, conversational, almost friendly. "You're right about that. But you should know something, boys. I surveyed this valley in nineteen sixty-one. I know where every man of you is standing right now, because I put the markers there myself. And I promise you—" his voice lifted, rang off the rock walls, became something almost joyful— "*you are standing in the wrong places.*"

The shot that answered him was not high and wide.

Astrid made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. She lunged for the edge of the shelf and I caught her—caught her jacket, hauled her back into the timber as more shots cracked through the understory, splintering bark above our heads, the riders firing blind and angry at the slope. She fought me for three full seconds, wild, and then went still, and then we ran.

We ran east through old growth with the light failing around us and the rain starting at last, fine and cold, beading on the wax-sealed satchel between us. We ran until the shooting faded behind us and the creek noise faded beside us and there was nothing left in the world but breath and stride and the terrible arithmetic of what we'd left on the near bank.

* * *

We stopped at dusk in the hollow beneath a windthrown spruce, its root plate reared overhead like a wave frozen mid-break. Astrid sat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, staring at nothing. Rain dripped through the canopy in slow irregular counts. I built no fire. Fire was a luxury belonging to people whose position wasn't known to armed men.

"He's alive," she said finally. It wasn't a question, but it wanted to be one.

"They wanted him talking," I said. "That voice on the creek—he gave him the chance to surrender first. Men shoot to kill without offering terms. They took him."

"Took him where? To do what?"

I didn't answer, because I didn't have an answer that wasn't a lie, and I had decided—I couldn't say when, exactly, but it was true—that I was done lying to her. So I said, "I don't know. But he's alive, and alive means leverage, and leverage means they'll keep him breathing until they understand what he knows. Which means we have time. Not much. Some."

She turned her head and looked at me, and in the last gray light her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "You're going to say we can't go back for him."

"I'm going to say," I said slowly, "that two of us walking into a security detachment to free one prisoner is not a rescue, it's a donation. That the notebooks are the only weapon that frees everyone—him, your father, Tomas, every person Meridian ever stepped on. And that Tomas knew all of that better than either of us, and chose it anyway, and the worst thing we could do to him now is waste it."

"I know." Her voice was small and hard. "I know all of that. Say it anyway. Say it out loud so it's real and I have to live inside it."

So I said it, out loud, into the dripping dark, and it was real, and we both had to live inside it.

After a long time she said, "You froze. On the shelf. When the shots started—you grabbed me, but there was a second first, a whole second, where you weren't anywhere."

She was right, and I hadn't known she'd seen it. Somewhere in that crack of gunfire the valley had become another valley, five years and a border away, and the sound had been rope and rockfall and a voice I don't write down even now. I had come back. I always come back. But the seconds are getting longer, and someday, I suspect, I won't.

"I'll tell you about it," I said. "All of it. Karst Ridge. What happened, what they said happened, what actually happened. You deserve the whole map before you decide how much of me to trust with your father's notebooks."

She studied me for a moment, rain-dark and unreadable. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I want to believe you're just a man who's tired."

I was. God knows I was. But tired wasn't the half of it, and we both knew it, and the debt we were carrying had grown heavier by one old man's freedom, and interest on that kind of loan compounds daily.

Somewhere behind us, down the black valley, Tomas Reyes-Idowu was in the hands of men who erased names for a living. Somewhere ahead, through days of rain and ridgeline, was a town with a magistrate who owned shares and a constable who looked away, and beyond it a capital, and beyond that a courtroom where truth might still mean something if it arrived in time.

I checked the satchel. Dry. Sealed. Intact.

"We move at first light," I said. "East ridge, then down to the river road. Four days if the weather holds."

The weather, of course, did not hold. But that is the next part of the story, and the rain was already writing it on the roof of the world.
