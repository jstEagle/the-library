## 15. The Crossing That Failed

The river had doubled overnight.

I knew it the way you know a stranger standing in your kitchen—by the wrongness of the sound. For two days the water had been a mutter below the trail, a gray thread stitching the valley floor. Now it was a voice, a full-throated animal voice, and when we came down through the last stand of alder and the trees opened onto the floodplain, I stopped so suddenly that Marguerite walked into my back.

"Ah," he said, in the tone of a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar.

The Grey Fork had swallowed its banks. What had been a stony shoal on Tomas's old survey maps—Tomas, who was somewhere behind us now in the hands of Meridian's riders, and whose name I could not say aloud without my throat closing—was a brown sea the width of a cathedral, churning with whole trees that sailed past upright, roots first, like drowned men walking. The rain came down in ropes. It had been coming down in ropes since dawn, and the sky above the ridge was the color of wet slate, and it was not finished with us yet.

"Tell me there's another way," I said.

"There's always another way." Marguerite stood with his hands on his hips, studying the flood the way he studied everything—sideways, as if direct attention might frighten it. "Four days north through the pass, then down the far side. Or we go back and take the logging spur."

"The riders are behind us. You said it yourself—Tomas bought us an hour, maybe two. By now they know which way we went."

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. We both stood there doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out the same: the river, or the rope.

Because there was a rope. I had seen it first from the ridge that morning, a pale diagonal stitched across the gray water, and I had felt the small lift of relief that travelers feel at any sign of human order in wild country. Now, standing on the floodplain with the rain running down my collar, I looked at it again and understood what it actually was: two cables strung between iron stanchions sunk in the rock on either side, a handline above, a footrope below, and between them—nothing. Air. Brown, screaming, tree-filled air.

A crossing, the map had said. Tomas had drawn it in his careful surveyor's hand, with a note: *Company-built, pre-war. Last inspected by me, four years ago. Use with caution.*

Four years ago. Before the flood season that had taken the lower bridge at Karst Ridge. Before the monsoon that was even now assembling itself over the ridge behind us like an argument no one could win.

"Marguerite." I said his name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "We can do this."

He wasn't listening. He was looking at the bridge, and his face had changed—not with fear exactly, or not only fear, but with a kind of recognition that was worse. It was the face of a man who has opened a door expecting a room and found a memory standing there instead.

* * *

I had seen him freeze once before, and I had mistaken it for calculation.

It was on the third day out from Camp Ashfall, when a rockslide had blocked the trail and he had stood looking at it for a long, silent minute while I counted the seconds and wondered if he'd lost his mind. Then he'd moved—fast, competent, finding the line through the debris like water finding a channel—and I'd understood that the pause hadn't been hesitation. It had been reading. He read country the way I read text: completely, before committing.

But this was not that. This was not reading. This was a man looking at a bridge and seeing a different bridge, in a different valley, five years and one lifetime ago. I had heard the name Karst Ridge spoken only once, on the night he'd told me about the collapse—the crossing that failed under his clients, the man who fell, the inquiry that blamed the guide and ruined him without trial. He had told it in his flat, inventory voice, the one he used for cargo weights and river distances, and he had told it like a man reciting someone else's sins. But I had noticed, even then, that he never looked at rope the same way he looked at other things. Coils of it in the Kestrel's bow. The line we'd used to lower the packs down the scree. His hands touched it and then didn't.

Now his hands were at his sides, and they were not moving at all, and the rain was filling the hollows of his face, and the river was eating the minutes.

"Marguerite." I touched his arm. It was like touching a post. "Hey. Look at me."

He looked at me. His eyes were present but not *here*—I knew that look, I had worn it myself in the weeks after my mother died, standing in doorways of rooms that had stopped being rooms. He was standing on a rope bridge in another country, five years younger, with a client's harness in his hands and the sound of the snap still in his ears.

"It's not that bridge," I said.

"I know what it is." His voice came out level. Too level. The levelness of a man holding a door shut from the inside. "Two cables, pre-war anchors, iron stanchions. Same build. Same span, give or take. Same river doing the same thing rivers do when the company builds cheap and the valley pays."

"Then you know the anchors are the question. Not the rope. Not the span. Not you." I stepped in front of him, into his sightline, so that he had to look at me or look away. "The Karst Ridge anchors were sabotaged. You said it. The inquiry buried it, but you said it, and I believe you. This isn't that bridge. This is a bridge that hasn't been cut."

"You don't know that."

"No. And you don't know the opposite. That's what a crossing is, Marguerite. It's not a guarantee. It never was, not on any bridge ever built. You cross anyway, or you don't."

The river roared. A half-submerged log the length of a church pew went by, spinning slowly, and struck one of the stanchions with a boom we felt through our boots. The footrope shivered like a struck string.

Marguerite looked at it. And I watched something happen in his face that I would spend a long time afterward trying to describe—the war not between courage and cowardice, because he was not a coward and never had been, but between two kinds of knowledge. The body's knowledge and the mind's. The mind said: *cables, anchors, physics, a line, a harness, go.* The body said: *I have been here before, and here is where everything ended.*

"You can't think your way across," I said. "I know that sounds wrong coming from me. I think my way through everything. But this one—" I put my hand flat on his chest, over the heartbeat that was going like a bird trapped in a room. "This one lives lower down. And the only way to get past it is to walk it through. One step. Then another. Your hands will remember what to do even while the rest of you is screaming."

"And if my hands don't?"

"Then I'll be behind you, and I'll be talking, and you'll have to listen to my voice the whole way, and you'll be so sick of me by the far side that you'll forget to be afraid."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ancestor of one.

"You talk too much, Vane."

"I'm told it's hereditary."

* * *

We rigged it the way he'd have rigged it for clients, because his hands did remember, even while the rest of him stood at the edge of the water like a man at the edge of a grave.

Harnesses first, from webbing, tied in the style he'd taught me on the scree above the claim site—*the rope takes the fall, not your spine*—and clipped to the lower cable with steel carabiners that he inspected twice, three times, turning each one over in the rain like a jeweler. Then the packs, slung and clipped, sent across first on a separate line so that nothing we carried would be lost if we were. The notebooks went against my chest, inside my coat, inside the waxed wrapping, tied down with cord. I would not send them across on any line. If the river wanted them, the river would have to take me too.

Marguerite checked my harness. Then he checked it again. Then he stood there with his hands on the buckles, not letting go, and the rain came down between us, and I understood that he was not checking the webbing. He was checking whether I would be all right if he fell.

"Marguerite."

"I'm checking the—"

"You've checked it. I know. I was there." I took his hands off the buckles and held them. They were cold and steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake. "You go first."

His head came up. "That's backwards. Guide goes last. Guide—"

"Guide goes last when the guide is guiding. You're not guiding right now. I am." I said it gently, but I said it like a fact, because it was one. "You go first because I need to see you cross. Because if I go first and you freeze halfway, there's nothing I can do but talk. But if you go first, then every step you take is a step I get to follow. You lead me across. That's the whole job. That's been the whole job since the dock."

He stared at me. The rain ran off his jaw. And then he laughed—a short, cracked, disbelieving sound, half drowned by the river—and shook his head.

"You're going to be the death of me, Astrid Vane."

"Probably not today. Today I'm going to be the opposite of that. Go."

* * *

I have crossed that bridge a thousand times since, in memory, and it is never shorter than it was.

He stepped onto the footrope the way a man steps onto ice he doesn't trust, weight back, arms up, hands closing on the upper cable one over the other. The bridge took him. It swayed—God, it swayed, the whole span flexing in the current's wind like a blade of grass—and the brown water reached up within arm's length of his boots, and a whole cedar went by beneath him close enough to touch, and he did not look down. He looked straight ahead, at the far stanchion, at the iron anchor in the rock on the far side, and he walked.

And I talked.

I talked the whole way, shouting over the river, telling him anything, everything—the color of the water, the smell of the alder, the way his left boot dragged slightly on the push-off, which I'd noticed on the first day and never mentioned, the name of the cloud formation stacking over the ridge, which I didn't know, so I invented one. I told him about my father teaching me contour maps, about the letter in the cache, about the taste of the river water in the Kestrel's leaking hold. I told him I was afraid of deep water and had been since I was nine and no one knew that, not even him, until now. I told him things I have no memory of deciding to tell him. The river pulled the words out of me like a current pulling thread from a spool.

Halfway across, the bridge dipped.

It was the current, or a log striking the lower anchor, I never learned which—but the footrope dropped a full arm's length, the upper cable snapped taut, and Marguerite went down to his knees on the wet rope with the river standing up around him, and his hands locked on the cable, and he stopped.

I saw it happen. I saw the exact moment the past took him—the moment his body said *this is where it happens, this is the dip, this is the snap*—and every muscle in him turned to stone, and the bridge swung, and the water roared, and he did not move.

"Marguerite!" I was at the near anchor, harness on, one hand on the cable, and every instinct in me screamed to go to him, and I knew—I knew with the same certainty he read country with—that if I stepped out and the bridge dipped again under two, it would take us both. "Look at your hands!"

He didn't move.

"Look at your hands! What are they doing?"

Nothing. For three full seconds of river-roar, nothing. And then, so slowly it was almost invisible, his right hand slid forward along the cable. Then the left. Then the right.

"They're holding," I shouted. "Your hands know what they're doing! Five years, Marguerite, a hundred crossings, a thousand clients—you've been doing this since before I was born! Your mind is the only part of you that thinks you can't! Move your hands and let the rest of you catch up!"

His right hand slid again. His knee came up onto the rope. The bridge swayed and he rode it, rode the sway instead of fighting it, the way he'd taught me to ride the Kestrel's roll in the rapids—*don't oppose the motion, join it, the water's stronger than you and always will be*—and then he was moving, hand over hand over hand, and I was moving behind him, out onto the span with the river roaring under my boots and the rain in my teeth, and the whole bridge was alive under us, flexing and dipping and singing, and I talked and talked and talked.

The last ten feet he did not walk. He hauled himself, arm over arm, and fell onto the far bank, onto the rock and the roots, and lay there on his back in the rain with his chest heaving like a landed fish.

I came off the bridge thirty seconds behind him, and my legs folded under me the moment my boots touched stone, and I sat down beside him, hard, in a puddle, and started laughing. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me like water out of a cracked boat, all the fear of the last hour converting itself at the last second into something else, something that shook my shoulders and made my eyes run even harder than the rain.

Marguerite turned his head and looked at me. He was gray-faced, soaked through, shaking—I could see it now, the shaking, the fine tremor in his hands that he'd held down for the whole crossing and could no longer hold—and he looked at me laughing in the puddle, and after a long moment his own mouth broke open, and he laughed too. It was not a pretty sound. It was ragged and cracked and half sob, and we lay there on the far bank of the Grey Fork laughing like two people who had just been told the world was ending and then, at the last moment, wasn't.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of the old stanchion, under a shelf of rock, with a fire that fought the wet and mostly lost. We didn't talk much. Some crossings you don't narrate afterward; you just sit on the far bank and let your hands remember what they did.

But near midnight, when the rain had thinned to a whisper and the river below had dropped its voice from a roar to a growl, Marguerite spoke into the dark.

"The bridge at Karst Ridge dipped," he said. "Right there. Middle of the span. Same as this one. And I froze—half a second, maybe less. Half a second, and the man behind me stepped forward onto a rope that was already failing, and the anchor went, and—" He stopped. I heard him breathe. "Half a second, Astrid. That's what I've been carrying. Not the collapse. The half second."

I reached across the dark and found his hand. It was still trembling, faintly, like a struck string that hadn't quite gone silent.

"Today you didn't freeze," I said.

"Today you talked me across."

"Today you crossed. The talking was just weather." I squeezed his hand. "Half a second isn't a sin, Marguerite. It's a weight. And weights can be set down."

He was quiet for a long time. The fire ticked. The river growled.

"It's not set down," he said finally. "It's lighter. That's not the same thing."

"No," I agreed. "But it's a start."

I was nearly asleep when he said the last thing, so quietly I might have dreamed it.

"The riders crossed at the ford, upstream. Tomas told me about it once—there's a ford two miles up, knee-deep in dry season." A pause. "It's not dry season."

I opened my eyes. In the dark, his face was turned toward the river, toward the black, swollen water that had nearly taken us and would not stop the men behind us for long.

"They'll have horses and ropes and no ghosts," he said. "We've bought a night. Maybe a morning. Come dawn, we walk—and we'd better walk fast."

Beyond the shelf of rock, far downstream through the rain, I thought I saw a light. One small, moving point, where no light should be—there where the river road bent toward the town, toward the magistrate and the constable and the courtroom at the end of everything.

It was gone when I blinked. But I did not sleep again.
