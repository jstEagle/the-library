## 16. Cave of the Long Rain

**Marguerite**

The sky had been stacking itself all afternoon—clouds shouldering over the ridge like freight being loaded, gray on gray, until there was no blue left anywhere and the light came down the color of old tin. Marguerite had watched it build for two days, reading it the way other men read faces, and by midmorning he'd stopped pretending they would beat it to the valley.

They didn't even try. The rain announced itself first as a sound far off in the high country, a long white hiss like static, like the radio at Coldwater camp, and then the wind arrived ahead of it, bending the alders flat, and then the world simply ended in water.

He found the cave because he'd marked it years ago—or a cave like it; the limestone country up here was riddled with them, hollowed by water that had been patient for ten thousand years. A seam of pale rock above a dry wash, half-hidden behind a curtain of fern. He pulled Astrid under the lip of it just as the rain hit the forest floor hard enough to bounce.

Inside, it went back farther than he'd hoped. Twenty meters, maybe thirty, the ceiling rising into darkness where their headlamps couldn't find an end. The floor was dry sand and old flood debris—driftwood, a tangle of root, the small bleached bones of something that had once been a bird. Dry. That was the whole miracle of it. Dry.

Astrid stood dripping just inside the entrance, watching the rain come down in sheets so dense the trees vanished. Her hair was plastered flat. Water ran off her jacket in ropes.

"Well," she said. "At least we won't die thirsty."

He almost smiled. Almost. His ribs still remembered the rapids, and his hands hadn't stopped shaking since the treeline, since Tomas's voice cracking through the brush telling them to *run*, goddamn it, run—and they had run, and Tomas hadn't.

"Get out of those wet things," he said. "Before you think about anything else."

She raised an eyebrow. Even soaked and shivering, she could make one eyebrow say more than most people managed in a paragraph.

"You'll freeze," he said. "Hypothermia doesn't care about propriety, and neither do I. Blanket's in the top of my pack if the wax didn't ruin it."

The notebooks were what mattered. He thought it while his fingers fumbled the pack buckles, cold-stupid, clumsy. The notebooks were Elias Vane's field notes, the photographs, the letter naming names—the whole rotten architecture of Meridian Consolidated written in a surveyor's careful hand. They'd sealed them that morning in melted candle wax and a flour sack, and now he unwrapped the bundle with the tenderness of a man handling an infant, turning it in the lamplight, checking the wax seal, finding it intact.

Dry. Everything else about them was soaked to the skin, hunted, half-starved, and grieving—but the truth was dry.

* * *

**Astrid**

She changed behind the driftwood screen with her back to him, peeling off layers that clung like second skin, and wrapped herself in the blanket, which smelled of woodsmoke and engine oil and, faintly, of him. It was absurd how much comfort there was in that. She sat against the wall where the limestone sweated cool and watched the rain erase the world.

Her father was somewhere inside that rain. Or under it. Or—he wouldn't let herself finish the thought—nowhere at all. Six weeks of silence, a burned camp, a ledger with his name struck through, boot prints heading inland toward a claim that had cost men their licenses and maybe their lives. And now this: crouched in a hole in a mountain with a blacklisted guide and a sack of evidence, waiting out a monsoon that would close every river and every road for days.

"Eat," Marguerite said, and put half a biscuit in her hand without ceremony. He ate his own share standing, facing the entrance, the way he did everything—like a man who expected the world to come at him from one direction only.

"How long?" she asked.

"Three days. Four if we're lucky." He chewed, swallowed. "After that the rivers stay up for weeks. We walk out or we don't."

"We walk out."

He glanced at her then, and in the lamplight she saw something cross his face—not doubt exactly. Recognition. As if she'd said something he'd already decided and hadn't let himself believe anyone else would.

"Your father," he said. It wasn't a question, but it opened a door.

Astrid pulled the blanket tighter. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like a barrel going downstairs.

"He taught me to read contour maps before he taught me to ride a bike. I was six. He'd spread them on the kitchen table and put my finger on a line and say, *follow it, Astrid, follow it home.*" She heard her own voice go strange, thinner. "I used to think that was a game. Now I think he was teaching me the only thing he knew how to give. Directions. Because he was never—" She stopped. Started again. "He was always leaving. Concessions, surveys, seasons in country I couldn't find on a map. My mother stopped waiting up. I never learned to stop."

Marguerite said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. Most men filled silence like a breach in a hull; he just held it open and let you decide whether to fall through.

"I told myself he'd send for me when he found what he was looking for. Isn't that stupid? Forty-one years old and I'm still waiting for my father to finish his adventure and come home." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And now I'm sitting in his evidence. That's what I have of him. Wax and paper."

"That's not nothing," Marguerite said quietly. "Paper is the most dangerous thing in the world. Ask any government."

It startled a real laugh out of her, brief and cracked. Then the silence came back, and the rain, and she found she wasn't finished falling through.

"What about you?" she asked. "You know everything about my father's ghosts. I don't even know your first name."

"You do. I gave it to the broker. You were standing right there."

"Marguerite. Which nobody calls you."

"No," he said. "Nobody does."

* * *

**Marguerite**

He'd known this moment was coming for days. Maybe since the dock. There was a debt between them that wasn't money—she'd paid triple for passage, but that wasn't it—and debts like this one collected interest in the dark, in the wet, in the hours when a man had nothing left to hold between himself and another person except the truth.

He sat down across from her, back to the rock, and looked at the rain instead of at her. Easier that way.

"There was a crossing at Karst Ridge," he said. "Rope bridge over a gorge. I'd led clients over it eleven times. It was rated, inspected, the works. My client that season was—" His throat closed. He made himself go on. "My client was family. My brother-in-law. Daniel."

Astrid didn't move. Didn't breathe loudly. She had the gift of listening like stone listens.

"The anchors failed. Both of them. Not frayed—failed, clean shear, like someone had done work on them. Daniel went into the gorge. I was on the far side. I testified at the inquiry that I checked the anchors that morning, and the inquiry said I couldn't have, and the inquiry was right, and I lost my license in two countries and my sister stopped answering letters, and for five years I've been walking tourists around lakes and counting miles because miles can't ask me questions."

He heard how flat he'd made it, how rehearsed, five years of rehearsal. He made himself add the part he never added:

"Here's what I've never said out loud. In the moment—when the rope went—I froze. Three seconds, maybe four. Daniel was calling to me and I stood there doing arithmetic, weight and angle and whether I could reach him, and the arithmetic took too long. So yes, the inquiry blamed me for the anchors. But I blame myself for the seconds. The anchors were sabotage. The seconds were mine."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere deep in the cave, water dripped with the patience of geology.

When he finally looked at her, expecting—what? Pity, which he'd have walked out into the monsoon to escape, or horror, or that careful polite distance people put around damaged men—

Astrid was crying. Silently, without drama, tears just running down her face as though they'd been waiting outside the whole time.

"My mother died thinking I blamed her for leaving," she said. "I never told her I didn't. I had four years to say it and I kept deciding tomorrow." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at it. "So don't sit there measuring your three seconds against my nothing. We're both carrying mail we never delivered."

He wanted to tell her she was wrong, that it wasn't the same, that her grief was clean and his was complicit. But the words wouldn't form, because some tired honest part of him knew she was right, or right enough, and that arguing with her would be just one more way of not delivering the mail.

"Daniel had kids," he said, because it was true and it needed air. "Two. I send money through a church in the capital. Anonymously. Like a coward."

"Like a man who can't knock on the door," she corrected. "There's a difference."

* * *

**Astrid**

The fire was small—they had fuel for small fires and no more—but it pushed the damp back a meter or two and turned the cave amber. Astrid fed it sticks one at a time and watched Marguerite check the wax-sealed notebooks a fourth time, then a fifth, laying them near the warmth but not too near, turning them like a man turning a sleeping child.

"You could have stayed invisible," she said. "That broker's job was supply run. Nobody asked you to hike inland, get shot at, lose your boat. Every step past that camp was a step you chose."

"Every step past that camp was a step you chose first," he said. "I just followed the person stealing space on my boat."

"Triple fare."

"Triple fare," he agreed, and there it was again, that almost-smile, the one that lived at the corner of his mouth like a bird that wouldn't quite land.

She studied him across the fire. Five years of mountains and guilt had weathered him, but underneath the weathering there was something she'd noticed on the river and kept noticing: a steadiness. When the hull tore open in the rapids he hadn't shouted. When the riders came over the ridge he'd put his body between hers and the treeline without appearing to decide to. He counted miles, he'd told her once, because miles couldn't leave you. She was beginning to understand that everything he said about distance was really about loss.

"What happens after?" she asked. "If we get the notebooks to someone who matters. If it works. What does Marguerite-who-nobody-calls-anything do with a cleared name?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"I genuinely don't know," he said finally. "I built a life shaped like hiding. I'm not sure it unfolds."

"Then we'll unfold it slowly," she said, and heard herself say *we*, and didn't take it back.

The fire ticked. The rain roared on beyond the entrance, a white noise so total it became a kind of silence, the two of them sealed inside it like the notebooks sealed in wax. She moved around the fire—not a decision, just gravity, just the oldest navigation there is—and sat beside him, shoulder to shoulder, sharing the blanket's edge because it was practical, because the fire was small, because there were a hundred practical reasons and one impractical one she wasn't ready to name.

He didn't move away. After a while his arm settled behind her, not quite around, a harbor a ship could choose to enter or not.

"My father used to say," she murmured, "that a trail isn't a line. It's a promise you keep re-making with every step. That you're never *on* a trail, you're always *making* one."

"Your father sounds like he talked too much," Marguerite said, "and walked enough to earn it."

"Yes," she said. "Both."

The drips in the dark kept their slow time. Her eyes grew heavy. Just before sleep took her, half-dreaming, she felt his hand close over hers—rough palm, careful grip, the hand that had held the rope for her at the flooded crossing—and she heard him say something so quiet the rain nearly kept it.

"Miles can't leave you," he said. "But they can't stay, either. I'm learning the difference."

She slept. And if either of them admitted anything that night, it was admitted the way rivers admit the rain—without announcement, without permission asked, simply rising, quietly, until neither bank could pretend anymore.

* * *

**Marguerite**

He woke once in the black hours, the fire down to coals, Astrid breathing slow against his shoulder. The rain had softened to a steady whisper. In the entrance of the cave, framed against the lesser dark of the storm, something moved.

He was on his feet with the knife out before his mind caught up—a shape, low and quick, at the edge of the lamplight. It paused. Turned. For one heartbeat the coal-light caught a face beneath a hooded oilskin: bearded, gaunt, familiar.

Tomas Reyes-Idowu put one finger to his lips.

Then he set something on the sand just inside the entrance—a small cloth bundle, tied with cord—and was gone into the rain as silently as he'd come, leaving nothing behind but wet footprints, already filling with water, pointing inland.

Marguerite stood a long time with the knife still in his hand, his heart going like a drum in the flooded dark, and did not wake her. Not yet. First he knelt and untied the cord, and saw what Tomas had left them, and understood that the game had changed again.

Inside the bundle, wrapped in oilcloth: fresh food, a stub of candle—and a strip of birch bark, scratched with charcoal in a surveyor's precise hand.

Four words, and a bearing.

*They know. Move tonight.*
