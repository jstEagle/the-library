## 17. What Astrid Carries

The rain did not fall so much as arrive, in sheets, in walls, as though the whole sky had been tipped over the valley and left to drain. Inside the limestone cave it came down to a sound—a vast, patient drumming on the stone lip above them, a white noise that erased the world beyond ten paces and made the cave feel less like shelter than like the inside of some enormous listening ear.

Astrid sat with her back against the cool rock and watched the water thread down the far wall in silver ropes. The notebooks were sealed now, wrapped in waxed canvas and coated in candle wax Marguerite had melted over their little flame until the parcels looked like strange pale fruit. He'd worked methodically, saying nothing, his hands steady in the way she had come to recognize as his only language for care. When he finished he set them in a dry niche above the flood line, and then he sat down across from her, and the fire between them burned down to a red eye.

They had talked, in the last day, more than they had talked in all the weeks before. About Elias. About Karst Ridge, about the crossing that collapsed and the client who died and the inquiry that had eaten Marguerite's name and spat out a blacklist. She knew the shape of his ruin now, or thought she did. He had given it to her plainly, without decoration, the way a man hands over a knife handle-first.

And still she was carrying something he didn't know about. It had gotten heavier every mile upstream, the way wet rope does, and she understood—with the peculiar clarity that exhaustion brings—that she could not walk into whatever came next with it hidden. Not after he had opened his own worst room to her.

The fire popped. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water found a hollow and dripped into it, a slow metronome under the rain's roar.

"Marguerite." Her voice sounded small against the storm. "There's something you need to know. Before Delphine, before any of it. Before you decide anything else on my account."

He looked up. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, five years of distance carved into it, but his eyes were patient. That patience frightened her more than anger would have. Anger she could have fought.

"I told you I came upriver for my father," she said. "That's true. But it isn't the whole truth, and the part I left out is worse than the part I gave you."

She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, an old posture, defensive, childish, and hated herself a little for it. The cave air smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and the tallow-sweetness of the melting wax.

"When I was twenty-three," she said, "my father missed my mother's memorial. Not the funeral—he made that, barely—but the memorial, the one-year, when everyone who loved her stood in the church in Coldharbour Bay and said her name out loud. He was in the field. Some concession survey in the northern basin. He sent a telegram."

She remembered it exactly. Eleven words. *Regret unavoidable stop survey critical stop will make amends stop Papa.* As if amends were a thing you could post. As if her mother's name could be balanced like a ledger column.

"I was furious," Astrid said. "Not sad-furious. The other kind. The kind where you want to break something and the only thing within reach is yourself." She turned her hands over, looked at her palms as if the signature might still be written there. "Meridian had been circling for years by then. They wanted the Vane holdings consolidated—the river leases, the timber rights, the little claims my grandfather staked before the war. They wanted it clean, all of it under one banner, and they wanted Elias's name off the title because Elias asked questions. So they sent a lawyer. A soft-spoken man with a leather case and a form already drawn up. All I had to do was sign."

Marguerite hadn't moved. The rain filled the silence she left, roaring and then easing, roaring and then easing, like something breathing.

"You signed," he said. Not a question. Just a hand extended in the dark, showing her he was following.

"I signed away my inheritance rights. Everything. To Meridian Consolidated, in trust, contingent on—" She stopped, because the next part required her to say the word *fraud* about herself, and her mouth resisted it the way a hand resists a hot iron. "Contingent on the surveys being valid. That was the clause. The transfer was conditioned on the boundary surveys being certified as true and correct. My signature witnessed the certification."

The fire had burned low enough that the shadows reached her feet. She watched them instead of him.

"For six years it meant nothing. A piece of paper in a drawer in Port Meridian, a tantrum fossilized into legalese. I forgot about it, mostly. And then we found the ledger page at Camp Ashfall, and Tomas told us what Meridian did to this valley, and I lay awake last night doing arithmetic I can't stop doing." Her voice cracked; she let it. "If the surveys are forged—if the boundaries were falsified across the whole valley—then the certification my signature witnessed was false. Which means my signature is part of the fraud. Not a victim of it. Part of it. Meridian doesn't just hold my inheritance, Marguerite. They hold a document that makes me complicit. And if we take those notebooks to Delphine, if we trigger a hearing, the first thing their lawyers will do—the very first thing, before they touch you, before they touch anyone—is pull that file and put it on the table and say: here is Elias Vane's daughter, swearing to the validity of these same surveys, taking Meridian's money for them. They'll make me the proof that everything was honest. They'll wear me like a mask."

The words ran out. The rain went on. She felt emptied and lighter at once, the way you feel after vomiting—wretched, but no longer poisoned.

Marguerite fed a stick to the fire. He did it slowly, deliberately, the way he did everything, and she realized he was giving himself time to answer well, and that this, too, was a kind of kindness.

"How old were you," he said, "when your mother died?"

"Twenty-two."

"And your father was in the field when she was buried?"

"He arrived three days after. With a rock sample." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "He gave me a rock sample from the concession. I kept it for years. I don't know what that says about either of us."

"It says you were both grieving in languages the other couldn't read." He set another stick down with care, aligning it. "I've watched you read contour maps, Astrid. You see the shape of ground from lines drawn on paper. Your father taught you that. And then you took a contract drawn by lawyers—who are also just people drawing lines on paper—and you signed it in grief, at twenty-three, against a company that has spent thirty years learning exactly how to put that paper in front of someone exactly like you. That isn't fraud. That's harvest."

"But my name is on it."

"Yes." He didn't soften it, and she was grateful; pity would have broken something. "Your name is on it. And names on documents are exactly what we're carrying to Delphine Okafor. Elias's field notes mean nothing without names attached—his name, the surveyor's names, the official who forged the certifications. Documents accuse through signatures. That's their whole grammar." He looked up at her then, and the firelight moved in his eyes. "So don't hand them your signature as a weapon. Hand it over as evidence. There's a difference, and it's the entire difference, and Delphine will see it in four seconds flat."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because I've been on the other side of a document like that." His voice went quiet, and the rain seemed to lean in to listen. "For five years I've been the name on a page that said *responsible*. Everyone who read it read guilt, because guilt was the only meaning they were permitted. But the page wasn't the truth. The page was a tool somebody used. And the day someone reads it right—" He stopped. Shrugged, a small motion, heavy as cargo. "The day someone reads it right, the name stops being a verdict and becomes a clue."

Astrid felt something shift in her chest, a settling, like ballast finding its place in a hull.

"So what do I do?" she asked. "Practically. If we reach the capital. If there's a hearing."

"You tell Delphine before Meridian's lawyers can tell anyone else. You give her the date, the circumstances, the lawyer's name if you remember it. You turn yourself from their exhibit into our witness." He almost smiled; it was a thin thing, worn at the edges. "You've been walking point since Camp Ashfall, whether you know it or not. Reading ground. Finding the trail nobody else can see. This is the same skill, Astrid. Same eyes. Different map."

She looked at her hands again. They were blistered from the rope at the flooded crossing, nicked from the treeline flight, stained with wax from sealing the notebooks. They did not look like the hands of a woman who had once signed papers in a marble office to hurt her father. They looked like working hands. Trail hands.

"I keep thinking," she said slowly, "that I came upriver to find him. Elias. And somewhere back at the burned camp that stopped being quite true. I think—I think I came to find out what he found. What was worth missing her memorial for. Worth six weeks of silence, worth—" Her throat closed. "Worth maybe dying for. Because if it was worth that much, then it was real, and if it was real, then the thing I signed away wasn't just money. It was the truth. And I want it back. Not the inheritance. The truth."

"Then you'll testify," Marguerite said.

It wasn't a question either, but she answered it anyway, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she expected.

"Yes. Even if they use my signature against me. Even if it costs what it cost you." She met his eyes across the dead fire. "You walked into this valley invisible, Marguerite. Five years of not being seen. I won't hide behind my signature to stay safe. That's just a different kind of blacklist—one you build yourself, one plank at a time, until nobody can reach you and you can't reach anybody. I'd rather be seen and struck at than safe and smudged."

The rain eased for a moment—not stopping, never stopping, but drawing breath—and in that hush they heard the mountain itself, water moving in its dark veins, the long slow work of stone becoming sand.

Marguerite nodded once. Then he reached into his pack and brought out the tin of wax and the last candle stub, and set them beside her.

"Then seal one more parcel," he said. "Your statement. Date, names, circumstances. Write it tonight while it's fresh, and we wax it with the rest. If Meridian wants your signature, they can have it—authenticated, dated, and on our terms."

She took the paper he offered. Her fingers trembled slightly as she smoothed it flat on her knee—not from fear now, she noticed, but from something closer to its opposite, some current running under the fear that had been there so long she'd mistaken it for the riverbed.

Outside, the storm went on hammering the ridge, but it had changed key. Older ears than hers would have heard it: the long front passing, the first slackening, the beginning of the end of the rain. Tomorrow, perhaps the day after, the trails would open. The descent to the district town would begin. And somewhere below them, in the drowned green country between here and Port Meridian, other things were moving too—she could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound, a pressure, a gathering.

She bent over the paper and began to write. *My name is Astrid Vane,* she wrote. *On the fourteenth of March, six years ago, I signed—*

The candle guttered, steadied, held. She wrote on, line after line, giving the darkness her name.

*

She woke near dawn to a silence so complete it startled her upright, heart slamming. The rain had stopped. Through the cave mouth the sky showed a bruised, rinsing grey going gold at the eastern edge, and the whole valley steamed like a beast cooling after labor, mist rising off the trees in slow columns.

Marguerite was already at the entrance, pack half-made, looking down-valley. He didn't turn when he spoke.

"Astrid. Come look at this."

She joined him. Below the cave, fifty meters down the slope where the trail bent around a fallen cedar, something hung from a low branch, bright against the wet green: a cloth bundle, tied with cord, unmistakably deliberate. Food-shaped. Recently placed—the leaves beneath it were still flattened, the mud still printed with boot marks.

Boot marks heading uphill. Toward them.

"Tomas?" she breathed.

"Maybe." Marguerite's voice was flat and careful, the voice of a man reading ground he didn't like. "Or maybe someone who knows we're hungry, and wants us to come down easy." He looked at the bundle a long moment, then at the trail beyond it, winding away toward the district town and everything waiting there. "Either way," he said, "we're not the only ones moving this morning."
