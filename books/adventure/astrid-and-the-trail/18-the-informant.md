## 18. The Informant

The rain did not stop so much as lose its conviction. For three days it had come down like a verdict, and now it thinned to a gray whisper, the kind of drizzle that seemed almost apologetic, as though the sky had said its piece and was waiting to see what we would do with it.

I stood at the mouth of the cave and watched the valley exhale. Mist lifted off the treeline in slow ropes. Somewhere below us, the river that had nearly taken us was settling back into its banks, still brown, still swollen, but no longer insane. The world after a monsoon has a particular smell—wet stone, crushed fern, the sweet rot of things drowned too fast—and I breathed it in the way a man breathes in a room he thought he'd never see again.

Behind me, Astrid was repacking the notebooks. She had wrapped them in oilcloth, then waxed canvas, then more oilcloth, layering them against water the way grief layers itself against hope. She worked without talking, her fingers quick and certain, and I let myself watch her for longer than was strictly necessary. There is a kind of competence that is indistinguishable from tenderness when you're tired enough.

"Trail's going to be soup," I said. "The first two miles down, anyway."

"Then we walk carefully." She tied off the last knot and looked up at me. Her hair had dried into something resembling order overnight, which felt like a small miracle given what the rest of us looked like. "How far to the town?"

"Port Meridian? Three days if the slides haven't taken the lower switchbacks. Four if they have." I turned back to the valley. "Five if someone's watching the trail."

She came to stand beside me. Neither of us said anything about the riders for a moment. We didn't need to. They were there the way weather is there—in the calculations, in the silences, in the way we both scanned the ridgeline out of habit before scanning anything else.

"They'll expect us to go down," Astrid said finally. "The town's the only place with boats."

"The town's also the only place where a wanted notice means something." I touched my ribs, where the knife gash had closed to an angry pink seam. It ached in the damp, a dull metronome reminding me of the fish market, of the rider who'd moved faster than a man his size should have, of how close the blade had come to doing arithmetic on my lungs. "Delphine Okafor is our only door, and she's behind whatever passes for law in Port Meridian."

"Then we knock."

I almost smiled. Five weeks ago this woman had stowed away on my leaking boat with a duffel bag and a lie about passage, and I had tried to put her ashore twice before giving up. Now she talked about knocking on doors in company towns the way other people talked about knocking on doors.

We broke camp while the mist was still deciding whether to be fog or memory, and started down.

* * *

The lower trail was exactly as bad as I'd promised. The monsoon had rearranged it with the indifference of a giant walking through a garden—sections of switchback simply gone, slid into the gorge, leaving raw red earth and exposed root systems like nerve endings. We moved along the intact spine of the ridge where we could, dropping into the trees when the slope opened up, and by late afternoon of the second day we had descended far enough that the air thickened and warmed and the birdsong changed key.

That was when I found the first cache.

It was tucked under a leaning slab of granite beside the trail, invisible unless you knew to look—which I did, because hiding things from people who are looking for them used to be my trade. A waterproof pouch, army surplus, wedged deep in the shadow of the stone. Inside: two tins of sardines, a brick of hardtack, a foil packet of instant coffee, and a strip of dried river fish wrapped in banana leaf.

Astrid crouched beside me and stared at it. "Is this yours?"

"No." I turned the pouch over. No markings. The knot on the drawstring was a bowline finished with a half-hitch—a working knot, tied fast, by someone whose hands knew rope the way mine did. "Someone left it here. Recently. The leaf's still green."

"Meridian wouldn't feed us."

"No. Meridian would rather we starve slowly enough to be caught." I weighed the pouch in my hand. Food from an unknown hand is either a gift or bait, and the difference between those two things is usually just how hungry you are when you find it. My stomach made its opinion known, loud enough that Astrid raised an eyebrow.

"We could test it," she said. "You eat first. If you die, I'll know not to."

"There's that famous Vane mercy."

"It's a family trait. Ask anyone who's ever sued us."

I ate one tin of sardines and half the hardtack and waited an hour, watching the trail both ways, and nothing happened except that I stopped feeling hollow. Whatever else the cache was, it wasn't poison. Poison is cheap; nobody wastes good dried fish on it.

We walked on. Two miles later, there was another cache—same pouch, same knot, same careful placement, this time with a box of matches and, inexplicably, a small tin of condensed milk. Astrid held the milk up and looked at me.

"Who feeds people condensed milk in the middle of nowhere?"

"Somebody who knows what comfort is," I said slowly. And then, because the thought arrived whole and unwelcome: "Somebody who's been living rough out here long enough to know what a person misses."

Tomas Reyes-Idowu had lived rough out here for eleven years. Tomas Reyes-Idowu had been dragged off a ridge by Meridian's riders three weeks ago, covering our retreat with a lever-action rifle and a courage I hadn't earned. I had carried his absence ever since, the way you carry a stone in your boot—not crippling, but present with every step.

"He's alive," Astrid said quietly. She had reached the same conclusion, or close to it. "He got away."

"Or he escaped and they're using him as a lure." I hated myself for saying it, but saying it was cheaper than believing it and being wrong. "One man can't outrun four riders through country he knows. Unless—" I stopped.

"Unless what?"

"Unless he isn't running. Unless he's tracking *us*." I looked back up the trail, at the wet green silence of the forest. "Think about it. He knows these hills better than the men chasing him. He knows the routes we'd take down. He could stay ahead of us, parallel, leaving food where he calculates we'll pass. He's been herding us, Astrid. Gently. Toward the town."

"Why?"

"Because he heard about Delphine Okafor somehow, or guessed it. Because he wants the notebooks to get there as badly as we do. Or because—" I hesitated over the last reason, because it was the one I believed. "Because he owes us. He thinks he owes us. He covered our retreat and paid for it, and a man like Tomas doesn't leave a debt standing."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Then she put the condensed milk in her pack, next to the notebooks, with the care of someone handling evidence.

"Then we say thank you," she said, "by getting there."

* * *

We descended all the next day through country that grew steadily more human. First it was small things: a rusted trap stake, old and orange with rust, nothing recent. Then a stretch of trail cut with real tools, the marks of a mattock decades old but the grade still true. Then, near dusk, a shrine—nothing elaborate, a shelf of flat stones under a fig tree with a chipped ceramic saint and a coil of incense ash—but kept, swept clean, the offering bowl holding fresh marigolds gone slightly soft in the damp.

Somebody lived in these hills. Somebody who swept their shrine and tied perfect bowlines and knew our route.

We made camp in a hollow above a stream, cold-camping, no fire, and I took first watch. The night was enormous and wet and full of small sounds, and around midnight I heard one sound that didn't belong: a footfall, single, deliberate, maybe forty meters downslope. Not a deer. Deer don't pause. This paused—once, twice—and then withdrew, unhurried, the way a man withdraws when he wants you to know he was there but doesn't want to be seen.

I didn't call out. Calling out to a ghost in the dark is how you start conversations you can't finish. But I sat with my hand on the Kestrel's spare tiller extension—I'd been carrying it as a club since the dock—and listened until the forest folded the sound away entirely.

In the morning there was a third cache at the edge of our hollow. This one had, besides food, a scrap of paper.

I read it twice before showing Astrid. The handwriting was cramped and slanted, the letters of a man who'd spent years writing survey notes in wind:

*TOWN IS WATCHED. RIDERS AT THE FERRY LANDING AND THE BRIDGE HOUSE. COME IN BY THE SALT ROAD, AFTER DARK. THE CLERK'S HOUSE HAS A BLUE DOOR. — T.R.I.*

Below that, a rough sketch: the layout of Port Meridian's waterfront, drawn from memory, with three X's marking positions. One at the landing. One at the bridge. One, ominously, at the head of the salt road itself—the very road he was telling us to take.

"He's telling us they're on the salt road," Astrid said, tracing the X with her finger, "and to take it anyway."

"He's telling us they're at the *head* of it. Fixed post. Which means it can be watched, timed, avoided." I studied the sketch. It was good work—better than good. Eleven years of living alone will sharpen a man's map-hand to a razor. "Or it means he wants us funneled somewhere specific, and the riders are wherever he says they aren't."

"You keep doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Believing him and doubting him in the same breath." She looked up at me, and there was no accusation in it, only exhaustion and something underneath the exhaustion that I was afraid to name because naming things gives them weight. "Which one is it, Marguerite?"

I folded the note along its crease. "Both. That's the honest answer. I've been burned by kindness that wasn't kindness, and I've been saved by strangers who had every reason to walk past. You learn to hold both possibilities without letting either one make your decisions for you." I tapped the sketch. "What I trust is the detail. The blue door. The ferry landing. Those check against what I know of the town. A liar inventing a trap wouldn't bother with accuracy—he'd bother with urgency. There's no urgency here. Just information, and a man's initials."

"And the footfall last night?"

"A man making sure we got the note." I allowed myself the smallest smile. "And making sure we knew he could have done worse than a note."

* * *

We came down off the ridge on the fourth morning, and Port Meridian announced itself the way company towns always do: by smell before sight. Diesel and drying fish and the sweet chemical tang of the processing sheds, layered over woodsmoke and mud. Then the roofs appeared through the haze—corrugated iron gone to rust-brown, a church spire, the crane arms of the ferry landing sticking up like heron necks—and below it all the wide brown shoulder of the river, carrying the whole drowned valley toward the sea.

We lay in the treeline above the town for most of the day, watching. Watching is a discipline I learned the hard way, in years when failing to watch meant losing clients, and I taught it to Astrid in the cave during the rain: pick your landmarks, count the changes, give every moving thing a reason or mark it as a question.

By mid-afternoon we had our answers. There were riders in Port Meridian—three that we could see. One lounged against the rail at the ferry landing, exactly where Tomas's X sat, chewing something and watching the gangway with the patience of a man being paid by the day. One stood at the bridge house, talking to the constable, who laughed at whatever he said in a way that told us everything about the constable. The third rode the streets in a slow circuit, unhurried, a man sweeping a floor he expected to stay dirty.

They weren't hunting. They were waiting. There is a difference, and the difference is that hunters move and waiters watch doors, and we were going to have to come through a door.

"There," Astrid whispered. She pointed to a thread of track branching off the main descent, west of town, dropping toward a line of low buildings wreathed in white smoke—salt kilns, their reek sharp even at this distance. The salt road. It curved wide around the town's eastern eye and came in behind the waterfront, past the kilns, past a jumble of net-drying racks, and let out onto the street perhaps sixty meters from a row of narrow shopfronts.

One of those shopfronts, according to a man who drew maps in the dark, had a blue door.

"After dark," I said.

"After dark," she agreed.

We waited out the last of the light in the fern brake, sharing the last of Tomas's sardines, and I found myself thinking about him with an intensity that surprised me. A disgraced surveyor, ruined by the same company that ruined me, who had chosen eleven years of solitude over surrender, and who had come out of that solitude—or out of a Meridian cage, whichever it was—to leave condensed milk and matchboxes along a trail for two people he'd known less than a week. There are debts the world keeps books on, and I had begun to suspect that mine to Tomas Reyes-Idowu was written in ink that doesn't wash out.

When full dark settled, we moved.

The salt road was clay and slick as soap, and we went down it slow, boots wrapped in burlap to kill the sound, Astrid ahead of me because she was lighter and because she'd insisted and because I had learned, somewhere between the rapids and the cave, that insisting back cost more than it bought. The kilns glowed orange on our left, throwing heat and shifting shadows, and we passed through their light in the gaps between the workers' rounds, pressed against a wall of stacked peat, hearts going like trapped birds.

Then the net racks. Then the street.

Port Meridian at night was quieter than I'd hoped and not as quiet as I'd feared. A generator droned somewhere. Music leaked from a doorway—guitar, out of tune, played by someone who didn't care. Rain had begun again, fine and warm, beading on everything, and the lamplight along the waterfront street turned each drop into a small gold event.

I counted doorways. Narrow shopfronts, shuttered, shuttered, shuttered—and then paint, faded but unmistakable even in the lamplight, the color of evening seen through water.

Blue.

I put my hand on Astrid's arm and we crossed the street in the space between two passing clouds of generator noise, and I raised my knuckles to the blue door of Delphine Okafor's house—

—and stopped.

There was a mark on the door. Small, chalked, at knee height, where only someone crouching would see it: a single line, crossed by three shorter ones. A surveyor's bench-mark symbol. Old habit, private shorthand, the kind of sign a man uses when he wants to say *I was here* to exactly one person and no one else.

Tomas had been here. Recently—the chalk was dry but unfaded, hours not days.

And beneath it, in the same cramped hand, one word:

*Hurry.*

The door opened before I could knock.

Not Delphine Okafor. A girl, fourteen maybe, holding a lamp, eyes wide. Behind her, from deep inside the house, a voice I recognized from a ridge and a rifle and a debt I couldn't repay—thinner than I remembered, hoarse, urgent, and unmistakably alive:

"Get inside. Both of you. They followed you down the mountain—they just don't know it yet."
