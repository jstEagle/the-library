## 19. Town Without Law

The river widened the way grief does—gradually, then all at once—and Port Meridian appeared out of the morning haze like something the water had decided to give back.

I had imagined a town. What I saw was a mouth: a brown delta of log booms and fuel drums and corrugated roofs, all of it arranged around a single purpose the way a body arranges itself around a wound. Stacked timber rose in gray cliffs along the north bank, cut and numbered and waiting. A dredge squatted midstream like a sleeping animal, its cables trailing into the current. And everywhere the same word, stenciled in faded white on crates, on barges, on the flank of a warehouse big enough to hold a church: MERIDIAN.

"Company town," Marguerite said from behind me at the tiller. He said it the way other men say *landslide* or *white water*—a name for a thing that kills people.

"You've been here before."

"No." A pause. The engine idled down as we slid past the last of the boom chains. "But I know what I'm looking at. When one company owns the mill, the dock, the store, and the payroll, it owns everything else too. It just hasn't gotten around to writing it down."

The Kestrel came alongside the public wharf—the only structure on the waterfront that didn't fly Meridian's colors, and even that felt like an oversight someone would correct eventually. I tied off while Marguerite killed the engine, and in the sudden quiet I heard the town working: the shriek of a bandsaw somewhere inland, the groan of loaded timber, men's voices pitched low and flat, the way voices get when they're being careful.

We had come down out of the high country over four days, following the food caches that had appeared for us like breadcrumbs left by a hand we couldn't see. Tomas, we hoped. Or hoped and feared in equal measure, because if Tomas could find us, so could the riders. My ribs still carried the memory of the flooded crossing; my hands still remembered the rope. But we were here, and the notebooks were here, sewn flat against my belly inside my coat in oilcloth and wax, riding against me like a second heartbeat.

Elias's notebooks. My father's handwriting, cramped and furious, documenting five years of forged boundary surveys. Somewhere in this town was a court clerk named Delphine Okafor who could make those pages mean something. That had been the plan since the cave. Plans, I was learning, were things the river tolerated right up until it didn't.

* * *

Port Meridian smelled of diesel and sawdust and, underneath both, the sweet rot of the millpond where the slab wood went to die. The main street ran uphill from the wharf—a street called, with a straight face, Independence Avenue—and it was lined with buildings that all seemed to lean slightly toward the company warehouse, as if drawn by gravity.

There was a general store. There was a hotel with a veranda and no guests. There was a church, a brothel, a cookhouse, and a building with brass letters reading DISTRICT MAGISTRACY that looked, notably, newer and better kept than anything else on the street except the Meridian office beside it. The two buildings shared a drainage ditch. They also, I suspected, shared more than that.

"They say the magistrate owns shares," Marguerite murmured. He'd gone quiet and watchful the way he did in rapids, eyes moving, reading the water of the street. "Tomas mentioned it. Bought them through his wife's name, or his brother's. Something clean-looking."

"And the constable?"

"The constable is paid out of a budget the magistrate approves." He shrugged. "You don't need to own a dog if you feed it."

I looked at him sideways. "You have a lot of opinions about towns you've never visited."

"I have a lot of experience with places that look like this." His jaw tightened around whatever came next, and swallowed it. Five years of practice at swallowing, I thought. Karst Ridge lived behind his teeth.

We needed three things: food, information, and Delphine Okafor. We could afford to be seen buying the first. The second and third required care.

The general store was cool and dim and smelled of kerosene and dried fish. The man behind the counter was old and watery-eyed and rang up our rice, salt pork, matches, and lamp oil without once looking at our faces, which was either courtesy or training. On the wall behind him, pinned among seed advertisements and a tide chart years out of date, hung a notice board.

I saw it the way you see a snake on a trail—not because you're looking, but because some older part of you is always looking.

Paper, yellowed at the edges but recently re-pinned, the pin bright and new. A printed header: WANTED FOR QUESTIONING IN THE MATTER OF THE KARST RIDGE CROSSING FAILURE. Below it, a photograph.

Marguerite.

Five years younger. Hair longer, face fuller, the lines around his mouth not yet carved into permanence. He was scowling at the camera with the particular fury of a man who has been told to stand still while his life is taken apart. Someone had written across the bottom corner in fresh ink: **REWARD DOUBLED — BY ORDER OF THE REGIONAL COMMISSIONER.**

I did not turn around. That was the first discipline of the mountains: when you see the drop, you don't look directly at it, you look at your feet and move them carefully. I counted the money into the old man's palm. I took the parcel. I turned, unhurried, and walked out into the light where Marguerite stood examining a rack of fishing line he clearly had no interest in.

"There's a notice," I said quietly, in the space between us, under the noise of the street.

"I know." He hadn't stopped looking at the fishing line. "Saw it through the window. Doubled?"

"Doubled. By order of the regional commissioner."

Something moved across his face—not fear. Something colder and more tired. "Doubled means somebody's nervous. Rewards don't go up unless the people paying them are worried about results." He set the fishing line down. "Five years that face has been worth money and nobody collected. Now suddenly it's worth twice as much, in the same season that notebooks exist." He met my eyes. "They're not hunting me, Astrid. They're hunting what you're carrying. I'm just the shape it hides in."

* * *

We took a room at the hotel because not taking a room would have been stranger than taking one, and because Marguerite said—correctly—that a town like this watched for people who behaved like fugitives rather than people who behaved like travelers. So we signed the register in names that were almost ours. He wrote *M. Reyes*. I wrote *A. Vane* and let the clerk assume it was common enough not to matter. Our packs went upstairs. The notebooks stayed against my body. They would stay there until they passed out of my hands into Delphine Okafor's, and I had already decided that if it came to it, they would pass out of my hands only into hers, or into fire, or nowhere at all.

The room had one bed, one chair, a window overlooking the millpond, and walls thin enough to carry conversations from the veranda below. We sat on the floor with our backs to the wall beneath the window and ate bread and tinned fish and listened.

Mostly it was mill talk—shift changes, a broken cable, the price of cedar. But talk in a company town circles like a bird over carrion, and eventually it circled where we needed it to.

"—assizes sitting in the capital next month, that's what my cousin says—"

"—won't matter, half the docket gets thrown out before it's heard—"

"—new clerk up from the capital, though. Woman. Okafor. She's been pulling the old files, asking questions nobody's asked in years—"

My hand found Marguerite's forearm in the dark and gripped it. He covered my hand with his own and pressed once: *heard it. Stay still.*

"—Okafor," another voice said, and this one had weight to it, the slow certainty of a man who'd had a few drinks and a few grievances. "She was here twenty years ago too. Before your time. She handled the Karst Ridge papers." A pause, the sound of a glass finding a table. "Never believed the verdict, they say. Never said so loud, neither. Smart woman."

"Smart women don't last long here," someone else said, and there was laughter, but it was the thin kind, the kind that checks the doorway first.

The veranda emptied soon after. Marguerite and I stayed on the floor a long time, shoulder to shoulder, watching the millpond light shift on the ceiling.

"She was at Karst Ridge," I said finally. "She read the inquiry. She never believed it."

"People who read closely rarely believe convenient things." He was quiet a moment. "It's why most people don't read closely."

"Do you believe her? That she'll help?"

He considered it honestly, which was one of the things I had come to trust about him—he didn't offer comfort he couldn't stand behind. "I believe she pulled the old files. I believe that costs her something every day she stays. Whether she'll risk what's left of her career on two strangers off the river—" He exhaled. "We'll find out. Tomorrow. In daylight, separately, and not near this hotel."

* * *

I slept badly, which was becoming less an event than a condition. Toward dawn I gave up and sat at the window, watching the town wake.

That was when I understood Port Meridian completely—or as completely as anyone can understand a thing they're afraid of. It wasn't that the law was absent. It was worse. The law was present, uniformed, housed in a fine new building with brass letters, and it belonged to the same hands that owned the timber, the dock, the store, the debt at the company store, the magistrate's shares, and the doubled reward pinned to the board with a bright new pin. Justice here wasn't broken. It was employed.

And Marguerite Coldharbour—five years invisible, five years counting miles instead of days precisely so that no ledger anywhere would hold his name—had walked into the one town in the valley where his name was literally nailed to a wall with a price beside it.

Below, the early shift crossed the square toward the mill, heads down, collars up. Two constables stood outside the magistracy, drinking from tin cups, doing nothing with great diligence. And propped against the notice board, updating it, a third man was unpinning papers and pinning fresh ones.

I leaned closer to the glass.

He smoothed a new sheet flat against the board. Even at this distance, even in the gray light, I knew the format—I'd grown up around my father's expedition notices, and government paper has a grammar all its own. This was bigger than a reward notice. This was a proclamation, a seal at the bottom, a border of official black.

The man finished, stepped back, gathered his old papers, and walked away. The square emptied. The new notice hung alone in the morning damp, ink beginning to bleed softly at its edges, announcing something to a town that was already awake and already careful.

Whatever it said, it had been posted at dawn, when only watchers would see it first.

I woke Marguerite with a hand on his shoulder, and when he opened his eyes I pointed at the board, at the small dark rectangle of fresh official paper, and said, "We need to see what that says before the rest of the town does."
