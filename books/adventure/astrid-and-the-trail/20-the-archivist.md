## 20. The Archivist

Port Meridian announced itself the way rot announces itself in fruit: by smell before sight. Diesel and drying fish first, then the sweeter stink of the sawmills upriver, then—underneath it all, so constant you stopped noticing until the wind shifted—a chemical tang from the tailings ponds that no one in town would ever name aloud. We came down off the ridge on the third morning after the rain slackened, Astrid ahead of me with the notebooks wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat, and I watched her stop at the tree line and look at the town the way you look at a dog you suspect of biting.

"It's smaller than I remembered," she said.

"Company towns shrink when they're ashamed."

She didn't answer that. She'd stopped answering most of my bitter observations somewhere on the second day of descent, not out of disagreement but out of a kind of discipline, as if she were rationing my cynicism the way we rationed the last of the rice. Her father's notebooks had changed something in her. She walked differently now—not lighter, but aimed. A woman with coordinates.

I knew what she was carrying. I'd helped seal the wax myself, kneeling under an overhang while the monsoon hammered the valley flat, watching her press the oilcloth closed over Elias Vane's field notes: boundary surveys, original measurements, photographs of stakes driven into ground that Meridian Consolidated's maps said was empty. Proof that the company had redrawn a valley to suit itself, and buried anyone who measured honestly. Tomas Reyes-Idowu had told us the shape of it in his cabin full of snares and old grievances. The notebooks told us the bones.

And I knew what it would cost to use them. Delphine Okafor's name had come out of Tomas's mouth like a coin from a riverbed—the one clerk who'd handled the Karst Ridge inquiry files and never believed the verdict, who'd retired rather than sign the summary, who lived now in Port Meridian because even exiles wash up somewhere. If anyone could authenticate those notebooks, make them more than paper, make them *evidence*—it was her.

If she still cared. If she wasn't bought. If she hadn't spent thirty years learning that believing things gets old women hurt.

We went in anyway.

* * *

The courthouse sat on the highest street in town, which told you everything about how Port Meridian understood hierarchy. It was a squat stone building with a tin roof gone orange with rust, and its steps were occupied by three dogs and a man selling roasted plantains who gave us the long look of someone who memorized faces for a living. I kept my head down. Five years of practice made it automatic—I became a tired man with a bad shoulder and river mud to his knees, a man whose face slid off memory like water off waxed canvas. But the plantain seller's eyes followed us up the street anyway, and I filed him away under *costs*.

Inside smelled of dust and lamp oil and old paper, that particular sweetness of documents aging past their usefulness into history. The registry hall was dim. A ceiling fan turned with the lazy reluctance of something being paid too little. Behind a counter scarred by decades of elbows sat a woman so old she seemed carved from the same material as the building—small, straight-backed, skin like dark walnut, reading glasses pushed up into white hair cropped close as a soldier's.

A placard on the counter read: **REGISTRY CLOSED FOR INVENTORY.**

"We're not here for the registry," Astrid said.

The woman looked up. Her eyes were extraordinary—pale amber, almost gold, set in that ancient face like coins in a riverbed, and they did not slide off me the way faces usually did. They landed. They stayed.

"Then what are you here for?" Her voice was dry and precise, a voice that had spent forty years reading legal language aloud without once letting it mean anything less than exactly what it said.

"My father is Elias Vane," Astrid said. "And I have his field notebooks."

Something moved behind the amber eyes. Not surprise—recognition, maybe, or the memory of recognition. She took off her glasses, folded them, set them on the counter with a small deliberate click.

"I'm going to ask you both to step outside," she said, "walk around the block once, and come back in through the side door on Cordwainer Lane. There's a man across the street who has been drinking coffee since eight o'clock this morning and has not once looked at his newspaper upside down correctly. When you come back, come back as people doing inventory business. Understood?"

Astrid glanced at me. I was already moving. Some instincts you don't discuss.

* * *

The side door opened onto a corridor lined with filing cabinets, and Delphine Okafor—she introduced herself with a handshake that gripped like a young man's—led us deep into the building's bowels, through a records room where the light came in dusty bars through high windows, to a worktable beneath a map of the province from 1961. She locked the door behind us. Then she held out her hand for the oilcloth bundle.

"Before you give it to me," she said, "you should understand what you're handing over. Once these are authenticated—if they can be authenticated—they stop being yours. They become exhibits. Exhibits have owners, and owners have schedules, and schedules have courts attached. Do you understand me?"

"They're not mine," Astrid said. "They're my father's. And he's not here to own anything."

Delphine unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon and the speed of someone who had done this ten thousand times. She lifted the top notebook, opened it, and went very still.

For a long moment the only sound was the fan in some distant room, turning its slow paid-by-the-hour turn. Delphine's thumb moved along the margin of a page, following a column of figures, and her lips moved slightly, checking arithmetic the way other old women mouthed prayers.

"Elias Vane," she said finally. "He surveyed the upper Grey Fork in '09. I remember his submissions. They were beautiful. Every chainage noted, every benchmark photographed, every closure error accounted for in his own hand." She looked up at Astrid, and there was something raw in the amber now, something that had been waiting a long time. "Beautiful work is how they caught them, you know. You can't forge beauty. A liar's numbers are smooth. An honest surveyor's numbers have texture—little imperfections, weather delays, re-measurements. These—" she tapped the page "—have texture."

"You knew about the forgeries," I said. It wasn't a question. My voice came out flatter than I intended, scraped thin by five years of holding the same question behind my teeth.

Delphine Okafor turned those gold eyes on me fully for the first time, and I felt it like a searchlight.

"And you are Marguerite Coldharbour," she said.

The room got very quiet. Astrid tensed beside me. Outside, somewhere, a truck changed gears.

"I never believed the Karst Ridge verdict," she went on, before I could decide whether to run. "I handled the intake on that inquiry. Every deposition, every photograph of the crossing, every anchor bolt in its evidence bag. I catalogued them all." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, resting on the notebook, had gone white-knuckled. "The anchors were cut with a torch, Mr. Coldharbour. A *torch*. Rope failure doesn't need a cutting tool. Sabotage doesn't get investigated by asking the rope why it broke. And then the file went quiet, and the summary came down from above already written, and I was asked to certify a conclusion I had watched get manufactured. I retired instead. Or was retired. The distinction stopped mattering."

"Why help us now?" Astrid asked quietly. "After all this time?"

Delphine smiled, and it transformed her face—suddenly I could see the girl she'd been, some sharp-eyed clerk's daughter in a district school, counting everything.

"Because I am seventy-three years old," she said, "and I have spent eleven of them keeping one file in my head, waiting for someone to walk through my door carrying the other half of it. Do you know what that does to a person? It makes her inventory her registry very, very carefully." She drew a key from around her neck on a cord, crossed to a cabinet marked DECEASED MATTERS — PRE-1970, unlocked it, and pulled out a slim folder tied with legal tape. "Karst Ridge. Original intake. Never destroyed, though it was ordered destroyed twice. I told them the furnace was broken." The smile again, brief and fierce. "It wasn't."

She laid the folder beside the notebooks, open, and for a moment she just looked at the two together—an old woman standing between two halves of a sentence twenty years long.

"The anchor bolts from Karst Ridge," she said, "were supplied by a subcontractor. Halloran Rigging & Supply. A subsidiary of—" 

"Meridian Consolidated," Astrid and I said together.

"Meridian Consolidated." Delphine nodded slowly. "The same subcontractor that supplied the survey equipment for the Grey Fork claims. The same company whose security chief signed the requisition for the torch that cut your rope, Mr. Coldharbour—and whose signature appears, if my memory serves, on the land transfer that swallowed the upper valley after the surveyors who wouldn't lie lost their licenses." She looked at Astrid. "Your father found the original field notes. That's why the camp burned. That's why he's missing. And that's why what's sitting on this table is the most dangerous paper in this province."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon. Delphine was methodical in a way that bordered on liturgy—every notebook page examined under a loupe, compared against known samples of Elias Vane's hand from the registry's own archives, the paper tested for age, the ink for consistency. She narrated as she worked, half teaching, half thinking aloud, and I found myself leaning in despite myself. It had been a long time since I'd watched someone do a job with total integrity. It was like watching someone navigate by stars in a country that had outlawed the sky.

"Authentication isn't a stamp," she said, holding a page to the window light. "Anyone can forge a stamp. Authentication is testimony. It's me, under oath, saying: I have examined these documents, I have compared them against verified originals, and I attest they are what they claim to be. Which means—" she set the page down and looked at us over her glasses "—it means I don't just authenticate the notebooks. I put myself inside whatever happens next. Are we clear?"

"Clear," Astrid said.

"Then hear the rest. The assizes sit in the regional capital twice a year. The current session closes in nine days. After that, nothing moves until the new year—new clerks, new dockets, and Meridian's lawyers will have months to find these notebooks, or find you, or find a fire that finds them first." She began wrapping the notebooks back in their oilcloth, her movements unhurried but her eyes urgent. "To be admitted, they must reach the capital registrar *before* session close, with my certification attached and my sworn statement available. Nine days. The river journey alone is six, if the water cooperates, and the monsoon has not been cooperating."

"The Kestrel can make it in four," I said. "Three if I push her and the rapids stay kind."

"They won't stay kind," Delphine said mildly. "Nothing stays kind this time of year. But yes. A boat." She studied me for a moment, and something almost gentle entered her expression. "You understand what happens after, Mr. Coldharbour. If this goes where it should go, there will be a hearing. Hearings have witnesses. Witnesses have names. Your name is on a warrant in this district—I've seen the file; I filed it, God forgive me. The moment you stand up in that courtroom, you hand yourself to the same system that buried you."

"I know what it costs," I said.

"Do you?" For the first time, the clerk's precision cracked, and underneath was something older and wearier and kinder. "I watched them take five years from you without a trial. I read every page of how they did it. So I will tell you what I told myself for eleven years: justice delayed is not justice denied—it's justice *stored*, like seed grain, waiting for a season. But seed grain rots if you wait forever." She tied the oilcloth closed and pressed the bundle into Astrid's hands. "Nine days. Go home, prepare your boat, and come back tomorrow at dawn for the certification papers. I'll have my statement drafted. And I'll have one more thing."

"What thing?" Astrid asked.

"A name in the capital. A registrar who owes me a favor from a case neither of us ever speaks about." Delphine allowed herself the smile one last time, and it was like sun through storm cloud. "You cannot do this alone. That is the first lesson of every honest trail: sooner or later, you must trust somebody, and the trick is choosing somebody worth the risk." Her gold eyes moved from Astrid to me and back. "I've decided you two are worth the risk. Pray I've chosen well."

* * *

We left through the side door as the light was going copper over the rooftops. The coffee-drinker across from the courthouse was gone; in his place stood a younger man reading the same newspaper, and I made a note of his boots—heavy-soled, city-made, wrong for a dockside town—and the way he didn't look up as we passed, which was itself a kind of looking.

"He'll report us," I said quietly, once we'd turned down toward the waterfront.

"Probably," Astrid said. She had the bundle slung across her body again, one hand resting on it like a rider's hand on a horse's neck. "Which means we have until tomorrow dawn, maybe less, before Meridian knows exactly what we're carrying and exactly where we'll be."

"Then we don't sleep at the boarding house. We load the Kestrel tonight, in the dark, and be ready to cast off the moment Delphine hands us those papers."

Astrid stopped walking. The harbor lay below us, gray-green and restless, masts knocking softly against each other like teeth. She looked out at it, then at me, and in the low light her face was unreadable and entirely readable at once.

"Marguerite. What she said in there. About the hearing. About surrendering yourself." Her voice was careful, the way it always was when she approached this subject, circling it like it might spook. "You heard her. You know what it means."

"I've known what it means since a cave in the rain," I said. "Since before that, maybe. Since the day I read my own blacklist and understood they hadn't ruined me—they'd *filed* me. Put me away where I couldn't testify." I looked out at the water, at the Kestrel riding her mooring with her patched hull sitting low and patient. "Five years I thought staying invisible was survival. Turns out it was just a slower way of disappearing."

Astrid said nothing, but her hand found my sleeve—just the sleeve, just for a moment, the way she touched things she meant to keep—and then we went down the hill together into the smell of fish and diesel, two people with nine days and a river that owed us nothing.

Behind us, on the courthouse steps, the plantain seller had stopped selling. He was looking at the sky, at the stacked clouds building over the western ridge, and he was shaking his head slowly, the way men do when they count the days left in a season and don't like the number.

Dawn was seven hours away. And somewhere between here and the capital, I already knew, the river was waiting to ask us what we were willing to pay.
