## 21. Twenty-Six Hours

**Marguerite**

The dock at Port Meridian smelled of diesel and brine and the sweet rot of fish heads thrown to the gulls, and it was empty in the way that meant watched. I'd stood on enough docks in enough countries to know the difference between an abandoned place and a cleared one. This one had been cleared. The night crews that should have been mending nets were absent. The fuel shack's lamp burned behind a shuttered window. And at the far end, where the pilings gave way to the mudflats, a man sat on an upturned crate with a cigarette he wasn't smoking, looking at nothing with the particular patience of someone being paid by the hour.

I counted him twice before I let myself look away. There was a second man loitering near the customs shed, too well-dressed for the hour, reading a newspaper in the dark. A third would be somewhere I hadn't found yet. There always was.

Twenty-six hours. That was what Delphine Okafor had given us, in her dry clerk's voice, over tea gone cold in chipped cups: *The assizes close tomorrow evening. If the notebooks aren't before the registrar by then, Meridian's lawyers will have an injunction filed by morning after, and everything we've bled for becomes paper in a drawer.* She'd said it without drama, the way she said everything, as though justice were simply a filing deadline like any other. Maybe that was the only honest way left to say it.

The last boat downriver left at dawn. Not dawn-plus-an-hour, not when the fog lifted. Dawn. Captain Sefu ran a strict schedule and a shallow draft, and neither waited for anyone.

Astrid stood beside me under the eave of the net-mender's hut, out of the drizzle that had begun to fall like something undecided about becoming rain. She was watching the same dock I was, and I could feel her doing arithmetic—the same arithmetic, probably, arriving at the same ugly sum.

"Three," she said quietly.

"At least."

"The one on the crate has been there since we came down from Delphine's street. He didn't check his watch once. Men who are waiting for something check their watches."

"You've been learning surveillance from a court clerk."

"I've been learning survival from you." She said it flatly, without flattery, which was how she said most true things. "So tell me. How do we get on that boat?"

I looked at the Kestrel, moored two slips down, riding low and patient, her patched hull holding the river out through sheer stubbornness and my bad caulking. She was too conspicuous. Everyone in Port Meridian knew the leaking boat with the blacklisted guide; I'd been buying tar and timber here for nine days under a name that fooled no one who mattered.

"We don't," I said. "Not as ourselves."

* * *

We planned it in the back room of the mission infirmary, where the nurse owed Delphine a favor going back twenty years and asked no questions beyond whether Astrid's stitches needed changing. They didn't. We sat among the smell of carbolic and old wood, lit by a single hurricane lamp, and I laid it out the way I used to lay out routes for clients—every hazard marked, every fallback named, because a plan without a retreat isn't a plan, it's a wish.

"They're watching the dock for us," I said. "Two travelers with luggage, one of them limping, one of them a woman with a coat she never takes off. So we don't give them that. We give them a departure they can see, and a departure they can't."

"Decoys."

"Decoys. Two pilgrims' bundles—bedroll, tin pot, oilcloth, the works. Cheap canvas, travel-stained. We hire someone to carry them down to the dock before first light, buy passage loudly, stand where the lamplight hits. The watchers report a couple boarding Sefu's boat. By the time anyone looks closely, we're not there—and even if they board and search, all they find is a bedroll and a tin pot."

"And us?"

"Fish hold. The *Marianne*, the boat that runs ice and catch downriver ahead of the market boats. She leaves an hour before dawn from the north slip, no passenger manifest, no questions. Old Bello owes me for pulling his propeller off a snag two seasons back. He'll take two more crates of fish, if the fish don't complain."

Astrid turned the lamp wick down a fraction, and the shadows leaned in around us. "And the notebooks?"

That was the part I'd been turning over since the courthouse steps. Paper burns. Paper soaks. Paper can be taken from a dead man's pack in ten seconds. Everything we had—Elias's field notes, the photographs, the letter naming the surveyor who forged the boundaries, Tomas's testimony written out in Delphine's careful hand—all of it fit inside an oilskin packet the size of a hymnal, and all of it was worth more than every life in this town, and everyone who might want it knew roughly where we slept.

"Not in the packs," I said. "Not on me. If they stop me, they stop me anyway—I'm still a wanted name here, whatever Delphine says about warrants and jurisdiction. But they'll hesitate with you. Barely. But barely is a currency."

"So they go on me."

"On your body. Sewn into the coat."

She looked down at the coat in question—the grey wool one, salt-stiffened, patched at the elbow, the one she'd worn from the Coldwater country down through the monsoon and out the other side. It hung on a nail by the door where the nurse had put it, dripping slowly, looking like exactly what it was: a woman's old coat that had been through a war.

"There's a seamstress two streets over," Astrid said. "Widow Ansa. Delphine mentioned her."

"Delphine mentioned her because Delphine has already spoken to her. Nothing in this town happens that that woman hasn't arranged three days in advance." I almost smiled. "Go tonight. Tell Ansa to open the lining along the left side, front and back, and set the packet flat against your ribs. Wax-sealed oilskin, double-stitched, waxed thread. It should sit like a second skin. If anyone pats you down, they find a woman's ribs and nothing else."

"And if the boat goes down?"

"Then the packet goes down with you, and the river keeps its secrets, and Meridian wins." I said it plainly because she deserved plainness. "There's no version of this where the documents survive and you don't. You're not carrying them. You *are* them, now. Until the registrar's stamp goes on the receipt, you and those field notes are the same object."

She was quiet for a moment. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, the drizzle thickened into honest rain, drumming on the infirmary roof like fingers testing a lock.

"My father used to say," she said finally, "that the difference between a courier and a coward is just whether you keep walking after you realize what you're carrying." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He also used to say never trust a man who plans only one route. So. What's our retreat, Marguerite?"

"If the dock goes wrong—if they take the decoys or board the *Marianne*—we split. You walk out the market road east toward the mission lands and put yourself under Delphine's cousin's roof until the assizes close, then come in by the capital road with a church party. Nobody searches a church party. I stay visible, draw them after me upriver, and buy you the hours."

"No."

"It's not a request."

"I know it's not. That's why I'm saying no." She leaned forward into the lamplight, and I saw the exhaustion in her face and the thing underneath the exhaustion that had carried her up a murdered valley and across a flooded river and through every mile since, and I understood, not for the first time, that I was planning around the wrong person. "You freeze at rope bridges and you take knife wounds meant for other people and you count miles because miles can't leave you. I know all of it. But you don't get to hand me the safe road while you walk into their hands. We go together or the plan dies here."

"Astrid—"

"Together," she said, "or nowhere. Those were the terms on the river and they haven't changed."

I wanted to argue. God knows I built the arguments—they lined up in me like cargo waiting to be loaded: *you're the documents, you're the irreplaceable one, I'm expendable, I've been expendable for five years and it's a comfortable fit.* But I'd watched her handle a line in white water. I'd watched her talk me across a bridge my own body had refused. Some people you argue with. Some people you simply fail to outvote.

"Together," I said.

* * *

**Astrid**

Widow Ansa worked by lamplight with the shutters closed, which told me everything about how many times she'd done this kind of work before. Her house smelled of camphor and hot iron, and her hands moved like she'd been born knowing them.

"Left side, against the ribs," she repeated, turning the oilskin packet over. It was heavier than it looked. Everything true is. "You'll feel it when you breathe deep. Like a hand resting there."

"I've had worse hands resting there."

The old woman laughed—a dry, surprised sound—and bit through the waxed thread. "Delphine said you'd either be frightened or funny. Frightened is easier to sew for."

I took off the coat and the shirt beneath it and stood in the cold while she chalked the seam lines on my skin, quick and impersonal as a mapmaker. Through the closed shutters I could hear the rain settling in for the night, and under the rain, farther off, the river—that low continuous conversation water has with itself, the sound I'd fallen asleep to for weeks now and would hear, I realized, for the rest of my life, the way you hear a language you learned too late to ever forget.

My father had taught me contour lines on a kitchen table when I was seven. *Every line is a promise,* he'd said. *The land keeps its shape even when you can't see it. You just have to know how to read the promise.* I'd spent six weeks believing he was dead, and four days knowing he was alive, and in neither state had I been able to reach him, and now he was somewhere behind me in a logging station with Meridian's men counting the days he was worth, and I was here, standing half-naked in a seamstress's kitchen having evidence of corporate fraud sewn into my clothing, and somewhere in the middle of all of it my signature sat on a document I'd signed in anger at nineteen, giving away my inheritance, making me—by their lawyers' arithmetic—a party to my own father's ruin.

Shame is a strange cargo. It doesn't weigh anything until you try to set it down.

"Hold still, child." Ansa's needle slid through wool and oilskin and the small hard truth of the packet. "You're shaking."

"Cold."

"Mm." She stitched on. "In my village we had a saying. The goat that carries the salt doesn't complain about the weight. It complains about the dogs."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not meant to be. It's meant to be accurate." She tied off, cut the thread, and pressed her thumb flat along the new seam, checking it the way Marguerite checked a knot. "There. Breathe."

I breathed. The packet sat against my ribs like a hand. Like a promise the land keeps even when you can't see it.

* * *

**Marguerite**

The decoys cost me forty minutes and most of my remaining cash, and the hardest part wasn't building them—it was finding the right couple to carry them. In the end it was Bello who solved it, jerking his chin at two of his deckhands' wives, women with broad shoulders and better acting than they knew. "They want passage money for their sister's clinic," he said. "They'll wear your bundles like they were born to them. Don't ask them to lie, though. Ask them to be confused. Confused is easier to sustain at dawn."

He was right, the way old rivermen usually are. At half past four, in the blue-black wet of the false morning, the two women came down the dock road with the pilgrims' bundles on their backs and Astrid's spare shawl around one of their heads, and they made straight for Sefu's boat with the loud, anxious purpose of people who have been told to hurry and don't know why. I watched from the shadow of the icehouse as the watcher on the crate stood up. Watched the newspaper man fold his paper at last. Watched a third figure detach from the customs shed—there he was, the one I hadn't found—and drift after the women like smoke finding a draft.

One of the women argued with Sefu about the fare. Loudly. Twice. Sefu, who knew exactly what was happening because Bello had bought his silence with a bottle and a story, shouted back about schedules and fools, and the whole little theater played out under the dock lamp like a play performed for an audience of three men in the dark, and the three men in the dark leaned in, and believed, because people believe what they've stayed up all night hoping to see.

The women boarded. The bundles went aboard. Sefu cast off into the fog with two passengers who would disembark at the first landing, paid and thanked, and by the time the riders sorted it out—if they ever bothered to—we'd be forty minutes gone and an hour downstream.

Which left the north slip, and the *Marianne*, and the last hour of dark.

Astrid met me behind the icehouse, wearing the grey coat with its new secret, her breath steaming, her eyes bright with the specific terror that looks, from a distance, like calm. We walked the long way, along the mudflats, past the drying racks where the fish hung silver and dead in the pre-dawn grey, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left to plan and everything left to fear, and silence was the only honest packing left to do.

Bello was waiting at the *Marianne*'s rail. He looked at Astrid, at me, at the sky, and spat thoughtfully over the side.

"Riders watched the main dock all night," he said. "Nobody watches the fish boat. Nobody watches the fish boat because nobody wants to be the man who watched the fish boat." He swung the gangplank across. "You're crates. Crates keep quiet, crates don't smoke, and crates get off at the capital fish quay, not the passenger dock. The assizes are the big stone building with the flag. Ten minutes' walk, uphill. Go before the fog lifts."

We went aboard. The *Marianne* pulled out into the black water with her load of ice and herring and two human beings trying to breathe like cargo, and Port Meridian sank away behind us into the rain.

Astrid stood at the stern rail watching it go, one hand pressed flat against her left side, against the seam, against the hand-shaped weight of everything we'd carried out of the high country. I came and stood beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched, which was as much language as either of us could afford just then.

"Twenty-six hours," she said.

"Less, now."

"When this is over—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "When this is over, I'm going to sleep for a week, and then I'm going to find my father, and then I don't know what. Isn't that strange? I've walked nine hundred miles on 'when this is over,' and I never once filled in what comes after."

I looked upstream, toward the country we'd come from, where the clouds were stacking again over the ridge, patient as creditors. Then downstream, toward the capital, the courts, the registrar's stamp, the whole fragile machinery of justice that we were betting two lives and a stolen fish boat against.

"After," I said, "comes the trail. There's always a trail. That's the one thing I know."

The fog was lifting. Ahead of us, faint on the grey water, the first lights of the capital showed like a scatter of coins at the bottom of a river—and behind us, far back at the dock we'd fled, a lantern began to move. Quick. Purposeful. Sweeping the north slip.

Someone had counted the crates.
