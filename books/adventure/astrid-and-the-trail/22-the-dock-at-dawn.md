## 22. The Dock at Dawn

Port Meridian smelled the way dying towns smell: fish and diesel and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a crate nobody would claim. We came down from Delphine Okafor's rooms before first light, when the lamps along the waterfront still burned yellow and the fog off the river was thick enough to drink.

Marguerite walked behind her. That had been his idea, and he'd rehearsed it on the stairs like a man learning a psalm. *You walk ahead. I'll follow. If they're watching the dock, they'll be watching for two people together—two people apart are just early risers.* Astrid had agreed without argument, which should have warned him. She only agreed without argument when she'd already decided the argument didn't matter.

The plan was simple, and simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with a morning like this one.

There were two departures at dawn. The *Coriander*, a fat river steamer bound downriver to the capital, loading cargo at the main dock—and a smaller boat, the *Tern*, leaving an hour later from the fish market landing, carrying dried fish and salt and whatever else the company hadn't yet found a way to tax. The decoy was the *Tern*. Two pieces of luggage, bought that afternoon from a secondhand dealer who asked no questions because he charged too much to afford them, would go aboard the *Tern* in the care of a hired porter—a boy of maybe sixteen whom Tomas's food caches had somehow paid for, though Marguerite never learned how. The notebooks were not in the luggage. The notebooks were sewn into the lining of Astrid's coat, flat against her spine, wrapped in waxed canvas, and she carried them the way she carried everything: as if the weight were her own idea.

They would board the *Coriander* separately, at the last possible minute, among the crowd of traders and deckhands and one hungover customs clerk who took coins and looked at nothing.

Simple. Marguerite had been telling himself it was simple all night, and by the time their feet touched the cobbles of the fish market road, he had almost believed it.

* * *

The fog did them one kindness and committed a hundred small betrayals besides. It hid them from the ridge road, where riders could sit a horse and watch the whole waterfront laid out below like items on a ledger. But it also muffled sound, turned every shadow into a man and every man into a shadow, and made the dock lamps bloom into halos that ate half the light they were supposed to give.

Astrid went ahead, twenty paces, hood up, hands in her pockets. Marguerite watched her back and counted.

He was good at counting. He had spent five years counting miles instead of days, distances instead of losses, and the habit had sunk so deep into him that now, walking through a town that wanted him hanged, he counted everything. Fourteen steps from the corner to the first fish stall. Three men on the *Coriander*'s gangplank, two of them crew, one of them wrong—the way he stood, weight forward, hands loose, watching faces instead of cargo. A lantern swinging at the market landing. The porter boy already there, the two decoy bags at his feet, looking bored in the practiced way of boys who have been told to look bored.

And then, at the edge of what the fog allowed him to see, a sixth sense older than any of his training spoke up—not in words but in the skin, the way it does before weather turns or before a rope parts under load.

Someone was watching Astrid. Not the dock. Not the bags. Her.

* * *

Later, going over it a hundred times, Marguerite would understand exactly how it happened, and hate how small the thing was. It wasn't her face—the hood covered her face. It wasn't the coat, or the bags, or anything she carried. It was her walk. Five days upriver and three days down, a week of trails and rapids and caves, and the woman had learned to move like the country moved: sure-footed, economical, her weight rolling from heel to ball of foot with a rhythm that no town woman had, that no woman who had grown up in drawing rooms had either. The rider had probably met her once, years ago, at some company function, and forgotten her face entirely. But bodies remember other bodies. And somewhere in the fog, a man watched a hooded figure cross a fish market with a guide's stride and felt the hair rise on his neck.

She reached the porter boy. He picked up the bags. She pressed a coin into his hand and said something Marguerite couldn't hear, and the boy grinned, and for one whole breath the plan worked. The boy started toward the *Tern*. Astrid turned away, unhurried, a woman whose business was done, drifting back toward the main dock and the crowd gathering at the *Coriander*'s rail.

Then the fog shifted, the way fog does, opening a lane like a door—and forty yards up the market road, a horse stamped, and its rider leaned forward out of the gray, and Marguerite saw the man's head turn and track Astrid the way a hawk tracks movement in grass.

Not her face. Her walk.

"Change," Marguerite breathed, though she couldn't hear him, and broke cover.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way violence always does—faster than thought, faster than fear, a sequence of events that only assembled themselves into a story afterward, when the blood was already cooling on his shirt.

The rider swung down from his horse. He didn't run; running draws eyes. He walked, quick and casual, cutting the angle toward Astrid, and Marguerite saw his hand go inside his coat and come out with something short and dark, and understood that this was not an arrest. Meridian's men didn't arrest. They collected, or they erased, and a knife meant erasure—quiet, deniable, another body the river could be blamed for.

"Astrid!" His voice cracked across the market like a whip.

She turned. She saw the man. And Marguerite learned something about her in that half-second that he would carry for the rest of his life: she didn't freeze. She read the situation the way she read water—fast, coldly, correctly—and she moved *toward* the crowd instead of away from it, because crowds were cover, and open ground was death.

The fish market at dawn was waking. Women with baskets. Boys hauling crates of silver-bellied catch, scales flashing like thrown coins. Smoke rising from a brazier where someone fried the night's unsold stock into breakfast. Astrid plunged into it, and the rider plunged after her, and the crowd parted around both of them without understanding why, sensing only that here were two people moving with terrible purpose and that purpose was contagious.

Marguerite ran the outside line, along the stalls, closing the distance while staying out of the rider's sightline. His ribs ached where the pack strap crossed them. His boots slipped on wet cobbles slick with fish blood and ice-melt, and he used the slip, let it carry him low and fast past a stack of crab pots.

The rider caught Astrid's shoulder at the mouth of the alley between the smokehouse and the salt shed.

She spun inside his grip—textbook, beautiful, the kind of turn Marguerite had tried to teach tourists a hundred times and never seen done right—and drove her elbow into his throat. The man staggered. The knife came up. And Marguerite hit him from the side like a falling tree.

* * *

They went down together onto the cobbles, and the world became very small and very simple: elbows, knees, the reek of the man's wool coat, the flash of the blade as it slashed and missed and slashed again. This was not a fight in any clean sense. There was no form to it, no craft worth admiring. It was two animals in a narrow space, and the space was full of fish guts and broken shell, and the only question was which animal stopped moving first.

The knife opened Marguerite's forearm on the second pass—a shallow line, hot, instantly ignored. On the third pass it found what it wanted. He felt it go in under his arm, along the ribs, a bright cold line that burned a heartbeat later, and he heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize, and he did the only thing left: he got his hands on the man's wrist and his forehead against the man's nose and pushed until something crunched.

The grip loosened. The knife clattered free, skittering across the stones.

Astrid's boot arrived a moment later and put it firmly under a crate.

"Up," she said. "Get *up*."

He got up. It cost more than it should have. The wound under his arm was bleeding freely now, warm down his side, soaking into his shirt in a spreading stain he could feel rather than see. Around them the market had gone quiet in the particular way markets do—not silent, but watchful, fifty people suddenly very interested in their own baskets. Nobody shouted for the constable. In Port Meridian, everybody knew who paid the constable, and it wasn't them.

The rider lay on his back, blood sheeting from his broken nose, blinking up at the fog. His eyes found Marguerite's face, and Marguerite saw the recognition arrive—the wanted notice, five years old, reward doubled—and saw the man begin, through the pain, to smile.

"He knows," the rider rasped. "By noon every man on the company roll will know you're—"

Marguerite hit him once, carefully, with the edge of his hand, and the man slept.

* * *

They walked. They did not run, because running was a confession shouted to the whole waterfront, and they walked the way innocent people walk, which is to say slightly too casually, hearts hammering, blood dripping quietly into a boot.

"The boat," Astrid said. Low, steady, her hand under his elbow in a way that looked like affection and was actually arithmetic—she was measuring how much of his weight he could spare. "Can you make the gangplank?"

"I can make the gangplank."

"You're bleeding through your shirt."

"It's a popular color this season."

"Don't." Her voice caught, just once, a hairline crack in stone. "Don't do that. Not yet."

So he didn't. They crossed the last hundred yards to the *Coriander* with the fog thinning around them, the sky going from charcoal to dirty pearl overhead, and Marguerite cataloguing his damage with the detached interest of a man reading someone else's inventory. Forearm: shallow. Ribs: deep enough to matter, high enough to miss anything vital, probably. Blood loss: real but not yet urgent. Time remaining before the wound went bad in this climate: he'd seen worse infections start from less, in country with better doctors than this town would ever have.

The gangplank. The deckhand took their coins without looking up. The customs clerk took his without looking anywhere at all. And then the rail was under Marguerite's hands, and the deck was under his feet, and the *Coriander* cast off into the brown morning water with a groan of rope and a churn of paddle wheel, and Port Meridian began, slowly, mercifully, to fall away behind them.

* * *

Astrid found him at the stern, sitting on a coil of rope because standing had become a theory rather than a practice. She knelt in front of him with her coat already open, the waxed parcel of notebooks lifted out and set aside with a tenderness she gave to nothing else, and then she peeled back his shirt and looked at what the knife had written on him.

Her face did something complicated and settled on fury.

"This needed stitching an hour ago."

"There's a needle in the kit."

"There's a needle in the kit," she repeated, in a voice that promised the needle would be used on him whether he liked it or not. She was already boiling water over the little spirit stove she'd insisted on buying in town, already tearing strips from a clean shirt, already working, and Marguerite sat in the growing warmth of the stove and watched her and understood, with the strange clarity that blood loss brings, that he was looking at the reason he had crossed a rope bridge he thought would kill him.

"How bad?" she asked, not looking up.

"I've had worse."

"That's not an answer, Marguerite."

He considered lying. He was, by trade and temperament, a man who managed information the way he managed a boat—giving passengers exactly what kept them calm. But she had sewn documents into her coat and walked into a fish market knowing what waited there, and she had put her boot on a knife without hesitation, and lies between people like that rotted fast.

"It's deep," he said. "It'll need real stitching, soon, by someone who knows what they're doing. And it's going to fever. Wounds like this always do, downriver, in this heat. Two days, maybe three, I'll be useless."

The water boiled. She cleaned the wound in silence, and he gave her the silence, gripping the rope coil and breathing through it the way he'd taught frightened clients to breathe through rapids. When she tied off the last of the field stitches—crooked, tight, honest work—he caught her wrist.

"Astrid. Listen to me. Two days from now, if I'm burning, you don't stop for me. You run the river alone. You know the channel markers as far as Blackpool Bend, and after that you follow the freight traffic and you ask no questions. The notebooks get to Delphine's contact at the capital assizes if I'm dead in the bottom of the boat. Do you hear me? That's the order."

She finished the knot. She looked up at him, and the fog-light made her eyes the color of the river itself, and she smiled—not gently, not kindly, but with the flat bright certainty of a woman reading water.

"Noted," she said. "Now hold still. You're bleeding again."

Behind them the paddle wheel beat its slow rhythm, and Port Meridian sank into the fog, and somewhere back on that dock a man with a broken nose was waking up with a name in his mouth—a name on a notice, a name worth money, a name that would travel downriver faster than any boat.

Marguerite closed his eyes and let the river take them, and counted, not miles, not mornings, but hours—twenty-six of them, give or take, until dawn on the far dock, and the assizes, and whatever waited there with its hands open.

The price tag, he thought, was still being tallied. He only hoped he'd live long enough to read it.
