## 23. Fever Miles

The fever came with the second dawn.

I knew it before I touched him. I had learned his body's language in the cave—where the ribs rose and fell, where the gash across them pulled when he laughed, which was rarely. I had learned the rhythm of his breathing the way he had taught me to read a river: not by looking at it but by listening to what it said underneath. And underneath, that morning, something had gone wrong. His breath came shallow and quick, like a man climbing stairs in his sleep.

"Marguerite."

He opened his eyes and they were wrong. Bright in the wrong way, glassy, the pupils swimming as if the light itself were moving. He looked at me and for one long moment did not know me.

"You're bleeding," he said.

"I'm not."

"You're—" His hand came up, trembling, and touched my cheek where there was nothing but river-damp and cold. "There's blood on your face. Karst Ridge. There's so much blood on your face."

I caught his wrist. Held it. "It's rain," I said. "It's only rain."

He believed me because he wanted to, or because the fever let him, and his eyes closed again, and I sat very still in the stern of the leaking Kestrel while the river carried us down through country I did not know, holding the wrist of a man who had spent five years teaching himself to be untouchable, and who now burned under my hand like a stove door left open.

* * *

I want to say I was calm. I wasn't. What I was, was busy.

The gash had closed on the surface—I had sewn it myself in the fish market's aftermath, three ugly stitches with sail twine and a curved needle from his kit, my hands steadier than I'd had any right to expect—but beneath the closure the flesh had gone hot and swollen, and when I pressed along the edge of it he made a sound he would have been ashamed of awake. The skin around the wound had gone the color of old brick. A thin line of red was crawling up toward his armpit, faint as a contour mark on a map, and I understood with a cold clarity that surprised me: this was a road too. Something was traveling it.

Infection. Sepsis, if it went far enough. I knew the word the way you know words for things in other countries—accurate and useless.

What I needed was a doctor, and what I had was two hundred miles of river, a boat with a hull like a colander, a man who could barely lift his head, and a monsoon still falling in long gray sheets that turned the banks into walls of water.

What I also had was Marguerite Coldharbour's voice, even burning, even half-lost, giving me the river.

"The channel splits ahead," he murmured, eyes shut, one hand resting on the gunwale as if he were steering in his sleep. "Left side looks open. It isn't. There's a bar under the surface—you'll see the water go smooth. Smooth water over a shoal is a lie, Astrid. Take the right. Hug the deadfall. It'll look like the wrong choice."

The channel split. The left side lay open and inviting, silvered with rain. The right side was a chaos of drowned timber.

I took the right. The Kestrel scraped something that groaned along her keel, and then we were through, and behind us the smooth water of the left channel broke white over a bar I never saw.

"How do you know this river?" I asked him. "You've never run it downstream."

"Rivers are rivers." His eyes stayed closed. "Water goes around weight. Read what it avoids and you know where everything is." A pause. The fever moved through him like weather. "My father taught me that. Before."

He had never mentioned a father. I filed it away with all the other fragments—the brother-in-law whose name he wouldn't say, the rope, the crossing—and kept my hands on the tiller.

* * *

That first day I learned the boat.

I had steered her before, in fair water, under his eye. This was different. This was the Kestrel alone against the monsoon river, and the Kestrel, I discovered, had opinions. She pulled to port where the caulking had failed. She wallowed in the troughs like a tired animal. She leaked—not dangerously, not yet, but steadily, a slow dark accumulation in the bilge that I bailed with a tin pan every hour, kneeling in the well beside him while he burned and muttered and gave me the river in pieces.

"Reef the—no. No reef. Just ease her. Let the current do the work. Fighting the current is how boats die."

I eased her. The current did the work. Rain hammered the canopy until speech was shouting, and between shouts we said nothing at all, and it was somehow the most honest silence we had ever shared.

At midday I found the first cache.

I nearly missed it—a strip of oilcloth tied to an overhanging alder, high above the flood line, and inside, wrapped in waxed paper: dried fish, a block of something like hardtack, and a twist of paper containing willow bark. Someone had left it deliberately. Someone who knew this stretch of water, and knew what travelers on it might need.

"Tomas," Marguerite breathed, when I showed him. For a moment the fever receded and his eyes were clear and fierce. "He's alive. He's ahead of us or behind us, but he's alive." Then the clarity guttered out like a lamp in wind, and he was somewhere else entirely, saying a name I didn't catch, over and over, soft as prayer.

I ate a little. I made him drink water with the willow bark steeped in it, bitter as regret, and he drank because I told him to, and there was something in the obedience that frightened me more than the fever did. Marguerite Coldharbour did not obey. Marguerite Coldharbour weighed, judged, decided, and steered. The man in the bottom of the boat did as he was told by a woman who had never run a river alone in her life, and trusted her with the only thing he had left, which was his life, and the trust was heavier than the fever.

* * *

Night came down like a lid. I anchored in the lee of a gravel bar, hauled the Kestrel's nose into the shallows, and rigged the tarp so it shed most of the rain. He shivered and burned by turns. I had no way to make a fire that wouldn't be visible for miles—we were still, presumably, being hunted, though the riders seemed to have lost us in the flooded country—and so I did the only thing I could think of, which was to lie down beside him and put my body against his shaking one and hold on.

"You're cold," he mumbled.

"You're burning. Between us we're roughly human."

A sound that might have been a laugh. His hand found mine in the dark, clumsy, and gripped it, and did not let go, and I lay awake most of the night listening to the rain and to his breathing and doing arithmetic I hated: distance remaining, hours of light, the rate at which the red line climbed his chest, the number of days since the knife, the number of days until the assizes closed, the number of ways this ended badly. I had spent my whole life avoiding arithmetic like this. My father had taught me maps instead of sums; maps, he said, told you where you were, and sums only told you how much trouble you were in.

But lying in the dark with a fevered man's pulse tapping against my palm like a telegraph key, I did the sums anyway. And then, because I was my father's daughter, I got up in the black rain and studied the river by feel, and made a map of it in my head, and planned the next day's water.

* * *

The second day was worse, and better, and worse again.

Worse, because the fever peaked near noon and took him somewhere I couldn't follow. He talked—fragments, names, whole sentences addressed to people who weren't there. To his sister. To a man called Anselm, whom he begged forgiveness from in a voice stripped down to the boards. To someone about a rope—*it wasn't frayed, I checked it, I checked it twice, I swear I checked it*—and then, quieter, in a register that stopped my heart: *I cut it. God help me, I cut it.*

Better, because between the peaks he surfaced, and when he surfaced he was still himself, and still teaching.

"The gorge after the confluence," he said, propped against the gunwale with his eyes half-focused, sweat pouring off him in the humid heat. "You'll hear it before you see it. When you hear it, get to the middle. Not the fast water in the middle—the seam just left of it. Every rapid has a seam. The place where two currents argue. Sit in the argument and let them cancel each other out."

"And if I can't find the seam?"

"Then you'll find out what kind of sailor you are." He almost smiled. "You held a line in the First Rapids when I'd have sworn you'd panic. You sewed a wound in a fish market with men shooting at us. Find the seam, Astrid. You're good at arguments."

I found the seam. The gorge roared around us, walls of wet stone close enough to touch, the river compressed and furious, and I sat the Kestrel in the narrow place where the currents fought, and she trembled like a living thing, and slid through, and the roar fell away behind us, and I realized my hands ached from gripping the tiller and that I had not been afraid. Not in the moment. Fear, I was learning, was a thing you paid afterward, in installments.

* * *

The second night I ran late, against my own rule, because the arithmetic had changed. The red line had reached his collarbone. His skin was hot as a sun-struck rock, and when I sponged him with river water he barely reacted, and I made the calculation that a doctor upriver was worth more than a safe anchorage, and ran the last hour of light with the rain coming sideways and the channel going gray and then black, navigating by the sound of the water against the banks and by the lightning, which came in silent sheets that printed the valley on my eyes for a half-second at a time—half-seconds in which I read the river like a page, like one of my father's contour maps turned liquid, every riffle and eddy a line of text.

I anchored at full dark, exhausted past thought, and bailed the boat, and forced water and willow bark into him, and sat with my back against the thwart and his head in my lap, and felt the fever radiating up through his skull into my thighs like sitting too close to a fire.

"If you die," I told him, conversationally, because he couldn't hear me and I needed to say it, "I will find Meridian's security chief myself, and I will testify, and I will spend the rest of my life being brave and competent and completely alone, and I will hate every minute of it, and it will be entirely your fault for teaching me that I could be brave and competent."

The rain answered. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled down the valley like a barrel of stones.

"But here's the thing," I went on, and my voice cracked, and I let it. "Here's the thing I figured out in the cave, when you were asleep and honest. I've been walking trails my whole life that other people drew for me. My father's concessions. Meridian's boundaries. Even coming after Elias—that was love, but it was also a trail somebody else had broken, and I was just following the damage. This is the first trail I've ever cut myself. Two days, one river, no instructions. And I'm good at it, Marguerite. I'm so angry that I'm good at it, because I learned it from watching you count miles instead of letting anyone matter to you, and I don't want to be that, but I am, tonight, and the boat is still floating, and you're still breathing, and that's the whole report."

His hand moved. Found my knee. Patted it, twice, weakly, with the grave absurdity of a man approving a ledger entry, and I laughed and cried at the same time, quietly, in the rain, and then I slept for two hours with my chin on my chest and woke and bailed the boat and checked the red line.

It hadn't moved.

* * *

Dawn of the third day came up the color of pewter, and with it, far ahead, a smudge of smoke on the southern bank that was too steady to be a campfire and too small to be a town. As the light strengthened I made out roofs, a dock, a chapel spire with a cross that leaned like a question mark.

A mission. He had mentioned it once, weeks ago, in the flat inventory voice he used for facts that mattered: *If anything happens to me, there's a mission forty miles below the gorge. Sisters. They patch up boatmen the company breaks.* Forty miles. At the pace the river gave us, four hours. Maybe five, if the water argued.

I looked down at him. He was sleeping—not the fever sleep of the last two days but something deeper, truer, his face slack and young-looking, the lines of vigilance smoothed away. The red line, when I checked, had faded to a dull pink shadow of itself. The swelling around the stitches had eased. Either the willow bark was winning, or his body had decided, in its stubborn way, that it was not finished with him yet.

Either way, I had gotten us here. I had run sixty miles of monsoon river alone, through a gorge, through nights, through the long argument of the currents, and the boat floated and the man breathed and the notebooks rode dry against my ribs, sewn into my coat like a second heartbeat.

I put the tiller over and aimed the Kestrel at the leaning cross.

He surfaced once, near the end, enough to see the spire and understand. His eyes found mine and there was something in them I hadn't seen before—not gratitude exactly, and not surprise, though both were there. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing, clearly, for the first time, the person standing next to him.

"You found the seams," he whispered.

"All of them," I said.

He smiled, and slept, and I ran us in past the mission dock where a sister in a gray habit was already standing, arms crossed against the rain, watching us come with the expression of a woman who had patched up a great many things the river sent her and expected to patch up a great many more.

And it was only when the hull kissed the pilings and I stood to make fast that I saw, nailed to the mission's gatepost, flapping in the wet wind, a fresh sheet of paper with ink that the rain had not yet reached—

a notice, newly posted, offering a reward,

and bearing a sketch of a woman's face that I recognized as my own.
