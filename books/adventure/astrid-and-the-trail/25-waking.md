## 25. Waking

The infirmary smelled of carbolic soap and river mud, and Astrid had learned, over three days, to hate both smells equally.

She sat where she had sat for most of those days—in the wooden chair beside the cot that some mission sister had dragged from a storeroom and padded with a folded blanket, because the sisters of Sainte-Anne-de-l'Eau believed in small mercies even when they could not afford them. The window behind her was open to the heat. Beyond it, the compound's clay yard baked white in the midmorning sun, and beyond that, the river ran brown and swollen with the tail of the monsoon, carrying sticks, leaves, half a canoe, and—she was certain—a great many things she did not want to think about.

Marguerite lay on his back with his eyes closed, and she had stopped believing, somewhere around the second day, that closed eyes meant sleep. Fever had its own geography. She had watched him travel it: the shallow coast of dozing, the deep interior where he muttered about anchors and water and a name she hadn't recognized at first—*René*—and the high cold passes where he went rigid and spoke in a voice stripped of everything, saying *I cut it, I cut it* over and over like a man trying to saw through something that would not part.

The wound itself was ugly but honest. A gash across the ribs, left side, from the fish-market knife; the mission doctor, a Belgian named Father Luc who smoked pipes he wasn't supposed to have, had cleaned it, stitched it, and pronounced it soundly sewn. It was the infection underneath that had nearly taken him—the red spreading like ink through cloth, the fever climbing to a height where Father Luc had looked at her over Marguerite's body and said, quietly, "Now we pray, and now we wait," as if these were two different procedures requiring separate consent.

She had done both. She was very good at waiting. Her father had taught her that too, though he'd taught it by example rather than instruction: Elias Vane could sit motionless at a surveyor's tripod for an hour while the light changed, and little Astrid, six years old, had learned to sit beside him and watch the same hill until it became a different hill.

Three days. The fever broke yesterday evening, all at once, the way storms break—no gentle recession but a sudden coolness, sweat soaking the sheet, and Marguerite opening his eyes for the first time in a day and a half and looking at the ceiling beams as if he'd never seen wood before.

Then he'd slept. Real sleep this time. She knew because his breathing changed, because his hands unclenched, because when Sister Bernadette came to change the dressing he grumbled about the iodine in a voice that was awake and annoyed and alive.

Now it was morning, and he was still asleep, and Astrid allowed herself to look at him properly, which she had not permitted herself while he might wake and catch her at it.

He looked terrible. He looked, she thought, like a contour map of himself—all the lines of his face drawn deeper, the hollows shaded in, five years of distance added in three days of fever. His beard had come in patchy and gray-streaked. The bandage across his ribs rose and fell. On the table beside the cot sat the Kestrel's spare tarpaulin, folded, which she'd brought up from the boat because he'd asked for it once, in delirium, in a voice like a child asking for a specific blanket: *the green one, the one that doesn't leak.*

She had sewn the notebooks into her coat lining herself, by lamplight, in the boathouse, while he slept off the first bad night. Thirty-one pages of her father's field notes, photographs of boundary stones that had been moved, the letter naming the official whose signature made fraud into law. They rode against her ribs now, under the linen, lighter than a bird's skeleton and heavier than the boat they'd nearly died in twice.

He stirred. She watched his eyes move beneath the lids, watched some current pass through him, and then—gradually, the way dawn comes up on a ridge, gray before color—he woke.

His gaze found the ceiling first. Then the window. Then her.

"You're sitting in my chair," he said. His voice was gravel and rust.

"It's the mission's chair."

"I've been using it."

"You've been unconscious in a bed. The chair was available." She leaned forward. "How much of the last three days do you actually remember?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave all navigational questions. "Pieces. Running the river. You steering." A pause. "Did I give you the line for the S-bend below the falls?"

"Left of the dead tree, then hard right before the shoal. You said it four times. Then you said it in French, and then you said it to someone named René, who I don't think was in the boat."

Something moved through his face—quick, submerged, gone. Like a fish turning under clear water. "No," he said. "He wasn't."

* * *

Father Luc came and went with broth and opinions. Sister Bernadette changed the dressing and pronounced the wound "closing clean, thanks be and despite you." Astrid ate half a plate of rice and beans without tasting it and refused to leave the room, and by early afternoon the mission had absorbed her presence the way old buildings absorb new people—without comment, permanently.

It was late afternoon when the light came long and gold through the west window and laid itself across the floorboards, that Marguerite said, without preamble:

"I need to tell you what happened at Karst Ridge."

Astrid set down the cup of tea she'd been holding without drinking. "You told me. In the cave. The crossing collapsed. Your client died. The inquiry blamed you, and you never got a trial, just a blacklist and a warrant."

"That's the shape of it." He was looking at the ceiling again. "That's not the truth of it."

Outside, a bell rang the hour—four slow strokes, bronze-voiced. Somewhere in the compound a dog began barking at the bell, as it did every day, and gave up every day, defeated by routine.

"The client's name was René," Marguerite said. "René Marchand. He was married to my sister."

Astrid kept her face still. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a stone in a riverbed showing its pale underside.

"He wasn't supposed to be on that crossing. He was a clerk—an accountant, actually, for a timber concern in the capital. But Colette was ill that spring, and he wanted to see the country she talked about, so he came along on the Karst Ridge job as a favor-trip, a holiday. I let him." His jaw worked. "I *let* him. That was the first decision. Everything after was built on it."

"The crossing was a rope bridge over the Vermeille gorge," he went on. "Two hundred meters of span, anchored at each end into rock. We'd used it three times that week. Porters, gear, me. René went last, on the fifth crossing, because I wanted the weight distributed and because—" his breath caught, released— "because I wanted to be on the anchor end when he crossed. Watching. Being responsible."

"And?"

"And the far anchor failed. The whole bridge swung down into the gorge like a door falling off its hinges. René was mid-span. He held the cables. I was at the near anchor, and I had a knife on my belt, and the bridge was dragging—his weight and the cable tension were pulling the whole structure sideways toward the broken anchor point, and there was a moment—I have replayed it ten thousand times, Astrid, I will replay it ten thousand more—where holding on meant the bridge might tear loose entirely and take the near anchor with it, and everyone else on the ledge, with it."

He turned his head at last and looked at her. His eyes were clear and terrible.

"I cut the rope."

The words fell into the room and lay there.

"I cut the near cable to save the anchor, to save the other four men on the ledge. And René fell. Two hundred meters. I heard—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I heard it end. And then I climbed down, or partway down, and what I found at the bottom is not a thing I'm going to describe to you, because you've had enough of finding people at the bottom of things."

Astrid thought of the burned camp. Of the ledger page with her father's name struck through. Of six weeks of silence and a letter she'd carried for years inside herself, the letter she'd never written him after their last fight, the one that started *you chose the mountains over us*—

"The inquiry," she said carefully.

"The inquiry found the anchors were sabotaged. Both of them. Pre-cut, partially, so they'd fail under load. Someone wanted that crossing to fail that week." He laughed, short and bitter. "Do you know what I did with that finding? Nothing. Because the finding also said—and this is the part they printed—that the guide's decision to sever the cable constituted gross negligence, that a competent guide would have found another means, that my cutting the rope was the proximate cause of death. Not the sabotage. My knife."

"But the anchors—"

"Were sabotaged by subcontractors working for Meridian Consolidated. That connection never made it into the published findings. Delphine Okafor has spent five years knowing it and being unable to prove it. The inquiry was steered, Astrid. The verdict was written before I ever testified. My blacklist wasn't justice. It was housekeeping. I was the convenient guilty party, and every year I stayed ruined was a year nobody looked too closely at why a rope bridge over a gorge nobody important cared about needed sabotaging in the first place." He closed his eyes. "There was a claim dispute in that valley too. There's always a claim dispute. Meridian's fingerprints were on the whole thing, and I was too broken and too scared and too *ashamed* to be the man who said so out loud."

The room was very quiet. The dog had given up on the bell. Down in the yard, two novices were arguing cheerfully about laundry.

"So you didn't kill him," Astrid said.

"I cut the rope."

"You saved four men."

"I dropped my brother-in-law two hundred meters into a gorge."

"You were the only person on that bridge who made a decision at all," she said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, harder than gentleness, because gentleness would have let him keep this, and she had learned in the last month that Marguerite Coldharbour treasured his guilt the way other men treasured gold—hidden, counted nightly, defended to the death. "Everyone else froze. The sabotage decided the crossing would fail. Meridian decided your brother-in-law would die. The only free act in that entire gorge was yours, and it was to save four lives instead of gambling five on a cable that was already failing. And then you let them write it up as murder-by-negligence because the alternative was believing you'd been destroyed on purpose, and that was worse. Being guilty was easier than being *used*."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion of the whole body, like a boat taking a wave broadside.

"You can't absolve me," he said. "You weren't there."

"No," she agreed. "But the inquiry was. And the inquiry proved the anchors were cut before anyone stepped onto that bridge. Before you. Before René. The failure was manufactured, Marguerite. The only thing you contributed was the impossible choice at the end of it." She stood, because sitting felt wrong suddenly, because she wanted to be above him or level with him, not leaning over a sickbed dispensing mercy like medicine. "Your blacklist isn't a sentence you served. It's a lid they put on a box. And we're carrying the papers that open it."

"They'll want me in custody to testify." He said it flatly, already navigating forward, already reading the next reach of river. "Delphine said it. A hearing means surrendering to the warrant."

"Yes."

"Five years, Astrid. Five years I built my life around not being seen. And you're telling me the way out is to walk into the smallest room in the country and let them lock the door."

"I'm telling you the way out is the truth, and the truth comes with a price tag, and you've been paying interest on a debt you never owed." She crouched then, so their eyes were level, and took his hand—his knuckles scarred, his grip still fever-weak, closing around hers anyway. "I'm not asking you to accept absolution today. I'm asking you to stop treating guilt as a home address."

For a long moment he said nothing. The light moved on the floor. Far downstream, thunder muttered—not a storm, just the river talking to itself around the bend.

"I heard him fall," Marguerite said finally, barely above a whisper. "Every night. For five years. Do you know what that does to a man?"

"I know what six weeks of silence does," she said. "I know what a struck-through name does. I know what it is to build a whole architecture of blame with yourself as the load-bearing wall." She squeezed his hand. "We're going to tear yours down in a courtroom, with witnesses. Mine we're going to tear down with my own signature, which is apparently part of the fraud. So neither of us gets to be the ruin alone anymore. That's the arrangement."

Almost—almost—he smiled. It was a small thing, exhausted and disbelieving, a crack in old masonry rather than a sunrise. But it was there.

"The anchors were sabotaged," he repeated, as if testing the weight of it, checking whether it would hold him.

"By Meridian's subcontractors. Proven, buried, and about to be *unburied*." She stood, retrieving her coat from the back of the chair, feeling the notebooks shift against her ribs like a second heartbeat. "Rest. Eat something. Tomorrow we go downriver, and the day after, we go to Delphine."

He nodded. Closed his eyes. And just as she reached the door, his voice caught her—quiet, clear, the navigator's voice, the one that read water:

"Astrid. The fever. What did I say? All of it."

She paused with her hand on the frame, weighing mercy against honesty, and chose honesty, because he had chosen it first, at last, at cost.

"You said *I cut it*. Over and over. And you said a name—René. And once, near the worst of it, you said—" she made herself finish— "*tell Colette I'm sorry.*"

Behind her, the silence had a texture, like deep water.

"She doesn't answer my letters," he said.

"Then she'll read about you in the assizes," Astrid said. "When they reopen the inquiry. When your name comes off the blacklist in print, not rumor. Give her that instead of a letter. Give her the truth with a court seal on it."

She stepped out into the gold light of the yard, and behind her she heard him exhale—long, shaking, the sound of a man setting down something he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it.

And it was there, standing in the warm evening with the river running loud below the mission walls, that Sister Bernadette found her, moving fast across the clay, her habit snapping at her ankles, a piece of paper in her hand.

"There's a boy," the sister said, breathless. "Come up from the logging road an hour ago, wouldn't talk to anyone but a woman traveling with a wounded guide. Said he was paid to find you specifically." She thrust the paper forward. "Said it was urgent. Said to tell you—it's about your father."

Astrid unfolded the note. The handwriting was cramped, hurried, pressed hard enough to tear the cheap paper in places—but she knew it instantly, had known it since she was six years old, watching contour lines become hills.

She read it twice. Then she looked up at Sister Bernadette, and her voice, when it came, was steady in the way of things balanced on an edge.

"Where is this logging station?"
