## 26. Delphine's Bargain

The mission infirmary smelled of carbolic soap and rain that had never quite finished falling. Marguerite had been awake for an hour before the sister came to change his dressing, lying still beneath a sheet gone gray at the edges, listening to the drum of water on the corrugated roof and counting the intervals between thunder the way he once counted miles. Four seconds. Three and a half. The storm was walking toward them, or away from them; with monsoons you could never tell which until it arrived.

His ribs had been bound twice now, tight enough to make each breath a negotiation. The gash from the fish market had closed into a hot red seam, and Sister Bernadette—she'd introduced herself by slapping a thermometer into his palm and saying, *You'll live, but you won't enjoy it*—had pronounced the infection beaten back, though not defeated. *Like everything else in this valley*, she'd said.

Astrid was asleep in the chair beside his cot, her head tipped against the wall, her coat still on. The coat with the notebooks sewn into its lining. She slept the way she did everything: upright, braced, ready to move. He had watched her for most of that hour without meaning to, the way you watch a fire you're not sure is safe to leave burning.

When Delphine Okafor came, she came quietly.

He heard her first as a change in the infirmary's rhythm—the sisters' footsteps pausing, a murmur at the door—and then she was standing at the foot of his cot, a small woman in a clerk's gray dress gone soft with age, carrying a leather satchel the way other people carried weapons: close to the body, one hand always on it. Rain beaded on her shoulders. She had walked up from the town through the storm's edge, and she looked like a woman who had made the walk many times in her life and expected nothing from any of them.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. I don't like talking over sleeping men. They agree too easily."

"I've been accused of worse," Marguerite said.

"Everyone in this valley has." She pulled a stool from the wall and sat, unhurried, arranging her satchel on her knees. "I'm going to speak plainly, Mr. Coldharbour, because I'm old and the rain is coming and I have no patience left for the indirect. Do you know what I did, before I retired?"

"Astrid said you were a court clerk."

"Thirty-one years. Registrar of assizes for this district for nineteen of them. I filed the Karst Ridge inquiry." She said it the way a woman might say *I buried my husband*. "I catalogued every exhibit. Every anchor shackle. Every length of rope. I read the testimony four times because I couldn't sleep, and I can tell you what the fourth reading told me, which is that the verdict was written before the inquiry opened. Someone handed the commissioners their conclusion along with their travel expenses. I knew it then. I know it now. And knowing it has sat in my chest for five years like a stone I swallowed."

Astrid stirred in the chair. Marguerite watched her come up out of sleep the way a swimmer comes up through dark water—instantly, completely, one hand already inside her coat. When she saw Delphine, some of the tension went out of her shoulders, but only some.

"You came through the rain," Astrid said.

"The rain and I have an arrangement. It falls; I keep walking." Delphine unbuckled the satchel. "I've spent two days with your notebooks, Miss Vane. With Elias Vane's field notes, and the photographs, and the letter naming Undersecretary Halloway, and the boundary surveys with Tomas Reyes-Idowu's original figures pasted beside Meridian's filed versions. I brought my loupe. I brought my knowledge of paper stock and ink batches and the filing habits of the Lands Office going back thirty years. I wanted very much to tell you these documents are false, so we could all go home."

She let the silence sit. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere down the hill a dog began to complain about it.

"They're true," she said. "Every page. The ink batch on the forged surveys matches a shipment the Lands Office received eleven days *after* the date written on the survey itself. That's not an error. That's a confession with a date stamp. The photographs show cut stakes where Meridian's maps show none. And the letter—" her voice tightened, and she pressed her lips together for a moment before continuing— "the letter names Halloway, and it describes a meeting I attended. Nineteen years ago, when I was young and thought the system would protect me if I just did my job correctly. Halloway asked me then whether certain files could be *misplaced*. I reported it to my superior. My superior thanked me and misplaced my report. So you see, Miss Vane, I am not a neutral witness. I am part of the pattern. Which makes what I have to say next both easier and harder."

Marguerite shifted against the cot, and his ribs reminded him they existed. "Say it anyway."

Delphine looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were dry and very steady, the eyes of someone who had spent three decades watching people learn the worst news of their lives and had learned to deliver it without flinching, because flinching was a luxury the messenger couldn't afford.

"When I authenticate those notebooks," she said, "when I sign my name to them and lodge them with the regional registrar, it triggers a hearing. Automatically. That's how the law works here—authenticated evidence of official corruption goes to the assizes, and the assizes must sit on it. There is no quiet way. There is no discreet channel. Meridian will know within a day, because Meridian owns half the people between here and the capital, and the other half owe them money."

"And?" Astrid said. But Marguerite already knew. He had known since the boathouse, since the moment he'd seen the constables' lanterns swinging up the track and decided, with a calm that had surprised him, to walk out with his hands visible. Some part of him had been doing this arithmetic ever since, in the back room of his mind where he kept the accounts he didn't look at directly.

"And," Delphine said, turning to face him fully, "there is a warrant outstanding against you, Mr. Coldharbour. Not the blacklist—that's an administrative stain, ugly but civil. A warrant. Failure to appear at the reopened inquiry into Karst Ridge, five years ago, when you left the district before the second session. It was never executed because you were never in jurisdiction. You are in jurisdiction now."

The rain filled the pause. Marguerite found he wasn't surprised. He'd known the ledger was unbalanced; he'd simply never let himself read the final line.

"If I authenticate the notebooks," Delphine went on, "and the hearing convenes, the first thing Meridian's counsel will do—the very first thing, before they touch a single document—is move to discredit the witnesses. And the man who found the evidence, the man who led the expedition to it, the man whose own ruin is woven through theirs? He cannot testify from the shadows. Testimony requires custody. You surrender to the court, Mr. Coldharbour, or your evidence arrives poisoned, and everything Elias Vane bled for, everything Tomas paid for, everything this girl sewed into her coat lining and carried through white water and gunfire—it gets dismissed as the conspiracy of fugitives. They will call it exactly that. I've watched them do it before."

"No," Astrid said.

It came out flat and fast, and Marguerite turned his head to look at her. She had gone very pale, but her voice held.

"There has to be another way. Delphine—you said yourself the verdict was fixed. You said the commissioners were handed their conclusion. We go around it. We take the notebooks to a newspaper. To the capital press. To—"

"To whom?" Delphine asked, not unkindly. "Name the editor who isn't bought, the ministry that isn't leased, the judge whose mortgage Meridian doesn't hold. I've had five years to look for one, Miss Vane. I found a mission infirmary and a leaking boat." She folded her hands on the satchel. "The courts are rigged, yes. But rigging requires the appearance of fairness, and appearance is a door. Once the notebooks are authenticated and lodged, once the hearing is public and the press is watching and the documents are in the record where they cannot be burned or bought back—then the rigging has to work in daylight, and daylight is expensive even for Meridian. That is the bargain. The law here is crooked, but it is crooked like a river, not like a wall. Water finds its level eventually. Walls just stand there."

"And the price of the water finding its level," Marguerite said quietly, "is me."

Delphine didn't soften it. He was grateful for that, in a distant, abstracted way, the way a man might be grateful for clean steel. "Testimony requires custody," she repeated. "You give yourself up. You sit in a cell while the hearing runs—days, possibly weeks, depending on how hard Meridian's lawyers drag it. And understand what they will do in that courtroom. They will exhume Karst Ridge. They will put the dead man's photograph in front of the commissioners and ask you to explain, again, why the crossing failed under your hand. They will call you a murderer wearing a guide's license. And if the hearing goes badly—if the replacement judge wavers, if the press loses interest, if the rain of attention dries up—they may not bother to acquit you afterward. Custody is custody. Men have entered cells on procedural warrants and stayed there for years while paperwork wandered."

"Marguerite." Astrid's voice cracked on his name, and she caught it, and steadied it, and he heard the cost of the catching. "Five years. You told me what five years of running did to you. Counting miles because miles couldn't leave you. And now you're going to hand yourself to the same system that—"

"That's exactly why," he said.

She stopped. Delphine watched them both with the stillness of a woman who had learned long ago that some conversations needed room.

Marguerite sat up slowly, paying the toll in his ribs, and swung his legs off the cot so he was facing Astrid properly. Her hands were fists in her lap. Her knuckles were white. He wanted to take one of them and didn't, because this wasn't a moment he had any right to make smaller.

"Listen to me," he said. "For five years I told myself the blacklist was weather. Bad luck. Something that happened to me, like a storm, something to be endured and outlasted. It was easier than the truth." He looked down at his own hands—rope-scarred, river-worn, the hands that had held the line at Karst Ridge and the line at the flooded crossing and her hand in the cave in the dark. "The truth is I ran. I ran before the inquiry finished because I couldn't bear to hear them say his name in a room I was standing in. Running felt like survival. It was actually just a longer way of drowning. And the whole time, the people who built the disaster, who sabotaged those anchors, who used my grief as a lid—they kept forging surveys. Kept ruining surveyors. Kept burning camps. Kept taking fathers." He nodded toward the coat she was guarding with her body. "Elias is alive because they need him silent. Tomas is gone because he wasn't useful silent. And every year I stayed invisible, I was one more piece of evidence they got to keep buried."

"So you trade yourself," Astrid said. "That's not justice, that's a ransom."

"It's a trail," he said. "And trails don't care whether you can see the far end. You taught me that. On the crossing. Hand over hand, when I couldn't feel my own arms." He almost smiled. "You talked me across a flooded river by telling me the fear was information, not instruction. This is the same crossing, Astrid. Bigger water. Same rule."

"This isn't the same. On the river I could hold the rope."

"You are holding the rope," Delphine said mildly. Both of them looked at her. She gestured at the coat, at the satchel, at the whole improbable architecture of what they'd carried out of the valley. "The documents survive because of her. The hearing happens because of her. If Meridian's counsel tears at anyone in that courtroom, they will find her harder to tear than most. I've read her deposition notes. She writes like her father." A ghost of something crossed her face—not quite a smile. "But none of it moves without him. That's the arithmetic. I won't pretend it's kind."

Silence again, except the rain, which had settled into a long steady roar, the sound of a sky emptying itself of everything it had been holding.

Astrid stood up. She paced the width of the infirmary—four steps, turn, four steps—a guide measuring a route she didn't want to guide. Then she stopped in front of Delphine.

"How long do we have?"

"The assizes reconvene in nine days. Authentication takes two, if I start tomorrow and don't sleep much. Lodging takes one. So—" she counted silently, lips moving— "call it six days before the machinery starts turning and Meridian knows the shape of what's coming. Six days to decide, to prepare, to say whatever needs saying." She rose, took up her satchel, and paused at the foot of the cot. "Mr. Coldharbour. One thing more, and then I'll leave you both to the rain."

He waited.

"I handled the Karst Ridge exhibits personally. I told you I read the testimony four times. What I never told anyone—not my superiors, not the commission—is that I kept copies. Anchors, shackles, the subcontractor invoices Meridian claimed didn't exist. Sealed, in a place Meridian's lawyers have never found in five years of looking, because they don't think a retired clerk is worth searching." She met his eyes. "If you surrender, you will not be alone in that courtroom. I want that understood. I have been a stone in my own chest for five years. I would like to set it down."

She left the way she'd come, quietly, her footsteps fading down the corridor into the sound of rain.

* * *

Night came early under the storm. A sister brought broth and lamp oil and left them alone, and Astrid sat on the edge of the cot and changed his dressing herself, her fingers careful around the red seam, her face turned away in the lamplight so he couldn't read it. He let her. There was a language in tending that neither of them had words for yet, and he was learning not to interrupt it.

"He'll say no," she said finally. "My father. When he hears. He'll say run, take the notebooks, get across the border, live."

"Probably."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Marguerite considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he had been asking it of himself since the constables' lanterns. The easy answers lined up and he sent them away: justice, principle, the greater good—all words that fit on a wanted notice and meant nothing in a cell at three in the morning.

"Because I've been invisible for five years," he said, "and invisibility turns out to be just a slower kind of dying. Because a man died at Karst Ridge and everyone believes I killed him, including, some mornings, me. Because Delphine kept copies for five years waiting for someone worth risking them on, and I refuse to be not worth it. And because—" he reached out and took her hand, the one still smelling of carbolic and wax, and she let him, her fingers closing around his with the grip of someone holding a line in white water— "because you sewed those notebooks into your coat and walked into a fish market knife-fight for them, and if I run now, then everything you carried was for nothing, and I would rather stand in front of Meridian's lawyers than watch that happen."

"You could get years," she whispered. "Even if we win. Even if the verdict comes back clean, they can bury you in procedure. Delphine said it herself."

"She also said water finds its level."

"Water drowned half our supplies in the first rapids."

He laughed, and the laugh pulled at his ribs, and he didn't care. "Then we'll travel light."

She leaned her forehead against his shoulder, carefully, on the uninjured side, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the rain spoke for them, hammering the roof like a crowd that had made up its mind.

"Six days," she said at last.

"Six days."

Outside, far down the valley, a generator coughed and steadied—the logging station, maybe, or some other machine of Meridian's grinding on through the wet dark, indifferent, certain of itself. Marguerite lay back and listened to it and thought about cells, and courtrooms, and the particular courage it takes to walk toward the thing you've spent five years walking away from.

And then, in the corridor, a sister's quick footsteps, and a knock, and her voice through the door, breathless:

"There's a runner at the gate. Wet through. Says he has a message—for the daughter of Elias Vane. Says it's about where they're keeping him."

Astrid was on her feet before the sentence ended, one hand already inside her coat.

Marguerite sat up slowly, his heart going hard against the bruised ribs, six days suddenly feeling like both a lifetime and no time at all.
