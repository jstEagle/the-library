## 27. Elias Vane, Alive

The message came on a Tuesday, though in the mission infirmary there was no such thing as Tuesday—only the rhythm of rain on the corrugated roof and Sister Anselm's soft footsteps at all hours, and Marguerite's fever breaking and returning like a tide that hadn't yet decided about him.

I had been asleep in the chair beside his cot, my head tipped against the wall, my coat—the coat with the notebooks sewn into its lining—folded under my arm even in sleep. I woke to a hand on my shoulder and a face I didn't know: a young man, river-browned, with the calloused hands of someone who poled cargo for a living and the apologetic posture of someone who'd rather be anywhere else.

"You're the Vane woman," he said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody's woman."

He flushed. "You're Astrid Vane. There's a message. From upriver." He glanced at Marguerite, sleeping with his mouth open, his ribs bandaged, his breathing shallow but even. "It's not for him. It's for you specifically. I was told that twice."

"Who told you?"

"A man who wouldn't give a name. He found me at the fuel dock two days ago. Paid me more than the message was worth, which is how I knew it mattered." He held out an envelope, cheap paper gone soft with damp, sealed with nothing but a fold. "He said you'd want to read it alone. He said—" The courier hesitated, and something in his face made my stomach drop before he said it. "He said your father sends his love, and he's sorry about the boat."

*Sorry about the boat.* My father had never once apologized for anything in his life except once, when I was nine, for missing my birthday—and he'd made it up by teaching me to read contour lines until midnight, both of us hunched over a survey map by lamplight while my mother slept. *A closed contour is a hill or a hole,* he'd said. *The trick is knowing which. Same shape, opposite meaning.*

I took the envelope into the chapel because it was the only room in the mission with a door that locked, and because I needed somewhere small. The chapel smelled of wax and mildew and old incense, and the rain had eased to a whisper on the roof, and I stood there for a long time holding the envelope without opening it, the way you stand at the edge of cold water.

Because I already knew what it would say. Some part of me had known since Camp Ashfall, since the ledger page with his name struck through—not crossed out the way you cross out a dead man, but struck through the way you strike through a name on a list you intend to come back to. Since the boot prints heading inland. Since every night since, when I'd dreamed of him alive and woken disappointed, then hated myself for being disappointed that my father might be dead instead of captive.

I opened it.

The handwriting was his. That was the first blow—that he was alive enough to write, that his hand still slanted forward like a man leaning into weather. The paper was stationery from somewhere institutional, the kind with faint blue rules, and the ink was pencil, which meant they'd let him have pencil and nothing else.

*Astrid. If this reaches you, you've gotten further than I dared hope, which means you have the notebooks, which means you are in more danger than I can say and less than they think. Listen carefully and don't do anything foolish, which I know is asking a great deal, given whose daughter you are.*

*I'm alive. They've kept me at the logging station above Grey Fork since June. Four men, one of them decent, three of them not. I'm well enough. They feed me. They want one thing: the field notes. As long as Meridian believes the evidence exists outside their reach, I'm worth more breathing than buried. The moment they learn it's been delivered—authenticated, copied, entered into any record anywhere—I stop being insurance and start being a loose end. Do you understand what I'm telling you?*

*Every step you take toward the assizes is a step away from me. I need you to choose the documents. Swear to me you'll choose the documents. Take them to Delphine Okafor, take them to the capital, take them to the newspapers if the courts fail. Don't come for me. Don't trade anything for me. I spent twenty years teaching you that some ground is not worth standing on, and this is the lesson, and God forgive me for making you learn it this way.*

*They let me write one letter a month. This is month four. I don't know how many months I have. Be clever. Be hard. Come find me after, if there is an after.*

*Your father,
Elias Vane*

*P.S. — Sorry about the Kestrel. I know what she means to him. Tell him the sluice gates at the logging station are the old mine gates, 1911 ironwork, and the wheel housing floods every monsoon. He'll understand.*

I read it three times. Then I sat down on the chapel floor, my back against a pew, and did not cry, because crying would have been a relief and I couldn't afford relief. Relief loosens you. I needed to stay tight as wire.

*Choose the documents.*

He was asking me to leave him. My father, who had vanished into concessions and silence for most of my childhood, who had taught me maps as a way of saying *I love you* because he couldn't say it, who had been held in a logging camp for months writing one careful letter a month—he was asking me to walk past him to the courthouse and let the machinery of justice grind forward over his body.

And the worst part, the part I turned over and over like a stone with something living under it, was that he was right. Every instinct I had said he was right. Delphine had said it too, in her dry clerk's voice: *Evidence has a half-life. Men in custody have a longer one, if they're useful.* Elias Vane, kept alive as insurance against exactly the papers sewn into my coat—if the papers reached the capital, the insurance lapsed. If the papers were traded, the fraud walked free and my father stayed a hostage forever, because men like Meridian's officers do not release hostages; they rotate them.

But he had written *don't come for me* in the same hand that had taught me contour lines, and I had spent six weeks learning that everything my father wrote and everything my father did pointed in opposite directions. He'd left us. He'd staked the mine. He'd gone silent. And all of it, I now understood, had been him trying to build something true in a valley built on lies—a survey that couldn't be forged, a claim that couldn't be stolen, a life that didn't require him to disappear. He disappeared anyway. Because the valley ate honest men.

I wasn't going to let it eat him too.

* * *

Marguerite was awake when I came out, propped on one elbow, gray-faced and clear-eyed, watching me with the particular attention of a man who reads weather. He'd been doing that since the fever broke—watching me the way he watched rivers, measuring, respectful, slightly wary, as if I were white water he hadn't run before.

"You got a letter," he said.

"How could you possibly—"

"Courier came to the mission asking for you by name. Sisters talk. Everything talks, up here." He shifted, winced, settled. "Well?"

I gave him the letter. I watched him read it, and I watched the thing happen in his face that I knew would happen: the stillness first, then the narrowing, then the slow movement of his eyes across the postscript, where they stopped and stayed.

"The sluice gates," he said quietly.

"He says you'll understand."

"I do." He handed the letter back with a care that was almost ceremonial, as if it were evidence, which it was. "Old mine gates. Cast iron, geared wheels, probably seized, but the housing sits below the compound yard. In monsoon flow, if those gates opened, the whole yard goes under. Four feet of water, maybe five, moving fast." He looked at me. "Your father just drew us a map."

"My father just told us to stay away."

"Both things can be true. He was always good at that, from what you've told me." Marguerite swung his legs off the cot, tested his weight, and went pale in a way that made me cross the room and push him back down with both hands flat on his chest. His heart was going fast under my palms. Too fast.

"You have a hole in your ribs that's been sewn shut for four days," I said. "You can't walk to Grey Fork."

"I can walk to Grey Fork."

"You'll tear the sutures by the second ridge."

"Then I'll tear them by the second ridge." He said it without drama, the way he said everything that mattered—as a fact about terrain. "Astrid. Sit down. Please."

I sat. On the edge of the cot, because the chair felt suddenly too far from him.

"Let's be honest with each other," he said, "since we're apparently the only two people in this valley who ever are. If we go to the capital now, if we deliver the notebooks, your father dies. Not maybe. Meridian doesn't keep witnesses. They burned a camp full of surveyors to bury paper; they will burn one man to bury a problem. And if we go to the logging station, we spend days we may not have, we risk the notebooks, we risk the hearing window, we risk—" he touched my wrist, lightly, "—each other. And if we lose the notebooks, your father dies anyway, and so does every name in that ledger, and Tomas's snare lines and the burned camp and Karst Ridge all mean nothing. There is no version of this where we're safe. There's only versions where more people are alive at the end."

"So you're saying rescue him."

"I'm saying I've done the arithmetic three ways and it comes out the same. The documents only matter if someone testifies to them. Your father is the witness who makes the notebooks mean something—he signed the original claim, he can identify the forgeries line by line. Delivering the papers without him is delivering a gun without ammunition. Delphine needs him. The assizes need him." His voice dropped. "And I need him. I've spent five years being blamed for a disaster Meridian's subcontractors built. He's the man who can prove it. So no, I'm not saying rescue him because I love you, though I do, and you can put that down however you like. I'm saying rescue him because it's also the correct move, and I'd rather tell you the truth than tell you something tender."

I looked at him for a long moment. Rain whispered on the roof. Somewhere below us a sister was singing vespers badly, off-key and unbothered.

"You love me," I said.

His jaw tightened. "That's what you take from all of it."

"It's the part I'll remember longest. The rest is tactics." I stood up. "Here's what I take from the rest: you're right about the arithmetic, and my father is wrong about the choice, and both of those things can be true, because he taught me that himself. A closed contour is a hill or a hole. Same shape, opposite meaning. He's reading his own life as a hole—something to keep me from falling into. It's a hill. We climb it together, we take the notebooks with us, and we get him to the capital in time to testify."

"That's three days' travel through monsoon country with a wounded man and a bag of evidence that half the valley's armed men are hunting."

"Yes."

"And the sluice plan requires opening gates that haven't turned since 1911."

"Yes."

"And you're proposing this to a man who froze at a rope bridge four days ago."

The words came out harder than I intended, and I saw them land—saw the flinch move through him like wind through grass. But he didn't look away. That was the thing about Marguerite Coldharbour that five years of blacklisting hadn't managed to break: he took the hit and stayed facing you.

"I froze," he said. "And then I crossed. Hand over hand, shaking, with you talking me across every foot of it. I'm not going to pretend that didn't happen. I'm also not going to pretend it decides what happens next." He reached for the letter again, smoothed it flat on his knee, and tapped the postscript once. "Your father knew I'd understand the sluice gates. Think about that. He's been sitting in that camp for months, listening to four guards and a generator, working out exactly who could open those gates—and he decided it was me. A man he's never met. A blacklisted guide with a knife wound and a bad reputation." He almost smiled. "Elias Vane has more faith in me than the courts of two countries combined. I'd hate to disappoint him."

I laughed—a short, cracked sound, half sob, that I disguised as clearing my throat. Outside, the light was going copper behind the clouds, the way it did before the evening rains, and I thought: three days, maybe four, before the rivers close for good. Before the assizes close. Before the insurance lapses or holds. Every clock in the world ticking at once.

"We'll need Tomas's snare-line trails to approach unseen," I said, already turning toward the door, already becoming motion. "And we'll need to know the guard rotation, and whether the generator runs lights or pumps or both. And Marguerite—you'll need to be able to walk by tomorrow."

"I'll walk by tomorrow."

"And if the sutures tear—"

"Then you'll sew them again. You're better at it than the sister; she says so herself when she thinks I'm asleep." He lay back, exhausted by four sentences, but his eyes followed me to the door with that river-reading attention. "Astrid."

I stopped.

"When we pull him out," he said, "and he asks why you didn't listen—what are you going to tell him?"

I thought of the chapel, and the letter, and the shape of a closed contour drawn by a hand I'd have known anywhere in the dark.

"I'll tell him the same thing he told me when I was nine and wanted to climb the scree slope alone," I said. "*Some ground isn't worth standing on—but family isn't ground. Family is the reason you survey it.*" I pulled my coat—the coat, the notebooks shifting softly against my ribs like a second heartbeat—off the chair and shrugged it on. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we go get my father."

Behind me, in the darkening infirmary, Marguerite began, quietly, to count aloud the things we'd need—rope, wax, a crowbar for the gate gears, dry powder for the radio—and I recognized the sound for what it was: a man measuring miles because miles couldn't leave him, walking the trail in his head before his feet were allowed to follow.

By dawn, we'd need every mile of it.
