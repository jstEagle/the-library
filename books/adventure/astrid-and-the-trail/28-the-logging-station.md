## 28. The Logging Station

We watched the station from the ridge for most of a morning, lying in the wet ferns with the monsoon breathing down our necks, and I counted what there was to count because counting was the only prayer I knew.

Four guards. That was the number, and it held through three hours of watching, which meant it was probably true and certainly not lucky. Two at the gate of the compound yard, one making a slow circuit of the perimeter every forty minutes—Tomas would have called it a lazy circuit, an insult to the profession—and one who never seemed to leave the long shed that housed the generator. The generator itself was the fifth guard, in a way: its diesel stutter carried across the valley like a sick animal's heartbeat, and everything at the station hung from it. The floodlights. The radio mast with its wire strung up through the cedars. The electric lock on the door of the bunkhouse where they kept Elias Vane.

I had seen men kept before. I had been kept myself, once, by a different kind of wall—the kind made of paper and verdicts instead of wire—but I knew the architecture when I saw it. They weren't holding Elias because he was dangerous. They were holding him because he was *worth* something, and worth was a thing Meridian Consolidated understood better than it understood timber or ore or the people it ground up between them.

Through my binoculars—I'd traded half our coffee for them in Port Meridian, and hadn't regretted it once—I watched the bunkhouse window. It was small, high, barred on the outside, which told me they expected their prisoner to want out rather than anyone to want in. Behind the glass, a shape moved. Gray. Slow. It sat down on what might have been a cot and did not get up again for a long time.

"That's him," Astrid said beside me. Her voice was flat in the way I'd learned meant the opposite of calm. "That's how he sits. He always sat like that, even when I was little. Like he was listening to something under the floor."

I passed her the glasses. She looked a long time. Rain beaded on the lenses and she didn't wipe them, so her father came to her in pieces, blurred and doubled, and maybe that was mercy.

"He's thinner," she said finally.

"Six weeks as somebody's insurance will do that."

She lowered the glasses. In the gray light under the trees her face had gone sharp as a contour line, all elevation change and no softness, and I thought—not for the first time—that I had never met anyone who could look so much like a map of hard country while standing still.

"Four guards," she said. "One generator. One door with an electric lock. One river running right past them, swollen to twice its summer width." She said it like she was reading off a supply manifest. "And sluice gates."

I turned and looked at her.

The logging station sat above the river on a shelf of cut ground, and above *it* sat the old sluice works—a flume and gate assembly the company had built decades ago to float logs down to the mill, abandoned when the mill closed but never dismantled, because dismantling cost money and Meridian had never once in its history spent money it could spend on lawyers instead. The gates were rusted orange. The flume was choked with deadfall. And behind those gates, backed up in the steep gorge above the station, the river had been gathering all week, all month, all season, the way grievances gather.

"You want to open them," I said.

"I want to open them."

* * *

We spent the afternoon going over it, crouched under a rock overhang while the first real rain of the front came down in ropes, and I will say this for Astrid Vane: she had drawn the whole thing in her head already, in detail, the way her father must have taught her to draw things, and she laid it out for me with a stick in the mud like a general who has already decided the battle is won and is only waiting for the terrain to catch up.

"The guards rotate at dusk," she said. "Gate pair changes over, the perimeter man comes in to eat. For about twenty minutes there are only three of them awake and none of them happy about it. That's when we move. You go up to the sluice works—it's a scramble, forty minutes if the rock's not greasy, an hour if it is. I come down through the treeline to the back of the compound, here—" the stick jabbed a groove in the mud "—where the fence is just wire, no lights, because nobody ever runs toward the gorge side. I cut the generator cable at the shed. Not the fuel line—the cable, where it runs into the building. Lights die, lock dies, radio dies. Elias walks out of a dark bunkhouse and meets me at the fence. You open the gates. The water comes down the flume channel and floods the yard—look at the grade, Marguerite, look at it—the whole compound slopes toward the river. The guards run for high ground and don't think about us at all, because men whose feet are underwater do not chase anybody."

I looked at the grade. She was right about the grade. She was right about almost everything, and that was the problem, because being right about almost everything left out the parts that killed you.

"Say it," she said, when I'd been quiet too long.

"Fine. Here's what kills us." I took the stick from her hand, because some things you have to draw yourself. "One. The scramble up to the sluice works is wet limestone and wet limestone in the rain is ice that hasn't gotten cold yet. I go down once, I break a leg, and you're alone in a compound full of armed men with a plan that needs two people. Two. Those gates haven't moved in twenty years. If the winch is seized, I stand there cranking a dead handle while your father watches the floodlights come back on and figures out his daughter came all this way to be shot. Three. When that water comes down, it doesn't come down politely. It comes down like the end of an argument. Anything in that yard that floats goes into the river, and anything that doesn't float gets buried in debris, and either one can be a person. Your father is sixty-some years old and six weeks underfed. He stands up wrong when the water hits and he's gone, and you spend the rest of your life knowing you drowned him yourself. Four—"

"Stop."

"No. Four. The guards. Men running for high ground don't chase anybody, you're right. But men who've just lost their prisoner and their pride in the same minute are not reasonable, and one of them has a rifle, and we'll be in the open between the fence and the treeline when they figure out what happened. Five." I drove the stick into the mud and left it standing there like a grave marker. "Five. Even if all of it works—even then—we come out of this soaked, hunted, carrying a half-starved old man through monsoon country with Meridian's riders somewhere behind us and the documents sewn into your coat getting rained on. Every single thing we've walked here to protect, floating downriver in a coat pocket."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below us the generator stuttered, caught, stuttered again.

Astrid looked at my five graves in the mud for a long moment. Then she reached out and smoothed them flat with her palm, one motion, unhurried, the way you'd smooth a bed.

"Yes," she said. "All true. Now tell me the alternative."

"There isn't one."

"Then we've settled the important question, haven't we?" She wasn't angry. That was the terrible part. She said it the way she'd once talked me across a flooded rope bridge, hand over hand, her voice level as a spirit gauge while the water tried to tear the world apart. "You've just spent an hour listing every way this kills us, and you already know you're coming anyway. I could see it while you were talking. So let's not pretend the calculation is still open. Let's spend the time making the plan better instead."

* * *

And that was what we did. We worked until the light went out of the gorge, and then we worked by touch, and somewhere in there I stopped being a man evaluating a plan and became a man preparing for one, which is a different animal entirely. Evaluation asks whether a thing will work. Preparation assumes it won't, and packs accordingly.

We improved it, piece by piece. Astrid wouldn't cut the cable blind—she'd loosen the shed's junction box cover first, during her approach, so the actual cut took four seconds instead of thirty. I wouldn't trust the winch; I'd carry a pry bar salvaged from the Kestrel's tool locker, and if the drum was seized I'd knock the pawl loose and let the gates fall open under their own weight, which was faster anyway and louder, and loud was fine, because loud was part of the point. We tied a rope around Elias in advance—or rather, Astrid sewed a harness into a bundle of dry clothes she'd leave inside the fence line, so that whatever happened, he'd have something to hold onto that wasn't her hand, because her hands needed to be free. We picked the extraction route along the high bank downstream, above the flood line, marked it in my head by three trees and a split boulder because maps were paper and paper died in this rain.

And when we ran out of improvements, we ate cold rice and sat shoulder to shoulder under the overhang listening to the storm assemble itself over the ridge, and neither of us slept much, and neither of us pretended to.

At some hour that had no name, Astrid spoke into the dark.

"You listed five ways we die tonight."

"Six, actually. I was saving the sixth."

"What was it?"

I watched the lightning walk along the far ridge, counting the seconds out of habit, measuring distance because distance couldn't leave me. "That we pull it off," I said. "Perfectly. And it changes nothing. Meridian buries it, buries us, buries the notebooks, and the valley keeps bleeding. That's the one that got Tomas taken. That's the one that got your father locked in a shed. The plan works and the world doesn't care."

She was quiet a while. Then, in the dark, her hand found mine—not grabbing, just resting there, the way you rest a hand on a rope to feel what it's telling you.

"Then we make the world care," she said. "Tomorrow. Tonight we just get my father out."

* * *

Dusk came down gray and early, the way it does in monsoon country, night arriving less like darkness than like deep water. Below us the rotation began: the gate pair trudging their exchange, the perimeter man sloshing in, the generator coughing against the wet. Twenty minutes. Maybe less, if the cook was slow with the rice.

Astrid stood at the treeline with the cutters in her belt and the rain already flattening her hair, and she looked at me the way she had looked at the rope bridge—no fear in it, or rather fear fully accounted for, entered in the ledger, signed off.

"Hand over hand," she said. Which meant: even if you can't see the far end.

"Hand over hand," I said.

She went down through the trees like water finding its level, and I turned uphill toward the sluice works, and the last thing I heard from her direction was the small clean sound of wire parting.

The climb was worse than I'd promised her. Wet limestone, exactly as advertised, each hold a negotiation, the gorge roaring below me with the voice of everything we were about to unleash. At the top, the gates rose out of the dark like the ribs of something drowned, and the water behind them pressed against the rusted iron with a hum I felt in my teeth more than heard—a held breath the size of a valley.

I got the pry bar under the pawl. Below me, the compound lights went out all at once, and in the sudden blackness I heard Astrid's whistle, one note, and then a second sound: a man's voice, weak and astonished, saying a name.

*Astrid.*

I knocked the pawl free.

The gates fell open with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and the river came down the flume channel toward the compound yard, and I threw myself sideways onto the rock as the whole valley below me turned white and roared.
