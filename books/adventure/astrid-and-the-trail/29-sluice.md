## 29. Sluice

**MARGUERITE**

The rain came down like a verdict.

It had been falling for six hours without pause, hammering the logging station's tin roofs into a single continuous sound, and Marguerite lay in the wet ferns above the compound and let it soak through his jacket because moving meant noise and noise meant dying. Below him, the yard was a lake of mud already, lamplight swimming in the ruts. Four guards. One generator. A long shed with a padlocked door, and behind that door, if the plan held, a gray old man who had once taught his daughter to read contour lines by candlelight.

He counted distances instead of days. He always had. The generator shed to the sluice gate: forty meters. The sluice gate to the guard post: sixty. The guard post to the padlock: thirty-five, past the woodpile, past the flooded drainage ditch that was now less ditch than river. Every distance had a cost attached, and he added them the way he'd once added rope lengths on Karst Ridge, before the arithmetic went wrong.

Beside him, Astrid lay flat in the ferns, rain sheeting off her hood, her breathing slow and deliberate—the breathing of someone who had learned it from him and then improved on it. She'd cut her hair shorter with his knife two nights ago in the cave, a practical act that had looked, in the firelight, like something older than practicality. Now she watched the compound with eyes that did not blink enough.

"Generator first," she said, for the fourth time. Not because she doubted it. Because saying it kept the fear in its cage.

"Generator first," he agreed. "You cut the fuel line or the ignition—"

"Fuel line. Ignition's boxed. I checked."

"I know you checked." He turned his head, and rain ran off his chin. "I'm saying it out loud so I can hear it too."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. In this weather, in this place, it was enough.

"The gates," she said. "When the lights die, they'll come out of the guard post with lamps. That's when you move. Forty meters of open ground."

"Forty meters," he said. "I've crossed worse."

"You haven't."

"No," he admitted. "But I've frozen on worse, and I didn't freeze forever."

She reached over without looking at him and gripped his wrist once, hard, the way you grip a rope. Then she was gone, sliding down through the ferns toward the generator shed, and Marguerite lay alone in the rain and listened to the water rising everywhere—in the ditches, in the river behind the ridge, in the sluice channel that fed the old log flume and stood, tonight, against a wooden gate that hadn't been maintained since Meridian stopped caring about anything but taking.

The gate was the whole idea, and the whole idea was insane. Astrid had seen it first, standing on the ridge in the last gray light: the sluice pond backed up behind the ridge, black and swollen with monsoon, held by a timber gate with an iron winch. Open the winch, and the pond would come down the flume channel and across the compound yard—a wall of water maybe half a meter deep, fast as a galloping horse, enough to knock men down, drown lamps, turn four armed guards into four drowning, blind, scrambling men for exactly as long as it took to do what needed doing.

Every way it could kill them, he had listed for her. The gate could jam. The water could carry *them* away. Elias might not be able to stand against a current. The guards might shoot blind and hit true. She had listened to all of it, nodded, and said, "And every way staying here kills us is longer list."

So here they were.

Below, a lamp moved in the guard post window. A man stepped out into the rain, stood under the eave, smoked something whose ember glowed and died. Marguerite counted his own breaths. Eleven. Twelve. Somewhere up the dark slope, the sluice pond waited behind its rotten timbers like a held breath.

Then the lights died.

Not flickered—died, all at once, the compound plunging from lamplight to a darkness so total that for a moment Marguerite saw nothing but the afterimage burned into his eyes. And in that moment, three things happened almost together: the smoker swore and fumbled back inside; a second guard's voice rose, annoyed, calling about the generator; and Marguerite came up out of the ferns and ran.

Forty meters of mud. He ran it with the winch bar over his shoulder, boots sucking, lungs full of rain, and the whole time some cold clerk in the back of his skull tallied the costs: *ten meters, fifteen, the guard post door opening behind you, twenty-five, don't look, thirty-*—

The gate loomed up out of the dark, timber and iron, streaming. He slammed the winch bar into the socket and threw his weight on it.

Nothing.

The mechanism was rusted solid, or silted, or both. He heaved again, teeth bared, and felt the bar flex and his shoulder scream, and behind him the guard post door banged open and lamplight swung wildly across the yard, and a voice shouted something in two languages at once.

*Come on.* He put his whole history into it—Karst Ridge, the blacklist, five years of forgettable trails, the woman cutting a fuel line somewhere in the dark behind him, the old man behind the padlock. *Come on, you rotten thing.*

The winch gave with a crack like a rifle.

Water did not flow out of the flume channel. It *arrived*—a black shoulder of the pond shouldering through the opened gate, tearing the last of the rotten timbers loose, coming down the channel with a sound like the ridge itself clearing its throat. Marguerite had one second to see it in the swinging lamplight, one second to understand that he had badly underestimated it, and then he threw himself sideways and clung to the gate stanchion as the flood hit the yard.

It took the woodpile first, scattering split logs like toys. It took the smoker off his feet and rolled him under the eave. It took the lamps—every lamp, drowned in a heartbeat—and then the yard was nothing but churning brown water, shouting, and the generator shed door banging open with Astrid already running through it, small and fast and impossibly upright in the flood.

"Astrid!" His voice tore. "The shed! Get to the shed!"

She heard him. Of course she heard him. She changed course through waist-deep water without breaking stride, and Marguerite let go of the stanchion and let the current take him—aimed it, the way you aim a fall—slamming into the long shed's wall beside the padlocked door just as a shot cracked somewhere and spanged off iron above his head.

The padlock. His hands were shaking so hard he could barely fit the bolt cutters. Behind him, in the roaring dark, guards were screaming at each other, one of them crying out that the river had come, the river had *come*, as though it were a judgment and not a winch.

The lock sheared. The door swung. And there, blinking in the wash of light from a guard's dropped torch, chained by one ankle to a ringbolt in the floor, sat an old man with white stubble and his daughter's exact gray eyes.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," Elias Vane said, in a voice worn down to gravel. "My daughter has terrible taste in rivers."

"Yeah," Marguerite gasped, working the bolt cutters on the chain. "Everyone says."

* * *

**ASTRID**

The generator had been easy. That was the thing no one told you about sabotage—that the hard part was never the machine. The hard part was crouching in the dark beside a humming box of steel, knowing that everything after the first cut belonged to chaos, and making your hands be steady anyway.

Astrid had made her hands steady. She had cut the fuel line clean, heard the engine cough and die, and run straight into the end of the world.

The water was worse than they'd planned for. She'd guessed half a meter; it was nearer a meter and still rising, tearing through the yard with logs and fence rails in its teeth, and she lost her footing twice and caught herself on things she didn't see, only felt—post, rail, the corner of the guard post itself. A shot went off somewhere above the roar and she flinched and kept moving, because stopping was how people died in rivers, and she had been raised on rivers even if her father had been raised on ledgers.

The long shed door was open. Inside, by the light of a fallen torch guttering in the floodwater, she saw them: Marguerite on his knees with the bolt cutters, and a shape sitting against the wall that made her chest do something painful and involuntary.

"Dad."

Elias Vane looked up. Six weeks of captivity had carved him down—he was thinner than her memory of him, grayer, one wrist swollen—but his eyes found her across the churning water with no hesitation at all, as though he'd known for years exactly where she would be standing.

"There you are," he said. "You're late."

"You're one to talk." Her voice cracked on the second word and she hated it and let it happen. "Six weeks, Dad. *Six weeks.*"

"I wrote you letters," he said. "They didn't post them." Then the chain parted with a snap, and Marguerite hauled him upright, and Elias swayed and would have gone down if they hadn't both caught him—one under each arm, her father's weight between them, lighter than she remembered and burning hot with fever.

"Can you walk?" Marguerite shouted over the water.

"He can float," Astrid said. "Dad, the boathouse—downstream side—we go with the current, we don't fight it, we steer. Can you hear me? We *steer*."

"I taught her that," Elias told Marguerite, with the ghost of his old pedantic pride. "Contour maps. Water reads the same way."

"Then read fast," Marguerite said, "because we have company."

Astrid looked up. Through the shed doorway, in the strobing confusion of a recovered lantern, she saw the guards regrouping on the high ground by the barracks—three of them, the fourth presumably still being punished by the river—and one of them raising a rifle, bracing it on the rail, finding his range.

Time did something strange. It slowed and thickened, and in the thickened time Astrid saw the muzzle, saw the water between them, saw her father's fever-bright face, and understood with perfect clarity that they were not going to make the boathouse.

Then the night answered her.

It came from upstream—a sound first, a long whistle-cut-whistle, three notes that did not belong to any bird, and then a figure out of the treeline on the far bank, moving along the ridge of the sluice channel with the sureness of a man walking his own hallway. He carried a machete in one hand and a length of chain in the other, and he swung the chain once, twice, and let it fly, and it wrapped the lantern arm of the guard post and *pulled*, and the lantern crashed down into the water and the dark came back like a lid closing.

In the new dark, the rifleman fired blind, twice, and hit nothing but rain.

"Tomas," Astrid breathed.

"Tomas," Marguerite confirmed grimly, hauling Elias toward the door. "Of course it's Tomas. The man can't stay caught."

They went out into the flood together, the three of them lashed into one struggling shape, and the water took them the way she'd promised her father it would—fast, but steerable. Astrid got her father's good arm over her shoulders and angled them downstream, reading the current by feel the way he'd taught her to read it by eye: the smooth tongue of deeper water, the V of a submerged obstacle, the slack water eddying behind the boathouse wall. Marguerite was on Elias's other side, and through the roar she heard him talking—not to her, to Elias, a low continuous stream of instruction and reassurance, *left foot here, lean with me, breathe when I breathe*—and she understood that this was what he had been on Karst Ridge, before: not a killer, not a coward, a guide. This was the voice that had been ruined and buried and was now, in the flooding dark, simply doing its work.

Something splashed heavily into the water ahead of them. She nearly screamed—then the shape surfaced, slicked hair and gleaming grin, and Tomas Reyes-Idowu fell in beside them like he'd been invited.

"Took you long enough," he said. "I've been in that treeline since Tuesday."

"You were *captured*," Astrid shouted.

"I escaped. There's a difference. It involves dignity." He grabbed Elias's collar without being asked, taking a third of the weight, and the four of them came around the boathouse wall into the slack water behind it, breathless, battered, alive.

Inside, among nets and rotting planks, they collapsed. Astrid got her father against the wall and pressed her palm to his forehead—too hot, far too hot—and he caught her hand and held it with surprising strength.

"The notebooks," he rasped. "Tell me you have the notebooks."

"Sealed in wax. Safe." She laughed, or sobbed, or both. "We came for *you*, you impossible man."

"And the guards?" Marguerite asked Tomas, at the door, watching the water.

"Two washed downstream. One won't leave the barracks till morning. The shooter's still up there, but he's got no boat and no stomach for the dark." Tomas grinned, teeth white in the gloom, and then the grin faded into something older and wearier. "They'll bring the rest of Meridian's dogs by dawn, though. This station will report silent, and someone senior will come to see why. You've got until first light. Maybe."

Outside, the rain kept falling, and the flood kept draining through the broken gate, and somewhere upstream the sluice pond sighed itself empty against the ruins of its timbers. Astrid knelt in the dark of the boathouse with her father's fevered hand in hers and the notebooks sewn against her ribs, and thought: *we pulled him out of one river. Now we have to get all of us down another.*

Marguerite crouched beside her, listening to the water, and when he spoke his voice was quiet and certain.

"The Kestrel's moored two bends down. If we move now, in the dark, while their boats are sunk and their courage is drowning—" he glanced at Elias, at the fever in his face— "we can be on the river before they regroup."

Elias coughed, a deep wet sound, and smiled at his daughter.

"Well," he said. "Lead on, guide. I believe I'm your cargo."

Marguerite looked at Astrid across her father's shoulders, and in the lightning-flash through the boathouse slats she saw the question in his eyes—*are we ready?*—and answered it the only way that mattered, by standing up and shouldering her father's arm.

"One more river," she said. "Together."

But as they slipped out the downstream door into the rain, none of them saw the lantern relight in the guard post window—or the man behind it, reaching for the station's spare radio.
