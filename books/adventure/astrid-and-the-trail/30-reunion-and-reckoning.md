## 30. Reunion and Reckoning

The boathouse sat at the elbow of the flooded compound, half its roof peeled back like the lid of a tin, and inside it smelled of creosote, wet rope, and old gasoline. Rain came through the broken slats in long silver threads, stitching light to dark. Somewhere behind us the sluice channel was still running high, a sound like a crowd that would not stop applauding.

Marguerite set Elias down on an upturned skiff as gently as if he were laying a keel. My father weighed nothing. That was the thing I could not get past—Elias Vane had once carried me on his shoulders for six miles of switchbacks without breaking stride, and now he was a bundle of gray sticks in a stolen coat, his wrists ringed with sores, his beard gone white at thirty fewer years than it should have been.

"Astrid." He said my name the way he used to say *summit*—with wonder, and a little disbelief, as though the word might not hold.

"Dad."

I knelt in the bilge water and put my arms around him and felt him shake. Not crying, exactly. Something deeper than crying—the tremor of a man whose body had forgotten it was allowed to be held. His hands found my shoulders, my hair, the shape of my face, mapping me like terrain he'd only ever known from aerial photographs.

"You got tall," he said into my ear, absurdly, and I laughed and sobbed at once, and the two sounds came out tangled, indistinguishable, which felt right for us. We had never been a family of clean feelings. We were a family of contour lines—everything rising gradually toward something we rarely named.

* * *

Tomas appeared in the doorway twenty minutes later, soaked to the collar, one sleeve torn where a bullet or a branch had taken it, and stood dripping while Marguerite checked the door bars and the one small window facing the treeline. The rain was thick enough to hide a regiment; it could also hide four guards coming back for their prisoner. Nobody said this aloud. Everybody thought it.

"Water's still rising," Tomas said. "By morning the yard will be a lake and the road out will be under two feet. They'll assume we drowned or flew."

"They'll assume," Marguerite said, "wrongly, that we're stupid enough to stay."

"We're staying till dawn," I said.

Both men looked at me. My father looked at me longest, and something moved across his ruined face—not objection, but recognition. He knew that tone. I'd learned it from him.

"Till dawn," he agreed. "Then the river."

* * *

We made a fire in an iron brazier that had once heated pitch for caulking, and Elias ate the way starving men eat—slowly, deliberately, as though food were testimony too, each swallow a fact sworn to. Marguerite kept watch at the window with the rifle cradled wrong for comfort and right for use. Tomas slept sitting up, instantly, completely, the sleep of a man who had learned that rest is a resource you take when offered because it may not be offered twice.

And my father and I talked.

It came out of him in pieces at first, the way water finds cracks. Six weeks in a Meridian logging station, in a bunkhouse with the windows painted over. Fourteen months before that, moving between stations, always "in transit," always with an escort who smiled too easily. Before that—the part I already knew, the part that had brought me upriver—a survey camp burned in a single night, seven colleagues scattered or dead, and himself carried inland by men who told him his daughter would be safer if he cooperated.

"They never hurt me," he said. "That was the genius of it. They didn't have to. They just let me understand, very clearly, what would happen to the people I loved if I stopped being useful." He turned his cup in his hands. "I signed things. Retractions. Statements saying the field notes were falsified by me. Every document they put in front of me, I signed, because every week I refused was a week someone checked whether you were still in Port Meridian, still predictable, still findable."

"I'm not angry," I said, and found that it was true. "Dad. I'm not angry at you."

"You should be furious at me. It's what I'd want. Fury keeps a person upright."

"I've done my fury. I did it years ago, at you, for disappearing. For choosing the mine over—" I stopped. Over us. Over Mother's grave. Over me. "It turns out most of it belonged somewhere else."

He reached over and took my hand, and his grip was still strong, still a climber's grip, callus and tendon. "Tell me what you know," he said. "All of it. Start with the camp."

So I told him. The burned survey station, the ledger page with his name struck through. The boot prints inland. Tomas and the rigged boundary surveys, the auditors stripped of licenses, the pattern of ruin that ran through everyone who had ever measured that valley honestly. The claim site, the cache, the notebooks sealed in wax. The letter naming the surveyor-general's office. The riders on the ridge. The crossing. The cave. Delphine Okafor and her promise. The dock at dawn, the knife, the fever, the sluice gates.

Marguerite listened from the window without turning around, but I could feel him listening, the way you feel weather change.

When I finished, my father was silent for a long time. The fire ticked. Rain stitched its silver threads.

"The notebooks are intact?" he asked finally.

"Wax-sealed. Dry. Delphine has copies being made tonight—Astrid's couriers, three routes, in case the originals are seized."

"Good girl." He closed his eyes. "You have no idea what those pages cost. Old Manoukian died carrying half of them. Did you know that? The night the camp burned, he went back for the strongbox. I heard him shouting my name in the smoke and then I heard nothing, and they put me in a truck, and for fourteen months I didn't know if the notes had burned with him." His voice cracked and steadied itself, the way old timber does. "They're all there? The 1911 resurvey? The boundary amendments?"

"All of it. Plus your own field book. Plus the letter."

"Then it's enough." He opened his eyes and looked at me with an intensity that frightened me more than the riders had. "Listen to me, Astrid. This is the part that matters, so listen the way you used to listen when I taught you bearings. You take the notebooks and you go south. There's a steamer out of Puerto Cabral every Thursday—you can make it if you leave tomorrow. You go to Chile, or Lima, anywhere with a free press and a consulate. You give the documents to a newspaper and a lawyer simultaneously, and you stay there, safe, until it breaks open on its own."

"That's not a plan, Dad. That's surrender with better scenery."

"It's survival."

"It's exile."

"It's *living*," he said, and his voice rose, and Tomas stirred, and Marguerite turned from the window at last. "You don't know what these people do when a court threatens them. I do. I watched them burn a camp full of my friends rather than let a re-survey reach a filing cabinet. The magistrate owns shares, you said—did Delphine tell you how many? Did she tell you he *countersigned* the amendments himself?"

The words dropped into the boathouse like a stone into still water.

"What?" Marguerite said quietly.

My father leaned forward, firelight jumping in his eyes. "You have the letter naming the surveyor-general's office. But the original amendments—the ones that moved the Coldwater boundary three kilometers east—they required judicial ratification. A magistrate's signature. I saw the register page myself, before the camp burned. The same hand that ratified the forged surveys sits on the bench in Port Meridian. He isn't merely compromised. He is a *signatory*. If those notebooks enter his courtroom, they will not come out again. And neither will anyone connected to them."

* * *

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my body simply refused the skiff any longer.

"Then we don't file in Port Meridian," I said. "Delphine already told us. The assizes sit in the regional capital. Different jurisdiction. Recusal provisions. She's spent thirty years as a court clerk—she knows exactly which doors this can't go through."

"And the roads to the capital?" my father asked. "Who patrols them? Who owns the steamship line that runs the river? Astrid, Meridian doesn't need to burn anything else. They need only delay you forty-eight hours, and the assizes close for the season, and the notebooks become paper. Paper burns beautifully. I've seen it."

"Then we don't give them forty-eight hours."

"You're talking about gambling your life on clerks and timetables."

"I'm talking about doing the one thing you never did!" It came out louder than I meant, cracking against the tin roof, and the rain seemed to hush around it. "You found the truth, Dad. You carried it. You let them bury you alive rather than risk it—and where did it get you? Fourteen months in a painted window. Old Manoukian dead in the smoke. And Meridian still drawing boundaries like God. If we run to Chile, we win nothing except distance. The valley stays poisoned. Tomas stays missing. Marguerite stays blacklisted for a disaster *they caused*. And you stay a ghost. Running isn't testimony. Running is just a longer version of what they did to you—being erased, but comfortably."

He stared at me. And slowly, terribly, he began to smile—not agreement, not yet, but the smile of a man watching his own face walk toward him out of a photograph twenty years old.

"Your mother used to argue like that," he said. "Like the truth was a rope and letting go of it was the only dishonor."

"She learned it from you."

"No." He shook his head, and his voice went soft. "She learned it from herself. I learned it from her, and I forgot it, and forgetting it is the reason I'm sitting in a boathouse instead of standing in a courtroom." He looked down at his ruined wrists. "I begged them with silence, Astrid. Silence was my whole strategy for two years. Look what silence bought."

* * *

Marguerite crossed the boathouse then, unhurried, and crouched by the fire, feeding it a slat of broken oar. When he spoke, it was to my father, but I felt the words pass through me like current through wire.

"Elias. I've been on the wrong side of an inquiry. Karst Ridge—maybe you've heard the name, maybe you haven't. Five years ago they blamed me for a crossing collapse without giving me a hearing, and I believed for five years that hearings were theater, that courts were just companies wearing robes." He turned the slat, watching flame climb it. "Then a clerk named Delphine Okafor told me she'd read the anchor reports and never believed the verdict, and she kept believing it alone for five years because that's what faith in procedure looks like when nobody's watching. And then your daughter sewed documents into her coat and walked past armed men to keep an appointment with a courtroom." He looked up. "I'm not going to tell you the courts will save us. I've seen them fail. But I'll tell you what I know about running: I ran for five years, and everything I ran from was waiting for me at the far end, grown larger. These people survive on erasure. Every person who flees with evidence is a person who can be disappeared quietly. The only thing they genuinely fear is a room they don't control, with a record being made in it."

"And you're willing to bet your freedom on that room," Elias said. It wasn't quite a question. "I know who you are, Marguerite Reyes. The warrant. Testimony means custody."

"I know what it costs."

"Do you? Because I've paid in custody, friend, and I'd pay it again rather than watch another camp burn—but I won't watch my daughter pay it. That's the difference between us and why I'm asking her to run."

"He's not asking me to run," I said. "He's asking me to choose. So I'm choosing."

* * *

My father was quiet for a long time after that. The fire burned down to a red eye. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the roof, and the flood in the yard murmured against the boathouse walls like a tide deciding.

"Inheritance," he said at last, to the fire. "Do you know what I intended to leave you? Shares. Concessions. A company with my name on the letterhead and my mistakes buried under it. That was the estate. That was what a father handed down." He laughed, a dry, cracked sound. "And here you are, having already inherited the only thing worth passing on: the field notes. The habit of checking. The refusal to sign what you know is false." He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and steady. "I renounced my inheritance when I signed their retractions. Every document I signed under duress was me handing my name to them. You're the one holding the family name now, Astrid. Whatever you decide to do with it—that's the legacy. Not blood. Choice."

"Then I choose the capital," I said. "Tomorrow. With the notebooks. With both of you, if you'll come."

"I'm hardly in a condition to—"

"You'll come," I said, "because if I'm gambling, I'm gambling with the witness present. Delphine will want you alive and locatable. And because I have spent fourteen months not knowing where you were, and I am not spending one more day of it."

He bowed his head. And then, in the firelight, my father began to laugh—quietly at first, then helplessly, shaking, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand, laughing the way men laugh at funerals and at summits, when relief and grief arrive in the same hour and there is nothing to do but let them share the tent.

"All right," he said. "All right. The capital. God help us, the capital." He reached out and gripped my hand again, hard. "But hear me, both of you—if it goes wrong, if the room turns against us, you run. Both of you. Promise me the running stays in reserve. A good mountaineer knows the descent route before she needs it."

"I know the descent route," I said. "It's the river. It's always been the river."

* * *

Near dawn, Tomas woke without being woken, the way he always did, and reported the yard transformed—a brown lake where the parade ground had been, the guard post roof-deep, the road gone. Marguerite stood at the door a long time, reading the water the way other men read newspapers.

"There's a log boom upstream of the compound," he said finally. "Flood's lifted it off its pins. We float out on the debris line, quiet as driftwood, and catch the current below the bend. By the time they realize the prisoner's gone with the flood, we'll be ten miles downriver."

"And the boat?" my father asked.

Marguerite glanced at me. "The Kestrel's moored two bends down, past the fish traps. Assuming nobody cut her loose."

"Assuming," I said, "is how we got into this. Let's go find out."

My father rose stiffly, leaning on my shoulder, and paused at the doorway with the gray light coming up behind him and the rain thinning at last to a fine drifting veil. He looked older than the mountains and lighter than I had ever seen him.

"One last thing," he said. "The magistrate's signature—I'll write down the register reference from memory. Page and folio. Delphine will want it exact." He almost smiled. "Turns out they should have let me keep a pencil. It's the last mistake they'll make with me."

Behind us, downriver, hidden somewhere in the rain and the miles, the Kestrel waited—or didn't.
