## 32. Holding Cell

They took him at the boathouse door.

I had imagined it a hundred ways on the walk down from the sluice—sirens, struggle, Marguerite vanishing into the dark the way he had vanished from two countries' worth of maps. Instead it was almost gentle. Two constables in oilskins, rain streaming off their hats, and Marguerite standing with his hands open at his sides as if he'd been waiting for them all his life. Perhaps he had. Perhaps every mile he'd counted since Karst Ridge was a mile walked toward this particular doorway.

"Marguerite Coldharbour," the older constable said, not quite a question.

"You have me," Marguerite said. And then, over his shoulder, to me: "The notebooks stay dry. That's your whole job tonight. Everything else is weather."

They didn't cuff him. I would remember that later—the small mercy of it, hands left free—and I would also remember how he looked walking away between them, tall and unhurried, turning once at the corner of the fish sheds to find me in the lamplight. He didn't wave. He did something better: he tapped two fingers against his chest, right over the pocket where I'd sewn nothing, where the real documents were not, because we weren't fools even in farewell. *Keep it close,* the gesture said. *Keep yourself closer.*

Then the rain took him, and I stood alone in a boathouse that smelled of tar and diesel and my father's rescue, holding a canvas satchel that weighed less than a bird and more than a mountain.

* * *

Delphine Okafor found me within the hour.

I heard her before I saw her—a cane knocking a careful rhythm on the boardwalk, unhurried, proprietary, the walk of a woman who had spent forty years making other people wait for her. She came into the boathouse out of the rain like weather changing its mind, shook water from a black umbrella with one crisp motion, and looked around at Elias gray-faced on a cot, Tomas bandaged and snoring by the stove, and me standing guard over a bag of paper as if it were a sleeping child.

"So," she said. "You're Vane's daughter."

"And you're the clerk who kept a copy of everything."

Something moved behind her eyes—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a younger face. "Forty-one years at the regional registry teaches you one law above all others: originals burn, get lost, get eaten by damp, or get subpoenaed by men who own the building they're stored in. Copies are civilization." She held out her hand. "Let me see what we're dying for."

I gave her the notebooks. Watching another person's hands touch my father's field notes was like watching someone handle an organ I'd grown myself. Delphine turned the pages with a conservator's delicacy, tilting them toward the lamp, reading fast the way old clerks read—skimming for structure, for dates, for the telltale gaps where pages had been removed. Her breathing changed twice. Once at the boundary surveys, when she saw the coordinates that could not be reconciled with any honest instrument. Once at the letter naming Undersecretary Halloway's signature on the certification stamps.

"Halloway," she said softly. "He was junior then. Hungry. Everyone knew Meridian fed him, but knowing and proving are different animals entirely." She closed the notebook and rested her hand flat on its cover, the way you'd still a frightened animal. "Listen to me, girl, because we don't have time for me to say it twice."

"I'm listening."

"By sunrise, Meridian's lawyers will know these documents exist. Not might—will. The magistrate owns shares; the constable takes his coffee from the company kitchen; the harbor master signs whatever Meridian's man slides across his desk. The moment word spreads that Vane's evidence surfaced, there will be an application filed to suppress it—chain of custody, authenticity, provenance, every Latin ghost they can summon. And if those notebooks spend one night in an evidence locker in Port Meridian, I promise you they will come back to court looking exactly like blank paper."

"So we move them."

"We move them, we copy them, and we scatter the copies so wide that suppressing them becomes like suppressing the tide." She was already moving as she spoke, pulling a folding stool from under the workbench, setting it down, sitting with the economy of a woman who conserved every motion. "But understand what you're asking of the night. This isn't courage. Courage is quick. This is procedure. Hours of it. Ink-stained, eye-straining, unglamorous procedure—and it will save us where heroics would hang us."

Outside, the monsoon worked at the roof. Inside, my father slept the sleep of a man whose body had been borrowed by strangers for eleven weeks and only now returned. I looked at him a long moment—the white stubble, the wrists too thin for their own bones—and felt the fear arrive properly, late and total, the way cold water finds the seam in a boot.

Then I sat down across from Delphine Okafor, uncapped the ink, and let the fear become procedure.

* * *

We copied by lamplight until the lamp burned low and Delphine showed me how to trim and refill it without losing more than a minute.

She was methodical about everything. Three copies of each notebook page—one for the assizes, one for a courier she trusted, one for a hiding place she refused to name. She made me write the copies myself for the first hour. "Your hand matters," she said. "If it ever comes down to whether these are genuine, a court will want to hear from the daughter who transcribed her father's notes at risk of her life. Juries believe ink and grief. Lawyers believe neither, but juries do."

So I wrote. Page after page of my father's small, precise figures, and copying him was its own strange communion. I knew his numbers before I understood them—I'd grown up watching that pencil move across contour maps on our kitchen table, the lines stacking up like the ridges themselves. Now I traced his survey of the Grey Fork valley line by line, and I could feel him in it: the patience, the double-checking, the little asterisks he put beside measurements he wanted to verify again. He'd been careful. He'd been so careful, and they'd ruined him anyway, and then buried him alive rather than let his care see daylight.

My pen shook once, on page thirty-something, and blotted a figure. Delphine watched me stare at it.

"Copy it again," she said, not unkindly. "Grief is allowed. Blots aren't. That's the whole discipline of the law, if you want to know."

Around two in the morning, Tomas woke, drank half a kettle of tea, and insisted on helping despite a shoulder that Meridian's riders had nearly taken apart. He couldn't write—his right hand wouldn't close—but he could fold, seal, and wax, and he knew the back trails out of town the way I knew my own signature. Between his knowledge and Delphine's list of trusted names, we built the courier plan the way you'd build a raft: piece by piece, testing each joint.

"Father Anselm at the mission gets a set—he owes me for three inquests I steered gently," Delphine said, ticking names off on ink-stained fingers. "The steamship purser on the *Cormorant* carries a sealed folio to the capital registrar, marked as private legal correspondence—that's lawful and untouchable. A third set goes into the wall of the old customs house, behind the third-floor archive shelf, where I've kept things safe through two changes of government. And the originals—" she looked at me— "the originals go on your body, into the capital, into the assizes, tomorrow. Tonight. Whatever the boat schedule says."

"My father needs a doctor. Tomas needs a hospital."

"And both of those are arguments for scattering the copies, which we're doing. But the originals cannot wait for comfort. Hearings have calendars, and Meridian has lawyers whose entire profession is making calendars move." She fixed me with a look I'd seen on no one else—dry, ancient, absolutely without pity and absolutely without cruelty. "How much sleep did you get last night?"

"An hour. Maybe."

"Good. You'll need to remember how that feels, because you won't get more tonight, and tomorrow you'll have to be sharper than everyone in that courtroom who slept eight hours. Fear is fuel, Miss Vane. Burn it deliberately or it burns you."

* * *

The couriers left before dawn, one by one, into the rain.

I watched each departure from the boathouse door, and each one felt like lowering something precious into a river I couldn't see the bottom of. Father Anselm went first, a spare man with a bicycle and a prayer book, the notebook copies wrapped in oilcloth inside his saddlebag among sermons. Then the purser's boy, running the folio down to the *Cormorant*'s berth through streets still dark and streaming. Then a fishwife Delphine trusted, who carried a wax-sealed packet under a false bottom in her basket between the mackerel and the salt, because, as Delphine observed, no lawyer in history has ever thought to subpoena a fish basket.

Each time the door closed behind one of them, I stood listening to the rain and doing arithmetic of dread. *Three sets gone. If one is intercepted, two survive. If two are intercepted, one survives. If all three—* I stopped the arithmetic there, because it led nowhere except to the edge of the map, and Marguerite had taught me, without ever saying it plainly, that you don't stand at the edge of the map staring down. You turn around and take the next step.

Tomas slept again. My father woke once, briefly, confused by the lamplight and the smell of ink, and asked me if I was drawing maps. "In a way," I said, and he smiled at something I couldn't see, and slept again. I copied twelve more pages with wet eyes and dry ink, and Delphine pretended not to notice, which was its own kindness.

Somewhere in the smallest hours, my hands stopped shaking. I noticed it the way you notice a headache leaving. It wasn't bravery. It was repetition—fold, write, seal, wax, wrap, repeat—until the fear had somewhere to go besides my spine. Delphine had been right about that. Procedure wasn't the opposite of feeling. It was feeling, given a job.

Near four o'clock, she paused over the last notebook, the one with the photographs, and held a print up to the light: my father, younger, crouched beside a survey marker in the Grey Fork valley, grinning like a man who has just caught the land telling the truth.

"He looks like you," Delphine said.

"He looks like a man who doesn't know yet."

"They never do." She slid the photograph into its waxed envelope with exaggerated gentleness. "That's why we keep records. So the ones who come after can know for them."

* * *

At first light the rain thinned to a drifting mist, and the harbor came up out of the dark in pieces—mast tips, gull cries, the groan of hulls against pilings. The last boat downriver left at seven. We'd missed the decoy plan we'd built with Marguerite; there was no time for false departures now, no clever misdirection. There was just me, the originals sewn flat against my ribs in an oilcloth lining Delphine had stitched herself with a sailmaker's needle, and a long day of river between here and the capital.

Before I left, I went to the constabulary.

It was a low stone building smelling of wet wool and old tobacco, and they let me see him through a grate because Delphine had already been there, already signed whatever papers clerks can sign, already made herself unavoidable. Marguerite sat on a bunk in a cell the size of a large cupboard, and he looked—rested. That was the shocking thing. Five years of running had carved lines into his face, and one night behind a locked door had smoothed some of them, as if custody, for him, was a kind of shore.

"You're copying documents at four in the morning," he said, "and you look better than I've ever seen you."

"Fear converts well. Delphine taught me."

"She would." He came to the grate and put his hand flat against the bars, and I put mine against his on the other side, iron between our skin. "Three copies?"

"Three, plus the originals, plus one in a wall I'm not allowed to know about."

"Good girl." He winced at himself. "Don't— I'm not your father. Don't let me start sounding like him."

"You sound like a man in a cell trying to give orders he can't follow." I leaned my forehead near the bars, close enough to feel the cool iron breathe. "Delphine says testimony means you stay in custody through the hearing. Days, maybe a week."

"I know what it costs. I priced it five years ago."

"And I'm supposed to just walk onto that boat and leave you in a cell in a town where the constable drinks Meridian's coffee?"

"Yes." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than any argument. "Because if you stay, everything we spent last night building gets spent guarding me instead of reaching the court. Walk upstream of your feelings, Astrid. Get the documents there. That's the trail. Everything else is scenery."

I wanted to fight him. I catalogued the arguments the way he'd taught me to catalogue rapids—reading the water, finding the line—and every argument ended the same way: with him right, and both of us knowing it.

"When it's done," I said, "when the notebooks are authenticated and the hearing starts—you'll testify, and I'll be in the front row, and I will not look away once. Not even at the ugly parts."

"Especially not at the ugly parts," he agreed. His fingers curled through the grate until they touched my knuckles. "Astrid. The night I met you, you stole space on my boat with a lie about your fare. Best theft anyone ever committed against me. Go steal your father's valley back."

* * *

The *Kestrel* was waiting at the dock, patched and recaulked, still leaking the way old boats leak and old men limp—tolerably, characterfully, home. I cast off alone at ten past seven with the originals warm against my ribs and Delphine's final instructions folded in my pocket: *Registrar's office, west wing, ask for the duty clerk by name, show nothing in the street.*

Port Meridian fell away behind me in the mist—its company warehouses, its magistrate's fine house on the hill, its wanted notices curling on the notice boards with Marguerite's five-year-old face staring out, reward doubled, relevant for exactly one more day. Ahead, the river ran high and brown with monsoon water, carrying whole trees, carrying everything downstream toward the sea except us.

Halfway through the first bend, I heard the engine.

It came up fast behind me—a long, low-powered launch riding heavy in the water, the kind of boat Meridian's security used, the kind I'd heard idling outside the logging station while my father wasted in a shed. I didn't turn my head. I trimmed the Kestrel's course, checked the current, and did the arithmetic Marguerite had drilled into me without either of us naming it: their draft was deeper than mine, the channel here ran narrow over the old weir stones, and the flood water favored the light hull that knew the line.

The launch matched my speed for a quarter mile, hanging off my stern quarter like a decision not yet made. Then the channel narrowed, the water went glassy and treacherous over the submerged stones, and I took the Kestrel down the seam between the two deepest channels—the line Marguerite had walked me through a hundred miles ago, back when we were strangers negotiating passage and neither of us knew we were practicing for this.

Behind me, the launch hesitated, sheared off toward the wider channel, and fell astern.

I didn't exhale until the next bend hid me. Then I laughed once, high and shaky, alone on the brown flood with my father's evidence against my heart, and put the bow for the capital.

By nightfall, Delphine's copies would be moving along three separate roads, in a saddlebag, a folio, and a fish basket. By morning, the assizes would open, and somewhere in the cells beneath the courthouse, a man who had spent five years learning to be invisible would stand up in front of God and the region and make himself seen.

I counted the miles and, for the first time in my life, didn't mind them. They were taking me somewhere.
