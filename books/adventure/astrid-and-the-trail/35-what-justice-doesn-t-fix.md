## 35. What Justice Doesn't Fix

The rain had stopped sometime in the night, and the capital woke smelling of wet stone and diesel and the green rot of the river. I stood on the dock at the end of the fish market road with my hands in my pockets and watched the light come up over the water like something being forgiven.

I had not slept. None of us had, really—not properly, not since the verdict. Sleep was a country I'd been exiled from for five years, and one afternoon in a courtroom hadn't bought me a visa back.

The Kestrel sat where I'd left her, tied off at the third piling with the knot Astrid had taught me—the one that held fast when it was loaded and slipped free with a single pull, which she said was the only honest kind of knot. She was riding low at the stern where the patched hull still wept a little, a slow dark bead of water gathering in the bilge no matter how many times I bailed her. Still leaking. Still mine. There was a comfort in that I couldn't have explained to anyone who hadn't lost everything once already: things that leak are things that can be mended. Things that sink clean to the bottom are the ones you grieve.

*Marguerite Coldharbour, acquitted.* The words kept turning over in my head like river stones, smooth and strange. Not *cleared*, exactly—Delphine had been careful about that word, sitting across from me in her office of yellowed paper while the ink dried on the order. "The blacklist is vacated," she'd said, tapping the page. "The inquiry is reopened. Those are facts. Whether your name comes out of this clean depends on what they find when they dig up Karst Ridge again, and how long it takes them, and whether anyone important decides it's worth their career." She'd looked at me over her spectacles with the flat kindness of a woman who had spent forty years watching courts do arithmetic with human lives. "But as of nine o'clock yesterday morning, you are a free man, Mr. Coldharbour. Try to remember what that felt like."

The trouble was, I did remember. That was the problem.

* * *

Freedom, it turned out, tasted like nothing.

I'd imagined it so many times—in the holding cell, in the fever, in the long grey hours of the crossing when my hands wouldn't stop shaking—that I'd built it into something with weight and flavor. I'd thought it would come down on me like weather. Instead it was just an absence. No warrant behind my shoulder blade anymore, no itch between my shoulder blades where the wanted notice used to hang in every post office from here to the coast. My face was just a face now. Strangers looked through me the way strangers always had, and there was no second look, no flicker of recognition, no hand drifting toward a constable's whistle.

For five years I had been invisible by force. Now I was invisible by choice, and I discovered I didn't know how to be seen.

I walked the market road because walking was what I knew. The stalls were opening, tarps peeling back off crates of silver-skinned fish, and a woman argued cheerfully with a boy about the price of limes, and none of it required anything of me. That was the strangeness of it. For twenty-six hours in Port Meridian I had been a man with a purpose so sharp it cut everything else away: get the documents to the boat, keep Astrid breathing, keep breathing myself. Purpose is a wonderful anesthetic. Now the wound underneath had woken up, and it was the size of five years.

Five years. I stood at the rail where the fish market overlooked the water and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the gavel fell. Five years of safe trails and forgettable clients. Five years of counting miles instead of days because miles couldn't leave me. Five years of my mother's grave going unvisited because visiting it meant registering my name in a district where the notices hung. Five years of Tomas Reyes-Idowu—

Tomas. The thought arrived the way it always arrived now, without warning, like a snare underfoot.

He had tipped the odds at the sluice gates. He had appeared out of the monsoon dark like something the river had finally decided to give back, half-starved and limping, and he had put himself between us and Meridian's security chief with a surveyor's chain in his fist and a laugh I could still hear if I let myself. And then, in the chaos of water and generator smoke and Elias Vane being dragged through the flood, Tomas had gone under—or gone back, or gone wherever men like him went when they had spent too long being hunted to trust rescue—and we had come downriver without him.

There had been no word. Delphine had made inquiries through channels that didn't officially exist. The logging station's surviving guards weren't talking, or weren't alive to talk. Every morning I asked, and every morning the answer was the same shape: nothing.

A man can survive losing five years. What I didn't know—what I was standing on the dock learning, the way you learn the depth of a pool by falling into it—was whether I could survive having cost another man his.

* * *

The valley was still poisoned. That was the other thing the verdict hadn't fixed.

I'd read the newspapers that morning over bad coffee at the mission—real newspapers, brought up from the coast, the first ones in years I could read without checking over my shoulder. Warrants issued for three Meridian officers. The magistrate recused, his signature on the forged surveys splashed across the front page in facsimile, that elegant looping hand I'd last seen on the boundary maps that had ruined Tomas. Shares down forty percent. *Scandal at Coldwater: The Valley That Was Sold Twice.*

And lower down, past the fold, in smaller type: preliminary assessment of tailings contamination in the Grey Fork watershed. Elevated arsenic. Restricted water use advised for eleven settlements.

Eleven settlements. Children drinking from streams that had been quietly killing them for a decade while men in the capital signed each other's names. The warrants would put officers in a courtroom. They would not put clean water in the Grey Fork. Justice, I was beginning to understand, was a thing that happened to papers. The valley would go on bleeding into its own rivers long after the lawyers had billed their last hour, and no verdict anywhere was going to unpoison a single meter of that ground.

I had spent five years believing that if I could only get my name back, everything else would follow. Standing there with the name restored and the paper in my pocket saying so, I felt like a man who'd clawed his way to the top of a ridge expecting a view and found only more ridge.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Astrid said behind me, "where you stand very still and count things."

I hadn't heard her come down the market road. That should have worried me—it would have worried me, two weeks ago—but with her it just felt like the world working correctly. She came to stand at the rail beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, wearing Elias's old canvas jacket because hers had never dried out from the cave, and she smelled of woodsmoke and river and the mission's carbolic soap.

"What things am I counting?" I asked.

"Miles. Years. Ways the world has wronged you." She said it gently. Everything she said now was gentle in a way that had been earned, that had been paid for in flooded rivers and fever nights and a knife-fight in a fish market. "You get a particular line between your eyebrows."

"And what does this particular line say?"

She studied my face with those grey eyes that read contour lines the way other people read sentences. "It says you won something and you don't know where to put it down."

I turned back to the water. The Kestrel knocked softly against the piling, patient as a dog. "My mother used to say that about my father. That he carried grudges like firewood. Always ready for a cold night that never came."

"Was she right?"

"She was usually right. It was infuriating." I looked down at my hands. They were steady now; the fever had burned whatever tremor had lived in them clean out, or else Astrid's voice on the river for two days had talked it loose. "I keep waiting to feel different. Free. It's supposed to feel like something."

"It feels like nothing," Astrid said. "That's the trick of it. Freedom isn't a feeling. It's just the absence of the thing that was crushing you. You notice it the way you notice a headache stopping." She shrugged inside the big jacket. "Elias is the same. Three days out of that logging station and he keeps touching doorframes. Checking they're real. He told me last night he dreamed he was back in the compound and woke up trying to dig through the wall with his hands." Her jaw tightened, and I saw the grief move through her and be set aside, deliberately, like a pack set down at camp. "It takes longer than a day to walk out of a place like that. Even after you're out."

"Tomas walked out of places like that for six years," I said. "Alone."

"I know."

"He covered our retreat. He let them take him rather than let them take us, and then he got free anyway, and the one time he chose to come back—" My throat closed. I made myself finish it, because Astrid had taught me that much: you finish the sentence or it owns you. "The one time he came back for someone instead of running, we lost him."

"We didn't lose him," Astrid said. "We don't know. There's a difference, and you of all people should know the difference, because for five years everyone decided you were dead in every way that mattered and here you are, leaking and stubborn, tied up at the third piling."

I huffed something that was almost a laugh. Down the dock, a gull screamed at another gull about a fish head, and the ordinary violence of birds went on.

"Delphine says the security chief's trial will need him," I said. "Whatever testimony he gave about the snare lines, about the camp—if he surfaced in the capital, if he talked to anyone, there'll be a record. She's pulling threads."

"Then we pull threads too. When you're stronger."

"When I'm stronger," I agreed, though we both knew strength wasn't the missing ingredient. Patience was. And patience, for me, had always been the hardest mile.

* * *

We stood there a while without talking, which was its own kind of conversation now. That was the thing I kept failing to find words for, the thing the whole ordeal had left in me like a splinter working toward the surface: somewhere between the rapids and the cave and the fever, Astrid Vane had become the fixed point my life swung around, and I had no idea when it had happened or what I was supposed to do about it. In the cave we had admitted things quietly, almost accidentally, the way you admit the rain is coming by moving your gear under cover. Neither of us had said the actual word. The word seemed too small for it anyway, the way *river* is too small a word for what the Coldwater becomes in monsoon.

"The Kestrel needs recaulking," Astrid said eventually. "Properly, not your emergency-pitch-and-prayer method."

"My method kept us afloat through the First Rapids."

"Your method kept us *mostly* afloat through the First Rapids, and I lost a case of radio batteries to the river because of it." She bumped her shoulder against mine, deliberate this time. "Come on. Let's look at her. You've been staring at her from ten feet away all morning like she's going to bite."

So we walked down to the third piling and I stepped aboard and she followed, and the familiar give of the deck under my boots did more for me than the verdict had. I opened the bilge and found the water sitting exactly where it always sat, two fingers deep, dark and patient. I got the bail bucket going out of pure habit.

"Still leaking," I said.

"Still yours," Astrid said. She crouched at the seam near the stern where the patch showed, ran her thumb along it the way her father must have run his thumb along a thousand field samples. "You know what I keep thinking about? The night before the claim site. When we fought about pushing on ahead of the storm. You told me I was using hope as a weapon against myself." She looked up at me. "You were right. But you were also wrong, because sometimes the hope is correct. Sometimes you push on through the dark and the thing you're hoping for is actually there. Elias was alive. The notebooks were real. We bet everything on a trail neither of us could see the far end of, and it held."

"Barely."

"Trails barely hold. That's what makes them trails instead of roads." She stood, brushed off her knees. "So what now? You're free. You can go anywhere. Take a guide's license in some country that's never heard of Meridian Consolidated. Ferry tourists up forgettable rivers until your knees go."

The words landed in the hollow place and rattled around. Because that had been the plan, hadn't it? That had always been the plan: stay small, stay safe, stay forgettable. And I opened my mouth to say *yes, something like that,* and found I couldn't, because the picture wouldn't form. Every river I tried to imagine was the Grey Fork. Every client was a settlement kid drinking arsenic. Every map was the one with the forged boundaries, waiting for someone honest to redraw it.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the most honest sentence I'd spoken in five years. "I know what I'm free *from*. I haven't figured out what I'm free *for*. It's like getting out of prison and discovering the outside doesn't come with instructions."

"Neither does a trail," Astrid said. "You just pick a direction and start walking, and the way reveals itself one mistake at a time."

Before I could answer, footsteps on the dock—a runner from the mission, one of the older boys, holding an envelope out of the drizzle-mist coming off the water. "Message for Miss Vane," he said. "From her father. He said it couldn't wait, and he said—" the boy squinted at the note he'd memorized— "he said to tell you both, because it concerns you both."

Astrid took the envelope. Broke the seal with her thumb. Read it once quickly, and then again slowly, and I watched her face do something complicated—hope and dread arriving together, like weather from opposite fronts.

"Elias?" I asked.

"He wants us at the hospital at four o'clock." She handed me the letter, and I read it in his precise surveyor's hand, the letters of a man who had learned to write legibly in mud and rain:

*Astrid — I've had an approach. Through a lawyer claiming to represent "interested parties in the family holdings." They know about the verdict. They know about the notebooks. And they are offering, in writing, terms for the transfer of everything my father built — the legitimate holdings, the good land, the original grants — to be signed over to you. Clean title, they say. A fresh start. All it requires is a meeting, and a signature, and perhaps a certain discretion about what goes into the reopened inquiry.*

*I raised you to read contour lines, my girl. So read this one carefully: whenever a map shows level ground where the terrain says there should be a cliff — someone drew it that way on purpose.*

*Four o'clock. Bring Marguerite. I want a second pair of eyes on the terrain.*

I lowered the letter. The gulls were still screaming. The Kestrel leaked, faithful and patient, two fingers of dark water rising in her bilge like a slow tide.

"They're not done," Astrid said quietly. "Meridian falls, and something crawls out of the wreckage wearing our name."

I folded the letter along its crease and looked upriver, where the water came down out of the high grey country, out of the valley, out of everything that was still unresolved.

"No," I said. "They're not done. And neither are we."
