## 37. News of Tomas

The letter came on a morning that smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, the kind of morning the valley made after rain—everything rinsed, everything glistening, as if the world had been taken down and hung out to dry. I was on the mission's back steps with a cup of tea going cold in my hands when the boy arrived from the capital road, mud to his knees, a canvas satchel slung across his chest. He didn't knock so much as arrive, the way boys do, and he held out an envelope with both hands like it might bite him.

"It's marked urgent," he said. "Came up with the mail boat yesterday, but nobody could read the address."

Nobody could read it because it was written in my hand—or a version of it, five years older and shakier, addressed to a name I hadn't used since Karst Ridge. Delphine Okafor had done that deliberately, I realized later. She'd addressed it to the man on the old warrant, knowing full well that only one person in this valley would recognize the handwriting and know where to find him.

I turned the envelope over twice before I opened it. Some part of me had learned, over five years of being hunted by paper, that envelopes were where bad news lived. Warrants. Summons. The cold formal language of institutions deciding what you were worth.

But the paper inside was Delphine's, and Delphine wrote the way she spoke: plainly, without ornament, each sentence set down like a stone on a cairn.

*Marguerite.*

*He came in on Tuesday. Walked into the assize registrar's office barefoot, half-starved, with rope burns on both wrists and a snare wire still knotted around his left ankle where they'd kept him hobbled. He asked for me by name. He'd remembered it from thirty years ago, from a boundary dispute in the eastern ranges that I clerked before either of us had gray hair.*

*He testified for six hours. The snare lines—he drew them from memory, every trap position along the ridge above Grey Fork, matched them against the survey photographs. The burned camp—he described the accelerant drums, the order the buildings went up, who gave which command. He named the security chief to his face across a courtroom, in front of Meridian's lawyers, and did not look away once.*

*The chief has been convicted. Twenty years. Two of his men have turned crown's evidence rather than follow him down. The magistrate's accounts are under audit as of Thursday. It is not everything. It is not even most things. But it is the load-bearing wall, and it is standing.*

*Tomas is at the mission hospital in the capital. He will not say how he got free, except to laugh and say the river did what rivers do. He asks after you. He says—and these are his words, I wrote them down because I thought you would want them exact—"Tell Coldharbour the debt runs the other way now. Tell him to stop carrying mine."*

*Come down when you can. The inquiry reopens formally next month. There are names on the witness list you should see before you see them in a courtroom.*

*—D.O.*

I read it three times. The tea went fully cold. Somewhere behind me, inside the mission, Astrid was arguing cheerfully with the cook about whether the stove could be coaxed into baking bread or merely torturing dough, and her voice came through the window in fragments, warm and ordinary, a sound I was still learning to trust.

I sat on the steps for a long while.

* * *

You have to understand what Tomas Reyes-Idowu had been to me before any of this—not much, and then everything. A stranger in a hut above Grey Fork who laughed at my name like it was a bad joke we were both stuck in. A man ruined by the same company, hollowed out by the same machinery, living off snares and rainwater and a bitterness so old it had fossilized into something almost like peace. He'd fed us. He'd drawn us maps. And when the riders came over the ridge, he'd stood in the open with a rifle he barely maintained and covered our retreat, and I had run, because running was the plan, because staying meant all three of us caught instead of two, because every cold arithmetic I'd ever learned said go.

I had carried him for weeks. Not the way you carry grief for the dead—that at least has a shape, a grave, an ending. This was worse. This was carrying a man who might be alive, might be in a hole somewhere, might be dead in an unmarked grave on a company lease, and not knowing which. Every night at the fire I'd found myself doing it again: replaying the moment the treeline swallowed us, the last glimpse of him standing small and stubborn against the riders, the crack of a shot I couldn't attribute, the silence after. I'd built him a coffin in my head a dozen times and then torn it apart a dozen times because there was no body, no proof, nothing to bury and nothing to stop hoping for, and hope in that form is just a wound that refuses to close.

Astrid found me on the steps eventually. She came out wiping flour on her trousers, saw my face, and sat down without asking anything. That was one of the thousand things I loved about her and had not yet said in words—the way she could read a silence the way she read her father's contour maps, tracing the steep places without needing them labeled.

"Well?" she said finally.

I handed her the letter. She read it faster than I had, then slower, then she read the last lines aloud, quietly, the way you handle something fragile.

"*The debt runs the other way now.*" She looked up. "He's alive, Marguerite. Barefoot and half-starved and rope-scarred, but alive, and he walked into a registrar's office and took Meridian's security chief apart in front of God and everybody."

"He shouldn't have had to."

"No," she agreed. "He shouldn't. Neither should you. Neither should Elias." She folded the letter along its creases, careful, deliberate. "But he did, and he did it, and the man who ordered the burning of that camp is going to spend twenty years thinking about it. That's not nothing. Don't you dare make it nothing."

"I'm not making it nothing. I'm making it—" I stopped. Looked out at the valley, at the terraced slopes going green and rank with post-monsoon growth, at the river running high and brown and honest below us. "I spent five years learning how to disappear. How to be a name nobody says. And the whole time, the people who actually paid for my disappearing were men like Tomas. Men who got erased quieter than I did, with less fuss, no warrant, no notice in the papers. Just a license revoked and a life closed like a door." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "He covered us. He knew what it would cost. He'd already lost everything once and he stood up anyway, and I ran."

"You ran because the plan was to run," Astrid said. "And then you came back. You crossed a flooded river with your hands shaking so hard you could barely hold the rope. You surrendered to constables in a boathouse with your palms out like a man showing he's unarmed. You sat in a cell for eleven days so those notebooks could reach a courtroom." She put her hand flat on my knee, a guide's gesture, steadying. "Tomas doesn't need your guilt. Guilt is heavy and it doesn't pack well. What he needs is for you to show up and hear him tell it, and let him have the satisfaction of watching you listen."

"When did you get wise?"

"Genetic," she said. "My father lectured me about contour lines for twenty years. Everything looks flat until you learn to read the intervals."

* * *

We went down to the capital four days later, on the mail boat, riding low with supplies for the mission hospital. The river had dropped since the monsoon broke; the Kestrel could have made the run, but her hull was opened up on blocks behind the mission, ribs exposed, waiting for caulking and varnish and the long argument about color that Astrid and I had been conducting with increasing pleasure. So we rode someone else's boat, which felt strange, like wearing another man's boots.

The capital in late monsoon-season was a city of dripping eaves and steaming streets, of lawyers' clerks hurrying with oilcloth-wrapped bundles, of the assizes building with its scaffolding half-struck and its windows thrown open to dry out the term's damp. We heard the verdict repeated in fragments everywhere—in the market, in the ferry queue, in the mission ward where Tomas lay. *Twenty years. The Vane notebooks. The surveyor who crawled out of the bush.* Meridian's name, spoken without the hushed deference it had commanded for forty years. Empires don't fall in a day, but you can hear the first stones shift if you're listening.

They had him in a corner cot by the window, propped up on pillows, one ankle bandaged where the snare wire had bitten through to bone. He was thinner than when we'd met him—there hadn't been much of him to lose—but his eyes were the same: quick, ironic, lit from underneath by a humor that had survived everything the world had done to it.

"Coldharbour," he said, before I'd cleared the door. "They told me you'd come. I told them you'd come slow, because you're a man who counts distances before he counts reasons." He grinned at Astrid. "And you must be the daughter. Your father talked about you for two hours in that logging station. Best two hours of a very bad month. He described your walk. Said you walk like you're arguing with the ground and winning."

"That sounds like him," Astrid said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and Tomas reached out and patted her wrist as if this were the most natural thing in the world.

I pulled up the stool by the window. For a while none of us said anything. Outside, rain dripped from the eaves in a slow irregular rhythm, and a cart went by, and a dog barked at it, and the ordinary world went on doing its ordinary work, and I understood suddenly that this was what Tomas had crawled out of the bush to buy back—a place in the ordinary world, a bed by a window, people who knew his name and used it kindly.

"The snare lines," I said finally. "Delphine said you drew them from memory."

"Every one." He flexed the bandaged foot experimentally, winced, seemed satisfied. "Forty-one traps between Grey Fork and the ridge line. I set them over nine years. You get to know a country through its snares better than through its trails—the animals tell you where the game paths run, and the game paths don't lie the way roads do." His grin faded into something harder. "When the riders took me, they marched me past my own snares. Their sergeant had a map of them. Meridian's map. They'd surveyed my traps the way they survey everything—to take it, catalog it, use it. So yes. I remembered every one. And I put them on the record, one by one, and watched the security chief's face while I did it."

"What did it look like?" Astrid asked.

"Like a man realizing the ground was shifting under him," Tomas said. "Like a man who'd spent his whole career believing the mountain belonged to whoever held the paper, finding out the mountain keeps its own ledger." He looked at me then, directly, and the irony drained out of his eyes and left something older plainer. "Coldharbour. Listen to me. In that hut above Grey Fork, when I covered your retreat—I want you to understand I wasn't sacrificing myself. I was finishing something. Nine years I'd sat up there telling myself the record didn't matter anymore, that the world had made its decision about men like us and the only dignity left was to stay out of its sight. Then you two came down that trail carrying Elias's notebooks, and I watched you do the thing I'd decided was impossible, and I thought: well. If they can walk toward it, I can stand in front of it for ten minutes." He shrugged, thin shoulders moving under the blanket. "Ten minutes was all it cost me. The rest was the river, and the river owes Meridian more than it owes me."

"Tomas—"

"Don't." He raised a hand, gentle but absolute. "I know what you're carrying. I've carried it myself—the arithmetic, the *if only*, the whole ledger of a man who thinks debts are his to manage. But here's what thirty years of surveying taught me, and it's the one thing I know for certain: you cannot square a boundary by walking it alone. Somebody else always has to hold the other end of the chain." He nodded at Astrid, at the window, at the city beyond with its shifting stones. "You held your end. Delphine held hers. The girl here held hers. I held mine. The line came out true. That's all any of us gets to do, and it's enough, and if you keep trying to pay me back you'll insult both of us, because what happened out there wasn't a transaction. It was a survey. And the line is true."

I looked out the window for a long time. The rain had stopped; light was coming through the clouds in bars, laying itself across the rooftops, and somewhere below a church bell was counting the hour with the patient indifference of bells.

"All right," I said at last. It came out hoarse. I tried again. "All right. No debt. A survey."

"A survey," Tomas agreed, satisfied. Then the grin came back, wicked as ever. "Now sit down properly and tell me about the sluice gates. The nurses won't let me drink, so I require entertainment, and I've been told you flooded a compound yard using a monsoon and a wrench."

* * *

We stayed until visiting hours ended. Astrid sat on the edge of his bed and told the story of the rescue with her hands, sketching the generator shed and the gate house in the air, and Tomas interrupted constantly with corrections and questions—*how deep was the water in the yard, what gauge was the sluice mechanism, did you really cut the fuel line with a fish knife*—until it became less a story than a joint reconstruction, three ruined professionals rebuilding a flood out of memory and expertise, laughing in the wrong places.

At the door, leaving, I stopped. Tomas was looking out the window again, at the wet street, at the ordinary evening assembling itself below.

"They'll reopen the inquiry next month," I said. "Karst Ridge. Delphine thinks they'll vacate the finding entirely. My license comes back after that."

"I heard." He didn't turn around. "What will you do with it?"

"There's a survey crew forming. First honest one into that valley in forty years. Astrid's co-leading it." I hesitated. "There's room on the roster. When your leg heals. If you wanted—"

He was quiet long enough that I thought I'd overstepped, that some wounds don't want the country they were made in offered back to them. Then he said, still facing the window, "I'd want to start at Grey Fork. Re-run the boundaries from the original monuments. Every line. All of it, in daylight, on the record." A pause. "Yes, Coldharbour. I'll hold the other end of the chain."

In the corridor, Astrid slipped her arm through mine, and we walked out into the washed evening light without speaking, because some things don't need narrating while they're happening.

That night, at the mission guesthouse, I took out my notebook—the small waterproof one I'd carried for five years, the one where I counted miles—and I turned to the page where, without quite admitting it to myself, I had started keeping a different list. Not distances. Names. People I owed, people who'd owed me, the whole human ledger of the last season. I found Tomas's entry, where for weeks I'd written and rewritten the same unresolved phrase—*taken, unknown, unpaid*—and beneath it, in the margin, I wrote the date, and beside the date, one word:

*Honored.*

Not repaid. Repayment was a fiction; Tomas was right about that. Some debts change shape the moment you try to settle them, and the only honest currency left is to live in a way that justifies what it cost. To hold the chain. To walk the line in daylight.

I closed the notebook and set it on the sill, where the last of the light could reach it, and downstairs Astrid was asking the landlady about boats—schedules, drafts, departure times—and her voice rose up the stairwell bright and purposeful, and I realized she was already planning.

She appeared in the doorway with a timetable in her hand and her eyes alight. "The mail boat goes upriver Thursday," she said. "But Marguerite—there's a launch tomorrow, chartered by the inquiry office, taking the investigating magistrates up to view the claim site and the burned camp. Official business. Witness transport." She held up the paper. "They need guides who know the water."

I looked at her, and at the timetable, and at the notebook on the sill with its new word drying in the lamplight.

"You've already spoken to them," I said.

"I may have mentioned," Astrid said, "that the two best river pilots in this valley were currently sitting in a guesthouse with nothing but time and a grudge against bad surveys." Her mouth curved. "They want an answer by morning."

Outside, the clouds were breaking apart over the capital, and the first stars were coming through, and somewhere upriver the valley waited with its forty years of false lines, ready to be measured honestly at last.

"Then we'd better sleep," I said. "It's a long way to Grey Fork."
