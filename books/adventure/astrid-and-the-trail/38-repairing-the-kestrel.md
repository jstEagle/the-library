## 38. Repairing the Kestrel

The dry dock at Port Meridian smelled of tar and river rot, of hot planking and the sweet green breath of the reeds along the bank. Marguerite had rented the slip from a boatwright named Ferro who asked no questions, which was itself a kind of luxury these days. The whole town was still talking about the assizes, about Meridian's officers in irons, about the surveyor who'd walked out of a courtroom a free man after five years of being a rumor with a face. Ferro had looked at him exactly once, spat into the sawdust, and said, *Slip three's yours. Bring her back clean.*

The Kestrel sat on the cradle like a patient mid-surgery, hull bared to the light for the first time since Marguerite had bought her off a debt-ridden trader seven years ago. He ran his palm along the starboard strake where the rapids had opened her up, feeling the old scar of his patching — epoxy gone amber, canvas gone brittle, a repair made in fear on a gravel bar with rain coming down. It had held. Most things he'd done badly had held, somehow. That was either grace or luck, and he'd stopped believing in luck around the time they took his license.

"You're going to sand that before you caulk it," Astrid said, "or I'm going to sand it while you sleep."

She came down the ramp carrying two mugs of coffee that was more chicory than bean, her hair tied back with a strip of red cloth she'd torn from a map case weeks ago and never replaced. She'd been up since dawn at Delphine Okafor's office, copying deposition pages, but she moved like someone whose body had finally agreed to be where she was.

"It'll hold," Marguerite said.

"It held *once*. There's a difference between holding and holding." She set the mugs on an upturned crate, took the scraper out of his hand, and set to work on the seam with the brisk violence of someone raised by a man who believed maintenance was a moral discipline. "My father used to say a boat is just a promise you keep renewing. Every season you renew it or you lie."

"Elias sounds like a man who never let anyone else do the lying for him."

"He tried to teach me. I was fourteen and furious about something — I don't even remember what anymore — and I told him I didn't care if the boat sank." She blew shavings off the seam. "He didn't get angry. He just said, *Then you'll swim, and swimming is also a skill, but it's a colder one.*"

Marguerite laughed, and the laugh surprised him, coming up rough from somewhere unused. Five years of guiding tourists on forgettable trails had worn his voice down to instructions: *watch your footing, hydrate, we turn back at the cairn.* He hadn't laughed at anything in longer than he could measure.

They worked through the morning. He taught her to drive caulking cotton with the iron and mallet — *not too deep, you're not punishing it* — and she turned out to have the kind of hands that learned fast and argued faster. By noon the port side gleamed with fresh oakum, white as teeth, and the smell of pine tar hung over the slip like weather.

It was when they got to the question of varnish that the war began.

"Spar varnish," Astrid said. "Amber. Seven coats."

"Six coats," Marguerite said. "Seven is vanity. Six is what the wood can drink."

"Seven is what the *river* can't take back. You want to be recaulking this in October?"

"I want to be *on* this in October, not looking at it."

"That," she said, dipping her brush, "is the first honest thing you've said all week." She laid the first coat anyway, long and even, and the amber went down over the greyed planking like light going into water, and Marguerite watched it and felt something in his chest unclench that he hadn't known was clenched.

* * *

They broke for food in the shade of Ferro's tarpaulin awning — dried fish, hard bread, mangoes bought from a woman at the market who refused Astrid's money because, she said, *you're the one who brought the magistrate down.* Fame, Marguerite was learning, was mostly people giving you fruit.

Astrid ate her mango with a knife, cutting precise chevrons, and watched him work a whetstone along the blade he'd taken the knife-fighter's slash with. The wound across his ribs had closed to a pink seam. In the mornings it ached; in the evenings it forgot itself. He thought that was probably how most wounds worked, given enough seasons.

"Can I ask you something," she said, "that isn't about varnish?"

"You can ask. I've gotten worse at refusing than I used to be."

"The miles." She nodded at him. "Tomas told me once — he said, *that man counts everything, distances, days, clients, losses, he keeps a ledger in his head and balances it nightly.* Was it always like that? Even before Karst Ridge?"

Marguerite turned the blade in the light. Somewhere downriver a barge horn sounded, low and patient, and the reeds along the bank bowed toward it and straightened.

"I started counting the year my marriage ended," he said. "Before that I counted other things — summits, clients, money I owed my sister. But after—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "After, counting was the only thing that stayed put. People leave. Weather leaves. Licenses leave. A mile is twelve hours ago whether you want it to be or not. Miles couldn't leave me. So I measured everything in them. How far to the next camp. How many klicks since the last bad news. How long since I'd spoken to anyone who knew my name and meant it kindly." He glanced at her. "You want the honest arithmetic? The day I met you at the dock, I'd gone four hundred and eleven days without that last one."

Astrid set down the mango skin. She didn't reach for him — she'd learned that about him, that he needed the space around a confession to stay open, that reaching too soon closed it like a hand over a bird — but she turned on the crate so her knee rested against his, warm and deliberate.

"And now?" she asked.

"Now I keep losing count." He almost smiled. "I was three hundred meters into the valley hike and I realized I didn't know what day it was. Just that the light was right for moving. It terrified me for about a minute. Then it didn't."

"Good," she said. "Because miles are a coward's calendar. They only go backward — you're always measuring from something behind you." She picked up the whetstone, took his hand, and drew it flat across her own knee, working the stone along the blade herself, slow strokes, teaching him by touch the way he'd taught her the caulking iron. "Measure in seasons instead. A season is forward-looking. You plant in one so you can eat in another. My father used to say the difference between a tourist and a traveler is that a tourist measures distance and a traveler measures time — what grows, what melts, what comes back."

"What comes back," Marguerite repeated. The words sat strangely in his mouth, half hope, half suspicion, like a coin he wasn't sure wasn't clipped.

"River levels. Birds. Rain." She looked up at him, and the afternoon light through the tarpaulin striped her face gold and shadow. "Me. If you'll have it that way."

He covered her hand with his, stilled the stone. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the slip creaked around them, and the Kestrel stood propped and open-sided between them like a heart on a table, seams showing, waiting to be made watertight again.

"I don't know how to do it slow," he said finally. "I know how to do it desperate. Desperate I'm excellent at."

"Then we'll start with desperate and sand it down." She resumed the stroke, once, twice. "That's all craftsmanship is, Marguerite. Doing the desperate thing carefully until it isn't desperate anymore."

* * *

They worked three more days, and the days had a shape he began to trust.

Mornings were hers: she rose first, made the chicory coffee, read Elias's letters — the old man was convalescing at the mission upriver, complaining magnificently about the food and secretly delighted by the nurses — and read them aloud in fragments, so that Marguerite was learning Elias Vane the way you learn a country, one border at a time. Afternoons were the hull. Evenings they walked the levee past the fish market where he'd bled, past the constable's office where the new warrants were posted, and the town tipped its hat to them both, and Marguerite was learning to be seen without flinching, though some mornings the flinch arrived anyway, punctual as fever.

On the fourth day they flipped the Kestrel's remaining sections and found the thing neither of them wanted to find: rot. Not dramatic, not sudden — just a softening in the garboard strake near the stern, the wood gone punky and dark, drinking thumbprints instead of shedding them. River damage, years of it, hidden under paint.

"We cut it out," Astrid said, already reaching for the saw.

"We scarf in a new section. That's a full day's work, maybe two."

"So we take two days." She said it simply, the way she now said most things that would once have been fights. Somewhere between the cave and the courtroom they had stopped treating disagreement as threat. "Ferro has stock. We patch it right or we don't cross the delta in spring."

He knelt and pressed his thumb into the rotten wood and thought about how much of himself matched it — damage hidden under paint, sound-looking, drinking pressure instead of shedding it. Five years of guiding safe trails. Four hundred and eleven days of silence. A brother-in-law's name he still couldn't say aloud without his hands going cold, though he'd said the rest of it, the rope, the anchors, all of it, to her and to a courtroom and to a judge, and the world had not ended. The world had, in fact, improved slightly.

"Astrid." He kept his eyes on the wood. "When spring comes. The survey crew. The valley. If it's hard — if it goes sideways the way things have gone sideways for everyone who loves me—"

"Then we measure it in seasons," she said, "and we fix the rot instead of painting over it." She sat back on her heels beside him, shoulder against his shoulder, both of them framed in the Kestrel's open ribs. "You're doing the thing again. Where you try to give me an exit before I've asked for a door."

"I'm doing the thing where I'm honest."

"You're doing the thing where you're scared honestly. It's different." She took his hand and pressed it flat against the good wood above the rot, cool and tight-grained, thirty years of rings. "Feel that. That's what a repaired thing feels like from the inside. Not new. *Denser.* All those seasons the tree stood through are still in there. That's us. I'm not asking you to be new, Marguerite. I'm asking you to be dense."

He laughed again — that rough, surprised sound — and leaned his head against hers, and above them the tarpaulin snapped in the river wind like a sail remembering its purpose.

* * *

They finished the scarf joint at dusk on the sixth day. New oakum, six coats of spar varnish — he conceded the seventh for the transom only, because she'd earned it and because the transom took the worst of the spray — and when Ferro came by with his lantern to inspect, he walked the length of her, thumped the hull twice with his knuckles, listened, and grunted the grunt of a man withholding praise as a professional courtesy.

"She'll float," he said. "Whether she should is between you and the river."

"That's all any of us are," Astrid said, and Ferro snorted and left them the lantern.

That night they sat in the slip with their backs against the Kestrel's hull, sharing the last warm bottle of beer between them, watching the river run silver-black under the rising moon. Upstream, faint in the dark, the mission's lamp burned where Elias slept. Downstream, the capital, the courts, the reopened inquiry, all the machinery of justice grinding slowly through its wet season. And beyond that, up the Coldwater, the valley itself — scarred, poisoned in places, but mapped now by honest hands, waiting for spring crews and honest surveys and whatever grew back.

"First of the season," Astrid said suddenly.

"First what?"

"Seasons. You're supposed to be counting them now. This is one. The repair season." She tapped the hull beside her. "Next will be the launch. Then the survey. Then whatever the monsoon brings, and then the dry season after that, and then—" She shrugged, and he felt the shrug travel through her shoulder into his. "It doesn't end. That's the point. Miles end. Seasons come back around."

Marguerite looked at the river, at the far bank dissolving into dark, at the water that had nearly killed them both and carried them here anyway, and he found that the ledger in his head had gone quiet. No distances outstanding. No debts accruing interest. Just the present, dense as good timber, holding.

"Mornings," he said. "I've decided I'm counting mornings too. Separately. It's not in your system, I know, but—"

"It's a subcategory of season," Astrid ruled. "Approved. Motion carries." She drained the beer and set the bottle down and settled against him, her weight easy, certain, the weight of someone who had decided where she stood and intended to stand there through weather.

He was nearly asleep when she spoke again, quiet, half to herself, the way people say the truest things.

"Ferro's boy came by while you were at the chandlery. Said there's a man asking after us at the dock office. Been there two days. Won't give a name." Her voice was even, but her hand had found his in the dark and gripped it. "Said he's got a scar on his cheek and walks like his knees are shot, and he keeps asking — these were his words — *whether the guide and the girl ever made it back down.*"

Marguerite opened his eyes. Above them the stars wheeled slowly over the black spine of the ridge, and the river ran on, patient, keeping everything, giving back what it chose.

"Tomas," he said.

"Or someone using Tomas to get close." She squeezed his hand. "Morning's in nine hours. Do we sleep, or do we go find out?"

Marguerite looked at the Kestrel, patched and gleaming faintly in the moonlight, six coats deep in the future, and made the first decision of the season that cost him nothing at all.

"We sleep," he said. "Whatever's waiting at that dock has waited two days. It can wait till the light's honest. But we go at first light — together."

And for the first time in five years, Marguerite Coldharbour fell asleep counting something other than miles: one morning, coming sure as the river, and two names to walk into it.
