## 39. The Trail Upstream

The license arrived on a Tuesday, in an envelope soft with handling, as though it had been passed from desk to desk and read by everyone before it reached me. The paper inside was heavier than I expected. Official stock, watermarked, the kind of thing that survives rain. My name printed at the top: *Marguerite Coldharbour, Licensed River Guide and Survey Assistant, Class One.* Below it, the seal of the regional survey authority, stamped fresh over the place where the old one had been struck through five years ago.

I sat on the dock at Port Meridian for a long time with that envelope open on my knees. The Kestrel was tied beside me, riding high and light because we'd stripped her for the winter, and the river ran brown-green with snowmelt, fast and loud, the way it always did in the first weeks of spring when the high country let go of its ice all at once.

Five years I had been a man without a name worth printing. Now here it was again, in ink, with a seal to hold it down.

Astrid found me there. She had a way of arriving without sound even on those groaning planks—some inheritance from her father, the habit of moving like someone reading the ground under her feet. She sat down without asking, which by then was her way of asking, and looked at the license upside down.

"Well," she said.

"Well."

"Does it feel different?"

I turned the paper over. The back was blank. "It feels like paper."

"It's not paper." She took it from me gently, the way you take something fragile from someone who doesn't yet believe it's real. "It's a door. You've been standing in front of it your whole life; somebody just finally unlocked it."

She was wearing her field coat, the one with the notebooks sewn into the lining long since removed—the originals were in Delphine Okafor's custody now, part of the public record, part of history—but she still carried herself like a woman with documents against her ribs. She'd been up since before dawn, I knew, at the mission hall where Elias was convalescing, going over maps. She was always going over maps. It was how she prayed.

"The crew list is final," she said. "Tomas confirmed for the upper section—he wants the snare country, says he knows every trap line between Grey Fork and the divide better than anyone alive, which is true, because he set half of them. Delphine's nephew is coming as clerk. Two of the mission men for labor. And me."

"I saw the roster."

"You signed off on me as co-lead."

"I did."

"Say why."

I looked at the river instead of at her. That was cowardice dressed as concentration, and she knew it, and waited me out with the patience of someone who had once talked a frozen man across a rope bridge hand over hand, one word at a time, while the water tried to take us both.

"Because you read contour lines the way your father taught you," I said finally, "which is better than anyone I've ever hired. Because you kept walking when I couldn't. Because the valley will trust you faster than it will ever trust me—I'm the man who came back with warrants, you're the man's daughter who came back with truth. And because—" I stopped. Started again. "Because I don't want to do this without you. Not the survey. Not anything after it either, if there is an anything after it. Which I'm told there might be, if I ask properly."

The dock boards creaked. A heron lifted off the shallows downstream, unhurried, offended by nothing.

"That wasn't proper," Astrid said.

"No."

"But it'll do." She folded my license along its crease and put it in my breast pocket herself, pressing it flat with her palm, over the heart, like a field dressing. "For the record, Coldharbour: I signed on before you asked. I've had the co-lead signature since Thursday. You're the last one to know everything, as usual."

* * *

We spent nine days fitting out.

Nine days of caulking and varnish and argument, which is to say nine days of being happy in the way I understood best, which was with my hands. The Kestrel came apart plank by plank in the boatyard behind Delphine's cousin's chandlery, and we found things in her hull like sediment layers of my whole misbegotten life: the original patch from the first rapids, tarred and failing; the emergency patch from the flooded crossing, done by lantern light with shaking hands; a fishhook; three coins of dubious mint; a child's marble that neither of us could account for and both refused to throw away.

Astrid recaulked seams like she'd been born in a boatyard. She hadn't—she'd been born in survey camps and boarding schools and one bad year in a city I won't name—but she learned the way she learned everything, which was completely and immediately, watching Tomas's hands once and then doing it better. We argued about varnish. This became a fixture. She wanted spar varnish, amber, traditional, because her father's boats had worn it and it darkened beautifully with age. I wanted the modern two-part epoxy clearcoat because it lasted longer and I had spent enough of my life maintaining beautiful dying things.

"Tomas sides with me," she said on day six.

"Tomas sides with whoever feeds him."

"He sides with tradition."

"He sides with trout."

In the end we did the hull in epoxy and the trim in spar varnish, amber, darkening already, so that the Kestrel went into the water looking like a compromise, which is what a good boat is. When we slid her off the ways and she took the river without a single seam weeping, Tomas spat ceremonially over the side and declared her sounder than any government vessel afloat, "which is not a high bar, but still."

Elias came down to see us off. He walked now, most days, with a stick and Astrid's arm, gray but upright, his mind running ahead of his body like meltwater ahead of the ice. He stood on the dock in a coat too big for what the logging station had left of him and looked at the loaded Kestrel—at the survey instruments crated and lashed amidships, the honest instruments, calibrated and certified, their serial numbers logged in triplicate—and something moved through his face that I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors during my own worst years. It was the look of a man seeing the work he loved come back to him wearing clean clothes.

"First honest survey of that valley in twenty years," he said. "Maybe the first ever. Meridian's people never surveyed it. They *claimed* it. There's a difference, and the difference is you two."

"Dad—" Astrid started.

"Don't make speeches at me, I'll fall apart and I've used up my allotment for the season." But he took her hands anyway, and held them, and said quietly, so only she could hear, or thought only she could hear: "Your mother would have liked this. The river part. She never trusted mountains. Said they were just rivers standing still."

Then he turned to me and shook my hand, formally, guide to client, and ruined the formality entirely by holding on. "Bring her back," he said. "Both of them. The daughter and the valley. In whatever order the river allows."

* * *

We cast off at dawn on the first of the spring month, because Astrid insisted on a dawn departure and because dawn departures are the only kind that mean anything. The mist was still on the water, thigh-deep and moving, and the town behind us was a suggestion—rooftops, the mission bell, the assizes building where a replacement judge was even then signing warrants for men who had believed themselves untouchable. Ahead of us the river opened north into country that had no honest map.

That was the work, when you boiled it down. Twenty years of forged boundary surveys, falsified elevations, phantom benchmarks, claims drawn in offices by men who had never been cold a day in their lives—and underneath all of it, a valley that had never once been truly measured. Our job was to measure it. Every contour, every fork, every fall line and flood plain, and to publish it, openly, in the public record, so that no company could ever again own a landscape by lying about it.

It was, I reflected as I took the tiller and Astrid took the bow line and Tomas settled amidships among the crates like a cat claiming a warm spot, the strangest expedition I had ever led. No gold. No concession stakes. No client with a quota. Just the country itself, and the truth of it, and the slow patient work of writing that truth down.

The first day was all familiar water—the lower river I'd run a hundred times, ferrying supplies to men whose names I'd later find in a ledger with lines struck through them. I felt those ghosts at the burned camp bend, where the current slowed and the bank showed the new green stubble of spring fireweed coming up through the ash. Astrid stood in the bow and looked at it a long time. Neither of us spoke. Some water you just have to row through.

But past the camp bend the river changed character, and so did the day. The channel narrowed, quickened, went from brown to the pale green of glacier feed, and the walls closed in—black spruce and raw granite, and above them, catching the midmorning sun, the white shoulders of the divide still holding winter. The air came off that snow cold and mineral and impossibly clean, the air of country nobody owned.

Astrid unrolled her father's old contour maps on the foredeck and weighted the corners with stones, and began, line by line, comparing the drawn world to the real one. I watched her do it while I steered. Her lips moved slightly, counting intervals. Where Meridian's forgery diverged from the terrain—and it diverged everywhere, lazily, contemptuously, because the forgers had known no one would ever check—she marked it in red pencil, small precise X's, little wounds in the lie.

"They didn't even try," she said at the noon halt, laughing in a way that had fury in it. "Look. They put a ridge here that doesn't exist. A whole ridge. They invented a mountain."

"Men who invent mountains get to own valleys," Tomas said, chewing. "Oldest trick in the concessions book."

"Not anymore." She smoothed the map flat. "Not after this summer."

* * *

On the fourth day we entered the high valley proper, and I did something I had not done in five years of guiding tourists on safe forgettable trails.

I stopped counting miles.

It happened without ceremony. We came around a bend below the Grey Fork confluence—the place where Tomas had lived his exile years, where the snare lines hung, where he'd laughed bitterly at my name and shown me the pattern behind the ruin—and the valley opened above us all at once, wide and green and loud with meltwater, a dozen streams falling off the shoulders of the divide in silver threads, and the light lay across it the way light does in spring at altitude, thin and fierce and total, and I realized I had no idea how far we'd come that morning and that it did not matter. For the first time since Karst Ridge, distance was not a currency I was hoarding against loss. It was just distance. The river was just the river. And the woman in the bow with the red pencil was not a passenger I was ferrying toward some inevitable goodbye.

"Mile marker should be here," Astrid said, tapping the old map. "Meridian's mile forty-two."

"There's no marker," I said. "There never was. They never came here."

"So we're off every map that exists."

"We're off every map that exists," I agreed.

She looked back at me then, windburned, hair escaping its braid, and grinned, and it was the grin of the woman at the flooded crossing saying *hand over hand, Coldharbour, that's it, again*—and I understood that this was what the whole ruinous road had been for. Not the vindication, though that had come. Not the cleared name, though I carried it now in my breast pocket with a seal on it. This. A boat, a beginning, a country with no honest map, and someone in the bow worth following upstream.

"Tomas," I called. "Take us up the west fork. We'll benchmark the confluence before dark."

"Aye." He spat over the side, for luck, for tradition, for trout. "West fork it is. Never been honestly measured."

"Neither have we," Astrid said.

And the Kestrel, patched and caulked and varnished in amber, leaking not one drop, turned her bow into the unknown water, and went up.
