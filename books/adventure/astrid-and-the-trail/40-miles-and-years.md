## 40. Miles and Years

**Marguerite**

I had counted miles for five years the way other men count rosaries—mechanically, superstitiously, one bead at a time, because the counting was easier than the praying. Miles didn't lie to you. A mile was a mile whether you were running from something or toward it, and if you kept enough of them under your boots you could convince yourself you were going somewhere.

That morning on the west fork of the Coldwater, I stopped counting.

It wasn't a decision. Decisions are things you make with your jaw set and your hands steady, and this was neither. It was more like setting down a pack you'd carried so long your shoulders had grown around its shape. I stood at the tiller while the light came up gray-gold through the mist off the water, and Astrid was in the bow with her father's old contour maps spread across her knees, weighted at the corners with a coffee tin, a coil of line, a river stone, and my spare knife, and I realized I had no idea how many miles lay between us and the headwaters.

And it didn't frighten me.

The river ran narrow here, braided around gravel bars still soft from the monsoon, and the current talked to itself in that low conversational voice rivers use when they think no one is listening. Mist stood in the cedars like a congregation waiting to be dismissed. Somewhere upstream a heron lifted off, all wings and patience, and beat its way into the fog until it was only a rumor of a bird.

"Depth's good," Tomas called from amidships, where he'd appointed himself sounder-in-chief with a knotted line and an air of enormous authority. "Four fathoms over the bar. Five past the snag. Water's honest today."

"Honest," I said. "That's a new word for it."

"Everything's honest now." He grinned, gap-toothed, sunburned, alive against every odd the valley had stacked against him. "You made sure of that."

I didn't answer. Praise still sat wrong on me, like a coat cut for another man. But I let the tiller rest easy in my hand and watched the banks go by—banks that would be surveyed this season by men and women whose instruments told the truth, whose field notes would be read by clerks who checked them, whose names would appear on documents that meant what they said. It seemed a small thing. It was not a small thing. I had spent five years learning exactly how large the small things were.

Astrid looked up from the maps and caught me watching her. She didn't smile, exactly. She did something better—she turned the map around so I could see it, as if I'd asked, as if I'd earned it.

* * *

**Astrid**

My father drew contour lines the way some men pray: daily, humbly, with attention to what was actually there rather than what he hoped to find. The maps spread across my knees were thirty years old, drawn in pencil so faint I had to tilt them to catch the light, and every elevation was written in his small, patient hand. 1,240 feet. 1,310. The river bending through them like a blue vein through the back of a hand.

When I was seven he'd taught me to read them on a kitchen table in a house we no longer owned. He'd put his finger on a closed circle and say, *a hill*, and on a circle with hachures like eyelashes and say, *a depression—the land remembers being dug*. I hadn't understood then that he was teaching me something larger than cartography. He was teaching me that the truth of a place is knowable, if you're willing to walk every foot of it and write down what you find without flinching.

Meridian had flinched. Meridian had never walked anything in their lives except ledgers.

Now I had a fresh sheet of drafting film clipped to a board, and a pencil worn to a stub, and I was drawing the west fork as we came up it—freehand, provisional, honest. My lines were shakier than his. My elevations were guesses checked against the aneroid and corrected twice an hour. But they were mine, and they were true, and somewhere ahead there was country no one had ever measured at all, which meant that everything I put down would be the first word ever said about it.

"You've got the bend wrong," Marguerite said behind me, mild as milk.

"It's a sketch."

"It's a chart. Charts get used. Someone runs aground because your bend was pretty instead of right." He nodded at the near bank, where the current curled back on itself in a long green eddy. "Draw what the water does, not what you want it to do."

"That," said Tomas sagely, from his knot-line, "is why she married you."

"We are not—" I began.

"Not yet," Marguerite said, and steered us into the bend, and I bent over my board so he couldn't see my face do whatever it was doing.

* * *

**Marguerite**

She'd said yes, eventually. Not to a question—I'd never managed to ask one properly—but to a morning, three weeks ago, when we'd waded the shallows below the mission checking the anchor stakes for the survey baseline, and she'd handed me the far end of the tape and said, "Hold this. I intend to keep holding the other end for a long time, so get comfortable."

That was Astrid. Proposals arrived as instructions. Vows arrived as work assignments. I had learned to translate, and what she'd been saying, in her language, was: *stay. Be found. Stop being a man who is only passing through.*

For five years I had been a man passing through. Even before Karst Ridge—even as a boy—I think I understood that loving things was a way of giving them the power to be taken away, and I had lost enough that I kept my holdings small: a boat, a kit, a reputation in ruins that at least weighed nothing. I measured in miles because miles couldn't leave me. You could always get more miles. They asked nothing but your feet.

But miles, I saw now, watching her draw the river into existence with a stub of pencil, were just distance wearing a disguise. What I'd actually been counting all those years was absence. Every mile was a mile between me and the crossing that failed, between me and Julien, between me and the man I might have been if the anchors had held. I wasn't traveling. I was pacing a cell whose walls I'd built myself out of horizon.

The Kestrel rounded the bend and the valley opened.

* * *

**Astrid**

It opened like a hand unclenching.

The west fork ran through a valley no map in my father's files described—his surveys had stopped at the confluence, thirty years ago, blocked by a canyon he'd noted as *impassable, return spring*. He'd never come back. Concessions and silence had eaten the years, and then Meridian, and then the fire at Camp Ashfall, and then the logging station, and all the rest of it that I would spend the rest of my life not fully understanding.

But the canyon had a way through—a slot the monsoon had scoured, walls close enough that the cedars on either rim leaned toward each other like conspirators—and beyond it the land rose in terraces to ridgelines no instrument had ever touched. Unmapped. Actually, literally unmapped, not erased or forged or burned, simply never known. In a world where everything had been claimed, sold, signed away, struck through in ledgers, here was a blank space with no owner.

I felt my father beside me the way you feel weather. *Well, girl,* he'd have said. *Get it right.*

We made camp that first night on a gravel bench above a riffle, and I cooked rice with dried fish and wild onion while Marguerite rigged the tarp and Tomas told a story about a bear that grew more literary with each telling. Afterward my father's maps came out again, and Marguerite sat close—not touching, but close in the way of two people who have shared a rope—and studied the oldest sheet by lamplight.

"He was good," Marguerite said. It cost him something; praise was still a foreign currency to him. "These lines are honest. You can tell. No wishful thinking anywhere in them."

"He taught me that the land doesn't lie. People lie about the land."

"Mm." He traced the faint blue vein of the Coldwater with one finger, up past the confluence, past Ashfall, past the claim site where we'd dug Elias's cache out of the earth, all the way to the canyon, to the edge of the paper. Beyond it, nothing. Blankness. "Your father stopped here."

"And we start here." I pulled the fresh film toward me and wrote at the top of the sheet, in letters I tried to make as patient as his: *West Fork — Survey of Record — A. Vane, lead. M. Reyes, co-lead.* Then, after a moment, I added a third name, because debts should be honored in ink: *T. Reyes-Idowu, soundings.*

"No relation," Tomas said, watching me write.

"Every survey crew needs a family," I said. "Ours is just badly sorted."

* * *

**Marguerite**

Dawn on the third day up the fork, and I woke before the others, as I always did, and made coffee, as I always did, and then did the thing I had never done.

I sat down and didn't count.

Not the miles to the headwaters. Not the days since the hearing. Not the years stolen, or the meters of rope, or the fathoms of water over Julien—that name rising in me now like a stone in a riverbed, visible when the water runs low, no longer sharp enough to cut, only heavy enough to feel. I sat with the mug warming my hands and watched the mist burn off the water and let the morning simply be a morning, the first of however many I would be given, each one uncounted and therefore infinite.

Astrid woke the way she did everything—abruptly, already mid-thought. She crawled out of the tent with her hair full of cedar needles and took the coffee I handed her without looking, eyes already on the ridgeline.

"Fog's burning off fast. We can make the terrace by noon if the channel holds."

"The channel will hold. I patched her myself."

"You patched her *with me*," she said. "Half those seams are mine. If she sinks, we sink together—it's in the vows we never took."

"Tomas will be relieved to hear the marriage is still unofficial. He has money on autumn."

"He has money on *nothing*, he's broke, he bets in fish."

Above us, the light came over the eastern ridge all at once, the way it does in high valleys—no gradual gilding but a single clean stroke, as if someone had drawn the day onto the land with one confident line. And I thought: *that's how it was. The verdict, the rain-washed steps of the courthouse, her hand in mine. Slowly, slowly, and then all at once.*

Five years ago I had walked out of an inquiry building with my name ruined and my future amputated, and I had learned to live on distances because distances were all I trusted. Now I sat on a gravel bar at the top of a river that had tried to kill us half a dozen times, next to a woman who had talked me across a flooded crossing hand over hand, with a repaired boat and a clean license and a warrant that no longer existed, and I understood at last what the trail had been trying to teach me the whole time.

You don't walk a trail to arrive. You walk it because walking is the arrival, repeated, morning after morning, and the far end is not a place. It's a person.

"Astrid," I said.

She looked at me. Fog in her hair, coffee in her hand, pencil behind her ear like a carpenter's nail.

"When we get to the headwaters—what then?"

She considered it seriously, the way she considered everything, turning it over like a stone to see what lived underneath.

"Then we come back down," she said, "and we start the next one. There's always a next one. That's the whole point of a trail, Marguerite. It doesn't end. It just gets longer the better you know it."

* * *

**Astrid**

We reached the headwaters on the sixth day.

They weren't dramatic. That surprised me, though it shouldn't have. The Kestrel could go no farther where the river divided into three threads no wider than a wagon track, spilling cold and glass-clear over beds of orange stone, and that was all. No monument, no summit, no view. Just water beginning, quietly, out of the ground, the way everything true begins.

Tomas filled a bottle from the middle thread and drank it ceremonially and declared it the finest water in the territory, having tasted none of the competition. Marguerite stood a long time looking up the valley at the ridgelines—my ridgelines now, our ridgelines, the ones I would spend seasons walking and measuring and writing down without flinching—and I watched him watch them, and I saw him set something down. Some last weight. Some final mile.

I got out the drafting film and pinned it to the board against my knees, and I began.

The first contour went in at the gravel bar where we beached: a closed loop, patient, honest. Then the thread of the river, then the rise of the south bank, then the terrace where the cedars stood. Behind me I heard Marguerite driving the first survey stake of the new season, the muffled knock of the maul, one strike, two, three—measured, unhurried, a heartbeat keeping time for a body of work that would outlive all three of us. My father's pencil lines faded on their thirty-year-old paper. Mine went down dark and certain on the film, and where his ended, mine began, and the two of them met at the canyon like a handshake across the years.

"First benchmark's in," Marguerite called. "Elevation confirmed. What do you want to call this place?"

I looked at the three threads of water beginning out of the ground. At the man with the maul. At the blank spaces upstream, waiting for their first true words.

"Beginning," I said. "Benchmark One. Beginning."

He laughed—rare, unguarded, a sound I intended to collect as many of as I collected mornings—and came to stand beside me, and together we looked up the valley into country not yet mapped, where the light was still coming over the ridges one line at a time, patient as pencil, honest as water.

The trail continued upstream. So did we.
