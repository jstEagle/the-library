## 1. Fuel, Weather, Rumors

The Kettle River ran low and green that morning, the color of bottle glass held up to the sun, and Orsen Dunmore read it the way other men read newspapers. Low water meant exposed gravel bars, meant the channel below Widow's Bend had shifted again, meant she'd have to lift the Beaver over the shallows with a half-load and pray the current hadn't moved the deadfall she'd been dodging since spring. The river kept its own accounts. She had learned, in ten years of flying it, never to be in arrears.

She was on the dock at Sixmile Landing before the mist had finished burning off, her boots wet to the laces, loading by the gray light. Medicine first — a crate of insulin packed in dry ice for the nurse at Talus Creek, breathing its cold fog like something alive — then the mail sacks, canvas and heavy with the season's letters, then the odd freight that made up the difference between flying and starving: a chainsaw blade wrapped in burlap, four cases of evaporated milk, a christening dress boxed in tissue for a baby three hundred miles from the nearest church. Someone downriver wanted a violin string. Someone else had ordered a wedding ring through the catalog, size seven, and Orsen carried it in her breast pocket the whole trip because she didn't trust the mail sack not to lose it, and because there was something about carrying a ring that made her careful.

The plane was a 1952 de Havilland Beaver, tail number N-4171K, registered — as far as any registry knew — to O. Dunmore. She'd had the name painted on the cowling herself, in letters small enough that you had to be standing close to read them, which suited her fine. *Dunmore* was a good name for hiding behind. It sounded like a place nobody went.

By seven she had six passengers signed into the book she kept under the seat: two trappers going home after selling pelts, a schoolteacher heading up to Grayling Fork for the fall term, an old woman named Mrs. Ahtila who rode the river route twice a year whether she needed to or not, just to see the country move past the window. And one man she didn't know.

He came down the dock while she was checking the tie-downs, a soft-bodied man in a canvas jacket too clean for the country, carrying a leather grip and wearing the particular smile of someone about to ask a favor. City shoes. Orsen noticed shoes before faces; they told you what a man thought the ground was going to do.

"Morning," he said. "You the pilot?"

"I'm the pilot."

"Orsen Dunmore." He said it reading off his paper, not knowing her, testing the shape of it. "I'm Rennick. I'm looking to get up to Talus Creek, or near it. Anywhere along there."

"Talus Creek's a stop, not a destination." She wiped her hands on her thighs. "You got people there?"

"Business." He climbed in when she pointed him to the rear bench, settling his grip at his feet like a man who traveled light because he replaced things rather than kept them. "Salvage work," he offered, when she didn't ask. "Marine salvage, mostly. We're scouting the territory."

*We*, Orsen noted. He'd come alone.

She got them off the water smooth as pouring cream, the Beaver climbing out of the river's grip with that old engine hauling like a team of horses, and set her course up the valley where the morning fog still pooled in the bends like spilled milk. Below, the country unrolled — black spruce to the waterline, muskeg blazing rust-red in the swales, the river braiding and rebraiding itself around islands that wouldn't exist next spring. It was, she thought, not for the first time, a country built for forgetting. Things went into the bush and the bush closed over them. That was why she'd come north. That was why she stayed.

Mrs. Ahtila sat up front beside her, as she always did, hands folded on her purse, watching the river go by underneath like a woman counting her beads.

"You're flying high today," she observed.

"Low water. I don't like surprises."

"No one does, at our age." The old woman smiled at her own joke. Then, without any change of tone at all: "There were men at the Landing yesterday asking questions. Down at Halvorsen's bar. Strangers."

Orsen kept her eyes on the channel. "Strangers ask questions. That's most of what strangers do."

"These weren't idle ones." Mrs. Ahtila had lived on this river longer than the planes had flown it; she remembered when the only traffic was poling boats and the trading scow, and she had the kind of memory that filed everything and forgave nothing. "They were asking about old wrecks. About a plane that went in the river a long time ago. Twenty years, maybe more. Before your time here."

The fog ahead thinned and the valley opened, and Orsen flew into the light and said nothing.

"There was a pilot," Mrs. Ahtila went on, conversational, terrible. "A woman, some said. Flying for one of those expedition outfits that used to come through before the war troubles overseas. Went down somewhere in the back channels and they never found much. My husband helped drag the river for three days. They found a wheel. Just the one wheel."

"A lot of planes went in back then," Orsen said. "Planes were new and rivers were patient."

"Mm." The old woman looked out the window at the water sliding past, green and opaque and keeping whatever it kept. "These men had a photograph of her. Passed it around the bar. Pretty girl, standing on a float in front of her machine. Nobody recognized her, but everybody remembered the story once it started. Rivers don't give things back often. When somebody comes asking, people notice."

*People notice.* Orsen felt the words settle somewhere under her ribs, cold as lake water. She checked her instruments though there was nothing to check. Manifold pressure steady. Oil temperature fine. Everything fine.

"They say who sent them?" she asked, and hated how casual she made it sound, how many years of practice went into making it sound casual.

"Some outfit with a long name. Salvage and recovery. The kind of company that has a logo instead of a face." Mrs. Ahtila shrugged her thin shoulders. "They paid for their drinks and left a card with Halvorsen in case anyone remembered anything. Halvorsen put it under the till. He says men who pay for information usually aren't buying."

Ahead, the Talus Creek strip came into view — a long gravel bar the nurse kept mowed with a hand scythe, white ribbon against the dark timber. Orsen started her descent and let the conversation die of natural causes, the way she let most conversations die, steering them gently toward weather and fuel and the price of gasoline, the safe shallow waters where she lived.

But the itch had started. That was the thing about invisibility: it wasn't a state, it was a discipline, and disciplines could fail. Ten years of being O. Dunmore, of answering to a name she'd chosen out of a newspaper obituary column because it belonged to a man with no family to correct the record, ten years of paying cash and owing nothing and telling no one — and all it took was one stranger with a photograph to make her feel the edges of herself again, the seams of the person she'd sewn shut. Somewhere under the calm of her hands on the yoke, something began to pace.

* * *

Talus Creek took twenty minutes — the insulin handed over, the nurse's list for next flight taken, a dog sniffed the tires and approved — and then she was airborne again with the day opening up ahead of her, five more stops strung along the river like beads on a wire. At Grayling Fork she dropped the schoolteacher and took on a load of fish. At the mission she traded gossip for bread. The country did what it always did, which was absorb her, and she did what she always did, which was let it.

Rennick stayed quiet through the middle of the day, dozing against the window, but somewhere past the divide, with the afternoon light going amber and the shadows of the spruce lying long across the water, he leaned forward and tapped the back of her seat.

"That wreck the old lady mentioned," he said. "You know anything about it? You've flown this river a while, they tell me."

"Longer than some." Orsen watched a moose wade out onto a gravel bar below, unhurried, ancient. "Not that long."

"It'd be a twenty-year-old loss. Floatplane. Expedition aircraft, chartered out of the coast. Company went under afterward — bad luck, bad press." He said it smoothly, rehearsed, a man reciting a file. "Our outfit picks up old losses sometimes. Insurance settlements, estate recovery. There may be cargo aboard worth finding. Instruments, records, that sort of thing."

"Cargo worth finding after twenty years underwater."

"Sealed containers hold." He smiled at the back of her head; she could feel it. "You'd know if you'd seen the wreck, I suppose. Low water years turn things up. Wingtips, wreckage in the back channels. Anything like that cross your path?"

The river bent beneath them, and for one second — one long, slow second — Orsen saw it: the back channel below the Kettle narrows where she'd cut a corner last August chasing a storm edge, and the pale shape she'd taken for a bleached log lying in the shallows, and the way it had caught the light wrong, too straight, too even, with rivet lines. She'd told herself *log* and flown on, because telling yourself the truth is a luxury the invisible can't afford.

"Can't say it has," she said.

"Well." Rennick settled back. "If it does. There's a finder's fee in it. Substantial. Our outfit pays well for local knowledge." He passed a card forward between the seats, and she took it without looking at it, and slid it into the map pocket with the mail receipts and the ring for the wedding, size seven.

She landed him at the mouth of the Sushitna fork where he said his contact would meet him, and watched from altitude as a gray boat with an outboard too new for the country came out of the reeds to take him off. Then she turned for home with the evening coming down purple out of the north, and the card sat in the map pocket like a splinter working toward the skin.

* * *

Halloran was waiting at her dock when she got in, which was how Halloran always seemed to arrive — already there, already comfortable, sitting on a fuel drum with his hat pushed back, as if the dock had been built around him. He was a small neat man with a bookkeeper's hands and a lender's eyes, and he ran the territory's loose ends: fuel contracts, freight liens, the debts that respectable banks wouldn't touch and couldn't afford to ignore.

"Dunmore." He nodded at the plane as she tied down. "Good day?"

"Good day."

"Heard you had a passenger with city shoes."

Word traveled faster than planes on this river. Orsen coiled her mooring line with more attention than it needed. "Tourist."

"Salvage man." Halloran produced a folded paper from inside his jacket, unhurried, letting her look at it or not. "His outfit wrote to me last month. Wanted river charts, landing rights, a local pilot on retainer. I told them the territory's pilots are spoken for." He smiled. "Yours especially."

"How much do I owe you this month, Halloran?"

"Same as ever. More than you'll clear." He stood, brushed off his trousers, and looked out at the river going dark, the last light lying flat and copper on the water. "You know what I like about you, Dunmore? You're careful. Careful people are predictable, and predictable people are easy to carry. Like freight." He tipped his hat. "Get some sleep. Weather's coming in behind you — big system off the gulf. You'll want to be on the ground for it."

He walked away up the dock, small and neat, and Orsen stood in the blue dusk with the smell of fuel and river water and listened to the itch get louder.

That night in the cabin she lit the lamp and ate beans and read the card she'd pocketed without meaning to. Heavy stock, embossed. *Meridian Recovery & Salvage, Ltd.* An address in a city she'd left a decade ago under another name. And beneath the address, small, printed like a printer's mark, almost a signature:

*A consortium interest.*

She'd seen that mark before. Once. On a document she'd signed with a hand that wasn't shaking only because she'd made it not shake, in a room with rain on the windows, ten years ago and a whole lifetime deep. She'd buried that mark so thoroughly that she'd stopped dreaming about it — mostly — and now it had floated up the Kettle River and into her map pocket on a piece of cardstock, asking about a drowned woman and offering money for memories.

Orsen banked the stove and lay down in the dark and listened to the wind come up out of the south, the big weather arriving right on schedule, rattling the tin chimney, pressing at the walls like something wanting in. Twenty years ago a pilot went into this river and stayed there. Tomorrow the low water would keep falling, and the river would keep giving up what it had held, the way rivers do in the end, patiently, without malice, everything.

She fell asleep thinking about a pale shape in a back channel with rivet lines, and woke once in the night certain she'd heard a floatplane overhead in the storm, circling, and lay listening until she understood it was only the wind, and lay listening anyway.
