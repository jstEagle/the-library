## 2. What the River Kept

The Kettle dropped four feet in August, the way it always did, and every year it gave something back.

Orsen Dunmore had lived on that river long enough to know its moods the way other pilots knew weather. In spring it was a bully, brown and swollen, shoving whole spruce trees downstream like a man clearing his throat. By late summer it turned shy and narrow-shouldered, sliding back into its old channels and exposing all the things it had swallowed during the high water — a moose carcass bleached to bone, a fish wheel someone had abandoned twenty years ago, once, memorably, an entire outhouse still bolted to its floor joists, sitting upright on a gravel bar as if waiting for a tenant.

She'd learned to read the exposed bars from the air. It was part of the job: medicine upriver, mail downriver, gossip both ways, and a running inventory of what the river had decided to surrender this season. Most of it she logged for Halloran's ledger or ignored entirely.

But on the last Tuesday of August, flying low over the back channel below Sow's Tooth Rapids with a load of insulin and one complaining dog in the cargo pod, Orsen saw something that made her hands go still on the yoke.

A wing.

Not a bird wing. Not a branch. An aircraft wing — aluminum, sun-bright where the water had scoured it, jutting from a gravel spit at the channel's elbow like a broken arm reaching out of a grave. The river had cut a new course around the spit during breakup, and now the low water stood shin-deep around it, and the afternoon light came off that metal so clean and hard it looked like a signal fire.

Orsen banked, circled twice, told herself she was checking for stranded boaters, which was even true. Then she flew home, unloaded the insulin, fed the dog's owner a story about fuel costs, and did not sleep well.

* * *

She went back two days later, alone, at first light, when the mist still lay in the channel like spilled milk.

Her Beaver sat down on the main channel's glassy water with barely a whisper, and she taxied into the mouth of the back channel and nosed her floats onto the gravel bar with the practiced care of a woman who had beached on worse. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled its disapproval. Otherwise there was nothing but the river talking to itself over the shallows, that endless low syllable of water over stone.

The wing belonged to a floatplane. She knew that before she got within fifty feet of it, knew it in her spine, in the way her stride shortened without her permission. The fabric of her careful life — twenty years of being no one in particular, of paying cash, of answering to *Dunmore* like it had always been her name — suddenly felt thin as the morning fog.

It was a Staggerwing-era airframe, older than anything still flying, and the river had kept it the way rivers keep things: thoroughly, patiently, without malice. The fuselage lay half-buried in gravel and silt, tail toward deep water, one float torn away and lodged forty yards downstream in a snag. The exposed wing was bent upward at an angle that said the water had been gentler than whatever put the plane here. There was no burn scarring. No explosion damage. The aircraft had come down more or less intact.

That was the first wrong thing. Planes that crash in the Kettle country come apart. This one hadn't.

Orsen stood in the shin-deep water for a long time, cold climbing through her waders, and made herself look at the registration plate area where the paint would have been. Gone — silted over, or removed. Of course removed. She crouched anyway and swept her gloved hand across the fuselage skin near the tail, and beneath a crust of river clay her fingers found shallow gouges. Tool marks. Deliberate, methodical, spaced like rivets. Someone had worked on this airplane before it ever touched the water.

She straightened up too fast and had to catch herself against the wing. The river noise seemed suddenly very loud.

Twenty years ago, before Orsen Dunmore existed, she had known a man who could make gouges like that look like fatigue cracks. She had known him through a consortium that paid in unmarked envelopes and never used names on paper, and she had spent a decade and most of her savings making sure she would never see his work again. The courier mark they used — a small stamped device, a circle quartered by a bar — she had seen it on crates she was paid not to ask about, and once, terribly, on a manifest she was paid to lose.

She had buried all of it. Buried it under a new name, a new territory, a reputation for reliability and nothing else. And now the river, patient as a debt collector, had dug up a piece of the exact past she'd interred, and set it gleaming in the sun where any passing pilot could see it.

*Walk away*, said the voice of survival, the one that had kept her alive through years she didn't revisit. *Report it as debris. Let the current take it back.*

But her hands were already unbuckling her wader straps.

* * *

The water in the back channel ran three feet deep over the cockpit, cold enough to stop breath, clear enough to show everything. Orsen went under with a dive mask she kept in the plane for inspecting floats, and the world narrowed to green-gold light and the slow drift of silt.

The cockpit was a tomb of soft shapes. Instruments furred with algae. A seat harness rotted to lace. She moved through it with the discipline of a woman who had salvaged wrecks before — hers, once, though that was another story — and told herself she was only assessing, only confirming, only gathering enough to decide whether to report it.

Then her hand found the cargo compartment behind the seats, and the compartment held a chest.

It was strapped down with webbing that had mostly become history, and it was heavy in the way that meant documents or instruments or money, and when she hauled it free and dragged it up through the hatch and broke the surface gasping, the morning light hit its lid and stopped her heart.

Stamped into the brass escutcheon, small and precise, was a seal: a mountain peak above three stars, ringed by lettering worn nearly smooth.

Orsen knew that seal. Everyone in the old days had known it, the way you know a scar. Vessland. The country that wasn't anymore — wiped off maps, dissolved into its neighbors' borders by treaty and money and men with the courier mark, its government exiled into hotel conference rooms and its archives scattered or burned. You didn't see the Vessland seal in the wild. Seeing it was like seeing a ghost wearing a uniform.

She sat down hard in the shallows with the chest across her knees, water streaming off her, and laughed once — a short, ugly sound with no humor in it — because the alternative was screaming, and screaming carried over water.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job heroically, she found papers. Water had found them too, at one corner, but the core was legible: manifests, correspondence, a sheaf of typed pages with the consortium's quartered-circle mark punched through each one like a brand. And tucked among them, bound in cracked leather gone soft as cloth, a logbook.

She opened it with fingers that wouldn't quite obey.

The ink had run where the water had gotten in, entries dissolving into blue ghosts. But the early pages, protected deepest in the binding, were sharp and sure, written in a small upright hand. Flight times. Weather. Fuel burns. The ordinary litany of a working pilot's day — except the aircraft type matched the wreck at her feet, and the dates were twenty years old, and the signature at the top of the first page read:

*A. Vale.*

Orsen knew the name the way you know a name from a headline you tried to forget. Astrid Vale. Expedition pilot. Lost with her aircraft in the Kettle country during a supply run, presumed drowned, the accident noted in two territorial reports and then forgotten by everyone except the people who had arranged it — if it had been arranged. For twenty years Orsen had assumed the story was exactly what the reports said, because assuming that was safer, because the alternative meant the consortium killed pilots who asked questions, and she had been a pilot who asked questions, once, briefly, until the price was explained to her.

The chest sat in her lap, dripping. The logbook lay open in her hands. The river murmured over the gravel, indifferent, having delivered its verdict after two decades of deliberation.

*Dread curdled into fascination* — that was the only way she could have described it, later, if anyone had asked, and no one would. Because dread said *put it back, sink it, let the river keep its dead.* But fascination had already opened the logbook to the last legible entry, and fascination noticed that the final page's writing changed — larger, faster, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next sheet — and fascination read the half-line that survived the water damage:

*If I don't come back, they'll say it was the weather. Check the—*

And there the ink became a blue bloom, and the sentence ended the way Astrid Vale had ended, mid-word, mid-thought, twenty years ago, in water just like this.

Orsen closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

She looked down the channel at the wreck, at those deliberate tool marks drying pale in the sun, and understood with terrible clarity that the river had not given her a curiosity. It had given her a responsibility. The chest was evidence. The logbook was testimony. And she was standing in the middle of the wilderness holding both, the last person on earth who knew what they were, and the one person whose entire life was built on never knowing anything again.

She loaded the chest into her plane behind the seat, wrapped in her spare tarp, and took off downriver with the mist burning off and her reflection flickering on the water below — a woman flying away from a wreck, carrying a wreck of her own.

By the time her floats left the surface, she had already started lying to herself about what she'd do next. That was the oldest skill she had.

But that night, in her cabin, she would spread the logbook pages flat under lamps, and dry them one by one, and read what Astrid Vale had written about being followed — and the handwriting would start to feel familiar in a way that had nothing to do with the words.

Because Orsen had seen that hand before. Long ago. On a manifest she was paid to lose.
