## 3. The Name on the Logbook

The cabin held its breath around her.

Orsen had lived in it eleven years, long enough to know its moods the way she knew weather — the way the stove pipe ticked when the temperature dropped, the way the north window fogged first because the seal had gone soft, the way the floorboards by the door announced a visitor three steps before any knock. Tonight it gave her nothing but its ordinary sounds, and that was worse. Ordinary was what she needed and couldn't have. She set the chest down on the table with both hands, as if it might go off.

Water still wept from its seams. It sat in a darkening ring of its own making, and the Vessland seal stared up at her from the lid — the double-headed wren, wings spread over a crown that hadn't existed on any map for twenty years. She'd seen it once before, stamped in wax on an envelope she was never supposed to open, back when she was another person flying under another name out of a strip that no longer appeared on charts either. That envelope had bought her a new life. She had never let herself wonder what it had cost anyone else.

Now the river had sent her a bill.

She lit both lamps, then the lantern hanging by the door, because one lamp felt like privacy and three felt like a workbench, and this was going to be work. She fetched towels, the good ones she saved for guests she never had, and spread them across the table in overlapping layers. Then she opened the chest.

Inside: oilcloth, wrapped tight and tied with cord that had swelled fat with water. Underneath, a document case — leather gone slick, brass fittings furred green — and beneath that, wedged into the corner where someone had packed it deliberately, deliberately mattered, the logbook. Standard flight-crew issue, canvas-bound, the kind every pilot in the territory had carried for decades. The kind she still carried. Its cover was stained the brown of steeped tea, and the pages inside were swollen into a single breathing block.

She worked the cover loose with a thumbnail, careful, the way you'd work a splinter out of skin. The pages came apart in clusters of five and six. She peeled them gently, laid each cluster flat on the towels, separated sheets with strips of paper towel torn from the roll above her sink. Ink had run in places; pencil had survived better. There was a smell rising off them, river-smell and old paper and something faintly chemical, like the ghost of a darkroom.

Then she saw the signature block on the first legible page, and her hands stopped working.

*Pilot: A. Vale. Aircraft: CF-VSK, Wren Mk III floatplane.*

Astrid Vale.

Orsen stood very still in her own kitchen while the lamps hissed and the fire ticked down in the stove. The name wasn't a surprise, exactly. She'd known whose plane it was the moment she saw the wing in the channel — everyone in the territory knew the story of the Vale wreck, the way they knew the story of the mine collapse and the flood year, stories told at a certain remove, with a certain shrug built in. *Bad weather. Young pilot. Twenty years ago.* The territory told its dead politely.

But knowing a name and reading it in a dead woman's handwriting were different animals. Reading it was like finding a stranger's coat in your closet — it fit the space too well.

She made tea she didn't drink and started at the beginning.

* * *

The early entries were nothing. Fuel states, wind readings, load weights, the small liturgy of a working pilot's day. *Up 0640. Fog burning off by Kettle Narrows. Pax: two geologists, quiet, tipped well.* Astrid Vale wrote the way good pilots fly — economical, exact, no wasted motion. Orsen found herself nodding along to entries twenty years old, recognizing the days: the same runs, the same weather traps, the same passengers who talked too much or not at all. Two women, two decades apart, flying the same river and writing the same complaints about carburetor ice.

It would have been almost pleasant, if the entries hadn't started changing.

The change came gradual, the way rot comes into wood — from the inside, invisible until you pressed. First it was just route notes. *Took the east channel again. Longer, but I don't like being predictable.* Then it was time-of-day. *Moved the mail run to afternoon. Someone was at the dock before dawn asking the fuel man when I'd be back.* Then, in an entry dated in the last spring of her life, plain as a woman saying it out loud:

*I am being followed. Not followed exactly — shadowed. A blue-and-white Bellanca that keeps appearing at the strips I use, always parked, never departing when I do. Today I taxied past it close enough to read the registration. It's registered to Meridian Cartographic Services. Nobody maps anything up here. I checked. Meridian's charter office is a mailbox in a building owned by six companies that don't exist.*

Orsen read that paragraph twice. Her tea went cold in her hand without her noticing.

Meridian. The name meant nothing to most people in the territory, and everything to a very short list of others. It was a shell folded inside shells, a name that surfaced on contracts and sank again, attached to survey crews that surveyed nothing, salvage outfits that salvaged nothing, cartographers who had never produced a map. Orsen knew it the way you know a scar — not by looking at it, but by what it cost to get. Eleven years ago, in another life, a man with a briefcase and a courteous voice had slid a paper across a table to her, and the paper had carried the Meridian courier mark: a stylized compass rose with one point broken off. *We take care of pilots who take care of us,* he'd said. And then, when she'd understood what taking care actually meant: *And we remember those who don't.*

She had taken care. She had lost a manifest, testified to nothing, signed her silence, and been paid in a new name and a one-way ticket north. The compass rose with the broken point had been stamped on every page of that agreement.

It was stamped here too. On the third-to-last legible entry, Astrid Vale had drawn it herself — copied it carefully into her logbook, point broken off and all, with a note beside it:

*Came to my hangar Tuesday. Man said he represented "logistics interests" and that my cargo contract was being amended. New instructions: pick up a sealed case at the Meridian dock, fly it to the coast, dump it in deep water en route. He said the weight allowance had changed. He smiled when he said it. You don't smile about weight allowances.*

*I didn't dump it. I want that written somewhere that isn't me. If this plane goes down, it won't be weather.*

Orsen set the page down on the towel with hands that were not quite steady, and walked to the window, and looked out at the dark, at nothing, at the black shapes of the spruces moving against marginally less black sky. Her breath had fogged the glass. She wiped it with the heel of her hand and it fogged again immediately, and she stood there anyway, because her legs wanted a decision her head hadn't made yet.

*If this plane goes down, it won't be weather.*

Twenty years the territory had told the story with a shrug. Bad weather. Young pilot. And all that time the truth had been sitting in the river, forty feet down, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for low water and a woman with a mask and fins and too much curiosity. Waiting for *her*, maybe. The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome: of all the pilots who flew that channel, of all the years the water level could have dropped, it had dropped now, for her, in the season her debts had come due and strangers had begun asking questions about a plane lost twenty years ago. Coincidence was a luxury she'd stopped believing in around the same time she'd stopped using her own name.

She went back to the table. There were only a few pages left worth reading, and she already knew how they ended — everyone in the territory knew how they ended — but there is a difference between knowing a story and hearing it in the voice of the person it happened to.

* * *

The final entries were written smaller. Tighter. As if the ink itself had gotten afraid.

*They know I kept the case. I can tell because the Bellanca doesn't hide anymore. It sits on the water at the mouth of the Kettle like it owns the river. Maybe it does. I've filed a complaint with the transport office in Fort Alder and been told, very kindly, that there's no record of any aircraft matching that description. No record. The whole territory is full of things with no record. I used to think that was freedom.*

*I've moved the cargo. Don't bother looking where I usually keep things. If I'm right about what's in this case — and I've had time to read enough of it to be sure — then it isn't mine to protect. It belongs to people who are still alive and don't know yet that their country exists. That sentence will sound insane to whoever finds this book. I hope somebody finds this book.*

*Weather tomorrow: they're calling for clear. Everyone says fly anyway, get ahead of them. But I've flown ahead of them twice and they were there both times. So tomorrow I'm going to do the thing they don't expect, which is the boring thing, the scheduled thing, the thing a careful pilot does. I'm going to be so visible they'll have no choice.*

*Last entry. Fuel topped, floats inspected, case secured under the aft baggage net where it rides best. If you're reading this, check the*

The sentence stopped there. Not faded, not smeared — stopped. The pencil line ran off the edge of a stroke mid-word, the way lines do when a hand is interrupted, when a page is closed on a life that expected to come back to it. Orsen had seen logs end before. Engines quit, weather closed in, pilots walked away from wrecks and never picked up the pen. But those endings had a shape, even the bad ones. This one had a hole. This one ended the way a radio transmission ends when the carrier drops.

She sat for a long time with the last page flat under the lamp, reading the unfinished word, and the cabin got colder around her by degrees she didn't bother to measure.

Check the what. Check the where. The aft baggage net — she'd read that part. *Where it rides best.* Astrid had hidden the real cargo somewhere else, somewhere she'd trusted more than her own aircraft, and the location had died with her, or nearly. Nearly was the operative word. Somewhere in these swollen, tea-brown pages, in a marginal note or a coded line disguised as a fuel calculation, the answer might still be drying under her lamps. Or it might be forty feet down in a back channel of the Kettle, in a crate slot that had been empty when the fuselage settled into the silt — empty, she suddenly remembered with a cold slide of the stomach, *empty*, because she had looked, today, with her own eyes, and the slot where a sealed case should have ridden was bare.

Someone had beaten her to it. Or Astrid had beaten everyone.

Orsen gathered the pages together, squared them, weighted them flat between two boards, and turned down two of the three lamps. In the reduced light the compass rose with the broken point seemed to move on the page, the way marks do when you're tired, the way shadows do when you've spent eleven years teaching yourself to see watchers in every treeline.

Here was the arithmetic she couldn't avoid, laid out as plainly as a weight-and-balance sheet. The consortium that had bought her silence was the consortium that had killed Astrid Vale. The mark on her own agreement and the mark in this logbook were the same mark, drawn by the same hand of the same organization, and the organization did not leave loose ends — she was living proof of that, living and renamed and debt-ridden proof. Which meant the chest in her cabin was not a curiosity. It was a fuse. And she was standing in a room full of lamp oil, holding it, while upstream somewhere a salvage outfit chartered planes and asked polite questions about a wreck twenty years old.

She should burn it. That was the safe play, the play the old Orsen — the buried one, the one with the real name — would have called obvious. Feed the pages to the stove, melt the seal off the chest, dive the wing back under the silt with a crowbar, forget the river ever spoke. Stay invisible. Invisible had kept her alive for eleven years.

But Astrid Vale had written *I hope somebody finds this book*, and had died mid-sentence with the location of the truth locked behind her teeth, and had spent her last weeks being hunted through the same skies Orsen flew now, alone, with nobody filing complaints on her behalf either. And Orsen had spent those same years telling herself that silence was survival, that the manifest she'd lost was a small thing, that the money was clean enough if you didn't look at it under lamplight.

Under lamplight, tonight, it didn't look clean. It looked like the other half of this logbook. It looked like the reason a young pilot had flown a straight, visible, doomed line up the Kettle River while a blue-and-white Bellanca waited at the mouth like a spider at the center of its web.

Outside, far off, an engine droned — high and thin, up at altitude, running with its lights out. Orsen froze, teacup halfway to lips she'd forgotten to drink from. The sound crossed the sky north to south, unhurried, and faded toward the coast, and might have been a medevac, might have a mail charter running late, might have been nothing at all. She listened until there was nothing left to listen to, then stood at the window a long while after, watching the place where the sound had been.

Invisible, she thought, had never once been the same thing as safe. It had only ever been the same thing as alone.

She banked the stove, hung the lantern back on its hook, and lay down on her bunk without undressing, boots on, the logbook's boards weighting the table three feet away. Sleep came shallow and intermittent, full of blue-and-white aircraft and unfinished sentences.

At some hour past midnight she woke to the sound of her own name — the false one — spoken clearly, just outside the door, in a voice she didn't recognize. By the time she reached the window, gun in hand, heart going like a failing engine, the clearing was empty. Only the spruces, and the frost starting to silver the grass, and down by the dock, tied off neat as you please, a rowboat she had never seen before, riding low in the water.

Whoever had come had left something on her doorstep. Orsen watched the dark doorway from the window for a long time, and understood, with the calm of a woman reading her own instrument panel in a stall, that the river had handed her more than a past.

It had handed her a countdown.
