## 4. Passengers

They came upriver on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for fuel drums and mail sacks and the slow arithmetic of accounts; nothing that changed a life had ever arrived on a Tuesday before.

Orsen heard the charter before she saw it — a twin-engine turbine whine cutting across the morning's usual hush, too polished for these parts, too clean. She was elbow-deep in the Beaver's cowling, chasing a fuel line fitting that had developed opinions, and she straightened with a wrench still in her hand and grease to her elbows as the white-and-grey aircraft swung wide over the strip and settled onto the gravel like a man taking a seat he considered his by right.

Three of them climbed down into the light. Even at fifty yards, even through the heat shimmer rising off the river flats, Orsen read them the way she read weather — quickly, instinctively, because misreading either could kill you.

The first was big without being heavy, moving with the unhurried economy of a man who had never once needed to hurry. Fair hair going grey at the temples, a face built for being trusted: open, weathered, arranged around the mouth into something that wanted very badly to be a smile. He carried a leather document case under one arm like a hymnal.

The second was young — mid-twenties, maybe — with sunburned cheeks and a canvas duffel nearly her own size, and she came down the airstair looking not at the strip or the river or the mountains stacking blue against the sky, but at *Orsen*, directly, with an expression Orsen hadn't been on the receiving end of in ten years. It took her a moment to name it. Admiration. Naked, unguarded, entirely undeserved admiration.

The third hung back on the stairs. Slender, dark, somewhere between forty and forever, wearing a surveyor's vest with too many pockets and carrying nothing at all except a small notebook, which he held closed against his thigh with two fingers, the way another man might hold a pistol.

*Well,* Orsen thought, wiping her hands slowly on a rag that made them no cleaner. *Here we go.*

The big man crossed the gravel with his hand already extended. "Ms. Dunmore? Ivar Lund." His grip was dry and warm and calibrated to be exactly firm enough. "We spoke with your agent in Fort Meridian. You're expecting us."

"I'm expecting *a* salvage crew," Orsen said. "I don't put faces to names until they're standing in front of me. Fuel's expensive and so is my time, Mr. Lund, so let's hear it."

Something flickered behind his eyes — appreciation, maybe, or its professional imitation. "Fair enough. We're looking for a wreck. Floatplane, lost twenty years ago, somewhere along the Kettle system. Expedition aircraft, private registration. The insurers have finally cleared the file, and the family—" a small pause, smooth as river stone, "—the family wants closure. We understand you know this country better than anyone flying it."

"I know every channel from here to the divide," Orsen said, which was true, and said nothing else, which was also deliberate. Behind Lund, the young woman had drifted closer and was now openly staring at the Beaver — at the patched cowling, the scarred floats, the hundred small repairs that told the story of a plane flown hard and loved anyway.

"You're the one who flew the Kettle in the flood year," the girl blurted. "The medevac run when the bridge was out. Everyone at flight school talked about it. They said nobody else would even try the gorge."

"That was someone else," Orsen said. "That pilot's dead."

It came out flatter than she intended, and the girl's face fell in a way that made Orsen feel like she'd kicked a dog. Lund smoothed it over without appearing to try. "Petra Voss, our dive lead. And Dekker Vane, our surveyor." The thin man inclined his head a centimeter. He hadn't blinked that Orsen had noticed. "We'd like to charter you. Two weeks, retainer plus daily rate, all fuel costs covered. There's a bonus structure if we locate the wreck."

"Who's paying?"

"Meridian Salvage Partners," Lund said. "A consortium of insurance interests and private backers. The paperwork's all in order — you're welcome to read every line."

*I'll bet it is,* Orsen thought. *I'll bet every line has been read by someone whose job is making sure lines say nothing.*

She should have said no. She knew that with the same certainty she knew the weight of her own fuel load, the same animal certainty that lived below thought. Twenty years ago a plane went into the Kettle with a pilot named Astrid Vale aboard, and three days ago the river had given Orsen a chest stamped with a seal that existed on no map, and a logbook signed with initials that had been sitting in her cabin like a lit match in a hay barn ever since. A salvage outfit asking after that exact wreck was not coincidence. Coincidence did not charter turbines and carry retainers.

But Halloran's name sat on her ledger like a bruise — four months past due, then renegotiated, then renegotiated again, each round costing her something she couldn't name in currency. And the daily rate Lund named, when he named it, was double what she'd have asked. Triple what she deserved.

"Two weeks," Orsen said. "I fly where I say it's safe, I land when I say it's safe, and if anyone argues with me about weather, the charter's done and the retainer's mine. I've pulled bodies out of this river. I won't add to the collection."

"Agreed," Lund said instantly, and shook her hand again, and his palm was still warm and dry and trustworthy as a forged check.

* * *

They spent the afternoon loading. Petra turned out to be tireless — hauling dive gear, compressors, a rubber Zodiac folded like a sleeping bat — and she talked the whole time, a bright running commentary aimed mostly at Orsen, who answered in monosyllables and hated herself a little for how much she didn't hate it.

"—and the visibility in cold rivers can actually be *better*, everyone thinks it's worse, but silt settles in the cold, it just hangs there less, you know? Dek says the Kettle's probably four meters vis below the thermocline. Is it true you once dove your own landing gear off the strip at Cold Lake? Because Marco at school swore you did it yourself with a mask and a wrench—"

"Somebody dove it," Orsen said. "I paid them in fuel and silence."

Petra laughed like that was the best thing she'd heard all month. She had a scar on her forearm, old and pale, and a way of touching it absently when she wasn't thinking, and she looked at Orsen the way people look at lighthouses — as though proximity alone were safety. Orsen found herself cataloguing the details against her will: the chipped front tooth, the dive computer worn on the wrong wrist, the way Petra's eyes kept going to the water whenever the river showed between the trees, hungry and unafraid.

*She dives for something,* Orsen thought. *Nobody works that hard underwater for money.*

Dek — he'd corrected Lund's introduction with a single word, "Dek," and nothing more — spent the loading hours doing precisely nothing useful. He walked the strip. He stood at the edge of the bank and watched the main channel for a long time, notebook closed in his hand. He examined the Beaver's mooring cleats, the tie-down points, the fuel cache tarp, the rusted stove pipe of Orsen's cabin, each for perhaps thirty seconds, each without expression. When Orsen caught him looking at her — not at her plane, not at her gear, but at *her*, at the set of her shoulders as she wrestled a drum into place — he nodded politely and looked away, and the nod stayed with her like a splinter working toward the skin.

At dusk they ate beans and bannock at the camp table outside her cabin, because Lund suggested it with such easy warmth that refusing would have cost more than hosting. He asked about the country — channels, water levels, the storm season — and his questions were good ones, a working man's questions, and Orsen answered them while a cold little voice in her skull kept repeating: *he's good at this. That's the whole point of him.*

"So," Lund said, ladling beans, "the wreck. Any local stories? Old-timers' tales? Sometimes the bush remembers what the paperwork forgets."

The fire popped. Somewhere downriver a loon called, that sound like laughter played backwards.

"Rivers keep their own records," Orsen said. "Mostly they keep them shut."

"Poetic," said Dek, first words since noon, and his voice was quiet and precise and landed on the table like a coin spun to see which face came up.

"Lots of time to think, flying solo," Orsen said, and met his eyes, and neither of them smiled, and Lund laughed heartily at something and passed the bannock.

* * *

Later, when the crew had rolled into their tents and the fire had burned down to a red memory, Orsen stood at her window with the logbook in her hands. She'd wrapped it in oilcloth and hidden it inside the false bottom of her ammunition box, but tonight she'd taken it out, and now she turned it under the lamplight like a man turning a card he already knew was losing.

*A. Vale.* The ink had faded to the color of weak tea, but the letters held. Twenty years drowned and still legible, still insisting on itself. Somewhere out there in the dark, twenty meters of green water downstream of a back channel, a wing waited in the silt with tool marks on it that Orsen hadn't yet let herself examine closely, because she knew — the way she knew weather, the way she knew Lund's handshake — what tool marks on a fuselage would mean.

Tomorrow she would fly these people to the wreck she'd already found. She would circle, she would search, she would perform discovery like an actress playing a part written by someone who'd never seen the river. And when Petra went down into the cold and came up with whatever the Kettle had decided to give back, Orsen would stand in the boat and watch, holding the truth under her tongue like a stone.

She thought about Petra's laugh. She thought about the scar the girl touched without knowing. She thought about the daily rate, and Halloran's ledger, and the seal on the chest that belonged to a country that no longer officially existed, and about Astrid Vale, who had climbed into a floatplane twenty years ago trusting the sky and the company that hired her, and had come down in pieces into a river that kept her secret better than any living person ever had.

*You could tell them,* said the voice that sounded like her mother, or like conscience, or like the river itself. *You could tell them tonight, by the fire, and see what their faces do.*

And then she imagined Lund's face doing what it would do — that warm arrangement rearranging itself into something else, something filed away and reported upward — and she imagined what would happen next, in rooms she'd never see, to people whose names she didn't yet know, and she put the logbook back in its oilcloth and the oilcloth back in the box and pressed the false bottom home until it clicked.

Outside, the river ran on in the dark, patient as everything that intends to outlast you. Orsen lay down without undressing and listened to it, and told herself the oldest lie in her inventory — *just until the debt's clear, just until I know what they are, just a little longer* — and slept badly, and dreamed of wings.

In the morning, the fog came off the Kettle in long white banners, and Ivar Lund stood on the bank watching it burn away, and said, cheerfully, "Perfect flying weather," and Orsen, checking her fuel, said, "Sure," and did not look at the ammunition box, and did not look at Dek, who was looking at her.
