## 5. The Debt Collector

The chest lived under Orsen's floor for six days before Halloran came looking.

She had not told anyone about it. She had told no one about the wing in the back channel either, though the river was doing its own telling — low water laying bare what it had kept for twenty years, and word traveling downriver the way word always did, faster than any plane she flew. She'd spent those six days telling herself that a wreck surfacing after two decades was a curiosity, nothing more. That men who ran salvage outfits out of Fairbanks and Whitehorse had better things to chase than a floatplane old enough to have moss for skin.

She almost believed it, right up until the Cessna 206 came in low over the lake at dusk with a paint job too clean for this country and a tail number she didn't recognize.

Orsen was splitting kindling behind the cabin when she heard it. She set the axe down carefully, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you won't let them, and watched the plane settle onto the water with the smooth economy of someone who flew for money that didn't come from hauling mail. The man who climbed out wore a wool coat worth more than her annual fuel bill and carried a leather case instead of a duffel.

Halloran.

He hadn't changed much in four years. A little greyer at the temples, maybe, a little softer through the middle, but the eyes were the same — pale, patient, the eyes of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because everyone around him learned quickly enough to lower theirs. He stood at the edge of her beach like a man admiring property he already owned.

"Dunmore." He said it pleasantly. "You're hard to find."

"You found me."

"I always find you. That's rather the point of me." He smiled, and it reached his mouth without ever reaching anywhere near his eyes. "Mind if I come up? My knees are older than my manners."

She wanted to say no. She said nothing, which in her country meant yes, and he took it, climbing the bank with the care of a man who considered gravel beneath him in every sense. She followed him up to the cabin, and while he looked around at the iron stove and the drying racks and the single cot with its army blanket, she put the kettle on because her hands needed something to do that wasn't reaching for the shotgun over the door.

"Cozy," Halloran said. "I've always admired this. The simplicity of it. A man could disappear very thoroughly in a place like this." He settled onto her one chair as if it had been built for him. "A woman, I should say. You've done it very well, Orsen Dunmore. Four years, and nobody's asked a single question about where you came from or why a pilot with your hours is flying medicine to villages that pay in fish."

The kettle began its low gathering hum toward a boil. Orsen leaned against the counter and folded her arms and waited. With Halloran, waiting was the only move that didn't cost anything.

He let the silence sit for a moment, enjoying it, and then opened the leather case on his knee. "I'll come to it, then, since you're not going to offer me tea. Do you know who holds your paper?"

"My paper?"

"Your debt. The note on the Beaver. The advance against next season's contracts. The — shall we call them courtesies — extended by certain suppliers who don't usually extend credit to anonymous women with no verifiable history." He drew a slim folder from the case and laid it on his thigh without opening it, the way a preacher lays a Bible. "Four years ago you were in a bad way. Somebody bought that paper from the people you owed. Quietly. Through intermediaries. You've been paying interest to a ghost ever since."

The kettle climbed toward its whistle. Orsen didn't move to take it off the heat. Her mouth had gone dry in a way water wouldn't fix.

"And now," Halloran continued, "the ghost has decided to introduce itself."

"The consortium." The word came out flat. She'd promised herself she would never say it aloud again, not in this cabin, not under this sky, and here it was, sitting in her kitchen like a relative she'd buried twice.

Halloran's eyebrows rose a fraction, a small performance of surprise. "So you do remember them. Good. That saves time, and time is the one thing I'm short of." He tapped the folder once with one finger. "They hold every cent you owe, Orsen. They have held it since the month you landed here with a duffel bag and a name you'd been using for less than a year. And they've been content — genuinely content — to let you fly your mail and pay your interest and grow old invisibly. Right up until three weeks ago."

"Three weeks ago."

"A salvage outfit chartered out of the coast. Lund Maritime Recovery. Ring a bell? It will shortly." He opened the folder at last and removed a single sheet, which he held but did not offer. "They've been asking questions in every river town between here and the divide. About a plane lost twenty years ago. A Vessland registry aircraft, gone down in the Kettle country with a pilot named Vale aboard. And about the woman who pulled something out of a back channel last week when the water dropped."

The room got very quiet. Outside, a loon called across the darkening lake, that long rising laugh that sounded like grief and warning in equal measure, and Orsen thought: even the birds know.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"No," Halloran agreed mildly. "Of course you don't. You never do. That's always been your best quality." He rose, crossed to her window, and looked out at the lake where his clean white Cessna rocked at her mooring buoy like an accusation. "Here's how it stands. In ten days, maybe twelve, that salvage crew arrives at your dock to charter you. You're the only pilot licensed for this stretch with a plane that can lift their gear, and they know it, and they'll hire you because there's no one else. Ivar Lund. A diver called Petra Voss. A surveyor named Dekker who isn't a surveyor. You will fly them wherever they ask."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then they charter someone else, and the consortium's interest in you becomes academic, because a pilot who can't take work can't pay a debt, and a debt unpaid becomes — " he turned back to her, and his voice stayed gentle, which was the worst part — "callable. All of it. At once. The note on the Beaver, the advances, the supplier credit. You'd be grounded by Thursday and bankrupt by Sunday, and the consortium would own whatever was left, including the wreckage of your good name, such as it is. Refusal has a price, Orsen. I'm paid to name prices plainly, so here it is plainly: everything you've built here. Gone in a week. And you'd start over somewhere new with nothing, at your age, with your history, in a world that has gotten considerably smaller since you last ran from it."

The kettle finally whistled — a thin, climbing shriek — and neither of them moved, and after a moment it subsided on its own, embarrassed, like a child shouting in a sickroom.

"And if I say yes?" Orsen asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What does yes buy?"

"It buys your debt. Cleared. Every cent, signed off, the paper burned in front of you if you like. It buys your anonymity, guaranteed — the consortium has no further interest in Orsen Dunmore once this matter closes. It buys you exactly the life you have now, forever, which I understand is all you've ever wanted." He returned to the chair and sat, folding his hands over the folder. "All it costs is information. What the crew finds. What they recover. Where they dig, what they lift, what they talk about when they think no one's listening. You report to me — a frequency, a post office box, whatever suits your tradecraft — and when the job ends, you walk away clean."

"Just information."

"Just information." He smiled again, that bloodless smile. "I'm not asking you to hurt anyone. I'm asking you to be the eyes you've always been. You watch everything anyway; it's written all over you. I'm simply asking you to write it down for someone."

* * *

After he left — and he left the way he'd come, unhurried, the Cessna lifting off the black water into the last violet light and turning south — Orsen sat at her table with the lamp unlit and listened to the lake breathe.

She pried up the floorboard and took the chest out into the lamplight she finally allowed herself, and looked at the seal stamped into the lead: the double-headed river hawk of Vessland, wings spread over a crown that no longer existed on any map. Beside it, the logbook lay open to the page she'd dried with hair from her own head and the warm side of the stove, the ink faded to the color of old bruises but legible, the handwriting quick and slanting and alive:

*They know I'm carrying it. Someone at the refuel field made a call on a radio I didn't recognize. If anything happens to me, the cargo matters more than I do. Tell them —*

And there it ended, mid-word, mid-breath, twenty years old.

Tell them what. Tell them who. Astrid Vale had died with half a sentence in her mouth, and the other half was sitting in a chest under a dead woman's floorboards, and now the same hands that had bought Orsen's silence once were offering to buy her eyes.

She thought about the price. Not the debt — the debt was arithmetic, and arithmetic had never frightened her. She thought about what *yes* would make her. A woman flying honest people into the wilderness and selling their footsteps behind them. She had done smaller versions of it before; that was the whole shape of her past, the thing she'd paid so dearly to bury. She knew exactly how it went. It started with information. Information was never just information. Information became a schedule, and a schedule became a window, and a window became a place where a plane went down in weather it should have flown around, and a pilot named Vale fell out of the sky because somebody sold her route to somebody who wanted her quiet.

Her loyalty, Halloran had said, without quite saying it, was a commodity. Someone else had already sold it once. The consortium had bought her silence four years ago the way you buy timber rights — cheap, from a desperate party, with no intention of letting the land recover.

Orsen closed the logbook. She pressed her palm flat against the lead lid of the chest, feeling the cold of it, the weight of everything sealed inside, and made herself look at the choice squarely, the way she looked at weather: not what she hoped it would do, but what it was going to do.

If she reported, the crew would fly into whatever the consortium decided they should fly into. The young diver with the scarred hands. The courteous leader with his retainer. The quiet surveyor who watched everything. People who trusted her before they'd met her, because trust was the currency of the bush and she traded in it daily.

If she refused, she lost the plane, the name, the life — and the crew would still come, still charter some other pilot, still fly into the same country with no one watching their backs who cared whether they came home.

There was no door marked *innocent*. There hadn't been for a long time. But there might be a door marked *something else*, if she was fast enough and quiet enough and willing to carry the weight alone.

She put the chest back under the floor. She banked the stove. And in the morning, when she walked down to her dock and found the first of them already standing there — a broad-shouldered man with a sea chest and a handshake like a treaty, saying *Ms. Dunmore? Ivar Lund. We're told you're the only pilot who can take us up the Kettle* — she heard Halloran's voice in her ear, naming the price, and she heard her own voice answer, steady, warm, professional:

"When do you want to leave?"

Because the trap had closed around her days ago, and the only question left was who else was standing inside it when it snapped shut — and Orsen Dunmore had spent four years learning exactly how much a person could carry and still keep flying.

She just hadn't yet learned what it cost to carry it alone.

* * *

That night she wrote two letters she did not send. One gave Halloran's post office box everything he'd asked for and more, detailed and damning and entirely invented — a fiction so precise it would take him a week to disprove, buying her time she didn't know how else to buy. The other was four words long, addressed to no one, and read: *I'm sorry. I'm coming.* She fed both to the stove, watched the flames take them, and began pulling charts of the upper Kettle River, tracing with one finger the route a dead pilot had flown twenty years ago, toward whatever was waiting for all of them in the interior.

Ten days, Halloran had said. The crew had given her eight.
