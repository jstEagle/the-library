## 6. Up the Kettle River

The Kettle River country began where the maps got honest about their ignorance. Orsen had flown the line a hundred times — mail, medicine, fuel drums lashed into canoes' worth of weight — and every time she crossed the first ridge she felt the territory change under her like weather. The spruce closed over the valleys in black water. The river itself braided and unbraided, silver threads through muskeg that steamed even in September, and there were no roads because roads needed a reason, and nobody had ever found one out here worth the gravel.

She flew lead with Ivar beside her, Petra and Dek wedged among the gear in the back of her old de Havilland. Lund's own machine — a boxy turbine Otter chartered out of Fort Meridian with two of his regular hands aboard — droned off her left wing, close enough that she could see the pilot's face when he glanced over: bored, competent, a man flying a route he'd flown before.

"Tell me again why we're going upriver instead of down," Ivar said. He had to lean close to be heard over the engine. Up close he smelled of coffee and wool, and there was something almost gentle in the way he asked questions, as if he expected to be told no and had made peace with it.

"Because the river remembers what it takes," Orsen said. "Downstream it gives things back to the sea. Upstream is where it keeps them."

He looked at her for a moment longer than the answer deserved. "You've been up here a long time."

"Long enough."

The truth was simpler and worse: she'd chosen the upper Kettle because it was far from the channel where the wing lay exposed, far enough that the salvage crew would spend days sweeping water that held nothing but old canoe wrecks and moose bones, while she figured out what in God's name she was going to do with a chest stamped with the seal of a country that no longer existed. It sat in her cabin now, wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards, and she thought about it the way you think about a tooth that needs pulling — constantly, wincingly, and without doing anything at all.

Petra leaned forward between the seats, headset around her neck so her voice came raw. "What's that lake?"

"Kettle Lake. Frozen over by Halloween most years." Orsen tipped a wing so the girl could see down into it, the water green-black and fathoms deep, holding its secrets the way cold water does — completely, and forever, unless it decides otherwise.

"It's beautiful," Petra said, and there was something in her voice that made Orsen glance back. The young diver was pressed to the window, both hands on the frame, watching the country go by underneath with an expression Orsen recognized from mirrors: the look of someone seeing a place they'd already decided to love.

* * *

The storm came out of the northwest an hour later, the way storms did up here — not arriving so much as being revealed, as if it had been standing behind the horizon all along waiting to be noticed. One minute the ridgelines were sharp against blue; the next they were gone, and the air went the color of wet slate, and then the rain hit the windshield like thrown gravel.

Orsen's hands knew what to do before her mind caught up. She'd landed in worse. But she wasn't alone now, and the strip she'd planned on — a gravel bar three miles below the confluence — would be underwater by now, the river up and brown and fast.

"There's a lake ahead," she said into the mic. "Big enough. We put down there and wait it out."

"You're sure about the depth?" Ivar asked.

"No," she said honestly. "But I'm sure about the rocks, and they're not in the lake."

The landing was ugly. The wind came quartering across the water in gusts that picked the plane up and set it down again like a dog shaking a stick, and the last fifty feet she was flying sideways, crabbing against a crosswind that wanted to put her into the trees. The floats touched hard, once, twice, skated, and then bit, and the old girl plowed a white furrow across the gray water and settled, rocking, while rain hammered the roof of the world.

Behind her, Dek said nothing at all, which somehow was louder than shouting. In the other plane she could see the Otter circling, waiting to see if she lived, before it too came down — smoother, heavier, throwing more spray.

They rafted the planes together near the north shore, where a gravel fan ran out into the shallows below a stand of birches already turning gold. By the time everyone was ashore the wind had climbed to a gale and the temperature had dropped ten degrees, and there was nothing to do but get the tents up and the gear under cover and accept that the Kettle River country had decided they were staying.

* * *

They built the fire in the lee of the overturned freight canoe, and it became the center of the evening the way fires do — everything arranged around it, everyone drawn to it, the light reaching only so far before the dark took over. Rain still fell, softer now, a steady whisper on the canvas. The planes rocked at their moorings, lights off, patient as animals.

Dek produced a pot, rice, canned sausage, and a competence in camp cookery that surprised everyone including himself. Ivar dealt cards from a deck soft as cloth. And Petra sat closest to the flames with her hands stretched out, palms open, fingers spread, as though she were warming something more than skin.

"Your hands," Orsen said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "They bother you."

Petra looked down at them as if noticing them for the first time. The knuckles were scarred, the nails cut short, and there was a fine tremor in the right one that the firelight made visible.

"Cold water nerve damage," she said. "From when I was nineteen. I dove a car out of a reservoir." She turned her hands over, studied the palms. "My brother's car."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"He drove into it," Petra went on, in the flat voice people use when they've rehearsed a thing so many times it's worn smooth. "Deliberately. With himself in it. They never found him — just the car, forty feet down, and me, because I'm the one who could dive, so I'm the one who went down and opened the door." She flexed her fingers, watched them shake. "The doctors say the nerves might come back. They won't. But I kept diving anyway, because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because down there, things are where they are. A wreck doesn't pretend to be anything else. It just sits in the dark being exactly what happened. And when you bring it up, when you get it into the light—" Her voice cracked, just slightly, and she covered it by feeding a branch to the fire. "Somebody should get to stop being lost. That's all. Somebody should get to be found."

Ivar laid his cards face down. Even Dek looked up from the ledger he was always writing in.

"That's why you took this job," Orsen said quietly. "Twenty years is a long time for a family to wait."

"Twenty years is exactly how long," Petra said, and smiled, and it was a real smile, tired and true. "The family that hired us. Some pilot named Vale. Astrid Vale. Her daughter's been looking for her mother's wreck since she was six years old. Can you imagine? Growing up knowing your mother is somewhere in the water, and nobody will tell you where, or why, or what happened?" She shook her head. "When Mr. Lund took the contract, I begged to be on the crew. I'd have worked for free."

Orsen felt the words go through her like cold water through a wetsuit — everywhere at once, touching everything. *Astrid Vale.* Said casually, gratefully, by a girl warming her ruined hands at a fire, while the woman who had pulled Astrid's logbook from the river sat four feet away with the name burning a hole behind her ribs.

"I can imagine," Orsen managed.

"You've lost someone?" Petra asked, gentle, the way divers are gentle, used to what the water gives back.

"Everyone loses someone," Orsen said. "Up here we just have more room to spread them out."

It got a laugh, small and grateful, and the evening folded itself back around the fire. Ivar dealt the cards again. Dek wrote in his ledger. Petra dozed sitting up, her head tipping toward Orsen's shoulder, and stayed there, trusting as a bird, and Orsen sat very still and let her, and hated herself with a thoroughness that felt almost like devotion.

* * *

Later — past midnight, when the rain had stopped and the clouds had torn open to show a scatter of indifferent stars — Orsen walked down to the water alone. The lake lay flat and black, breathing faintly against the gravel, and the planes rode their lines like sleeping things. She stood with her boots in the shallows and let the silence have her.

She thought about the chest under her cabin floor, sixty miles south. She thought about the logbook drying page by page in her stove's warmth, Astrid Vale's handwriting surfacing from the water-rot like a body rising slowly. She thought about *cargo I was ordered to dump*, and the courier mark she knew better than her own signature, and the final entry that stopped mid-word, mid-breath, mid-life.

And she thought about telling them. Right now. Walking back up the gravel, waking Ivar, saying: *the wreck you're looking for isn't up this river, and I know that because I've already found it, and there's something in it that isn't salvage, it's testimony, it's a murder, it's a country that got erased, and I need help.*

She could feel how it would go. The relief of saying it. The way Petra's face would change. The way Ivar's courtesy would cool into calculation, because he had a retainer from somebody, and Orsen had seen the consortium's mark often enough lately to wonder whose money actually paid for this expedition. And Dek — quiet, watchful Dek, who wrote everything down and showed nothing — Dek would write this down too.

Belonging, she was learning, was the most expensive thing on the river. It cost exactly what you couldn't pay: the truth. Every hour these people were kind to her, the lie got heavier. Petra asleep against her shoulder. Ivar asking her opinion like it mattered. Even Dek's silent watching had begun to feel less like suspicion than attention — the particular attention of a man deciding whether to trust you, which was worse, because it meant she could fail it.

The chest could stay buried under her floorboards. That was the safe arithmetic. Fly them up the empty channels, let them sweep clean water, collect the charter fee, keep her name false and her past drowned. Twenty years of practice said so. Twenty years of practice had kept her alive.

But twenty years of practice had never had to sit next to a girl whose hands shook because she'd opened her brother's car door at forty feet, and hear her say *somebody should get to stop being lost.*

Orsen crouched and put her hand in the lake. The cold climbed her arm like something alive. Somewhere under that black surface, in channels she could name from memory, a wing waited in the shallows of a back channel, silvered with silt, holding whatever was left of the truth.

"Another day," she said aloud, to the water, to Astrid, to herself. "One more day."

The lake didn't answer. Lakes never did. But far downstream, or maybe only in her imagination, she heard the river moving in the dark — patient, unhurried, keeping what it kept — and she understood with sudden clarity that it was not keeping those things for her.

It was waiting for her to decide what she was going to do about them.

Behind her, a tent flap rustled. She turned, heart jumping — but it was only Dek, standing at the edge of the firelight with his ledger under his arm, watching her the way he always watched her. Not accusing. Not friendly either. Just watching, the way a man watches weather he hasn't decided to trust yet.

"Storm's passed," he said.

"Not all of them," Orsen said, and walked back up the gravel toward the fire, feeling his eyes on her back the whole way.
