## 7. Petra's Hands

Petra learned to dive in water the color of old tin, in a flooded quarry outside a town whose name she had never bothered to learn because she'd already decided to leave it. The quarry had swallowed a tractor and two cars and, according to local legend, a church bell that rang on certain nights if you put your ear to the surface. She was eleven. The instructor — a patient man with lungs like bellows and hands like rope — told her the first rule of diving was that the water doesn't lie.

*Everything else lies,* he said. *The light lies. Your eyes lie. Your own body will lie to you about how long you've been down. But the water tells you exactly what it is, every time.*

She thought of him now, twenty feet down in the Kettle River's back channel, her breath rasping through the regulator in the rhythm he'd taught her — in for four, out for six, never hold, never fight — while the wreck of a floatplane rose out of the murk beneath her like something surfacing from a dream it had been having for twenty years.

The morning had come in cold and clean, the kind of northern morning where the air itself seems rinsed. Frost furred the grass along the bank. Orsen Dunmore had ferried them upriver in the Beaver with the economy of motion Petra had come to quietly worship — no wasted words, no wasted fuel, the plane set down on the channel's glassy reach so gently that the ripples barely reached the shore. Then Orsen had stood at the controls while they anchored the buoy line, arms folded, watching the water with an expression Petra couldn't read and hadn't stopped trying to since the day they'd landed at her strip.

*You'll want to work the upstream side first,* Orsen had said. *Silt settles downstream of the hull. Visibility's better if you let the current do your sweeping for you.*

It was good advice. It was also strange advice, Petra thought now, hovering above the wing root, and she turned the strangeness over as she swam, the way you turn a stone over to see what lives underneath. How did a bush pilot know the silt patterns around a wreck she claimed never to have seen? She filed the question away with the others — the way Orsen's jaw tightened when Ivar mentioned the cargo manifest, the way she'd known the depth of the channel without sounding it — and kept swimming, because the water didn't lie, and right now the water was telling her something enormous.

The plane lay on its back at a slight list, one float torn away, the other buried to the strut in river clay. Twenty years of sediment had clothed it: a grey-green pelt of silt and algae, threads of weed streaming from the wing struts like hair. A pike slid out from under the fuselage as Petra approached, unhurried, ancient-eyed, and vanished into the tea-dark water beyond the visibility line. Small fish hung in the open door of the cabin, facing into the current, holding their places against the flow with tiny muscular corrections, as if standing watch.

Petra checked her gauge. Nineteen feet. Cold enough to ache through the suit at her wrists and the hinge of her jaw. Bottom time generous but not infinite. She began at the nose, as the plan said, working forward to aft along the port side, running her gloved hand just above the skin of the aircraft, feeling before seeing. This was her favorite part, the part no one who hadn't dived could understand: reading with the palms. Metal under silt has its own grammar. Rivets speak in rows. Seams speak in lines. Corrosion speaks in softness, in the give of metal gone to flake.

Her hand crossed a seam and stopped.

She backed up. Ran it again.

There — a line across the fuselage skin behind the cockpit, maybe eighteen inches long, running diagonally where no seam should run. Not a stress fracture; those wandered and feathered. This was straight-edged. Deliberate. She swept the silt away from it in slow strokes of her glove, careful, reverent, the cloud rising around her wrists and drifting off downstream, and the water cleared, and she saw.

Someone had cut this airplane.

Not hacked — cut. Two parallel kerfs, close together, the metal between them peeled back in a neat flap like the lid of a tin, the edges bright even now under their fur of algae where the exposed aluminum had resisted twenty years of river. And below the flap, half-buried in clay, the tool that had done it still rested where it had fallen or been dropped: a pry bar, short-handled, its steel gone brown, wedged under the frame of the cabin door.

Petra hung there in the cold green light and felt the whole shape of the job tilt under her.

Planes crash. She knew that; everyone in salvage knew that. Planes hit weather, hit wires, hit water too fast, and when they do they tear — ragged, chaotic, skin ripped in starbursts from the impact point, rivets popped in crowns, metal bent in ways that tell you exactly how hard and from which direction the world hit it. Petra had spent three seasons pulling wrecks out of lakes and harbors and one memorable swamp, and every wreck had spoken that same honest language of violence.

This spoke a different language. This spoke in straight lines.

She followed the kerf aft. It wasn't alone. A second cut ran along the doorframe, precise as a surgeon's incision, and where the door had been forced the hinges were bent outward — outward, away from the pressure of any impact, pulled open by hands. Inside the cabin, when she eased herself through the gap with a care that had nothing to do with technique and everything to do with a sudden crawling sense of trespass, the story continued. The cargo netting had been slashed, not torn. A tie-down ring had been unscrewed from the floor track and left lying in the muck, its mounting bolts backed out thread by thread. Someone had opened this plane after it came down. Someone had taken their time.

And someone had left a hole.

Aft of the cabin, bolted to the floor between the main spars, was a cargo cradle — a shallow steel tray with lashing points, the kind used for a single heavy crate, the kind you don't install unless you're carrying something whose shifting would kill you. Petra measured it by eye, then by hand, spanning it with her arms: big enough for a chest, small enough for a strongback to carry. The lashing straps were still there, still buckled, cinched down tight around nothing.

Empty.

She knelt on the fuselage frame inside the drowned cabin, her bubbles ticking upward through the broken roof windows in silver strings, and looked at that empty cradle for a long time. Around her the fish held their stations. The current combed the weeds. Somewhere far above, distorted into a low groan, she could hear the river moving ice-out debris along the main channel, the sound of a season turning.

The water doesn't lie. But people do, and Petra had spent enough of her life around people to know that the empty cradle was asking a question the manifest hadn't answered. Ivar's paperwork described a geological survey flight lost with all hands, cargo listed as instruments and samples. Instruments and samples did not get their own lashed cradle. Instruments and samples did not need a plane opened afterward with a pry bar by divers or salvors who had cut neat flaps in the skin and taken one specific thing and left everything else — including, she now saw, a flight bag wedged under the pilot's seat, its leather stiff as bark, its buckle still fastened.

They'd left the personal effects. They'd taken only the crate.

Petra backed out of the cabin slowly, unwilling to disturb more than she had to, and made two decisions in the green silence. First: she photographed everything, the little camera's flash strobing pale ghosts across the fuselage, each picture a small act of testimony. Second: she worked the flight bag free, gently, and carried it out with her, tucked under her arm like something rescued from a house fire.

She ascended along the anchor line, watching her depth tick down, doing her stops, and all the while the quarry instructor's voice ran under her breath like a current: *the water tells you exactly what it is.* The water had told her. Now she had to decide what to do with the telling.

* * *

The surface hit her like a slap of ordinary reality — wind, light, Dek's gloved hand hauling her up the ladder onto the work barge, the smell of gasoline and spruce smoke and coffee somebody had scorched on the camp stove. Her lips were blue-lipped numb, her fingers clumsy in the gloves, and she stood shivering in the weak sun while Dek stripped her harness and Ivar pressed a thermos cup into her hands without asking whether she wanted it, which was one of the hundred small things about Ivar Lund that made people trust him, and which Petra was beginning to suspect was also a kind of armor.

"Anything?" Ivar asked. Casual. Professional. His eyes, though, were already reading her face the way she'd read the fuselage — feeling before seeing.

"There's a lot," Petra said. "Can we — " She glanced at Dek, at the barge hand whose name she still tripped over, at the tree line, general and inclusive. "Privately?"

Something moved behind Ivar's eyes. Not alarm, exactly. More like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he'd believed solid.

They walked to the stern, past the coiled lines and the spare tanks, far enough that the others were a blur of orange and noise. Petra set the flight bag on the deck boards between them, dripping, and watched Ivar look at it. He didn't touch it. That interested her. Most men would have touched it.

"The plane was opened," she said. "After it went down. Cut open — pry bar's still wedged in the doorframe. Clean cuts, two of them, deliberate. Cargo netting slashed, a tie-down ring unbolted. There's a cargo cradle bolted to the floor, built for one crate, and the straps are still cinched around nothing." She made herself say it plainly, no drama, the way you report a finding, because drama was a way of editorializing and findings shouldn't be editorialized. "Whoever came down here took one thing and left everything else. Left the pilot's effects." She nudged the flight bag with one boot. "Left this."

Ivar was quiet.

He was a man who was usually quiet in a comfortable way, a way that made room for other people's words, and Petra had always liked that about him. This was different. This quiet had weight and temperature. He crouched slowly beside the flight bag, forearms on his knees, and studied the leather, the buckle, the initials too worn to read, and Petra watched the color change in his face — not draining, exactly, but reorganizing, the way water reorganizes when something large moves beneath it.

"Ivar," she said. "Straight cuts. Nobody cuts a wreck open unless they're looking for something specific. And nobody finds it unless they know what they're looking for."

"I know," Ivar said.

His voice was level, but it was the levelness of a man walking on ice he no longer trusted, testing each step. He stood up. He looked at the flight bag a moment longer, and then — and this was the thing Petra would remember, the thing she would turn over at night in her sleeping bag for weeks afterward — he looked upriver, toward the Beaver moored in the shallows, toward the figure of Orsen Dunmore visible at a distance, checking her tie-downs with her cap pulled low.

He looked at her for perhaps three seconds. Then he looked away.

"We'll log it," Ivar said. "Full documentation. Nothing leaves the site until we understand what we have." He picked up the flight bag, and his hands were careful with it, almost formal, the way you handle evidence or a dead man's letters. "Good work, Petra. Get warm. You've got a surface interval coming."

"Ivar—"

"Get warm," he said again, gently, and walked aft with the bag, and stood at the rail looking down into the brown water for a long time, and did not say anything else to anyone for the rest of the afternoon.

* * *

That evening the camp settled into its rituals — stove fed, gear dried, Dek hunched over his survey charts muttering coordinates like a man praying in a language of numbers. Petra sat on a supply crate near the fire with her boots steaming and turned the day over in her mind, and found that it wouldn't sit flat.

Because here was the thing she kept circling: Ivar hadn't been surprised. Surprised men ask questions — *what kind of cuts, show me, are you sure* — and Ivar had asked none. He had received her findings the way a man receives news he has been expecting from a direction he didn't predict. And then he had looked at Orsen Dunmore, and looked away, and gone silent in a way that had nothing comfortable in it.

Petra pulled her notebook out — the cheap waterproof one, its pages warped from honest service — and wrote down what she'd found, itemized, timed, photographed. Straight cuts. Pry bar. Empty cradle. Flight bag recovered. And then, underneath, in smaller letters, she wrote the question that had been sitting in her chest since the cold green dark of the cabin:

*What was in the crate?*

And underneath that, because she was honest with herself even when it cost: *And why did Ivar look at Orsen when I told him?*

Across the fire, Ivar sat with the flight bag on his knees. He hadn't opened it. He turned it over slowly, studying the underside, and then went very still, and Petra leaned forward despite herself, following his gaze to the leather where the firelight caught it.

Stamped faintly into the bag's base, half worn away, was a mark — a seal pressed into the leather long ago, a device of some kind, geometric, official, belonging to some country or company or authority Petra had never heard of.

Ivar's thumb moved over it once, twice, like a man touching a scar.

Then he closed his fist around the mark, hiding it from the firelight, and looked up — and found Petra watching him.

Neither of them said anything. The fire cracked. The river ran on in the dark, carrying whatever else it kept.
