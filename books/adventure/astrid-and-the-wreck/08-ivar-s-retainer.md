## 8. Ivar's Retainer

The lamp in Ivar Lund's tent burned low, the way it always did when he was thinking instead of sleeping. He had told himself for twenty years that he was a man who slept well — a man with clean hands and an honest ledger, a salvager who raised what the water took and asked no questions about how it got down there in the first place. The river did not judge. That was the whole religion of salvage, and he had believed it the way other men believed in weather.

But tonight the ledger was open on his camp table, and the numbers would not sit still.

He turned the page with one blunt finger. Fuel. Wages. The charter fee owed to Dunmore Air. And there, in the column where it had no business being, a single entry from eleven months ago, written in his own careful hand: *Retainer received. Vessland Recovery Services. Consultation fee.*

Vessland Recovery Services. He said the name silently, tasting it again the way he had tasted it the first time, in a hotel bar in Kestrel Bay that smelled of cigar smoke and rain on hot pavement. He had thought then that it sounded like a law firm. He had thought, in the way a man thinks when he wants very much not to think, that the name didn't matter. Money doesn't have a smell. That was another article of faith, and like all articles of faith it existed precisely because it was tested daily.

* * *

Eleven months ago. He could still reconstruct the evening if he let himself, and he let himself now, because sleep had left him anyway and memory is a kind of work.

The man who had approached him called himself Mr. Aldous, though Ivar had learned since that the name appeared on no document anywhere in the world. He was small and neat and gray as driftwood, with the particular stillness of a man who has never once needed to raise his voice because other people raised theirs for him. He bought the second round without asking. He knew Ivar's rates. He knew the *Maren K* had sat rusting at her moorings through two bad seasons. He knew things a stranger should not know, and he wore that knowledge lightly, the way a card sharp wears a good suit.

"Reputation management," Aldous had said, turning his glass a quarter-turn on the wet coaster. "There's a plane lost upriver. Twenty years back. It belonged to people who are no longer — how shall I put this — organized. Our client has an interest in seeing that certain materials associated with the loss do not surface."

"Materials," Ivar had repeated.

"Documents. Correspondence. The usual paper a company generates over a lifetime." A small shrug, apologetic, almost humble. "Nothing dramatic. No bodies, Mr. Lund. We're not in that business. We're in the business of tidiness."

Tidiness. Ivar had been raising wrecks for thirty years and he had never once heard a sunken airplane described as untidy. But the fee was four times his best season, paid half in advance into an account he hadn't known he'd open, and the work itself — well, the work itself was nothing. Confirm a location. Retrieve a chest. Burn the contents unopened, or better, deliver them sealed to a designated contact and let someone else's hands do the burning. Deniability ran downhill in that organization like water; he understood that even then, even before he understood anything else. His job was to be the man who touched it last so that no one above him ever had to touch it at all.

He had asked exactly one question, and he remembered it now with a shame that sat in his chest like ballast: he had asked whether the contents were valuable.

"Sentimental only," Aldous had said, smiling for the first time, and the smile had been warm and brief and entirely false, and Ivar had taken the money anyway.

* * *

Outside the tent, the Kettle River talked to itself in the dark. Low water season; the channel had dropped nearly two meters since spring, and the wreck they had come three thousand kilometers to find lay exposed along its margin like something the river had grown tired of keeping. He could hear Petra's tent zip, hear Dek's cough, hear the small honest sounds of a camp full of people who trusted him.

That was the part that had begun to rot.

He had run crews for twenty years. He knew what trust weighed. It weighed more than any cargo he'd ever lifted out of cold water, and it was harder to keep dry. Petra Voss was nineteen years old and she had signed onto this expedition because she wanted to dive real wrecks, real deep history, and because she'd read about Astrid Vale in some salvaged archive and thought the dead pilot was brave. Dek Halvorsen watched everything and said little, which Ivar had filed under surveyor's temperament, though lately the file felt wrong, mislabeled, like a chart with the soundings transposed. And Orsen Dunmore flew them upriver through weather that would ground braver pilots, and looked at him sometimes across the camp stove with eyes that seemed to be waiting for him to say something true.

And he had built the whole thing on a lie he had told himself first.

Because here was the arithmetic he could no longer avoid, sitting alone with the lamp: Aldous had hired him to find a wreck that had already been found. Not might be found — *had been*. The retainer had come with coordinates. Precise ones. To the meter. Twenty-year-old river silt shifts channels every flood season; no archive on earth holds coordinates that fresh. Someone had known where that plane lay, recently, presently, and had paid Ivar Lund a small fortune to go and stand on top of it.

You don't pay a man to find something you've already found. You pay a man to be found *with* it.

He had understood this, he realized, for some time — had understood it in the wordless way you understand a hull is taking water before the gauge confirms it. He had simply declined to say it aloud to himself. Saying it aloud made it real, and real things required decisions, and decisions required a kind of courage he had spent thirty years not needing, because salvage asks only for patience and strong hands and the humility to lose arguments with rivers.

But then today, at the wreck, Petra had surfaced with blood in the water around her — just a nick, a scraped knuckle on the fuselage skin, nothing — and had climbed the ladder with her eyes lit like signal fires, and had said the words that had ended his ability to look away:

"There are tool marks on the frame, Ivar. Inside. Deliberate ones. Somebody cut that plane open from the inside before it went down."

Deliberate. From the inside. Before it went down.

He had gone quiet. He knew he had gone quiet; he had felt his own face close like a hatch. Petra had waited for him to be excited with her — she had earned his excitement, God knew, she had hung off a ladder in glacial water all afternoon — and instead he had given her silence, and silence from a captain reads as doubt, and he had watched the light in her dim by one degree and hated himself for it.

Because the tool marks fit. They fit the tidy story. A pilot locked out of her own controls, cutting her way toward something, or cutting something loose, or sealing something *in* — and then a plane in the river, and a nation erased from the maps, and a consortium so anxious about paper that it paid strangers a fortune to burn documents sight unseen and called the work reputation management.

No bodies, Aldous had said. We're not in that business.

Astrid Vale had been in that business. Astrid Vale had been the body.

* * *

Ivar got up and stood at the mouth of the tent. The night was clear and enormous, stars in the cold profusion you only get far from towns, the river a black ribbon stitched with moonlight. Somewhere upstream an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

He thought about his father, who had fished herring out of Hvalvik harbor for forty years and had one rule that he'd repeated so often it had worn smooth as sea glass: *Know whose boat you're pulling on.* Every net, every winch, every wage — know whose money moves your hands, because hands remember their owners long after the mind forgets. His father had meant it as advice against debt. Ivar had taken it as a joke about independence, and had spent his whole life proud of owing nobody anything.

Except he owed somebody now. Four times his best season, half paid in advance, and the balance held over him like a hook through the gill. He had told himself the retainer was finished — location confirmed, contract fulfilled, the rest of it never triggered. But men like Aldous did not pay balances; they deployed them. The second half of that money was not payment. It was a leash coiled neatly by the door, waiting for the day it would be picked up.

And what would the day ask of him? Burn the contents. Simple. Except the contents were testimony now — he was nearly sure of it, sure in the way you become sure of weather — testimony from a murdered woman, and the crew standing between him and the burning were a girl who idolized dead pilots and a quiet man who watched too carefully and a pilot who flew like the sky owed her money.

A decent man realizing his loyalty was purchased. That was the sentence his mind kept returning to, turning it over like a stone looking for what lived underneath. Because loyalty purchased isn't loyalty at all; it's inventory. Aldous had not bought his judgment or his courage or his care for his crew. Aldous had bought the *appearance* of those things, rented the shape of an honest man, and the shape had worked so well that Ivar himself had lived inside it comfortably for eleven months.

He went back inside and looked at the ledger again. Then, deliberately, he did something he had never done in thirty years of bookkeeping: he wrote in the margin, small, in pencil, where it could be erased but wouldn't be.

*Whose boat am I pulling on?*

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

Sleep came finally, shallow and gray, and broke apart before dawn with the sound of the radio crackling in the main tent — Orsen's voice, flat and professional, confirming the supply flight window, weather holding, two days maybe before the front moved through. Routine words. He lay listening to them and felt the old reflex rise, the reflex to manage, to smooth, to keep the machine running and the questions unasked.

Then he heard the second voice on the transmission, faint under the static, and it wasn't Orsen's.

It was clipped and unfamiliar and it used her call sign like it owned it, and something in its cadence — the slight pause before her name, the way the sentence ended a half-beat early, a door closed gently rather than shut — made the hair on his forearms lift. He had spent eleven months learning to hear that particular politeness. Men like Aldous were always polite right up until the moment they stopped needing to be.

The transmission ended. Footsteps outside, Orsen crossing toward the galley fire, unhurried, normal.

Ivar lay in the dark with his heart going hard and thought: *she heard it too.* He didn't know how he knew, but he knew — the same way you know a deck is shifting underfoot before anything slides. Something had arrived in their quiet camp riding a routine supply schedule, and whatever it was, Orsen Dunmore had gone very still around it, the way people go still when they're deciding whether to run.

He should ask her. That was the decent thing, the captain thing, the thing his father's ghost would demand. Ask her straight: who was that, and what aren't you telling us?

But asking meant answering too, and he was not yet ready to stand in front of his crew and say *I sold my hands eleven months ago and I'm only now reading the receipt.*

So instead he rose, stoked the stove, and put the coffee on — the small domestic liturgy of a camp that still believed in itself — and when Orsen came in from the cold with shadows under her eyes and a folded piece of paper she tucked away too quickly, he poured her a cup and said nothing at all.

Two days, the forecast said, before the front.

He had the sudden, sick certainty that they would not need all two.
