## 9. Halloran's Message

The supply flight came in on a Thursday, riding a tailwind out of the south and a smell of kerosene that clung to the air long after the wheels touched the gravel bar. Orsen heard it first as a change in the river's voice — a drone beneath the water-sound — and stepped out of her cabin with a mug of coffee going cold in her hand, watching the twin-engine grow from a speck to a shape against the low grey ceiling of cloud.

She knew the plane. That was the first wrongness. Supply runs up the Kettle were usually whatever Halloran could charter cheap: patched Cessnas, a tired Beaver with fabric peeling off its wingtips like old sunburn. This one was clean. A Navajo with fresh paint and no company markings at all, which in this country meant either money or trouble, and the two so often traveled together that she'd stopped distinguishing them years ago.

The pilot was young. He taxied up the bar with more confidence than the gravel deserved, killed the engines, and swung down with a crate of staples and diesel filters and a flat mail packet under his arm.

"Dunmore?" he said, though he'd never met her, and something about how he said it — checking it off a list rather than asking a question — put a cold finger on the back of her neck.

"That's me."

"Got your order. And this." He held out an envelope. Plain manila, no stamp, her name typed. "Told to put it in your hand. Not the mail bag."

"Who told you?"

He shrugged with the whole upper half of his body, a boy-shaped gesture that didn't quite fit the eyes above it. "Guy who paid for the fuel."

Orsen took the envelope. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. She signed his clipboard without reading it, watched him reload, watched him go — the Navajo climbing away south with that same unbothered confidence, threading the valley like it knew every turn before it made them — and then stood alone on the gravel bar with the river talking its endless talk and the envelope sitting in her palm like a stone pulled warm from a fire.

She didn't open it by the plane. She walked back up to the cabin first, checked the tree line out of habit, and only then slit it open with a filleting knife at her kitchen table.

Inside was a single sheet, typed, and a smaller card of heavy cream stock. The sheet was businesslike:

*Ms. Dunmore — Our mutual acquaintance extends his regards and notes that your current charter has proven productive beyond expectations. He further notes that certain items recovered from the Kettle River system remain outstanding, and that their prompt return would resolve certain financial obligations to everyone's satisfaction.*

*Should you prefer to handle this discreetly, instructions follow. Should you prefer otherwise, please consider the welfare of your passengers, whose insurance coverage is, we understand, somewhat informal.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The consortium's correspondence had a flavor she recognized the way you recognize a poison you've swallowed before — smooth going down, cold in the stomach, and then the long slow work of it through everything.

The cream card was smaller. On it, in precise block capitals, was a single line:

*THE CHEST. ALONE. FRIDAY NIGHT. OLD TRAPPER'S STRIP, 40 MILES DOWNRIVER. COME BY AIR. COME BY YOURSELF.*

* * *

She read it four times. Then she built a fire in the stove even though it wasn't cold, because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking, and sat with the note propped against the salt cellar while the flames found their rhythm.

They knew.

Not suspected. Knew. Knew the chest was out of the water, knew it was near her, knew enough about the crew's movements to time a supply flight to her strip. Which meant someone upstream or downstream was reporting. A radio operator at a relay camp. A bartender in Fort Meridian where pilots drank. Some pair of binoculars on some ridge she'd flown past without ever once looking up.

Twenty years of practiced invisibility, and it had taken them three weeks.

Her first instinct was the oldest one: run. Load the chest into the Otter tonight, tonight, before Friday, and go — north, east, anywhere the map went thin. But run where? The consortium owned fuel caches across half the territory. They owned debt, and debt was a leash longer than any range. And there was Petra, seventeen and fearless in the water; Ivar, decent and deceived; Dek, who watched everything and said less than he saw. If she ran, they'd follow the trail to her, and then they'd follow it to each other, and the consortium did not leave loose threads. She knew that better than anyone alive. She had been a loose thread once. She knew what it cost to have yourself cut free.

*Consider the welfare of your passengers.* They'd written it like a courtesy. That was the worst part. The menace was so well-mannered you could almost miss the teeth.

She got up and fed the fire another split of birch and made herself think like a pilot instead of a rabbit. Facts. One: they wanted the chest, not her — not yet. Two: they'd named a place and a time, which meant they expected her to comply, which meant they believed she still valued her own skin above all else. Three: they thought she was alone in this. As far as the note went, the crew was leverage, nothing more — a list of names on someone's clipboard.

That last fact gave her the only door in the room.

If she went alone — truly alone, telling no one, taking the chest out herself to the trapper's strip or anywhere else — then whatever happened at that strip happened to her. The crew stayed clean. Uninvolved. Safe in the particular way that people are safe who don't know enough to be dangerous.

It was a lie, of course. It was the same lie she'd been living for twenty years, just pointed at new people: *I am protecting you by keeping you ignorant.* She turned it over in her mind and found, as always, the flaw shining through it like a bone through skin — that ignorance hadn't protected anyone yet. Not Astrid Vale, dead in the river with her logbook cut off mid-sentence. Not Orsen herself, buried under a false name, paying interest on silence. Ignorance was what the consortium farmed. It grew their whole crop.

And still she chose it. Because the alternative was to sit Ivar down by the stove and say *here is what I found, here is what I owe, here is what I've done*, and watch the trust go out of his face like heat from an opened door. Because Petra looked at her like she hung the moon over water, and Orsen could not bear — literally could not bear, her chest closed around the thought like a fist — the idea of those young eyes changing.

So: alone. Friday night. But not to the trapper's strip.

She spread the sectional chart on the table and weighted the corners with the coffee mug, a spool of safety wire, the knife. Forty miles downriver, the old strip sat in a clearing that had been growing back toward forest for thirty years — one approach, over rising ground, no escape route except the way you came in. A place you flew into when you'd already agreed to lose.

But forty miles downriver was also within reach of the coast if you cut west over the divide, and the divide had weather coming — she'd smelled it in the wind for two days, that green mineral edge of a front building over the Pacific. Weather was a weapon nobody owned. If she lifted Thursday night instead of Friday, ahead of the front, ahead of their expectation, she could have the chest two hundred miles gone before anyone thought to check whether she'd kept the appointment.

Alone. In the dark. Through mountain passes she knew only in daylight, carrying cargo worth killing for, with a storm on her tail like a hound.

It was a bad plan. She catalogued its badnesses honestly, the way you check a landing gear twice when you're scared. Single aircraft, night, IMC likely, no flight plan filed because filed plans left records and records left trails. If the engine coughed over the divide she'd spend the rest of a very short life learning whether the mountains had gotten softer in twenty years.

Against that: the crew, asleep in their tents at the wreck site, trusting her. Against that: Astrid Vale's logbook drying page by page in her cabin, its final entry still waiting for her like a letter she was afraid to open. Against that: twenty years of being no one, and the sudden, unbearable discovery that she no longer wished to be no one badly enough to keep paying for it.

She began to pack.

* * *

The next two days she wore her ordinary face like a mask fitted by a master. She flew the crew to the site each morning — the short hop up the Kettle, the wreck's exposed wing glinting in the shallows like a fish turning — and made small talk and checked fuel states and laughed at Dek's dry observations and let Petra talk her ear off about lift bags and bottom conditions. She ate their food. She drank Ivar's terrible coffee, boiled twice and proud of it. Twice Petra asked her why she seemed quiet, and twice Orsen said *tired, that's all*, and watched the girl accept it, and felt the lie settle into her chest another inch, the way silt settles in a harbor — grain by grain, harmless-looking, until one day nothing draws water there anymore.

Thursday evening she told them she needed to run down to Fort Meridian for parts — a fuel pump gasket, nothing dramatic — and would overnight at her cabin and rejoin them Saturday morning. Ivar nodded. Dek looked at her a moment too long, the way he did, saying nothing, and she held his gaze with the flat calm of twenty years' practice and thought: *you see everything, old man. See this. Please see this, and please say nothing,* though she couldn't have said what she wanted him to see, or what she'd do if he said it.

At the cabin she loaded by lantern light. The chest went in wrapped in a tarp, lashed behind the rear seats where a load of freight would sit. The logbook went inside her jacket, against her ribs, because the chest could be replaced by a bargain if it came to that but the logbook could not be replaced at all. Fuel topped off. Flashlight, flares, the chart folded to the western crossing. She sat in the pilot's seat a long moment with the doors open and the night breathing in around her, moose-smell and river-damp and the first real bite of the coming front in the wind, and let herself feel the fear properly, all of it, head to heel — because a pilot who lies to herself about fear dies of it, and she intended, whatever else happened, to fly well tomorrow.

From the tent camp upriver, faint on the wind, came the sound of Petra laughing at something. It carried the way sound carries over water at night, clear and close and impossibly far away.

Orsen closed the cabin door. She did not look at the stove where the cream-colored card still lay. She walked down to the plane, climbed in, and reached for the starter — and that was when she saw, in the wash of her own landing light sweeping the tree line, the figure standing at the edge of the gravel bar.

Dek. Hands in his pockets. Watching.

He didn't move. He didn't call out. He simply stood there in the light and the dark at once, an old man silhouetted against black spruce, and waited — for her to explain, or to lie, or to ask him aboard. The engine caught and settled into its idle rumble, and the propeller began to blur, and Orsen Dunmore sat with her hand on the throttle and the chest lashed behind her and the whole dark river of her choices running fast around her, and did not yet know which way to push it.
