## 10. The Night Flight

Orsen woke before the alarm she hadn't set, the way she always did when a plan was coiled tight in her chest — some older instinct than thought pulling her up out of sleep like a fish on a line. The cabin was dark and cold enough that her breath showed faintly in the gray light leaking through the frost-furred window. Three-forty by the tin clock on the shelf, its hands glowing a dull green. She lay still for a moment, listening.

The camp was quiet. Petra's breathing through the plank wall, slow and even. Ivar's snore, low and rhythmic as a pump. Somewhere out under the lean-to tarp, Dek's tent flap had been tied down against the wind that had come up at dusk from the northwest, smelling of ice and far-off snow. The wind was still there now, working at the eaves, worrying the loose corner of the stovepipe with a small metallic worry-worry-worry that Orsen had learned to hear past and tonight could not.

She dressed in the dark. Wool first, then the canvas over-shirt, then the flight jacket whose collar still carried, if you pressed your face into it and lied to yourself, a ghost of kerosene and river water that no amount of washing would kill. She did not turn on the lamp. She knew this room the way she knew the inside of her own mouth — where the floorboard groaned, where the rifle leaned, where the duffel sat with the chest inside it wrapped in her spare tarp and lashed with cord she'd spliced herself so it wouldn't rattle.

The chest weighed what a dead man weighs. She'd carried it out to the woodshed three nights ago, buried under split spruce, and every day since she had walked past it twice and felt it the way you feel a tooth you've decided not to have looked at. Now she crouched over it in the black of the shed, cut the cord, and lifted it, and its weight went up through her arms and settled somewhere behind her sternum, where she supposed she'd be carrying it regardless.

*One run,* she told herself. *Up the lake chain before first light, over the Kettle divide while the air's still cold and smooth, down to Meridian Flats. Dek's exile court, or whatever it is. Hand it off. Be back before the crew's coffee boils.*

*And then what?*

She didn't answer that. She'd gotten very good, these ten years, at not answering questions asked in her own voice.

Outside, the world had gone soft. The fog was already standing in the trees when she came around the corner of the cabin with the chest on her shoulder — not the ground kind that burns off by seven, but the river kind, rising off the open water below the rapids where the current never froze, climbing, thickening, eating the stars one by one. Above the ridgeline there were still a few hard points of light, but down along the strip of gravel she called an airstrip the visibility was closing like a hand.

She stood in the dark and looked at it and made the calculation she'd been making since midnight: cold air, calm surface, fog localized to the river valley. She could climb out over the ridge before she touched any of it. She'd flown out of worse. She'd flown out of worse with men shooting at her, though that was a sentence she kept in a locked room in her head and visited only when she couldn't sleep anyway.

The Otter sat on its mooring wheels at the top of the strip, gray-white in the last starlight, dew already silvering the struts. She worked fast and quiet: fuel check by feel, control surfaces free, the chest lashed into the cargo bay behind the second row of seats where it wouldn't show unless someone opened the door and looked, which no one would, because everyone was asleep, because she had planned it that way, because planning was the one thing she still trusted.

She was cinching the last strap when she heard the gravel shift.

Not a footstep. Gravel doesn't announce footsteps; it announces weight settling, someone stopping rather than starting. Orsen went absolutely still, one hand on the strap, and turned her head.

Dek stood at the edge of the strip where the alders began, maybe thirty feet away, wrapped in his coat, his surveyor's clipboard — the prop, the fiction, whatever it was — tucked under one arm like a hymnal. He wasn't sneaking. He wasn't advancing. He was just standing there in the dark with his breath showing, looking at her, and he had been looking long enough that the watching had a texture to it, patient and complete.

Her heart did something complicated and painful. She let none of it reach her face. Ten years of practice: the face was a hangar door, and behind it nothing flew.

"Couldn't sleep," she said. Kept her voice level, bored, slightly annoyed — the voice of a woman doing preflight chores at an inconvenient hour because that was simply the life she led. "Fog'll burn off late today. Figured I'd get ahead of it."

Dek said nothing.

That was the thing about him. In two weeks of flying him up creeks and over moraines while he filled his little notebooks, she had catalogued everything about him except the thing that mattered, and now, standing in the dark with a sealed chest stamped with a nation that didn't exist lashed six feet behind her seat, she understood that he had been doing exactly the same to her, and had finished first.

He didn't ask what was in the plane. He didn't ask why she was loading cargo at four in the morning instead of checking fuel. He didn't ask anything. He just watched, and the watching was worse than any question, because a question you could answer and a lie you could tell, but silence was a mirror and she could see herself in it: a woman in the dark, sweating despite the cold, cinching a strap with hands that wanted to shake, telling a story to a man who had already stopped buying.

"Wind's out of the northwest," Dek said at last. Not a question either. Just information, offered flatly, the way he read off elevations. "River fog'll thicken before dawn. It always does this time of year."

"I know this country."

"I know you do." A pause. His breath plumed and dissolved. "Fly safe, Orsen."

And he turned and walked back toward the tents, unhurried, gravel crunching softly, until the alders took him, and Orsen stood alone on the strip with her pulse hammering in her ears and the certain knowledge that she had just been seen — not caught, not accused, but *seen*, which was somehow more frightening, because being caught meant a confrontation and confrontation meant words and words were territory she could defend. Being seen left nothing to argue with.

She should have unloaded the chest. She knew that even as she climbed into the pilot's seat and pulled the door shut on its worn latch. She knew it the way you know you shouldn't touch a hot stove, cleanly and completely, and she touched it anyway, because the plan was all she had and a plan abandoned is a person exposed, and she had spent ten years learning that exposure kills.

The engine caught on the third swing of the primer, coughed, caught properly, settled into its familiar uneven thunder. The sound rolled down the valley and came back off the ridge, and somewhere in the camp a dog — there was no dog, but something stirred anyway, a tent zipper, a cough — registered it and let it go.

She taxied to the end of the strip with her lights off. The fog was worse than it had been ten minutes ago. That was the thing about river fog: it didn't negotiate. It arrived. Already the far end of the strip was a rumor, the black line of the timber dissolving into a paleness that had no bottom to it, and above, where the ridge should have been, there was only a suggestion of higher darkness, like a memory of mountains.

*You can still turn around.*

She ran up the engine instead. Full power, tail coming light, the gravel hissing under the tires, the Otter accelerating into a wall of white that swallowed her windshield whole — and there it was, the old arithmetic, the one that had kept her alive through years she never talked about: *speed is safety, motion is safety, keep moving and nothing can pin you down.* She pulled back on the yoke at the point she'd chosen blind, trusting the strip's length the way you trust your own heartbeat, and the wheels left the earth, and for eleven seconds she was flying.

Then the fog took her.

It wasn't gradual. One moment there was dim starlight through the murk and the loom of the ridge off her left wing; the next there was nothing, a whiteness so total it had texture, pressing against the glass like wet wool, and the horizon — the thing every pilot is married to, the fixed point of the whole profession — simply ceased to exist. Her inner ear, that traitor, immediately began insisting she was in a climbing right turn. The needle on the artificial horizon said otherwise. She trusted the instrument the way she trusted nothing else on earth and held the wings level by faith alone, climbing, climbing, sweat running cold down her spine despite the chill, the engine noise enormous and blind.

At eight hundred feet she broke out.

The stars were waiting above the fog deck like a city seen from offshore — hard, brilliant, indifferent, spread from one edge of the sky to the other, and the moonless dark between them was clean and cold and utterly silent after the roar of the climb. Below her the fog stretched away in every direction, a luminous gray sea with the ridge peaks standing out of it like islands, their snowpatches catching starlight. It was, she thought, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and she felt nothing at all.

That was the crack. Not the fog, not the near-miss, not Dek's eyes in the dark. It was this: alone above the cloud deck with the chest behind her and the whole night hers, she felt nothing. No relief. No triumph. Just the engine drone and the cold and the growing, sickening understanding that she had done this before — fled upward, alone, in the dark, carrying something heavy — and that the last time she'd done it, a woman named Astrid Vale had been the one left behind in the wreckage.

*You're not fleeing toward anything,* she thought. *You're just fleeing.*

Meridian Flats was ninety miles northeast. The route crossed the Kettle divide, then forty miles of featureless muskeg where, if the fog pooled in the basins the way it did on still October nights, there would be no landmarks at all, no lights, nothing but the compass and her own increasingly suspect judgment. She'd flown it a hundred times by day. By night, alone, VFR into country with no lights and no rescue for hours in any direction, with a fog deck below her that was thickening by the minute —

She checked the fuel. She checked the clock. She did the arithmetic again, honestly this time, the way Dek would have done it, the way Astrid Vale would have done it, and the honest number came back the same in every column: marginal. Marginal fuel, marginal weather, marginal margin for error, and a cargo she could not afford to lose and a body the territory could not afford to lose either, though the territory had never much cared what anyone could afford.

Ahead, the divide's highest saddle — the gap she had to thread — showed a smudge of gray on its leeward side that hadn't been there five minutes ago. Fog spilling over the pass like water over a dam. In twenty minutes that spill would fill the whole eastern basin, and she'd be crossing it with the deck rising to meet her, hoping to punch through on the far side, hoping the flats were clear, hoping, hoping — the currency of pilots who die of it.

Orsen held her heading for another full minute. Two. Long enough that it counted as trying. Then she banked south, gently, the wings tilting against the stars, and began the descent toward the only hole in the fog she knew: the river channel below her own strip, where the open water kept a seam of clearer air, thin and unreliable, but a seam.

The letdown was the worst part. Going back into the white, trading the clean starlight for the wool-pressed blindness, instruments only, hands light on the yoke, listening to the engine and the wind and her own blood. The fog deck rose to meet her the way water rises over a sinking man. At three hundred feet she saw it — the black ribbon of the river, materializing out of the murk like a vein surfacing under skin — and followed it downstream, half a mile, a mile, until the pale gravel of her strip slid into view beneath the left wing, and she set it down in the space between two fog banks with a gentleness that surprised her, as if the airplane deserved better than what she'd just asked of it.

She taxied to the top of the strip and shut down, and the silence closed over her like river water.

For a long time she didn't move. The engine ticked as it cooled. The fog breathed against the windows. Behind her, in the dark of the cargo bay, the chest sat exactly where she'd lashed it, patient as a stone, going nowhere.

*The plan failed.* She made herself think it plainly, without varnish. *The plan failed and you're alive and the chest is still here and Halloran's deadline is four days out and Dek saw you.*

Dek saw you.

She turned that over, and it wouldn't sit still. If he'd wanted to stop her, he'd had every chance — a shout, a hand on the strut, a word to Ivar. If he'd wanted to report her, the report was already written. Instead he'd told her the wind direction and wished her safe flying, and walked away, and the meaning of that was a door standing open in a wall she'd believed solid, and she was afraid to look through it because of what it might mean: that he knew what she was carrying, and had known for days, and had been waiting — patiently, the way he did everything — for her to stop running on her own.

*Secrecy is safety.* She'd built ten years on that foundation, stone by careful stone. And tonight the fog had taken one look at it and laughed, because secrecy hadn't kept her safe; it had put her alone in the dark at four in the morning, hauling a dead nation's evidence through weather that nearly killed her, lying to the first people in a decade who'd been kind to her, while the truth sat in a tent fifty yards away wearing a surveyor's clipboard and waiting for her with more patience than she had left.

Her hands were shaking now, finally, now that it was over and no one could see. She pressed them flat against the yoke until they stilled.

She got out. Unlashed the chest — it took longer than it should have, her fingers stupid with cold and adrenaline — and shouldered it, and carried it back through the fog to the woodshed, and buried it under the split spruce again, and stood there a moment in the reeking dark with her palm flat on the tarp, feeling through it the hard edge of the seal.

"You win," she told it quietly. "For now."

Back at the cabin, the gray was beginning. Not sunrise — just the world admitting it existed. Through the window she could see the camp stirring: Petra emerging from her tent, hair wild, breath pluming, stretching toward the pale sky with the unselfconscious ease of someone twenty-three and unburdened. Ivar's stovepipe was already breathing smoke. And at the edge of the alders, Dek stood with a tin cup in both hands, facing the strip, facing her cabin, not pretending otherwise.

When their eyes met through the glass, he raised the cup an inch. Not a toast. An acknowledgment. *I know you went. I know you came back. Whenever you're ready.*

Orsen stood in her cold kitchen with the fog pressing at the windows and the chest rotting under the spruce and four days on Halloran's clock, and felt the first true fissure run through the wall she'd lived behind — not collapse, not yet, but a crack wide enough to see daylight through, and the daylight looked like a camp stove and three people who trusted her, and it terrified her more than the fog ever had.

She put the kettle on, and went out to face the morning.
