## 11. Astrid's Last Entry

The lamp burned low and yellow in the cabin, and outside the wind had settled into that long winter patience that made the whole valley feel like a held breath. Orsen sat at her table with the logbook open before her, its pages spread like something dissected.

She had been drying them for three days now — interleaved with blotting paper, weighted under flat stones from the riverbank, turned twice a day with tweezers because her fingers were too coarse and too eager and she'd already torn one corner early on. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft as moth wing where the water had worked longest. Ink had run in places into gray ghosts of themselves. But Astrid Vale, it turned out, had written like a woman who expected her words to be read by enemies: small, even, pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath. Where the ink washed out, the grooves remained. Orsen had learned to tilt the pages against the lamplight and read the wounds the pen had left in the paper, the way you could read old tracks in snow after the snow itself had melted.

She'd learned other things too, these three nights. That Astrid took her coffee black and complained about it anyway. That she counted her fuel in liters and her luck in smaller units than that. That she was afraid — not in the way most pilots were afraid, which was of weather and engines and the particular silence of a propeller stopping — but in the way of a person being hunted who has decided to keep flying regardless.

*Someone circled my field at 0300 again,* one entry said. *Same engine note as Tuesday. A Continental, I think. Whoever it is wants me to know they're there.*

Orsen read that line four times. She knew that engine note herself. She knew what it meant to lie awake listening to someone tell you, politely, over and over, *we can reach you.*

She poured a finger of whiskey she wasn't supposed to have, didn't drink it, just held the glass for the warmth. The stove ticked. Somewhere under the floor a mouse went about its small economy. She turned the page.

* * *

The middle entries were the ones that made her hands unsteady.

Astrid had been carrying cargo out of Vessland — or what had been Vessland; on the maps it was all consortium territory now, the border erased so cleanly that schoolchildren in three countries were taught the land had always belonged to whoever held the deeds. The logbook didn't say *evidence.* It said things like *the papers* and *the survey folios* and, once, in a hand pressed harder than usual, *the borders.*

*I met with the old surveyor today,* Astrid wrote, six days before the end. *He is eighty-one years old and he walked eleven kilometers through the forest to bring me his father's field notes because he trusted me more than he trusts any living institution. Eleven kilometers, at eighty-one. He wept when he handed them over. Not for himself. For the lines. He said: they drew us off the map, but the map was never the country. The country remembers where it ends.*

Orsen set the glass down. Her own hands weren't steady either, and she noticed this the way you notice weather changing — distantly at first, then all at once.

She had spent ten years telling herself a story about the chest, about the wreck, about the whole drowned business upriver. The story was simple: a plane went down, a pilot died, accidents happened in the Kettle country every season, and whatever grief or guilt she carried about the edges of it was hers alone, private, harmless. She had built her life inside that story the way you build a cabin — carefully, plank by plank, until you forgot there was ever anything outside its walls.

But here was the surveyor's ghost saying otherwise. Here was Astrid Vale writing, in grooves the water couldn't erase: *If I make it across, the borders go back on the map. If I don't, someone else carries them. There are enough of us now that killing me would only be an edit, not an ending.*

They had killed her anyway. And then they had edited.

* * *

It was near midnight when she reached the last legible pages, and the cold had crept in around the stove's diminishing heat, and she did not get up to feed it because some part of her understood, in the animal way, that what she was about to read was going to require her full attention and possibly her full courage.

The final week of Astrid's life, day by day:

*Day minus five:* *Refueled at Kettle Fork. The man at the fuel dock asked twice where I was headed. Twice. He's been there nine years and never once cared where anyone was headed.*

*Day minus four:* *Dumped the decoy crates at the mouth of the Gray as instructed. My handler says the consortium's people will find them and stop looking. I don't like deceiving even the men trying to kill me. It feels like spending money I'll need later.*

So there had been decoys. Orsen thought of the salvage crew, of Ivar Lund's courteous questions, of the empty crate slot Petra would eventually find or wouldn't. Someone had spent twenty years looking for crates that were never there. And somewhere else — somewhere the consortium's divers had never thought to look — the real papers might still be waiting, patient as seed.

*Day minus two:* *I have moved the true cargo. Location recorded nowhere except here, and here is written so that only a pilot who has flown the Kettle in low water will understand it. If you are reading this and you do not understand, that is the design. Fly the river yourself first. Then come back.*

Orsen read the passage that followed — coordinates braided into landmarks, distances given as engine-times rather than miles, a route described the way only a bush pilot would describe it: *past where the channel splits at the burned snag, down to where the gravel bar shows its teeth in August, to the standing water under the cut bank where the cold comes up green.* She read it and felt the hair rise on her arms, because she knew that water. She had landed on that gravel bar. She had drunk coffee on that cut bank, alone, years ago, for no reason she could ever explain to anyone, drawn there by nothing she could name.

The river hadn't handed her the chest by accident. Low water had exposed the wing; fine. But she had been flying that back channel for a decade without knowing why.

*Day minus one:* *Tomorrow I fly north with the folios sewn into the seat cushions and a chest of worthless papers in the cargo hold, because the consortium watches cargo holds. My contact across the line will meet me at—*

The sentence ended there. Not mid-word — mid-*name*. The next line was a smear of gray where the water had taken the ink entirely, and beneath it, the last entry, written in a different pressure of pen, hurried, the grooves deep and slightly slanted as though the plane were already moving:

*Engine check complete. Weather holding. If anyone reads this, I was not lost. I was found out. Tell my daughter the difference matters.*

Then nothing. Blank pages. The embossed ghost of a signature half-finished — *Astrid V* — and the pen lifted forever.

Orsen sat for a long time with her palm flat on the page, as if she could hold the woman still, keep her on the ground, rewrite the morning. The stove had gone nearly cold. Her whiskey had gone warm. Outside, the wind had stopped entirely, and the silence was enormous.

*Tell my daughter the difference matters.*

Somewhere in the world there was a daughter who had been told, presumably, that her mother was lost. Lost: passive, blameless, an act of weather. Found out: hunted, deliberate, murdered. Twenty years of a lie told gently to a child, and here was the correction, in pencil-grooves, in a drowned book, in Orsen's kitchen.

* * *

She should have stopped there. Any sensible woman would have banked the fire and slept and let the rest wait for daylight. But the logbook had one more thing to give her, and it gave it the way rivers give up bodies — slowly, face-down, at the worst possible hour.

In the back pages, past the flight logs, Astrid had kept notes on the aircraft that shadowed her. Pilot habit: know your pursuer's machine better than your own. There was a sketch of a high-wing monoplane with a modified exhaust stack. A registration fragment, half-legible: *…-K-7*. And beside it, in Astrid's tight hand:

*Consortium contract pilot. Flies out of Halloran's strip. Name: Corben Ashe. He waved at me on the taxiway yesterday. He wanted me to see him wave.*

The name went through Orsen like a fishhook finding flesh.

Corben Ashe.

She knew the name the way you know a scar's history — not by thinking about it but by touching it accidentally and feeling everything underneath. Corben Ashe had flown the consortium's runs through the Kettle country in the years when Orsen was young and broke and newly nameless, flying under papers that weren't hers, keeping her head down, keeping her mouth shut. Ashe had been the senior man on those runs. He'd taught her shortcuts. He'd bought her drinks at Halloran's bar and told her, laughing, that the first rule of this country was *don't ask what's in the boxes.* She'd obeyed that rule for ten years. She'd built her entire second life on the foundation of that rule.

And Corben Ashe — cheerful, competent, generous-with-shortcuts Corben Ashe — had been the man in the high-wing monoplane. The man with the Continental engine who circled fields at 0300 to let a woman know she was watched. The man who, on a clear morning twenty years ago, had put rounds into a floatplane over the Kettle River and then filed a report about mechanical failure and collected his fee and gone on waving at taxiways.

Orsen stood up. Her legs didn't quite agree to it, and she caught the table edge, and the lamp flame shivered and threw her shadow huge and wrong across the wall. She stood in her own cabin, in her own careful invisible life, and understood — fully, finally, with no place left to hide from it — that she had known him. Had liked him. Had accepted his advice, his drinks, his protection on the bad strips. Had laughed at his jokes while he was, by then or soon after, a murderer on retainer.

And worse — the thought arrived not as a realization but as a verdict, something already long since decided, merely now delivered — she had heard things, back then. Fragments. Ashe talking to Halloran in the office with the door half-closed. The word *Vessland* spoken once, contemptuously, like a stain on a carpet. A mention of a female pilot who was asking too many questions and would be *handled.* She had been twenty-four years old and terrified and she had walked out of that office and told herself she'd imagined it, misheard it, and she had kept flying their boxes and never asking, and the rule had held her up like a floor for ten years.

Her silence hadn't been neutrality. It had been ballast. It had helped keep the whole rotten aircraft stable and level and on course.

Astrid Vale had died carrying a nation's borders in her seat cushions, and Orsen Dunmore — the woman who now called herself nothing, who had paid so dearly for her buried past — had refueled the killers' planes, kept their secrets, and called it survival.

She went to the window. The stars over the Kettle country were hard and close, and the river ran black below them, keeping what it kept. Somewhere downstream, in a town with a fuel dock and a bar, Halloran was sleeping the sleep of a man whose ledgers balanced. Somewhere farther, Corben Ashe might even still be alive, old now, maybe, retired, waving at somebody's taxiway.

*Tell my daughter the difference matters.*

Orsen pressed her forehead against the cold glass and breathed until the fog of her breath filled the pane and faded and filled it again.

She could not give Astrid back her morning. But the logbook said the true cargo was still out there, hidden in pilot's language along a river Orsen knew better than anyone alive. And tomorrow — or the day after, or whenever the salvage crew's charter brought them upriver — she would be flying that stretch of water with three strangers who trusted her, one of whom was almost certainly reporting to the same consortium that had bought her silence once and was preparing to buy it again.

The lamp guttered. Orsen looked at the logbook lying open on the table, at the half-finished signature, and made herself a promise with no comfort in it: *I will finish the sentence.*

She was still standing at the window when the first gray came up over the eastern ridge — and with it, far off downriver, faint but unmistakable, the sound of an engine climbing out of Halloran's strip into the cold morning air.
