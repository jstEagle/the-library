## 12. Dek's Ledger

Dek had counted the days the way other men counted money — carefully, and with a private ledger no one else was permitted to see.

Twenty years, four months, eleven days since the Vessland court-in-exile, sitting in a rented hall in a city that wasn't home to anyone in it, had opened a file with a single line in it: *Astrid Vale, pilot, missing presumed lost, Kettle River drainage, cargo unaccounted for.* He had been twenty-six then, a junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs and a conviction that paperwork could outlast guns. The older men on the commission had smiled at him the way you smile at a child who believes in something harmless. Two of them were dead now. One had taken consortium money and retired to a coast he never named. The file had passed to Dek like an inheritance nobody wanted, and he had carried it through nine countries and three false professions, and now he carried it up a river in the hold of a floatplane, disguised as survey notes.

He watched Orsen Dunmore across the morning fire and added another entry to the ledger.

She was mending a torn tie-down strap, working the needle through wet canvas with the economy of someone who wasted nothing — not motion, not word, not feeling. Her hands were good hands. He'd noticed that the first day, at Halloran's strip, when she'd shaken his own: calloused in the right places, steady, and held back just slightly, like a handshake that had been taught to keep secrets. Everything about her was like that. Competent, generous within limits, and held back. A woman living behind glass she'd installed herself.

Petra was down at the water rinsing dive gear, humming something tuneless. Ivar had gone upriver with the canoe to set markers on the channel. The camp smelled of spruce smoke and coffee gone bitter on the coals, and the river talked to itself in its gravel voice, low and constant, saying nothing and everything.

Dek turned his tin cup slowly on his knee. He had rehearsed this conversation in eleven languages over twenty years, and none of the rehearsals survived contact with her actual face.

"You fly like you've done it before," he said.

Orsen didn't look up from the strap. "That's usually how flying works."

"I mean before here." He kept his tone idle, the way you'd discuss weather. "Before this river. Before the name Dunmore."

The needle stopped. Just for a half-second — a hesitation so small most people would have missed it, but Dek had spent two decades training himself not to miss half-seconds. They were where the truth lived, in the gaps between a person's sentences.

"Everyone's from somewhere before," she said, and pulled the stitch tight.

"Not everyone flies like the sky owes them something."

She looked up then. Her eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and they did the thing they always did when he pushed too near the hull of her: they went flat and reflective, showing him his own face instead of letting him see hers. *Careful,* the eyes said. *You're looking at the wrong thing.*

But she said nothing, and neither did he, and the fire popped, and somewhere down the channel Petra laughed at a kingfisher or a fish or nothing at all.

* *

He made his move that evening, after supper, when Ivar had rowed out to check the markers by lantern light and Petra had fallen asleep sitting up over her dive log, pencil still in her hand, young enough to sleep anywhere and mean it as a compliment to the world. Orsen was washing pans in the shallows, sleeves rolled, forearms pink in the cold water, and Dek walked down to stand a few feet off on the stones, close enough to talk, far enough that the conversation would have room to be what it needed to be.

"The wreck's not where your chart says it should be," he said.

Her hands went still in the water. She didn't turn around. "Charts are old."

"These are mine." He let that sit. "I drew them myself. Fourteen years ago, from the salvage reports. The impact point is two hundred meters downstream of where we've been diving. Which means either the plane moved after it went in—" he paused, watching her shoulders, watching them not move, watching how hard she worked to make them not move, "—or someone found it first and moved the story instead."

The river filled the silence. Water slid over her wrists. When she finally spoke, her voice was level, almost amused, and Dek admired the craftsmanship of it even as he catalogued it as evidence.

"You've been reading too many of your own survey notes, Dek. Hypoxia does strange things to a man."

"Astrid Vale," he said.

This time she did turn around. Slowly, water dripping off her fingers, the last light going copper behind her head so that her face was all shadow and her stillness was all there was of her. And in that stillness he saw it — not guilt, exactly, and not fear. Something older and more tired than either. Recognition. The look of a woman who has been waiting years for a knock on the door and is almost relieved to finally hear it.

"Say that name again," she said quietly.

"Astrid Vale. Expedition pilot. Shot down over the Kettle River in—" 

"—autumn," Orsen said. "It was autumn. The report says spring. Everyone always gets it wrong." She stopped, hearing herself, and closed her eyes briefly, and Dek felt something twist in his chest that was not triumph. It was closer to grief. Twenty years he had wanted this moment, and now that it stood in front of him dripping river water, he understood that for her it was not a door opening. It was a wall coming down, and walls fall on people.

* *

They sat on the overturned canoe above the tideline of stones, and Dek took out the ledger.

Not the fake one — not the survey book with its careful columns of depths and bearings that he showed Ivar every evening. The real one. Leather, cracked at the spine, swollen from being rained on in six countries. He opened it on his knees to the page he knew by touch, the way you know a page you've read five hundred times, and turned it toward her.

She didn't take it. She read it where it lay, arms wrapped around her knees, and he watched her face by lantern light as she read, and it was like watching ice come off a lake in fast motion — crack by crack, something frozen coming loose beneath her skin.

"It's a list," she said at last. Her voice came out rough.

"It's a ledger. Every person who ever touched this case. Every payment, every silence, every document that surfaced and was bought back or burned or buried." He touched the entries one by one, gently, like a man touching graves. "The magistrate who ruled it an accident — paid. The reporter who started asking questions in '09 — paid, then disappeared, then paid again, his family. The diver who claimed he'd seen the wreck in '13 — paid. The charter company that destroyed its own maintenance logs — paid." His finger stopped on an entry near the bottom of the page, and he made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness; everyone in this story had been lied to with such elegance. "And the courier. The man who moved the payoff money north the week Astrid died. Who delivered the order to the pilot who flew the intercept. That entry has no name. Only a mark."

Orsen had gone very white. In the lantern light the color left her face like water leaving a lock, all at once, and her hand rose halfway to her mouth and stopped.

"You know the mark," Dek said. Not a question.

"I've seen it." Barely a whisper. "Once. A long time ago. On papers I was paid not to read."

"And whose papers were those?"

She looked at him then, fully, and the flat reflective surface of her eyes was gone, broken up like wind on water, and underneath it he saw the woman the glass had been built to hide: frightened, furious, and so lonely it came off her like heat off stone.

"That's my business," she said. "That's mine."

"Yes," Dek agreed. "It is. All of it is yours. That's rather the point."

* *

He told her the rest because she had earned the rest, and because a choice offered without full information isn't a choice, it's a trap, and he had spent twenty years refusing to be anyone's trap.

He told her about the exile court — the small, stubborn institution that met in borrowed halls in cities that would have them, keeping alive the legal fiction that Vessland still existed somewhere, if only on paper, if only in testimony. He told her about the borders erased from every atlas, redrawn overnight by treaty and money while the world looked elsewhere, and about the consortium that had engineered the erasure and profited from it and hunted, for two decades, every scrap of paper that could prove it. He told her that Astrid Vale hadn't been carrying cargo. She had been carrying a country — documents, maps, sworn statements, the whole evidentiary skeleton of a nation that could be reassembled if anyone ever got it into a courtroom.

And he told her what he suspected, watching her face as he said it, watching her already know it: that the chest was real, that it had surfaced with the low water, that Orsen Dunmore had found it weeks ago and told no one, and that she had been carrying it around inside herself like a coal, burning alone, because she did not know whom to trust and had learned, long ago and expensively, that trusting was the thing that cost.

"How do you know?" she asked finally. "About me finding it. You can't know."

"I know you stopped swimming at the back channel after the third day. I know you came back from that walk with river silt under your fingernails and a look on your face like a woman who'd seen a ghost and liked it better than the living. I know you've barely slept in two weeks and you check the southern sky every time an engine passes." He closed the ledger. "I don't need to know where you hid it. I only need to know you have it. Do you?"

The question hung between them. Down at the waterline, a loon called, that wild wavering note that sounds like laughter and mourning at once. Orsen stared into the middle distance for a long time, and Dek waited, because waiting was the one skill twenty years had perfected in him.

"If I do," she said at last, carefully, testing each word like thin ice, "what happens? If I give it to you. What happens to me?"

"That depends on which thing you give me."

"Explain."

Dek set the ledger aside and turned to face her fully, and he chose his next words the way she probably chose her landings — reading the ground twice, committing once.

"There are two ways this goes," he said. "The first: you hand the chest to me tonight, anonymously, and vanish. I take it to the court. Testimony gets read into the record. Borders get argued about in halls you'll never see. And you stay invisible. New name, new river, new glass house. No one hunts you, because no one knows you were ever anything. You spend the rest of your life being very good at not existing." He paused. "I've watched people do it. It works. That's the honest selling point. It works right up until the day it doesn't, and then it costs everything at once."

"And the second way?"

"The second way, you testify." He said it simply, without ornament, because ornament would have insulted her. "Under your true name. On the record. Your knowledge of the courier mark, whatever you saw, whatever you were — all of it entered as evidence. The court can shield witnesses. It's not much of a shield. It's a legal fiction protecting a legal fiction. But names spoken into a record are hard to un-speak. The consortium burns documents. It cannot burn testimony once it's read aloud in front of thirty witnesses and a stenographer." He looked at her. "The second way ends your hiding. Permanently. Either because you won't need to hide anymore — or because you'll be running for the rest of your life. I genuinely don't know which. Anyone who promises you otherwise is selling something."

Orsen was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed — short, harsh, surprised out of her.

"You're offering me a choice between disappearing forever and being seen forever, and you honestly think that's a hard one."

"I think it's the hardest one there is," Dek said. "I've offered it to four people in twenty years. Three took the chest-and-vanish option. One testified." He was silent a beat. "She writes to me sometimes. From a place I'm not allowed to say. She says she sleeps badly but she sleeps as herself. The other three — I don't know where they are. Nobody does. That was the point, and it was also the price."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Somewhere inland an owl asked its one question and got no answer. Orsen pulled her jacket tighter around herself, and when she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the practiced levelness, the voice of the woman under the glass.

"You said the courier's entry has no name. Only a mark." She was looking at the coals, not at him. "If I told you I could put a name to that mark. If I told you I knew that courier — knew him well enough that his name in open testimony wouldn't just prove the murder, it would break the whole chain, payments, orders, all of it, because everyone above him would suddenly be reachable." Her jaw tightened. "Would the court want that?"

Dek felt his pulse knock once against his throat. Twenty years of ledger entries, and here was a woman on an overturned canoe offering him the keystone.

"The court," he said, "would want it more than it has wanted anything since the day the maps were redrawn."

"Then I need a night," Orsen said. "One night. There's something I have to finish reading first. There's a sentence in that logbook that cuts off mid-word, and I made a promise to finish it, and I don't break promises to dead pilots." She finally turned and looked at him straight on, and her eyes weren't flat anymore, weren't glass, weren't river — they were just a tired woman's eyes, deciding. "After that, Dek, I'll give you your answer. But hear me now: whichever door I choose, I choose it myself. Nobody carries me through it. Not you, not the consortium, not the ghost of Astrid Vale. Me."

"That," Dek said, rising, gathering his ledger, "is the first sensible thing anyone has said to me about this case in twenty years."

He walked back toward his tent, and behind him he heard her stay where she was, on the canoe, alone with the coals and the loon-cry and whatever arithmetic she was doing in the dark. He did not look back. Looking back was for men who doubted their questions, and he did not doubt this one. He had only ever doubted whether anyone would answer it honestly.

He was unlacing his boots when he heard her cross the camp, quiet as snowfall, and stop outside his tent flap. A pause. Then her voice, low, through the canvas:

"Dek. The mark on the courier's papers. It was a black heron, wings folded, inside a broken circle. Yes?"

His hands went still on the laces.

"Yes," he said.

A long silence. Long enough that he thought she'd gone. Then:

"One night," she said. "Then I'll show you what the river kept."

Her footsteps faded toward the tree line, toward wherever the chest lay hidden, and Dek sat in the dark with his ledger open on his knees, and for the first time in twenty years he did not write anything down. Some entries, he thought, you wait to make until you know how they end.

Far to the south, past the ridgeline, an engine droned — high, distant, circling where no honest plane had reason to circle. Dek listened to it fade, and reached over, and pinched out the lamp.
