## 13. The Crew Meeting

The storm had spent itself by dusk, and what remained of it hung in the spruce as a fine cold mist that beaded on the stovepipe and dripped, slow and metronomic, into the tin cup Orsen had set beneath the leak three days ago. She had not moved the cup. It had become a kind of clock. When it filled, she emptied it into the fire and set it back, and somewhere between fillings she was supposed to decide.

She had not decided.

The camp was a salvage outfit's camp now, whether anyone said so or not — two wall tents and the old trapper's cabin she'd lent them, a cache rack, the Beaver tied off at the bank with her engine cowled against the damp. Dek had strung a clothesline of survey flags between the tents for no navigational reason Orsen could determine; they flapped in the dying wind like small red tongues. Petra's dive gear hung from the cabin eaves, dripping. The wreck lay forty feet down in the black water of the back channel, and above it, invisible from the bank, the chest sat in Orsen's cache under a tarp and a lie.

Supper was rice and caribou, and everyone ate it the way people eat when they are waiting for something they can't name. Ivar ladled second helpings without being asked. Petra talked about tomorrow's dive — visibility, thermocline, the way the fuselage doors had been *peeled*, she said, not torn — and stopped herself mid-sentence, glancing at Ivar, who looked at his plate. Dek watched Orsen watch them all, and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech.

Orsen washed the pots. That was her role here: the pilot who did the dishes, who kept to herself, whose silences were read as northern manners rather than what they were. She scrubbed the pot until her knuckles ached and understood, with the clarity that comes only too late or exactly on time, that she could not do this one more night. The note from Halloran's supply flight was folded in her shirt pocket like a coal. *Deliver it or the crew becomes collateral.* She had planned to run alone at dawn. Fog had ended that. And every hour she waited, the lie grew another ring, like a tree.

"Petra," she said, before she could stop herself. "Ivar. Dek. Come inside. There's something you need to hear."

* * *

The cabin was small enough that four people made a crowd. Petra took the bench by the window, knees drawn up, still half in her thermal layers, hair wet at the temples from the evening's gear check. Ivar lowered himself onto the bunk with the careful economy of a big man conserving himself. Dek stood by the door with his back against the frame, arms crossed, and Orsen thought: he knows. He has known since the night he caught me loading. Whatever I say next is for the other two.

She put the stove between herself and them, because she needed something to hold.

"I found the wreck first," she said. "Before any of you. Before I ever flew you upriver."

Petra's head came up slowly, the way an animal raises its head when the forest goes quiet.

"Low water in August. A wing in the back channel, where nobody's looked in twenty years because everybody already knew where it went down." She heard her own voice, flat and even, the voice she used for weather reports, and hated it, and kept going. "I dove it. Alone. There's a cargo chest in the forward bay. I took it out."

"You *took* it," Petra said. Not loud. Worse than loud.

"It's sealed. Stamped." Orsen reached under the bunk and brought out the logbook, dried now, pages stiff as autumn leaves, and laid it on the table between them. "And there's this."

Dek didn't move. Ivar leaned forward, and Orsen watched him read the name on the flyleaf — *A. Vale* — and watched something behind his face rearrange itself, furniture being moved in a dark room.

"Astrid Vale," Ivar said quietly. "That's the pilot. That's who we're salvaging for."

"That's who you're salvaging *around*," Orsen said. "Because your retainer doesn't want the plane, Ivar. It wants the chest. It wants everything in that chest burned before anyone reads it. And I think part of you already knows that."

The stove ticked. Outside, the mist had thickened again, and the drip into the tin cup had followed them somehow, or maybe it was just the sound the night made now, in Orsen's ears, always.

"The consortium," she said. "You've heard the word. Everyone upriver has heard the word and learned not to say it. They owned freight contracts, fuel, credit, half the outfits on this river including, once, mine. Including me." She pulled the folded paper from her pocket but didn't open it. "Twenty years ago I flew for them. One job. One run I shouldn't have asked about, and didn't, because asking was how pilots stopped flying. They paid me to not know what I carried. I took the money because my sister was dying in Whitehorse and the money was the size of a miracle. That's the whole story. That's the coward's version and the true version both."

"And your name?" Dek said softly. "Orsen Dunmore?"

Orsen looked at him. He gave her nothing back, just the patience of a man watching a confession arrive on schedule.

"Orsen Dunmore is eleven years old," she said. "I bought her from a fixer in Watson Lake after the consortium decided I'd seen one thing too many. New papers, new registration, new woman. I've been her so long I sometimes forget—" Her voice did something she hadn't authorized. She pressed it flat. "My real name is on a grave marker outside a town that isn't on maps anymore either. Vessland. I was born there. I left before they finished erasing it."

Petra had gone very still. "So when we told you about the tool marks on the fuselage—"

"You already knew it wasn't an accident," Ivar finished. His voice had dropped into something low and level, a man walking a floor that might give way. "You knew, and you flew us up anyway, and let us dive it."

"Yes."

"Why?" Petra stood. The bench scraped. "Why would you— we trusted you. I *idolized* you. Do you know what I told my mother about you? That I got to fly with Orsen Dunmore, the best bush pilot on the river? You let us put our hands on that wreck not knowing what killed her, and you knew, and you sat at this stove eating our food—"

"Your food," Ivar said mildly, "was bought with consortium money."

Petra turned on him. "What did you just say?"

* * *

It came out of Ivar then the way water comes through a dam — not all at once, but in a pressure that had clearly been building for days, maybe weeks, since a young diver surfaced holding a photograph of tool marks and a decent man started doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish.

"Three months ago a broker in Yellowknife offered me a contract," Ivar said. "Reputation management, he called it. A client wanted confirmation of a wreck location and disposal of 'legacy materials.' Standard fee, generous. I've done cleanup jobs before — drums, fuel caches, things companies want forgotten. I said yes because the fee would keep the outfit alive through two bad seasons." He rubbed his jaw. "They gave me a description of the chest. Dimensions, seal, contents classed as 'documents, historical, no commercial value.' No commercial value. You don't pay what they paid me for no commercial value."

"So you knew," Petra whispered.

"I suspected. Then your tool marks confirmed it, and then—" He looked at Orsen, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow more terrible. "Then I realized I wasn't the contractor. I'm the deniable part. If this goes wrong, if anyone ever asks, the record shows Ivar Lund, independent salvage operator, recovered and disposed of materials per contract. The men who ordered it never touched anything. I'm the glove. They don't even need me to succeed. They need me to be *blamed*."

The fire shifted. Somewhere out in the dark a spruce grouse boomed twice, absurdly domestic.

"They know the chest is found," Orsen said. She unfolded Halloran's note at last and put it on the table beside the logbook, because the hour for half-truths was over. "This came up on the supply flight. Coded, but I've read their codes since before any of you could spell them. It says deliver the chest or the crew becomes collateral. It's signed with a courier mark I'd know anywhere, because it's the same mark that was on the envelope that bought my silence twenty years ago." She paused. "Halloran holds my debt now. He bought it from the outfit I owed. He offered to clear it this morning — clear it, erase my old name from their files entirely, make me safe forever — if I report everything you find."

Petra stared at the note. At Orsen. Back at the note, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable. "Collateral," she repeated. "He means us. He means *me*. I'm twenty-three years old and I'm a line item in somebody's threat."

"He means leverage," Dek said. "It's how they've always worked. They don't kill unless they must. They purchase."

"And you were going to handle this how?" Ivar asked Orsen. "Alone? Pre-dawn, solo, with the chest and a storm front sitting on the pass?" He shook his head slowly. "I saw the weight-and-balance sheet you scratched out. You weren't planning to tell us at all."

"No," Orsen said. "I was planning to save you by lying to you. It's the only skill I have left, and I'm told I'm very good at it."

"Don't joke." Petra's voice cracked on it. "Don't you dare stand there and make it funny. You knew Astrid Vale was murdered. You knew who did it. You've known for weeks, and every day we were out on that water having a good time, learning the channel, telling stories — you were deciding whether we were worth the truth."

"Yes," Orsen said. "I was. And I got it wrong, and then I got it wronger, and the reason I finally opened my mouth is that there was no version left where my silence kept you safe. Silence doesn't keep anyone safe. I've had eleven years to test that theory and the data is in." She looked around the cabin, at each of them. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking what we do with the next four days."

"We," Petra said bitterly. "There's a *we* now?"

"There was always a we," Dek said quietly. "That's what none of us understood. The consortium built it that way. Every one of us was purchased separately and pointed at the same wreck. Lund's contract. Halloran's debt. My—" He stopped.

Everyone looked at him.

"My travel authorization," Dek said, after a moment, "is issued by a court that hasn't existed on any map for twenty years. I'm not a surveyor. I never was. I'm an investigator for the Vessland exile tribunal, and I have been looking for Astrid Vale's testimony since before Petra was born." He straightened away from the door. "I boarded Ivar's crew in Yellowknife because it was the only way onto this river. I let a young diver risk her life on a sabotaged wreck because I needed what's inside it. So before we vote on who betrayed whom, understand that everyone at this stove has sold something. I simply had the cleanest invoice."

The silence afterward was enormous. The mist ticked against the window glass. Petra sat back down slowly, not because she was calm but because her legs had made the decision independently.

* * *

What happened next was not a discussion so much as a fracture, witnessed.

Petra walked to the window and stood with her back to all of them, shoulders rigid, and when she finally spoke it was to the glass, to her own reflection ghosted against the black spruce. "I came up here because of her. Astrid Vale. My mother had her photograph on the shelf — they trained together, before. When I got my dive certification, I told myself someday I'd find her plane. And instead I find out the woman I admired most in the world knew where it was all along and served me supper over it." Her breath fogged the glass. "Do you know what the worst part is? I'd have helped you. From the first day. If you'd asked. I'd have dived that channel in a snowstorm. You stole that from me. You stole my *choice*."

"I know," Orsen said. "It's the same theft someone committed against me, once. That's not an excuse. It's just the shape of the thing — it gets passed down."

Ivar sat with his elbows on his knees, staring at the logbook as though it might be audited. "If I walk away," he said, "the contract still exists. They'll send someone else, worse than me, and my name is already on the paperwork. If I stay, I'm choosing sides in something I don't fully understand, against people who own my outfit's debt too — did you know that? Half my equipment is collateral to the same machine." He laughed, short and without humor. "We're not a crew. We're a portfolio."

"Then vote," Dek said. "All of us. Tonight. Because Halloran's deadline won't wait for anyone's feelings, and neither will the weather. Two clear windows before the front closes the pass for a week. Maybe less."

"Vote on what?" Petra asked, still facing the glass.

"On what happens to the chest," Orsen said. "On what happens to us. Whether we hand it to Halloran and scatter and spend the rest of our lives being careful — I can tell you exactly what that costs, I have eleven years of receipts. Or whether Astrid Vale gets to finish her last entry."

Petra turned around. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and, underneath the fury, something else was surfacing, the way the wing had surfaced in the low water — something the river had kept. "And if we vote to run it," she said, "who flies? Because I trust exactly no one in this room right now, and that includes me."

"You can trust the pilot who's flown this river blind since before you were certified," Orsen said, "and who is done lying. That's all I've got, Petra. It's not much. But it's real, starting now, and it's the only currency I have left."

Ivar looked at the logbook a long time. Then at the note with its courier mark. Then, last, at Orsen — and whatever he found in her face seemed to settle some account of his own.

"Stove's getting low," he said, which meant nothing and everything, and reached for the split birch.

Outside, far down the lake, an engine sound drifted across the water — distant, wrong for the hour, wrong for the direction — and every person in the cabin went silent, listening, while the tin cup beneath the eaves filled drop by drop toward morning.
