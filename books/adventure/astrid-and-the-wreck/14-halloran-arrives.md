## 14. Halloran Arrives

The morning came in gray and flat, the kind of light that made the river look like poured pewter and turned every sound sharp — the creak of the dock line, the tick of cooling metal on the Beaver's cowling, Petra's laugh carrying up from the water where she was rinsing her dive gear. Orsen heard the plane before she saw it, because you always did, out here. A low drone from the south, wrong pitch for Dek's charter contacts, wrong rhythm for the mail run.

She was standing at the woodpile with an axe in her hands before she'd consciously decided to pick it up.

The plane resolved out of the haze as a twin-engine charter, white-bodied, unmarked except for a registration number that meant nothing and a paint scheme that meant everything — that particular corporate cleanliness, no dings patched with rivets, no oil streaks down the belly. Money kept its aircraft like that. Money kept everything like that.

It came down the lake long and flat, touched once, and taxied to the dock with the unhurried confidence of something that had never been refused landing rights anywhere.

Halloran stepped out first.

He looked exactly as he had eleven years ago, which was somehow worse than if he'd aged. Same gray coat, same soft cap pulled low over eyes the color of dishwater. He stood on the float and took his time looking around — at the cabin, at the drying racks, at the Beaver tied at Orsen's own dock — cataloguing it all with the mild interest of a man inspecting property he already owned on paper.

Behind him came two men. They didn't get out so much as unfold, one after the other, tall and quiet and dressed like they were going to a funeral for someone they hadn't liked. They stayed on the floats. That was deliberate too. Everything Halloran did was deliberate; it was why he'd survived so long in a business where the polite word was *intermediary* and the honest one was *knife*.

"Morning, Dunmore." He said her false name easily, warmly even, the way you'd greet a niece. "Nice strip. Shallow approach off the north end — I always wondered how you got a heavy load out of here."

"You've got my charts," Orsen said. Her voice came out level. She'd had years of practice making it come out level. "Why pretend you need them?"

"I don't." He smiled and stepped onto the dock, boots ringing on the planks. He glanced back at the two men. "Stay with the plane, gentlemen. This is a friendly call."

Petra had gone still in the shallows, dive mask pushed up on her forehead, watching. Down by the fire ring, Dek had risen from his maps without hurry, but Orsen saw the way his hand rested near the canvas bag where he kept his documents. Ivar emerged from the tent, coffee tin in hand, and stopped when he read the geometry of the scene — the stranger on the dock, the men on the floats, the way Orsen held herself.

"Your friends should hear this too," Halloran said pleasantly. "Saves repetition."

"They're not my friends," Orsen said. "They're clients."

"Of course they are." He said it the way a priest says *amen*.

* * *

They gathered at the fire ring because there was nowhere else, and Halloran seemed to enjoy that — sitting down on Orsen's own log bench, accepting a tin cup of coffee he didn't drink, setting a slim leather folder on his knee like a man settling in for a sermon. The morning had warmed not at all. Smoke leaned sideways off the fire. Somewhere across the lake a loon called, absurdly peaceful.

"Let me summarize," Halloran began, "so we're all clear on the legal situation." He opened the folder. "This is a certified order from the territorial court, provisional jurisdiction, salvage claim filed nine days ago by Meridian Recovery Group on behalf of an unnamed principal. It asserts title to any wreckage, cargo, or documentation recovered from or pertaining to the aircraft lost on this river twenty-two years ago." He let the paper catch the light, just enough that the seal showed. "It's signed. It's stamped. It is, I'm sorry to say, entirely legitimate. Your courts move fast when the money's right."

"There's no wreck recovered yet," Ivar said carefully. "We're still surveying."

"Mm." Halloran looked at him with something almost like affection. "Mr. Lund. Ivar. May I? You took a retainer through a subsidiary of the same principal. Standard reputation-management scope: locate, confirm, dispose. You were told the client wanted embarrassing old cargo kept out of the press. Which was true, in the way that a knife wound is technically a skin condition." He turned back to Orsen. "You see the position everyone's in. Everyone at this fire has already been paid by the same hand. It's only the amounts that differ."

Nobody spoke. Petra's jaw was tight. Dek hadn't moved at all, but Orsen could feel him listening the way other people listened to music — hearing what was under the words.

"So here's the offer," Halloran said, and his voice softened into the register he used for closing deals, warm as banked coals. "Orsen. The debt. Two hundred and forty thousand, plus the interest that's been growing on it like moss since the year you came upriver. Gone. Not forgiven — *cleared*. There's a difference. Forgiven means someone was generous. Cleared means it never existed, same as your name never existed, same as the woman who signed those papers never existed." He tapped the folder. "And in exchange, you report to me what the crew finds. The chest. Any papers. Anything with a certain seal on it. You give it to me before anyone else sees it, and you keep flying mail and medicine and gossip until your knees go, under whatever name you like, owing nobody anything, ever again."

"And if I don't?" Orsen asked. She knew she shouldn't ask. Asking was the tell. But the crew was sitting right there, and Halloran wanted them to hear the answer.

Halloran sighed, genuinely regretful, and looked around the fire ring — at Petra, at Ivar, lingering longest on Dek. "Then the order stands, and my colleagues here enforce it, and salvage law being what it is, anyone obstructing a registered claim becomes party to it. Fines. Seizure of aircraft." He paused. "Seizure of persons, if it comes to that. And separately — and I say this with real sorrow, Orsen, because I've always liked you — the debt doesn't clear itself. Interest compounds. Collections escalate. You know what our collections look like. You've seen the inside of that machine. These good people would just be... collateral weather. Rain falling on someone else's parade."

There it was. Said politely, wrapped in legal paper, offered with a smile: *give me the truth, or I'll spend these three like small change to get it.*

Orsen looked at the fire. Eleven years ago she'd sat across a table from this man in a room with no windows and signed away her name to make a problem disappear, and she remembered thinking the price was steep but survivable. She'd been younger. She hadn't known yet that a debt like that never clears — it just changes hands.

"One night," she said.

Halloran raised his eyebrows.

"You want my answer clean, you get it clean," she said. "I'm not deciding anything in front of my crew with your muscle sitting on my floats. One night. I'll have the chest — or word of where it is — by first light. You can land at dawn."

"And if first light comes and you're gone?"

"Then you'll know my answer," Orsen said, "and you'll still have their names on your claim, and you'll have to live with doing it the ugly way. I don't think you want the ugly way. Ugly ways make noise, and noise is the one thing your principals can't buy back."

For a moment something moved behind Halloran's eyes — respect, maybe, or its cold cousin, appraisal. Then he smiled and stood, brushing pine needles from his coat.

"One night," he agreed. "Dawn tomorrow. Don't make me come back twice, Orsen. I hate repeating myself, and I hate more what happens after I do." He tucked the folder under his arm, nodded courteously to each of them in turn — Petra, Ivar, and last Dek, whom he regarded a half-second too long, like a man noting a serial number — and walked back down to the dock with his unhurried stride.

The twin lifted off the pewter water and climbed south into the gray, and the sound of it faded until the loon and the fire were the only things left in the world.

* * *

They stood around the dead fire a long time after the engine noise died. Nobody lit it again. The cold crept in through the silence like water through a hull seam.

"He knows about the chest," Petra said finally. Her voice was thin. "He said *the chest*. How does he—"

"He's known since before we did," Orsen said. "Probably since before we chartered. Maybe since the day the wing surfaced." She looked at Ivar. "Did you know?"

Ivar set his coffee tin down slowly. His face had gone the color of birch bark. "My retainer said reputation management. Locate and confirm. I swear to you, Orsen, disposal was a word I told myself meant paperwork." He rubbed both hands over his face. "I signed with a subsidiary. I never followed the thread up. That's not innocence. That's laziness wearing innocence's coat."

"And you?" Petra turned on Dek, sharp with fear now, fear curdling toward accusation. "You watch everything. What are you?"

Dek met her gaze steadily. "Tomorrow," he said quietly. "Tonight, what matters is this: Halloran gave us a deadline because deadlines split crews. He wants us spending the night suspecting each other instead of planning. So let's disappoint him." He looked at Orsen. "You asked for a night. Let's use it."

Orsen nodded, but inside her something had already begun to move — the old reflex, the solo reflex, the one that said *you got yourself into this, you fly yourself out*. She looked at the Beaver riding at the dock, at the chest hidden under the tarp in the woodshed, at the three people standing in her clearing willing to stand there.

One night. She'd bought them one night with a promise she already knew she couldn't keep honestly — because every honest version of compliance ended with Astrid Vale drowning a second time, and every dishonest one ended with these three paying her bill.

First light. Halloran would come at first light.

The question was who else would be airborne before then.
