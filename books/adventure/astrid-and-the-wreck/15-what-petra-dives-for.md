## 15. What Petra Dives For

The river had gone quiet in the way it did before weather — a held-breath quiet, the water lying flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of old tin. Petra stood on the gravel bar in her wetsuit with the hood down, letting the cold air bite her ears, and listened to it. She had learned to listen to water before she'd ever learned to swim in it. Her father used to say rivers told you everything if you were patient enough: where they kept their dead, where they kept their secrets, where they were willing to trade.

This one had been keeping Astrid Vale for twenty years.

She zipped the hood up and checked her gear a third time, though she'd checked it twice already. The ritual mattered. Hands busy, mind steady. Below the surface there was no room for shaking hands, no room for the thing that had been sitting in her chest since Orsen's confession two nights ago — since the chest came out of the shadows of the plane's cabin and the logbook was laid open on the camp table under lamplight, and Petra had read *they are following me* in a dead woman's careful hand.

*They are following me.* Written like a woman noting the weather. Like it was almost ordinary.

Petra had felt betrayed by Orsen — she still did, a raw wire of it running through everything — but underneath the betrayal was something worse and stranger: recognition. Because Petra knew what it was to carry something for someone who couldn't ask you to carry it. She'd been doing it since she was nineteen.

"Petra." Ivar's voice from behind her, low. "You don't have to do this one alone."

"I want to do this one alone."

He didn't argue. He'd stopped arguing with her somewhere over the Kettle River, after the tool marks, after his own confession about the retainer. Now he just crouched at the edge of the water and helped her check her weights, and said, "Current's slack until midafternoon. Visibility might hold at three meters if the silt stays down. Don't push your bottom time."

"Yes, Captain."

"Don't call me that."

"Then stop acting like one."

Almost, he smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. Ivar Lund had been walking around camp for two days like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to, and Petra understood why. He'd taken consortium money to find a box. He'd found out the box had a body attached. There was no clean way back from that arithmetic; Petra had done her own version of it years ago, in a hospital corridor in Tromsø, and she knew the only way through was forward, into the cold, hands busy, mind steady.

She spat in her mask, rinsed it, settled it on her face. The world went green-rimmed and small.

Then she stepped backward off the bar and let the river take her.

* * *

The cold hit first, as it always did — not pain anymore, after all these years, but a kind of announcement. *You are somewhere else now.* Then the light changed, the gray sky smearing into a ceiling of trembling silver, and Petra dropped through the thermocline into water so dark and tea-stained with tannin that her torch beam seemed less like light than like a rumor of light.

The wreck rose up to meet her.

Even now, even knowing every rivet of it, her heart did the thing it always did — that slow swell, half awe and half grief. The floatplane lay on its side in the channel scour, nose buried to the cowling in silt, one wing folded back against the current like a bird that had landed wrong and never gotten up. Twenty years of river had dressed it: a pelt of algae, freshwater mussels clustered along the trailing edges like barnacles on a ship, a fallen log wedged under the tail that some flood had carried in and left like an offering. Fish flicked away from her light in startled silver stitches.

Astrid Vale's airplane. Astrid Vale's coffin. Astrid Vale's message in a bottle, depending on how you looked at it.

Petra swam the length of the fuselage slowly, running her gloved fingers along the skin, feeling for what she'd felt before — the tool marks. They were still there, of course. Marks didn't wash out. Near the wing root, where someone had worked a blade or a chisel into a fuel line or a control run, the aluminum bore a row of bright crescents under the grime, deliberate as handwriting. Sabotage. Murder, written in metal. On her first dive she'd found them and reported them to Ivar and watched him go quiet in a way that had frightened her more than the marks themselves.

But that wasn't why she was here today.

Orsen had told them everything two nights ago — the chest, the logbook, the debt, the false name, Halloran's note. Everything except one thing, and it was the one thing Petra couldn't stop turning over. The consortium's divers had come here once, years ago. Dek had found the record: a contracted salvage survey, six days on station, officially abandoned due to conditions. Six days, and they'd hauled up nothing — or claimed nothing — because the chest hadn't been found until low water exposed the wing and Orsen pulled it free.

Six days of diving by professionals who wanted very badly to find something, and they'd missed it.

Petra didn't believe they'd missed it. She believed they'd looked where a salvage crew looks: the cabin, the cargo bay, the baggage compartment behind the seats. Places where cargo goes. And whatever Astrid Vale had died carrying — the original documents, the real evidence, the thing worth murdering a pilot over — had not been in any of those places. Because Astrid Vale had known she was being followed. Had written it down, calm as a weather report. A pilot who knows she might be shot out of the sky doesn't put her treasure in the cargo bay.

A pilot puts it where only a diver would ever think to look.

Petra hung in the black water above the inverted fuselage and let herself feel it — the whole shape of it, the dead woman's foresight, the twenty-year patience of it. Somewhere under the silt and the mussels, Astrid Vale had hidden her voice. And everyone who came looking had searched the plane the way men search planes, and the river had kept the secret the way rivers keep things: completely, until it chose otherwise.

Petra had dived wrecks for eleven years. She'd started because of her brother, though she told people it was because of the money, and then later she told people it was because she loved it, which was also true in the way that loving something that broke you is true. Jonas had drowned in a lake outside Tromsø when she was nineteen, a car through the ice, and the recovery divers had brought him up on the third day, and Petra had watched from the shore and understood with a clarity that never left her: somebody has to go down and get them. Somebody has to bring them home. Not their bodies — their bodies were beyond help — but their *truth*. Whatever the water was holding that the living needed. Somebody had to be brave enough and cold enough and stubborn enough to go down into the dark and carry it back up into the light.

That was the job. That had always been the job. She'd just spent eleven years pretending it was about hulls and cargo.

Today it wasn't.

She checked her gauge. Forty feet, nine minutes, water at four degrees Celsius. Enough. She turned to the underside of the fuselage — the side that faced the sky when the plane flew, the side that now faced down into the scour — and began to work.

* * *

It took her forty minutes to find it, and she would remember every one of those minutes for the rest of her life.

Not because it was hard. Because it was easy, once she stopped thinking like a salvager and started thinking like a pilot who was scared. Scared pilots don't hide things cleverly. Clever hiding requires calm, and calm is a luxury of people who believe they'll live. Scared pilots hide things *close*. Where their hands can reach without thinking. Where muscle memory can find them blind, in the dark, in a hurry.

Petra thought about what a pilot touches last before a flight. The door handle. The seat rails. The emergency release.

She was working along the frame of the cabin door — the door that had torn half away in the impact and now hung like a broken jaw — when her glove caught on something that wasn't algae. A strap. Webbing, nylon, still supple after twenty years in cold anaerobic silt, tucked flat against the doorframe and lashed down with a knot that made her breath catch in her throat.

She knew the knot. Every bush pilot in the territory knew the knot. It was a quick-release lash, the kind you tied around anything you might need to cut loose in a hurry — a raft, a load, a life. Orsen used it. Half the pilots Orsen's age used it. It was the knot of people who planned for the day everything went wrong.

Astrid Vale had lashed a package to the inside of her own cabin door, where her hand would fall on it the moment she reached for the release. Where she could tear it free in one motion and hold it in her lap, in the last seconds, in the water.

*Take me with you,* the knot said. *Whatever happens, take me with you.*

Petra's eyes stung behind the mask, which was stupid and dangerous, and she blinked it away and got her knife under the webbing. The silt around the package had cemented into a shell; she worked it loose with her fingertips, gentle, the way you'd work a fossil out of stone, the way you'd dig anyone out of anything. It came free all at once and settled into her hands heavier than its size — a hard case, maybe forty centimeters long, sealed, wrapped in waxed canvas and tarred cloth, waterproofed by someone who had done it carefully, kneeling on a hangar floor somewhere, knowing exactly what the river would do to lesser materials.

There was no lock. There was a seal — wax, under a fold of the canvas, pressed with a stamp. Petra angled her torch to it and her breath snagged in her regulator.

A double-headed eagle, drawn thin and strange, wings swept like blades. She'd seen it two nights ago on the chest Orsen pulled from the wreck, and seen it again in the pages of Dek's folder: the seal of Vessland. The nation that wasn't on maps anymore. The nation that existed now only in exile halls and erased borders and the stubborn ink of people who refused to stop signing its name.

Astrid Vale had signed it too. Right there in wax, at the bottom of a river, for twenty years.

Petra hugged the case to her chest and kicked for the surface.

* * *

They met her at the shallows — Ivar waist-deep with both hands out, Dek crouched on the gravel with a blanket already unfolded, and Orsen standing apart, arms crossed tight against the cold or against something else, watching the water with an expression Petra was learning to read. Guilt wore a lot of costumes on that woman. Today it wore stillness.

Petra surfaced, spat her reg, and held the case up out of the water before anything else.

Nobody spoke. The river talked instead, sliding past the bar, patient, unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world and, until this morning, it had.

Ivar took the case like it was sleeping. Dek wrapped it in the blanket without touching the seal. And Petra stood in the shallows shaking — not from cold, or not only from cold — while Orsen waded out and put a hand on her shoulder through the neoprene, and said, quietly, "What did you find?"

"Her," Petra said. "I found her."

* * *

They opened it in the tent, on the camp table, with the stove ticking and the storm front grumbling somewhere over the western ridgeline like a dog dreaming. Dek insisted on gloves and procedure — photographs first, notes, the seal documented before it was broken — and for once nobody rushed him. Some things deserved ceremony. This was a courtroom happening in a canvas tent on a gravel bar at the end of the world, and they all knew it, even Petra, who had never set foot in one.

Under the tarred cloth: the case, aluminum, sealed with a gasket that had held. Inside, packed in oiled wool like something precious being shipped across an ocean —

Three bundles of documents, dry. Dry after twenty years. Dry as the day a frightened pilot had knelt in a hangar and wrapped them, because she had known, with the terrible clarity of the followed, that paper was the only part of her that would survive.

Dek lifted the top bundle with tweezers and read the first page, and Petra watched a man who had spent two decades hunting something watch it walk into the room. His hands shook. He set the page down, took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirt, put them back on, and read it aloud, because some words demand air:

Survey records. Boundary commission testimony. Signatures of commissioners, dated the year before Vessland was erased — before the consortium's mapmen redrew the lines and called the empty places theirs. The originals. Not copies. The kind of paper that courts require and empires burn.

And beneath the documents, wrapped separately in a scrap of silk gone gray with age, a letter. Addressed on the outside in the same careful hand as the logbook: *For my daughter, Mireille. If I cannot give it to her myself, give it to someone who will.*

The tent was silent. Outside, the wind had begun to move through the spruces, testing them.

Petra thought of her brother, brought up out of the lake on the third day. She thought of the recovery divers' faces when they handed him over — not triumphant, not sad, just *finished*, the look of people who had completed the only task that matters, the ferrying of truth from the dark to the light. She had waited nineteen years to understand that look from the inside.

Now she had it. Grief didn't go away. It just finally had somewhere to put itself down.

"She knew," Orsen said. Her voice was rough. She was staring at the letter, at the name on it. "She knew they'd come for the plane. She hid it where only someone looking for a person would find it — not cargo. A pilot hides things where her own hands go."

"And the consortium's divers tore this plane apart," Dek said quietly, "looking in every place a thief would look. And never once asked where a mother would put a letter to her daughter." He closed the case with exaggerated gentleness, as if Astrid might feel the draft. "Tomorrow Halloran lands with his court order and his men. If this leaves this tent in their hands—"

"It won't," Petra said.

Everyone looked at her. She realized she'd said it the way you say things underwater — plainly, without decoration, because decoration is a luxury of people with air to spare.

"They had twenty years," she said. "We've had twenty minutes. But we're the ones holding her." She looked around the tent — Ivar with his ruined arithmetic, Dek with his shaking hands, Orsen with her whole buried life sitting in her face like a sunrise she couldn't decide whether to let happen. "She wrote *they are following me* and then she tied her truth to her own door and jumped anyway. The least we can do is finish the flight."

Outside, the wind stood up. The spruces bent. Somewhere over the western ridges, the storm they'd have to cross was assembling itself out of cloud and cold, and none of them moved, because they were all thinking the same thing, and only one of them was going to say it first.

It was Ivar. He looked at the case, then at Orsen, and said, "So. Who's flying?"
