## 16. The Split

The storm came in off the Kettle like a debt collector, and for once Orsen was glad of it.

She'd watched it build all afternoon from the cabin door — a bruised wall of cloud stacking up over the divide, swallowing the peaks one by one, the light going the color of old pewter. By dusk the wind had swung around to the northeast and begun to moan in the spruce tops, and the first spits of sleet ticked against the windows like fingers testing a lock. Bad flying weather. The worst she'd seen this late in the season.

Which meant Halloran couldn't come. Not tonight, not tomorrow, maybe not for two days.

Two days was a lifetime. Two days was enough.

She stood at the stove with her back to the room and listened to them argue behind her, and let herself feel, for just a moment, how strange it was that there *was* a room full of people arguing behind her. A week ago her evenings had been the stove, the radio static, and the sound of her own breathing. Now there were three voices she knew by their cadences — Ivar's low and deliberate, Petra's quick and bright even when it cracked, Dek's dry as gravel — and they filled the cabin the way heat fills a cold room: gradually, then all at once.

"We don't run," Ivar was saying. "We hold position. That's what he expects us not to do."

"He's got two men and a court order," Dek said. "You've got fine print. Forgive me if I don't love those odds."

"The fine print is better than you think." Ivar's chair creaked; Orsen could picture him leaning forward, elbows on knees, the way he did when he was building an argument the way other men built walls. "The retainer names me as salvage master with exclusive authority over recovered materials pending verification of provenance. Verification requires documentation. Documentation is in transit. In transit means delayed. I can delay him for days on paperwork alone. It's the one language his lawyers respect."

"And while you're delaying him," Petra said, "the rest of us do what?"

Orsen turned around.

They'd arranged themselves around the camp stove without ever discussing it — Ivar by the door, Dek at the table with his ledger open in front of him like scripture, Petra cross-legged on her bunk with Astrid's logbook in her lap, its dried pages soft as cloth under her thumbs. The chest sat between them all, empty now, its Vessland seal dulled but unmistakable. The real cargo — the documents, the letter, twenty years of murder folded into oilcloth — was wrapped and weighted and sitting inside Orsen's flight bag by the door, where she had put it an hour ago without telling anyone, because some part of her had already made this decision and was only waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

"We fly," Orsen said.

The room went quiet the way a river goes quiet over deep water.

"Through that?" Petra nodded at the window, where the sleet had thickened into a horizontal hiss.

"Through the front, ahead of it, or not at all." Orsen crossed to the table and spread the chart flat, weighting the corners with a tin cup, a wrench, the logbook's brass corner. Her finger traced the route west-southwest, over the divide, down the long valley to the frozen lake Dek had named — the staging point, the last leg before the exile court's seat. "This front isn't moving fast, but it's moving *solid*. Once it settles in, nothing crosses for days, and Halloran doesn't need good weather. He needs a charter pilot willing to be paid badly. There's always one." She looked up. "But right now, at first light, there's a gap. Six hours, maybe eight, between the leading edge and the main body. Low ceiling, ice above four thousand, turbulence in the passes. Ugly. Flyable. Once."

"You've flown worse," Petra said. It wasn't a question. She said it the way other people said a prayer.

"I've flown worse," Orsen admitted. "I've also turned back from better. Weather doesn't care which one you are when it finds you."

Dek hadn't touched his ledger. He was watching her with those pale, patient eyes, and Orsen understood suddenly that he had been waiting for her to say exactly this — that the whole evening had been a shape built around the hole where her decision belonged. "And me?" he asked. "Where am I on this chart?"

"You're coming." She said it flatly, and saw something flicker across his face — surprise, quickly mastered, then something underneath it she suspected he'd deny to his grave. "You know the route notes. You've spent twenty years learning every name on those documents. If something happens to me over the divide, the evidence can't go down with the plane. It goes with you. You're not cargo, Dek. You're the second copy."

He looked down at the ledger. When he spoke again his voice was rougher than she'd ever heard it. "Twenty years I imagined this flight," he said. "In my head it was always simpler."

"Everything's simpler in your head," Petra said gently. "That's why we keep our bodies around."

The laugh that went around the cabin was small and strained, but it was real, and Orsen held onto it like a handhold.

* * *

They worked through the night, and the work kept the fear at a distance the way a lit lamp keeps the dark at arm's length — present, patient, held off but never beaten.

Ivar repacked the empty chest himself, folding decoy papers he'd drafted in his careful surveyor's hand: provenance claims, chain-of-custody forms, a salvage manifest listing contents that would take Halloran's lawyers a week to debunk. He sealed it with wax from a candle stub and pressed the Vessland seal into it using the original stamp they'd found wired beneath the chest lid, and when he finished he sat looking at his own handiwork with an expression Orsen couldn't read.

"I spent eleven years doing honest work after I left the consortium's orbit," he said, to no one in particular. "Eleven years, and it takes one afternoon to remember how to lie in their language." He rubbed the wax smooth with his thumb. "It comes back like a mother tongue."

"It's not lying," Petra said from her bunk, where she was oiling her dive knife with more force than the task required. "It's stalling a murderer's errand boy. Those are different words."

"They're different words in English," Ivar said. "In contract law they're the same word, and I'll have to live with whichever one the court eventually picks." He looked up at Orsen. "When he opens that chest and finds paper instead of proof, he'll know it was me. Not you. Me. My name is on the retainer, my seal is on the wax. Whatever comes after — and something will come after — it lands on me. I need you all to understand that I've done the arithmetic and I'm at peace with it."

"No one's asking you to—" Petra started.

"I'm asking *me* to," Ivar said. "There's a difference, and it matters."

Orsen met his eyes across the cabin. Of all of them, Ivar had the farthest to fall — the reputation, the outfit, the careful respectable life he'd rebuilt out of the wreckage of whatever the consortium had first made of him. And he was setting it down voluntarily, the way a man sets down a pack he's decided he no longer needs, on the far side of a river he intends to burn behind him.

"Ivar," she said. "If it goes wrong—"

"If it goes wrong," he said, "then you'll be over the divide, and it will have gone wrong *here*, which means it went wrong exactly as far from you as I could throw it. That's the whole design. Don't ruin it with gratitude."

* * *

At two in the morning the wind dropped, and the silence woke Orsen faster than any noise could have.

She lay in her bunk listening to the absence — the spruce tops still, the sleet gone to a fine dry whisper of snow, the kind of hush that meant the front's leading edge had passed and the gap was opening. She dressed in the dark, moved through the cabin without waking anyone, and stepped out onto the porch.

The sky had torn open along the western horizon. Behind the tear, stars. Ahead, the main body of the storm sat on the divide like a sleeping animal, vast and slow-breathing, its edges glowing faintly with moonlight caught in ice crystal. Between here and there lay six hours of ugly air and one narrow window.

Her breath hung in front of her. Somewhere downriver, a wolf spoke once and was answered, and the sound traveled through the cold like a wire.

*Fly toward it,* she thought. *Toward exposure. Toward the court, the record, the hall full of strangers who will write your true name down and read it back to you.* Every instinct she had honed for ten years screamed against it — the forged papers in her strongbox, the registration number painted on a plane that belonged to a woman who had never existed, the whole architecture of anonymity she'd built plank by plank since the day she'd signed away her name to make a problem disappear. That architecture had kept her alive. It had also kept her small, and alone, and complicit, and she understood now — standing in the cold with the storm breathing on the far side of the divide — that she had mistaken the cage for the wings.

She wasn't running anymore. She was flying toward the thing she feared most, on purpose, with witnesses.

And she wanted to. God help her, she wanted it the way she'd once wanted altitude in a failing plane — desperately, completely, with both hands on the stick.

Behind her, the cabin door opened. She didn't turn. She knew the footsteps — Dek's uneven tread, favoring the knee the river had given him trouble over.

"Couldn't sleep either?" he said.

"Checking the sky."

"What does it say?"

Orsen studied the gap in the clouds, the stars burning cold and steady beyond it. "It says first light. It says don't be late."

Dek came to stand beside her at the rail. For a while neither of them spoke, and the snow fell between them, fine as sifted flour.

"When I found you," he said finally, "in chapter and verse, over these twenty years — every lead, every rumor, every pilot who might have flown for them — I told myself I was hunting a witness." He paused. "I was hunting a ghost. And then I found a woman mending float struts in a river camp who flinched at the sound of a charter engine, and I thought: there she is. Not the ghost. The person. And I thought, God forgive me, I hoped you'd say yes. Because I needed you to. Because the truth doesn't fly itself."

"It does tonight," Orsen said. "I'm just holding the stick."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh, and squeezed her shoulder once — brief, hard, the touch of a man unaccustomed to touching — and went back inside.

Orsen stayed a moment longer. Below the porch, the river ran black and patient under its new skin of ice, carrying everything it carried toward the sea, keeping what it kept. Somewhere upstream, in a back channel, the wreck of Astrid Vale's plane lay under fresh snow, lighter now by one sealed case, its long vigil nearly ended.

"You're guiding us," Orsen said quietly, to the water, to the woman, to the dead. "Just so you know. Your route notes. Your weather calls. We're flying your way home."

The river said nothing, the way rivers do. But the wind shifted, very slightly, out of the southwest — a tailwind, if she chose to believe in such things — and Orsen chose to.

* * *

First light came gray and grainy, the color of dishwater, and by the time it did the whole crew stood on the shore packing the plane by lantern-light, breath rising in clouds, working fast and silent as a burial party.

Petra lashed the flight bag into the rear compartment herself, checking each strap twice, then a third time, her jaw set in a way that dared the straps to fail. Dek climbed aboard with his ledger inside his coat, against his chest, like a second heart. Orsen walked the preflight in the lantern glow — fuel, controls, surfaces, the wings already wearing their first rime of frost, which she swept clear with the back of her glove, again and again, a Sisyphean labor against the falling snow.

Ivar stood apart, the false chest under one arm like a man carrying an infant he intended to give away.

"So," he said, when Orsen came to stand beside him. "Six hours to the lake. Then ground travel, then the court. If the weather holds the way you think it will."

"If the weather holds the way I *hope* it will," Orsen corrected. "Hold the fort. Keep him talking. Feed him paperwork until he chokes on it."

"And if he doesn't choke?"

Orsen looked at him — big, deliberate Ivar, with his courteous voice and his purchased loyalty and his fine print, standing in the falling snow with a box of lies under his arm. "Then stall anyway," she said. "Stall with your body if you have to. Every hour you buy us is another hour the storm closes behind us. He can't follow what he can't fly through."

Ivar nodded slowly. Then he did something she didn't expect: he took her hand in both of his, and shook it, formally, like a man closing a deal or beginning one.

"Fly well, Orsen Dunmore," he said. "And when you get there — say the name properly. All of it. The whole thing. Twenty years is long enough for anyone to go by half a name."

Orsen's throat tightened. She managed a nod.

Petra hugged her — fierce, quick, face buried against her shoulder — and then pulled back and looked at her with wet, furious eyes. "Come back," she said. "You come back and you land this thing and you tell me everything, every single detail, and I get to be there when they read it into the record. That's the deal. That's my price for letting you go."

"That's a terrible price," Orsen said. "I accept."

Dek settled into the rear seat and began, immediately, methodically, to unfold Astrid's route notes across his knees, anchoring them against the turbulence to come. Orsen swung up into the pilot's seat, ran the checklist with hands that had done it ten thousand times and were shaking anyway, and hit the starter.

The engine coughed, caught, roared — a living thing in the freezing air, shaking frost off the cowl. She taxied out onto the river, swung into the wind, and paused with the throttle at her palm, looking once down the length of the water at the shrinking figures on the shore: Ivar with his box of lies, Petra with her hand raised, small and steady against the gray.

Then she pushed the throttle forward, and the floats came free of the river, and the world tilted up into snow.

The climb through the pass was ugly from the first minute — turbulence slamming the plane sideways, the ceiling pressing down like a lid, snow streaming past the windshield in horizontal rivers. Orsen flew by instruments and instinct and the dead woman's notes, climbing toward the gap Dek called out from the rear seat, his voice pitched calm over the engine noise, reading altitudes and headings like a man reciting scripture in a burning church.

Ahead, the divide rose white and enormous into the murk. Behind her, the storm closed like a door.

Orsen set her heading, gripped the yoke, and flew straight at the wall of weather — toward the court, the record, the name she hadn't spoken aloud in ten years — and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that her fear had stopped being a cage somewhere over the river and become, instead, simply fuel.

The radio crackled once, faintly, through the static — a fragment of a voice, a syllable, gone.

Then the divide swallowed them, and the real crossing began.
