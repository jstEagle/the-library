## 17. Storm Crossing

The storm front sat on the horizon like a wall someone had built out of spite — a bruised purple rampart of cloud running the whole width of the sky from north to south, lightning flickering inside it the way a lamp flickers behind a curtain. Orsen stood on the dock at Kettle Narrows with her hands in her pockets and studied it the way she'd once studied men who came asking questions: measuring it, hating it, knowing she'd have to go through it anyway.

"We could wait," Dek said beside her. He said it mildly, the way he said everything, but his eyes were on the front too. "Twelve hours. Maybe eighteen."

"Eighteen hours is long enough for Halloran to fuel his charter and file a flight plan." She pulled her hands free and flexed them against the cold. "He's not waiting for weather. Weather is a courtesy people like him don't extend."

Petra came up the dock at a jog, duffel over one shoulder, Astrid's logbook wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her chest like something born rather than carried. Ivar had left an hour ago with the empty chest and a face like a man walking into a dentist's office wearing his best suit, and now there were only three of them, and the Otter sat low on its floats with full tanks and too much weight, and the wall of cloud kept building.

"You've crossed fronts before," Petra said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've crossed two." Orsen looked at her. "Both times I had more airplane than this and less to lose."

"And both times you landed."

Orsen almost smiled. "That's the part they remember you for."

* * *

They went wheels instead of floats — the skis were still in the hangar at Narrows, so it was tundra tires off the gravel bar above the narrows, a run Orsen had made maybe forty times and never once enjoyed. The Otter staggered into the air heavy, nose down, airspeed bleeding until she coaxed the gear up and let the river valley take them. Below, the Kettle ran brown and swollen with the storm's first rain, and the water was already climbing the banks the way it did before a real blow, greedy, reaching.

Dek rode up front with her, chart spread across his knees, radio headset clamped tight. Petra was wedged in back between the crates — decoy crates, filled with rock and spare parts, because if Halloran's charter caught them she wanted him to see cargo being thrown overboard and believe it. The real evidence rode in a single case under Orsen's seat, small enough to hold in one hand, heavy enough to hold a nation.

"Contact with the court's field frequency in ninety seconds," Dek said. "If we get through."

"If we get through," Orsen agreed, and pushed the throttle forward another inch, and the old Pratt growled like something waking up angry.

The first hour was the worst kind of flying — not dangerous yet, just ugly. Turbulence off the foothills threw the Otter around like a dog shaking a rag. Rain came in sheets that turned the windshield into a river. Orsen flew by feel and by memory, hands light on the yoke, feet working the rudder in small constant corrections, and she thought about nothing else, which was the mercy of flying: it gave you nowhere else to be.

Then the radio coughed.

Not static — static they had plenty of. This was different. A wet, dying crackle, then Dek's voice, tight: "Comms are degrading. I've got the court field at maybe half strength." He tried again. The voice on the other end came through like a man speaking underwater — fragments, a wind report, a bearing, gone.

"Antenna ice?" Petra called from the back.

"Maybe. Maybe the set." Dek pulled the headset down around his neck and looked at Orsen. "How do you feel about navigating without a radio?"

"I feel like Astrid Vale did it with less."

She heard Petra laugh once, short and startled, from the dark of the cabin.

* * *

The front hit them at eleven minutes past four in the afternoon, though you'd never have known it was afternoon; the world went the color of slate and then the color of nothing at all. Orsen had planned to thread a gap she remembered from years ago, a low corridor along the Sable River where the front usually broke apart against the high ground. But the front wasn't breaking anywhere today. It was one solid thing, forty miles deep, and they were in it.

Ice came first as sound — a faint ticking on the cowling, like fingernails. Then as weight. The Otter began to fly heavy, mushy, the stall warning chirping ten knots early. Orsen's mouth went dry. She'd seen ice kill better pilots than herself in airplanes better than this one, and here she was at nine thousand feet with a load of stone ballast and a dead radio and a passenger list of two people who trusted her.

"Pitot heat's holding," she said, mostly to herself. "Wings aren't. We're going down to warmer air or we're going to be a rumor."

"How far down?" Dek asked.

"Below the freezing level. Which means below the tops of things." She didn't say *below the tops of mountains*, but she saw him do the arithmetic anyway. The route notes in Astrid's logbook had been drawn by a pilot who knew these ranges when the maps didn't — pass elevations scrawled in pencil, valleys marked with little arrows, one notation Orsen had read three times before departure: *the second gorge lies lower than it looks; trust the water, not your eyes.*

"Petra!" she called back. "Logbook. Route notes, page marked with the blue grease pencil. Read me the next leg."

There was a rustle, the snap of oilcloth. Then Petra's voice, steady, reading words a dead woman had written twenty years ago in a cockpit much colder than this one:

*"Crossing the divide, hold the north bank of the Sable until the falls, then climb right of centerline. The second gorge lies lower than it looks; trust the water, not your eyes. If the ceiling drops below the rim, put the river under your belly and follow it west — it bends south at the burned stand, and after the bend the ground gives up on being mean."*

A pause. Then, softer: "It bends south at the burned stand."

"That's our way down," Orsen said. "River under the belly. Everybody strap in like you mean it."

She rolled the Otter into the gray and let the earth come up to meet them.

* * *

Descending through cloud in mountainous terrain without radar, without radio, without a working gyroscope she entirely trusted, was the closest thing to blindness Orsen had ever experienced while fully awake. The ice built on the wings in silence — she couldn't see it, couldn't reach it, could only feel it stealing the airplane a pound at a time. The stall horn sang its thin note and she nursed the nose down, trading altitude for clean air, watching the altimeter unwind like a debt coming due.

Eight thousand. Seven. The turbulence turned from random violence into something rhythmic, which meant wind over ridgelines somewhere close, which meant terrain somewhere closer.

"Six thousand five hundred," Dek read. His voice hadn't changed at all. Later, Orsen would think about that — how a man trained to sit in archives and read dead languages could keep his voice level while the ground reached for him. "Still in cloud."

"Five thousand eight hundred." Her own voice surprised her. Calm. Almost bored. Twenty years of practicing invisibility, and it turned out the skill transferred: be no one, feel nothing, fly the airplane.

Then the cloud tore open like wet paper and there it was — the Sable River, brown and furious, running through a gorge whose walls rose sheer on either side, close enough that she could see individual spruce trees leaning out over the water. The falls roared ahead, white in the murk, throwing spray a hundred feet up.

"Right of centerline," Petra recited from the back, and Orsen climbed right of centerline, wingtip seeming to brush the canyon wall, the whole airplane shuddering in the falls' updraft, and then they were over the top and the gorge unrolled ahead of them like a road someone had carved on purpose.

"She knew," Petra said. Her voice cracked on it. "Twenty years ago she flew this exact — she wrote it down so somebody could—"

"Read me the next line," Orsen said gently.

*"After the bend the ground gives up on being mean,"* Petra read, and laughed, wet-eyed, terrified, alive. *"Fuel note: the lake past the burned stand freezes late and lands easy. A pilot could do worse than to love this country. A.V."*

* * *

They flew the river for an hour. It was, Orsen would admit later, in the few hours she allowed herself to admit anything, the finest flying of her life — low, slow, precise, the Otter skimming down a corridor of rain with ice shedding off the wings in chunks that rattled away into the gorge, Dek calling headings off a chart annotated in two hands twenty years apart, Petra reading Astrid's voice into the cockpit line by line. Somewhere in the middle of it Orsen stopped being afraid. Not because the danger lessened, but because the work took all the room fear might have used, and what was left over felt strangely like company. Three people and a ghost, threading a needle in a storm.

Then Halloran's charter found them.

It came out of the murk behind and above — a twin-engine turboprop, black against black, running lights blinking in a pattern Orsen recognized because she'd watched that aircraft circle the narrows twice last week pretending to survey. It didn't close. It didn't need to. It just settled into their six o'clock, patient as a debt, and stayed there.

"He can't force us down," Dek said. "Not in this."

"No. But he doesn't have to." Orsen's hands were steady on the yoke. "He just has to make sure the court knows exactly where we land, and who's waiting when we get there. He's not chasing us. He's escorting us."

"Can you lose him?"

"In this soup? Maybe. But losing him means leaving the river, and the river is the only reason we're alive." She glanced at Dek. "We fly our plan. Let him watch. Ivar's buying us time on the ground — every hour we make him spend following us is an hour he isn't spending on Ivar."

From the back, Petra said quietly, "Ivar knew we'd think that. That's why he smiled when he left. Did you see? He never smiles."

Orsen had seen. She'd thought it looked more like a man saying goodbye. She didn't say so.

* * *

The radio died completely as darkness fell — not fading, just gone, a final pop and then the particular silence of equipment that has stopped pretending. Dek stripped the panel and found the wire burned through at a junction no one would ever have checked in fair weather. He spliced it bare-handed with a knife and a length of safety wire from the tool kit, tongue between his teeth, while Orsen held the airplane level in gusts that wanted to fold them into the hills. When he reconnected the battery the set came back at a whisper — barely enough to receive, useless to transmit.

"They can hear us," Dek said. "We can't answer. And I can't raise the court field anymore — we're below the ridge shadow."

"So we're deaf-ish and mute," Petra said, "being followed by a plane full of men who want the chest, heading toward a strip nobody knows we're landing at."

"Yes," Orsen said. "And the strip has no lights, no tower, and a runway I've seen twice, both times in summer."

Silence from the back. Then Petra, sounding younger than Orsen had ever heard her: "Why does that not scare me as much as it should?"

"Because you're too cold to be scared," Orsen said. "Give it a minute."

But it wasn't that, and they all knew it wasn't that. It was the strange alchemy of the day — the ice survived, the gorge threaded, the dead woman's handwriting steering them true — that had done something to the three of them. Somewhere over the burned stand, where the charred snags stood up out of the rain like the ribs of something enormous, Orsen had caught herself thinking *we*. Not *I*. She'd flown alone so long that the pronoun arrived in her head like a stranger, and she'd welcomed it in out of the weather without examining its papers.

* * *

The lake appeared at 19:40, exactly where Astrid's note promised: a long finger of black water lying in a bowl of hills, its surface steaming faintly where the warmer rain met the colder air. Freezes late, lands easy. Orsen dropped the flaps a notch, tested the ice-heavy wings, and brought the Otter down through the last layer of rain onto water that received them with hardly a slap.

The charter passed overhead a minute later, high, its running lights winking. It circled twice, unhurried, cataloguing them. Then it turned north, toward the nearest place a man could make a phone call, and the night closed behind it.

They anchored in the lee of the eastern shore and sat in the cabin while the rain ticked on the roof, none of them moving to unload, all of them unwilling to break whatever it was the day had made. Petra had the logbook open on her knees even though there was no light to read it by. Dek was cleaning his knife with the thoroughness of a man avoiding conversation. Orsen stared through the windshield at the black water and thought about the frozen lake waiting for them tomorrow, eighty miles east, and the borrowed hall beyond it, and the name she would have to say out loud.

"The second gorge," Petra said suddenly. "Trust the water, not your eyes. She wrote that like a letter. Like she knew someone would need it someday."

"Maybe she did," Orsen said.

"Orsen." Petra's voice was careful. "When we get there. When you testify. What name will you use?"

The rain filled the quiet. Orsen found the true name rising in her throat like a bird that had been caged so long it had forgotten how to sing and only remembered how to leave.

"The one my mother gave me," she said.

Petra nodded slowly, as if that settled something. Dek sheathed his knife. Outside, far off across the black water, headlights swept the far shore — slow, deliberate, two vehicles moving along the lakeshore track where no vehicles had any honest business being at night in a storm.

Halloran's charter hadn't gone home after all. It had gone to fetch the men who'd driven up from the railhead, and they were already here, and the frozen lake was eighty miles further than any of them had thought.

"Everybody stay low," Orsen said, cutting the dome light. "And somebody hand me that logbook. If we're crossing the ice in the dark, we're going to need everything Astrid Vale ever wrote down."
