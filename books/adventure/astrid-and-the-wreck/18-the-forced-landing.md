## 18. The Forced Landing

The lake came up out of the white like a held breath finally let go.

Orsen saw it first as a change in texture — the forest thinning, the spruce giving way to a pale scar of ice, and then she was already committed, throttle back, flaps down, the Otter sinking through air that felt thick as wool. Ice on the wings had been shedding in sheets since the pass; every gust took a piece of the airplane and gave back a shudder. Behind her, Petra rode with both arms wrapped around the document case, holding it against her chest the way you'd hold a child in rough water. Dek sat with his eyes closed, lips moving — counting, maybe, or praying, or doing whatever it was exiles did when they were about to find out whether twenty years of patience was going to pay off or end smeared across a frozen lake two hundred miles short of anywhere.

"Three hundred feet," Orsen said, mostly to herself. "Ice looks solid. Wind's quartering off the ridge."

Nobody answered. She hadn't expected them to.

She flared too high — the ice made depth impossible to judge, all glare and no shadow — and the Otter dropped the last six feet like a stone with opinions. The landing gear took it. The fuselage groaned once, a long complaint of metal and rivets, and then they were sliding, skis hissing across lake ice that rang beneath them like a struck bell, out past a point of black rock, past a wind-carved drift that stood up off the surface like a frozen wave, until the plane slowed and stopped and the silence afterward was so complete that Orsen could hear her own pulse in her ears.

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Petra laughed — a small, cracked, disbelieving sound — and Dek opened his eyes and said, "Where are we?"

"Kettle Lake," Orsen said. "Or close enough. The court's seat is at Fort Meridian. It's a hundred and forty miles northeast." She unbuckled herself and turned around. Her hands were shaking now that they didn't have anything to do. "We're short. Radio's been dead since the pass, and I'm not taking this airplane back up until I've looked at the wings."

Dek's face did something complicated. "A hundred and forty miles," he repeated. "And Halloran knows we're coming."

"Halloran knew before we left," Orsen said. "His charter was on our tail for half the crossing. He'll have radioed ahead. If he's not flying, he's driving — there's a winter road that comes up the east side of the lake. Cat trail, mostly. Two days by ground if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then nobody goes anywhere, including us." She pushed the door open and the cold hit them all at once, a physical blow, air so dry and hard it seemed to ring. She stepped down onto the ski strut and tested the ice with one boot, then her whole weight. Solid. Three feet of it at least, frozen since October, groaning faintly under its own weight the way old ice does, a sound like whale song carried through the floor of the world.

They got the case out and the fuel drums and the tent fly, and by the time the sun swung low behind the western ridges they had a camp: tarp strung between the Otter's wing strut and a boulder on the shore, stove ticking, the chest — the empty chest, the decoy, Ivar's part in the plan — lashed inside the plane where anyone looking through a window would see it and believe what they wanted to believe. The real documents were in the document case, and the document case never left Petra's arms, and Petra slept with it zipped inside her bag with her boots still on.

Orsen took first watch because she knew she wouldn't sleep anyway.

* * *

The night came down clear and enormous. Stars in their thousands, hard and white, and above the northern horizon the faint green breath of aurora moving like smoke underwater. The cold deepened until the lake itself began to talk — long cracking reports rolling across the ice from shore to shore, the sound of a frozen world contracting, each boom followed by a silence so wide it seemed to have mass.

Orsen walked the perimeter with the rifle she'd brought and didn't expect to use, and thought about names.

Her real name. She'd be saying it soon, in a room full of strangers, into a record that would exist forever. She turned it over in her mind like a stone kept in a pocket for years, feeling its weight again after so long pretending it wasn't there. It fit differently now. Everything fit differently. For ten years she had built her life the way you build a snow cave — carefully, hollowly, everything designed to keep the weather out — and in three days she had torn a door in the wall and found people standing outside it with lanterns.

Petra stirred by the fire and said, without opening her eyes, "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"I will. After you." Petra sat up, hair full of frost, seventeen kinds of stubborn in one small body. "Ivar would say something annoying right now. Something about how pilots who don't sleep crash planes, statistically."

"Ivar's not here."

"No," Petra said quietly. "He's not." She looked at the dark eastern shore, where the winter road would come from, and Orsen watched her do the same arithmetic everyone on the expedition had been doing for days. "Do you think he'll come?"

"He signed a contract," Orsen said. "With fine print. And Ivar Lund reads fine print the way other men read scripture."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Orsen agreed. "It isn't."

Petra pulled her bag — and the case inside it — closer to the fire and lay back down. Just before sleep took her she said, "He'll come. He stalled Halloran with an empty chest. That's not a man who stays bought."

Orsen didn't answer. But she watched the eastern shore a long time after that, and somewhere around the coldest hour of the night, far off across the ice, she heard engines. Not aircraft engines. Ground engines — diesel, laboring, the unmistakable grind of tracked vehicles working up a cat trail in the dark.

She woke the others without a word.

* * *

They came at gray dawn, two machines crawling along the eastern shoreline with their headlights still on against the last of the night — a Bombardier and a tracked supply sled behind it, throwing up plumes of blue exhaust that the dead-cold air refused to disperse. Orsen counted heads through her binoculars from the cover of the boulder: four men in the Bombardier, two on the sled. Six. Halloran himself wasn't visible, which meant nothing and less than nothing; Halloran had spent thirty years making sure other people did his arriving.

"They'll want the chest," Dek said, crouched beside her, breath smoking. "They'll ask for it politely. They always start polite."

"And when polite doesn't work?"

"Then they stop being polite, and we're six against three, on open ice, a hundred and forty miles from a court that doesn't know we're alive." Dek's voice was flat, but Orsen could hear the engine underneath it, running hot. "I should have gone overland from the pass. My mistake."

"It was my landing," Orsen said. "My mistake. We share it."

"That's very noble. It changes nothing."

"No," Orsen said. "But it's true, and I've spent ten years avoiding those, so I'm keeping this one."

The Bombardier stopped four hundred yards out on the ice. Engines idling. Nobody got out. A long minute passed, and then a man climbed down from the lead machine — big, parka hood back despite the cold, deliberate in every movement, a man who wanted to be seen clearly — and walked toward the plane alone, hands visible, empty.

Orsen recognized him even before the binoculars confirmed it. Voss. Halloran's senior man. The one who'd delivered the coded note. The one who, ten years ago, had stood in a hangar in another territory and watched while Orsen signed away her name.

"He's alone," Petra whispered.

"For now."

Voss stopped fifty meters from the Otter and called out, his voice carrying flat and strange across the ice. "Miss Dunmore! We know you're armed and we don't care. We're not here for a fight. Mr. Halloran wants the salvage material. That's all. Hand it over and we'll tow you to Fort Meridian ourselves — fuel, repairs, the lot. You'll be there by tomorrow night."

Dek exhaled slowly beside her. "There it is. Polite."

"What do we do?" Petra's hand was white-knuckled on the case strap.

"We stall," Orsen said. "We're good at stalling. We've had practice."

She stood up out of cover — deliberately, visibly, rifle slung rather than held — and walked out onto the ice with her hands loose at her sides, and the cold came through her parka like water through cloth. Voss waited for her, patient as a fence post.

"You're a long way from your charter," Orsen said when she got close enough not to shout.

"Weather grounded it." Voss smiled without warmth. "Ground's more reliable this time of year. You of all people know how unreliable the sky is, Miss Dunmore. Or is it Dunmore?" He let that sit. "Mr. Halloran sends his regards. He says the debt stands cancelled upon delivery. Clean slate. New papers, if you want them. Better ones."

"The chest is in the plane."

"I know the chest is in the plane. I can see it through the window. What I want to know is what's in the chest."

"Salvage," Orsen said. "Rotted paper and river mud. Twenty years at the bottom of the Kettle."

"Then you won't mind if we take it."

"I mind on principle," Orsen said. "It's not mine to give. It belongs to the salvage outfit that chartered me. Contract law. Ask your lawyers."

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes — not anger, something more like recalculation. "The salvage outfit," he said. "Lund's outfit."

"Lund's outfit."

Voss looked past her shoulder at the Otter, at the small figures by the fire, and Orsen watched him decide not to push it — yet. "We'll wait," he said. "Mr. Halloran's on his way. He should arrive within the day. You can discuss principle with him directly." He turned, then paused. "One thing, Miss Dunmore. Whatever arrangement you think you have with Lund — contracts expire. Loyalties get repriced. Everyone in this business learns that eventually. Some learn it standing on ice."

He walked back to the Bombardier, and the machines backed off half a mile and settled in to wait, exhaust plumes rising straight as flagpoles in the still air.

* * *

They waited through the morning. The sun crawled up and laid long blue shadows across the lake, and the temperature climbed to a brutal minus thirty, and nobody on either side of the ice did anything at all. Orsen serviced the plane. Dek wrote in a waterproof notebook, fast and continuous, recording times and positions and everything Voss had said, building a record even here, even now. Petra sat with the case and stared at the eastern road and jumped every time the lake boomed.

It was early afternoon when Orsen heard the second engine.

Different note — lighter, higher-revving, an aircraft engine being worked hard at low altitude. She was out from under the wing before she'd finished identifying it, binoculars up, heart doing something unprofessional in her chest.

It was the Beaver. Their Beaver. Ivar's paint scheme, Ivar's patched cowl, coming up the lake valley low and slow through the afternoon glare, circling the camp once — a wing rock, almost jaunty, absolutely Ivar — and putting down on the ice a quarter mile west of the Otter with a long, controlled slide that ended precisely, deliberately, between Orsen's camp and Halloran's machines.

He taxied the last hundred yards. Climbed out alone. He was wearing his good coat, the one he wore for meetings, and under his arm he carried a leather document case, and he walked across the ice toward Voss's position without so much as a glance at Orsen's camp, and Orsen felt something in her stomach drop through the ice and into the black water below.

"No," Petra breathed beside her. "No, no, no—"

"Wait," Orsen said.

"Wait? He just landed next to them—"

"Wait," Orsen said again, and gripped her shoulder, and made them both watch.

Ivar reached the Bombardier. There was a conversation — she could see Voss's posture change, see the man's hands come up in a gesture that was either greeting or protest. Then Ivar opened the leather case, took out papers, and handed them over. Voss read. His shoulders changed again. One of the other men came down from the sled to look, and then another, and the reading went on a long time, and the longer it went on the stiller Ivar stood, hands in pockets, weight on his heels, a man watching a slow leak spring in someone else's boat.

Finally Voss looked up. Said something sharp. Ivar shrugged — Orsen knew that shrug, had seen it over engine quotes and fuel bills and the price of everything, the shrug that said *the numbers are the numbers* — and pointed, unhurried, at the Otter, at the chest visible through its window, and then at himself, and then at the papers in Voss's hands.

Then he turned and walked toward Orsen's camp, leaving Voss standing on the ice holding a contract that was apparently eating him alive.

He arrived grinning, frost in his beard, and said, "Somebody order a lawyer?"

"You absolute—" Petra started forward and then couldn't finish, and just stood there glaring at him with wet eyes.

"Hello to you too." He looked at Orsen. "Voss says you're claiming the chest belongs to the salvage outfit. Correct?"

"That's what I told him."

"Good instinct. Because it does. Charter agreement, clause fourteen, sub-section three: all recovered materials remain the property of the contracting party — Lund Salvage and Survey — until such time as title is formally transferred, and any third party asserting claim must present original documentation of ownership to the contracting party, in person, at the site of recovery." He tapped the leather case. "Which I am. And they can't. Halloran's consortium doesn't officially exist. No registration, no articles, no paper trail — you know how they operate. Which means legally speaking, they are nobody. And nobody has no claim. Voss's own retainer — the one I signed, the one they're paying me under — defines the client as 'the principal,' unnamed, unregistered. Unenforceable against a titled owner. I checked it four times on the flight up. Twice out loud, which Petra would tell you is normal for me."

"You flew here," Orsen said slowly, "to stand between us and six armed men, using fine print."

"I flew here," Ivar said, "because I read the fine print in my own retainer three weeks ago and I've been sick about it ever since. Clause nine. 'The contractor agrees to destroy or render inaccessible all recovered materials.' Destroy. I thought it meant reputation management. Scrubbing a wreck site so some rich family's shame stayed buried." He shook his head. "Then your diver found tool marks on a fuselage, and I started reading everything twice." He looked out at the Bombardier, at Voss still standing there with the papers. "So yes. Fine print. It's the only weapon they gave me, so I'm going to beat them with it until it breaks."

"How long does it buy?" Dek asked, coming up with his notebook already open.

"Until Halloran arrives. He's the principal. He can re-paper the claim, or buy the title outright, or make me an offer I can't refuse in front of witnesses. A day. Maybe two." Ivar met Orsen's eyes, and for once there was no shrug in his face at all. "After that, Miss Dunmore, you need to be gone. Fuel's in my pods — enough to top you off and run the gap north before the next front. Take the case and fly tonight, if the weather window holds. Don't wait for me to win. Winning was never the point. Standing here was."

Behind him, out on the ice, Voss had retreated to the Bombardier, and the machines were turning, repositioning — closer now, five hundred yards, headlights sweeping once across the Otter's tail like a slow, deliberate touch.

And far to the south, down the valley, Orsen caught it through the binoculars: another plume of exhaust on the winter road, bigger, slower, unhurried.

Halloran was coming.
