## 19. Halloran's Truth

The lake had frozen in the night like a held breath finally let go.

Orsen stood at the edge of it with her hands buried deep in her coat pockets and watched the light come up gray over the far shore, where the spruce stood in a ragged black line against a sky the color of old tin. The Beaver sat out on the ice a hundred yards from shore, tied down with ropes to two frozen boulders, her wings furred white with rime from the storm crossing. Somewhere under that ice, forty feet down, lay Astrid Vale's wreck — and somewhere in Orsen's duffel, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the chest that had started all of this.

Her breath came out in slow silver plumes. She counted them the way she counted everything now: engine cycles, fuel gallons, hours of dark left before Halloran's men arrived by ground.

Dek had said they'd come by ground because the weather had closed every strip within three hundred miles. That meant snow machines or a tracked truck on the winter road, which meant they'd be cold, tired, and angry by the time they got here. Angry men made mistakes. She was counting on that too.

"You should eat something."

Petra came up beside her, boots crunching on the fresh snow, and pressed a tin cup of tea into Orsen's hands. The girl's fingers were still raw from yesterday's dive — she'd gone down one last time through a hole they'd chopped in the ice at first light, in water so cold it could stop a heart in four minutes, and come back up with Astrid's personal case strapped to her chest like a child. Her lips had been blue for an hour afterward. Now there was color in her face again, and something else too, something harder and brighter than color.

"I can't eat," Orsen said.

"You flew us through a thunderstorm over a mountain pass with a failing radio." Petra blew on her own tea. "You can eat."

It wasn't an argument worth having. Orsen drank the tea instead. It was terrible — Ivar's coffee grounds stretched twice, brewed in melted snow — and it was the best thing she'd tasted in years, because Petra had made it for her, and because Petra still looked at her like a pilot worth looking up to, even after everything Orsen had confessed around the camp stove two nights ago. Even after the false name. Even after the debt.

"They'll be here by midmorning," Orsen said. "Maybe sooner."

"I know." Petra looked out across the ice. "Ivar says he can hold them until the court sits tomorrow. His contract — he showed me the fine print. It's beautiful, actually. Like watching someone fence with a legal document."

"It won't be enough."

"No," Petra agreed quietly. "But it buys you the morning."

* * *

The morning bought them nothing.

At half past nine, Orsen heard them before she saw them — the flat two-stroke buzz of snow machines carrying across the ice like wasps in a bottle. Three of them, coming down the frozen river channel from the south, kicking up a plume of powder that hung in the dead air long after they'd passed beneath it.

She walked out onto the lake to meet them. Alone. That had been her decision, argued through the night and settled at dawn: if this went wrong, it would go wrong for her and no one else. Dek had called it noble nonsense. Petra had called it worse. But Ivar, of all people, had understood. "You're the one he came to buy," Ivar had said, adjusting his glasses with that careful, courteous gesture of his. "And a man who comes to buy doesn't want witnesses to what he's buying. Go alone. We'll be close enough."

Close enough meant Dek and Petra standing at the tree line with Astrid's case between them, and Ivar sitting in the tent with the retainer contract spread out on the table like a drawn weapon. Close enough meant the Beaver loaded and warm, though Orsen had told no one but herself what she'd do if it came to flying.

Halloran stopped his machine thirty feet away and killed the engine. He swung off, stamped his boots, and looked around at the lake, the wreck site, the little camp, with the expression of a man inspecting property he already owned. Behind him, his two men dismounted and stood with their hands in their arctic mitts, faces wind-burned and unreadable. One of them Orsen recognized — Sorel, who had driven her to the airfield in Kestrel Bay eleven years ago, the night she signed her name away. He wouldn't meet her eyes.

"Miss Dunmore," Halloran said. "Or should I say—"

"Don't," Orsen said.

He smiled. He was a compact man, neat as a ledger line, with gray at his temples and a voice like a handshake — warm, firm, forgettable. He'd been fixing things for the consortium for twenty years, and he wore the work lightly, the way some men wore good suits.

"Fair enough. Names are such slippery things out here." He gestured at the lake. "You have something of ours. A chest, recovered from a wreck we hold salvage rights to. My client is prepared to be generous. Your debt — all of it, every cent you owe, cleared this morning by wire. Your papers renewed. Your name—" he paused delicately "—your real name, kept exactly where it's been for eleven years. Buried. You fly home tonight a free woman, Miss Dunmore, and none of this ever happened."

"And the crew?"

"The crew goes home. Mr. Lund fulfills his contract — the chest contents are destroyed, condition unverified, standard clause. The girl keeps her certification. The surveyor—" Halloran's smile thinned by a degree "—the surveyor was never here. Nobody was ever here. That's the beauty of this country, isn't it? It forgets."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment. The wind had died completely; the silence was enormous, the kind of silence that makes your own heartbeat audible.

"You've been doing this a long time," she said.

"Long enough to know when a deal is good."

"It's not the deal I want to talk about." She reached into her coat, slowly, letting both men see the movement, and drew out not the chest but a bundle of pages — photocopies, made in Dek's portable kit, of the logbook's most damaged entries. She'd spent two nights with a magnifying glass and a hair dryer coaxing the ink back into legibility, and what she'd found had rearranged the architecture of her whole life. "I want to talk about August 19th, twenty years ago. A floatplane out of Vessland, headed for the border lakes. Carrying documents."

Something moved behind Halloran's eyes. It was very small and very fast, and if Orsen hadn't spent eleven years learning to read men who lied professionally, she might have missed it entirely.

"Ancient history," he said.

"Not to me." She unfolded the top page. "Astrid Vale logged a courier contact at Kestrel Bay. A fixer who arranged her fuel, held her mail, took delivery of a sealed case she was told contained survey charts. Except it didn't contain charts. It contained a duplicate set of the documents — enough for the consortium to know exactly what she was carrying and exactly where she'd hide the originals. The fixer sold her route, her schedule, and her cargo manifest to the consortium's operations desk. Three days later she was shot down over the Kettle River."

The wind stirred, just barely, moving the frost smoke along the ice in slow ribbons.

"That's a story," Halloran said. His voice was still pleasant. His hands were perfectly still at his sides. "A sad story. Unprovable."

"The logbook names him." Orsen held his gaze. "Not the name you use now. The name you used then. And it describes him — right hand missing the top joint of the ring finger, lost to a propeller strike in '41, per the Kestrel Bay aerodrome medical file that Dek pulled last week from the territorial archive." She let that sit. "You want to see how steady my hands are, Halloran? I've been flying with these hands for twenty years. They don't shake."

Sorel shifted his weight. The other man — younger, broader — glanced sideways at his partner, and Orsen saw it happen: the small, involuntary recalculation of a hired man discovering that the job has changed shape. Men like these didn't sign on for murder trials. They signed on for muscle and mileage, and the difference between the two was usually explained to them never.

"You're bluffing," Halloran said. "Even if that logbook says what you claim, it's water-damaged garbage. No court in the territory would admit it."

"This isn't the territory's court." Orsen took a step closer, boots ringing on the ice. "That's the whole point of exile courts, Halloran. They take testimony the other kind won't touch. Dek has been building this file for twenty years. Astrid's flight records. The consortium's charter payments — routed through three shells, but money leaves footprints even you can't sweep. And now the testimony of the courier himself, delivered under oath, in a proceeding your principals can't buy, can't bury, and can't bomb into a river." She paused. "Unless, of course, the courier decides to cooperate. Witnesses do better when they come forward voluntarily. I'm told the court looks kindly on it. Reduced exposure. Protection. A future."

For the first time, Halloran's smile died altogether.

"You'd testify," he said slowly, "against me. To save yourself."

"I'd testify about you," Orsen said. "There's a difference. Everything I did — the silence I sold, the years I hid — that's mine, and I'll carry it into that hall myself, under my own name, and let them weigh it. But you?" She shook her head. "You weren't silent, Halloran. Silence is passive. You were active. You read her mail. You sold her route. You handed a living woman to the men who killed her, and then you spent twenty years collecting debts from people like me to make sure nobody ever said it out loud." Another step. The ice groaned somewhere beneath them, a long tectonic sigh, as if the lake itself were leaning in. "I was a coward. You were a courier. Cowards get forgiven sometimes. Couriers get tried."

The younger man had stopped looking at Orsen altogether. He was looking at Halloran, and his face had gone carefully blank in the way faces go blank when a man is doing arithmetic he doesn't like. Sorel was staring at the middle distance, jaw working, and Orsen understood that he was remembering too — the night drive, the sealed case, the small transactions that had seemed like paperwork at the time and were starting, here on the ice, to look like something else.

"Gentlemen," Halloran said, without turning his head. "Check the aircraft."

"For what?"

"Whatever she's hiding in it. Move."

Neither man moved. The word hung there, ordinary and enormous, and Orsen felt the moment tilt beneath her like a floatplane catching a crosswind — that instant where everything balances on the pilot's next input.

"She's offering you a way out," Sorel said quietly. It was almost a question.

"I'm offering you a way out," Orsen corrected. "Both of you. Walk to the tree line, sit down by the fire, and when the court asks, tell them exactly what you were hired to do and by whom. That's all. You're not defendants. You're evidence. There's a difference too, and it matters."

The younger man took one step backward. Just one. But it was the loudest sound on the lake.

Halloran turned then, finally, to look at his men, and Orsen watched him understand — watched the comprehension move through him like ice shifting, the slow collapse of a structure that had held weight for twenty years. He had built his career on the certainty that everyone's loyalty had a price, because his own had, and because he'd set the prices himself. He had never once considered that a person might choose a price that cost them everything, and pay it anyway.

"You'll destroy yourself," he said to Orsen. "Your name, your license, your clean record. You'll stand up in a borrowed hall in front of exiles and radicals and hand them your whole life to pick apart. For what? A dead woman who can't thank you. A country that doesn't exist anymore."

"That's where you're wrong," Orsen said. "It exists. It exists right here." She touched her coat, over the pocket where the logbook rode. "It existed in her, every hour she flew toward that border knowing what was chasing her. It exists in the girl who dove into water cold enough to kill her to bring that woman's voice back up into the daylight. You erased it from the maps, Halloran. You never once managed to erase it from anybody." She looked past him, at the two men who were no longer quite his. "That's the thing about truth. You keep treating it like cargo — something you can dump mid-route when the weight gets dangerous. But it's not cargo. It's ballast. It's the only reason any of us stay level."

Halloran stood very still. The frost smoke curled around his knees. Somewhere behind Orsen, faintly, she heard the tent flap — Ivar coming out, contract in hand, punctual as a court clerk — and she knew without looking that Dek and Petra had stepped out of the trees as well, three small figures at the edge of all that white, witnesses at last.

"You think you've won," Halloran said. His voice had gone quiet, and quiet, Orsen thought, was more frightening than anything loud he might have done. "You think a room full of exiles reading documents changes anything. My client has survived wars, sanctions, three governments. In a month this court is a footnote and you're a fugitive with no papers and no name worth having."

"Then I'll fly anyway," Orsen said. "I've done it before. Turns out I'm good at it."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Halloran reached inside his coat, and Orsen's heart slammed once against her ribs — but what he drew out was not a gun. It was a satellite phone, its antenna stubby and black against the pale sky. He looked at it. He looked at Orsen. And Orsen saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished — saw him weigh the consortium's reach against a courtroom, a debt against a docket, twenty years of careful work against one woman's sworn testimony — and saw which side the weight came down on.

He did not call anyone.

Instead he held out his hand, palm open, and said, in the voice of a man setting down something very heavy: "The debt. What are your terms?"

Orsen almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was steadier than she had any right to expect.

"No terms. You don't buy your way out of this one, Halloran. That's the entire point." She nodded toward the tree line, toward the fire, toward the borrowed hall waiting a day's flight south. "You walk over there, you sit down, and tomorrow you raise your right hand same as everybody else. You give them the names, the accounts, the order that came down from the operations desk. All of it. And maybe — maybe — the court remembers you cooperated."

"They'll take my business. My licenses. Everything I've built."

"Yes," Orsen said. "That's what testimony costs. I checked the price myself. It's steep." She let a breath go, long and white in the freezing air. "Believe me. I paid it last night, when I burned my false papers in the stove. Eleven years of Dunmore, gone up the flue. And you know what's left?" She spread her empty hands. "Me. Just me. Turns out she was enough."

Halloran closed his fist around the phone. When he spoke again, his voice was nearly human, and that was somehow the worst thing yet.

"You know what she asked me, that night at Kestrel Bay? Vale. She looked at me across the counter and asked if the charts were safe to carry. And I told her yes." He stared out across the ice, at the black shape of the wreck beneath them, invisible and everywhere. "Twenty years I've told myself I didn't know what they'd do. Twenty years."

"Now you say it where it counts," Orsen said gently, "or you never say it at all."

And Halloran — neat, careful, unforgivable Halloran — walked.

He walked past her without a word, boots crunching, shoulders bowed under a weight no wire transfer had ever lifted, toward the tree line and the fire and the witness chair waiting at the end of it. His men followed. Sorel paused beside Orsen, just for a second, and said, so quietly the wind nearly took it: "The night they sent me to collect your signature. I'm sorry. I didn't know what I was carrying either." Then he was gone, and the lake was hers again — the ice, the wreck below, the plane above, the whole vast white morning opening like a page.

Petra reached her first. She didn't hug her, exactly; she stood close and put one chapped hand briefly on Orsen's sleeve, which from Petra was a parade. Dek was already talking — logistics, the flight south, the seating order in the borrowed hall — and Ivar was folding his contract away with the satisfaction of a man who has, at last, read the right document.

Orsen let them plan. She walked out alone onto the ice instead, to the spot above the wreck, and stood there while the sun finally broke the eastern ridgeline and laid a long gold road across the frozen lake, all the way to her boots.

Below her feet, in the dark and the cold, Astrid Vale waited to speak.

Tomorrow, Orsen thought. Tomorrow we carry you home.

Behind her, already, Dek was shouting about the weather window — and pointing south, where the horizon had begun, very faintly, to bruise with the season's next storm.
