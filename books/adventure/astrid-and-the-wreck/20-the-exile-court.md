## 20. The Exile Court

The hall had been a granary once, before the exile court borrowed it, and it still smelled of rye dust and old timber — a sweetness under everything, like bread baked in another country. Dek Varga stood at the back near the double doors and watched the light come down through the high windows in long dusty columns, catching the motes the way it catches them in churches, and thought: twenty years I have imagined this room. Twenty years, and in every version of it I was alone.

He was not alone now. The hall held perhaps sixty people on folding chairs and mismatched benches: exiles in good coats gone shiny at the elbows, an old woman with a Vessland ribbon pinned to her lapel though the color had faded to almost nothing, two clerks with fountain pens and paper so fine it whispered when they turned it, a stenographer whose fingers hovered over a battered machine like a pianist waiting for the downbeat. At the front, beneath a banner of blue and silver that someone had sewn by hand — you could see the uneven stitches if you looked, and Dek looked, because he had spent his life learning where things were imperfect and therefore true — sat the three magistrates of the exile court. Not judges; they were careful about that word. Magistrates. Keepers of a record no nation currently recognized, in a hall that smelled of grain, in a country that was not a country anymore except in the memories of everyone present.

And on the table before them, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the same cord Orsen Dunmore used on her cargo nets, lay Astrid Vale's testimony.

Dek's hands were steady. He noticed this with a kind of distant surprise, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a moment of crisis — *oh, that's still going.* For twenty years his hands had not been steady. They had shaken in the archive in Torvask when they told him the expedition files were sealed. They had shaken in the hotel room in Meridian City when the consortium's lawyer slid the photograph across the table — young Dek, younger than Petra, holding a Vessland survey flag, *your family emigrated illegally, Mr. Varga, we'd hate for that to become a problem* — and Dek had signed the silence they offered him and hated himself with a thoroughness that became, over time, simply the texture of who he was. His hands had shaken the night he first read the waterlogged logbook pages Orsen had dried above her stove, pages that smelled of river mud and kerosene, and seen the courier mark pressed into the corner of the final entry like a signature on a death warrant.

But today his hands were steady, and he understood why only as the first magistrate rose to speak: because there was nothing left to protect himself from. The hiding was over. Whatever happened in this room, it would happen to him under his own name — his real one, the one with the soft consonants of the Vessland interior in it, the name his mother had called across a farmyard that no longer existed on any map. He had given it to the clerk that morning, spelling it twice, watching the pen move across the page, and something in his chest had unclenched that he had not known was clenched.

"Let the record show," said the senior magistrate, a woman named Ilse Brandt whose voice carried the flat authority of someone who had spent thirty years being the last adult in the room, "that on this day, in session of the exile court of Vessland, testimony is entered concerning the loss of aircraft V-114, pilot Astrid Vale, and the circumstances thereof."

The stenographer's fingers came down. The sound filled the hall like rain.

* * *

They read the documents aloud.

That was the part Dek had not anticipated — that it would be voices, not paper. He had imagined the evidence entering the record the way evidence entered consortium ledgers: silently, in columns, deniable forever. But Magistrate Brandt had insisted. "A document read aloud," she had said the night before, in the borrowed office that smelled of lamp oil, "is a witness speaking. A document filed is a body in a drawer. We have enough bodies in drawers."

So they read. A junior clerk stood at the lectern with the oilcloth-wrapped bundle and unwrapped it the way you unwrap something fragile and possibly alive, and began with the flight manifest of the Kettle River expedition, twenty-two years old, the ink browned but legible. Then the survey annexes — the ones that made the old woman in the faded ribbon press her hand flat against her sternum — establishing, in the dry language of triangulation and benchmark elevation, that the border of Vessland ran forty kilometers further east than any map printed after the Annexation showed. Forty kilometers. Dek watched the number travel through the room like a current. An old man two rows ahead of him took off his hat. A woman born in exile mouthed the names of villages along the line — *Ostrava Fen, Kell Hollow, the White Steps* — villages she had never seen except in her parents' voices.

Then the harder documents. The consortium's internal memorandum, stamped with the courier mark Dek knew better than his own handwriting, authorizing "disruption of survey operations." The requisition for a charter pilot, payment routed through Halloran Freight & Salvage. And finally the last pages of the logbook, dried and flattened and photographed, read aloud in the clerk's careful neutral voice while the original lay open on green felt:

*"They've recalled my escort. The new charter man won't say who hired him. Cargo is sealed and I am ordered to fly the eastern route alone tomorrow, weather be damned. If anything happens to me—"*

The clerk stopped. Looked up. "The entry ends here," he said. "The remainder of the page was destroyed by immersion."

The silence afterward was the kind Dek had only heard twice before in his life: at his father's funeral, and once in a border station when a guard read out a list of names of people who would not be coming home. Sixty people not breathing. Somewhere outside, a crow argued with the wind, indifferent, alive.

Astrid Vale had been dead for twenty-two years, and in this room she spoke anyway. That was the whole point of the court, Dek realized — not justice, not yet, not with no nation to enforce its rulings. The point was that the dead got to finish their sentences here.

* * *

They broke at midday for bread and salted fish and strong tea poured from dented urns, and Dek found Orsen standing outside the side door, looking at the street.

She was dressed in what she clearly considered her best — a wool coat brushed clean, boots polished with candle wax — and she wore both like a disguise, the way she wore everything. She had refused tea. She was watching a delivery truck back up to the fishmonger's across the way with the total attention of a woman memorizing escape routes, and Dek thought: she has been doing that for ten years without knowing it. You can take the woman out of the running, but the running stays in the shoulders.

"They're going to call me soon," she said, without turning.

"Yes."

"I've been practicing." She said it flatly, but her hand went to her coat pocket, where Dek knew she kept the burned corner of her false papers — she hadn't been able to throw even that away yet, not entirely, not until tonight. "Saying it. My name. In the cabin, out loud, to the stove. It sounds wrong. Like a word in a language I used to speak."

"It'll sound right in there," Dek said. "It sounded right when I gave mine this morning."

Orsen finally looked at him. Her eyes were the gray of the Kettle River in November, and right now they were frightened in a way she would deny to her grave. "What if the consortium—"

"The consortium's reach ended at the storm front," Dek said. "Halloran's men are being dealt with. Ivar's contract bought us the week. And even if none of that were true—" he paused, chose honesty, because honesty was the only currency this room accepted, "—even if they came through those doors, it would be worth it. You know that. You flew through ice to get here."

"I flew through ice because stopping felt worse."

"That's also how most true things get said."

She almost smiled. Then the door opened behind them and a clerk said, "Dunmore? Orsen Dunmore?" — and Dek watched her flinch at the false name like a hand raised in warning, and then, visibly, deliberately, straighten.

"Not for much longer," she said, and walked inside.

* * *

Dek took his seat at the back and did not breathe properly for the next hour.

They swore her in — the oath was the old one, the Vessland one, about speaking truth though the sky fall, and Dek saw the words land on her strangely, a liturgy she'd never learned but recognized the shape of. Then Magistrate Brandt asked her to state her name for the record.

And Orsen Dunmore stood in a borrowed granary in a country that wasn't on any map, in front of sixty strangers and three magistrates and a stenographer whose keys waited like held breath, and she was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Dek leaned forward. Long enough that the old woman with the ribbon tilted her head.

"My name," Orsen said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she started again, and this time it came out steady and low and completely unhidden, "is Sable Ishikawa. I was born in Corren Bay. I changed my name eleven years ago because the consortium bought my silence and I let them, and I have flown under a lie ever since. This is the first time I've said my own name out loud since."

The stenographer's keys clattered. The clerk wrote it down. *Sable Ishikawa,* entered into a record that would outlive every map that had ever erased her.

Something moved through the hall — not applause, nothing so simple. The old woman with the ribbon bowed her head. The man who had removed his hat earlier put it over his heart. It was the gesture people make at gravesides, Dek thought, and also at births, and he could not have said which it was here, and suspected it was both.

Then she testified.

She told it plainly, in the unadorned language of a woman who had spent a decade refusing adjectives: the wing in the low water, the dive, the chest with the seal. The logbook drying above her stove, page by page, like a wound closing. Halloran's offer and Halloran's threat. The night flight into the fog. The crew meeting around the camp stove where she had confessed everything and Petra had turned away from her and the fire had popped and nobody had spoken for a full minute. She did not embellish. She did not excuse herself. When Magistrate Brandt asked whether she had known, before pulling the chest from the river, that the consortium was involved in Astrid Vale's death, Orsen said, "I knew what they were. I told myself the wreck was just a wreck. That was my contribution — eleven years of telling myself convenient things." And the hall absorbed it, and no one condemned her, because everyone in that room had done some smaller version of the same thing to survive, and they knew the taste.

When she described the storm crossing — ice on the wings, the radio failing, Petra navigating by a dead woman's route notes — her voice changed for the first time. It warmed. "She was guiding us," Orsen said. "Astrid. Every waypoint in that logbook was a place she'd trusted enough to write down. We just followed her home." And Dek, who had spent twenty years following a dead woman through archives and border stations and the cold corridors of other people's guilt, felt his throat close, because that was exactly it, that was the whole of it, and she had found the words in an afternoon that he hadn't found in two decades.

At the end, Magistrate Brandt asked the final question, the one the whole session turned on: "Do you swear that the documents presented today are authentic, and that your account is true?"

Orsen looked at the chest on the table. At the logbook. At the empty space in the air where, somewhere, a pilot named Astrid Vale might have been listening.

"I swear it," she said. "Though the sky fall."

* * *

It was over by late afternoon, and the light through the high windows had gone gold and long, and the magistrates entered their findings into the record: that Astrid Vale died by deliberate act; that the consortium bore responsibility; that the borders of Vessland, as surveyed, be entered into permanent testimony against the day — and here Magistrate Brandt paused, and looked around the granary at the exiles and the faded ribbons and the clerks with their whispering paper — *against the day* they might mean something again.

No one cheered. That wasn't the custom. But when the session closed, the old woman with the Vessland ribbon rose and began, very quietly, to sing — four bars of something old, a harvest song or a lament, Dek couldn't tell and didn't need to — and one by one the others joined, and the sound rose into the rafters among the rye dust and the golden light, and Dek Varga stood at the back with his steady hands at his sides and let it wash over him and did not sing, because someone had to keep watch, even now. Even at the end of the watching.

Orsen found him there. Her face was wet and she hadn't bothered wiping it, which from her was a revolution.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Now?" Dek looked out the high windows, at the crows and the darkening sky and the small plane on the field beyond, already wearing its new registration, already wearing her name. "Now the record exists. Now it can't be unmade. And now—" he smiled, tired and true, "—now you go home, Sable Ishikawa, and find out who you are when nobody's hunting you."

She nodded slowly. Then her gaze drifted past him, toward the doors, where a figure had just come in from the cold — travel-worn, carrying nothing but a small bag, asking the clerk at the desk a question Dek couldn't hear. The clerk pointed. Toward Orsen.

The figure turned. A young woman, dark-haired, with Astrid Vale's exact stubborn jaw, standing in the doorway of the hall with snow melting on her shoulders, looking across sixty heads directly at the pilot who had brought her mother home.

"Orsen," Dek said quietly. "I think someone's been looking for you longer than I have."
