## 21. Aftermath, Upriver

The frost came early that year, and with it a stillness Orsen had never known the territory capable of.

She noticed it first in the mornings — the way the Kettle River ran quiet under its skin of new ice at the edges, the way the spruce stood without wind, holding their snow like a held breath. For ten years she had read this country as a place that concealed things: her plane, her money, her name. Now it seemed to be simply waiting, patient as bedrock, for whatever came next.

The consortium's reach broke the way ice breaks — not all at once, but in long reports from downriver, each one arriving like a crack traveling through a frozen lake.

First it was Halloran's office in Fort Meridian, shuttered by the second week of October. Then the fuel contract — the one that had kept every independent pilot in the territory dancing to terms set in a city none of them would ever see — was voided by a court order Dek himself had helped draft, his careful exile-court hand visible in every clause. The charter companies that had quietly fed the consortium's interests began announcing "restructuring" in language so bland it could only have been written by lawyers who knew exactly what was coming.

And Halloran himself was gone.

Not dead — Orsen checked, more than once, in the way of a woman who has spent a decade learning what survival costs. Gone. Flown out on a chartered turboprop two days after the testimony ended, before the warrants could be typed, let alone served. Some said he'd gone south to Vessland's old capital, to answer questions there in exchange for immunity. Some said he'd simply vanished into the same machinery that had once employed him. Orsen found she didn't much care which was true. What mattered was that when she landed at Fort Meridian now, nobody looked at her sideways. Nobody knew her name and flinched.

That took some getting used to.

* * *

Ivar Lund came upriver on the last barge of the season, carrying crates of salvage equipment and a look on his face like a man who had finally set down something heavy.

He found her at the dock, re-caulking a seam on the *Kestrel*'s float while the morning fog burned off. He didn't call out; just stood at the end of the planks with his hands in his coat pockets, watching her work until she straightened and wiped her hands on her thighs.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm deciding how to say something." He came down the dock, boots ringing on wet timber. "Petra and I talked it over. The outfit — Vale Salvage, what's left of the retainer, the gear, the contracts. We're keeping it."

Orsen set down her caulking iron. "That's yours. You earned—"

"We're renaming it." He said it plainly, no ceremony. "Astrid Vale Salvage and Survey. Honest work. Wreck recovery, river mapping, cargo runs where the cargo isn't a secret. Petra's going to run dive operations come spring. Dek drew up the papers." A pause. "We'd like you to fly for us. Under your own name. On letterhead."

She looked out at the river. The fog was lifting off the water in long white ribbons, and somewhere upstream a loon called once, twice, and went silent.

"Ivar," she said carefully, "you know what I did. I flew for them. Before any of this. I carried things I shouldn't have, and I kept my mouth shut about worse, and if I'd been braver twenty years ago Astrid might have—"

"If you'd been braver twenty years ago," Ivar interrupted, not unkindly, "you'd be dead, and the chest would still be in the river, and Astrid's daughter would still believe her mother died drunk into a canyon." He shrugged. "The court didn't need you to be innocent, Dunmore. It needed you to be honest. Turns out those are different jobs."

It was the closest thing to absolution anyone had offered her, and it came wrapped in an insult, which felt right. She put out her hand. He shook it, grip dry and firm, and that was that.

"Fair warning," she said. "I'm expensive. And I land hard in crosswinds."

"Petra says you land like a prayer being answered. Her words. I'd have said something less flattering."

* * *

The letterhead arrived three weeks later, printed in Fort Meridian on paper heavy enough to feel like a promise: **ASTRID VALE SALVAGE AND SURVEY — Licensed River Operations, Kettle Basin**. Below it, in small type, the roster. *Pilot-in-command: O. Dunmore.*

Not a false name. Not a borrowed one. Just hers — the one she'd been born with, the one she'd paid a fixer good money to bury, the one she'd spoken aloud for the first time in a borrowed hall while a clerk recorded it in testimony that would outlive everyone in the room.

She pinned the letterhead above the workbench in her cabin and found she couldn't stop glancing at it, the way you check a bruise to see if it still hurts.

* * *

The repainting took four days, because she refused to rush it.

The *Kestrel* sat on the shingle below her cabin, hull propped on rollers, while she stripped the old registration off the tail with heat gun and scraper. The false number — the one tied to a dead man's paperwork in a registry three countries away — came away in brittle curls that she burned in the woodstove, one handful at a time, watching the flames take them.

Then she masked out the new letters and painted.

She did it by hand, with a brush, because a spray rig would have been faster and faster wasn't the point. The new registration matched her real name now, filed properly, witnessed by Dek, legal in every jurisdiction that mattered. Each stroke of the brush was slow and deliberate, white over weathered blue, and she worked through two days of light snowfall under a tarp strung between the spruce, cursing the cold, warming the paint can by the stove, starting over twice when the lines wobbled.

On the fourth day she pulled the masking tape back and stepped off into the snow to look.

The letters stood crisp against the tail: clean, official, hers. Anyone with a pair of binoculars could read them from the air. Any customs officer, any creditor, any man from the consortium's old network could look them up and find a woman with nothing left to hide — no aliases, no shell registrations, no buried past worth digging for because it was all in the public record now, testified, sworn, done.

It should have felt like exposure. Instead, standing in the falling snow with paint on her knuckles, Orsen felt lighter than she had since she was twenty-three years old.

She went inside, poured two fingers of the good whiskey Ivar had brought, and drank it looking at the letterhead on the wall.

* * *

Winter settled in properly after that, and with it a rhythm she hadn't lived since before the running started.

Mornings at the cabin: stove lit before dawn, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in, the radio crackling with the territory's traffic — Ivar checking in from the salvage yard, Petra reporting ice conditions on the dive sites, Dek's dry voice reading out court dates and filing deadlines from Fort Meridian like a man reciting scripture he half-believed in but respected deeply.

Afternoons flying short runs when the weather allowed: mail, medicine, parts for the trapline crews. The same work she'd always done. But now when she landed at a river camp and someone asked who was flying her, she said her name out loud, plainly, and watched it land without consequence. Small thing. Enormous thing.

Evenings she read. Astrid's logbook, mostly — the pages fully dried now, pressed flat under glass on her table, the handwriting steady and sure right up until the final entry cut off mid-word. Dek had copies. The exile court had copies. But Orsen kept the original, and some nights she read it not for information but for company, the way you'd sit across from someone you wished you'd known.

One evening in late November, Petra flew in unannounced, landing on the river ice below the cabin in a borrowed ski-plane, and stayed two nights. They didn't talk about the chest, or the trial, or the diving. They talked about engines, and routes, and whether a certain bend in the Kettle froze safe enough to land on by January. Petra laughed easily now, in a way she hadn't during the expedition, and Orsen realized, watching her gesture wildly over a chart by lamplight, that the girl had grown years in a single season.

"Spring survey starts in April," Petra said, lacing her boots by the door on the last morning. "Ivar wants to run the full upper basin. There's talk of a government contract — actual government, not the kind that owns people." She grinned. "You in?"

Orsen looked past her, through the window, at the *Kestrel* sitting white-rimed on the ice, her new name bright on the tail even in the grey light.

"Yeah," she said. "I'm in."

* * *

Christmas came and went quietly. Dek sent a card with a court seal on it and nothing else, which from Dek was practically a sonnet. Ivar sent a crate of engine spares and a note: *For the spring. Don't crash.* Petra sent nothing, then showed up in person two days later with a bottle of aquavit and a story about a moose on the runway at Meridian that had her laughing so hard she cried.

New Year's Eve, Orsen flew alone.

Just a short hop — up the Kettle to the high lake where they'd made the forced landing, where Ivar had stood on the ice between the crew and Halloran's men with nothing but a contract's fine print and his own stubborn spine. She landed on the frozen surface at dusk, shut down, and sat in the cockpit with the engine ticking as it cooled, looking out at the darkening water under its lid of ice.

Somewhere under there, further out toward the deep channel, the wreck still lay. The salvage crew had raised what mattered in the autumn — the fuselage sections, the personal effects, the sealed case with its letter and documents. The rest they'd left, by agreement with the exile court, as a grave. There was talk of a marker come summer, a cairn on the shore with Astrid's name and the truth of how she died, carved in stone that couldn't be bought or burned.

Orsen got out and walked a little way onto the ice, boots crunching, breath smoking. The sky overhead was doing that northern thing it did on clear winter nights — going from blue to black to a depth that seemed to hold every star ever named and most that weren't. Aurora flickered low on the northern horizon, green curtains swaying in a wind she couldn't feel.

"Astrid," she said aloud, to no one, to the ice, to the woman underneath. "They know your name now. All of it. Everywhere."

The aurora shifted. The river said nothing, rivers being rivers. But standing there in the cold with her real name stitched into her collar and painted on her tail and entered into the record of two nations, Orsen felt — for the first time she could remember — that the silence around her was not hiding anything.

It was just quiet.

She walked back to the plane, climbed in, and fired up the engine. The *Kestrel* lifted off the ice smooth and clean, climbing into the dark on the river's line, heading home, and behind her the lake lay still and honest under the stars, keeping what it kept, having given up what it gave.

Tomorrow there would be fuel orders to file, and the spring survey to plan, and a hundred small ordinary tasks that belonged to a woman with nothing to run from.

She found she was looking forward to every one of them.
