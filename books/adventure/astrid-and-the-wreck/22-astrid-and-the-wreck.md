## 22. Astrid and the Wreck

The morning of the flight came in clean and cold, the kind of light that makes a pilot honest. Frost had silvered the dock boards overnight, and the Kettle River ran low and glassy beneath it, carrying the last of autumn's leaves seaward like small bright boats. Orsen stood on the float of her plane with a rag in one hand, wiping dew off the cowling, and let herself feel the whole weight of what she was about to do.

She had flown this route a thousand times. Medicine upriver, mail downriver, gossip both ways. She had flown it under a name that wasn't hers, with papers that weren't hers, wearing her anonymity like a coat she'd forgotten how to take off. But today there was a passenger waiting at the dock, and today the flight had a destination that wasn't on any manifest, and today — for the first time in longer than she could count — Orsen Dunmore was flying as herself, registered, licensed, named in ink that no court had contested since the winter the consortium's reach finally broke.

Her real name still felt strange in her mouth. She practiced it sometimes, alone, saying it to the cabin walls the way you'd test an old scar to see if it still hurt. It didn't hurt anymore. It just felt new.

* * *

Mira Vale arrived at eight, exactly as promised, carrying a canvas satchel and a bundle wrapped in waxed paper that smelled faintly of sweet peas and river water. She was twenty-six — Astrid's age when the river took her, though nobody said so out loud. She had her mother's jaw, sharp and level, and her mother's habit of looking directly at things other people only glanced at.

"You're sure about the lake?" Mira asked, settling into the passenger seat and buckling the harness with quick, competent hands. A pilot's daughter's hands.

"Sure enough," Orsen said. "Water's down two feet from spring. The wing should be showing."

"And the plane's still there."

"The river keeps what it wants to keep." Orsen turned the prop, climbed in, and checked her gauges twice, not because they needed checking but because her hands wanted something steady to do. "It kept her twenty years. It'll keep her another twenty if we ask it to."

Mira looked at her then — that direct look — and Orsen understood that the girl knew exactly what this flight was. Not a salvage survey. Not a memorial flyover. A visit.

They took off into a sky scrubbed blue by the night's frost, climbing over the town in a slow banking turn that gave them the whole valley: the sawmill smoke standing straight up in the windless air, the church steeple, Halloran's old office with its windows dark now for three months, the airstrip where Ivar's rebuilt outfit hung its shingle — LUND SALVAGE, HONEST WORK, HONEST RATES, painted by Petra's own hand, the letters slightly crooked because Petra had insisted on painting it herself while dangling from a ladder and laughing. Somewhere down there Dek was drafting testimony summaries for the exile court's second session, and somewhere upriver Petra was diving a different wreck entirely, a legitimate one, a sunken barge with a cargo of nothing more criminal than machinery.

Orsen flew north and west, following the Kettle's braided channels into country where the maps went vague and the terrain did the navigating. The chest had gone to the court months ago, its documents read into the record, Vessland's erased borders spoken aloud in a borrowed hall until they were borders again in every way that mattered except the ones that took armies. The logbook too — all but the final pages, which Orsen had kept back, and which rode now in the map case between the seats, wrapped in oilcloth, dry at last after twenty years underwater and one long winter above her stove.

She hadn't told Mira about those pages yet.

* * *

The lake appeared the way it always did, sudden and complete — a long lens of water set in granite, fed by a creek at the north end, drained by a channel at the south that in low water barely whispered. Twenty years ago it had swallowed a floatplane in ninety seconds flat. Now, in the October shallows, the wreck lay visible from two hundred feet: the fuselage settled on its belly in twelve feet of clear water, one wingtip breaking the surface like a black finger pointing at the sky it never reached.

Orsen brought her in against the light wind, flared, and touched down so gently the lake barely noticed. She taxied to the middle distance and shut down, and the silence afterward was enormous — the engine's tick and settle, water lapping at the floats, a loon calling somewhere near the creek mouth with that sound like grief remembering how to laugh.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

Then Mira unbuckled. "Can we get closer? On the floats?"

"We can stand on them, if you're careful."

They worked their way out along the struts, Orsen leading, until they stood at the float bows with the wreck below them, close enough now to see detail through the water's clarity: the crumpled nose, the shattered windshield, the seat harness hanging loose where the current had worried it for two decades. A grayling drifted through the cockpit window, unhurried, sovereign in its ruined kingdom.

Mira knelt. She unwrapped the waxed paper bundle, and inside were flowers — not cut flowers but living ones, roots and all, gathered from the exile settlement's gardens where Vessland soil had been carried in barrels across two borders and planted anyway, because some people refused to let a nation die just because its maps were burned. Sweet peas, and something blue Orsen didn't have a name for, and a spray of white blossoms that Mira said were called *minnen*, memory-flowers, which bloomed latest of everything and were traditionally left for the dead who had unfinished business.

"She had unfinished business," Mira said quietly. "Twenty years of it. We finished it for her."

She leaned out and laid the flowers on the water. They spread slowly, caught in the faint surface drift, and began — with terrible gentleness — to arrange themselves around the wingtip. Around the black finger pointing home.

Orsen watched them go and found she couldn't speak. There was a pressure behind her sternum, twenty years deep, and it wasn't guilt anymore. Guilt was what she'd carried up the Kettle River with a sealed chest in her hold, believing she'd been complicit, believing her silence had helped kill this woman whose plane lay under her boots. But the court had sorted the arithmetic since then. Halloran had been the courier. Halloran had sold the route, sold the timing, sold Astrid Vale to the men in the second aircraft. Orsen's silence had bought her nothing and cost her everything, and the man who'd profited from both their deaths-in-advance was gone — fled, dissolved, a name on a warrant now instead of a debt ledger.

What she felt now was simpler and harder. It was the feeling of standing over a grave you'd been afraid to visit.

"I used to fly that route," Orsen said at last. Her voice came out rough. "Not this exact line. But the same runs. Same rivers. I knew the consortium's pilots. I knew their schedules." She looked down at the wreck. "I spent ten years thinking that made me guilty. Turns out it just made me a witness who took too long to testify."

Mira reached over and put her hand on Orsen's arm. Her grip was strong. Pilot's daughter's grip.

"She would have liked you," Mira said. "I've read everything she wrote. Everything that survived. She wrote about pilots a lot — about how the good ones are the ones who are scared of the right things." A pause. "You're scared of the right things."

* * *

They ate lunch on the dock-less shore, on granite warmed by a thin sun, sharing coffee from Orsen's battered thermos and hardtack with honey. And it was there, watching Mira skip a stone across the shallows with her mother's exact motion — Orsen had seen that motion in a photograph once, Astrid mid-throw on some expedition beach — that Orsen decided.

She went back to the plane and took the oilcloth packet from the map case.

"These came up with the chest," she said, walking back. "The last pages of her logbook. They were too damaged to read into the record cleanly — the court got transcripts of what we could recover, but the originals..." She weighed them in her hand. "I kept them. I told myself it was for preservation. That was half true. The other half is that one of them has my name in it, and I couldn't bear to read it until I was someone worth reading it."

Mira went very still. "She wrote to you?"

"Not to me. About me. Maybe. I've never been able to make out the entry. The water took most of it." Orsen sat down on the granite and smoothed the pages flat, and Mira sat beside her, close, shoulder to shoulder, the way you sit with someone entering a room you can't follow them into.

The pages were brown as dead leaves, soft as cloth. The ink had gone the color of weak tea. But a winter above the stove had fixed what the river had loosened, and in the slanting afternoon light, with the lake glittering beyond, the words rose out of the damage like stones at low water.

The date was four days before the crash.

*...cargo confirmed intact. If anything happens to me, the chest goes to the bottom — better the river than their hands. The river gives things back eventually; they never do.*

*New pilot assigned to shadow me tomorrow. Dunmore, O. Young. Nervous hands but honest eyes. I've watched her load freight — she checks the tie-downs twice even when nobody's looking. If it comes to it, if they use her the way they used Haloran [sic] before me, I want it known: whatever she does or doesn't do, she was kind to me at Wexley Field. Bought me coffee when the whole strip was pretending I was already dead. Kindness counts. In the end it may be the only thing that counts.*

*They think I don't know about the second plane. Let them think it.*

*Tomorrow I file a false route. If anyone reads this —*

And there it ended. Mid-sentence. The river's thumbprint across the rest.

Orsen read it twice. Then a third time, her lips moving without sound, because the words wouldn't go in any other way.

She remembered Wexley Field. She remembered it with the sudden terrible clarity of something buried rising — a rain-slicked afternoon, a woman in a flight jacket buying nothing, staying late, asking Orsen questions about weather patterns no courier needed answered. Orsen had been nineteen and terrified and had bought her coffee because the woman looked cold, and had thought nothing of it, had thought of it a thousand times since without ever once connecting it to this.

Astrid Vale had known her name. Had written it down. Had weighed her — *nervous hands but honest eyes* — and in the last days of her life, with murder assembling itself around her like weather, had spent one of her final sentences on an act of ordinary decency performed by a frightened girl at a fuel counter.

Kindness counts.

Orsen put the page down very carefully, the way you put down something that might break, and found that Mira was crying silently, and discovered that she was too.

"She saw you," Mira said, when she could speak. "Before any of it. Before the crash, before the chest, before any of us knew your name — she saw who you were." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, unembarrassed. "That's why the river gave you the chest. I've thought about it for months. Everyone thinks it's chance, low water, luck. But my mother believed the river gives things back eventually. She wrote it. She trusted it." She looked at Orsen with Astrid's level gaze. "It gave you back."

* * *

They stayed until the light went long and gold, and then they flew a slow circuit of the lake — one pass, wings dipped, the old-fashioned salute — and turned south for home with the sunset pouring through the windshield like something spilled and generous.

Mira slept part of the way, her head against the window, and Orsen flew alone with her thoughts in the way she loved best: hands busy, heart quiet, the country unrolling beneath her in purple and copper. At the fuel stop at Wexley Field — rebuilt now, new hangar, new owners, but the same wind off the same ridge — she topped off the tanks and bought two coffees from the machine in the corner, out of habit, and drank them both, and laughed at herself alone on the apron in the dusk.

The registration on her tail had been repainted in September. Her true name, in letters a foot high, legal and visible and hers. Some nights she still caught herself glancing back at it on the mooring, surprised, the way you're surprised by a healed wound that doesn't ache in the rain.

They landed at the town dock in full dark, under a sky rinsed with stars, and Ivar was waiting with a lantern and a thermos of soup, because Ivar was Ivar, and word traveled faster than planes in a town that size. He helped Mira ashore, asked no questions, offered soup, and mentioned — casually, in his careful English — that the exile court had sent a letter, and that Dek had it, and that it was apparently good news, and that he would say no more because the letter should be read first.

Orsen dried her plane down, checked the tie-downs twice even though nobody was looking, and walked home along the frost-hard boardwalk with the letter-shaped silence sitting comfortably in her chest.

In her cabin, she lit the stove, pinned the recovered pages to the corkboard above her desk — next to the chart of the Kettle, next to the photograph of a crew around a camp stove, next to the pressed sprig of *minnen* Mira had given her — and sat down to write for the first time in years. Not a log entry. Not a report. A letter, addressed to a woman twenty years dead, in a nation reborn:

*Astrid —*

*You were right about the kindness. It counted. It carried me further than the fear ever did.*

*The river gave you back. So did I. We're even, and neither of us owes the other anything, and I'm going to keep checking my tie-downs twice whether anybody's looking or not.*

*Fly easy.*

*— O. Dunmore*

She signed her full name. All of it. First, middle, last — the whole buried length of it, surfaced at last, forgiven, and flying.
