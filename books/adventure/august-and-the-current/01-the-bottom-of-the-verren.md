## 1. The Bottom of the Verren

The river was at its clearest an hour before the sun cleared the eastern bluffs, and Petra Voss was in it before that hour arrived.

He went down the bank at the old ferry slip where the willows grew thick enough to hide a man from both shores, and he did what he had done a hundred times in a hundred rivers: he checked his gear twice, then a third time, not because he doubted himself but because doubt was cheaper than drowning. Weight belt. Knife on the right hip, shears on the left. The small wrecking bar lashed flat along his thigh so it wouldn't snag. Air line coiled in the skiff he'd bought three towns back with money earned under a name that wasn't his, from a man who hadn't asked for one.

The Verren ran green-gray and slow here, forty feet deep over the channel, deeper in the hole where the barge had gone down. He could see nothing of it from the surface. That was fine. He didn't need to see it yet. He needed to know it the way you know a room with your eyes shut—by counting steps, by memory of the sound your knuckles make against each object.

He spat into his mask, rinsed it, pulled it down. The water took him without comment.

Cold first, always cold first, a hand closing around his chest and squeezing until his body remembered it had done this before and stopped arguing. Then the light dimmed from gray to brown to a darkness that wasn't dark so much as thick, silt hanging in the water like breath held too long. He followed his own bubbles up for orientation, then rolled and let himself sink, fins barely moving, descending the way silt descends—without ambition.

Ten feet. Twenty. Thirty-five. His gloved fingers found the first timber at thirty-eight, a rib of black oak furred with weed, and he stopped and hung there in the cold and listened to the river through his own bones.

The barge lay on its starboard side, half-buried already after only two years in the mud. Rivers are impatient undertakers. They take a hull and begin closing the grave the same week. He worked along the rib line by touch, counting—four frames to the collapsed section amidships, six more to the stern post—and built the wreck in his head as he went, the way he built every wreck: a map drawn in the dark, accurate to a hand's width, kept nowhere but behind his eyes.

That was the rule. Not written anywhere, never spoken aloud, because rules you speak become things other people can hold you to. The rule lived in him the way the cold lived in him, and it had two halves, and the two halves were really one:

Take what can be carried. Leave nothing that can be traced.

No name on any manifest. No mark on any map. No witness who knew his face and his trade in the same sentence. In ten years he had stripped eleven wrecks and surfaced out of none of them as anyone at all. The brokers paid cash into hands they never shook. The harbormasters logged a salvage permit under initials that belonged to no living man. When a job ended, Petra Voss ended with it, folded up like a chart, and whatever man walked out of the next port had a different gait, a different story, a different set of scars if he could arrange it.

It was not a life anyone would envy. That was how he knew it was working.

* * *

The cargo hold opened under his hands like a mouth.

The forward hatch had buckled when the barge went down—he'd read the damage reports secondhand, the way he read everything, through intermediaries who thought they were talking to someone else—and now the planking lay peeled back against the mud, and the space beneath was a long throat full of river-dark and settled silt. He went in on his side, finning slow, the wrecking bar leading like a blind man's cane.

Cargo barges that sank in the Verren carried what the republic's interior wanted moved without questions: machine parts, tinned goods, sometimes arms, once—so the stories said—a whole disassembled printing press bound upriver to a town that had never asked for one. This one, according to the broker's note, had carried iron ingots and crates of preserved food, all of it insured, all of it written off, all of it fair salvage by every law except the one that mattered, which was the navy's law, which was: *this stretch of river is closed.*

Closed rivers were the best-paying rivers. Closed rivers meant somebody upstream or downstream cared about what lay in the mud, and caring men paid well for silence. But closed rivers also meant patrol boats with instruments he didn't pretend to understand, sweeping the water the way a man runs his fingers through his hair looking for a burr.

He'd give himself ninety minutes. Eighty, to be careful.

The silt in the hold was knee-deep even lying prone, and he worked through it the way he always did, in lanes, right to left, hands reading the bottom like a page. Ingots first—iron, worthless per pound against the cost of raising them, and he left them, marking their location anyway because iron told you where a hold's balance point had been, and the balance point told you where the valuable things would have been stowed to keep the barge riding true.

Aft of the balance point. Under the strongest deck. Where a prudent mate secures what he least wants crushed.

His fingers found the corner of it at the forty-minute mark.

Not a crate. Crates gave you splintered edges, nail heads, the hollow knock of air still trapped in dead wood. This gave him none of those things. This gave him iron banding gone smooth with rust, and beyond the banding a surface that his gloves read as dense, close-grained, deliberate. He swept the silt off it in slow strokes, working by feel, patient as the river itself, and the shape came clear under his palms a foot at a time.

A chest. Iron-strapped, oak-banded, brass corners turned green. Four feet long, maybe two deep. And heavy—not the heavy of wet wood and trapped mud, which has a certain give to it, a looseness, but the heavy of things packed tight and meant to stay packed. He got his fingers under the near edge and lifted and felt the whole weight of it settle into the riverbed like a stone refusing to be argued with.

Three hundred pounds, dry. More with the mud holding its skirt. No man raises that alone, not from forty feet, not through a hatch mouth this narrow, not with a skiff topside that would roll and drown him for the insult of trying.

He should have moved on. There was salvage enough elsewhere in the hold—a crate of brass fittings aft, lamp housings, a ship's bell he could hear when his knuckles brushed it, bronze singing dully through the silt. A week's honest work. A month's quiet pay. The chest was not his problem. The chest was too big to be his problem, and men who survived as long as he had survived by knowing the exact weight of their problems and refusing ever to carry one ounce more.

His hands stayed on the iron banding.

Because here was the thing his fingers knew and his discipline pretended not to: the banding was wrong. Salvage banding was functional—crude straps, hammered staples, whatever the dockyard had at hand. This was fitted. The straps followed the chest's lines the way a harness follows a horse, and at each corner the brass reinforcement was stamped, and though the river had softened the stamps he could feel the geometry of them under his thumb: a device, centered, formal. A crest. Something official enough that someone had paid a craftsman to make it beautiful before making it secret.

And the navy had closed the river over it.

He hung there in the brown dark with his heart going slow and hard, and he made himself think it through in order, because order was the only thing he owned outright. One: a chest with a crest, sunk with a cargo barge, in a reach of river the navy forbade. Two: forbidding cost money; navies did not spend money on empty mud. Three: whatever was inside had been worth sealing, worth strapping, worth sinking—or worth letting sink—and worth guarding afterward by decree rather than by diver, because divers could be bribed and decrees could not.

Four: none of that was his business.

He almost believed it. He was good at believing it; he'd had ten years of practice. But his hands lingered another moment on the cold strap, and in that moment he understood something about himself that he usually managed not to look at directly, the way you avoid your own reflection in dark water: that the rule—*take what can be carried, leave nothing that can be traced*—had never really been about safety. It was about erasure. He had spent a decade becoming no one, and no one does not open sealed chests, because no one has no curiosity, no history, no hunger, no name that anything could attach to.

The chest didn't care about his rule. The chest sat in the mud with its crest turned upward like a face, waiting.

* * *

He marked it.

Not with paint—paint fades, paint invites. He took the measurements instead, the way he took everything, into the only vault that couldn't be searched. Bearing from the stern post: seven meters, true. Depth to the banding: forty-one feet at current river stage. Distance off the north bank, paced later from the willow line: sixty paces past the third dead tree. The hatch mouth it lay beneath, the angle of the hull, the lean of the collapsed deck plank that leaned over it like a roof beam fallen across a door.

Then, because memory alone was a risk he didn't take with money or wrecks, he did the one physical thing his rule allowed: he drove a length of rebar into the mud six feet off the chest's seaward corner, low, flush, invisible unless a man's hand went looking for exactly it. Divers call them dead-man sticks. He'd left four of them in four rivers in ten years, and he'd come back to three, and the fourth had been a mistake he didn't intend to repeat.

This one he intended to come back to. He hated how clearly he intended it.

On the way out he took the brass fittings after all—the small crate aft, light enough to carry, anonymous enough to sell. Take what can be carried. He cradled it against his chest and rose through the brown water in slow spirals, venting, equalizing, watching the light above him go from nothing to gray to the pale washed silver of a river just before sunrise, and he broke the surface in the shadow of the willows with the mist lying on the water in long white ropes, and for a moment he simply floated there, breathing, alone, the way he was always alone, the way being alone had long ago stopped being a condition and become a craft.

He was good at it. That was the truth underneath everything. He had made loneliness into a discipline the way other men made carpentry or soldiering into one, and he was, by any honest measure, excellent at it. No debts. No attachments. No name that a clerk anywhere could run a finger down a column and find. Ten years without a single person on earth who could say *he was here*—

—and one iron-strapped chest with a dead nation's crest on it, sitting in the mud like a debt he hadn't agreed to owe.

* * *

He was two miles downstream and tying off at a rotten private jetty when he saw the patrol boat.

It came upriver against the current at an easy pace, gray hull, no running lights, no wake to speak of, and it was doing the thing he had heard about from the broker and hadn't quite believed: towing something low and flat between itself and the far bank, some instrument slung beneath the waterline, sweeping. It passed the jetty at perhaps three hundred yards. Nobody looked at him. Nobody ever looked at a man mending nets alone on a rotten jetty at dawn, and that was the entire architecture of his survival.

But as it drew abreast of the bend—the bend, he noted, that stood directly over the barge—the boat slowed. Just slightly. Just enough. The towed thing below the waterline hummed, or the river around it hummed, or perhaps he imagined the hum entirely, standing in the cold with his ears still full of forty feet of silence.

Then it sped up again and went on around the bend and was gone, and the river lay flat and innocent, and the mist began to burn off the water in patches, and somewhere upstream a bird started its idiot morning noise.

Petra stood on the jetty a long time with the brass fittings in their sack at his feet and the sun coming up gold behind the eastern bluffs, and he did the arithmetic he was best at, the arithmetic of risk. The sweepers would find the wreck eventually—maybe today, maybe this week. When they found it, they would either raise what they wanted and leave the rest, or they would seal the reach entirely, post boats, end salvage forever. Either way, the window was closing. Either way, the chest with the crest of a country that no longer existed would go down into the record books of whichever ministry had closed the river, and vanish the way Kaldavia itself had vanished—off maps, out of ledgers, out of memory, into the mud.

Or a man with no name could go back tonight, with cable and lift bags and a borrowed winch, and take it first.

He picked up the sack. He walked up the jetty toward the road and the town beyond it, Halden's Reach, where a broker waited who never gave names and never asked for one, and whose purse was currently holding exactly enough money to rent a winch.

He told himself, all the way into town, that he hadn't decided anything yet.

He was lying, and the river knew it, and the river keeps what it is given.
