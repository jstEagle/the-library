## 2. A Contract with No Signature

Halden's Reach smelled of tar and herring and the particular rot of a town that had once been important. Petra came down off the hills at midmorning with river water still drying in the seams of his boots and took a room above a chandlery under the name Doss—his fourth name that year, his ninth in a decade. He paid for three nights in advance because paying in advance made a man forgettable. Men who paid by the day got remembered.

The room had a bed, a chair, and a window facing away from the water, which he considered a courtesy. He did not like windows that faced the water. The water was where he worked, and a man did not sleep in his workshop.

He counted what he had. Eleven crowns and some small silver, a coil of good line, two knives, the diving suit folded into its canvas bag like something dead. The salvage from yesterday's dive—a crate of brass fittings, a ship's bell green with age, four bottles of wine gone to vinegar but the bottles themselves worth something to collectors—sat wrapped in oilcloth under the bed. He'd sell it through channels that asked nothing. That was the whole architecture of his life: channels that asked nothing.

Except now there was the chest.

He had marked it with a lead tag on a length of twine, tied to the frame of the barge's aft hatch, forty feet down in the green murk of the Verren. He had told no one. He had told no one the way a man swallows a key—not hiding it so much as making it part of himself, somewhere he could get at it but nobody else could reach without opening him up.

He thought about it while he ate bread and hard cheese at the chandlery counter. A chest bolted to a dead barge, too heavy to raise alone, sitting in a stretch of river the navy had closed to traffic three years ago and never explained why. Closed rivers were common enough—ammunition dumps, drowned ordnance, a quarantine after some factory upstream had poisoned the fish. But those closures got signs. This one got patrol boats.

He finished his bread and went out to make money, because thinking was cheaper with a full purse and he didn't have one.

* * *

The broker worked out of the back of a sailmaker's loft on Cordage Street, and like all brokers of his kind, he had no name anyone used twice. In Halden's Reach they called him the Notary, which was a joke, because nothing he touched ever got written down. He was a soft-bodied man with careful hands and the eyes of someone who had learned long ago that looking surprised got people killed.

Petra laid the brass fittings on the trestle table without ceremony. The Notary turned them over one at a time, holding each to the light from the loft's high windows, and said nothing for a long time, which was how he negotiated. Silence was his scale.

"River brass," he said finally. "Verren stock. Good condition."

"Good condition" was generous. The fittings were pitted. But the Notary paid for generosity the way he paid for everything—at a discount against future convenience.

"Forty crowns."

"Sixty."

The Notary set down a fitting and looked at Petra properly for the first time. "You're new to my street."

"I'm new to every street. That's the point of me."

Something almost like amusement moved behind the man's face and died there. "Fifty," he said. "And I'll tell you something for free, since you're new." He leaned back. "There are men asking about divers this week. Navy men, or dressed like navy men, going around quiet. They want to know who works the Verren below the old toll line. They're paying for names."

Petra kept his hands still. "What kind of navy men?"

"The kind that don't post notices." The Notary began wrapping the fittings in paper, unhurried. "I don't sell names, you understand. It's bad business. But other people on other streets are less scrupulous, and a diver who works alone is a diver who can disappear without anyone filing a complaint." He slid fifty crowns across the table in a worn leather purse. "So. Take your money, take a week somewhere inland, come back when the weather turns and the navy remembers it has a coast to worry about."

"What are they looking for?"

"If I knew, I'd be selling it, not telling you I don't know." The Notary smiled with his mouth only. "But between us: they brought instruments. Not guns first—instruments. Boats running lines across the water, slow, all morning, like women combing hair. Whatever's down there, they don't know exactly where it is. Yet."

Petra picked up the purse. Fifty crowns was more than he'd held at one time in two years. Fifty crowns was a winter in a boarding house, or passage south, or six months of not diving at all while the heat cooled off whatever the navy was cooking.

"Fifty," he said, "for the lot. And I'll ask one question back, free."

The Notary waited.

"The closure on the river. Three years. Who signed it?"

"No signature." The Notary's smile widened by a degree. "That's how the best orders work. You've noticed."

Petra had noticed. He had built an entire life inside the space where signatures should have been.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he always did in a new port: learning it without appearing to learn it. He bought rope he didn't need from a chandler near the fish market and stood where he could see the harbor mouth. He drank one beer slowly at a dockside tavern whose windows faced the water—the one exception to his rule, because today he needed eyes on the river—and watched.

Two patrol boats were working the reach below the old toll line. Gray hulls, navy pennants, but flying low and lazy, the way boats fly when their crew has been doing the same dull task for weeks. Each boat towed something on a long cable astern. At first he took it for a sweep—minesweeping gear, or a grapple drag. But minesweeps ran fast and straight. These boats crawled. They crossed the river, turned, crossed again, spacing their runs with the patience of surveyors, and the thing they towed left no wake worth noting.

Twice, a launch put off from the larger boat carrying a box the size of a coffin. Men lowered it over the side, let it sit submerged for ten minutes, hauled it up, conferred, moved on. Listening gear, probably. Hydrophones. The republic's navy had been buying hydrophones in quantity since the war—everyone had—but you used hydrophones to find submarines, and submarines did not go up the Verren. The Verren wasn't deep enough for anything bigger than Petra himself.

They weren't listening for something moving. They were listening for something that had stopped moving. Something sitting on the bottom, maybe giving off a slow tick of metal settling, or maybe just blocking a sound it shouldn't block.

Or they were mapping. Running a grid, building a picture of the riverbed, inch by inch, until the picture showed them everything that didn't belong—including, eventually, a chest with a foreign crest bolted under a hatch cover, and a lead tag on a length of twine.

The tag. He'd tied a tag. It had seemed prudent at the time, forty feet down with his blood singing and his lungs burning, a way to promise his future self that the moment had been real. Now it seemed like a signature. He'd spent ten years leaving no trace of himself anywhere, and he'd gone and signed the riverbed.

His instinct said leave. Pack tonight, walk the south road, be a different name in a different town by Thursday. Fifty crowns in his pocket made leaving possible, even comfortable. The Notary had offered him the exit politely, and exits were rare gifts in his trade.

But.

But the chest was Kaldavian. He hadn't opened it—he'd tried the lock underwater with numb fingers and given it up—but he'd seen the crest stamped into the lid, and everyone past forty carried Kaldavia somewhere in them, the way you carried a scar from a war you'd only watched. Twenty years gone from the maps. Whole cities renamed, whole archives burned, whole families scattered into the republic's census like salt into snow. Nobody salvaged Kaldavia. Nobody was allowed to. Which meant the navy wasn't protecting the river from looters.

They were protecting whatever was in that chest from being found.

And they didn't know where it was yet. That was the arithmetic that kept him in the chair with the empty glass. The Notary had said it plainly: they were combing. Combing meant searching, and searching meant time, and time was the one currency Petra had always been rich in. He could raise the chest in two nights if he rigged the barge's own winch and had a lift bag or two. Two nights against however many weeks a grid survey took.

His instinct said leave. His purse, though—he looked at it honestly, the way he looked at a gauge before a deep dive—his purse said otherwise, and not just because it was light. Fifty crowns was survival. The chest was something else, and he distrusted the something else, distrusted it the way he distrusted warm currents that pulled you off your anchor line. But he'd felt it down there, through the neoprene and the cold and the green dark: the weight of a thing that mattered. He had spent a decade handling objects that mattered to other people and none to him, and he had liked it that way, and the chest had spoiled it in the space of one breath through his regulator.

One more dive. Raise it, move it somewhere the combs would never pass, open it in daylight, and then decide. Decide whether it was treasure or evidence or just a dead nation's luggage. Decide whether to sell it, sink it deeper, or hand it to whoever the Notary couldn't name.

He finished the beer and went back to the chandlery and did not pack.

* * *

Night came down on Halden's Reach the way it came down on all river towns, in layers—first the gulls went quiet, then the fish market lights, then the taverns one by one until only the harbor lamps remained, smeared orange on the black water. Petra sat in his room with the lamp unlit and the window cracked, listening to the town settle, and ran the plan the way he ran every plan: three times forward, once backward.

Forward: tomorrow night, slack water around two. Gear staged at the ruined pilings upstream of the toll line, where the patrol boats' lanterns didn't reach. Lift bags—two, thirty-kilo each, borrowed from a fishing shed whose owner was drinking himself insensible at the Anchor. The barge's winch drum was seized but the cable was sound; he'd checked. Rig the chest, fill the bags, walk it up the barge's rail and across the shallows to the pilings. Ninety minutes of work if the river behaved.

Backward: the ways it failed. The cable snags—that had nearly killed him once already on this wreck, and a snagged cable at night with no surface tender was a short road to a quiet grave. The bags burst. A patrol boat's searchlight swung upstream on a whim. The fishing shed's owner sobered up early and mentioned the missing bags to someone with a badge.

And the way it failed that he couldn't plan for: the one he hadn't thought of yet. There was always one. The trick was to assume it existed and keep enough reserve—air, strength, lies—to survive it.

He lay back on the bed without undressing and listened to the river. Somewhere downstream, faint as a pulse, an engine idled. The navy working late, or standing watch, or simply being the navy: present, patient, certain that the world would eventually come to it.

He thought about the crest again. The double-headed bird, wings spread, stamped deep enough to survive twenty years of silt. His brother would have known what it meant precisely—Tomas had collected maps the way other boys collected marbles, and he'd traced Kaldavia's borders in ink until the day the borders stopped existing, and after that he'd traced them anyway, in pencil, lightly, like a secret. Tomas, who had believed that things written down stayed true. Tomas, who had been wrong about that in the end, in the way that got a boy of nineteen taken off a train at a checkpoint and returned to his family as a file number.

Petra had stopped writing himself down after that. No registry, no guild rolls, no contracts with his name on them, no debts, no letters. A man who existed nowhere could be taken from nowhere. It had worked. Ten years, and it had worked.

And now he lay in a rented bed under a rented name, planning a second theft from a sealed river, of a chest bearing the crest of the country that had eaten his brother—and some low, drowned part of him understood that this was not salvage anymore. Salvage had rules. Salvage was take the brass, leave the bones. This was something with teeth in it, and he was walking toward it with his eyes open because his purse was empty and his caution, after ten years of perfect service, had developed a crack.

The engine sound downstream faded. Shift change, maybe. Or the boats pulling out to run their combs in the dark, where the water was quietest.

Two nights, he told himself. Then it stops being mine.

He slept badly, and toward dawn he dreamed of green water and a voice reading names off a list, and woke to knocking below—the chandlery boy, with word that a woman had come asking after him at the loft on Cordage Street. A naval woman. Polite. Patient.

Asking for the diver who'd sold river brass.

Petra was out the window and onto the roof before the boy finished speaking, the fifty crowns already heavy in his coat, the plan shrinking from two nights to one.
