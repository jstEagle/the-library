## 3. What the River Keeps

The Verren at night was a different animal. By day it ran brown and patient, wide as a road, carrying its silt like a man carries debt. After dark it went black and thoughtful, and the current spoke to itself in low voices against the pilings of the old ferry slip, saying things Petra didn't want translated.

He went in anyway. He'd been going in for ten years.

The trick was not courage. Courage was for men who had something to lose and wanted the world to watch them keep it. The trick was procedure. He checked his lamp twice, checked his knife, checked the spare air bottle lashed under his left arm, and then he sat on the edge of the slip with his legs in the water and let the cold climb up through his suit like a slow honest question: *are you sure?*

He was never sure. That was the point. A diver who was sure was a diver who had stopped counting.

He slid in without a splash.

* * *

Down below, the river forgot about him immediately, which was one of the reasons he preferred it to land. On land, people remembered your face, your name, the shape of your shoulders in a doorway. The river held nothing. It took everything you gave it and gave back only what it had already decided to give, and it did not care who you were or what you were running from. Ten years ago that indifference had felt like punishment. These days it felt like mercy.

His lamp cut a cone through the murk, and the murk pushed back. Visibility was an arm's length, maybe two on a good night, and this was not a good night. The recent rains had swollen the tributaries and the Verren was drinking them all, so the water tasted of topsoil and rotted leaves and, faintly, of diesel from the navy patrol boats that had spent three days sweeping the channel with instruments nobody civilian owned.

That was the other reason he was diving at night instead of waiting out the patrols. Whatever they were looking for, they hadn't found it yet. Which meant it was still down here. Which meant it could be his.

He followed his own marker line down along the barge's starboard rail. The wreck lay on its side in forty feet of green-black water, settled into the mud like a drunk into a ditch, and he knew her now the way he knew few living things—every seam, every rust weep, every place where the hull had split and the river had begun the long work of swallowing her whole. She'd gone down heavy. That much he'd known since the first dive. Cargo barges don't sink level and quiet unless they're carrying more than their plates can argue with.

The chest waited where he'd left it, wedged in the forward hold beneath a collapsed beam, half-buried in silt that had taken him two full dives to clear by hand, a handful at a time, careful as a surgeon, because careless divers stirred clouds that ate their own light.

It was iron-banded oak, maybe four feet long, and even half-buried it looked wrong. Not damaged-wrong. *Deliberate*-wrong. Someone had chained it to the keel frame before the barge went down—not cargo lashing, but real chain, ship's chain, the kind of thing you used when you expected someone to come looking. And the crest on the lid—

He'd seen it on the last dive and told himself he'd imagined it. Now his lamp found it again and there was no imagining it away: a tower over crossed keys, stamped deep into a lead seal the size of his palm, bolted to the wood.

Petra Voss did not believe in omens. But he believed in crests, because crests were how dead things signed their names.

Kaldavia.

The word arrived in his head without permission, the way words do when you've spent years not thinking them. Kaldavia, which had been a country the way a body is a person, right up until it wasn't. Twenty years gone. Erased so thoroughly that schoolbooks now had a blank chapter where it used to live, and maps showed the region with a border drawn straight through it like a scar over an old wound, labeled with somebody else's name.

What a Kaldavian state seal was doing chained to the keel of a barge in the Verren River, four hundred miles from where Kaldavia used to be, was a question with only bad answers.

He should leave it. Every instinct he'd sharpened over a decade said leave it: take the brass fittings, take the salvage, take the small safe money, and let the river keep its secrets, because the river was better at keeping them than he would ever be. The navy's instruments sweeping the channel weren't looking for scrap divers. They were looking for exactly this.

But his purse held eleven coppers and a button, and winter was coming, and besides—there was a third thing in him, older than caution and hungrier than need, that had surfaced the moment his lamp touched that lead seal. It was the thing that had made him a good diver and a terrible citizen. Curiosity with no survival instinct attached.

He got to work.

* * *

The chain was the first problem. He worked the links with a hacksaw blade he'd taped to a wooden grip, and the river watched him do it, patient as a creditor. Each stroke sent up a whisper of rust, and each minute cost him air, and he counted both without letting himself think about either. Forty feet down, in black water, cutting a chain off a chest that a national navy wanted nobody to find. If he'd said it aloud it would have sounded like the opening of a story told about a dead man.

The link parted on the ninth minute. He felt it go with a shudder through the frame, and the chest shifted—a hand's width, settling deeper—and a bloom of silt swallowed his lamp cone whole.

Blindness underwater is not darkness. Darkness is simple; you wait it out. Blindness underwater is the silt, a brown fog so total that your own lamp reflects back at you, and every direction becomes every other direction, and the current starts speaking very close to your ear. Petra went still and breathed shallow and counted to sixty, and the fog thinned enough to show him the chest's outline, and he clipped his lift bag to the lifting eyes he'd rigged on the previous dive.

Forty feet of water above a four-foot iron-banded chest is a lot of weight pretending to be nothing. The bag took it grudgingly. He fed it air in short bursts, feeling the load come alive on the line, and began to rise with it, hand over hand, the way he'd done a hundred times with a hundred less interesting objects.

At twenty feet, the cable snagged.

Later, he would reconstruct it precisely: the chest had swung as it rose, swung into the collapsed beam, and the beam had taken his lift line and bent it around a jagged stub of steel, and now the line was bar-taut and he was tethered to a sinking wreck by a rope attached to four hundred pounds of iron-banded oak, and the current, feeling him struggle, leaned in.

He tried the knife on the line. Wrong angle. He tried to work himself down the cable toward the snag, and the current took that moment to shove him sideways, and his regulator hose caught on the same stub of steel and pulled free of his mouth.

The river came in.

It came in the way it always did—not angry, just thorough. Cold slammed his skull flat. His lamp spun somewhere above him, a mad gold coin in the murk, showing him nothing. He found the hose, fumbled it, got it to his lips, and sucked river-flavored air, half water, and coughed, and coughing underwater is the beginning of the end, because a cough empties you when you most need to be full.

Panic offered itself. He declined. Not bravely—he declined the way a man declines a drink at nine in the morning, out of long practice. He put one hand on the cable, one on the snagged beam, and made himself stop moving entirely, hanging in the black while his heart tried to break out of his chest, and he thought, with strange clarity, of nothing at all. That was the discipline. That was the whole religion of it: when the river has you, want nothing.

The coughing eased. The air came cleaner. Above him the lift bag strained at the snag, and the chest hung below it like a hanged man, patient, certain.

He cut the lift line.

It cost him the chest—or so he believed for the length of one long breath. The bag shot upward into the dark with nothing to carry, and the chest dropped, and he kicked clear of the beam as four hundred pounds of oak and iron fell past him into the hold, crashing home into the mud with a sound he felt in his teeth rather than heard.

Then he did the arithmetic that saved his life: the chest had landed across the keel frame, high side up, and the frame was solid, and the lifting eyes were still rigged. He could re-rig. He had air for six more minutes if he stopped wanting things.

He re-rigged. He fed the new line around the beam instead of over it, a clean lead, and sent the bag up again, and this time the chest rose obediently, swinging gently, almost apologetic, and Petra rose with it into the cold upper water, then the gray, then the astonishing ordinary black of the night surface, where rain was falling and the far shore was a rumor of lights.

He hauled himself and his salvage onto the rocks below the ferry slip and lay there breathing like a landed fish, and the river behind him went back to its low private conversation, keeping what it kept.

* * *

He built a fire in the lee of the slip's stone footing, small and shielded, and opened the chest with numb hands because he could not make himself wait until morning, and this too was unlike him, and he noted the unlikeness the way you note a change in the weather.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job perfectly, lay paper.

Not gold. Not arms. Paper—reams of it, ledgers and letter files bound in string, and a lacquered document case, and beneath all of it, fitted into a molded tray like something meant to be presented rather than stored, a seal of office: brass and jade, the tower and crossed keys, heavy as a hammer head.

Petra dried his hands and picked up the top ledger and read by firelight, and the firelight was enough, because whoever had packed this chest had expected it to be read someday by desperate light.

The ledger was a fleet manifest. Kaldavian naval registry, dated the year before the end. Ship names he half-remembered from childhood radio broadcasts—the *Vessa*, the *Iron Meridian*, the *Kindly Star*—hulls he'd assumed were at the bottom of some sea, because that was the story everyone had been given: the fleet scuttled, the nation surrendered, tragedy, war, closed chapter, move along.

Except the manifest didn't say scuttled. It said *transferred*. Dated signatures, bill of sale language, prices in a foreign currency. The Kaldavian fleet had been sold. Sold weeks before the enemy ever reached the harbor, sold by the men whose job was to defend it, and the buyer's agents' names were written plainly in the margin in a neat bureaucratic hand.

And the letters—there were dozens, and he read four, and stopped reading because his hands had started to shake and shaking hands drop pages into fires. The letters were between members of the founding council of the republic that rose from Kaldavia's ashes. Men whose portraits hung now in government buildings. Men whose names named streets. In these letters they discussed, in the bored comfortable tone of men arranging a harvest, the disposition of a country: which officials to arrest, which records to burn, which witnesses to move east and which to lose entirely.

One letter contained a list. Names, and after each name a single word. *Silenced. Exiled. Kept.* And beside one name near the bottom, a word he had to read three times because his own blood was loud in his ears:

*Lost.*

He knew that word's grammar. He had spent ten years being that word's grammar.

Petra sat back from the fire and looked at the river, and the river looked back with its flat black patience, and he understood—suddenly, completely, the way cold water understands a lung—why the navy swept this channel with instruments no civilian owned. Why the patrols. Why the silence. Why a broker in Halden's Reach took contracts with no signature and paid in cash and never asked questions.

Somewhere in the republic, the men who had sold a country were still alive, still in office, still signing things. And somewhere in this river, for twenty years, the proof had lain in the dark, waiting. The navy hadn't sunk the barge. The navy had *lost* it—and had been quietly dredging the Verren ever since, hoping the river would finish the job history started.

The river had kept it instead. And now the river had given it to him.

He should burn it. The thought arrived calm and reasonable, wearing his own voice: burn the papers, melt down the brass seal, take the silver fittings off the document case, and be a poor anonymous man again by morning. The fire was right there. The river would forgive him. The river forgave everything.

He looked at the list of names. At the word *lost.*

Ten years ago, a boy with his brother's face had stood on a quay in a city that no longer appeared on maps, and soldiers with new flags had checked papers, and the brother had stepped forward first, because he always did, and Petra had learned what the word *lost* meant in practice. He had spent a decade making himself unfindable—no name, no record, no address, no face worth remembering—as though invisibility were penance, as though if he erased himself thoroughly enough the ledger of the world might balance.

The men in these letters had done the opposite. They had written themselves everywhere. Their names were carved into the republic's foundations.

The fire crackled. The chest sat open, breathing oilcloth and old ink.

Petra Voss, who took salvage and left no trace, who had a rule for everything and a rule above the rules—*never carry anything the river wants back*—looked at the seal of a dead nation lying in its molded tray like a sleeping thing, and understood that he had already decided. He had decided somewhere between the snagged cable and the word *lost*, maybe earlier, maybe ten years earlier, on a quay he never let himself visit.

He closed the chest. He buried the fire. And as he lifted the handles and felt the true weight of it settle into his arms—paper, brass, and twenty years of deliberate silence—he heard, far downstream toward Halden's Reach, the low engine note of a patrol boat running without lights.

It wasn't searching the channel anymore.

It was searching the shore.
