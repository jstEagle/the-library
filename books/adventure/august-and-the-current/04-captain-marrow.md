## 4. Captain Marrow

Petra saw her before she saw anyone—because that was the whole trick of her. She came into the Salt Ladder at half past nine in the morning, when the tavern was empty except for the cook and a pair of dockhands sleeping off the night shift, and she did not come in like an officer. She came in like a customer. Oilskin coat, plain boots, hair tied back without ornament, a canvas satchel over one shoulder that could have held bread or could have held a man's entire life in paper. She took the corner table by the window. The table with sightlines to the door and to the stairs.

He knew the table because it was his table.

Petra sat in the shadow of the stairwell with a cup of tea he had no intention of drinking and watched Captain Ilse Marrow order breakfast the way other people ordered breakfast. Eggs. Bread. Coffee, black. She thanked the cook by name—the cook's name was Doss, and nobody thanked Doss—and she asked him, in the tone of someone making conversation, whether the river had been good to the town this season.

"River's been strange," Doss said. "Navy boats up and down it all week. Scaring the fish."

"Navy's always strange," Marrow said, and smiled, and let it go.

That was the first thing Petra learned about her: she never pulled twice on the same thread. She touched a thing lightly, once, and moved on, and you only understood later that the thread had been attached to something enormous.

* * *

Ilse Marrow had been in Halden's Reach for two days before she walked into any tavern at all, and those two days had been spent the way she spent everything: carefully.

She had started at the harbor office, where a clerk named Pellin kept the salvage manifests, and she had not asked for anything unusual. She'd asked to see the last month of declared recoveries, complimented his handwriting, and left him a tin of good coffee from the capital as thanks. Pellin was a lonely man. By the time she left, he had told her which divers worked the Verren, which ones didn't bother declaring, and which broker handled the quiet jobs—the ones where the cargo wasn't quite legal and the paperwork wasn't quite real.

The broker's name, as far as anyone in Halden's Reach knew, was Ferren. He kept a counting room above a chandler's shop and did business through intermediaries because intermediaries were cheaper than lawyers. Marrow visited him at eleven o'clock on her second morning, alone, unarmed, and asked him about a barge.

"I don't know what you mean," Ferren said.

"Of course not," Marrow said pleasantly. "You wouldn't. You're a legitimate businessman." She set a ledger on his desk—not hers, his, the one from the drawer under the false bottom in his own counting room, the one he thought no one alive knew about. "You lost this. I found it. I'm returning it, because I'm honest that way."

Ferren looked at the ledger and then at her, and Marrow watched him do the arithmetic every guilty man does: what does she want, what will it cost me, is there a version of this where I keep my dignity. There wasn't. He arrived at that conclusion quickly, which she appreciated. Stupid men took longer.

"There was a job," Ferren said. "Stripping a wreck downriver. The client came through a third party. No name."

"But you have a description."

"Tall. Careful hands. Paid in mixed coin so it couldn't be traced. Wouldn't give a name even for the contract."

"No name," Marrow repeated softly. "How refreshing. A professional." She took the ledger back and tucked it into her satchel. "Captain Vess's barge went down eleven years ago carrying cargo the navy has spent eleven years pretending it didn't carry. Someone raised something off that wreck four nights ago. The instruments on our patrol boats registered a cable load near the old channel marker at two in the morning. So somewhere in this town is a diver who can hold his breath and his silence, and I would very much like to buy him breakfast."

"And if I don't know who he is?"

"Then you'll introduce me to whoever might." She stood, straightened her coat, and gave him the smile she saved for men she intended to ruin later if it became necessary. "Thank you for your honesty, Mister Ferren. It's the rarest salvage on this river."

* * *

By noon she had spoken to six people, and none of them would have called it an interrogation. That was the craft of it. She bought rope from a rigger and asked whether the navy's new survey instruments were interfering with local gear. She drank one glass of wine at the chandlery and asked the owner, idly, whether any of the quiet divers had been spending differently lately. She stopped at the ferry landing and helped a woman haul a crate, and while her hands were busy she asked how deep the channel ran past the old marker buoy, and whether anyone still dove there now that the navy had fenced it off on paper but not in fact.

Every answer went into her satchel. Not written—she wrote nothing, ever, in public—but into the satchel anyway, into the place behind her eyes where she kept things the way other people kept ledgers, cross-referenced and permanent.

The picture assembled itself by mid-afternoon. A lone operator. No crew, no partner, no debts worth mentioning—which meant either no vices or extraordinary discipline. Worked dawn and dark, never midday. Had taken one contract through Ferren in the spring, clean, unremarkable, and then a second one ten days ago that Ferren swore he knew nothing about, which meant the diver had gone back to the barge on his own initiative.

On his own initiative. Marrow turned that phrase over during the walk back toward the harbor. A man who stripped wrecks for money doesn't return to a forbidden site unpaid unless he saw something down there that made him curious or made him afraid. Curiosity she could work with. Fear she could work with better.

She checked into the Salt Ladder just before dusk, paid for three nights in advance, tipped the girl who showed her to the room, and asked—apologizing for the imposition—if the kitchen could manage an early breakfast, as she kept harbor hours.

Then she went down to the common room at half past nine the next morning and took the corner table by the window, and began, patiently, to be visible.

* * *

From the stairwell shadow, Petra performed the oldest arithmetic in his trade: how long, how fast, which door.

The front door opened onto the street and the street onto the harbor road; the harbor road was already patrolled. The kitchen door opened onto the alley, the alley onto the tannery lane, the tannery lane out past the smokehouses to the reed flats, where a man with a diver's lungs could wade chest-deep through the channels and come out beyond the town's memory. He'd mapped it the day he arrived, the way he mapped every room he slept in. He had never needed the map before. He was annoyed at himself for the small cold satisfaction of needing it now.

Because it was her. He'd heard the descriptions secondhand by evening—Doss telling the cook, the cook telling the dockhands, the story moving through the Salt Ladder like water through a hull. Navy woman, polite as a priest, asking after divers. And Petra had sat in his rented room above the cooperage with the shutters cracked and felt the particular vertigo of a man whose profession is invisibility watching someone walk the streets making it visible again, question by courteous question.

She wasn't hunting loudly. That was what frightened him. He had run from loud things his whole career—revenue cutters, harbor police, a husband in Corvast with a boat and a grudge. Loud things you could hear coming. This woman was doing the opposite of hunting. She was doing the thing Petra himself did underwater: moving slowly enough that the current never noticed her, touching nothing hard enough to leave a mark, and letting the wreck tell her where the damage was.

And she had taken the corner table. His table. Whether she knew it or not—and he suspected, with a sinking feeling, that she knew exactly what she was doing—she had sat down inside his method and lit a lamp in it.

At noon she rose, thanked Doss again by name, and left a coin on the board. Petra counted to three hundred, finished his cold tea, and went out into the street to follow his own hunter's advice: know the thing that's following you better than it knows itself.

* * *

Marrow knew she was being watched. She had known since the second hour of the first morning, when the feeling arrived the way weather arrives—no single sign, just pressure. Someone in this town had gone quiet around her. Shopkeepers answered her questions too readily and then found reasons to end the conversations. Twice she caught a reflection in a window: a tall man, careful hands, gone before she could turn.

Good, she thought. Running men made mistakes, and careful men made fewer but worse ones. Either way, the watching told her he existed, and existence was the whole battle. You cannot arrest a rumor. You cannot drown-proof a ghost. But a ghost who is curious about you is a man, and a man can be met.

She spent the afternoon at the harbor office again, ostensibly reviewing tide tables, actually giving Pellin the pleasure of her company and letting the pleasure travel. By evening, half the waterfront knew the navy captain was looking for a diver, and knew it in the soft way knowledge travels when someone wants it to travel—unofficially, deniably, arriving at exactly the ears that needed to hear it.

Let him decide, she thought, walking the seawall as the light went copper on the water. Let him sit in whatever dark room he keeps and weigh me. Men who live without names are men who have practiced disappearing, and practiced men are dangerous, but they are also tired. Ten years of vanishing wears grooves in a person. She had seen it in smugglers, in deserters, in her father, in the mirror some mornings. The grooves always ran the same direction: away.

All she had to do was stand at the bottom of the groove and wait.

* * *

Petra packed in the dark with the lamp unlit, the way he'd packed a hundred rooms before, and hated how familiar his own hands were at it.

Clothes rolled, not folded. Tools wrapped in oilcloth. Coin sewn into the waistband of his spare coat, where a search would find nothing worth finding. The chest itself was the problem—it weighed as much as a drowned man, and it did not roll, and it did not fold, and every plan he made bent around its stubborn rectangular fact. He'd stashed it in the reed flats under a sunken skiff, weighted with chain, breathing through the mud like something buried alive. Getting it out quietly would take a night dive and a borrowed cart and luck, and he had never in his life budgeted for luck.

He should burn it. The thought arrived on schedule, punctual as a tide. Burn the papers, sink the chest in deep water, take the honest salvage contracts in some port far south of here, remain the man who had never existed. The navy wanted silence; silence was a commodity he had dealt in for a decade; he was, God help him, the best-qualified man on this river to provide it.

But he lay on the narrow bed in the dark above the cooperage and thought about the seal pressed into the lead—Kaldavia, the double-headed heron, the nation that had been on maps when he was a boy and was nowhere when he grew up—and he thought about the pages he'd read by candlelight in the skiff, ink still legible after eleven years underwater, names and dates and signatures in a hand too calm for what it described. And underneath the fear, in the place where he kept everything he refused to look at, something had shifted its weight and settled, the way a wreck settles when you finally cut it loose.

They had signed it. Men had sat in warm rooms and signed the death of a country, and then they had sunk the proof in a river and posted boats over it, and the river had kept it the way rivers keep everything, patiently, waiting for someone stupid enough or hungry enough to come down with a lamp.

His purse said dive again. His instinct had said leave since the moment he broke the surface with the chest trailing cable behind him. Both of them, he realized, were wrong. What remained was neither caution nor need. It was the third thing, the thing he had no name for because he had spent ten years refusing to name anything, including himself.

Boots on the cobbles below. Two men, unhurried, passing the cooperage. Ordinary. Probably ordinary.

He was at the window anyway, flat against the wall, watching the street, before his heart agreed to slow down.

* * *

In the common room of the Salt Ladder, Ilse Marrow ate her breakfast and listened to the building breathe.

Old houses talked. This one talked through its floors: the girl going up to change linens, the cook's rhythm in the kitchen, the dockhands' boots, and—one floor above, in the room rented to a silent man who paid in mixed coin—a set of movements that were not the movements of a man sleeping late. Rolling. Wrapping. The particular pause-and-shift of someone distributing weight across their body so the floor wouldn't report it.

She finished her eggs. She left coins for Doss and a kind word for the girl, and she climbed the stairs at an easy pace, and she knocked on the door of the room above the cooperage the way you knock on a colleague's door.

No answer. Of course not. Through the wood she could hear nothing at all, which was itself an answer—the careful man holding his breath on the far side, listening to her listen.

"Mister Nobody," she said pleasantly, to the door, to the hallway, to the town. "I know you're in there. I know you've been in there since before dawn, deciding. So let me save us both the theater." She kept her voice level, conversational, pitched to carry no farther than the door. "I'm not here for the navy. The navy wants this matter closed, and closed means you at the bottom of the Verren with your lungs full of river, and I don't intend to be the one who does that. I'm here because there are pages in that chest with my family's name on them, and I have spent twenty years trying to find out why my father hanged himself in a boathouse, and I think you are currently sitting on the reason."

Silence. Long enough that she almost believed the room was empty.

Then, faintly, through the floorboards, the sound of a window sash rising at the back of the building—and the small, disciplined scuff of careful hands on a drainpipe, going down.

Marrow closed her eyes briefly. Ran, then. Good. Running men headed for the reeds, and the reeds funneled to the tannery lane, and the tannery lane ended at the flats where a certain sunken skiff sat over a certain weight of chain.

She picked up her satchel, nodded to Doss on her way out, and stepped into the morning.

"Two coffees to go," she said. "One of them's for a friend who's in a hurry."
