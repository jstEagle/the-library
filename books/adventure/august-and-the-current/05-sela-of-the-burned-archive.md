## 5. Sela of the Burned Archive

Sela Iven had been living in the reed marshes above Halden's Reach for eleven days when the diver came upriver.

She knew it was him before she saw his face. She had spent three nights watching the river road from the blind she'd built out of cut reeds and stolen tarpaulin, and in that time she'd catalogued every traveler: carters, pilgrims, a pair of navy couriers riding too casually, a knife-grinder with a soldier's walk. None of them moved like a man carrying something that could kill him. They all moved like people going somewhere. This one moved like a man being followed by a place he'd already left.

He kept to the towpath on the far bank, walking fast in the gray hour before full light, a canvas pack high on his shoulders and a second bag—long, heavy, wrapped in oilcloth—slung across his chest like a bandolier. He didn't look at the water. That was the tell. Everyone who lived near the Verren looked at the water; it was habit, superstition, boredom. A man who refused to look at a river was a man who had taken something out of one.

Sela slid back into the marsh and began to work.

She had been an archivist for nine years before the burning, and the habits did not leave you. You learned to build indexes in your head, cross-references, chains of association. The diver at the wreck below Halden's Reach—the fisherwoman had described him to Sela for the price of a boiled egg: young-ish, careful hands, dove alone at dawn, surfaced twice without a marker buoy. The navy boats sweeping the river with their strange instruments two days later. The broker's shuttered office. And now this man, moving east along the Verren with a package shaped exactly wrong for tools or trade goods.

She didn't know what he carried. She only knew what shape the hole in the world around him made, and holes were her profession. She had spent her career reading what was missing from records—the redactions, the gaps, the pages razored out of ledgers—and the burned archive had taught her the final lesson of that craft: when enough things go missing in the same pattern, the pattern itself becomes a document.

Someone had gone into the river and come out with the missing thing.

* * *

Her name had been worth something once. In the archive at Vellmark she had been the youngest reader ever granted access to the Restricted Cipher Room, because she had a memory that held languages the way other people's held songs. Kaldavian court cipher. Old Verreni mercantile shorthand. The naval flag-code variants from before the Reunification. Her supervisor used to say she could read a locked letter through the envelope.

Then came the night the archive burned, and the fire inspectors found accelerant traces in three wings but no culprits, and the government offered condolences and a modest pension, and Sela understood—with the same cold clarity she felt reading a forged signature—that the fire had a schedule and her wing had been last on it. She had taken nothing when she fled. That was the joke of it, the bitter private joke she turned over on bad nights: they'd assumed she would steal, so they watched the doors, and she walked out carrying the only thing they couldn't inventory, which was everything she'd ever read.

Eleven days in the marshes, eating eels and cattail root, watching the river town digest its secrets. She had come upriver following rumors of the wreck because Kaldavia was the word under all her rumors, always, the way water is under a boat. Twenty years since the maps erased it. Twenty years since the purges took the fleet, the ministries, the language from the schools, and the republic said *there was never such a country* in the gentle voice you use with the grieving. Sela had read the real documents once, in the Restricted Room, before they were withdrawn and later, presumably, burned. She remembered them anyway. That was the curse and the purpose of her.

And now a man was walking east with a sealed case and the posture of a hunted animal, and Captain Ilse Marrow—Sela had seen her too, two evenings ago, standing on the quay at Halden's Reach asking questions with the courtesy of a surgeon—was somewhere behind him on the road.

Two people who wanted the same silence broken. It wasn't trust. It was arithmetic.

* * *

She caught him at the ferry ruin at Krast Bend, where the old toll bridge had fallen in and the crossing narrowed to a single stone ford.

She did it the way you corner any wild thing: slowly, from downwind, with your hands visible. She stepped onto the ford path twenty paces ahead of him and stopped, and let him see her—a small woman, mud-brown clothes, hollow-cheeked, thirty years old looking fifty from the marsh weeks—and she said, clearly, before he could decide what to do about her:

"The crest is Kaldavian. Double-headed gull over crossed anchors. I know, because I've seen it on documents that officially don't exist."

The diver went very still.

It was a remarkable stillness. Not fear—she'd seen fear freeze men, and this was different, this was a whole body becoming a closed door. His eyes did the arithmetic she'd expected: distance to the ford, weight of the pack, whether a witness was a problem to be solved. She watched him decide not to solve her, and watched him decide it reluctantly, and filed both facts away.

"Wrong person," he said. His voice was flat, river-country accent sanded down to nothing, deliberately nothing. "I'm a contractor. Salvage work."

"Yes," Sela said. "For a broker with no name, on a wreck the navy forbids, and now you're walking away from payment with your contract still half-done, which no salvage man does unless the cargo changed the terms." She took one step closer. He didn't move. "I'm not navy. I'm not police. I'm not anyone, which I suspect is also true of you, and I mean that as a compliment."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite humor, more like recognition of a dialect. People who have erased themselves recognize each other the way deaf people notice sign. There is a grammar to not existing, and she had just spoken a sentence of it fluently.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," Sela agreed. "You don't. But I do." And she told him.

Not everything. She gave him the shape of it: the archive at Vellmark, the fire with a schedule, the Restricted Room and the ciphered correspondence she had memorized between Kaldavian ministry officials and certain republican names in the years before the purge. She watched his face while she talked, and his face confirmed everything his body wouldn't. When she said the words *the founding council sold the fleet*, his jaw tightened—a small muscle, deep, involuntary, the kind of tell no amount of discipline reaches.

"You've read those papers?" he said. Quietly. Almost to himself.

"I *was* those papers," Sela said. "They live in my head now. That's why the fire missed me and won't stop missing me." She let that sit. Then: "Whatever you pulled out of the Verren—if any of it is ciphered, if any of it is in the old ministry hand—you can't read it. Nobody alive can read it. Except me, and maybe four other people, and I can find two of them and the other two are dead."

The wind came off the water and flattened the reeds behind him. He looked at her for a long time, and she understood that the look wasn't suspicion anymore. It was cost accounting. She could see him weighing her the way he'd weigh salvage: what she was worth, what she'd cost, whether raising her would sink him.

"No," he said finally. "Whatever this is, I do it alone. That's how I stay alive, and it's worked for ten years."

"How has it worked?" Sela asked, genuinely curious. "Alone?"

"It's worked fine."

"You're walking east with a dead nation on your back and the navy's best fixer behind you, and your plan appears to be a direction." She shook her head. "I'm not asking to be your friend. I'm not asking for a share. I'm telling you that you are carrying a lockbox written in a language only I can open, and you are about to carry it into the mountains, and every day you can't read it is a day you might die for paper you don't understand. That's not caution. That's superstition."

"Maybe I don't want to understand it," he said, and there it was—the first true sentence he'd spoken, surfacing like a body coming up out of green water. He heard it himself; she watched him hear it. Then his face closed again, door shut, bar dropped. "No. Final answer. Stay away from me. If you follow me, I'll lose you in the hills."

"All right," Sela said pleasantly.

He frowned. "All right?"

"You said no. I heard you." She stepped aside off the ford path and gestured him through with mock courtesy. "Go on, then. Lose me in the hills."

He hesitated—one heartbeat, two, a man who had just been given exactly what he demanded and trusted it less than resistance—and then he crossed the ford, fast, water boiling white around his boots, and took the east road at a pace meant to insult pursuit.

Sela stood in the reeds until he was a smudge against the morning haze. Then she turned upstream, cut inland on the shepherd track she'd scouted two days earlier, and began to walk the parallel line through the hills that would keep her a valley ahead of him until the roads converged at the toll bridge at Krast.

She had been refused. She had also, she noted, been believed. He hadn't called her mad. He hadn't threatened her past the ritual amount. He had listened to the words *founding council sold the fleet* with the expression of a man hearing a diagnosis he'd suspected for years.

That was enough. Trust was a currency neither of them carried, but they could extend each other credit.

* * *

She nearly lost him at Krast after all, because he was better than she'd estimated.

He came through the checkpoint at midday in a salt cart, having bribed a carter she later learned he'd met an hour before, and Sela—arriving on foot from the hills, dusty, passportless, relying on the checkpoint guard's boredom and a forged transit chit she'd lettered by firelight—watched from the queue as Marrow's agents photographed travelers at the bridgehead with a camera on a tripod. She saw the diver see the camera too. She saw him turn his face into his collar with a smoothness that looked like nothing at all, and she thought: *he's done this before. Many times. He is fluent.*

The bribed carter drove on across the bridge and did not stop for him on the far side, and Sela watched the diver realize his ride had ended and simply begin walking, unbothered, a man accustomed to rides ending.

She caught up with him at dusk, six miles east, where the road climbed away from the river into scrub pine. She didn't sneak up on him this time. There was no point; he'd known she was there since the bridge, or should have, and pretending otherwise would only make her seem like a liar rather than a nuisance.

He heard her boots and turned, and this time his hand was inside his coat, and she stopped at the edge of the dark and said:

"Page forty-one of the ministry correspondence. The one beginning *'Regarding disposal of the northern squadron.'* I can recite the first line from memory. Want to hear it? Because if I can recite it, I can read whatever's in your case, and if I can read it, you need me, and if you need me, then shooting me is the stupidest thing you will ever consider doing, and you don't look stupid. You look tired."

The hand stayed inside the coat for a long moment. Pine trees whispered. Somewhere downslope, a night bird made its idiot clockwork sound.

"You followed me across a checkpoint," he said at last, "past photographing agents, on a forged chit, through hills, in shoes that are falling apart."

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was the question she'd been waiting for, and she found she didn't have the clever answer ready—the answer about leverage and mutual utility and the arithmetic of shared enemies. What came out instead was the true thing, which she hadn't said aloud in eleven years:

"Because they burned my life's work to make a country disappear, and everyone let them, and I ran, and I have been running for eleven years, and running is just burning that takes longer." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it and kept going. "You pulled something out of that river that says we didn't imagine it. That it happened, and who made it happen, and on what paper. I have spent my whole life keeping records that other people destroy. I am not going to watch you carry the last ones into a snowdrift because you'd rather be alone than be helped."

Silence. The pine whispering. His hand came out of the coat, empty.

"I have a rule," he said. "No names. No histories. We don't ask, we don't tell. If you travel with me, that's the price."

"Sela," she said.

"That's not the deal."

"I know. Take it or leave it." She stepped forward into the last gray light so he could see her face, mud-streaked, stubborn, half-starved and entirely unafraid of him, and she watched him understand that she was not going to be lost in the hills, not going to be deterred, not going anywhere. "My name is Sela Iven. I was third reader at the Vellmark Archive. They burned my building and erased my post and I am still here, and so are you, apparently, so maybe we're both worse at disappearing than we think."

He stared at her. And then—he didn't smile, nothing so generous—but something in his shoulders came down half an inch, the smallest surrender, a drawbridge lowering the width of a rope.

"Petra," he said, and immediately looked annoyed at himself, as if the name had escaped custody.

"Petra," Sela repeated, tasting it. A false name, probably. A shell. Fine. Shells were how she'd survived too; you wore them until the wearing became the living. "Now that we've broken both our rules in the first hour, shall we discuss the case?"

"There's nothing to discuss."

"You haven't opened it since the river."

His head came up sharply. "How—"

"Because you carry it against your chest," she said, "like a splint on a broken rib, and you check it every eleven minutes, and you sleep with your arm through the strap, and none of that is how a man handles cargo. That's how a man handles a wound." She held out her hand, palm up, in the dark, an offer and a dare. "Let me read it, Petra. Whatever it costs you to know what you're carrying—I'll carry that part too. It's the only thing I know how to carry."

He didn't give her the case that night. He didn't refuse again either. He built a small fire in a fold of the hill, screened from the road, and cooked barley in a dented tin, and divided it into two portions without discussing it, and when she woke in the black hours before dawn she saw him sitting up against his pack, the oilcloth bundle across his knees, turning it slowly, slowly, like a man learning the shape of a thing by touch alone.

By full light they were on the road east together, and behind them, far down the river valley, Sela saw a rider in a navy-gray coat pause on the ridge at Krast and look toward the mountains for a long, long moment before turning back.

"We need to move faster," Petra said.

"Yes," Sela said. "We do."
