## 6. The Toll Bridge at Krast

The river narrowed at Krast, and the road narrowed with it, funneling everything—carts, cattle, pilgrims, liars—onto a single stone span guarded by a customs hut the color of old teeth. Petra saw it from a kilometer out, the way he'd learned to see anything that could kill him: not by looking at it, but by noticing what his eyes did without permission. The pull in his gut. The counting of exits.

Two soldiers at the near end. A third on the far bank, bored, leaning on the rail. A fourth inside the hut, visible through a window as a shape and a pipe-smoke haze. Standard toll. Standard graft. He'd crossed forty bridges like it in ten years of being no one.

What was not standard was the man standing slightly apart from the queue, under a canvas awning strung between two poles, operating a camera on a wooden tripod.

Petra had seen navy photographers exactly twice before, both times at wrecks they wanted documented for reasons never explained to salvage men. This one wore no uniform. Grey coat, grey hat, grey face, and the camera's black bellows opening and closing like something feeding. Every traveler who passed the toll point stepped into a chalked square on the cobbles, faced forward, and waited three seconds while the shutter drank them.

He stopped walking.

Sela, a half-step behind him with the pack straps cutting her shoulders, walked into his stillness and bounced off it. "What?"

"Bridge," he said.

"I can see the bridge."

"Look left of the toll hut."

She looked. He watched her do the same arithmetic he'd done—the camera, the chalk square, the soldier holding a ledger open to a page of faces—and watched her arrive somewhere worse than he had. Her hand went to her collar, an old habit, as if she could still be recognized from a photograph taken twenty years ago by men who'd burned her archive.

"They're not collecting tolls," she said quietly. "They're collecting people."

"They're collecting names." Petra turned off the road, unhurried, a traveler stretching his legs, and led them down into a drainage ditch that ran parallel to the approach. Above the reeds he could see the bridge, the queue, the awning. "Which means someone upstream is paying for names. Which means someone upstream thinks certain names are coming this way."

"Us."

"Us is a strong word. I don't have a name. You—" He glanced at her. "You might."

"I've been Sela Iven for six years. Before that I was nobody official." She said it flatly, but her jaw was tight. "It's you they want. The diver. Marrow's been asking about divers since Halden's Reach."

"And now she's asking about everyone." He settled into the reeds, making himself comfortable the way he made himself comfortable in bad places: completely, because discomfort made men move, and moving men got noticed. "So we don't cross today."

"We have to cross today. The caravan master said the salt wagons go through at midday, and after that the road east empties until the mail rider comes tomorrow."

"The caravan crosses with us, then. In the middle of thirty strangers, one more stranger is furniture."

"One more stranger is a photograph," Sela said. "That machine doesn't care how many people are around you. It takes you alone. Three seconds, your face, your name in a ledger, and then that page travels faster than we ever will."

Petra looked at her. Under the grime and the borrowed coat she was sharper than she let herself appear—he'd known that since the night she'd caught up with him on the towpath, breathless, furious, holding a scrap of Kaldavian seal-wax like a relic. But this was the first time he'd heard fear in her voice that wasn't for herself.

"You're worried about my face," he said.

"I'm worried about what happens when Marrow gets a photograph of you and sends it downriver to every port from here to the sea. You've spent ten years making sure there's nothing to match it against. Don't hand her the original."

* * *

They waited in the ditch through the morning, sharing hard bread and warmer silence, watching the bridge work. It was instructive, the way ugly things were instructive. Farmers paid their tolls and their faces went into the book. A priest argued about the indignity of it and paid anyway. Two drovers tried to skirt the chalk square and were steered back into it with a courtesy so smooth it was almost gentle, and Petra understood that these weren't soldiers doing a job—they were men doing a favor for someone whose name they didn't know either.

At half past eleven the salt caravan came down from the north, eight wagons drawn by mules, drivers in stained aprons, the white dust of the pans ground into every seam of every man. Petra counted heads. Thirty-one, plus a woman selling dried fish who'd attached herself to the train somewhere behind them.

"Thirty-two," he murmured. "We're the last two wagons' worth of nobody."

Sela pulled the chest's strap tighter under her coat. They'd split its weight between them days ago—she carried the documents wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, he carried the empty iron box itself, stripped of its crest, sold in theory to a tinker in Vetch who'd never see a coin. The papers were lighter than the box ever was. That seemed important. He didn't say so.

They joined the tail of the caravan at the last bend, falling in behind a wagon whose driver was asleep sitting up. No one challenged them. Salt people moved like weather; you didn't ask where the rain had been.

Then the chalk square.

The soldier at the toll point was young and had the particular boredom of a man who'd been told the photography was above his pay grade and had decided to resent it. He waved the salt drivers through with barely a glance—apparently even Marrow's net had mesh sized for fish worth catching—but when Petra stepped up, the soldier's eyes came up too, and stayed.

"Toll's four coppers."

Petra put five on the plank. "Keep it."

The soldier didn't take the coin. Behind him, under the awning, the photographer had gone still over his tripod, and Petra felt the man's attention land on him with physical weight, the way current pushes on a diver in deep water—not grabbing, just insisting.

"Name?" the soldier said.

"Frey. Hauling salt out of Vetch pans." He jerked his chin at the wagon behind him. "Ask him."

The sleeping driver, miraculously, woke, squinted, and said, "Frey, yeah," with the magnificent indifference of a man protecting his nap, and the soldier—boredom reasserting itself over instinct—waved them on.

But as they crossed, Petra passed within three meters of the awning, close enough to see the photographer's ledger lying open on a folding table. Close enough to read, upside down, the column heading, in a clerk's careful hand:

**UNIDENTIFIED MALES — EASTBOUND — BY ORDER C.M.**

C.M. Captain Marrow. She wasn't chasing them anymore. She was building a wall of paper between them and everywhere else, and every traveler who stood in that chalk square was a brick.

On the far side, past the hut, past the bored soldier on the rail, Sela walked beside him for a hundred meters without speaking. Then, when the road bent and Krast dropped out of sight behind willows:

"He wrote your name down. Frey."

"Frey's dead. Died crossing a bridge once. Terrible business."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." But his hands weren't steady, and he put them in his pockets where she couldn't see. Ten years of method, and one sleepy salt driver had stood between him and the net. Luck wasn't a plan. Luck ran out the way air did, and always at depth.

* * *

They made camp that night in a culvert beneath the embankment, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide under a tin sheet Sela had bought in Vetch. The chest's contents lay between them in their oilcloth wrapping, and Sela had the first deciphered page flattened on her knees, weighted at the corners with stones.

She'd worked on it in stolen hours for three days—ciphered columns, a merchant's cipher she recognized from her archive years, the kind used by ministries that wanted records to look like shipping manifests. Now she was done, and she hadn't spoken in a long time, and Petra had learned that Sela's silences had weights, and this one was heavy.

"Read it," he said.

"I don't think you understand what it is yet."

"No. So read it."

She read. Her voice was level in the way voices are level when the alternative is not.

"It's a disposition list. Kaldavian officials, senior ones—admiralty, treasury, the church chancellery. Forty-one names. Each name has a column for disposition." She touched the entries. "'Transferred.' 'Retired with pension.' 'Detained.'" Her finger stopped. "'Removed.'"

"Removed where?"

"That's the thing. There's no 'where.' Just 'removed,' and then a date, and then—" she turned the page toward the firelight, "—a signature block. Authorization for the whole list. One signature repeated at the bottom of every page."

Petra leaned in. The name meant nothing to him. It was a good republican name, the kind carved over courthouse doors, the kind that ended up on commemorative stamps. He said as much.

Sela looked at him with an expression he'd come to dread, the expression of a woman about to make him carry something heavier.

"This man chairs the Founding Council's successor committee. He holds a ministry now. His portrait hangs in every customs house in the republic—including, I'd wager, the one we crossed this morning." She folded the page along its existing creases, precisely, archivist's hands. "This isn't evidence that someone powerful did something wrong twenty years ago. This is evidence that someone powerful is still doing it. The purge didn't end, Petra. It got promoted."

The fire ticked under its tin lid. Out on the dark plain, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Petra found himself thinking about the bridge. About the chalk square and the camera and the ledger marked **BY ORDER C.M.**, and about the courteous soldiers steering travelers gently back into frame. He'd spent a decade believing the past was a drowned thing—you could raise pieces of it, sell them, but it stayed down, it stayed quiet, unless you brought it up. He'd believed the country had buried Kaldavia so thoroughly that the burial itself would rot, forgotten, harmless.

But the burial hadn't rotted. It had been maintained. Men were still paid to maintain it. The camera at the bridge wasn't hunting a diver—it was the same instrument, running the same old work, the list and the dispositions and the removed, just with better lenses.

The past had teeth. It had always had teeth. He'd simply been swimming below where they reached.

"Sela." He kept his voice low. "The signature. If that name is still in power, then whoever sent Marrow isn't cleaning up an embarrassment. They're protecting an ongoing operation. Which means this stops being about a stolen chest the moment anyone senior reads those pages."

"Yes."

"Which means they won't stop at river towns. They'll close the passes. They'll close everything."

"Probably." She fed the page back into its oilcloth with the tenderness other people reserved for children. "There were forty-one names on that list. Do you know how many columns said 'removed'? Thirty-eight. And do you know what the other three said?" She tied the wrap. "'Retired with pension.' Somebody decided three men were worth more alive than dead, and signed it, and went home to dinner, and is dining still."

Petra lay back against the culvert wall and stared at the underside of the embankment, at the roots hanging down through the cracks like the rigging of some inverted wreck. He thought of his brother, and stopped thinking of his brother, the way you close a hatch against flooding.

Above them, far off on the eastern dark, the mountains were invisible, but he could feel them the way you feel a storm front—pressure, waiting, distance closing. Somewhere behind them, Ilse Marrow was reading the same roads he was. Somewhere ahead of them, snow was deciding when to fall.

And somewhere in the republic's capital, a man with a courthouse-door name was sleeping soundly in the belief that forty-one signatures had stayed underwater forever.

"First light," Petra said. "We walk. And from here on, we don't cross anything with a roof on it unless we can see all four walls first."

Sela blew out the fire. In the sudden dark her voice came dry and close:

"There's a smelter ruin off the east road, if the maps in my head are right. Four walls. No roof at all."

"Perfect," Petra said. "I'm starting to trust ruins more than people."

He didn't know yet how much further he'd have to trust them—or what he'd have to tell her inside those four walls to earn the right to hear her secrets back.
