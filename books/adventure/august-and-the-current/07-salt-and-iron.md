## 7. Salt and Iron

The smelter sat a mile off the salt road, half-swallowed by birch scrub, its chimney leaning like a drunk against the sky. Petra had found it the way he found everything—by looking for what other people had stopped seeing. A rusted tram line running into the trees. A drainage cut too straight to be natural. The building itself was a long iron shed with a collapsed roof at one end and, at the other, a drying room that still had its door.

He'd chosen it for the door. Doors mattered now. Since Krast, since the photographing of travelers and Sela's first deciphered page, doors were the only argument he trusted.

They'd been in it two days. Long enough for the rain to pass through and leave the world rinsed and cold. Long enough for Sela to fill three notebooks with her cramped cipher-work, sitting cross-legged on an old ore cart with a candle stub balanced on the cart's lip, reading the dead nation's arithmetic aloud only when she couldn't help it.

Petra spent the days doing what he always did: nothing, expertly. He walked the perimeter twice at dawn and once at dusk. He counted the seconds between sounds on the road—a cart, a dog, a cart again—and built a rhythm of the place until anything off-beat would ring like a cracked bell. He slept in shifts, or pretended to. He cleaned the diving knife he hadn't needed since the river and told himself the ritual was maintenance and not prayer.

The chest sat under a tarp of rotted sacking behind the drying room's second door, which opened onto a coal chute—a second way out, down and into the dark, if it came to that. He checked it each morning the way other men checked their pulse.

On the third evening, Sela broke the silence.

"You flinch when I read the names."

She didn't look up from her notebook. She said things like that—flat, observational, the way she catalogued damaged manuscripts—with no apparent understanding that she was handling live ordnance.

"I don't flinch," Petra said.

"You do. A small one. In the jaw." She turned a page. "You did it again when I read the naval officers' list. Not the civilians. Only the navy."

He said nothing. The fire was small—he kept it small, screened behind a sheet of boiler plate so no light escaped the broken windows—and it made the iron walls tick as they cooled. Outside, the birches moved in the wind with a sound like slow water.

"Vetch is two days on," Sela said. "The caravan master wants us there before market day. We could walk tonight."

"We walk tomorrow. Tonight the road will have eyes. Market day means checkpoints get bored and sloppy, but the night before, everyone's nervous."

"That's not why we're staying."

"No," Petra agreed. "It isn't."

She looked up then. Candlelight did something kind to her face that daylight refused to do; out in the world she was all angles and suspicion, a woman who flinched from touch like a burned cat. Here, in the iron dark, she just looked tired, and young, and like someone who had once been laughed at by friends in a university courtyard in a country that no longer existed.

"You've been circling it for three days," she said. "Whatever the navy list means to you. You circle things the way I used to circle the burned shelves. You think nobody notices because you're quiet about it. But quiet people are the easiest to read. There's less noise covering the signal."

Petra fed a stick to the fire. Watched it catch.

"My brother was navy," he said.

Sela went very still. It was the stillness of someone who understood that a door had just opened and that rushing it would close it forever.

* * *

His name had been Aldric Voss. That was the name Petra had buried along with everything else—along with the fishing town of Lowmark where they'd grown up, along with the mother who'd died believing both her sons were honest men. Ten years ago, when he'd paid a broker in Halden's Reach to make him into no one, he'd chosen *Petra* from a stonecutter's sign he passed on the way to the meeting. Stone. Something that stayed put and said nothing. It had seemed funny at the time. Nothing had seemed funny since.

"Aldric was four years older," Petra said. "Stronger, louder, better with people. The kind of boy who'd fight your fights for you whether you wanted him to or not. When I went into salvage, he went into the fleet. Kaldavian fleet, before—" He gestured vaguely eastward, toward the mountains, toward the erased thing. "Everyone did, on the coast. It was good pay and it was ours, kind of. Half our town was Kaldavian, or married to it, or had been until the maps changed."

"The fleet that got sold," Sela said quietly. "The one in the ledgers."

"The one that got sold." The words came out of him like something raised on a cable, dripping, heavier than expected. "I didn't know that's what happened. Nobody knew. We were told the fleet mutinied. That Kaldavian officers turned pirate and the republic had no choice but to disband everything, purge the rolls, scatter the crews. Aldric's name went on a list. Not a trial list. Just—a list. Names of men who'd served. Their families were supposed to report them."

"He ran?"

"He came home." Petra's jaw did the thing Sela had described; he let it. "Came home in the autumn, thin as wire, wouldn't say what he'd seen. Sat in our mother's kitchen for six weeks fixing nets he never took out. Then men came. Not soldiers—men in plain coats with a navy warrant and no signature on it. A contract with no signature. Same as mine, same as every piece of paper that runs this country. They asked where he'd been. He said he'd ask them the same question, and Aldric being Aldric, he asked it loud."

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the shed's bones, wind found a gap and moaned through it, low and tonal, like breath across a bottle mouth.

"They took him to Verrenport for questioning. That's the word they used. Questioning. My mother wrote letters for four months. Every one came back unopened. Then a package came—not from the navy, from a clerk, anonymous, the way mercy arrives in this country. Inside was his watch. His watch and a receipt for cremation, dated eleven days after his arrest. Eleven days. No charges filed. No body. A receipt."

Sela's hand had risen halfway to her mouth without her seeming to know it.

"I asked questions," Petra said. "For about a year I asked questions. I wrote to the naval office, to the magistrate, to a deputy minister whose name some sailor's widow gave me. And then the deputy minister's office wrote back—not to answer me, but to my landlord. Asking about me. My habits. My visitors. And I understood that the questions weren't going to find Aldric. The questions were going to find *me*. So I did the arithmetic every coward does. I put the watch in a lockbox, changed my name, and went underwater, where nobody asks anything and the dead don't mind company."

"You weren't a coward."

"I was exactly a coward. The efficient kind. The kind that survives." He looked at her across the fire. "You want to know the worst part? For ten years I told myself I stayed invisible to stay alive. But that's not true, or not all of it. I stayed invisible because visibility is what killed him. He had a name people knew. He had a voice. He stood in a kitchen and asked a question loudly, and the loudness is what signed his death warrant. So I became a man with no voice at all. Silent work, silent contracts, silent exits. I thought if I made myself small enough, quiet enough, weightless enough—the water would keep me. And it did. The water keeps anything that gives up enough of itself."

Sela set down her notebook. She did it carefully, aligning its edge with the cart's edge, the gesture of an archivist putting a fragile thing back in order.

"At the archive," she said, "the last day, when they came with the barrels of lye—I carried out what I could under my coat. Fourteen pages. That's all. Fourteen pages out of forty thousand." Her voice didn't break; it compressed, like material under pressure. "For nine years I've told myself fourteen was something. Some nights it is. Most nights it's ash with a number painted on it."

"And now you've got a chest full of the rest."

"Now I've got a chest full of the rest." She almost smiled. "And you've been guarding it like it's a bomb."

"It is a bomb."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Bombs are for other people. This—" she touched the tarp over the chest, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—this is a mirror. Everything in there has a shadow standing somewhere in the republic wearing its coat. Your brother's name might be in those rolls, Petra. The purged crews are itemized. Volume three, I think. If Aldric Voss served, he's in there. Not as a victim. As a *record*. Dated. Countersigned. Somebody wrote your brother into a ledger and closed the book on him, and that book has been sitting in a river ever since because somebody powerful needed it drowned."

Petra stared at the tarp.

He had spent three weeks treating the chest as cargo. Weight, buoyancy, risk, disposal. He had planned routes around it, bribes for it, hiding places for it. He had thought of it in the grammar of salvage: *asset, liability, evidence.* He had never once thought of it as a grave marker. And here was this exiled woman with ink-stained fingers telling him that volume three might contain the only honest sentence anyone had ever written about his brother—that Aldric Voss, fisherman's son, fleet man, fixer of nets, had existed, had served, had been erased by men whose signatures were still wet with power twenty years later.

"Read the rolls," he said. His voice came out rough, dredged.

"Tonight?"

"Tonight."

* * *

Volume three was bound in gray leather gone green with river-mold, and it smelled of the bottom of the Verren—silt and iron and old cold. Sela worked by candle and fire together, the cipher key spread beside her, and Petra sat closer than he had ever sat to the chest, close enough to touch it, watching her finger travel down columns of names.

Outside, the wind rose. The birches hissed. The chimney moaned its one long note.

The names went past: officers, engineers, quartermasters, boys of nineteen listed as *able seaman* with their fates reduced to abbreviations. *Transferred. Detained. Released. Unaccounted.* That last one most of all. *Unaccounted* was the ledger's word for taken, and everyone reading it then and everyone reading it now knew what it meant, and the men who wrote it had slept fine afterward, Sela said, because ledgers let you sleep. That was their whole purpose. To turn blood into bookkeeping.

Then her finger stopped.

She didn't say anything at first. He watched her check the cipher key, check it again, and he knew before she spoke, the way you know a cable has snagged before the strain gauge moves—some deep animal knowledge arriving ahead of the data.

"Aldric Voss," she read. "Lieutenant, third gunnery. Detained Verrenport, eleventh of Harvest-month." She looked up. "That's the date on your receipt."

"Keep reading."

"'Transferred to naval intelligence custody. Disposition: unaccounted.'" She paused. "There's a countersignature block. Initials only—standard practice for the intelligence sections, they never signed whole names. But there's a routing stamp." She turned the page toward him, angling it to the light, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing. "The routing stamp is a double anchor. Petra—it's the same stamp that appears on the founding-council letter I deciphered at Krast. The sale of the fleet. The man who routed your brother to intelligence is the same man who routed the fleet to its enemies. One pair of hands. One initials-block, appearing on both documents, twenty years apart."

The fire ticked. The chimney moaned. Petra knelt there on the smelter floor with river-mold on his knees and felt ten years of careful weightlessness fail him all at once, like a hull giving way—not catastrophically, not all at once, but with that first groaning flex that tells you the sea has won and it's only a question of how fast.

Aldric wasn't a footnote anymore. Aldric was a thread, and the thread ran upward, out of a gray ledger, through a routing stamp, into the hand of a man whose initials sat on the founding council's greatest crime—and that man, Sela had told him at Krast, reading by checkpoint lantern-light with her voice shaking—*that man still held office.*

"They questioned him because of the fleet," Petra said. The words came slowly, fitted together like salvage, piece by piece from the dark. "He'd seen something. On a ship, in a port, somewhere—he'd seen the fleet being sold, or heard it, and he came home because home was the only place he could think of. And they killed him for it and wrote *unaccounted*, and the man who ordered it put his initials two lines apart in history and went on to a ministry."

"Yes."

"Ten years I thought I was hiding from them." Petra looked at his own hands, diver's hands, scarred and pale as something cave-bred. "I wasn't hiding. I was *evidence*. Walking around loose. The brother of a man marked *unaccounted* by a double-anchor stamp. If I'd ever pushed hard enough, I'd have ended up in a river myself, and not for salvage."

Sela reached across and closed volume three gently, her palm flat on the cover.

"So now you know why the navy sweeps the Verren with instruments," she said. "Now you know why Marrow burns towns looking for us. It was never about a stolen chest. It's about a stamp. One set of initials, repeated. And we're carrying the only copy in the world."

Petra sat back on his heels. Above the broken roofline, through the smoke-hole, he could see the first stars coming out over the black shoulder of the hills—and beyond them, faintly, impossibly far, the white suggestion of the Ashen Range catching moonlight, weeks early, like a deadline written in the sky.

The chest no longer felt dangerous. That was the strange thing. Three weeks of fear, of weight and pursuit and the constant animal math of survival—and now, kneeling in a dead smelter with his brother's fate closed under Sela's palm, the chest felt like the heaviest and lightest thing he had ever carried. It was Aldric's watch in a lockbox. It was fourteen pages under a coat. It was every receipt issued for every life this country had quietly spent. It was not cargo.

It was a debt, and he was the courier.

"We move at first light," he said. "Not tomorrow afternoon. First light. And Sela—" he met her eyes, and heard himself say the next words as if someone else were speaking them, someone with a name, "—when we reach Velin Ost, if it costs my skin to put that stamp in print, then my skin is finally worth something."

Sela nodded once, archivist-precise, and blew out the candle.

In the dark, the wind shifted, and brought up the road a sound that did not belong to the rhythm of the place: wheels, many of them, moving fast, and beneath the wheels the unmistakable jingle of cavalry chain.

Petra was on his feet with the knife in his hand before his heart finished its first hard beat.

Someone had found the smelter.
