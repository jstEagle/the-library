## 8. Marrow's Net

The smuggler's name was Doss Ferren, and Ilse Marrow learned it from a boy selling eels on the Halden's Reach quay.

She paid him a copper for the information and a second copper for forgetting he'd given it. The boy took both coins with the same flat competence she'd once seen in harbor rats across three navies, and vanished into the fish-smoke before she could ask anything else. Good. Children who lingered around questions had short lives in river towns where the navy was asking them.

Doss Ferren ran cargo up the Verren in a flat-bottomed barge called the *Kindly Tern*, which was the kind of name smugglers gave boats when they wanted customs officers to smile instead of search. He'd been working the middle reaches for eleven years. He knew every slough, every drowned snag, every stretch of bank where a man could beach a skiff and disappear into reeds. And four nights ago, according to the boy and two other sources who didn't know they were corroborating each other, he'd been moored below the old quarry bend when something heavy came up out of the water on a cable.

Marrow stood at the rail of the navy cutter *Vigilant* and watched the morning fog burn off the river, and thought about cables.

The report from the survey team had been precise in the way reports were precise when they wanted to avoid saying anything: anomalous metal signature, consistent with salvage activity, quadrant seven, no vessel observed. Anomalous metal signature. As if the river had grown a tooth overnight. She'd read it three times and then read the diver's file that shouldn't have existed—a broker's ledger page, half-burned, recovered from a stove in a room the broker had stopped renting two days before anyone thought to look for him.

The page listed six names. Five of them were dead men or ghosts. The sixth was an entry with no name at all, just a payment code and a notation in the broker's cramped hand: *the quiet one. Verren work. No paperwork. Pays clean.*

No paperwork. In ten years of chasing people for the navy, Marrow had learned that *no paperwork* was never modesty. It was armor. A man who left no trace of himself had been leaving no trace of himself for a long time, and men did that for one of two reasons: they were running from something, or they were so good at what they did that invisibility was simply part of the craft.

Sometimes both.

"Captain." Ensign Brakk, young enough that his collar still sat wrong on his neck, appeared at her elbow with a clipboard she hadn't asked for. "Ferren's boat is moored at Krast landing. Do you want us to board?"

"Not yet."

"We have authority to—"

"I know what we have authority to do, Ensign." She kept her voice pleasant. She always kept her voice pleasant; it made people underestimate how carefully she was listening to herself. "Boarding him tells him we're looking. I want him to tell us we're looking. There's a difference worth a day's delay."

Brakk nodded as if he understood, which he didn't, and withdrew. Marrow turned back to the water.

Below that gray surface, somewhere between the quarry bend and the sea, lay a chest with a crest on it that she had seen exactly once before in her life, twenty years ago, on the shoulder boards of an officer who used to come to her father's house for dinner.

* * *

By noon she had the net rigged.

It wasn't a complicated net. River towns were small and greedy and afraid in stable proportions, and rewards worked on all three qualities at once. She had notices printed in Halden's Reach, Krast, Vetch ferry, and four landings smaller than any of those—notices offering two hundred crowns for information regarding salvage diving on the Verren above the quarry bend, no questions asked about how the information was obtained. Two hundred crowns was more than a deckhand earned in a season. It was calibrated. Enough to tempt, not enough to make a man feel like a traitor for taking it. Traitors hesitated. Greedy men moved fast.

Within a day, the net began to hum.

A tavern keeper in Krast remembered a lean man drinking alone and paying with river-silt-stained coin. A ferrymaster's wife recalled a woman asking after overland routes east—unusual, that time of year, unusual alone. A salt trader's clerk swore he'd sold rope and lamp oil to a stranger with diver's hands, callused in the pattern rope-work leaves. None of it was proof. All of it was direction. The man was moving east, and he wasn't moving alone anymore, and that interested Marrow more than she let show on her face.

Because a man who erased himself did not pick up companions. Companions were witnesses. Witnesses were liabilities. If the diver had taken someone with him, either he'd been forced to, or—

Or the companion mattered to whatever he'd pulled out of the river.

Marrow sat in the stern cabin of the *Vigilant* that evening with the burned ledger page flattened under a brass weight, and let herself think the thought she'd been not-thinking since Halden's Reach.

Kaldavia.

The name didn't appear in any document she had access to anymore. It had been sanded out of atlases, out of treaties, out of the navy's own hydrographic charts—the Kaldavian coast renamed, the Kaldavian fleet struck from the registry as if ships could be un-built by ink. Twenty years ago the republic had fought a war nobody called a war, against a nation nobody would name afterward, and when it ended, the ending had been conducted with such thoroughness that a whole country now existed only in the memories of people old enough to be embarrassed by them.

Her father had been there. Her father had signed things there. She knew this the way children of important men know things—by osmosis, by overheard conversations that went silent when she entered rooms, by the way certain names made his face do something complicated and quickly hidden.

And she knew, with the certainty that had carried her through nineteen years of naval service and two inquiries into her own conduct, that whatever was in that chest, the navy's interest in it did not come from the top down. It came from sideways. From men whose names weren't on the orders but whose money was behind them. Admiral Vessic had given her this assignment personally, in a room with no clerk present, and had said the words *recover and destroy* without ever saying *destroy*, and had looked at her with the particular patience of a man waiting to see whether she was smart enough to hear what wasn't said.

Recover and destroy. Not return. Not investigate. Destroy.

You destroyed evidence that convicted someone.

Marrow poured herself two fingers of the bad brandy the cutter carried and drank it slowly, watching the ledger page in the lamplight. The broker's notation stared back at her. *The quiet one.*

Somewhere east of here, a man with no name was carrying a chest full of the past toward the mountains, and she was supposed to take it from him and feed it to a furnace, and she had been doing this job long enough, and well enough, that no one had ever thought to wonder whether she might one day want to know what she was burning.

* * *

Doss Ferren told her everything on the third night, though not because she asked.

She'd had him watched, not approached—two of her own men, plain-clothed, rotating shifts, nothing a professional would spot. On the third night Ferren got drunk in a Krast tavern called the Split Oar, drunk in the specific way of a man carrying something too heavy, and started telling the room that he'd seen lights on the river below the quarry bend, and that the navy was lying about the wreck being stripped already, and that whoever was diving it had found something worth killing for because a navy launch had nearly run him down the next morning and the officer aboard had looked at him like—

He never finished the sentence. The room laughed it off. Smugglers saw navy launches every week.

But Marrow's watchers reported it, and Marrow did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic said Ferren had seen the dive itself—moored below the bend, lights, cable, the shape of something coming up—and had then, fatally, seen the navy's response, which meant he could place the wreck within a quarter mile and the timeline within a night. That was too much. That was the kind of knowledge that, repeated in the wrong tavern to the wrong listener, would bring other divers to the river with grappling hooks and greed.

She went to see him herself.

He was on his boat, nursing the morning after, and he took one look at her coat—navy gray, cut plain, no insignia visible until she wanted it visible—and his face went through surprise, fear, and calculation in the space of a breath. Calculation last. That was typical. Men like Ferren died of calculation.

"Captain," he managed.

"Mister Ferren." She stepped aboard uninvited and sat on his cargo hatch like she owned it. "You saw something on the river four nights ago."

"I saw some lights. Could've been—"

"You saw a dive operation below the quarry bend. You saw a heavy object raised on a cable. And yesterday you started talking about it." She let that sit. Outside, gulls argued over something dead in the shallows. "I'm going to tell you how this goes, and then you're going to have a choice, and I want you to understand that I'm giving you the choice honestly, because I don't enjoy what happens otherwise."

Ferren's throat moved. "What happens otherwise."

"The navy can't have witnesses to classified recovery operations. You know this. Every man who works this river knows this. Normally we handle it with a formal oath and a pension and everyone pretends the pension isn't a leash." She paused. "But you talked. In a public house. To strangers. So the normal handling is off the table, and what's left is the handling nobody puts in regulations."

"I'll swear anything. I'll sign anything. Captain, I've got a daughter in—"

"In Vetch. I know." She'd known before she boarded. She always knew the family details first; they were the load-bearing walls of a man's silence. "Here's your choice. You leave the river. Tonight. You go to Vetch, you collect your daughter, and you don't come back to the Verren for five years. You find honest work or dishonest work somewhere the water doesn't connect to this water. In exchange, the navy has no record of you, no warrant for you, and no reason to look for you. Or you stay, and keep talking, and in that case—" she stood, brushed off her coat, "—you should know that the men I answer to are less patient than I am, and considerably less honest about the choices they offer."

"That's it? Just—leave?"

"That's it. Consider it the pension, paid in advance."

He took it. Of course he took it. She watched him cast off within the hour, the *Kindly Tern* wallowing downstream toward Vetch with its cargo of nothing, and she felt the small cold satisfaction of a problem solved cleanly, and beneath the satisfaction, the older colder thing that had been sitting in her chest since Halden's Reach.

Because she had lied to him. Not about the choice—that had been real—but about the reason. The navy's regulations said witnesses were handled with oaths and pensions. What she'd actually been protecting was the operation itself, and beyond the operation, whatever the operation was protecting. She had threatened a smuggler's life, gently, in order to keep a hole in the world closed around a chest she hadn't seen.

And she found she needed to see it. That was the wound of it, the thing she probed at night in the stern cabin with the brandy: not doubt about the mission, but hunger for its object. Twenty years of her father's silences. Twenty years of the word *Kaldavia* existing only in the negative space of documents, a country-shaped absence. And now a diver with no name had pulled the absence itself out of the river, and her orders were to destroy it unread.

Unread. As if she were still the kind of officer who could follow that order.

* * *

On the fifth day, the net caught something better than rumor.

One of her agents in Krast—a customs clerk with gambling debts she'd purchased along with his loyalty—brought her a photograph. The bridge checkpoint photographed every traveler crossing east; it was routine, and routine photographs went into routine files that nobody examined. But the clerk had been told what to look for, and he'd found it: a lean man in a fish-oil slicker, face turned half away from the camera, hands wrong for a laborer and right for a diver. Beside him, a woman with the posture of someone who had spent years bent over desks, carrying a canvas bag she held like it contained scripture.

And between them, on a handcart, wrapped in tarpaulin, a rectangular mass the clerk had noted in the margin: *heavy. iron-bound. neither would let porters touch it.*

Marrow held the photograph under the lamp for a long time.

The man's face was mostly shadow, but the jaw was visible, and the set of the shoulders, and something in the way he stood slightly ahead of the woman—not protective, exactly. Positioned. Like a man who had spent his whole life making sure his body was the thing between trouble and everything else.

"There's a reward notice posted in every town from here to the foothills," Brakk said beside her. "Two hundred crowns. Someone will sell them out within the week. Caravans talk, waystations talk—"

"Yes."

"And when we have the location?"

Marrow set the photograph down. Through the cabin's salt-fogged glass, the Verren slid past, brown and patient, keeping everything it kept.

"When we have the location," she said, "I go up myself. Small team. No launch, no colors. This stops being a navy matter the moment we leave the water."

Brakk frowned. "Admiral Vessic authorized—"

"Admiral Vessic authorized me." She picked up the photograph again, and her thumb moved, without her quite deciding it, to the edge of the frame—to the space beyond the two travelers, the empty road climbing east toward mountains that were already wearing their first white. "He wants the chest recovered and the matter closed. How I close it is my discretion. That's what discretion means, Ensign. Write it down somewhere you'll remember."

She dismissed him and sat alone with the lamp and the photograph and the burned page of the broker's ledger, and finally she allowed herself the question she'd been circling for six days, the one with her father's handwriting at the center of it.

If the pages named the men who had ended Kaldavia—if that was why they had to burn—then the pages were a list. And lists had signatures. And her father had been there, at the ending, signing things.

She was not chasing a diver. She understood that now, fully, the way you understand a current only once you're in it. She was chasing the moment she opened that chest and looked for one name among many, and found it, or didn't. Both outcomes would change everything. She'd simply chosen, somewhere between Halden's Reach and here, that she would rather know than obey.

Marrow blew out the lamp. East of the river, the Ashen Range stood up against the stars, white already at the shoulders, weeks ahead of schedule.

Snow was closing the passes early this year. Whatever she intended to do, she would have to climb fast—and somewhere on those slopes, a man with no name and a woman with a canvas bag were climbing faster.
