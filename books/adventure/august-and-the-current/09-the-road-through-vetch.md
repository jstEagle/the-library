## 9. The Road Through Vetch

The salt road began where the river gave up pretending to be useful. Sela saw it first from the ridge above the ford: a long brown ribbon of carts and mules crawling east through the scrub, trailing dust like a wound that wouldn't close. She counted eleven wagons before she lost her nerve for counting.

"Salt traders," Petra said beside her. He said it the way he said everything now—flat, economical, a man spending words like coins he wasn't sure he'd earn back.

"You know them?"

"I know the route. Vetch to the high country, then the range. They run it every autumn, last caravans out before the snow." He lowered the battered spyglass he'd bought off a dead man's mule two towns back. "They'll take paying passengers if the passengers don't ask questions."

"And do we ask questions?"

"We don't talk at all, if we can help it."

Sela had learned in three weeks of running that Petra's silences were not empty. They were load-bearing. He built them between himself and the world the way other men built walls, and she had learned to read what stood behind them: the way his eyes moved over a crowd, sorting threats; the way he touched the chest strap at his shoulder every few minutes without seeming to notice he did it; the way he never, ever walked in front of her when they turned a corner, always behind, always between her and whatever came.

She was beginning to suspect that this was as close to tenderness as he knew how to get.

They went down to the ford.

* * *

The caravan master was a broad woman with a face like a slammed door and a name she offered freely, which surprised Sela until she understood the economy of it: Maretta Vosk could afford a name because she had nothing to hide. Everyone on the salt road knew her. That was her armor, and it was better armor than any Sela had ever owned.

"Passengers," Maretta said, looking them over the way a butcher looks over a carcass. "You walk or you ride?"

"Walk," Petra said.

"You pay either way. Five silver each to Velin Ost side of the range. You eat what we eat, you work if I say work, and if soldiers stop us, you're my cousins from the coast, dumb as posts, and you let me do the talking." She spat to one side, ceremonially. "You cause trouble, I put you off wherever we happen to be, and I keep your silver. Yes?"

"Yes," Petra said.

Sela watched him count out the coins and noticed something she'd been noticing for days and refusing to name: how little was left in the purse. The waystation raid had taken most of it—their money, their spare clothes, the forged papers Sela had spent two feverish nights preparing—and what remained was a thin rattle of silver and copper that would not survive many more Marettas.

Five silver each. Petra paid it without blinking, which meant he'd already done the arithmetic and decided they were worth more moving than the money was worth keeping. It was the closest thing to optimism she'd seen him express.

Maretta jerked her chin toward the wagons. "Ride with the Kessarin family. Old man talks too much but he's harmless. Don't tell him anything true."

* * *

The Kessarins turned out to be four generations deep in the salt trade, folded into and around a single wagon like letters in an envelope. There was old Dov Kessarin, who talked too much exactly as advertised; his son Bram, who drove and said nothing; Bram's wife Yeva, who knitted with the grim efficiency of a woman arming a small army against winter; and the child.

The child was a girl of perhaps seven, named Pell, and she was the reason Sela understood, by the second day, why Maretta had put them here. A family wagon was camouflage. Nobody searched the wagon with the grandmother and the baby things and the knitting. Nobody looked twice at travelers folded into domesticity.

Sela hadn't been folded into anything domestic in six years. Not since the archive burned and her name went onto a list and every door she'd ever known closed at once. She'd forgotten what it felt like—to sit in a moving wagon among people who assumed you belonged there, who passed you bread without weighing whether you might be a liability, who complained about the price of harness leather within earshot of strangers because strangers were just weather, something that passed through and left no mark.

She kept waiting for it to hurt. It didn't. That was the frightening part.

* * *

Petra rode on the wagon bench beside Bram and said nothing, which suited Bram fine. Sela sat in the back among the salt sacks and the knitting and watched him from behind, and after a while she realized she was cataloguing him the way she used to catalogue documents—noting provenance, noting condition, noting the places where the record had been altered.

Because it had been altered. Everything about him was an alteration. The way he held his shoulders was a forgery of ease. His accent drifted—coastal when he spoke to Maretta, highland when the old man asked him something, neutral when he spoke to Sela—as though he'd sanded down his voice so often it no longer had a grain. Even his hands, those careful diver's hands, moved like hands performing the memory of themselves.

He had erased himself so thoroughly that Sela sometimes wondered what was left underneath. And she wondered—this was the thought she flinched from, so she kept having it—whether he wondered too.

On the third morning, crossing the flats below Vetch, the child found him.

* * *

Pell Kessarin had decided, sometime during the first day, that the silent man on the bench was hers to interrogate. She was too young to have learned that some adults are locked doors. She simply climbed up beside him whenever the road allowed it and asked questions in the relentless, cheerful way of children everywhere: Why do you have a scar there. Do mules dream. Is the sea really made of salt like everyone says.

Petra answered maybe one question in five, always briefly, always factually, and Pell treated these answers like treasure, hoarding them, repeating them to her grandmother at night. Sela watched this ritual develop with something caught in her throat that she refused to examine.

On the morning of the fourth day, the flats opened wide and gold under a sky scoured clean, and Pell climbed up beside him and asked the question.

"What's your name?"

Sela, in the back, felt the whole wagon go still around the words—or rather, felt herself go still, because no one else had heard anything unusual in them. It was a child's question. Children asked it constantly. It cost nothing.

Except that she saw Petra's hands tighten on the bench edge. Saw the pause stretch—one heartbeat, two, three—long past the point where a normal man would have answered. He was not deciding what fake name to give. She knew his fakes by now; he could produce them like coins from a conjurer's sleeve. *Tarn. Vesik. Marek.* He'd used all three in the last week alone, smooth as water.

This pause was different. This pause was a man standing at a door he'd welded shut ten years ago and finding, to his horror, that the hinges still worked.

"Pell," Yeva said mildly, not looking up from her knitting, "leave the man be."

"It's all right." Petra's voice came out strange. Roughened. He looked down at the girl, and Sela saw his face do something she had never seen it do in three weeks of hard travel and harder silence: it opened. Just slightly. Just enough to see the light behind the shutters.

"My name is—" he began.

And stopped.

Whatever lived behind that door looked out, took one breath of morning air, and retreated. Sela watched it happen—watched the light go back behind the shutters, watch the face close plank by plank, and when he spoke again the voice was smooth and false and coastal:

"Tarn. My name is Tarn."

Pell nodded, satisfied, and demanded to know whether mules dreamed, and the moment dissolved into the ordinary noise of the road. But Sela had seen it. She had seen the almost, and the almost was worse than any lie, because it meant the real name was still in there, alive, beating like a heart under floorboards.

She looked away, out at the gold flats, and discovered her own eyes were burning, and did not know—could not have said under torture—whether she was grieving for him or for herself.

* * *

That evening they camped in the lee of a dry wash, twenty fires strung along the caravan like beads, and Sela sat with her back against the wagon wheel and listened to the Kessarins argue about whether the northern pass would hold open another month. Old Dov said yes. Bram said no. Yeva said the mountains didn't care what either of them said, which ended the argument.

Petra was at the far fire, helping Bram check a harness fitting, and Sela watched him work and thought about names.

Her own name was ash. Sela Iven, assistant archivist, third grade, burned with the rest of the Kaldavian records on a night she still measured time against—before, after. She had kept it anyway. That was the difference between them, she was beginning to understand. He had thrown his name away like ballast, and she had carried hers like a stone in her shoe for six years, hurting with every step, refusing to put it down because putting it down would mean the fire had won.

Two strategies for surviving the same drowning. She wondered which one actually kept you alive.

"You're staring," Yeva said, beside her suddenly, needles clicking.

"I'm thinking."

"Thinking's free. Staring costs." The old woman's eyes were sharp as tacks above her knitting. "You two aren't cousins. I've had cousins. Cousins fight about food."

Sela said nothing.

Yeva clicked her needles. "You don't have to tell me. Maretta says don't ask, so I don't ask. But I'll tell you what I know, since nobody else will." She nodded toward Petra's silhouette at the far fire. "That man has been alone so long he's forgotten it's a choice. People like that—they don't join caravans. They hide in them. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and someday somebody ought to point it out to him." A pause. Click, click. "I'm pointing it out to you, because you're the one sitting here instead of over there."

"He wouldn't thank you for that."

"No," Yeva agreed placidly. "They never do."

* * *

Vetch came up out of the plain on the fifth day like something exhaled—a town of white walls and blue doors built around a well so ancient its rim had been worn to a saddle by centuries of rope. The caravan halted in the market square to take on water and provisions, and Maretta gave them two hours and a warning: stay in sight of the wagons.

Sela stayed in sight of the wagons. Petra did not.

She found him eventually at the edge of the square, standing very still in front of the town's notice board, and she knew before she reached him that something was wrong, because his stillness had changed quality. This was not resting stillness. This was the stillness of a man reading his own death warrant.

The board held the usual things—grain prices, a lost dog, a proclamation about bridge tolls. And pinned neatly in the center, fresh paper, crisp ink, an official seal she recognized with a lurch of cold in her stomach:

By order of the Naval Directorate. Information sought regarding two persons traveling eastward...

There were no likenesses. Just descriptions—hers accurate, his deliberately vague, as though whoever wrote it couldn't quite fix his face in words—and a reward figure that made her breath catch. And beneath the official text, in smaller print, a line that told her everything about the mind that had composed it:

*These persons are believed to be carrying stolen property of sentimental value to certain families. Its return will be handled discreetly.*

Discreetly. Sela read the word three times. It was a promise written in the grammar of a threat. Whoever had drafted this notice wanted the chest back without noise, without trial, without witnesses—which meant the reward was for information only, and what happened after the information arrived would happen in cellars.

"She's close," Sela said quietly.

"Mm." Petra hadn't moved. "Three days. Maybe less. This went up yesterday—the ink's not faded, the pin's not rusted." He turned from the board, and his face was the flat gray of deep water. "We ride with the caravan as far as it goes, then we move faster than it does. The pass won't wait and neither will she."

"Petra—"

"*Tarn,*" he corrected automatically, and then heard himself, and something flickered across his face—disgust, maybe, or exhaustion—at how fast the lie came now, how it had become the reflex instead of the truth.

But it was the other thing he'd said that lodged in Sela and refused to leave. *She.* No name. No explanation. Just the pronoun, dropped like a stone into the conversation, and the way his jaw set around it told her this was not the first time he'd carried that particular weight. There was a history in that *she*, a shape of someone specific, and he had never once spoken of it, and she understood—with the archivist's instinct for what a document refuses to say—that the woman hunting them was the one person alive whose name he never falsified, because he never spoke it at all.

They walked back to the wagons through the blue-doored streets, and the market noise closed over them, and Sela carried two things out of Vetch: the knowledge that Captain Ilse Marrow was three days behind them and climbing, and the memory of a man at a wagon bench opening his mouth to give a child his true name—

—and the sound, faint and terrible, of the door beginning to swing shut.
