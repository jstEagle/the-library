## 10. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation at Drem's Hollow sat where the salt road bent north out of the river country, a long stone barn and a low inn with a stable yard full of mud, built by someone who had expected the road to grow and been dead fifty years before it didn't. The caravan stopped there for the night because the drovers always stopped there, the way water stops in a bend, and Petra understood that this was exactly the kind of place he had spent ten years avoiding: one door, one yard, one road out, and everyone inside knowing everyone else's business down to the color of their boots.

But the caravan was the cover now. That was the arithmetic Sela had taught him without meaning to—a man alone is a question, but a man among forty is furniture—and so he ate the gray stew and drank the thin wine and let the noise of the salt traders wash over him like silt settling.

He did not like it. He noted that he did not like it, the way he noted a current pulling wrong, and then he made himself sit with it, because the whole point of the caravan was to be a man who could sit anywhere.

Sela sat across from him, her back to the wall—she'd learned that much from watching him—and worked by lamplight on a page she'd copied from memory, her lips moving over the ciphered syllables like a woman praying in a language she was only half sure of. She had gotten faster. Three weeks ago a single page took her a night; now she could strip one in an hour if the light held.

"Anything?" he asked.

"A requisition order." She turned the page toward him without looking up. "Grain shipments out of Kaldavia's southern provinces, six months before the collapse. Signed receipts showing the grain never arrived where it was supposed to go."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning somebody starved a province on paper." She took the page back. "It's not the fleet document. But it's the same hand. Whoever signed this signed everything. He was busy that year."

Petra looked at her hands—ink-stained, cracked from the cold, steady as a surgeon's—and felt the thing he had been refusing to feel since Vetch, which was that he trusted her. Not completely. Trust wasn't a wall you finished building; it was a current you swam in and checked every stroke. But three weeks of shared bread and shared watches and shared silence had worn a channel in him, and the chest rode easier between them than it ever had alone.

That was the thought he was having when the stable boy came through the room, and that was the thought he would remember afterward, when everything went to pieces, because paranoia is a teacher that only grades you after the exam.

* * *

He woke past midnight to the sound of a dog that wasn't barking.

That was how he'd learned to listen, years ago, diving black water: not for the sounds, but for the absences. A reef goes quiet before something big moves through it. The yard dog at Drem's Hollow had been complaining all evening at every cart and owl and drunk, and now it was silent, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was men moving carefully.

He was out of his blanket and across the cold floor before his mind finished the sentence. Sela was already awake—he'd stopped being surprised by that; she slept like a diver, one ear on the surface—and she had the chest's straps in her hands before he reached her.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Don't know. Front's watched or they'd have come loud. Back?"

"Kitchen. There was a wood door."

They went through the kitchen with their boots in their hands, and Petra put his eye to the gap in the wood door and saw the yard, silvered with a rising moon, and saw nothing—which meant the back was open, which meant they wanted the back open, which meant—

"They're herding," he breathed. "Front and sides closed. They want us out the back into the trees, where it's quiet."

"Then we go out the front," Sela whispered, "where everyone can see us."

He stared at her. It was insane. It was also, he realized with a lurch, correct. Marrow's men wanted no witnesses; they'd taken the smuggler in Krast quietly, a body found in the river with his pockets sewn shut. Forty sleeping traders were a problem. A raid in the open yard, in front of the caravan, in front of the innkeeper and the stable boys—that was a different animal entirely. Fixers worked in the dark. Light was armor.

Or it got you killed faster. There was no version of the next five minutes that was safe. There were only versions, and he had to pick one.

"The chest stays with me," he said. "You carry nothing. If I go down, you run east and don't stop."

"You've said that before."

"And you've ignored it before. Consistency matters."

She almost smiled. Then the front windows of the common room went dark—not blown out, shuttered, from outside—and the arithmetic changed, because men who close shutters are planning to stay a while, and the yard was suddenly the only lit place left in the world.

"Front," Petra said. "Now."

* * *

What happened in the yard happened fast and mostly sideways, the way real violence does—nothing like the clean geometry of stories.

They came out the inn's front door into moonlight and shouting, and the shouting was theirs, deliberately: Sela screaming *fire*, high and ragged, the one word that empties buildings in any language on the road. Doors banged along the wagon line. Drover lanterns bloomed. Somewhere a horse screamed and kicked its stall to splinters.

And in the sudden crowd of half-dressed men with cudgels and lanterns, four shapes in dark oilskins moved wrong—too coordinated, too calm, closing on the inn door they'd just left.

One of them saw Petra and the chest strap across his shoulder and said, quietly, "There," and the word carried more certainty than any shout.

Petra ran.

Not away—through. Through the wagon line, under the belly of a mule that lashed out and caught one of the oilskins in the teeth, buying him two seconds, around the well, over the low wall into the trampled beet field beyond, Sela's breath ragged at his left shoulder. Behind them the yard boiled with confused, furious traders, and a voice—the caravan master's, huge with outrage—was demanding to know who was breaking his peace, and for one bright moment Petra thought the crowd itself might hold them.

Then a pistol went off, once, into the air, and the crowd's courage broke the way crowds do, and the four shapes came over the wall after them into the field.

Petra ran the way he dove: counting. Counting breaths, counting strides, counting the distance to the tree line at the field's far edge. Two hundred strides. His lungs were iron bands. The chest hammered against his spine with every step, sixty pounds of lead-lined history trying to drag him down, and he did not drop it, would not drop it, though some cold clerk in the back of his skull ran the numbers and reported that the chest weighed more than his odds.

A second shot. Mud spat up ahead of Sela's feet. She jinked left without breaking stride—an archivist's instinct, dodging, he'd have thought, nothing but falling books—and then the trees took them.

The trees were old beech, planted in ranks by some dead lord, and the ranks gave them lanes to run in, straight and moonlit, which cut both ways. Petra cut off the lane into the tangle between the rows, hauling Sela after him, and they went through branches that stripped their sleeves and faces, and behind them the pursuit spread out and slowed, because four men hunting two in dark woods will always choose the lanes first.

They stopped, finally, in a dry streambed a mile on, both of them folded double, breathing like bellows. Petra's arm was bleeding where a branch had opened it. Sela had lost a shoe somewhere and stood in the frozen mud in one stocking foot and hadn't said a word about it.

"They knew the room," she gasped. "They came to our door first. I heard them pass it—they didn't check the others."

"I know."

"Petra. Nobody knew which room. We registered under the caravan's list, we paid with the master's chits—"

"I know." He straightened up. The cold had come into him now, the old cold, the deep-water cold, and it was almost a relief, like putting on a coat he'd owned for years. "Somebody sold us."

* * *

They walked most of the rest of the night, off the road, following the streambed south until it bent east, and at gray dawn they climbed a wooded ridge and looked back at Drem's Hollow, where smoke rose from the inn chimney as if nothing in the world had happened.

The caravan was gone. Of course it was gone; the master had a schedule and forty tons of salt and no reason at all to wait for the two strangers who'd brought armed men into his yard. Petra couldn't even blame him. He'd have done the same.

Which left them here: one shoe, one bleeding arm, no money—their purse had been in Petra's coat, and the coat was still hanging on a peg in a room in Drem's Hollow, because a man doesn't run through woods wearing a coat that catches on every branch—and no caravan, and the Ashen Range still nine days' walk north, and somewhere behind them, coming up the road at her own pace, Captain Ilse Marrow.

"We lost the money," Sela said. She said it flatly, inventorying, the way she inventoried documents. "We lost the caravan. We lost the cover. Anyone looking for a diver and an archivist traveling with salt traders now knows to look for a diver and an archivist traveling alone."

"Yes."

"And your arm needs stitching."

"Yes."

"And we're sitting on a ridge in the open having this conversation, which is the stupidest thing either of us has done tonight, and I include running toward the front door."

Despite everything, something in Petra's chest loosened. "You're learning to think like a criminal."

"I'm learning to think like an archivist whose archive burned," she said. "It's the same skill set."

They moved again, along the ridgeline, under the trees, until they found a fold of ground with rock at its back and thorn at its face, and there Petra let Sela stitch his arm with sail thread boiled in wine, because she claimed her mother had been a field medic in the Kaldavian army and he had no better option and no money for a better one. She stitched badly and honestly, and he watched the hills instead of the needle, and the whole time the cold clerk in his skull laid out the ledger.

Somebody sold them. Who?

The stable boy who'd come through the common room at supper, who'd lingered too long near their table, who'd asked Sela—in the friendly, bored way of stable boys—whether they'd be going on with the caravan tomorrow or striking off alone. He remembered that now. He remembered dismissing it. That was the wound of it: not that betrayal existed, but that he'd seen it coming and called it weather.

Or the innkeeper, paid to note faces. Or one of the drovers, bought with a month's wages. Or—his mind offered this last one gently, the way you offer a knife handle-first—Sela herself, some signal given in Vetch, some mark left on a door.

He hated himself for the thought even as he completed it. That was the architecture of paranoia; it built every room the same, and eventually you lived in the one with your friends' names on the doors. Ten years ago he'd have packed right now, slipped away while she slept, gone east alone with the chest and his own bad company. Ten years ago he'd have been certain it was survival.

He looked at her instead. She was rewrapping the thread, squinting at the needle in the gray light, muttering cipher syllables under her breath out of habit, guarding pages that existed only in her head. Her one-shoed foot was blue with cold. She had followed him out of a burning life with nothing, and she had run through a beet field in the moonlight screaming *fire* to save a stranger's chest of papers, and she had never once asked what the papers were worth to *her*.

"Sela."

She looked up.

"In Vetch. The market. Did you speak to anyone? Write anything? Leave any kind of message?"

The question landed on her face like a slap, and he watched her understand exactly what he was asking, and he watched her decide not to flinch from it.

"No," she said. "Did you?"

"No."

"Then either one of us is lying, or somebody outside this ridge sold us, and I know which one I believe." She tied off the thread and bit it clean. "Check my pack if it helps you sleep. Read my copies. I'd rather you checked and stayed than trusted and gone."

And that—God help him—that undid him more than the shots in the yard. Because trust that survives being tested isn't innocence; it's load-bearing. He'd forgotten there was such a thing.

"I don't need to check," he said.

"You should anyway. Someday. When we're not freezing." She pulled her blanket around them both, practical as a ledger line. "Sleep. You take first watch after."

* * *

He took the watch as the sun came up properly, and the world turned gold and ordinary, and from the ridge he could see the salt road unspooling north toward the gray smudge of the mountains, and he made himself look at it the way he'd once looked at wreck sites: methodically, piece by piece, asking what each feature cost and what it offered.

Nine days to the passes on foot, slower with no money to buy rides. Snow already on the high peaks, weeks early. Marrow behind them, and now Marrow's people ahead of them too, seeded along the road like rust in iron, because the raid hadn't been a capture attempt—it had been a flush. Drive them out of the caravan, strip their cover, make them visible and desperate and alone. Let the road itself do the killing.

She was good. He granted her that the way you grant a storm its strength.

Behind him, Sela slept the hard sleep of the exhausted, one hand curled near her bag of copied pages, and Petra sat with his back against rock and his eyes on the road and understood, with the clarity that only comes at the bottom of things, that the fragile thing he'd started building these three weeks had not broken in the night. It had been tested, and it had held, and holding was worse in a way, because now there was more to lose.

Down in the valley, small with distance, a rider in a navy-gray coat came up the salt road at an unhurried trot, paused at the turning to Drem's Hollow, and raised something to his mouth—a whistle, a horn, Petra couldn't tell—and far up the road, another rider broke from the trees to meet him.

They weren't chasing anymore, Petra realized. They were passing word forward. The net was moving north ahead of them, into the hill country, into the places where the road narrowed and the villages grew poor and a purse full of coin could buy anything.

He woke Sela with a hand on her shoulder, and she came up out of sleep instantly, already reaching for the pages.

"Roads are done," he said. "From here we walk the high ground, and we don't buy, beg, or speak to a living soul."

"And the monastery?" she said. "Saint Ovelun's? The abbot's the only man left alive who can verify what I'm reading. You know we need him."

Petra looked north, at the white teeth of the range, and thought about refuge, and what it had cost them the last time anyone had offered it.

"Then we pray," he said, "that monks keep secrets better than stable boys do."
