## 11. The First Snowline

The foothills changed the way a river changes—gradually, then all at once. One morning Petra woke with frost on his eyelashes and realized the grass had been whispering under them all night, dry stalks gone brittle, the green of three weeks ago surrendered without a fight.

He stood at the edge of their shelter—a hollow beneath an overhang that some herder had lined with sheep dung and straw generations ago—and looked east.

The Ashen Range filled half the sky.

He had been walking toward it for so long that he had stopped seeing it, the way you stop hearing water. But this morning it had come forward. The peaks stood sharp against a hard blue dawn, and on their shoulders, catching the first light like a wound catching fire, was snow.

Not the gray scree and brown stone they'd been climbing toward for days. White. Clean, indifferent white, laid down in a single night's work, as if someone had decided the mountains were finished being climbed this year and had closed them early.

Petra counted ridgelines out of habit. Four passes visible from here, maybe five—the northern one, the one the salt traders' maps called the Pass of Hollow Bells, sat lowest, a pale notch between two dark shoulders. Even from this distance he could see the notch wasn't pale from rock.

It was pale from snow.

"Sela." He said it quietly, the way you speak near a sleeping animal.

She was already awake. She'd been awake most nights lately, deciphering by the last coals, her lips moving over Kaldavian syllables like a woman praying in a language God had forgotten. She crawled out beside him, wrapped in the wool blanket they'd bought off a dead man's cart two valleys back, and followed his gaze up.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"That's wrong," she said finally. "That's wrong, isn't it? It's too early."

"It's early."

"How early?"

Petra thought about the almanac page he'd memorized years ago and never needed until now. "Three weeks. Maybe four. That snowline should be a thousand meters higher."

Sela pulled the blanket tighter. Her breath came out in small ghosts. "Then we have more time than I feared."

"We have less." He turned to look at her. In the flat morning light her face was all angles and exhaustion, cheekbones sharpened by weeks of bad food, eyes red-rimmed from cipher work. She'd lost weight he couldn't afford for her to lose. "Snow doesn't fall evenly, Sela. It falls where it wants. A storm tonight could close the pass for the season. A warm week could buy us a month. We don't get to know which. That's what makes it worse than Marrow."

"Marrow we can hide from."

"Time you can't. Time doesn't check faces at checkpoints."

* * *

They came down out of the overhang and onto a track that widened as it climbed, worn smooth by centuries of hooves. This was drover country now—stone walls stitched across hillsides, cairns marking paths that only made sense to people born to them. Twice they passed empty pens, the flocks already moved down or moved up; Petra didn't know which, and not knowing felt like another small door closing.

By midday the wind had teeth. It came down off the range in long gusts that smelled of iron and cold stone, and it pushed at them sideways on the exposed stretches so that walking became a negotiation. Sela stumbled twice. The second time Petra caught her arm and held it longer than he needed to, and she let him, and neither of them mentioned it.

They ate hard bread and harder cheese in the lee of a wall, hunched like conspirators, and Petra found himself doing arithmetic he hated.

The pass was four days' walk if the weather held. Five if Sela's boots gave out—they were held together with wire and stubbornness. Six if they had to detour around anything, and there was always something. Behind them, somewhere down in the river towns, Marrow was climbing. He'd stopped believing she'd given up around the time the reward notices started appearing in villages too small to have noticed them before. Someone was feeding her their line of travel. He no longer suspected Sela. He suspected everyone else.

Four days ahead of a woman who knew exactly where they were going, racing a snowline that had already moved a thousand meters down the mountain to meet them.

"Petra." Sela was looking past him, down the slope. "Someone's coming."

A single figure, small with distance, moving along the track below with the unhurried gait of a man who owned every stone on it. A crook over one shoulder. A dog flowing ahead of him like spilled ink.

Petra's hand went to the knife at his belt out of pure reflex, then relaxed by degrees. No one sent a lone shepherd to do a hunter's work. But lone shepherds saw everything, and remembered it, and sold it cheap when soldiers asked politely.

"Let me talk," Sela said.

"Why you?"

"Because you look like a man deciding whether to run, and he'll read that in ten seconds. I look like a tired woman, which is true, and tired women don't frighten shepherds."

The dog reached them first—a shaggy black thing that circled them once, thoroughly unimpressed, and sat down at a respectful distance with the air of a customs official. The shepherd arrived a minute later: old, wind-burned, eyes the color of the sky going gray behind him. He looked at them the way he'd probably look at two strayed ewes—assessing condition, deciding whether they were worth his trouble.

"Bound up?" he asked. His accent was high-country, vowels flattened by altitude.

"The pass," Sela said. "Hollow Bells. Is it open?"

The shepherd looked at her, then at Petra, then up at the mountain, and something in his face did a slow, complicated thing—not quite pity, not quite amusement. The look of a man being asked whether winter is real.

"You see that white?" He pointed with his chin. "That wasn't there yesterday. My father herded these slopes forty years. His father before him. There's a saying up here—" he shifted the crook to his other shoulder, settling in, a man preparing to enjoy being the bearer of bad news— "*When the snow sits on the Shepherd's Tooth before the geese go south, the bells will ring early.*"

"I don't know what that means," Sela said.

"It means the geese went south eight days ago." He nodded at the peak to the north of the pass, a jagged spire like a broken molar. "And the snow's sitting on the Tooth. And the Hollow Bells pass will be shut inside the month. Shut proper, mind—not closed like a door you can knock on. Closed like a grave. Wind packs it, cornices build, and nothing comes over till thaw. Nothing. Not armies, not saints, not fools." He looked at Petra directly for the first time. "Which are you?"

"Travelers," Petra said.

"Travelers." The shepherd tasted the word and found it undercooked. "There were soldiers in Vetch asking after travelers. Two of them, walking east. A man who walks like a diver and a woman who reads." He shrugged elaborately. "I don't read. I don't ask. But I count my sheep, and I count strangers, and I'm telling you what I told them, which is nothing, because they weren't polite and you haven't been rude yet."

Petra kept his voice level. "How many soldiers?"

"Two. Riding. Well-mounted, well-fed, riding like people paid by the day rather than the mile." He smiled with half his mouth. "They're a day behind you. Maybe less, if they push. They won't push. Paid men don't climb in weather."

Behind him. A day. Petra felt the familiar cold settle into his chest, the old companion, and was surprised to find that this time it came mixed with something almost like clarity. For weeks the pursuit had been a fog—shapes, rumors, notices on walls. Now it had a speed. A direction. A schedule. So did the snow. Two clocks, running at him from opposite sides, and he stood precisely where they would meet.

"If we make the pass in four days," Sela said carefully, "we cross ahead of both."

"If," the shepherd agreed pleasantly. "Weather says maybe. Soldiers say maybe. Mountain says what it always says." He whistled, and the dog rose. "There's a hut above the tree line, half a day short of the pass. Old toll shelter. Dry, if the roof's held. You want my advice—"

"We don't," Petra said.

"—you'll take the eastern switchbacks, not the valley road. Valley road is faster but it funnels. Switchbacks are slower but a rider can't follow them in wind." He turned to go, then paused. "One more thing, since you haven't been rude. The monks at Ovelun keep a beacon lit when the pass is safe. When it's shut, they ring the hollow bell—you can hear it clear down to the valley, they say. Carried for the dead, my grandmother called it. Ringing for travelers who didn't listen." He clicked his tongue and walked on, the dog flowing after, and neither of them watched him go because both were looking up at the pass.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the mountain climbed faster.

That was the trick of it, Petra thought—the way the range seemed to grow as you approached, each ridge revealing another behind it, higher, whiter, closer to the sun and colder for it. By late afternoon the air had thinned enough that Sela paused every hundred steps, hands on knees, breathing like a woman surfacing. He matched his pace to hers without comment. Speed meant nothing if she collapsed on a switchback with a day between them and shelter.

At dusk they crested a shoulder and the whole eastern world opened beneath them: valley after valley falling away into blue haze, and beyond, impossibly far, the lowlands where rivers ran and nations drew lines on maps. Somewhere down there was Halden's Reach, and the Verren, and the barge rotting on the bottom with its secret finally gone. Somewhere down there Marrow was warming herself at a fire, patient as tide.

Above them, the pass. Close enough now to see detail—the notch in the ridge, the black teeth of rock, the snow lying in the shadows like something poured there. And above the pass, the summit crags, white to their throats.

Sela sank onto a boulder. "I need to say something and I need you not to argue."

"That's never worked before."

"Then don't argue quietly." She pulled the chest's key cord from inside her shirt—it hung there now, next to her own little archive of notes, the two of them tangled together like roots. "If the pass closes with us on the wrong side of it, everything we've carried dies with us. Nobody publishes a document frozen in a snowdrift. If it looks like we won't make it—if the weather turns, if Marrow gets close—we burn it."

Petra looked at her.

"I mean it," she said. "I've spent twenty years believing paper is sacred. It isn't. Paper is just truth waiting for a reader. If there's no reader, it's ash with better posture. Better we choose the fire than let the mountain choose it for us."

"No."

"Petra—"

"No." He heard how flat his own voice came out, and made himself explain, because she deserved more than flatness. "You think I've been carrying this chest because it's evidence. I've been carrying it because it's the first heavy thing I've lifted in ten years that was worth the lifting. Everything else I've carried has been ballast—tools, coin, lies about my name. This is cargo. If we burn it, we go back to being what we were before the river. I was nobody with a good work ethic. You were a woman who used to be an archivist. I'm not doing that again. I'd rather freeze at the top carrying it than live warm having dropped it."

Sela stared at him for a long moment. The wind pulled at her hair, at the blanket, at the edge of everything.

"That's the most words you've spent on yourself since Krast," she said.

"Don't get used to it."

Too late. Something had loosened in her face—not a smile, nothing so generous, but the look of a person who has finally confirmed a suspicion she'd been living on. He turned away from it and studied the pass instead, because the pass was simpler: it either opened or it didn't, and it would tell him honestly, which was more than he could say for anyone, including himself.

Below them, faint but unmistakable, a thread of smoke rose from the valley road. A campfire. One fire, disciplined, small—the fire of people who didn't waste wood.

Marrow. A day behind, maybe less, burning exactly as much fuel as she needed and no more.

"They'll camp there tonight," Sela said, following his gaze. "We have the night and half a day."

"Then we spend both of them climbing."

"In the dark?"

"In the dark." He was already repacking the chest straps, checking buckles by feel. "The moon's up by midnight. Cold walking, but cold keeps riders in their beds, and it keeps avalanches asleep too. We move when the moon does."

Sela looked at him, then up at the pass, then at the smoke thread dying in the valley. When she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and certain, the voice she used for readings that mattered.

"Do you hear that?"

He listened. Wind, his own blood, the creak of the chest straps. Then, underneath—so faint he might have invented it—a sound rolling down from the high country. A single bronze note, deep and cracked at the edges, hanging in the thin air like a rope thrown down a well.

From the monastery, somewhere above and behind the ridge. The hollow bell.

Ringing for the dead, the shepherd's grandmother had called it. Ringing for travelers who didn't listen.

Or ringing, Petra thought, tightening the last buckle, because somewhere on this mountain, a monk had seen two figures climbing into the dark—and decided, for reasons of his own, to mark their passing while there was still time to mark it.
