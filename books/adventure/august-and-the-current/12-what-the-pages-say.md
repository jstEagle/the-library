## 12. What the Pages Say

The smelter had been Sela's idea in the end, though Petra would never admit it. The old ironworks above the Vetch road had a roof that still held, a chimney that drew the smoke up and scattered it thin enough to pass for weather, and—most important—a single door, visible from three directions. She had learned to think like an archivist even in flight: you chose rooms the way you chose vaults, for what they kept out.

Now she sat close enough to the fire to feel it through her coat, and the last ciphered page lay flat on a board across her knees.

She had been working on it for six days.

Six days of graphite rubbings and candle smoke, of holding pages at angles to the light the way she'd been taught in the archive before the archive burned—*tilt until the watermark confesses*—of copying letter-groups into a notebook she kept sewn inside her shirt, against her skin, where sweat and fear would have to work together to take it from her. Six days of Petra watching her over the fire with the particular patience of a man who has decided not to ask questions he cannot afford answered.

Tonight there were no more groups left to copy. Tonight the page was only words.

She read it once silently. Then, because some things do not survive being read alone, she read it again aloud, quietly, in the trade-tongue they shared, translating as she went, her voice steadier than her hands.

"'Memorandum of settlement,'" she said. "'Concluded between the Provisional Council of the Republic and the naval envoys of the Corvath Compact. Regarding the disposition of the Kaldavian fleet.'"

Petra stopped feeding sticks to the fire.

"Go on."

"'The Council affirms that no fleet will sail under Kaldavian colors, now or hereafter. The hulls at Verren Anchorage, together with their armament, charts, and crews' registers, are ceded to the Compact in consideration of—"'" Sela paused. Her mouth had gone dry somewhere around *crews' registers*, because she knew what a register was, what it meant when a state handed one over. "In consideration of certain sums deposited, and of the Compact's undertaking to refrain from interference during the period of transition.'"

"Transition," Petra said.

"That's the word they used." She turned the page toward him, though he couldn't read the cipher and they both knew it. It wasn't information she was showing him. It was the object itself—the paper, the age of it, the official hand that had written without tremor. "Transition. That's what they called it. Eleven thousand people died in the transition, Petra. I catalogued the lists. I was twenty-three years old and I spent four years cataloguing the lists, and the whole time the men who sold the fleet were signing memoranda about it in a neat secretarial hand."

He didn't say anything. He had a way of not saying anything that was almost a kind of shelter, and she hated how much she needed it tonight.

She read on. The sums were itemized. The deposits were traced through two banks whose names meant nothing to Petra and everything to her—one of them still operated out of the capital under its original charter. The final clause was a mutual denial: neither party would acknowledge the existence of the agreement, upon pain of—

"There's no penalty listed," she said. "You notice that? Every other clause has a penalty. This one doesn't. Because the penalty was understood. The penalty was Kaldavia itself. If anyone ever spoke, the whole arrangement came apart, so nobody spoke, and the ones who did speak—" She thought of the list from Krast, the page she'd deciphered on the toll bridge with Marrow's agents photographing travelers forty feet away. The officials and their fates. Drowned. Drowned. Died in custody. Died in custody. Vanished. "We know what happened to the ones who spoke."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind moved down the valley and set the loose sheets of the smelter's roofing ticking against each other, soft and rhythmic, like fingers drumming on a table.

"Names," Petra said. "How many signatories?"

"Seven for the Council. Four for the Compact." She ran her finger down the column. "I have seven names I can't verify and four I can't pronounce. But this one—" She tapped the fifth line. "This one I know. Everyone knows this one. Chancellor-Delegate Aurel Danek. He signed twice—here, and on the ratification page. He was Undersecretary of the Treasury then. He's—" She made herself say it plainly, the way you'd read a casualty figure. "He's First Minister of the Republic now. Twenty years in office. They named a bridge after him in Halden's Reach. We walked under it."

Petra looked at the fire for a long time.

When he finally spoke, his voice had the flatness she'd learned to distrust, the diver's flatness, the voice he used when the water was deepest. "Say what the document is. Plainly. Like you're testifying."

She almost laughed. *Testifying.* As if there were any court left that would hear it. But she did what he asked, because he was right—that was the discipline, that was the whole discipline of the archive, reduce the thing to its bones so the bones could be carried.

"The founding council of the Republic sold the Kaldavian fleet—its ships, its guns, and the registers naming every officer and crewman aboard—to the nation that then helped them destroy Kaldavia. The sale was ratified by seven councilors. One of those councilors is currently the head of the republic's government. The document is signed, sealed, dated, and written in the council's own cipher, which I can read, and which I can teach another reader to verify." She closed her notebook on her knee. "That's what it is."

"And what happens if it's read?"

Sela looked at him. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, a face built for not being remembered, and she realized she had stopped trying to remember it anyway—which frightened her more than anything on the page, because forgetting was the first skill of the erased and she had sworn she would never learn it.

"If it's read," she said, "the government falls. Or we fall. Those are the outcomes. There isn't a third one where we hand it to someone reasonable and they handle it quietly. Men like Danek don't get handled quietly. They get handled the way Kaldavia got handled." She fed the notebook back inside her shirt. "And there's no third outcome where we just keep walking, either. You understand that too, don't you? We've read it. Knowing it is already a crime. Marrow isn't chasing a chest anymore. She's chasing witnesses."

Petra didn't sleep, and neither did she, and sometime past midnight they ended up sitting on opposite sides of the dead fire with the chest open between them like a patient on a table.

It was Petra who broke the silence, which surprised her. He talked the way he dove, she was learning—rarely, but all the way down.

"When my brother died," he said, "they told my mother it was an accident at the shipyard. A crane failure. There was no crane failure. There was a list, and his name was on it, and I've spent ten years assuming the list came from somewhere official, because that's how lists work—they come from offices. But I never let myself wonder who wrote it." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if checking them for rope burns. "Now I'm looking at the office."

"You think Tomas was fleet?"

"I think everyone from the anchorage towns was fleet, or family to fleet. The registers went over with the ships, Sela. Names, berths, next of kin. Whoever bought that paper bought eleven thousand addresses." He said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "Danek may never have known my brother's name. He just sold the book it was written in."

Sela hugged her knees. The grief coming off him was old and cold, permafrost grief, and she recognized it because hers was the same temperature. "Do you want it to be true?" she asked quietly. "The document. Do you want it?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything. "No."

"No?"

"No. If it's true, then it wasn't chaos. It wasn't madness, or bad luck, or history doing what history does. It was arithmetic. Somebody sat in a warm room and made the numbers work, and my brother was a rounding error." He picked up a cold stick and snapped it. "If it's false, I can go back to believing the world is just cruel instead of organized. Cruel I know how to survive."

"But it's not false."

"It's not false."

They sat with that. Outside, the wind rose a register, and the roof ticked faster, and Sela found herself thinking about the archive—not the burning of it, which she had replayed so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, but the ordinary mornings before. The smell of sizing paste. The sound of other readers turning pages in the great hall, dozens of hands moving through the past at once, the whole building breathing paper. She had believed, in those mornings, that preservation was a neutral act. That keeping was enough. That if you held the truth safely, someone would eventually come for it.

Nobody had come. She had waited nine years and nobody had come, because the truth in the archive had been safe, and safe meant buried, and buried meant exactly where the men who wrote this memorandum wanted it.

"Sela." Petra's voice pulled her back. "The press. Beyond the mountains. The one the abbot's map points to. What do you actually know about it?"

"Rumor. Archive rumor, which is better than tavern rumor but worse than fact. After the purges, some printers went east over the Ashen Range—Kaldavian church presses, mostly, the ones the monks had run for centuries. The free territories let them keep operating. They print in Kaldavian. They print the lists. They print the names of the dead on the anniversary, every year, and the republic pretends it's folklore." She smiled without any humor in it. "I used to write to them. Before the archive burned. Care-of addresses, dead drops, the whole liturgy. They printed one letter of mine. A correction. Someone had gotten a date wrong in a memorial notice, and I sent the verified date with sources, and they printed it, and it was the proudest day of my life." She heard how it sounded and didn't apologize for it. "A correction. That was the biggest thing I ever put into the world. One corrected date."

"And now you're carrying the whole book."

"Now I'm carrying the whole book." She looked at the chest—at the plain wood, the salt-stained iron bands, the crest of a country that existed nowhere except in places like this, hidden rooms and stubborn memories and a printing press beyond a mountain pass that would close within weeks. "And I'll tell you what frightens me, Petra. It's not Marrow. It's not Danek's people. It's that once this is printed, it stops being ours. It becomes everyone's. And everyone includes the ones who'll use it badly—who'll want revenge instead of record, who'll burn things. Truth doesn't come with instructions. I've seen what people do with less."

"So we don't print it."

"We print it." She said it fast, before the fear could finish forming, the way you jump into cold water. "God help me, we print it. I didn't spend nine years keeping ashes to negotiate with myself at the end."

Dawn came gray through the broken clerestory windows, and with it came the thing Sela had been dreading since Krast, arriving not as a sound or a sight but as a certainty in her gut, the way archivists develop instincts for which boxes have been opened before.

Petra saw it too. He was on his feet before she spoke, looking down the slope toward the Vetch road, where the morning mist hung in long unbroken banks—and where, threaded through the mist, a thread of woodsmoke rose that did not belong to them.

"Somebody's on the road," he said. "Below us. Maybe a kilometer. Travelers make small fires. That's a camp fire."

"Caravan?"

"Caravans move. That's not moving." He was already gathering the chest straps, his hands quick and sure, and Sela watched him transform into the man from the river—the methodical stranger, the man with no name—and felt something twist in her, because six days ago she had started thinking of him as *we*, and now the mist was full of smoke and *we* might already be over.

"They followed the caravan hand," she said. "The one at the waystation. Someone sold us."

"Someone always sells us." He cinched the last strap and looked at her, and for a moment the flatness cracked and she saw underneath it: not fear, exactly. Inventory. A man counting what he had left. "North or south?"

"North is the foothills. North is snowline." She swallowed. "North is the pass."

"Then we go north." He shouldered the chest and stood a moment longer, listening to the smoke below, and then he said the thing that settled it, the thing she would carry with her over the mountains along with everything else:

"They can burn every copy but the one in your head, Sela. So whatever happens down there—you keep reading."

They slipped out the north door into the mist, and behind them, faint and getting fainter, the campfire smoke climbed straight up into a sky going white at the peaks—the first white of the season, weeks too early, falling on the Ashen Range like a deadline.
