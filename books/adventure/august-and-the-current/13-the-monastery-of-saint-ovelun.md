## 13. The Monastery of Saint Ovelun

Petra saw the monastery before he understood what it was—a gray shape stitched into the mountainside above the tree line, as if the rock had grown a roof. Smoke rose from a single vent. A bell tower leaned against the wind like a man braced in a gale.

"We can't," Sela said.

"We're dead if we don't." He said it without drama, the way he'd say *the air's thin here* or *the current will take you*. Facts. He had learned to make even desperation sound like procedure. "We have no food, no money, and your hands haven't stopped shaking since the pass. That's not a debate."

"They'll send word down-mountain."

"Probably."

"And you're willing to risk that?"

He looked at her. Her lips were cracked, her eyes rimmed red from three days of wind, and she was still—still—arguing on behalf of principle while her body failed around her. He found it infuriating and, in some corner of himself he didn't inspect closely, admirable.

"Sela. I've been risking everything since I pulled that chest out of the Verren. One more door is just a door."

The path to the gate was switchbacked and iced over. Twice Petra went down on his bad arm—the one Marrow hadn't broken but had made a liar of—and twice Sela hauled him up without comment. By the time they reached the iron-studded door, both of them were breathing like divers surfacing too fast.

Sela knocked. Then, when nothing came, she knocked again, and this time she knocked in a pattern—three, pause, two—that meant something to someone, because the door opened before the echo died.

A monk stood in the gap. Old, wide-shouldered, with a face weathered down to its essentials. His eyes went from Sela's face to the chest strapped to Petra's back, and something moved behind them—not surprise. Recognition.

"You carry Kaldavia's seal," the monk said.

It wasn't a question. The crest was stamped into the chest's lid, and the lid was scarred but legible: the double wave beneath the seven-pointed star. Petra had spent weeks trying not to look at it. This man looked at it the way other men looked at the faces of their dead.

"We do," Sela said.

"Then you are either thieves or pilgrims," the monk said, "and thieves don't climb to four thousand meters in the first snows. Come in before God changes his mind about you."

* * *

Inside, the monastery was warm in the way stone buildings are warm—slowly, grudgingly, holding heat the way a wreck holds silt. They were given a cell with two pallets and a brazier, bread and goat cheese and tea that tasted of pine, and left alone for an hour that Petra spent mostly asleep with his boots on.

He woke to voices. Low ones, through the wall. Sela was gone.

He found them in the scriptorium—a long room of desks and lamplight where forty monks had once copied liturgies and now perhaps five did. Sela sat at a table across from the old monk who had opened the gate, and between them lay pages from the chest, spread out like a patient opened for surgery.

She looked up when Petra entered, and her expression stopped him in the doorway.

"He knows the cipher," she said. "Not our cipher—the older one. The pre-purge ecclesiastical hand. It took me two days to work out what I could. Brother Anselm read the first page in a minute."

"I served the Chancery of Saint Ovelun in Vell," the monk said. "Before." The word carried twenty years of weight. "I sealed documents with that star myself. Sit, diver. You look like a man deciding whether to run."

"I'm always deciding whether to run," Petra said, and sat.

Anselm turned a page toward him with fingers that trembled slightly—not from age, Petra thought, but from something closer to reverence. "Your archivist has told me what these are. Do you understand what you carry?"

"I understand what it costs."

"That is not the same thing." The monk folded his hands. "These pages name the men who sold Kaldavia's fleet to the Verren League. They name the councilmen who signed the order that let the League's army cross the border unopposed. And they name—" he paused, choosing, "—a man who still sits in the republic's Hall of Ministers. Your chest is not salvage, diver. It is a verdict waiting for a courtroom."

"Or a grave," Petra said. "I've seen how the republic handles verdicts."

"Yes. So have I." Anselm's eyes were steady. "Which is why I am going to tell you something, and then you are going to be angry, and then you are going to listen anyway."

Petra felt it before the monk said it—the cold sliding under the door of the conversation.

"There is a village at the foot of the mountain called Drem. Once a month, a mule train brings us flour, oil, and news. Three days ago, the train's master told Brother Colm that soldiers had come through asking after travelers out of the river country. A woman leading them. Navy."

Marrow. Of course. She'd climbed into the range after them, or sent others ahead. Petra's hand went unconsciously to his splinted forearm.

"The pass below us closes within the month," Anselm went on. "When it does, no soldier climbs, and no traveler descends until spring. You are safe here only so long as the mountain agrees. But safety bought with silence is not sanctuary—it is just a slower arrest. So I have done what abbots do. I have written to the bishop at Vell, requesting guidance regarding pilgrims bearing relics of the fallen nation."

"You've reported us," Sela said quietly.

"I have asked my church what it remembers," Anselm said. "There is a difference. Though I grant you—" and here something old and tired crossed his face, "—the difference has narrowed considerably in twenty years."

Petra stood. The chair scraped. "How long until an answer comes back up the mountain?"

"With the next mule train. Nine days. Perhaps fewer, if the weather holds—which it won't."

Nine days. Petra did the arithmetic he hated doing: nine days of warmth and food, or walking now, half-starved, into a range already wearing its winter whites. He looked at Sela. She was watching him, and he realized she was letting him decide. When had that happened? When had the archivist who followed him against his will started deferring to him?

"We stay," he said. "But the chest doesn't sleep in your archive, Father. It sleeps in our cell, and one of us is always awake."

Anselm inclined his head. "A wise thief."

"A careful diver," Petra said. "Thieves want things. I just want to put this down."

* * *

Ilse Marrow read the abbot's letter by lamplight in the customs post at Drem, and laughed—one short, humorless syllable that made the young lieutenant across the desk flinch.

*"Regarding pilgrims bearing relics of the fallen nation."* The old man might as well have written *they are here* in letters a meter high. Twenty years of the church learning to speak in code, and this was the best it could manage.

"Sir?" the lieutenant said.

"Bishop's correspondence," she said, folding the letter. "Nothing that concerns the garrison."

It concerned everything. She had come up-mountain against explicit orders—her superiors wanted the matter buried, not solved, and buried meant no reports, no teams, no trail. So she had come alone except for Lieutenant Osk, who believed he was escorting her on an inspection of border chapels, and three men from her old company who owed her debts they preferred never to discuss.

And she had come because of a signature.

She had the copy Sela's deciphering had left behind at Krast—or rather, the page the archivist had photographed before fleeing, which Marrow had taken from the waystation raid and read eleven times. Page fourteen. The fleet transfer order. Seven signatures at the bottom, each one a man who had sold a nation's navy and been paid in ministries and directorships.

The fourth signature belonged to Edvard Marrow. Her father. Deputy Minister of Ports, decorated servant of the republic, the man who had taught her to sail on the Verren and taught her, without ever saying it, that the purge had been necessary. That Kaldavia had been a wound the republic lanced to save itself.

She had spent her whole career believing him. Believing the navy's silence was discipline rather than guilt. And now his name sat on a page in a stolen chest, in ink twenty years dry, beside six other names, and every night she woke with the page floating behind her eyes like something seen through dark water.

She wanted it destroyed. That was the truth she would not say aloud, not even to herself in the dark hours. Burn the chest, bury the divers, go home, keep believing. It would be so easy. It was what her superiors wanted. It was what her father would expect.

And underneath that want, quieter, colder, was the other thing: she wanted to know if it was true. If her father had signed away a fleet and thirty thousand lives, she needed to see it with her own eyes, because a daughter's doubt was a kind of drowning, and Ilse Marrow had never once let water beat her.

"Captain?" Osk again. "The mule train to Saint Ovelun leaves tomorrow at first light. Should we—"

"No." She closed the letter drawer. "We climb ourselves. Small party. No uniforms above the snowline."

Osk hesitated. "The abbot's people will see us coming."

"Good," Marrow said. "Let the old man sweat. Men who send letters make mistakes, and mistakes talk."

She looked out the window at the mountain, white to the shoulder now, the monastery invisible somewhere in the cloud above. Somewhere up there, a diver and an archivist were sitting by a fire with a verdict in a box, and she was climbing toward them with her father's name in her mouth like a stone.

She wondered, not for the first time, which of them she intended to save.

* * *

On the third night, Petra couldn't sleep and went to the chapel.

It was a small space, older than the rest of the monastery, and it had not been redecorated when Kaldavia fell—or rather, it had been redecorated badly. Under whitewash on the eastern wall, faint shapes showed through: a fleet of ships painted sailing into a seven-pointed star. Someone had plastered over the nation and forgotten to paint deep enough.

He sat in the back with his bad arm cradled and listened to the wind work the bell tower. In ten years he had not prayed, partly because he'd stopped believing anyone was listening, mostly because he'd stopped believing he deserved it. You don't erase yourself from every record in the republic and then ask the record-keeper upstairs for favors.

Footsteps. Anselm came down the aisle and settled onto the bench beside him with the groan of old joints, uninvited and unhurried.

"You knew my brother," Petra said. He didn't know why he said it. Perhaps the wall did it—the ships under the whitewash, the dead nation's colors bleeding through. "Tomas Voss. He was a chancery clerk in Vell. He stayed when the League came. He thought being innocent would protect him."

Anselm was quiet a long moment. "I knew a Tomas Voss," he said finally. "Young. Careful handwriting. He argued with me once about whether archives serve the living or the dead." A pause. "He said the dead. I said the living. We drank terrible wine and neither of us conceded."

Petra's throat worked. "He was right, then."

"He was right that we failed him," Anselm said. "The whole church. We whitewashed the wall and called it peace." He nodded at the ghost-fleet on the plaster. "You know why I wrote to the bishop, diver? Not to report you. Because if there is one man left in the hierarchy who remembers what's under the paint, I need to know his name. And if there isn't—" his voice hardened, an old man's flint, "—then these pages leave this mountain with you, whatever it costs me, and I will stand in the doorway while they go."

Petra stared at him. "You'd defy your own bishop."

"I've defied worse men for less reason." Anselm rose, joints cracking like river ice. "Sleep, Tomas Voss's brother. Tomorrow I will show your archivist the rest of the cipher. And in nine days, we will learn together what kind of church we still have."

He climbed the aisle and was gone. Petra sat alone under the whitewashed ships, and for the first time since the Verren, the chest strapped to his back in the cell above felt less like a thing he was carrying and more like a thing carrying him.

Then, far off down the valley, a sound rolled up through the wind—too heavy for a mule bell, too rhythmic for rockfall.

Boots. Many of them, on the frozen road, climbing.

They had nine days. Someone had decided they shouldn't use them.
