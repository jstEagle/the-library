## 14. Marrow Ascending

The order came down from the Admiralty in a folder with no letterhead: *suspend the search. The Verren matter is closed.*

Ilse Marrow read it twice, then folded it into quarters and put it in her coat pocket, where she kept the things she intended to disobey.

She had been hunting Petra Voss for eleven days. She knew him now the way you come to know a man whose trail you've followed through mud and paperwork—his habits, his economy, his hatred of witnesses. He paid cash. He slept in rooms with two exits. He had erased himself so thoroughly that finding his real name had taken her three days and a favor owed by a customs clerk who would regret it. *Petra Voss* was a shell. Beneath it was a boy from the Kaldavian river towns, a brother of a drowned man, a diver who had spent ten years teaching himself not to exist.

And he was climbing into the Ashen Range with a chest full of paper that could unmake the republic.

Her superiors wanted it buried. That was the word they used, though they said it softly, the way men say things they'd deny under oath. *Buried.* Not recovered. Not secured. Buried, along with everyone carrying it.

Marrow had served the navy for nineteen years. She understood what burial meant. She had performed it herself, twice, in cases she did not let herself think about at night.

But there was a page in that chest with a signature on it, and the signature was her father's.

---

She told no one where she was going. Officially, Captain Ilse Marrow was on leave—her mother's health, a fiction her mother would have found hilarious. Unofficially, she took three men she trusted more than she trusted the institution that paid them, and even that trust she rationed like hardtack.

Drenner she'd worked with for nine years; he asked no questions because questions were how men got hurt. Osk was young, competent, and ambitious enough to be dangerous but honest enough to warn her about it. And Vasch, the tracker, who had once tracked a deserter across forty miles of peat bog and brought him back alive when everyone expected a corpse. Vasch read ground the way priests read scripture.

They went up out of Halden's Reach before dawn, four riders and two pack mules, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridgeline they were already above the last of the river towns, where the road turned to track and the track turned to stone.

"You want to tell me what we're actually doing?" Drenner asked, riding beside her. He kept his voice low. He always did, in the mountains, as if the rock were listening.

"We're recovering stolen government property."

"From divers."

"From thieves."

He grunted. "The Admiralty pulled us off. You noticed that."

"I noticed."

"And we're going anyway."

"I noticed that too."

Drenner looked at her for a long moment. Nineteen years in the service teaches you to hear the sentence underneath the sentence. "Whose property is it, Ilse? Really?"

She didn't answer, and he didn't push, which was why she'd brought him.

---

The truth was this: her father had been a junior naval attaché twenty-two years ago, a minor functionary in the fleet administration, the kind of man who stamped documents and went home to his family and never made decisions larger than what to plant in the garden. When Kaldavia fell—when its ships burned in their own harbors and its officials vanished into prisons that officially did not exist—Ilsa's father had been exactly the sort of man history used as a pen.

She had learned this the way she learned most things: patiently, sideways, over years. A name in an old promotion file. A letter her mother burned but not thoroughly enough. A dinner-party conversation between two old admirals who thought the children were asleep, about *the Verren arrangement*, spoken in the hushed tone men reserve for things that are profitable and shameful in equal measure.

She had spent two decades telling herself it was nothing. That her father—a decent, dull, kind man who taught her to swim in the harbor and cried at funerals—could not have put his name to anything monstrous. That whatever the old admirals whispered, it was the ordinary rot of any government, and hers was no worse than another's.

Then the navy sealed a river. Then orders came down to bury, not solve. Then she held a photograph of a salvaged chest bearing the crest of a nation that no longer existed, and every soft story she had built over twenty years collapsed at once, quietly, like wet cardboard.

Her father was dead now. Three years gone, a stroke in his garden, the trowel still in his hand. He could not answer for anything. But his name could, and his name was hers, and she had carried it into every room of her career like a lantern she'd never lit.

She needed to see the page. That was all. She told herself that was all. She needed to see the ink, the date, the shape of the letters—and then she would decide what a daughter owed to the dead, and whether it was more or less than what a captain owed to the flag.

---

They made the foothills by evening and camped in the lee of a granite shoulder, above the tree line's ragged edge. Vasch came back from scouting at dusk with snow in his hair, weeks too early for snow, and a look on his face Marrow had learned to respect.

"They went up the shepherd road," he said, crouching by the fire. "Two of them. The diver and the woman. They bought mules in Vetch and left them at the last waystation—paid a hand to hold them, I'd wager, which means someone sold them, which means half the valley knows they passed through." He fed a twig to the flames. "There's also this."

He handed her a scrap of oilcloth, folded around a button. Navy issue. She turned it in her fingers without expression.

"Ahead of us," she said.

"Two days ahead, maybe less. A team of six, mounted, moving fast. Somebody else didn't obey the order either—or somebody gave a second order, the quiet kind." Vasch glanced up. "Could be they're ours. Could be they're not."

Marrow thought about the folder with no letterhead. About the word *buried*. If the Admiralty wanted the chest sunk in some hole no map recorded, they would send men who did not exist, carrying orders that did not exist, and the diver and the archivist would simply stop appearing in the world, and no report would ever be filed.

"They're not ours," she said. "Ours would have waited for me."

Osk, feeding the mules, looked over sharply. "Captain, if there's another team—"

"Then we move faster." She stood, brushing frost from her coat. "First light. We climb through the night if we have to."

Drenner watched her from across the fire, saying nothing, doing the arithmetic she could see him doing: *a captain defying orders, racing a black team into the high passes, for a chest of dead papers.* Whatever conclusion he reached, he kept it behind his teeth. That was Drenner.

Marrow lay back against her pack and looked up at the range. Even in starlight she could see the new snow on the high shoulders, thin and wrong, a season arriving ahead of schedule as if the mountains themselves had been bribed. Somewhere up there, a man who had spent ten years refusing to have a name was hauling one toward the light. And somewhere above him, six men with unwritten orders were climbing to make sure it never arrived.

And her father's name was in the middle of it, in faded iron-gall ink, waiting for her the way all unfinished things wait.

---

They climbed through the next day and into the following night. The shepherd road gave way to switchbacks cut into the mountain's flank, and the switchbacks gave way to scree, and the mules went lame one after another until they cached the packs and carried what mattered on their backs: rope, cold rations, the short carbines wrapped in oilcloth, and Vasch's uncanny nose for a trail.

At midnight on the second day, Vasch stopped so abruptly that Osk walked into him.

"Fire," he breathed. "Old. Two days. And—" he crouched, touched the ash, rubbed it between finger and thumb, and held something up to the moonlight. A scrap of waxed paper, torn. Packing paper. Chest packing.

Marrow took it. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook, somewhere deep, where she kept the things she didn't look at.

"They camped here," she said. "They opened it here."

"Why open it on a mountainside?" Osk asked.

"Because they finally finished reading it," Drenner said quietly. "Or because they needed to know what they were dying for."

Marrow crouched at the cold fire ring and let herself imagine it: the diver and the archivist, huddled in the wind, prying open the lid while the republic's whole rotten founding spilled into the firelight. She imagined the archivist reading aloud. She imagined the moment the diver heard a name he recognized.

She wondered if her father's name had been among them. She wondered if a stranger had now read it aloud in the dark before she had.

Something in her chest turned over, cold and slow, like a body rolling in current.

"Captain," Vasch said. He was standing, facing upslope, utterly still. "Storm coming out of the north. Two days, maybe one. After that, these passes close until spring."

"Then we have one day," Marrow said.

"One day to catch them, and a black team somewhere between us and them, and weather that kills." Vasch smiled thinly. He was the only man she knew who smiled more the worse the odds got. "You picked a fine thing to disobey orders over, Captain."

"Yes," she said. "I did."

---

They climbed through what remained of the night, ropes on the ice-glazed sections, breath tearing white in the starlight. Marrow led where the route was worst—not because she was the best climber among them, but because she needed her body to be too tired to think. Thinking was the enemy now. Thinking led to her father's garden, the trowel in his hand, the letter her mother had burned, the question that had been growing in her for twenty years like frost in a wall:

*What do you do when the country you served is the crime?*

She had given her life to the navy because the navy was order, and order was the thing that stood between people and the dark. She had believed that right up until the folder with no letterhead. Now she was climbing a mountain in defiance of her own service, chasing a thief and an exile toward a border she had sworn to defend, and the terrible, liberating truth of it was that she had never felt more like an officer in her life.

Near dawn they came over a shoulder of rock and saw, far below and across a hanging valley, two figures on the shepherd road—small as punctuation marks, moving steadily east, a sled or sledge dragging between them.

"There," Osk whispered.

Marrow raised her glass. The diver—she knew his stride now, that deliberate, weight-saving walk of a man who treated his own body as equipment—and the archivist, bent under a harness line. Between them, on a improvised drag-sled, a shape wrapped in oilcloth.

The chest. Right there. Close enough to reach before nightfall if the weather held, and the weather was not going to hold.

Then Vasch, scanning the ridgeline opposite, went rigid.

"Smoke," he said. "Fresh. North ridge. Six men, maybe seven." He lowered his glass and looked at her, and for once there was no smile in him. "Captain—they're between us and the pass. And they've seen the smoke too, same as we've seen theirs. By tonight, everybody on this mountain knows where everybody is."

Marrow stood on the frozen shoulder of the Ashen Range with her father's name in her pocket and a storm gathering in the north, and made the only decision left that was truly hers.

"We don't wait for dark," she said. "We go now. Straight down the face. And God help whichever of those teams reaches the diver first—because if it isn't mine, there won't be enough left of him to bury."

She started down. Behind her, three men who had followed her out of the service and into the sky shouldered their packs and came after, and above them the first flakes of the storm began to fall, fine and dry and endless, like ash.
