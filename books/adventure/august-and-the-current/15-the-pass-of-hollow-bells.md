## 15. The Pass of Hollow Bells

They left the monastery at four in the morning, before the abbot could change his mind about them or they could change theirs about the mountain.

Petra had counted the bells the night before. Saint Ovelun's tower held nine of them, and every one of them was cracked. When the monks rang for compline, the sound came out wrong—hollow, like breath through a broken tooth. He'd asked a young brother about it while Sela copied her notes by candlelight.

"The foundry that cast them burned," the brother said. "Kaldavian bronze. We ring them anyway. A bell that sounds wrong still tells you what hour it is."

Petra hadn't slept much after that. He kept thinking about bells that told the truth badly but told it anyway.

Now the truth was a gray track climbing out of the monastery's shadow, switchbacking up through black pine toward the notch in the ridge the abbot called Hollow Bell Pass. The sky above it was the color of dishwater, and behind the notch, where the Ashen Range should have been visible, there was only cloud stacked like masonry.

"The shepherd said three weeks," Sela said, tightening the strap on her pack. She'd cut her hair short at the monastery with kitchen shears and looked younger and harder for it. "The snow said otherwise last night."

"I saw." Frost on the tent canvas at dawn, a finger's width of it. Weeks early. The mountain was closing its mouth ahead of schedule, and Marrow was somewhere below them with a team and a grudge and, if the abbot's messenger had done his work, a map to their route.

"So we go now."

"We go now."

She looked at him a moment longer than the sentence required. Ten days ago he would have read suspicion in that look. Now he wasn't sure what he read. Something had changed between them since the smelter, since the fire and the story about his brother, and Petra didn't have a word for it because he'd spent ten years refusing to need words like that.

"Your arm," she said.

"It works."

"It clicks."

"It clicks *and* it works. Move."

* * *

The first day was kind to them, which Petra distrusted immediately.

The track was old—older than the republic, maybe older than Kaldavia—cut into the mountainside by hands that had wanted trade to flow this way. In places you could still see the chisel marks, neat as ledger lines. They passed a shrine at midday, a stone niche holding a figure worn faceless by weather, its lap full of pebbles left by travelers making small bargains with whatever god had been forgotten here. Sela added a pebble without breaking stride. Petra didn't. He'd stopped making bargains a long time ago; he'd never believed anyone was listening, and lately he suspected that was the one prayer he'd gotten answered.

By afternoon they were above the treeline. The pines gave out all at once, the way trees do, surrendering not gradually but in a single ragged line, and beyond it the world was rock and lichen and wind. The chest rode in Sela's pack now, redistributed after the smelter—heavier things in her load since his arm. He hated that. He hated more that she never once made him say thank you.

They camped in a hollow behind a spur of granite, and the stars came out so hard and close that the sky felt like a ceiling about to drop. Sela slept. Petra lay awake doing the arithmetic he always did: distance gained, food remaining, the number of hours until light, the probability, expressed as a feeling rather than a figure, that Ilse Marrow was already on the mountain.

He decided she was. It was the safer assumption. Hope was a current, and currents took you where they wanted; he'd built a whole life on swimming against them.

* * *

The second day, the mountain stopped being kind.

It started as a smell—the strange mineral coldness that comes off snow before snow arrives—and then a wind out of the northwest that carried grit with it, stinging their faces raw. By noon the clouds behind the notch had come forward to meet them, low and bruised, and the temperature fell so fast that Sela's breath turned to frost on her scarf and stayed there.

"We're exposed," she shouted over the wind. They stood on a shoulder of scree with nothing between them and the sky. "If this turns—"

"It's going to turn."

"Then we need shelter. Now."

Petra scanned the slope. There—a seam in the rock two hundred meters up and to the right, a dark line too straight to be natural. Not a cave, probably. An overhang. Maybe an old mine adit; the range was full of them from the silver years, holes dug by men who'd died broke.

They climbed for it as the light went the color of dirty wool. Halfway there, the first flakes came down—not falling so much as flying sideways, hard little bullets of ice. By the time Petra pulled Sela under the overhang and discovered, feeling along the back wall in the dark, that it opened into an actual chamber deep enough to stand in, the world outside had been erased. White on white on white. The pass, the track, the way they'd come—all of it gone, as if the mountain had reached up and wiped the day off itself.

Sela got the small stove going in the lee of the entrance, cupping the flame with her body. They had fuel for two days. Food for three, carefully. The storm, judging by the pressure in Petra's bad arm, intended to stay longer than either.

"Hollow Bell Pass is another six hours," Sela said. "In good weather."

"In good weather," Petra agreed.

Neither of them said the rest of it. If the storm held two days, they might still make the pass. If it held three, they'd be crossing the summit ridge in fresh snow, breaking trail through drifts, burning calories they couldn't replace. And somewhere below them, moving slower but moving, would be a woman who did not need to outrun the weather because she only needed to follow their tracks into it.

Petra sat against the wall and listened to the wind try to get in. It sounded, faintly, like cracked bells.

* * *

The storm owned the night entire. It screamed down the valley in long pulses, and each pulse drove spindrift through the entrance in a white breath that hissed across the floor and died. The stove made a bubble of warmth no bigger than a blanket. They sat inside it, shoulder to shoulder, because the geometry of survival demanded it, and Petra was aware of Sela's weight against his side the way he was aware of the stove: something warm that could go out.

"You should sleep," she said.

"After you."

"That's not how we've been doing it."

"We've been doing a lot of things wrong."

She huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, quietly, in the voice she used for reading documents aloud: "Tell me about the bells."

"What bells?"

"The ones in your head. You've been hearing them since the monastery. I watch your face when the wind changes pitch."

Petra looked at the stove flame. Outside, the whiteness was total. There was nowhere to be seen going, no tracks to keep, no horizon to measure himself against. Just the cave, the fire, the woman, and the noise. He thought: I could tell her anything here and the mountain would keep it.

"My brother's name was Tomas," he said.

Sela went very still, the way people do when they understand that a door has opened that doesn't open twice.

"Not Voss. That name came later. Tomas Thornbury—we were river kids, both of us, Halden's Reach born, diving off the pilings before we could swim properly. Our father ran salvage gear, small stuff, honest. Tomas was better at it than me. Everyone said so. He could hold his breath four minutes and come up laughing." Petra turned his hands over, looked at the scars. "When the purges came east, they didn't come for us. That's the thing nobody understands. They came for names on lists, and we weren't on any list. But the harbor sealed, and the fleet contracts died, and the navy requisitioned everything that floated, and my father's business drowned without ever taking water. And then the men who'd done it—the council, the admirals, the ones whose signatures are in that chest—they put out proclamations about sacrifice and renewal, and boys like Tomas lined up to serve them, because there was no other work and because the proclamations were beautiful."

"He enlisted."

"He enlisted. Third Riverine Flotilla. They sent the flotilla up the Verren in the last year of the Kaldavia war—not the war they tell you about, the other one, the one the ledgers describe. The one where the fleet was already sold." His voice came out flat, which surprised him. He'd expected it to break. "Tomas drowned when his boat was scuttled. Not sunk. Scuttled. From inside. Because the men who sold the fleet needed the loss to look like enemy action, and a boat full of river boys was cheaper than the truth. Fourteen dead. The republic gave their families medals and a closed coffin apiece, and my mother opened hers, and what was in it wasn't my brother."

Sela's hand found his forearm in the dark. She didn't squeeze. She just rested it there, over the scarred wrist, over the arm that clicked.

"I spent two years trying to prove it," Petra said. "Wrote letters. Petitioned. Dug through records. Every door was a wall, and every wall had the same five signatures on it, and eventually I understood that the signatures weren't hiding anything. They didn't think they needed to. Who was going to stop them? A salvage diver?" He laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as the bells. "So I stopped trying to prove it and started trying to disappear. Changed the name. Burned the papers. Took jobs nobody legal would take, dove wrecks nobody legal would touch. I told myself it was strategy. Stay small, stay alive, wait for an opening." He shook his head slowly. "That was a lie, Sela. I've known it was a lie for years. I wasn't waiting for an opening. I was punishing myself. Every dive was a penance I never finished paying. Every year under a false name was a year Tomas didn't get. I erased myself because living felt like something I hadn't earned, and being nobody was the closest thing to being dead that still let me breathe."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere above them, snow was burying the pass, and he found he didn't care, and found that not caring was its own kind of terror.

"And then," Sela said softly.

"And then a chest came up out of the Verren with those same five signatures in it, and I understood that the river had been keeping my brother's killer's confession twenty meters under my feet the whole time." He finally looked at her. Her face was lit orange from below, eyes shadowed, absolutely attentive. "I didn't take the job for money. I've told myself the money story too. I took it because some part of me knew. Rivers give back what you sink in them. I just never thought it would give him back."

"Tomas," she said. Testing the name. Giving it back to the air where names belonged.

"Tomas." It tasted strange in his mouth, like a word in a language he'd stopped speaking. "Ten years I haven't said it out loud. You know what I'm afraid of? Not Marrow. Not the storm. I'm afraid that when this is over—if it's ever over—I won't know how to stop running. Punishment is easy. You just keep moving. But stopping—" He gestured at the white blindness outside. "Stopping means standing still in front of everything I did and didn't do, and letting it be seen. Letting *me* be seen. I don't know if I can do that."

"Yes, you can," Sela said.

"You don't—"

"I've watched you do it for a month." Her voice was steady, archival, the voice of someone citing sources. "You showed me your brother's name in a hole in a mountain during a blizzard. That's standing still. That's being seen. You already started; you just haven't noticed yet." She paused. "Besides. You'll need the practice. The press will want your testimony. Your real name, in print, forever. You know that."

He knew it. He'd known it since Krast, since the first deciphered page, since the moment the past grew teeth. Some part of him had been signing that document in his head for weeks, practicing the letters of a name he'd buried.

Outside, the storm's pitch changed—dropped, steadied, became something almost rhythmic. Wind through stone. Bronze through a crack. Bells telling the hour wrongly but telling it.

"There," Sela murmured. "Hear it? The mountain's ringing for you."

"Don't be mystical. You're an archivist."

"I'm a tired archivist in a cave with a man who's finally telling the truth. Mysticism is permitted." She leaned her head against his shoulder, and this time neither of them pretended it was about warmth. "Sleep now. We move when it breaks."

"If it breaks."

"When it breaks. And Petra—" She was already half asleep, words slurring at the edges. "Whatever's waiting on the other side of that pass. Whatever she does, whatever the weather does. Don't start running again. Promise the mountain. It keeps things."

Petra lay back against the cold stone, his brother's name sitting openly in his chest for the first time in ten years, and listened to the hollow bells, and did not promise, because he had learned on this mountain that promises made in storms are debts collected in clear weather.

He was still awake, near dawn, when he heard it beneath the wind: a sound that was not wind at all.

Voices. Far below, muffled by snow, rising up the pass.

More than one. Moving fast.
