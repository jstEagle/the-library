## 16. Caught Above the Clouds

The mountain told Ilse Marrow everything she needed to know, if she was willing to listen.

It had been telling her for three days. The cairns along the pilgrim trail were stacked wrong—rebuilt by someone in a hurry, stones balanced rather than fitted. The meltwater seep under the eastern scree had been churned by boots that came through before dawn, when the crust held and left clean prints. And at the shrine of Saint Ovelun's sister, where travelers traditionally left a coin for safe passage, two coins sat on the ledge, bright with recent frost. Nobody climbed this high anymore except smugglers, monks, and fools.

She was three of those things herself, depending on the hour.

Marrow crouched at the shrine and turned one of the coins with a gloved finger. Verren mint. Struck within the last five years. The divers had come up from the river country, then—not down from Halden's Reach as her first reports suggested. They'd doubled back east while she was wasting her time shaking informants loose in the port towns, before she abandoned official channels entirely and started climbing with four men she trusted more than the navy that paid them.

"Tracks fork at the col," said Vossen, her sergeant, coming up the switchback with his breath smoking. He was a river man by birth, thick through the shoulders, unhappy above the treeline in a way he didn't bother to hide. "One set goes north toward the pass proper. The other cuts west along the ridge."

"The western track is a feint," Marrow said. "They want anyone following them to think they're not attempting the pass yet." She stood, working feeling back into her fingers inside the gloves. The cold up here wasn't weather; it was a condition, like grief. You carried it. "But the western track is real too. They'll use it until the last possible junction, then cut north hard. It's what I'd do."

Vossen looked at her sideways. "You've been saying 'it's what I'd do' since the river, Captain. Starting to wonder if you're hunting them or auditing yourself."

Marrow didn't answer. Below them, the cloud deck lay over the lowlands like a second sea, white and featureless, and somewhere beneath it were the republic, the navy, the ministry, the whole soft machinery of lies she had spent twenty years oiling with her competence. Up here there was only rock and wind and the thin blue honesty of altitude. She found she could breathe it.

Her father had been a naval cartographer once. Before. He used to say the mountains were the only maps that couldn't be redrawn by committee.

She wondered if he'd thought of that line when he signed the page.

* * *

They caught sight of the divers at midday, from half a kilometer off—a dark pair of figures moving along the shoulder below the summit ridge, one leading, one lagging, and between them dragged on a improvised sledge of tent poles and rope: the chest. Even at that distance Marrow knew its silhouette the way you know a face from a funeral photograph. She'd read the salvage manifests. She'd interviewed the broker's widow without telling her why. She had spent eleven weeks learning everything about Petra Voss—or whatever his name was—and every thread ended at that box.

"He's slower than he should be," Vossen observed, glassing the ridge. "The big one. Favoring his left side."

"The arm," Marrow said. Her informant in the waystation town had described the raid—the caravan hand who sold them out, the night raid, the escape into the hills. Her men had fired wild in the dark on her explicit order. She'd wanted them flushed, not dead. Dead men couldn't carry chests, but dead men also couldn't tell her things, and Marrow had learned in the purges' aftermath that the republic preferred its problems silent. She had spent her career being the silence. She was tired of it in a way she hadn't yet found words for.

"Orders?" Vossen asked.

"We take them at the ridge saddle, where the ground funnels. Nobody shoots unless I shoot first." She lowered her own glass. "And nobody shoots unless I'm shooting at something that's shooting back. Are we clear?"

"You've given that speech twice a day for three days, Captain. We're clear." He hesitated. "The others are asking. Not me—the younger ones. They're asking what we do if they run for the border side. Technically our writ ends at the watershed."

"Technically," Marrow said, "our writ ended when my superiors ordered me to stop looking. Everything since has been insubordination wearing a captain's coat." She resettled her pack. "So we won't worry about technicalities. Move."

They moved. The ridge narrowed as they climbed, wind scouring the snow into long gray banners that streamed off the crest like smoke from a burning ship. Marrow counted her breathing, counted her steps, did all the small arithmetic of altitude that keeps a body honest. And underneath the arithmetic, the other thing ran on, the thing she had not said aloud to anyone, the thing that had put her on this mountain against orders:

*Adrian Marrow, Deputy Minister of Ports and Fleets.*

Sela Iven's deciphered pages had reached the ministry in copy form six weeks ago—one sheet, intercepted, passed hand to hand until it landed on the desk of a director who went pale and burned it and told Marrow the matter was closed. But Marrow had seen the sheet first. She had stood in the records room with it in her hands for four minutes, long enough to memorize the signature block, long enough to feel the paper's cheap wartime stock under her thumb, long enough to understand that her father—young then, a lieutenant commander with a beautiful future—had signed his name to the sale of a nation's fleet, and that the nation had been sold with it, and that twenty years later he was Deputy Minister of Ports and Fleets and she was the instrument that kept such pages from surfacing.

She hadn't known, when she took the assignment. That was the sentence she rehearsed on the climb, over and over, testing it for cracks. *I didn't know.* And it was true, and it changed nothing, because a person could spend twenty years guarding a door without knowing what was behind it, and the door did not care about her ignorance, and neither did the dead of Kaldavia.

Ahead, the figures stopped. One of them turned and looked back along their backtrail with the stillness of a man who has been hunted before and has opinions about it.

Marrow dropped behind a boulder. Too far to be seen clearly—but the gesture told her everything. He knew. He'd known for hours, maybe. Good. She preferred opponents who saw her coming. The ones who didn't see her coming tended to die badly, and she had filled enough graves.

* * *

They came up on the saddle an hour before dusk, when the light went the color of old brass and the shadows stretched blue across the glacier below. The divers had made the mistake she'd predicted: at the final junction they'd cut north, straight toward the pass, and the pass approach funneled through a saddle barely wide enough for two abreast, walled on the south by rock and open on the north to a slope of wind-loaded snow that fell away four hundred meters to the icefall.

Marrow stepped out onto the saddle alone.

It was a calculated exposure. Four rifles covered the funnel from the rocks above; her men were invisible unless she wanted them seen. She walked forward with her hands loose at her sides and her hood back, letting the wind take her gray-streaked hair, making herself legible. *I am one person. I am not an army. Look at me.*

The tall one—the diver—had the chest strapped across his back now, which slowed him and steadied him both. His broken arm was bound against his body in a sling of rope. Beside him stood a narrow woman in a patched archive coat, and Marrow felt a jolt of recognition that had nothing to do with dossiers: Sela Iven had signed out of the Burned Archive eleven years ago, and Marrow had processed the paperwork herself, in another life, in another uniform. The archivist had looked at her then with the same expression she wore now. Not fear. Inventory. As if Marrow were a document with a forged seal.

"That's far enough," the diver called. His voice was roughened by wind and altitude. "You're out of your jurisdiction, Captain. The watershed's behind you."

"So it is." She kept walking, ten paces, fifteen, until the distance was conversational. "I resigned my jurisdiction somewhere around the smelter, I think. Or the toll bridge. It's hard to keep track when you're committing crimes in order."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile. "Then what are you?"

"Curious," Marrow said. "Which is more dangerous than armed, most days." She stopped. Twenty meters of wind-scoured snow between them, and the chest riding his back like a hunched third figure. "Put it down, Voss. Whatever your name is. Put it down and walk south, and I give you my word you reach the lowlands alive."

"And the woman?"

"The woman is a former state archivist who absconded with classified material. Her fate isn't mine to promise." Marrow let that sit, honestly. Lies were tools, but a lie here would cost her the only currency she had left, which was the appearance of being exactly what she was. "I can try. That's all I can offer. Trying."

"You hunted us across half a republic," the diver said. "Killed Dremmer the smuggler's boy in Krast—" 

"I didn't kill him." The words came out harder than she intended. "My superiors did, through channels I refused to use, and then they sent men to do it anyway, and I have been climbing away from those men ever since. Ask yourself why I'm standing here with four soldiers and no arrest warrant. Ask yourself why I'm talking instead of shooting." She spread her hands. "I need to see what's in the chest. That's all. I've crossed a mountain range for the privilege of reading a document, which either makes me the most dedicated officer in the fleet or the maddest woman on this ridge, and I've stopped caring which."

The archivist spoke for the first time. Her voice was thin and clear and carried strangely well in the cold. "You can't see it, Captain. Reading it changes people. We've watched it happen."

"I'm counting on it," Marrow said.

Silence, except the wind. Above them the summit ridge stood black against a sky going violet at the edges, and below them the glacier caught the last brass light and threw it back dull as old tin. Marrow became aware, distantly, of how beautiful it was, and of how little beauty had ever once stayed her hand, and of how much she suddenly wanted it to.

The diver shifted his weight. "No," he said. "We're close to the pass. We're close to the press. I've given up everything else. I'm not giving up this."

"Then I take it," Marrow said quietly, "and I am sorry for it."

She raised her right hand—the signal—and her men rose from the rocks above the funnel with rifles leveled, and the moment broke like ice underfoot.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way bad moments do.

The diver bolted north. It was the only move available and it was the wrong one: the slope beyond the saddle was wind-loaded slab, the kind of snow that looks solid and is lying. Marrow shouted—*stop, the slope, stop*—and he didn't stop, because why would he believe her, and Sela Iven ran after him because she was braver than sensible, and Marrow ran after both of them because she had stopped giving orders she wouldn't follow herself.

Twenty steps onto the slope, the mountain answered.

It wasn't dramatic, at first. A sound like a door closing, deep underfoot. Then the slab released—a seam cracking across the slope above them with a crack like a rifle shot, and the whole white face of it shivered, and began to move.

"Avalanche!" Vossen's voice, from the rocks. "Captain, *move!*"

Marrow grabbed for the nearest of them—it was the archivist, close enough to touch—and hauled her south, downslope, toward the saddle, toward the rock anchor of the ridge wall. The diver had turned at the crack, chest and all, and for one suspended instant the four of them were arranged on the moving snow like figures in a painting: hunter, hunted, witness, weight.

Then the slide took the slope's skin off in a long white exhalation, and Marrow threw the archivist down behind a spur of rock and covered her with her own body as the avalanche roared past—a river of it, a freight train of it—missing the spur by meters, carrying snow and stone and silence down into the glacier's throat.

When it stopped, the ringing in Marrow's ears was total. The world came back in pieces: sky, breath, blood singing, the archivist coughing powder-white under her elbow. She rolled off, got her knees under her, looked north.

The slope where the diver had stood was gone. Scoured to bare blue ice. The chest—she scanned, heart slamming—there. Forty meters down-slope, wedged against a boulder the slide had exposed, half-buried, straps torn. And beside it—

He was alive. She could see him move, dragging himself upright with one arm, the broken one hanging wrong, snow crusted across his shoulders. Alive, alone on the ice, with the chest at his feet and four hundred meters of new avalanche debris between him and anything resembling safety.

"Captain!" Vossen, sliding down to the saddle. "Do we go get him?"

Marrow stood very still. This was the crossroads; she had known it would look like this eventually, though she'd imagined it would arrive with more ceremony. On one side: the order she could give, the easy one, the one her career had prepared her for. Leave him. Take the chest when the slope settles. Two witnesses fewer, one problem solved, the republic's secret kept, her father's name safe in the dark where it had lived for twenty years.

On the other side: a man on the ice with a ruined arm, and a page with a signature on it, and the truth, which she had chased up a mountain like a woman chasing her own shadow.

Below, the diver had reached the chest. He was trying to lift it. One-armed, half-frozen, he was trying to lift it, and failing, and refusing to stop.

Marrow unslung her rifle and laid it down in the snow, deliberately, where everyone could see.

"Captain?" Vossen again, uncertain.

"Rope," she said. "All of it. And somebody get me down to that ice before the light goes." She looked at Sela Iven, who was watching her with that inventory stare, and heard herself say, in a voice scraped raw by wind and decision:

"You said reading it changes people. Start talking, Archivist. Tell me about the signatures. Tell me about Adrian Marrow."

The archivist's eyes widened—and then, slowly, she nodded, and reached into her coat for the pages she had copied by firelight, page by page, all the way up the mountain.

Above them, unseen, the cornice at the summit ridge groaned under the day's last warmth, and began, very quietly, to lean.
