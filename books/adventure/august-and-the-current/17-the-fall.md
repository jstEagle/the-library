## 17. The Fall

The storm found them at the ridge.

It came the way Marrow had come—without announcement, without mercy, as if it had been tracking them for days and simply chose its moment. One breath the sky was a bruised grey ceiling; the next the wind arrived with a sound like a door slamming shut behind them, and the snow began to fall sideways, hard as gravel.

Petra pressed his shoulder against the rock face and pulled Sela in after him. The chest rode between them on the rope sling he'd rigged that morning, banging against his hip with every step. His broken arm was a white fire under the splint Sela had made from a tent pole and strips of her coat. Three days since the cornice. Three days of climbing one-handed while she hauled half his weight on the rope and said nothing about it, which was its own kind of kindness.

"Ridge is close," she shouted. "I can see the cairn."

He couldn't see anything. The world had narrowed to the width of his own breathing. But he nodded, because she needed him to nod, and they moved.

The cairn was real—a stack of stones taller than a man, furred with ice, marking the saddle between two peaks where the pass dipped before the long descent into Kaldavia's old territories. Beyond it, if the abbot's directions held, the slope fell away toward valleys no republic map bothered to draw. Beyond it was the press. Beyond it was an end to running.

He let himself believe that for maybe forty steps.

Then the wind dropped all at once—the way it does at altitude, a held breath—and in the sudden silence he heard voices. Not the wind's voices. Human ones. And boots on scree, more than one pair, coming up the eastern flank.

"No," Sela said. She'd heard it too. Her hand closed on his sleeve. "No, no—she can't have—"

"She climbed," Petra said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Of course she climbed."

* * *

They made the saddle with the chest swinging between them and Marrow's team thirty meters below and closing, three dark shapes moving through the whiteout with the unhurried competence of people who had done this their whole lives. Petra knew the type. He'd been the type, once, underwater: men who did not hurry because hurrying was how you died, who simply kept coming because stopping was not among their options.

There was nowhere left to go. Behind them, the pass they'd climbed was already filling with drift; ahead, the descent was a white void; above, the ridge line ran knife-sharp toward a summit lost in cloud. He set the chest down against the cairn and put his body between it and the slope.

"Sela." He had to say it twice before she turned. "If I go down, you take the chest and you don't look back. You hear me? You've read enough of those pages to tell it yourself. You don't need me."

"I need you to stop talking like that."

"It's arithmetic."

"It's cowardice wearing arithmetic's coat." Her face was raw with cold and fury, and for a moment she looked less like an archivist than like the pages themselves—all that burned knowledge given a mouth. "You didn't carry this thing over a mountain so you could die guarding stones. It's paper, Petra. Paper survives. People are what's hard to replace."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted to tell her that people were exactly what he'd spent ten years proving were easy to lose. But there was no time, because the first shape resolved out of the snow, and it was her.

Marrow came up the last pitch alone, her two men hanging back at the edge of visibility with rifles slung—not lowered, but slung, which in Marrow's language was a sentence still being written. She stopped six meters away. Snow crusted her eyebrows and the fur of her hood, and her breath came in slow white plumes, and she looked at Petra the way she'd looked at him across the tavern in Halden's Reach a lifetime ago: patiently, courteously, as though they were the only two people in the world and she regretted the necessity of the meeting.

"Diver," she said.

"Captain."

"You're a long way from your river."

"You're a long way from your navy."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "My navy wants this matter buried. I've decided I'm not my navy anymore." She glanced at the chest, then back at him. "Give it to me and walk south. Both of you. There's a shepherd track off the western shoulder. My men won't follow you. I give you my word."

"Your word." Petra tasted blood where the cold had split his lip. "In Halden's Reach you hanged a smuggler from a crane arm."

"I executed a man who was going to sell your location to three different buyers and get us all buried in a navy hole. You're welcome, by the way." The almost-smile again. "You've been reading my reputation instead of my record. Divers do that. Assume the surface tells the whole story."

"And what does your record say, Captain?"

For just a moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—her eyes went somewhere else. Somewhere with a desk in it, and a pen, and a signature. He'd seen that look on Sela's face a hundred times on this climb, whenever the cipher gave up another name. Grief doing bookkeeping.

"My record says I'm tired," Marrow said quietly. "Give me the chest, diver. It's the only version of this day where everyone walks off this mountain."

"The chest goes to Velin Ost."

"The chest goes into a stove, if my superiors have their way, and you two go into a hole beside it. I'm offering you the exit. Take it."

Sela stepped forward, into the space between them, and Petra's heart seized—not with fear for himself but with the sudden, unbearable recognition of what she was about to do, because he knew that stance, he'd seen it in taverns and on docks his whole life, the posture of a person about to spend themselves on a point of principle.

"Read page forty-one," Sela said.

Marrow went very still.

"You've read the rest by now—I know you have, you had the chest for two days before we took it back at the customs hut, and you're too good an officer not to have gone through every page. So you've seen the fleet manifests. You've seen the troop dispositions. But page forty-one is ciphered differently, and you don't have the key, and I do." Sela's voice didn't shake. The wind tried to take it and failed. "Page forty-one names the officer who signed the transfer order for the Kaldavian coastal batteries. The order that opened the harbor at Vell to the bombardment. The order that killed eleven thousand people in one night."

"I know what's on page forty-one," Marrow said.

The words landed in the snow between them like something dropped from a great height.

"I know," she said again, softer, "because I've spent nine years trying to prove it. And every archive I touched was burned, and every witness I found was dead or bought, and every superior I trusted told me to stop asking. So yes, archivist. I know what's on page forty-one. I've known its shape since I was nineteen years old. What I don't know—" and here her voice finally cracked, a hairline fracture across all that discipline, "—is whether giving it to you gives it to the world, or just gets three more people erased. That's the arithmetic I'm doing. Up here. In the snow. While you two play at martyrdom."

Petra felt something shift in his chest that had nothing to do with the break. He had hated this woman since Halden's Reach. He had built her out of fear and called the building accurate. And now she stood in front of him carrying the same wound Sela carried, the same wound he carried, and the hatred had nowhere to stand.

"The world," Sela said. "It gives it to the world. That's what presses are for. That's why yours burned theirs."

Marrow looked at her for a long moment. Then at Petra. Then at the chest.

And that was when the mountain decided.

* * *

Later—in the weeks after, in the life he would eventually build out of the wreckage of this day—Petra would replay those four seconds hundreds of times, and each time they would come apart differently in his hands. The truth, as near as he could ever assemble it, was this:

The wind returned.

It came off the summit ridge in a single flat slab, the kind of gust that at altitude isn't weather but geology, and it hit the three of them standing in the saddle like a hand. Sela, closest to the eastern edge, staggered back a step. Two steps. Her heel went over the lip of the cornice that had built up along the saddle's drop during the night—a ledge of compacted snow as innocent-looking as a sidewalk.

Petra lunged. His good arm caught her wrist. The weight of her, and the ice under his boots, and the arm that wasn't there to catch him—it all happened in the same instant, one continuous falling that only later sorted itself into parts. They went down together. The cornice groaned beneath them, a deep structural sound, the sound of something enormous changing its mind.

And then Marrow was there.

He never saw her cross the distance. Six meters of glare ice and she covered it like it was a deck in a swell, dropped flat at the lip, and got her fist into Sela's collar as the cornice let go. The whole shelf of it—tons of snow, weeks of wind-work—peeled away down the eastern face with a roar like the river at flood, taking Petra's feet out from under him, taking his grip, taking everything except the two points where flesh held: Marrow's hand on Sela's collar, and Sela's hand, wrenched nearly out of its socket, locked around Petra's fingers.

He dangled. The drop below was not visible. It did not need to be visible. He could feel it the way you feel depth through a hull.

His arm screamed. The broken one, jammed against the cornice edge, the splint grinding, and he heard himself make a sound he hadn't made since he was a boy—short, involuntary, animal. Sela's fingers were slipping in his, slick with snow-melt, and he watched her face above him and thought with perfect clarity: *this is where I stop.* Not at the bottom of the Verren. Not in some navy hole. Here, at the top of the world, holding onto the first person in ten years whose name he knew and wanted to keep knowing.

"Hold," Marrow grunted, hauling, her boots skidding backward centimeter by centimeter. "Hold, hold—"

One of her men appeared beside her—Vasch, the big silent one—and anchored her harness, and between the three of them they walked Petra's weight up the broken slope hand over hand, an eternity measured in breaths, until his knees found snow and his good arm found stone and he was lying on the saddle gasping like a landed fish while the storm poured itself over all of them indifferently.

Sela was crying. He'd never seen her cry. She cried the way she deciphered—methodically, silently, without any apparent awareness that she was doing it.

Marrow stood over them both, chest heaving, snow plastered to her front, and looked down at the place where the cornice had been. Where the drop was now a clean shear of blue ice falling away into cloud.

"That," she said finally, "was stupid. All of it. Every choice anyone has made on this mountain was stupid." She pulled off a glove with her teeth and flexed her fingers, checking them. "I saved your archivist, diver. Consider what that costs me, and then consider whether you want to keep treating me like the thing you feared in Halden's Reach."

Petra pushed himself up against the cairn. Everything hurt in a way that felt new even against the established catalogue of his hurts. "Why did you save her?"

"Because eleven thousand people died at Vell and the man who signed the order was my father." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd report a sounding. "Because I have spent my entire career being the competent instrument of the people who covered it up, and I am done. Because someone once told me the dead don't need avengers, they need witnesses, and I intend to be one if it's the last competent thing I ever do." She looked at the chest, sitting against the cairn, dusted white, unbothered by any of it. "But understand me. I'm not giving it to you out of mercy. I'm taking it with me."

"What?"

"Downslope. To Velin Ost. By the southern route, which is open, which my men know, and which you—" she gestured at his arm, at Sela's wrenched hand, at the two of them steaming and shaking in the snow, "—cannot reach before the storm closes everything. Look behind you, diver."

He looked. The pass they'd climbed was gone. Not difficult—gone. A white wall of spindrift stood where the slope had been, and even as he watched it thickened, erasing the last of their tracks, erasing the cairn's lower courses, erasing the world they'd come from. The storm had sealed the pass behind them, exactly as the shepherd had warned, exactly as the deadline had demanded, and they had beaten the winter by hours and lost everything else in the same bargain.

"You'll take it yourself," Petra said. "To the press."

"I'll take it myself. With my testimony attached, signed with my full name and rank, which will end my career, quite possibly my freedom, and certainly any relationship I might have pretended to salvage with a family that doesn't deserve one." Marrow shouldered the chest's sling herself, testing the weight, and something in her face eased when she took it—as though she'd been carrying a heavier version for years and had only now been handed the actual object. "Your part's done, diver. You got it over the mountain. Let someone else carry it the last few kilometers. That's not surrender. That's how anything real gets built—more hands than you planned on."

"And if you don't come?"

"Then burn my name off whatever you publish." The almost-smile, one last time, and this time it reached her eyes. "But I'll come. I've been coming to this moment for nine years. A little weather won't stop me now."

She turned to go. Stopped.

"Diver. What's your name? Your actual one. If I'm to be a witness, I should know whose wreck I'm testifying about."

The wind howled. Sela looked at him. And Petra—who had been no one for ten years, who had sanded himself down to a function, a pair of hands and a rule about leaving no trace—heard himself say it clearly, into the storm, for the second time in a decade:

"Petra Voss."

Marrow nodded once, like a captain logging a fix. "Hold on to it this time," she said, and then she was gone, downslope into the white with her two men, and the storm closed over her like water.

* * *

They lay that night in the lee of the cairn under the tent fly, sharing one blanket, sharing body heat, sharing the last of the pemmican. Below them the eastern face dropped away into nothing. Behind them the pass was a wall. Ahead was a descent they couldn't see and a valley they didn't know and a press that would receive its cargo from the wrong hands—or the right ones; he genuinely no longer knew which.

His arm was worse. Sela had looked at it by lantern light and gone quiet in a way he didn't like, and packed it tighter, and said only, "Downhill fast. Warmth. A surgeon." All the things they had least of.

"We're alive," she said into the dark, eventually. "Do you understand that yet? We're actually alive, and it's done, and she's carrying it."

"I know."

"You're furious."

"I'm—" He searched for it. The word *furious* fit, technically. But underneath it was something stranger: the sensation of a man whose load has been taken mid-crossing, who keeps bracing for a weight that isn't there anymore. Ten years of carrying himself, and now the chest was gone, and the secret was gone, and the running was over, and what remained was just a man with a broken arm on top of a mountain, named.

"I'm empty," he said. "That's all. I'll fill up with something better on the way down."

Sela laughed—a small, cracked, real sound—and put her forehead against his shoulder, careful of the arm.

Below them, far down through a tear in the clouds, a single light burned in the black valley. A window. Maybe a village. Maybe the southern route, and Marrow moving through the night toward it with the dead nation on her back.

"Come on," Sela said, and started packing.
