## 18. Descent Without a Map

The far side of the pass was a country Sela had never imagined, because no one had ever bothered to describe it. Maps ended at the Ashen Range the way sentences end at a period—everything beyond was blank paper, and blank paper, in her experience, meant either nothing or everything.

It meant everything.

They came down out of the storm on the second morning, Petra half-carrying his splinted arm against his chest like something he'd stolen and couldn't put down, Sela walking backward more often than forward so she could catch him when the scree shifted under his boots. The snow stopped an hour after dawn, as if someone had simply closed a book, and the clouds tore open to show them a valley so wide and so green it looked like a lie told by the mountain.

"Velin Ost is down there," Sela said. "Somewhere."

Petra didn't answer. He had stopped answering most questions somewhere above the cloud line, in the cave where he'd said things about his brother that Sela would carry for the rest of her life and never repeat to another living soul. Since then he'd gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than the storm had. Storms ended. Whatever was happening inside him seemed to have no scheduled conclusion.

She checked the abbot's directions again. He'd written them on the back of a psalter page in a hand that belonged to another century: *Follow the meltwater until it forgets its name. Where three cairns stand like brothers quarreling, take the middle path though it looks the worst. The press keeps house beneath a roof of blue slate, and the door answers to a question, not a knock.*

*The door answers to a question.* Sela had spent six years in the burned archive of Kaldavia's ministry of records, cataloguing what survived the fires, and she recognized the abbot's style immediately. Archivists wrote directions the way they wrote everything else—as tests. You didn't find the free press by following instructions. You found it by proving you could think the way people who kept secrets thought.

*Follow the meltwater until it forgets its name.* A river lost its name when it joined a larger one. So: descend along the stream until it met something bigger.

That part was easy. The hard part was that every step down was a step Petra couldn't take alone.

* * *

His arm was worse than he admitted, which was itself an admission, because Petra Voss lied about pain the way other men lied about money—automatically, and always upward. Sela had watched him set the break himself in the cornice collapse's aftermath, using a ski pole from their abandoned caravan gear and a length of rope, without a sound. She'd watched him do it and understood, finally, the full architecture of the man: ten years of erasing himself had not been cowardice. It had been discipline, the kind you build around a wound so you can keep walking.

But discipline only carried a body so far. By midday the arm had begun to swell past the splint, and his color had gone the gray of river water in February, and when he stumbled for the third time in an hour she made a decision she knew he'd hate.

"We stop."

"We're losing light." His voice came out flat, scraped thin by altitude.

"We're losing *you*, which is worse. Sit down."

"I don't—"

"Sit down, Petra." She heard her own voice crack on his name—not his real name, the borrowed one, but still the closest thing to him she was permitted—and something in him folded. He sat.

She built the fire while he watched her with an expression she couldn't read, and then she did the thing she'd been rehearsing in her head since the summit: she took off her pack, dug out the last of the willow bark and the linen strips, and said, "Give me your arm."

"I can manage."

"You can't. That's the whole point. You can't dive, you can't fight, you can barely walk downhill, and I can do all the things you can't right now, so the arithmetic is simple." She knelt beside him. "Give me your arm."

For a long moment he just looked at her, and she saw the war behind his eyes—the same war she'd fought her whole life, the one between needing help and believing need was a debt that always got called in. She knew that war intimately. She'd lost it for six years in an archive, surrounded by documents that proved every act of trust in Kaldavia's history had eventually been used against somebody.

Then he gave her his arm.

It was heavier than she expected. She unwound the splint, rewrapped it tighter, higher, packed the swelling with snow in a cloth, and worked with the same methodical care she'd once applied to damaged folios—clean hands, steady pressure, no wasted motion. When she finished, he flexed his fingers experimentally and let out a breath that might have been relief or surrender.

"You've done this before," he said.

"Rescued divers?"

"Taken care of something broken."

Sela tied off the last knot. "I spent six years keeping dead things intact enough to be read. It's the same skill." She fed the fire a branch. "You've been carrying me since Krast. Let me carry you for a day. It won't kill either of us."

He almost smiled. "You don't know that."

"No," she agreed. "Nobody knows anything. That's why we keep records."

* * *

They descended through the afternoon into a country that grew softer as they went—scree giving way to pine, pine to meadow gone gold with late autumn, the meltwater stream fattening beside them until it was less a stream than a young river, loud and certain of itself. Sela navigated. She'd never navigated anything in her life except the maze of a destroyed archive, but it turned out the skills transferred: read the terrain, remember what was said, test each assumption before betting your life on it.

Petra walked behind her now. She'd suggested it gently—heavier packs go downhill last—but she understood what it actually meant. For ten years this man had led with his body, diving first into black water, checking every room before anyone else entered it. Now his body was a liability and his mind was fogged with fever and pain, and the only way down the mountain was to follow a disgraced archivist who hadn't held a weapon in her life.

She expected resentment. What she saw instead, glancing back, was something she could only call wonder—as if he were watching a door open in a wall he'd believed was solid.

At dusk they reached the confluence. The meltwater poured itself into a broad gray river without ceremony, and just as the abbot promised, it forgot its name—or rather, the valley gave the bigger river a name in a language Sela didn't know, carved into a boundary stone so old the letters had gone soft.

"Three cairns next," she said, consulting the psalter page by firelight-less twilight. "Like brothers quarreling."

"They'll be up the valley." Petra's voice was slurred with exhaustion. "Cairns mark passes. We crossed one today. There'll be a fork ahead where trails split toward two more."

She looked at him. "How do you know that?"

"Salvage work. You learn how mountains think, even underwater ones." He settled against his pack. "You learn how everything thinks, if you pay attention long enough. Rivers, wrecks, men." A pause. "Women."

"Careful," Sela said. "Flattery is a record too. I'll remember it."

This time he did smile—a small, cracked thing, but real. She banked the fire and took first watch, and as he slept she reread the abbot's page until the words blurred, and thought about what it meant to be trusted with someone's survival. It felt less like weight than she'd feared. It felt, absurdly, like being handed a book and asked to read it aloud.

* * *

The three cairns appeared near noon the next day, exactly as Petra predicted: three stacked stone figures on a shoulder of hillside above a trail junction, weathered into postures that did look like an argument. The left path descended gently through open meadow. The right path dropped steeply into dark timber. The middle path—which the abbot insisted was correct—was barely a path at all, a scar of loose rock threading between two walls of thornbrush, looking precisely as bad as advertised.

"The middle path," Sela said.

"It looks the worst."

"That's the instruction. *Take the middle path though it looks the worst.*"

Petra studied all three routes with his good eye, and she watched him do the thing he did—read terrain the way she read text, extracting meaning from structure. "Left path is open ground. Visible for miles. Right path funnels into timber—easy to ambush, easy to be tracked. Middle path is miserable but nobody uses it, which means nobody watches it." He glanced at her. "Your abbot thinks like a smuggler."

"He thinks like an archivist. Same thing, historically."

They took the middle path. It was miserable. The thornbrush took blood from both of them, and twice Petra fell and Sela caught him, and the second time he stayed leaning against her longer than balance required—just a few seconds, his forehead nearly touching her shoulder, breathing—before pushing upright and saying nothing, and her saying nothing back, and the nothing between them feeling larger than any conversation they'd managed since Halden's Reach.

Near the bottom of the scar, wedged in the rocks, Sela found a marker: a flat stone, deliberately placed, painted with a single blue stripe. Fresh paint. Someone maintained this route.

"Somebody walks this path," she said quietly.

"Somebody who doesn't want to be seen walking it." Petra crouched, wincing, and examined the stone. "Blue. The abbot said blue slate."

"The roof of blue slate. The door that answers to a question." Sela stood, scanning the valley below, where the river bent around a fold of hills. Smoke rose from chimneys somewhere—a town, or a village pretending not to be one. "We're close. Which means we're dangerous. Anyone we meet from here on could be Marrow's, or the republic's, or just a farmer who sells information to buy winter feed."

"So we don't meet anyone." Petra straightened. "We watch the town from the ridge until dark. Then we go in the way you'd go into a wreck—slow, quiet, exits mapped first."

Sela blinked at him. "You're navigating again."

"I'm advising. You're navigating." He picked up his pack one-armed. "There's a difference. I'm learning it."

* * *

They reached the edge of Velin Ost after full dark, moving through orchard rows gone wild, and stopped in the shadow of a hay barn to study the town. It was small—two hundred roofs, maybe fewer—and unremarkable except for one thing. On the low hill at the town's center stood a building with a steep roof that caught even the starlight strangely, and Sela realized the slate tiles were the deep blue of deep water.

"There," she whispered.

"And there's our problem." Petra nodded toward the building. Two figures moved on the hillside below it—not guards, exactly; they carried no lanterns and walked like men trying to seem casual. But they walked a circuit. And circuits meant watching.

"Marrow?" Sela breathed.

"Or the republic's people. Or the press's own pickets, checking for followers." He grimaced. "We can't know. And we can't walk up and ask, because if they're hostile, we've delivered the chest to the people it exists to destroy."

Sela stared at the blue roof, and the abbot's words turned over in her mind. *The door answers to a question, not a knock.*

"Then we don't go to the door," she said slowly. "We go to the *question*. Think—if you were hiding a free press in a border town, how would you design the approach? You wouldn't want strangers finding it. You'd want people who already understood something. People who could prove—" She stopped. Her heart was suddenly loud. "People who could prove they'd come from the other side. From Kaldavia."

Petra turned to her.

"The chest," Sela said. "The seal. The ciphered pages. The press won't reveal itself to anyone who knocks. But it will reveal itself to anyone who arrives carrying proof of why it needs to exist." She pulled her collar tighter against the cold. "We don't sneak past the watchers. We walk up to them, in plain sight, and we show them the crest of a country that isn't on any map anymore. If they're the press's people, we're home. If they're not—" She swallowed. "If they're not, then we were dead anyway, and at least we die having tried to deliver it."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan that matches the evidence."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Then he laughed—softly, painfully, one-armed, holding his ribs. "Ten years I've survived by assuming everyone is the enemy. And now my life depends on walking up to strangers and trusting a dead nation's seal to vouch for me." He shook his head. "You realize what you're asking."

"I realize exactly what I'm asking." Sela met his eyes. "I'm asking you to stop running. One street early. That's all. One street."

Below them, the watchers completed another slow circuit of the blue-slate roof, patient as tide. Somewhere in that building, type was waiting in its cases, and paper was waiting in its reams, and twenty years of buried truth was waiting for the only thing it had ever needed: someone willing to carry it across a mountain and say, out loud, whose hands had held it.

Petra exhaled, long and white in the cold air.

"All right," he said. "Lead the way."
