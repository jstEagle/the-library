## 20. Beyond the Range

The border, it turned out, was a line of paint.

A stripe of faded yellow across the frozen road at the bottom of the pass, half scuffed by cart wheels, flanked on either side by nothing but larch forest and the long white shoulder of the mountain behind them. No wall. No wire. Just the paint, and beyond it a road that descended in long lazy switchbacks through country that looked no different from the country they'd left—except that every step of it belonged to people who had never surrendered to the republic, and never would.

Petra stopped at the line and stood there longer than he meant to.

Ten years. Ten years of crossing borders the way water crosses them—under fences, in ballast tanks, inside other men's paperwork—and here was one he could simply walk over. His boots were ruined. His right arm hung in Sela's sling, strapped tight against his chest where she'd bound it that morning with strips of her own shirt and two splints of green pine, and the ache of it ran from his shoulder to his fingertips like a hot wire. The pass had cost him the arm for the winter, maybe longer. It had cost Marrow everything else.

He stepped over the paint.

Nothing happened. That was the strange part. No whistle, no shot, no hand on his collar. The world did not rearrange itself around the fact of him. He crossed a border drawn in yellow paint and the mountains went on being mountains, and somewhere behind him the snow came down on the pass they'd nearly died on, sealing it until spring, and ahead of him the road just kept going down.

"You're smiling," Sela said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible. Like watching a man try to remember how."

He wiped his face with his good hand and found that she was right, and that made it worse, so he started walking.

* * *

They reached Velin Ost at dusk on the third day, coming down out of the foothills onto a plain stitched with frozen fields, and the town announced itself first by smell—woodsmoke and ink and something yeasty from a brewery—and then by sound: bells, real ones, ringing the hour from three different towers without anyone shooting at them for it.

It was bigger than Petra had imagined. He'd carried the phrase *the last free press* up the whole length of the Verren and over the top of the Ashen Range like a stone in his boot, and he'd pictured some cellar with a hand press and four frightened men. Velin Ost was a town of slate roofs and crooked gables, ten thousand souls at least, with market stalls shuttered against the cold and children throwing snowballs off a bridge into a canal that steamed faintly, geothermally, the way mountain towns' canals sometimes did. There were soldiers here too—but they wore no republic grey. Their coats were the deep faded blue of the old Kaldavian navy, moth-eaten at the cuffs, and they checked no papers and stopped no one, and one of them nodded to Sela as she passed as if nodding were simply what people did here.

Sela had gone quiet somewhere on the second day of the descent and hadn't come back. She walked through those streets with her head turning slowly, taking in doorways and lintels and the carved letters above a schoolhouse gate—letters in an alphabet Petra couldn't read, all loops and hard angles—and when she finally spoke her voice came out sanded down to almost nothing.

"I catalogued this alphabet for six years," she said. "In a room underground. I could describe every character in it. I never once heard it spoken aloud."

"Say something," Petra said.

She looked at the schoolhouse letters for a long moment. "Later," she said, and her eyes were wet, and she pretended they weren't, and he let her pretend, because that was a courtesy he understood.

The press was exactly where the abbot's directions had promised it would be: behind a chandler's shop on a street called the Weft, down stairs that smelled of tallow and lamp oil, through a door with no sign at all except a small brass plate worn blank by a century of thumbs. A boy of perhaps fifteen opened it before they knocked, as if he'd been told to expect them—which, Petra supposed, they had been, in the only currency that mattered here: the abbot's word, carried down-mountain weeks ago by a monk with good boots.

The boy took one look at the sling, one look at the canvas-wrapped chest between them, and stepped aside.

Inside was heat and noise and light. A cellar that had been dug deeper than any cellar needed to be, its rock walls hung with drying paper, three presses standing in a row like altars, and a dozen people—old men, young women, a pair of twins who might have been sixteen—moving among them with the unhurried urgency of people who had done illegal work so long it had become liturgy. The air was thick with the smell of ink and hot metal. Someone was setting type by lamplight, the little steel letters clicking into the composing stick like rain on a roof.

An old woman came toward them wiping her hands on an apron gone grey with ink. She was small and square as a stove, with spectacles pushed up into white hair and forearms that could have cracked walnuts, and she looked at neither of them at first. She looked at the chest.

"Well," she said, in a voice like gravel settling. "Open it."

Petra looked at Sela. Sela looked at him. Between them passed the whole argument they'd already had a hundred times on the road down—the arm, the cipher, whose hands, whose right—and ended, as it always ended now, with Sela stepping forward.

She knelt and unwrapped the canvas. The chest's iron bands had gone brown with river rust and mountain frost, but the Kaldavian crest on the lid still showed clear under the lamplight: the triple wave and the star above it. The old woman went very still when she saw it. Behind her, the noise of the cellar began, one person at a time, to die.

Sela worked the locks—she'd learned their trick back at the smelter, months ago, another life—and lifted the lid, and the smell of the river rose out of it one last time: cold silt and old iron and something like drowned wood. She lifted out the top ledger, the one with the ciphered columns she'd spent the whole journey cracking page by page by firelight, and held it out.

The old woman took it the way you take a child.

She opened it. She read. Petra watched her lips move, translating the cipher aloud in fragments, and he knew which page it was because he knew it by heart now himself—the fleet manifest, the sale, the signatures. He watched the old woman's face do what faces did when they read these pages: first confusion, then arithmetic, then a kind of grief that had waited twenty years for permission.

Her knees went. Not dramatically—one of the twins caught her elbow, steadied her, and she waved him off and stayed standing, but something in her had sat down even if the rest of her hadn't.

"Read it aloud," someone said from the back of the cellar.

"No," the old woman said. "Not yet." Her voice had changed; there was iron under the gravel now. "Not until it's verified. Not until it's true beyond touching." She turned and looked at Petra, really looked at him for the first time, spectacles coming down onto her nose. "Who are you?"

And there it was. The question. The whole question, the one he'd spent ten years making sure no one could ever ask with any hope of an answer, asked now in a cellar full of strangers who had every right to it.

"Sela Iven," Sela said, before he could speak. "Archivist, third class, burned archive of Kaldavia. And this is—" she paused, and he felt her hesitate, giving him the out, offering him the last door out of his own name—

"My name is Petra Voss," he said.

It came out steady. That surprised him most—not the saying of it but the sound of it, ordinary, unremarkable, a name like any other name, after ten years of treating it like a live grenade. Nobody in the cellar gasped. Nobody cared. The old woman wrote it down in a ledger of her own, in careful blocky script, and moved on to asking about the wreck, the river, the route over the pass, and Petra Voss answered her questions like a man reporting for work.

That night they slept on pallets between the presses, in the warmth of the boiler, and Petra lay awake listening to the type-setters working the late shift, the click and slide of it, and thought: *this is what it sounds like when a name costs nothing.* Then he thought about Captain Ilse Marrow walking down out of the mountains with a chest she'd chosen not to keep, into a country that would call her a traitor and worse, and he thought: *no. It costs exactly what it's worth.*

* * *

They came on the fourth day.

The old woman—her name was Maren Ost, and the town was full of Osts, cousins of cousins, which was why the press had lasted here twenty years when free presses elsewhere lasted twenty months—had sent riders east and south within hours of opening the chest, and the riders came back followed by the men and women they'd gone to fetch. They arrived in twos and threes, travel-stained, wary, armed in the quiet way of people who'd been officers once and had never quite put it down. An admiral's aide-de-camp grown grey and stooped, running a ferry crossing now. A signals officer who'd escaped the purge by being posted abroad and had spent twenty years writing letters no one answered. A gunnery captain with a burn-scarred neck who stood in the corner of the cellar and didn't sit and didn't speak.

They gathered around the table in the press's back room, eleven of them plus Maren and Sela, and Petra stood by the wall with his bad arm and watched them verify his river.

It was Sela's show. She'd earned that on the mountain and everyone knew it. She laid the ledgers out in sequence, walked them through the cipher—two of the old officers recognized it instantly, one of them laughing once, horribly, at the sight of it, "we used this for coal requisitions"—and then turned to the core document, the founding council's order, the sale of the fleet, and read the operative lines aloud in Kaldavian and then again in the trade tongue while the room went silent as a held breath.

Then they did the thing Petra hadn't expected. They argued.

For two hours they argued—over dates, over ink, over whether the council seal on the fourth page matched the seal dies used in the naval registry in the year in question, over whether the watermark in the paper could have been forged, over handwriting, over margins, over a smudge of river silt that one man insisted proved tampering and another proved authenticity because *who would forge a document and then bury it in a river*. They handled the pages with cotton gloves. They photographed nothing. They measured things with calipers. It was the most forensic, most merciless, most loving examination Petra had ever seen anything undergo, and somewhere in the middle of it he understood that this was the ceremony—that for twenty years these people had been waiting not for revenge but for *proof*, proof clean enough to survive the republic's inevitable counterattack, and now it lay on a table in front of them smelling faintly of the Verren, and they were going to make certain of it if it took all winter.

Near dusk, the grey aide-de-camp—the oldest of them, a man named Corvel who'd signed the ferry over to his son years ago just to have fewer possessions to lose—straightened up from the last page and took off his gloves finger by finger.

"It's genuine," he said. "All of it. God help us, it's genuine."

Nobody cheered. Petra had expected cheering, or weeping, or both—he'd seen printers weep over the pages two nights ago, Maren Ost holding a ledger against her apron with her shoulders shaking—but this was different. This was eleven old soldiers arriving together at the edge of a cliff and looking down. Because they all understood, in the way that only people who had lived through the first purge could understand, what verification meant. It meant the document was real. It meant the war was real. It meant that publishing it would bring the republic down on this town like weather, and that the names at the bottom of those pages—men still in office, men with fleets and ministries and long memories—would not send lawyers.

Corvel looked across the table at Petra.

"The diver," he said. "You raised this."

"With help," Petra said.

"From the bottom of the Verren. Through a navy cordon. Over the Ashen Range in November." Corvel's eyes went to the sling, then back. "Why?"

And Petra, who had answers ready for that question—money, accident, ignorance, all the lies he'd kept oiled for ten years—found that none of them would come. What came instead was the truth, plain and stupid and heavy, the way truths always sounded when you finally set them down.

"Because my brother's name is probably in one of these ledgers," he said. "I've never checked. I haven't been able to. But somebody has to know what happened to him, and somebody has to know it *out loud*, in print, where it can't be taken back. That's why."

The room was quiet. The gunnery captain in the corner spoke for the first time, his burned throat making the words come out rough: "Which ledger?"

"The personnel one. Third volume. Entries after the fall of the harbor districts."

The captain crossed the room, took the third volume, and set it in front of Petra with both hands, like a man laying a wreath.

"Then look," he said. "Tonight. Before we print anything. You should know what you're carrying."

* * *

They gave him the back room alone, which was a kindness, though it didn't feel like one. A lamp. The volume. The door closed.

Petra sat for a long time with his good hand flat on the cover, not opening it. Outside he could hear the presses starting up—not his pages yet, Maren had been explicit about that, but the ordinary contraband, the news sheets, the forbidden histories, the daily work of staying free—and the rhythm of it came through the wall like a heartbeat.

He thought about the rule. The old rule, the first rule, older than salvage: *take nothing, leave no trace, be no one.* He had built a whole life on it, a decade of empty ledgers and borrowed names, and he had told himself on the mountain, in the cave, in the white silence, that he'd finally understood why—that it had never been discipline, it had been punishment, penance paid in anonymity for a brother he hadn't saved and a country he hadn't mourned. Fine. Understood. Confessed.

But confession wasn't the same as looking. He knew that now, sitting here, with the lamp burning and the volume under his hand. You could carry a thing across a mountain range and still not open it. Courage, it turned out, came in installments, and this was the last payment due.

He opened the book.

The entries were in the ciphered shorthand Sela had taught him to recognize, but the personnel sections used a simpler substitution, and she'd drilled him on the common characters during the long descent, patiently, night after night, saying *you'll want to read your own documents someday*—and God, she'd known, she'd known all along what he was carrying and what it would cost, and she'd taught him anyway.

He turned pages. Harbor district, harbor district. Names he didn't know. Dates. Dispositions—a cold bureaucratic word for what had been done to people. The republic's clerks had been thorough; that was the horror of it, the thoroughness, the neat columns, the way atrocity looked exactly like inventory.

Then, a third of the way in, a familiar name.

Not his brother's.

*Marrow, Edvard — Naval Registry, Kaldavia Fleet Office — retained for testimony — transferred — disposition: see annex.*

Edvard Marrow. Ilse's father. The signature he'd seen on the core document, the name Sela had read aloud on the summit ridge, the wound the captain had climbed a mountain carrying. Here it was in the personnel rolls too, filed and numbered, *retained for testimony*, and Petra sat looking at those three words and understood suddenly, completely, what Ilse Marrow had known since the customs hut: that her father had not been a victim of the purge. He had been staff.

He closed the volume gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

His brother was further in. He knew it the way you know a wreck is down there before your lead line finds it—a pressure in the chest, a certainty without evidence. He turned more pages. The lamp hissed. Outside, the presses ran on.

And there—at the bottom of a page, in the last entries before the ledger breaks off mid-year, the records dissolving into chaos as the republic's forces overran the coast—two words in the disposition column that were not *executed*, not *transferred*, not *unaccounted*:

*Escaped east.*

Petra read it five times. The date was nine days after the fall of the harbor districts. Nine days. His brother had been alive nine days after the end, and had run, and had run *east*—east, the direction of mountains, of passes, of a painted line on a road and a town full of presses and blue-coated soldiers who nodded at strangers.

Petra Voss sat in a lamplit back room in the last free town in the world, with his broken arm pressed to his chest and his heart going like a diver's heart at depth, and understood that the document he'd carried over the Ashen Range was not the end of anything.

It was a trailhead.

Somewhere east of this room—maybe in this town, maybe in a village past it, maybe dead these twenty years, maybe old and alive and drinking coffee at this very hour within a mile of him—was a man who had escaped east, with a name Petra had spent ten years refusing to say aloud.

He stood up, knocking the stool over, and opened the door, and the noise of the presses rolled over him, and down the corridor Sela was already turning, already reading his face, because she always did.

"Petra?" she said. "What is it?"

He held up the ledger. His hand was shaking. He found that he didn't care.

"He's alive," Petra said. "Or he was. Sela—he got out. He came *east*."

And behind her, in the doorway of the press room, Maren Ost lowered the sheet she'd been proofing and looked at the two of them over her spectacles, and said quietly, "Then you'd better tell me everything, because tomorrow morning we go to press—and after that, child, nothing in this town will ever be quiet again."
