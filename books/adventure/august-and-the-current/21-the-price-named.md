## 21. The Price Named

The press house in Velin Ost smelled of ink and cold iron, a smell Petra hadn't encountered since childhood—since before the river, before the wrecks, before he had taught himself to be no one. It came up through the floorboards of the old granary where the printers worked, rising with the heat of the presses like something alive, something that had been waiting down there all along for him to come back.

They had given him a room above the press floor. A cot, a chair, a window that looked out on a cobbled square where women sold apples from handcarts and children chased a hoop with sticks. Ordinary life, going on as if nations didn't burn. He had watched it for three days without leaving the room.

Sela had not left his side since the customs hut. She ate at the long table downstairs with the printers, argued with them about typography, learned their names. She had stopped flinching at doors. Petra noticed these things the way he noticed currents—the small shifts that told you what the water intended.

On the fourth morning she climbed the stairs without knocking and set a document on his cot. The deciphered pages, copied out fair in her careful archivist's hand, bound with cord.

"It's ready," she said. "They want testimony attached. A witness statement. Who found the chest, where, how it came into our hands." She paused. "It needs a name."

Petra looked at the pages without touching them. Through the floor he could hear the press starting its slow work, the rhythmic clatter of it, like a heart learning to beat again.

"You know what I was," he said. "You know what I made myself."

"I know what you're afraid of."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Sela agreed. "But it lives in the same house."

She left the pages and went back down. He sat with them for a long time, the way he had once sat at the bottom of the Verren with his hand on a chest too heavy to raise, feeling its weight through the silt, knowing everything would change if he brought it up.

He had brought it up anyway.

* * *

Marrow found him at dusk in the square, sitting on the fountain rim with an apple he wasn't eating. She wore civilian clothes now—a gray coat, plain boots—and looked smaller for it, diminished by the absence of uniform, or perhaps freed by it. Hard to tell with her. It had always been hard to tell with her.

"They'll print tomorrow," she said, sitting beside him without invitation. "The full run. Copies go east on the mail coaches within the week."

"I know."

"Then you know what happens after." She turned the apple over in his hand, took it, bit into it herself. "There are men in the republic who have survived twenty years by burying exactly this kind of paper. They don't send armies across borders. They send one person, quiet, patient. I used to be that person."

"And now?"

"Now I'm a woman eating an apple in a town that isn't mine, waiting to find out who I am when the navy stops paying me." She chewed, swallowed. "My father's signature is on page eleven. Did Sela tell you?"

"She did."

"I read it forty times in that customs hut. His handwriting. I'd know it anywhere—he wrote my birthday letters in that same hand, every year, until he died believing himself a good man." She looked at the fountain, at the water catching the last light. "I climbed a mountain to bury those pages, Petra. I want you to understand that. Whatever you decide tomorrow, remember that the woman standing witness chose the other thing only at the very end, and paid everything for it."

"I remember."

"The reprisals won't come for the printers first. They'll come for the witness. The man who put his hand on the chest." She finished the apple and tossed the core into the fountain. "So think carefully about whether the world needs your name, or whether you need it, and be honest with yourself about which."

She stood, brushed off her coat, and walked back toward the press house. At the door she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "I spent ten years being exactly what they built me to be. It fit perfectly. That's how I knew it was a lie."

* * *

He went down to the press floor near midnight, when the machines were silent and the printers gone home, and found Sela there alone with a lamp, setting type by hand, letter by letter, the way her burned archive must once have been assembled before the fire took it.

She heard him on the stairs but didn't turn. "Well?"

"There's a name," he said. "I haven't used it in ten years. I've said it so few times I half expect it not to fit anymore."

"Names stretch." She set another piece of type. "That's what they're for."

"My brother's name was August." The words came out of him slowly, like salvage coming up on a cable, dripping. "August Voss. He drowned in the second winter after the purges, crossing a river with smugglers because there was nothing left east of the border for us. I was supposed to go with him. I stayed. I told myself it was caution." He watched the lamp flame bend. "It was punishment. I've been underwater ever since. Every record erased, every name abandoned—I thought if I became no one, the current couldn't take anything else from me."

Sela set down her composing stick and finally looked at him.

"And now?"

"Now I have a chest full of proof that the men who emptied our country sold it like scrap iron, and it doesn't mean anything unless someone stands behind it. Unless it has a name attached that can be hurt." He exhaled. "Sela. My name is Petra Voss. Brother of August. Son of Kaldavia. I'm done being no one."

She crossed the floor and took both his hands in hers, ink-stained and steady.

"Then let's set it in type," she said.

* * *

They worked until dawn. She showed him how the letters slotted into the frame, mirrored and backward, so that the truth only became readable when pressed hard against clean paper. He set his own name himself, letter by letter: P-E-T-R-A V-O-S-S. Backward. Waiting.

At first light the printers returned and the presses began, and the sound filled the granary like a river in flood. Page after page came off the drum, warm and damp, the Kaldavian ledgers reproduced entire, the founding council's signatures exposed, and beneath the final testimony, the statement of recovery and witness:

*I, Petra Voss, do swear...*

Marrow stood beside him at the output tray as the first true copy slid out. She picked it up, read his name, and nodded once—the smallest motion, a salute between people who had tried to kill each other on a mountain and instead saved each other.

"Witnessed," she said, and signed beneath his name in her own careful hand: *Ilse Marrow, formerly Captain, Republican Navy.*

Three names on one page. Three people the republic had made into ghosts, pressing themselves back into the record together.

Petra held the warm paper and felt something he hadn't felt in ten years—not safety, not peace, but weight. Good weight. The weight of a chest raised honestly from the bottom, hauled into daylight, opened.

Somewhere east, across the mountains, the mail coaches were already loading. Somewhere in the republic, men were reading their own signatures in print for the first time and reaching for their telephones. The current was moving again, and this time he was swimming with it, named, visible, and unafraid.

Outside the window, the children were chasing the hoop again, and one of them had fallen, and the others had stopped to help her up.

Petra watched them, and smiled, and did not look away.
