## 22. August and the Current

The river at Velin Ost ran green and slow, nothing like the Verren. It had no wrecks in it worth the name—no barge loaded with a nation's sins, no sealed chest, no navy instruments sweeping its bed for secrets. It was just water. Petra stood on the stone quay in the last week of August and watched three students flounder in it, and felt something he had not felt in ten years.

He had nowhere to be.

The thought arrived without drama. That was the strange part. He'd spent a decade assuming that stillness would feel like drowning—that if he ever stopped moving, everything he'd outrun would catch him at once, like a cable snagging a diver's ankle and hauling him down. Instead it felt like this: morning light on the quay, bread from the baker who now knew his name, and three young divers making his mistakes for him thirty feet down.

"Voss," called Marta, the eldest of them, surfacing with her mask pushed up. "The hull section's pinned. We can't shift it."

"Then stop trying to shift it," he said. "Read it first. What's holding it?"

She frowned. "Silt?"

"Silt doesn't pin anything. Look again."

She went under. He waited, chewing his bread, unhurried. This was the whole of his teaching method, as far as he could tell: patience, performed out loud. When she came up again she was grinning.

"Iron strap. From the old crane on the quay. It's hooked through the gunwale."

"There. You didn't need strength. You needed to look." He crouched at the edge of the stones. "That's the trade. Anyone can dive. Reading is the work. A wreck tells you how it died, and most of what killed it was done by people. So when you read a wreck, you're reading people. Remember that, because someday someone will pay you to *not* read one. And that's the day you decide what kind of diver you are."

Marta looked at him the way students do when they suspect a story is behind the lesson but are too polite to ask. The others did too. They all knew. Everyone in Velin Ost knew by now—the testimony had been public for six weeks, printed in four languages, his name set in type above it like a keel mark. *Petra Voss, salvage diver, of Halden's Reach.* Not his real name, not quite; that name belonged to a boy with a brother, and he kept it in the drawer where he kept the brother. But Voss was his now, worn smooth as river glass. It fit.

They went back down. He stayed on the quay with the sun on his shoulders and did nothing at all, and discovered, again, that it cost him nothing.

* * *

The archive had taken over the old customs house on the square—a building of pale stone with tall windows, chosen, Sela said, because it had once been built to keep things *out* and would now spend its life letting things *in*. She found that funny. Petra found it fitting. Buildings, like men, could be repurposed.

He visited on Thursdays, which had become a habit before he noticed it forming. The chest sat in the central hall under glass, lit from above, open. Sela had argued for leaving it open. "Sealed, it's a relic," she'd told the committee. "Open, it's an argument." The ledgers lay displayed at the page where the fleet manifests met the transfer payments, the two columns that, set side by side, ended a country. Schoolchildren came through in crooked lines. Old Kaldavians came through alone, slowly, some of them touching the glass the way you'd touch a grave marker.

Petra usually avoided the hall itself. He'd seen enough of the chest underwater, in waystations, in caves, in Marrow's hands on a ridge above the clouds. But today he walked in, because a man was standing there whom he didn't know, and the man was crying.

He was perhaps seventy, dressed in the careful shabbiness of someone who had once owned better. He stood before the ledger with his hat in both hands, and his shoulders shook without sound, the way men cry when they learned long ago that sound was dangerous.

Petra stopped beside him. Didn't speak. After a while the man said, in accented but fluent trade-tongue, "I was a clerk. In the naval ministry at Dzhoran. I processed the requisitions." He swallowed. "I never saw the other column. Twenty years I told myself there was no other column."

"There was," Petra said. "I raised it myself."

"Yes." The old man wiped his face with his sleeve, unashamed, or past shame. "Thank you. For looking."

He put on his hat and left. Petra stood a while longer in the cool hall, listening to the building breathe, then went up the stairs to find Sela.

* * *

She had an office now, or a room with a desk in it that functioned as one, buried in paper the way she'd been buried in exile for twenty years—except this paper was hers, chosen, catalogued by her own hand. The ciphered pages of the chest hung in framed facsimiles along the wall. Her working translation covered a table like snowfall.

"You're late," she said, not looking up.

"It's Thursday. I'm always Thursday."

"You're always Thursday *at noon*. It's half past." She finished her line, blotted it, and looked at him properly. She was thinner than on the road, and better rested, and there was a new ink stain on her left cuff that seemed permanent, like a tattoo. "Sit. I want to read you something."

"If it's another footnote—"

"Sit down, Petra."

He sat. She pulled a single sheet from the drift and held it flat, and he saw at once that it wasn't a translation. The hand was different. Older. The paper was soft with handling.

"This came last week," she said. "Through the press network. From a woman traveling under the name Ilse Ferren, currently resident in the free territories, occupation listed as *none*." She let that sit. "It's addressed to you. She asked me to check whether you were ready to receive it. I said that was your decision, not mine, so I'm giving it to you unread. Mostly unread." A flicker of guilt. "I read the first line. I'm sorry. I'm an archivist; it's a disease."

"What does the first line say?"

Sela turned the sheet around and slid it across the desk.

*You will not remember me kindly, and you should not.*

He read it twice. Then he put it face down on the desk, carefully, the way he'd once handled a cracked air line.

"She saved your life on the ridge," Sela said quietly. "And she gave back the chest at the border when every instinct she had—and every interest her family had—said to burn it. Whatever else she owes, she paid it. But that doesn't mean you owe her a reply."

"No," Petra agreed. "It doesn't."

He picked the letter up anyway and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where papers went when they mattered. Sela watched him do it and said nothing more, which was its own kind of mercy.

"The republic's response came this morning too, if you want the news with your sentiment," she added. "The council has announced an inquiry. Closed session. The word *irregularities* appears eleven times in their statement. Admiral Renner—" the name that signed the fate lists, the name that still held office— "has resigned his commission pending review, which in their language means they're deciding whether to shoot him or pension him."

"And the navy?"

"The navy's silence cracked open, just like the papers said it would. Three captains have testified already. There's talk of ships being unsealed at Dzhoran—records, logs, the whole drowned bureaucracy hauled up into daylight." She smiled thinly. "You'd appreciate it. The biggest salvage operation in history, and nobody's charging for it."

"They'll charge for it," Petra said. "Later. Someone always invoices the truth eventually."

"Then we'll have the receipts." She gathered her pages. "Go home, Voss. You've done your Thursday. You look tired."

"I'm not tired."

"You look *restless*, then. Which for you is worse."

* * *

He wasn't restless. Or he was, but not the old kind—not the itch between the shoulder blades that had kept him moving for ten years, town to town, name to name, always diving away from something. This was different. This was the feeling of a man standing at the edge of a dock knowing the water below was safe, and finding that he'd forgotten how to simply fall in.

He walked home along the river. Velin Ost in August smelled of cut hay and tar and frying onions, and the evening market was setting up in the square, and a boy selling roasted chestnuts—out of season, out of region, probably stolen—called out to him by name. "Diver! Diver Voss! Chestnuts?" Petra bought a twist of paper full of them because the boy had used his name, and names were still, after everything, a currency he couldn't quite stop collecting.

His rooms were above a chandler's shop near the water stairs. Two rooms, whitewashed, sparse. On the wall by the window hung the only thing he'd salvaged from the whole affair for himself: nothing from the chest, nothing from the wreck. A small brass sounding weight, the kind used to measure depth, bought for two coppers from a junk stall the week he arrived. He'd hung it on a nail because it was honest. It only ever told you how deep you were.

He sat by the window with the chestnuts going cold and took out Marrow's letter.

He read it slowly, the way he read wrecks.

*You will not remember me kindly, and you should not. I hunted you across half a country for reasons I told myself were duty, and they were not, or not only. My father sold a fleet and a nation, and I spent my career guarding the men who helped him do it, calling it order. You know what I was. You carried the proof of it in your arms through a mountain pass while I tried to take it from you.*

*I write to tell you three things. First: I have given my deposition to the inquiry here, freely, against counsel's advice. My name is now in the record beside my father's, where it belongs. Second: I do not ask forgiveness. I ask accuracy. When they write the histories, write that Captain Ilse Marrow of the Republican Navy did what she did for the worst reasons, and then, at the end, for one good one. That is the truest sentence about me I can construct.*

*Third. You teach divers now. I am told you teach them to read honestly. Good. Teach them this also: the current always seems like the enemy when you're fighting it, and it is only ever carrying you somewhere. I fought it for forty years. You stopped swimming against it and arrived. One of us learned to float.*

*The mountains here are not the Ashen Range, but they are mountains. There is a lake. I am told it has a bottom.*

*If you ever come north, you will know where to find me. If you never come, that too is an answer, and I will accept it as such.*

*— I.M.*

Petra read it twice, then folded it along its creases and set it on the sill in the last of the light.

A lake with a bottom. He almost laughed. Forty years of hunting men, and in the end she offered him the only gift she could imagine meaning anything: water, unguarded, with something at the bottom of it worth visiting.

He didn't answer letters. He hadn't answered a letter in ten years; answering meant an address, and an address meant a trail. But that rule had died somewhere on the far side of the pass, along with the others—the rule about never staying, the rule about never teaching, the rule about the name. Rules were ballast. He'd been jettisoning them for months now, and discovering he rose instead of sank.

He got up, found paper and pen—the chandler's daughter kept a stock of both and charged him fair prices—and sat back down.

*Captain,* he wrote. Then crossed it out. *Ilse.* Crossed that out too, and stared at the page until the honest version surfaced, the way honest things do when you stop thrashing.

*Marrow. The lake sounds cold. I'll come in September, when the students can spare me. Bring a sounding line. I'll bring the rest.*

*— Voss*

He read it over. It was short, undecorated, and true, which was, he had learned, the entire art of writing and possibly of living. He sealed it and set it by the door for the morning post.

Then he opened the window and looked out at the river going dark, and the lamps coming up along the quay one by one, and the market noise fading into the particular evening quiet of a town that expected tomorrow to resemble today. Somewhere downstream a bell rang the hour. Somewhere beyond the mountains, a republic was tearing itself open over pages he'd pulled from the mud, and would go on doing so for years, messily, imperfectly, with inquiries and counter-inquiries and men like Renner pensioned instead of shot—but open. Cracked wide. Light getting in.

None of it required him to run. None of it required him to hide. That was the discovery, the one he kept making over and over like a man relearning a language: stopping cost nothing. The current hadn't been chasing him. It had only ever been going somewhere, and now he had gone there too.

Down on the quay, faintly, he heard Marta's voice, scolding one of the younger students about iron straps and silt. His students. His quay. His name, spoken without fear by people who'd remember it tomorrow.

Petra Voss ate the last cold chestnut, blew out the lamp, and slept without dreaming of water at all.
