## 1. The Weight of Water

The debt ledger came on the last dry wind of September, delivered by a boy on a motorbike who wouldn't meet Felix Dunmore's eyes.

She read it standing in the doorway of her rented room above the parts shop in Corazón Flats, the paper trembling not from her hands but from the wind that had followed him in. Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve credits. Interest accruing. Property lien pending. The Vane Syndicate's seal at the bottom was a stylized raindrop inside a circle—elegant, corporate, obscene. A raindrop. In a country where it hadn't rained properly in eleven years.

Felix set the paper on the scarred desk, sat down on the edge of the bed with its mattress worn thin as an apology, and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since the chase season ended in wreckage outside Perdido Gap.

The Stormrunner was totaled. Her rig—the one she'd rebuilt twice with her own hands, the one that had carried her through nine seasons of chasing the cyclone belt's landfall storms for survey contracts and sensor-placement bounties—was now a twisted sculpture of salvage in a Syndicate impound yard. The season's earnings had gone to the tow operator, then the medic who'd stitched her scalp, then the fuel merchant who'd extended her credit through three bad months. What remained had gone into this room, this town, this slow sinking feeling that she was watching her own life from a great distance, through glass, unable to touch any of it.

Forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve.

Outside, the dust haze turned the afternoon light the color of old bone. Somewhere down the street a wind chime made of spent shell casings clinked its empty music. Felix had lived in places like Corazón Flats before—towns that existed because the mapmakers needed something between two larger emptinesses—and she knew their grammar fluently: the shuttered clinic, the water queue at dawn, the way everyone spoke about rain the way other people spoke about dead relatives. With love. With distance. Without expectation.

She measured her life in miles. It was a discipline she'd taught herself young, after the year that took her mother, and the year after that took the farm, and the year after that took everything else down to a duffel bag and a driver's license. Years only ever took things. Miles, at least, gave you something back—a horizon crossed, a storm threaded, a paycheck that bought another few hundred miles. You could trust a mile. It never pretended to be anything more than what it was.

So she counted: forty-one thousand credits, at chase-season rates, was roughly eleven thousand miles of contract work. At her current rate of employment—which was zero—it was infinity.

Which was why, when the message came through the relay office two days later offering her a contract that paid half the debt outright, she didn't ask enough questions.

* * *

The client was waiting for her at the Saltgrass Roadhouse, forty klicks east of town, sitting alone at a corner table with his back to the wall and a glass of water he wasn't drinking.

He was older than she expected. Older than anyone had a right to be out here. Seventies, maybe eighties, with a face like sun-cracked clay and hands folded on the table as if they'd been arranged there by someone else. An oxygen concentrator hummed beside his chair, its line running up under his collar, and his eyes were the pale, washed-out blue of glacial ice—which is to say, of something that belonged somewhere very far from here.

"You're Dunmore." Not a question. "They said you were the best storm driver left in the territory. They also said you wrecked your rig." His voice was dry as the roadhouse floor. "Both things can be true."

"I can drive anything with wheels," Felix said. "Rig's my problem."

"Your problem," he said, "is that you owe the Vanes forty-one thousand credits, and they don't forgive debts. They collect them." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit down, Ms. Dunmore. I've been dying for six months. I'd rather not do it standing up."

She sat. Up close she could see the illness written on him plainly—the gray undertone of his skin, the careful economy of his breathing, the way his wrists emerged from his sleeves thin as tools. But his eyes held steady, and there was something in them she recognized from mirrors: a person who had already done all their grieving in private and come out the other side into practicality.

"August Reyes," he said, and offered a hand dry and light as paper.

"Felix."

"I know. I asked about you. Nine seasons in the belt. You've threaded the eyewall at Catarina Wash twice, once in a category four. You placed sensors for the federal survey in '31 that nobody else would take." He smiled faintly. "I've spent fifty years reading weather the way priests read scripture, Ms. Dunmore. I know devotion when I see it in a service record."

"What's the job?"

"An expedition. Into the belt. Deep." He drew something from his jacket and laid it on the table between them—not unfolding it fully, just enough that she saw the corner of a hand-drawn map, ink faded to the brown of dried blood, contour lines dense as fingerprints. "There's a survey station out there. Abandoned. Pre-drought. Station Meridian, it was called. Nobody's been inside in fifty years."

"Survey stations are a dime a dozen in the badlands. Most of them are stripped to the studs."

"This one isn't. And it holds something worth more than gold." He said it without drama, which was the only reason she believed it. Con men performed; this man simply stated, the way you'd state the weight of a load or the depth of a ford. "Enough to clear your debt. Enough to buy your rig back and a new one besides. Enough that you'd never have to sell a season of your life to the Vanes again."

Felix looked at the map corner, at the contour lines climbing toward some high basin in the deep belt where even the dust devils thinned out from lack of anything to lift. "What's the catch?"

"The timing." He took a slow breath from the concentrator, and she watched him spend it deliberately, a man rationing his own body. "It has to be before autumn landfall. The big storm. Three weeks, maybe four, before the season's largest system makes its run at the coast and drags the whole belt into chaos behind it. After that, the roads drown and the window closes until spring." His pale eyes fixed on hers. "And I may not have until spring."

"How bad?"

"The doctors at the roadside clinic gave me a number. I told them to keep it." A ghost of humor moved across his cracked lips. "Let's say every day is borrowed, and I intend to spend them all in one place."

Felix leaned back. The roadhouse fan turned overhead, pushing hot air around without improving it. She thought about the ledger on her desk, the impound yard, the boy on the motorbike who wouldn't look at her. She thought about miles—how many this contract represented, how many it could buy her back.

"Escort work," she said slowly. "Get you in, get you out. That's it? No digging, no claims-jumping, no trouble with the Syndicate's territorial people?"

Something flickered behind his eyes then—fast, quickly buried, but she caught it. A man who knew exactly where the bodies were buried, and had just decided not to mention the graveyard.

"No trouble," August Reyes said, "that I can't account for."

Every instinct she owned, honed by nine seasons of reading skies and men, told her that sentence was a locked door. Every bill in her name told her to walk through it anyway.

* * *

She signed the contract that evening at the clinic, where August was staying in a cot by the eastern window because the roadhouse rooms cost too much and he said he liked being able to see the sky when he woke up. Even the sky here was a compromise—dust-hazed, drained of color, a ceiling instead of a heaven.

The terms were simple. Half the debt cleared on signature; the rest on completion. Her pay independent of whatever the station held, which should have reassured her and instead itched like a splinter. A man dying of nothing in particular didn't fund expeditions into the deep belt out of curiosity. Curiosity didn't look at you the way his eyes had when he said *worth more than gold*—not greedy. Afraid.

She signed anyway. Weary pragmatism, she told herself, was just grief that had learned to fill out paperwork.

August watched her sign with an expression she couldn't parse, and when she finished he said, quietly, "You didn't ask what happens if I die out there."

"I figured we'd both prefer not to find out."

"But if I do?"

Felix capped the pen and looked at him—at the oxygen line, the paper wrists, the ice-blue eyes that had seen fifty more years of this country than hers had. Outside the clinic window, the first stars were coming out over the badlands, hard and white and indifferent.

"Then I bring you home," she said. "However far that is. It's in the contract."

"It's not in the contract," August said. "I wrote the contract. There's no clause about my body." He smiled, and for a moment the illness fell away and she saw the young man he must have been, the surveyor's son or the surveyor himself, someone who had once walked these wastes with instruments and certainty. "I'm asking you anyway. Not the contract. You."

She should have refused. She should have kept it clean, professional, transactional—the way she kept everything, because transactions couldn't be taken from you the way mothers and farms and futures could. Miles, not years. Contracts, not promises.

But she was tired, and he was dying, and the desert outside was the same desert that had taken everything else, and something in her—some stubborn, buried, unprofitable thing—said yes before her arithmetic could stop it.

"All right," Felix said. "I'll bring you home. Whatever we find."

August closed his eyes, and exhaled, and looked—for the first time since she'd met him—like a man putting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Thank you," he said. "You'll want to start recruiting tomorrow. You can't run an expedition alone, and—" he paused, choosing words with a dying man's precision, "—you'll need someone who understands water. Real water. Where it hides and why it stopped showing up. Ask for Priya Nair. She used to work for the Bureau hydrology division, before the Bureau stopped wanting answers."

Felix frowned. "Before it stopped wanting answers?"

August turned his face to the window, to the starved sky, and his voice when it came was so quiet she almost missed it under the hum of the concentrator.

"The reason the rain stopped, Ms. Dunmore, is written down somewhere. I'm the last one alive who knows where."
