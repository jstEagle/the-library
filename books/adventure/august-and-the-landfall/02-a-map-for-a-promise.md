## 2. A Map for a Promise

The clinic smelled of iodine and old rain that had never fallen.

August Reyes lay on the cot in the back room of the Halverson Cross roadside clinic and listened to the building breathe. Every structure out here breathed if you knew how to hear it—canvas and corrugated tin expanding in the day's heat, contracting after sundown, ticking like a cooling engine. He'd spent fifty years listening to buildings breathe, to the ground hum under his boots before a storm arrived, to the particular silence a town made when its last well went dry. He was, he supposed, a man who had made a profession of listening to things that were about to end.

His lungs were doing it now. A wet whistle at the bottom of each breath, faint but getting less so. The clinic's one doctor—a tired woman named Okoye who had come out from the coast on a water-relief rotation and never quite left—had told him what the shadow on his chest film meant without using any ugly words. She'd said *advanced* and she'd said *weeks, not months*, and then she'd looked at him with the particular kindness people reserve for the dying, which August hated more than the diagnosis itself.

So. Weeks.

He turned his head on the flat pillow and watched the dust motes drift through the light above the doorway. Somewhere out front, the storm chaser woman was arguing with the clinic's generator.

He could hear her through two walls: a low, clipped voice, patient with machinery the way some people were patient with children. *No, not the intake—the coupling. You're flooding it.* Then a pause, then the generator coughed, caught, and settled into a steady chug, and the lights overhead steadied from brown to white. He heard her say something else, quieter, maybe to herself, and he thought: *there*. That was the voice of someone who fixed things because fixing them was easier than feeling anything about them.

Felix Dunmore. He'd done his asking around, same as she'd no doubt done hers. Wrecked chase season. Rig totaled outside Corazon Flats when a microburst dropped out of a clear sky—a freak thing, an act of God, except nothing out here was an act of God anymore, though only a handful of living people knew why. Debt to the Vane Syndicate deep enough that they'd taken her salvage rights as collateral. A reputation for being the best driver in the belt and for having no one, anywhere, waiting on her to come home.

That last part was why he'd chosen her. Not despite it. Because of it.

* * *

She came into the back room without knocking, which he approved of. Knocking was for people with something left to protect.

"You'll want to sit," August said. "I'm about to make you an offer you'll want to refuse slowly."

"I don't do escort work." She stayed by the door. Tall, weathered, maybe thirty-five, with the sun-cracked hands of someone who'd gripped a wheel through too many seasons and the flat gray eyes of someone who counted exits when she entered a room. "I chase. I don't chauffeur."

"Good. I'm not hiring a chauffeur." He pushed himself up against the wall, and the effort cost him more than he let show—he'd gotten good at hiding costs over the years; it was practically the family trade—and nodded at the chair beside the cot. "Sit down, Ms. Dunmore. I've got a map I need to show you, and maps are like storms. You don't read them standing up."

Something flickered across her face at the word *map*. He watched her weigh it. He'd bet everything he had left—which wasn't much, and wasn't money—that she was already calculating what a map might fetch, whether it could be leveraged, sold, traded against the number the Syndicate held over her. Good. Let her think this was a transaction. Transactions were safe. Promises were not, and he needed her to arrive at the promise sideways, the way you approached a spooked animal.

She sat. The chair complained.

"Reyes," she said. "August Reyes. You were Syndicate survey, thirty years back. Before they blacklisted you."

"Before I blacklisted myself," he corrected. "Small distinction. Large consequences." He reached under the cot, slow, letting her watch his hands the whole way—she struck him as a woman who watched hands—and brought out the oilcloth bundle.

It was wrapped twice, tied with waxed cord gone soft as string cheese from handling. Fifty years of handling. His father's hands had touched it, and his grandfather's before that, though the map itself was younger than all of them. He set it on the blanket between them and didn't untie it yet. Anticipation was a tool. He'd learned that from the Syndicate men who'd taught him surveying, back when he still believed they were teaching him something worth knowing.

"What is it?"

"A route." He began untying the cord. "Into the deep belt. Past Meridian Wash, past the salt pans, to a place that isn't on any chart the Syndicate sells or any county keeps."

"The belt's dead," Felix said. "Everyone knows that. Nothing out there but dry towns and dry holes and the storm corridor."

"Everyone knows that," August agreed, "because everyone was taught it." He got the last knot free and unfolded the oilcloth, and there it was: the map, drawn in ink faded to the color of weak tea, on paper that had been folded and refolded along the same creases so many times the creases had become geography of their own. Little mountain ranges of wear. He smoothed it flat across his knees with both hands, the way you'd calm a bird.

Felix leaned in despite herself. He saw her do it—saw the professional catch up with the caution, the tracker's eye taking over. Her gaze moved across the contour lines, the hand-inked elevations, the small precise annotations in a draftsman's script. He watched her recognize quality. Whatever else the Vane Syndicate had taken from her, they hadn't taken her ability to read terrain off a page.

"This is survey-grade," she said slowly. "Pre-drought drafting standards. Where did you get pre-drought originals?"

"I drew some of it." He tapped a section near the center, where the lines converged on a marked depression ringed by elevation readings. "The rest I inherited. My grandfather surveyed this country when it still had rivers in it. Real ones. And my father worked the last of the Syndicate's deep surveys, before the company decided the interior was worthless and pulled everyone out." He looked up at her. "They didn't pull out because it was worthless, Ms. Dunmore. They pulled out because of what the surveys found."

Her eyes came up from the map to his face. Flat, wary, reading him now the way she'd read the sky.

"And what's that?"

"A station. Station Meridian. Buried, mostly—sand took it within ten years of abandonment, the way sand takes everything. But the structure's sound underneath. Concrete and steel, built to last, built by men who expected to come back." He paused. "And inside it, there's the reason the rain stopped."

The generator chugged out front. A moth ticked against the window screen, drawn by the light, dying by inches against the mesh. August let the silence stretch until Felix broke it, because he'd known she would—she was a woman who filled silences with questions rather than comfort, and that was going to be useful.

"That's a story," she said. "People tell stories about the drought. It sells water futures."

"It does." He folded his hands over the map. "I was twenty-six years old when I helped carry the last crates out of Station Meridian. I was young, and I was ambitious, and I was told that what we were sealing away was proprietary geological data. Trade secrets. I believed it because believing was easier than the alternative, and because they paid my mother's medical bills." His voice came out level. He'd rehearsed this part for fifty years and it still came out level, which was its own kind of shame. "It took me another decade to understand what I'd helped bury. Another decade after that to find someone I thought might survive the trip back."

"So sell it to them," Felix said. "You found your buyer. I'm sitting right here."

"No." He shook his head, and the motion cost him a coughing fit, deep and wet, that bent him double. She shifted forward—half-rising, hands half-out—then caught herself and sat back, and he noted that too. Instinct first. Caution second. Underneath the flat gray eyes and the exit-counting, there was a person who moved toward trouble instead of away from it. That was rarer than driving skill. That was the whole game.

When he could breathe again, he said, "Not selling. Hiring. One contract. You take me and whatever crew I can scrape together into the belt, to these coordinates, before the autumn landfall seals the corridor. We go in, we see what's there, and then—" he held her gaze—"we bring the expedition home. All of it. Whatever we find."

"Whatever we find," she repeated. "Including whatever's valuable enough that the Syndicate blacklisted you over it fifty years ago."

"Including that."

"And you're not selling it to me, or to anyone. We just... look at it."

"We document it. We carry it out. And then we decide—together—what happens next." He watched her jaw tighten. He could see the arithmetic running behind her eyes: debt, interest, the Syndicate's collectors, the wrecked season, the years of staying small and moving fast and owing everyone. He could see her wanting to name a price, because prices were clean and promises rotted.

"Name your fee," she said finally. "I can't pay much. But I can front fuel against the salvage of whatever we find, and—"

"Ms. Dunmore." He said it gently, and something in the gentleness made her stop. "Look at me. I'm sixty-eight years old and I have weeks. What would I do with your money? Buy a nicer cot?" He laid his palm flat on the map, over the marked depression, over the buried station, over the reason the rain stopped. "Here's my fee. Here's the whole contract. You give me your word—not signed, not witnessed, just given, the old way—that you bring this expedition home. Everyone who goes in comes out. Whatever we find, however bad it gets, however much it costs. You don't leave anyone in that belt, and you don't trade any of us for anything. That's the price. That's the only price."

Felix Dunmore sat very still.

He watched her fight it. He'd spent a career reading weather and he recognized the signs of a different kind of storm building behind her face: the flinch of a woman being asked to promise something she couldn't control, by a man she'd known for eleven minutes, in a room that smelled of iodine. She wanted to laugh it off. She wanted to walk out. He could see both impulses standing in the doorway behind her like hired men.

"Why me?" she asked at last. Quietly. "There are better-funded crews. There are crews that wouldn't blink at whatever's in that station."

"Because they'd blink in the wrong direction," August said. "A funded crew takes orders. Orders change. And a crew that doesn't blink has already been bought." He tapped the map once. "I need someone with nothing left to lose except her word. Word's the only collateral that appreciates out here, Ms. Dunmore. Everything else depreciates. Ask the Syndicate—they've built an empire on it."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the wind had come up, pushing grit against the clinic's west wall in long soft sibilant strokes, the sound the desert made when it was thinking about weather. The moth beat itself against the screen. The generator chugged.

"My word," she said finally, "isn't worth much. People who deal with me tend to end up owed."

"People who deal with the Syndicate end up owed," August said. "Different thing entirely." He held out his hand, palm open, across the map. "One word. Bring us home. That's all I'm asking, and it's the only thing I've asked anyone for in fifty years."

She looked at his hand. She looked at the map—at the contours, the converging elevations, the little inked depression where something waited under the sand with the weight of a drowned sea and the gravity of a crime. He watched her decide, and he watched the decision cost her, and he respected her for both.

"If I take this contract," she said, "and you die out there—"

"I will die out there," August said simply. "Probably. That's not the risk, Ms. Dunmore. That's the schedule. The risk is what happens to what we find if nobody carries it home. So. Do we have terms?"

The wind pressed against the wall. Somewhere down the road, a dog started barking at nothing, or at the pressure drop, or at the season turning.

Felix Dunmore took his hand. Her grip was dry and hard and brief, a handshake like a signature on a bill of lading, and she said, "We have terms. Everyone comes home."

"Everyone comes home," August agreed, and felt something in his chest loosen that had nothing to do with lungs. Fifty years alone with a secret is a long time to hold your breath. He hadn't realized how long until this moment, when he finally exhaled.

She stood, folding the map along its worn creases with surprising care, and handed it back to him—or started to. He closed her fingers over it.

"Yours now," he said. "Guard it like it's alive. It's the only one."

She tucked it inside her jacket, and he saw her hand press flat against it once, unconsciously, the way you check a wound or a wallet, and he thought: *good, she's already protecting it.*

At the door she stopped. Didn't turn all the way—just her head, the gray eyes catching the light.

"The reason the rain stopped," she said. "That's not a figure of speech, is it."

August lay back against the pillow, suddenly, enormously tired, and smiled for the first time in days.

"No," he said. "It's coordinates."

She held his gaze a moment longer—measuring him, measuring the distance between his words and their weight, finding, he suspected, that the two matched—and then she was gone, boot heels on linoleum, the front door swinging shut behind her.

August stared at the ceiling and listened to the clinic breathe, and beneath the generator's chug and the wind's long thinking, he heard it: far off, past the salt pans, past the dead towns, a low rolling murmur like the earth clearing its throat. The belt, waking up for the season. The landfall coming, weeks out, patient as a creditor.

He had weeks too. Enough, if the weather held. Enough, if she held.

He closed his eyes and began, silently, to recite coordinates he'd sworn for fifty years he'd die before speaking aloud.
