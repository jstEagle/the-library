## 3. Crew of Strays

The trouble with hiring people, Felix had learned, was that they stayed.

She sat in the back booth of the Saltgrass Diner in Merrow's Bend with a legal pad and a pen that leaked, making lists the way other people made prayers—stubbornly, without much faith. The convoy needed three things she didn't have: a water scientist who could read the badlands' dead rivers, a mechanic who could keep two trucks alive on duct tape and spite, and money, which no amount of listing would conjure.

August slept in the cab of her truck outside, his breath fogging the glass in shallow, uneven pulses. The clinic in Merrow's Bend had given him pills and a prognosis and neither had cost anything, which told Felix everything about how long he had left.

*Hydrologist*, she wrote, and underlined it twice until the paper tore.

There was exactly one hydrologist within four hundred miles, and Felix knew where she was because everyone in the drought towns knew where Priya Nair was: teaching night classes in a converted grain elevator to farmers' kids who wanted to know why their wells tasted like rust. Once, Nair's name had been on the federal register of licensed water scientists for the whole eastern basin. Then there'd been a hearing, a revoked license, a scandal involving data she claimed she hadn't falsified and couldn't prove she hadn't. The Syndicate's lawyers had done the proving for her.

Felix found her at the grain elevator at dusk, chalk dust on her sleeves, erasing an aquifer diagram from a slate board while six teenagers watched her like she was the last interesting thing left in the world.

"Dr. Nair."

"Don't." She didn't turn around. "I'm not a doctor. I'm a woman with a bucket of chalk and opinions." But she turned around anyway, and looked Felix over with the flat professional assessment of someone used to being handed problems. "You're Dunmore. The storm chaser. You wrecked a rig at Cane Hollow last spring."

"The wind wrecked it. I just signed for it."

"That's what we all say about our wrecks." She set down the eraser. "What do you want?"

Felix laid it out plainly, because Nair struck her as a woman allergic to varnish. An expedition into the cyclone belt. A dying prospector with a map. A survey station nobody had reached in fifty years. No budget worth mentioning, no insurance, no extraction plan if things went wrong—which, in the belt during landfall season, they would.

"And you need me," Nair said, "because if this station is what Reyes thinks it is, you'll need someone who can tell groundwater from a mirage."

"Something like that."

Nair wiped her hands slowly on her trousers, and Felix watched her do the arithmetic—the same arithmetic Felix did every time she looked at August sleeping in the truck. *Not enough seasons left to be careful anymore.*

"I'll come," Nair said. "On one condition. When we find whatever we find, I get to publish it. Under my own name. Even if the license never comes back."

"You're publishing findings from an expedition that hasn't happened yet."

"I'm publishing my reputation back," Nair said. "The findings are incidental." She picked up her coat, said something quiet to the teenagers—*same time Thursday, don't let Mr. Aldous flood the model again*—and walked out with Felix into the blue dark, and that was that. Two strays now.

*

Theo Okonkwo she didn't hire at all. That was the thing she'd have to explain later, to whoever asked how a fifteen-year-old ended up in the cyclone belt, and she still didn't have a good answer except that some people are born already halfway out the door.

She met him at Ruiz's salvage yard on the edge of town, where she'd gone to buy axles and got talked instead into buying a story. Theo was small and quick and grease went up past his elbows like he'd been dipped, and he diagnosed her lead truck's transmission by sound alone, leaning into the wheel well with his eyes shut like a doctor listening to a heart.

"Third gear's dying," he said. "Not dead. Dying. You've got maybe eight hundred miles of gentle driving or two hundred miles of whatever you're actually planning."

"And what am I actually planning?"

He opened his eyes and looked at her, and there was nothing boyish in the look at all. "Belt run," he said. "Landfall season. Everybody who comes through Ruiz's going north this month is either running from something or toward it, and your truck's too loaded for running." He wiped his hands. "I can fix third gear. I can fix it better than new, if you give me two days and access to your parts budget."

"My parts budget is forty dollars and a bag of washers."

"Then I'll work for the ride." He said it fast, before she could refuse, and then kept talking, faster, the way people do when they know the door is closing. "My uncle owns this yard. Technically I'm apprenticed. Actually I'm free labor, and when he dies the bank takes the yard, and I'll be standing in a parking lot holding a wrench with nowhere to use it. I know engines. I know storms—I've watched 'em off the ridge since I was six. I'm not asking for wages. I'm asking to be useful."

"No," Felix said.

It wasn't even a hard no. It was reflexive, the no of a woman who measured every attachment against its eventual cost. A kid in the belt, in landfall season, with hunters possibly behind them—she could do that math too, and the answer came up in red.

"He'll slow us down," she told Nair that evening, as they loaded fuel drums in the yard's amber sodium light. "He'll get hurt. Or worse."

"Yes," Nair agreed mildly, handing her a strap. "Probably. People get hurt in the belt regardless. At least this one would get hurt doing something he chose." She cinched the drum tight. "You asked me why I'm coming. Ask yourself why you're arguing so hard against someone you haven't said yes to yet."

Felix didn't have an answer, so she threw a tarp over the drums and hated how seen she felt.

*

They left Merrow's Bend on a morning the color of skim milk, three vehicles and four souls—or three souls and a stowaway, though nobody knew that yet. August rode in Felix's truck, propped upright among bedrolls, mapping the sky through the windshield with his eyes and murmuring pressure readings like a man saying grace. Nair took the second truck with the water-testing kit strapped down like ordnance. The third vehicle was a flatbed they'd bought for parts, driven by nobody, towed behind Nair's truck on a chain that clanked a rhythm Felix would hear in her sleep for weeks.

They made sixty miles the first day, following the ghost of the old Meridian highway through country that had once been wheat and was now a lesson in patience. Dead orchards stood in rows like congregations waiting for a sermon that stopped coming decades ago. A church roof had folded in on itself, soft as wet cardboard. Wind moved the dust in long slow shoals across the road, and Felix drove through it thinking about miles, only miles, because miles were honest—they added up, they didn't take anything away.

It was Nair who noticed the boy.

"Your flatbed driver," she said over the radio that evening, dry as the landscape, "is doing a remarkable job steering a vehicle he isn't sitting in."

Felix pulled over so fast the trailer chain screamed. She walked back along the flatbed's shadowed side, and there, wedged between the spare axle and a crate of August's instruments, wrapped in a moving blanket with a toolbox hugged to his chest like a teddy bear, was Theo Okonkwo, asleep with his mouth open and his cap over his eyes.

She stood over him for a long moment, feeling the anger arrive and then, annoyingly, keep going somewhere else entirely.

He woke the way animals wake—all at once, coiled—and saw her, and didn't apologize, which she later decided was the most honest thing anyone had done all week.

"Before you say anything," he started.

"You stowed away on a vehicle towed by a woman who knows you're fifteen."

"You didn't say no to the *ride*. You said no to the *wages*. There's a difference. Legally speaking—" He caught her expression and abandoned the argument. "Look. I left a note. My uncle knows I'm with you. Sort of. The note says I'm with you."

"The note says—"

"It says *gone with Dunmore, back when the season turns.* Which is true. Directionally."

Felix looked at him for a long time. Behind him the sun was going down over the dead orchards, and the light came through the gaps in the trees in bars, gold falling on nothing, and she thought: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it starts. One old man with a map, one disgraced scientist, one kid in a toolbox nest, and before you know it you've got something that can be taken from you.*

Turning back meant a day lost, and days were the one currency August didn't have. Turning back also meant admitting that the kid had read her correctly—that underneath the pragmatism she'd already counted him as crew, had maybe counted him from the moment he named her transmission's death sentence with his eyes closed.

"Rules," she said. "You drive when I say. You wear a harness when I say. When I tell you to leave a vehicle, you leave the vehicle, and you don't argue, and you don't come back for tools. Am I understood?"

"Yes ma'am." He was already climbing out, grinning, and the grin was the worst part, because it was warm, and warmth was the thing she'd spent ten years learning to live without and apparently ten minutes to lose.

"And Theo. If your uncle calls the law on me, I will personally deliver you back to that salvage yard tied to the axle."

"He won't call." Theo shouldered his toolbox, suddenly quieter. "Nobody's calling anybody. That's kind of the point of me."

They rolled out at first light the next morning, four of them now, the flatbed demoted to cargo and the chain's clank softened with rags Theo had wound around the links before anyone asked him to. By noon the land had changed—the last fence posts gave way to open range, the horizon flattened, and far to the northwest a smudge stood on the sky that might have been cloud and might have been dust and was, Felix knew, the first breath of the storm belt.

August tapped the glass. "There," he said softly. "That's the edge of it. Everything past that line, the weather belongs to itself."

Nair pulled alongside and matched speed, and through both windows she mouthed something to Felix, something she'd been chewing on since Merrow's Bend, judging by her face. Felix cracked her window to the wind.

"The rainfall records," Nair called. "I pulled what's public before we left. The drought line doesn't follow the geology, Felix. It follows the *property lines*. Every place the rain stopped, the Syndicate owned the water rights after." Her eyes were bright and troubled. "Whatever your prospector's map leads to—it isn't treasure. It's a confession."

Ahead, the smudge on the horizon thickened, and began, very slowly, to turn.
