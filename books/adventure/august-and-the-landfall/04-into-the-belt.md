## 4. Into the Belt

Priya Nair had spent eleven years learning to read water, and the land had spent three days teaching her that it had forgotten how.

She rode in the second vehicle—Felix's patched-together chase truck, the one with the cracked windshield and the anemometer bolted to the roof like a flag of surrender—with her knees against a crate of sample vials and August Reyes breathing shallow in the seat beside her. Through the glass the badlands unrolled in shades of bone: pale clay, bleached rock, the ghost-white crust of alkali flats where lakes had died standing up. Dust devils walked the horizon in loose processions, dozens of them, tall as church steeples, spinning slowly like things deciding whether to pray or curse.

"Beautiful," Theo said from the back, where he was wedged among fuel cans with his boots up on a jerry-rigged shelf. He had the tone of a boy who meant it. Sixteen years old and he looked at a dead country the way other people looked at cathedrals.

"It's not beautiful," Priya said. "It's wrong."

She hadn't meant to say it out loud. But Felix glanced at her in the rearview—one quick flicker of grey eyes—and didn't argue, which was worse than arguing. Felix Dunmore drove the way she did everything: with the economy of someone who had stopped expecting the world to give refunds.

They crossed into the belt at midmorning, past a leaning sign that read WELCOME TO HARROW FLATS — POP. 1,204, though the town itself was a rumor. A water tower stood over nothing, its legs buckled, its tank split along one seam so that rust wept down it in long brown tears—the only moisture in forty miles. A church had lost its roof but kept its steeple, and swallows poured out of the belfry when the convoy rattled past, a black exhalation against the white sky. Somewhere a wind chime still hung on a porch, turning without sound because there wasn't enough wind to ring it, only enough to move it. That detail lodged in Priya like a splinter. Even the air here was rationed.

* * *

She ran the numbers at the noon stop, crouched in the shade of the lead truck with her field tablet propped against a tire, pulling down the regional archive while Theo kept watch for signal towers they both knew weren't there.

The drought record went back sixty years, and she'd seen versions of it before—in grant applications, in disaster assessments, in the polite euphemisms of committee reports. *Persistent aridification. Multi-decadal precipitation deficit. Climatological anomaly.* The kind of language scientists used when they wanted to describe a wound without accusing anyone of holding the knife.

But she had never plotted it herself. Not like this.

The rainfall series should have been noisy. That was the first thing. Real climate is chaos wearing a pattern like a loose coat—wet years stacked on dry ones, spikes and troughs, noise you smooth at your peril. What lay on her screen instead was a cliff. Forty years of ordinary variance, and then, in a single decade, a drop so clean and so total that the graph looked less like weather than like surgery. Precipitation hadn't declined across the region. It had been *removed*, uniformly, across every gauge in a two-hundred-mile radius—all at once, all together, as if a hand had come down over the whole basin and pressed.

And the wells. She pulled the aquifer drawdown data next, and her mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with the heat. The water table hadn't fallen the way falling water falls—slowly, unevenly, responding to use. It had been drawn down in a coordinated cone, deepest at the center, radiating outward in perfect concentric rings centered on a point in the deep badlands. Centered, when she overlaid the coordinates, on a survey grid marker she recognized from August's map.

Nobody pumped like that by accident. Nobody's drought fell like a curtain.

"Priya." Felix's voice, low, behind her shoulder. "You've been staring at that screen for twenty minutes."

Priya turned the tablet toward her. Felix studied it the way she studied storm cells—jaw tight, eyes moving in quick professional sweeps, cataloguing threat.

"This isn't a drought," Priya said. "Droughts have texture. This has *edges*. Someone pulled the water table down in a controlled cone, and the rainfall collapse tracks the same decade exactly. I've modeled watershed failure my whole career. Natural systems don't do this. They can't."

Felix was quiet for a long moment. A dust devil wandered through the camp, scattering grit against the trucks, and nobody moved to brush it off.

"You're saying somebody did this," Theo said. "On purpose. To—" He stopped, because the end of the sentence was lying all around them in the dead town, in the split water tower, in the silent wind chime.

"I'm saying the numbers are man-made," Priya said carefully. "What I don't know is why. Or who could even *do* it." And then, because she couldn't stop herself, because the hydrologist in her was screaming: "You'd need control of every major diversion upstream. You'd need decades and capital and legal cover. You'd need to be—" 

"The Syndicate," Felix said flatly. "Vane holds the water rights for half this territory. They've been selling relief shipments to these towns at interest for twenty years."

The words sat in the heat between them. Priya watched Felix's face do something complicated—not surprise, she realized. Recognition. The look of a woman fitting a new piece into a puzzle she'd already suspected was ugly.

"August knows," Priya said. "That's what's out here. That's what he's walking us toward."

Felix closed the tablet. "We're four hundred miles from anywhere and committed either way. Get some sleep. We roll at dawn."

* * *

But August didn't sleep, and neither, really, did the night.

Priya heard him coughing in the lead truck's cab where he'd insisted on keeping his bedroll, that wet, tearing sound she'd been pretending not to catalogue for three days—the rattle in the lower lobes, the cyanosis lurking at his nail beds, the way his sentences came shorter now, rationed like everything else in this country. She climbed down from her bunk and found him sitting up in the dark, map spread across his knees, not reading it. Reciting it.

"Meridian station sits under the third bench of the Serrano escarpment," he said, before she'd even fully climbed in. His voice was gravel rolled in honey, worn smooth by fifty years of saying things to no one. "Approach from the north cut. The old road's buried but the culverts mark it—look for the twin stacks. Coordinates are thirty-four point one eight eight, negative one-oh-nine point seven seven two. Say it back."

"August—"

"*Say it back.*"

She said it back. He made her say it twice more, then made her write it in two places—in her notebook and inside the cover of his map—and initial both.

"In case I get sloppy," he said.

"You're not going to get sloppy. You're going to see a doctor in—"

"In what?" He almost smiled. In the cab's dim amber light his face was all planes and hollows, a topographic map of a life lived outdoors. "Girl, I've been dying since before you filed your first grant application. The only variable is whether I die useful." He tapped the notebook. "There's more. Sub-level access is behind the core library, east wall. Combination's a date—March ninth, 1961. You'll understand when you see it. And Priya—" he caught her wrist, and his grip was still strong, still prospector-strong, iron wrapped in paper, "—when we get there, you'll want to run samples first. You'll want data. Data's safe. Don't hide in it. Some things you have to look at straight."

Her throat tightened. "What's down there, August?"

He looked past her, out the windshield at the stars over the belt—more stars than she'd ever seen, because the towns were dead and the lights had gone with them—and for a moment he looked less like a dying old man than like a young surveyor standing at the edge of something enormous.

"The reason the rain stopped," he said. "I've been carrying it for fifty years. It's heavier than any ore." He let go of her wrist. "Get some sleep. We make the escarpment tomorrow if the sky holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we outrun it," he said, "or we learn what we're made of. Either way, write the coordinates down again in the morning. Memory's a thief."

* * *

The sky held until midafternoon, and then it didn't.

Priya felt it before anyone said anything—a pressure change in her ears, a taste of ozone and hot mineral in the air, the particular hush she'd spent her career chasing and now, for the first time, feared. On the southern horizon, where the belt went violet and endless, a storm was building with the patience of something that had done this ten thousand times. Towering cumulonimbus, anvil spreading like ink in water, and beneath it a brown curtain of dust and rain that erased the line between earth and sky.

Felix stood on the lead truck's roof with binoculars, reading it the way priests read scripture. "Landfall system. Early, and mean. Two days out, maybe less." She dropped down. "We reach the escarpment tonight or we're driving in that."

They drove.

The last hundred miles blurred into a litany of dead places—Corazon Wells, where the school gymnasium roof had collapsed into a swimming pool gone to cracked mosaic; Dos Rios, where a hundred empty houses faced a dry river confluence like mourners facing an open grave; a crossroads store with a hand-painted sign still legible after all these years: *ICE • WATER • COLD DRINKS*. Somebody had painted a second sign beneath it, newer, angrier, letters dripping: *WHERE DID IT GO?*

Theo read that one aloud, quietly, and then didn't talk for an hour.

August stopped speaking altogether somewhere past Dos Rios. When Priya checked on him he was gray-lipped and burning, and he pressed his map case into her hands with both of his, folding her fingers over the leather.

"All of it's in your head now," he rasped. "I made sure of that. Now it's in your hands too." His eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "One more thing to memorize, girl. The reason the rain stopped—that's not just a thing you find. It's a thing that finds *you*. When it does, remember whose fault it is. Remember who signed."

"Signed what? August, who signed?"

But his eyes had rolled toward the storm, and whatever answer he had was swallowed by the first far-off growl of thunder rolling up out of the belt like a door being opened somewhere underground.

That night they camped in the shadow of the Serrano escarpment, a black wall of stone rising out of the dark, and Priya sat awake with her notebook full of coordinates she now knew by heart, listening to the storm build in the south. Wonder had brought her out here—an aquifer, a sea beneath the badlands, the discovery of a lifetime. But wonder had curdled somewhere back at Harrow Flats, and what filled her chest now was colder and sharper.

Because she had plotted the death of a hundred towns on her screen, and the numbers had edges, and edges meant hands, and hands meant someone had chosen this.

Tomorrow they would dig into the escarpment and find out whose.

Above them, the first fat drops of a dying storm hit the canvas—three drops, then five, then nothing, a mockery of rain—and in the silence afterward, August began, very softly, to recite numbers in his sleep.
