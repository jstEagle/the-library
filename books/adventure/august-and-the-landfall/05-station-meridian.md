## 5. Station Meridian

The land told us we were close before the instruments did.

For three days the badlands had been folding themselves into stranger and stranger shapes—hoodoos like drowned men, mesas split by lightning scars, washes where the flash floods of some wetter century had left their signatures in stone. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, the dust changed. It went from the usual pale ochre to something darker, richer, almost black in the hollows, and Priya knelt and rubbed a pinch of it between her fingers and said nothing for a long time.

"That's not desert soil," she finally said. "That's lakebed."

"Lakebed," Theo repeated, from under the hood of the water truck. "Out here?"

"There was water here. A lot of it. Not long ago, geologically speaking." She stood, wiping her hands on her thighs, and looked at August through the truck's open window with an expression I couldn't read. "August. What did they survey out here?"

August smiled the way he'd been smiling more and more lately—the smile of a man who had stopped rationing his secrets because he could see the bottom of the jar. "Keep driving, Felix. You'll see it yourself better than I can tell it."

So I drove.

The convoy crawled north along the dry spine of an ancient riverbed, three vehicles in single file: my chase rig in the lead, the water truck behind, and Theo's patched-together scout bike ranging ahead and doubling back like a nervous dog. The sky was doing something I didn't like—building heat over the flats, stacking cumulus on the western horizon like plates on a shelf. Storm season wasn't done with us yet. The autumn landfall was still a week out by every model I trusted, but the belt didn't care about models. The belt cared about nothing at all.

By midday the riverbed walls rose around us, red sandstone streaked with white mineral lines—old waterlines, high-water marks from a sea that had been drained away. And then Theo's voice crackled over the radio, thin with static and something else.

"Felix. You gotta see this. There's a door in the cliff."

* * *

It wasn't a door, exactly. It was a breach—a seam in the canyon wall where the rock had been cut back and faced with poured concrete gone the color of old bone, half-swallowed by fifty years of sand and cryptobiotic crust. A loading ramp descended into darkness beneath it, wide enough for trucks, its surface drifted deep with dunes that had blown in and never blown out. Above the entrance, carved into a concrete lintel and nearly effaced by weather, letters:

**VANE HYDROLOGICAL SURVEY — STATION MERIDIAN — EST. 2041**

I sat in the cab and stared at that name for a long time. Vane. My debtors. The people who owned the paper on my rig, my season, my life. The people whose fixers had been polite to me in ways that always felt like hands resting lightly on my shoulders.

"You knew," I said to August.

"I knew there was a station." He was breathing hard just from the effort of sitting upright, and I hated how much I noticed it now, how every small labor cost him. "I didn't know what you'd find inside. Nobody knows what's inside. That's rather the point."

"The Syndicate built this."

"The Syndicate's *founders* built this. Before it was the Syndicate. Before it had a name worth fearing." He looked out at the canyon, at the white waterlines climbing the stone above our heads. "Felix. When we go down there, whatever you feel—and you will feel something—I need you to remember one thing. Data doesn't care who owns it. It only cares who carries it."

I killed the engine and the silence came down like a lid.

Theo cleared the ramp mouth with a shovel while Priya ran air readings—fifty years sealed, and even a buried station breathes, but you don't gamble on it. The air came back breathable, stale and mineral, smelling of cold stone and machine oil and something faintly organic, like the inside of a cave. We rigged lights. We went down.

The ramp sloped for what felt like a quarter mile, our headlamps carving cones out of the dark, our boots raising echoes that came back wrong—too many footsteps, as if the station were already inhabited by ghosts wearing our gait. And then the ramp leveled out, and the dark opened, and I stopped walking because my body simply refused to take another step until my mind caught up.

We were standing on a gantry platform above a cavern.

Not a room. A *cavern*, a man-made void the size of a cathedral, cut into the bedrock, and filling most of it was machinery—rank upon rank of great cylindrical tanks, pumps, valve manifolds thick as tree trunks, pipes running off into the dark in every direction like the roots of an iron forest. Dust lay over everything in a gray velvet. But the machines were intact. Untouched. Built and then abandoned, whole and new, like a ship launched and never sailed.

"It's a pumping station," Priya whispered. Her voice bounced strangely; the space swallowed sound and gave it back altered. "A big one. But—" she swept her light across the ranks of tanks, counting, recalculating, and I watched her face do something complicated. "But sized wrong. This capacity isn't for a town. This is for a *city*. Multiple cities. Who were they going to pump water *for*?"

"Down here," August said quietly. "Look down here first."

There was a stairway off the gantry, descending toward the cavern floor, and beside it a plaque, and beside the plaque a window—a thick porthole of glass set into the bedrock wall, lit now by our lamps. We crowded to it.

Below us, visible through the glass and the grid of a monitoring well, was water.

Not seepage. Not a sump. Water rising up the well casing, dark and patient and utterly still, a black mirror forty feet below us reflecting our own faces back at us. Priya dropped a sensor line without a word, and we watched the readout on her handheld climb as the probe descended: thirty feet, sixty, ninety, and the numbers kept coming, and her hands started shaking.

"Oh my God," she said. "Oh my God, it doesn't stop. Felix—it doesn't *stop*."

An aquifer. Not an aquifer—a sea. A fossil ocean trapped beneath the badlands, ancient beyond reckoning, enough water to green every drought town from the border to the coast for two hundred years. Enough water to end the drought. All of it, all these decades, lying a few hundred feet under land where children learned the word *dust* before they learned the word *rain*.

Theo made a sound I'd never heard from him—not a word, just a raw syllable, the noise a person makes when something too large gets inside them. He pressed his palm flat against the porthole glass, over the reflection of that black water, as if he could warm it.

"They knew," he said. "They always knew. And they let us—all those towns, my mom's town, they let everybody—"

"The ledgers," August said. "Records office. End of the gallery. That's why we're really here."

* * *

The records office was a low-ceilinged annex off the main cavern, steel shelving and climate-sealed cabinets, and it was there that awe died in me—there, among the paper, that I understood what we had actually driven into.

Priya found the hydrological surveys first, and read them aloud in a voice that got flatter with every page. Aquifer mapping, complete and meticulous, dated 2041 through 2044. Yield projections. Distribution schematics. And then the correspondence, and the correspondence was worse than anything.

Because the early letters weren't about finding water. They were about *timing*.

I stood under Priya's lamp and read a memo on onionskin paper gone brittle as moth wings, signed by a name I recognized from the Syndicate's own founding mythology—Elias Vane himself, the patriarch, the man whose portrait hung in every Syndicate office like a saint. The memo discussed the aquifer in the language of men discussing a harvest. It discussed the surface towns in the language of men discussing weather. And in its final paragraph, in phrases so bland they took a moment to turn to poison in the mind, it discussed *sequence*: the deliberate deflection and diversion of the region's surface flows, the upstream captures, the slow strangulation of rivers and springs that fed the valley towns—implemented not despite the discovery of the aquifer but *because* of it. Dry the land above. Make the rain fail. Make the wells die. Let the towns break, buy them for pennies, sell them water by the gallon forever.

The drought hadn't come. The drought had been *installed*.

Forty years of dead orchards. Forty years of towns like Theo's mother's, bleeding out one departing family at a time. Forty years of dust storms burying homesteads, of clinic waiting rooms full of kids with dust in their lungs—and underneath it all, this sea, this saved and hoarded sea, held back like a ransom.

"They poisoned the watershed," Priya said. She was very calm, which frightened me more than screaming would have. She held up a page. "Here. Chemical treatment of upstream catchments to accelerate salinization. They *salted the earth*, Felix. There are requisition orders. Invoices. Signatures."

"How many copies?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's. "How many of these exist?"

"This is the master archive. Carbon copies, originals, everything. If there's another set anywhere, no one's ever found it." She looked up at me, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet and furious. "Do you understand what this is? This isn't evidence. This is the only evidence. This room is the only reason the truth can exist."

I looked at August. He had lowered himself onto a supply crate, gray-faced, conserving breath, and he met my eyes with an expression I'd seen once before—on the face of a man handing me his map in a roadside clinic, asking not for money but for a promise.

"Now you know the reason the rain stopped," he said.

And I did know. And knowing it, I understood—with a cold, sliding certainty, the way you understand a storm cell has turned before the radio confirms it—that this knowledge was not a treasure. It was a weapon, primed and pointed, and it was pointed at every one of us standing in this room. Men had built a fortune on this silence. Men had let a region die for it. Such men do not leave loose pages lying in the desert out of sentiment. Either they believed this place was lost, or they believed anyone who found it would never leave it.

The station's comm panel was dead, but Theo, poking through the annex with the restless hands of a boy who cannot leave machinery alone, suddenly went quiet. Then he said, in a small voice:

"Felix. There's a maintenance log on the wall. Last entry's fifty years old. Except—" he turned his headlamp beam to a clipboard hanging by the door, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "Except somebody's been here recently. Look at the dust. Somebody's swept in here. Within the last month."

We stood in the lamplight, five hundred feet underground, holding the only proof of a forty-year crime, listening to the silence of Station Meridian change around us—from the silence of abandonment to the silence of being watched.

Far above us, faint as a heartbeat through the bedrock, I heard the wind pick up over the canyon mouth.
