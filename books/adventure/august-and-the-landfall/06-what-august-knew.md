## 6. What August Knew

The infirmary of Station Meridian smelled of dust and old antiseptic, a smell like the ghost of a promise nobody had kept. August Reyes lay on a cot that had been built for men half his weight and twice his hope, and he watched the water drip from the ceiling seam into a tin cup Priya had set beneath it. One drop. Another. Each one rang against the metal like a small bell in an empty church.

Fifty years, and he had never once heard water fall where it wasn't supposed to.

"Felix," he said, because his voice was going the way of the rest of him—thin, papery, a wind through a cracked window—and she was there before the second syllable finished. She was always there now. She slept in a chair dragged to his bedside, her boots on, her jacket over her shoulders like armor she'd forgotten to take off. He could have told her that armor doesn't work when you're sitting down. He didn't. Some kindnesses were better left unspoken.

"You're awake," she said. Not a question. She'd learned to read his breathing the way he'd once read cloud banks—by the smallest changes, the ones that meant everything.

"Barely." He tried for a smile. It came out crooked, which was honest at least. "Sit down before you wear a groove in my floor."

"It's not your floor."

"Everything down here is mine." He gestured at the dark beyond the doorway—the long corridors of Station Meridian, the humming silence of it, the sea of water sleeping under their feet like something out of scripture. "I signed for it. That's what I came to tell you."

She sat. The chair creaked. Outside, far above them, the badlands wind moved across the mouth of the canyon, and August thought about how sound traveled down through rock the way guilt traveled down through years—slowly, patiently, arriving eventually no matter how deep you buried it.

"I was twenty-three," he said.

* * *

He'd rehearsed this confession for five decades, in truck cabs and clinic waiting rooms and the gray hours before dawn, and every version of it had been a lie. In the versions he told himself, he'd been deceived. Duped. A good man caught in bad machinery. But lying flat on his back with death already reading over his shoulder, August found he only had the strength for the truth, and the truth was simpler and uglier: he had known. He had known exactly what he was doing, and he had done it anyway, because they paid well and he was young and the future seemed like a country other people would have to live in.

"The Vane Syndicate hired me as a junior surveyor in the spring of—" He stopped. Recalculated. "God. Fifty-one years ago. There were six of us on the watershed team. We mapped the headwaters of the Corrizal basin, all the feeder aquifers, the seasonal washes. Beautiful work. The best mapping of my life." His hands lay on the blanket like two dried riverbeds. "We weren't mapping it to protect it, Felix. We were mapping it to break it."

Felix said nothing. She was good at that—listening the way stone listens, taking everything in without giving anything back. It made her easy to confess to and hard to apologize to, and he suspected she'd heard both in equal measure over her lifetime.

"There was a fault line under the eastern recharge zone. Unstable. We flagged it in the first survey—a seismic risk, a fracture risk, the whole western slope's groundwater running through broken rock. Any competent engineer would have said: reinforce, reroute, leave the aquifer sealed." He closed his eyes. Behind them he could see the maps again, the beautiful terrible maps, blue ink on cream paper, his own careful lettering on the legend. "Instead we drilled the test bores anyway. Fourteen of them. Straight into the fracture zone. And then we pumped."

"Pumped," Felix said quietly.

"To prove it would fail. To make it fail. The Syndicate wanted the small towns dependent on their pipelines, and you can't sell water to people who have their own. So we broke theirs. Deliberately. Contaminated the shallow wells with drilling fluid, collapsed two of the recharge channels, and wrote the report saying the drought was coming regardless—that the region was dying naturally, and the only mercy was Vane water, sold by the gallon, forever." A drop fell into the cup. Another bell. "I drew the maps that proved the lie. My lettering. You've seen it. On the map I gave you."

Her face changed. He watched it happen—the slow understanding moving through her like weather.

"That's why you knew the coordinates," she said. "From memory. You weren't remembering a route. You were remembering your own crime scene."

"I was remembering the only honest thing in the whole file." He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and she held the cup to his lips without being asked. The water tasted of mineral and stone and fifty years underground. It was the sweetest thing he'd ever swallowed. "The station's real records are still here. Sealed in the archive vault, sublevel two. The original surveys. The pumping logs. Internal memos—I kept copies of those too, God forgive me, I kept souvenirs of my own damnation. Everything the Syndicate burned in the city offices survived down here, because the men who buried it were too arrogant to think anyone would ever come looking under a hundred meters of rock."

"And you ran."

"For fifty years." He said it plainly, because there was no softer way to say it. "Every town I passed through with its cracked earth and its ration lines, every kid with dust in their lungs—I did that. Not alone. But me too. And I told myself I'd come back someday and fix it, and someday is a place you can live your whole life without ever arriving."

* * *

He slept after that, or something near sleep, and dreamed of rain.

Not storm rain—chaser's rain, violent and brief—but the soft autumn kind he remembered from childhood, before the belt dried out, rain that fell all day and made the world smell like iron and green things. In the dream his mother stood in the doorway of their house calling him in from the mud, and he was young, and the ground drank everything the sky offered, and there was no such thing as a debt yet.

When he woke, the lamp had been turned low and Felix was still in the chair, awake, watching him with those eyes that measured everything in miles.

"How long do I have?" he asked her. "Honestly. You're the only one who'll say it."

"Days," she said. "Maybe less. Your heart's working at altitude it can't reach."

"Good." He saw her flinch and raised a hand, weak as a moth. "Don't. Don't apologize for telling me true. Everyone else in my life spent fifty years telling me comfortable lies, and look where it got the world." He worked himself up higher on the cot, and the effort cost him, and he paid it gladly. "Now listen. Because I haven't got the breath to say it twice."

She leaned in.

"Everyone's going to want you to bury this. The Syndicate will offer you money first, then threats, and they're very good at both—they've had fifty years of practice keeping this quiet. And some part of you, the part that's tired and broke and wants to survive, is going to want to take the deal. I know that part. I invented that part." His voice cracked; he pushed through it. "So I'm not asking you to keep a secret. Secrets are what killed these towns. I'm asking you to carry it home. All of it. The ledgers, the logs, the memos, my statement if I can get it written down. Carry it to people who'll use it—not to a court that'll lose it, not to a reporter who'll soften it. To the towns. Let the people who drank the poison be the ones who decide what happens next."

"That's not justice," Felix said slowly. "That's a bomb."

"It's both." He smiled, and this time it came out right. "Most true things are."

* * *

They brought him paper that evening—Theo found a ream of it in the station offices, yellowed but sound, and Priya produced a pen from somewhere in her endless pockets—and August wrote his confession by lamplight while the three of them pretended to be busy elsewhere and listened to every scratch of the nib.

It took him four hours. He had expected it to take his whole remaining life, and in a way it did. He wrote his name at the top the way he'd written it on the original surveys, and he wrote the dates, and the bore numbers, and the names of the other five surveyors (two dead, one retired rich, two he'd lost track of), and he wrote *we knew*, and *we were paid*, and *the drought was manufactured*, and each sentence came out of him like a splinter leaving flesh—painful, then cleaner, then painful again deeper in.

At the end he added a line for Felix alone, and folded that page separately:

*I am sorry. Not for dying—dying is just leaving, and everyone leaves. I am sorry for staying silent so long that leaving became the only honest act I had left. If the water comes back, let it fall on the towns first.*

Near dawn, with the station's air recyclers humming their old patient hymn, he called her close.

"Swear it again," he whispered. "Not the old oath. The new one."

Felix took his hand. Her grip was strong and dry and steady, a chaser's grip, a grip that had held onto roll bars through rotating walls of wind. "I swear," she said. "Whatever it costs. It goes home."

"Whatever it costs," he repeated, and held her eyes. "Say that part like you mean it, girl. They'll test it. Men like the Vanes don't send lawyers first—they send weather."

"I've outrun weather my whole life."

"No." His voice was almost gone now, a thread, a tide going out. "You've outrun weather that didn't know your name. This time it knows. This time it's coming for you specific." He squeezed her hand with the last strength he had, which was more than she expected, enough to feel it. "And when it comes—when you see it on the horizon and every instinct you own screams at you to run and save yourself—you remember an old man in a hole in the ground who ran for fifty years, and where running got him. Dying clean is worth more than living hidden. That's the whole lesson. That's everything I know."

He slept then, truly, and Felix sat in her chair and watched the water drip into the tin cup, drop by drop, each one a small bell ringing in the dark.

Above them, far to the south, over the warm drowned coast where the season's great storms are born, the pressure began to fall.

August died three days later, in the last hour of the night, between one drop and the next.
