## 7. Hunters on the Horizon

Theo Okonkwo had learned to read engines the way other kids learned to read faces, and by the third day in the belt he could tell you what the convoy was thinking just by putting his palm flat on a hood.

The lead truck — Felix's rig, a battered chase vehicle she called *Magpie* because it collected parts from prettier machines — was running hot and running honest, both of which worried him. Hot he could fix. Honest meant it wasn't hiding anything, which meant when something finally broke, it would break all at once, without warning, the way honest things did. He preferred liars. Liars gave you time.

He was lying under Magpie's chassis at the fuel cache with a wrench in his teeth and dust sifting into his collar when he heard the sound that didn't belong.

Not the wind. The wind out here had a vocabulary — a low moan through the canyon walls, a hiss across the scree, a dry rattle like paper in the sagebrush. This was none of those. This was an engine held at low revs, patient, moving without headlights, maybe two ridges east.

Theo slid out from under the truck slow as syrup and lay still in the shadow of the fuel drums, listening. Behind him, camp was quiet: Priya asleep in the second vehicle with her data slate glowing against her chest like a firefly, August's breathing thin and whistling through the canvas of the tent, Felix on watch with her back against a boulder, her silhouette a black notch against the last violet of dusk.

"Felix," he breathed, barely louder than the wind.

She was already looking at him. He'd never figured out how she did that — how she seemed to hear things before they happened, or maybe just believed they would, always, and so was never surprised. She raised one hand: *stay*. Then she was up and moving, low, fluid, crossing the camp like water finding its level, and she crouched beside him in the drum-shadow and went still too.

"Two engines?" she whispered.

"I think so. East ridge. They've cut their lights."

"Syndicate doesn't cut lights."

"No," Theo said. "Trackers do."

Something moved in her face then, something he'd only seen once before, the night she'd told them what August's map might be worth and watched them decide to come anyway. Not fear exactly. Fear's older sibling. The thing fear grows into when you stop being afraid for yourself.

"They found the cache," she said. "Which means they found our trail, which means they've been behind us since the pass." She was doing arithmetic in her head; he could almost see the numbers ticking behind her eyes. "Fuel, ammo, August, the ledgers. Everything we can't replace is sitting in one bowl of rock."

"So we move. Now. In the dark."

"In the dark, through country I don't know, with a dying man and half a tank." She shook her head once. "They want us to run. Running is how you lose people in the belt."

The engines on the ridge went quiet.

That was worse. That was much worse, because it meant whoever was driving them had stopped caring about being heard.

* * *

It came apart fast after that, the way bad things do — not building but arriving, whole, like weather.

Felix woke Priya with a hand over her mouth and got August upright and wrapped in a blanket, and Theo threw the camp into the trucks in ninety seconds flat, hands working on muscle memory while his brain screamed at him to hurry, *hurry*, because every second the ridge stayed silent was a second closer to whatever was coming down it. They were rolling — Magpie in front, Priya's hauler behind, August wedged in the hauler's cab where he could see the stars he'd spent fifty years navigating by — and they'd made maybe four hundred meters toward the canyon mouth when the first shot cracked off the rocks above them.

Not at them. Above them. A statement.

Then the ridge lit up with engine noise, two vehicles cresting in a spray of gravel, and this time there was no patience in it at all.

"Theo!" Felix's voice through the radio, stripped down to wire. "Cache road, hard left — we go back through, we can put the drums between us and—"

He understood one heartbeat before she said it. The cache sat in a narrows, fuel drums stacked three high against the canyon wall, and if the Syndicate took that narrows they took everything — the reserve diesel, the spare parts, the water, the route south. And if the Syndicate couldn't take it—

"They're going to burn it," Theo said aloud, to no one, and wrenched the wheel left.

What happened next lived in his memory afterward as a series of bright, disconnected frames, the way crashes do. Magpie's taillights swinging ahead of him. The narrows opening up around them, walls rising sheer and black. Headlights flooding in behind — two sets, three, closing fast. Felix's truck sliding sideways in a handbrake arc that belonged in a stunt show, putting her bulk between the convoy and the guns. Muzzle flash stuttering off the rock face, sparks like a welder's torch. Priya screaming his name over the radio, and August's voice underneath hers, calm and terrible, saying coordinates, always coordinates, even now, even dying, dictating the way home to a man who was already shooting at them.

And then the drum.

One round, maybe a stray, maybe not, found the stack of reserve diesel, and the night turned orange.

The heat came through the windshield like a physical blow. Theo felt it take his eyebrows, felt the paint on the hood begin to blister, and he did the only thing there was to do, which was nothing sensible at all: he stopped the hauler, popped the door, and ran *toward* the fire, because the spare parts crate — the one with the water pump, the one with August's medicine box strapped to its lid — was sitting ten meters inside the burning narrows, and nobody else was going to get it.

"Theo, *no*—" Felix's voice, raw, over the roar.

He got two steps. Maybe three. The world went white and sideways.

Later, piecing it together from the burn scar on his shoulder and the dent in his skull and Priya's shaking account, he understood: a secondary drum had gone up, and the blast wave had picked him up like a doll and introduced him to the canyon wall. What he remembered was the ground hitting him, and the taste of blood and diesel, and — clearest of all, stranger than anything — the sound of Felix Dunmore screaming his name in a voice he had never heard her use, a voice with no pragmatism left in it anywhere, and the weight of her hands dragging him backward through the smoke while rounds snapped off the rocks above them both.

He remembered thinking, with the strange clarity of the nearly dead: *she came back for me. She didn't have to, and she came back.*

Then the dark took him, kindly, for a while.

* * *

He woke to engine vibration and the smell of burnt hair — his own — and Priya's fingers pressed to his wrist, counting.

"Don't move," she said. "Your head is held together with tape and stubbornness, and I need both of those intact."

"The truck—"

"Gone. Magpie's gone." Her voice caught on it. "Felix rammed it into the narrows mouth after the drums went. Blocked the road. They'll spend hours cutting it clear, and by then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "By then we're ghosts."

Theo lay still and let the truth settle over him like ash. Magpie. The truck Felix had rebuilt bolt by bolt across six wrecked seasons, the truck that had been the last thing she owned outright, the last thing the Syndicate hadn't already taken from her. She'd fed it to a canyon mouth like scrap meat to buy them thirty minutes.

He turned his head. Through the hauler's rear window he could see her at the wheel, dawn coming up gray behind her, and she wasn't watching the road. She was watching the mirror. Watching the smoke column rising off the cache like a black finger pointing at the sky, marking where they'd been, telling anyone who cared to look exactly how far the Syndicate would go.

"She knows," Priya said quietly, following his gaze. "She finally understands. It was never about the debt. They were never going to let any of us walk away carrying what August carried. Burn the ledgers, burn the station, burn the witnesses. That was the plan from the beginning."

August coughed from the cot behind them, wet and long, and when Theo looked back the old man's eyes were open, fixed on the ceiling of the hauler, seeing something fifty years away.

"Told her," August rasped. "Told her they'd come. Told her what the rain was worth." A ghost of a smile. "Worth killing for. Always was."

* * *

They stopped at midmorning in a slot canyon deep enough to swallow the sky, and Felix gathered them between the hauler and the rock wall, and Theo saw that something had finished breaking in her and something else had finished being built.

"We are done running scared," she said. No performance in it. Just fact, laid out flat like tools on a bench. "I thought if we moved fast enough and stayed small enough, they'd lose interest. They won't. There's no version of this where we slip away quiet, because what August found isn't a treasure — it's a confession, and confessions don't get ignored, they get buried, along with everyone who read them." She looked at each of them in turn, and Theo felt it land on him like a hand on the shoulder. "So we stop being cargo. From here on, everybody drives, everybody watches, everybody fights if it comes to that. We get these ledgers home or we die trying, and I'm done pretending those aren't the only two doors."

Priya nodded once, jaw set. August, propped against the wheel well with his blanket around his shoulders like a mantle, raised two fingers off his knee — a surveyor's salute, old-fashioned and formal, the kind of gesture men used when words had gotten too heavy to lift.

Theo just grinned through split lips, because his hands were already itching, already cataloguing: the hauler's fuel line needed patching, the radiator was weeping, and somewhere in the salvaged crate — the crate he'd walked into fire for — there was a water pump and enough parts to keep them alive another week.

"You'll want the pump swapped tonight," he said. "We've got maybe eight hundred klicks of badlands between here and the drought towns, and every meter of it belongs to people who want us dead."

Felix almost smiled. Almost.

"Then we'd better outrun the rain," she said, and pointed the hauler north, toward the storm line building black and beautiful on the horizon — the season's landfall, huge and hungry, sitting square across the only road home.
