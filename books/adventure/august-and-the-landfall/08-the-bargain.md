## 8. The Bargain

The fixer came at dusk, when the light went the color of old brass and the wind off the flats carried the smell of rain that never fell.

Felix saw him first as a heat-shimmer on the eastern rise, a single vehicle moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once needed to run. Not a hunter's rig—no dust plume, no aggressive angle of approach. Just a long gray sedan riding the old survey road like it owned the gravel under its tires, which, in every way that mattered, it did.

She was on watch atop the lead truck, a pair of cracked binoculars pressed to her eyes, and she watched the car come for eleven minutes without calling out. Eleven minutes to think. To weigh. To feel the shape of what was arriving settle over her like a change in pressure before a storm—the way her ears had learned, over years of chasing, to hear weather before weather existed.

The sedan stopped two hundred meters out. A man got out. He stood beside his door with his hands visible and empty, an old courtesy, and waited.

"Felix," Priya said from below, voice tight. "That's a Vane plate."

"I know."

"Then we should—"

"We should let him talk." She climbed down, joints aching from three days of bad sleep. "Hunters shoot first. This one wants something. Men who want something always tell you where they're weak."

Theo appeared at her elbow, wrench still in hand, grease to the elbows, sixteen going on sixty. "I can rig the fuel line to blow if you give me ten minutes."

"Ten minutes is nine more than he'll wait." She looked at him—at the kid who'd stowed away in a crate of spare parts because anywhere was better than the town that had forgotten him—and felt the thing in her chest she'd been refusing to name since Station Meridian. Since August died with her promise in his mouth like a coin held between teeth. "Stay behind the engine block. Both of you. If this goes wrong, you drive north and you don't stop for anything, including me."

Priya opened her mouth to argue. Felix walked out alone.

* * *

The man introduced himself as Corben Ashe, and he had the flat, pleasant face of someone whose profession required him to be forgettable. He set up a folding table between the vehicles—he'd brought the table, which told Felix everything about how planned this was—and laid out a thermos, two cups, and a leather folio.

"Ms. Dunmore." He poured without asking. "You look tired."

"You look expensive."

He smiled as if she'd paid him a compliment. "I'm authorized to make you an offer. I'd ask that you hear all of it before responding, because it won't be repeated."

"Men like you repeat things all the time. That's your whole job."

Something flickered behind the pleasantness—amusement, maybe, or recognition. "Fair." He opened the folio and turned it toward her. On the left page: a ledger of her debts, itemized in the Syndicate's immaculate hand. The wrecked chase season. The salvage liens. The interest compounding like rot. A number so large it had stopped feeling like money years ago and become a kind of weather, a permanent low-pressure system sitting on her life. On the right page: a single line of text, and beneath it, a space for her signature.

*Full satisfaction and release of all obligations.*

"Forgiveness," Ashe said. "Complete. Your slate, wiped clean tonight. In exchange: the ledgers you recovered from Meridian, August Reyes's map and notes, and your silence. You were never in the belt. You never found anything. You go home a free woman and live however free women live."

The wind moved across the flats, dragging its dry fingers through the saltbush. Somewhere behind her, Theo's truck ticked as its engine cooled.

"And my crew?"

"Included. Their debts, their records, their faces in your files—all of it released. Nair's license reinstated by spring. The boy's—" he consulted a page "—the Okonkwo juvenile record sealed. Everyone walks away clean."

It was elegant. That was the worst part. It wasn't a threat dressed as mercy; it was mercy, real and total, priced exactly against the thing she carried. The Syndicate didn't need to kill them if it could simply buy the truth back, fold it into a folio, and drive it away into the dark.

And God help her, some part of her wanted it. Some part of her that was twenty-six years old and wrecking her rig in a whiteout to pay down a number that never shrank; some part that had promised herself, bleeding on the shoulder of a dead highway, *survive, just survive, whatever it costs, whoever it costs.* She had built her whole life on that promise. It was the only vow she'd ever kept.

"The ledgers are copies anyway," she said. "You know that. Burn mine and the truth's still out there."

"Is it?" Ashe closed the folio gently. "Copies need believers. Believers need credibility, and credibility needs the originals—the watermarks, the signatures, the chain of custody. We've spent forty years making sure no one believes anything about us. One more rumor dies easily." He met her eyes, and for a moment the pleasantness dropped and she saw the actual man underneath: tired, precise, and not entirely comfortable. "Ms. Dunmore, I'll say something off the record. I've read the Meridian files. All of them. What our founders did to that watershed—to those towns—I have children who drink bottled water because of people like me. So understand that I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you what happens after you refuse. Because you will refuse. People like you always do. And then the hunters come, and they don't bring folding tables."

* * *

She stalled him for two hours.

It wasn't hard. She asked questions she knew the answers to, demanded clauses, made him explain the release terms twice, watched his patience the way she watched a storm cell—looking for rotation, for the tell that would show her what he really was. And through all of it, her mind kept splitting along a fault line she couldn't close.

On one side: duty. August dying in the station infirmary with her hands around his, making her swear again—not to protect the secret, but to carry it home. *They drank the drought, Felix. Every dry year, every dead orchard, every kid who grew up small because the water was gone. Carry it home.* His compass in her pocket now, its needle settling faithfully north no matter how lost its owner had been.

On the other side: love, though she hated the word. Priya, who'd lost her license and half her self to a Syndicate smear campaign and still read aquifer data by headlamp like it was scripture. Theo, who'd taken shrapnel at the fuel cache and hadn't cried, just asked if the truck could be saved. Three days ago they'd been passengers. Now they were hers, and she was theirs, and Ashe was offering to buy that fact back too—clean records, safe roads, everyone walking away—as if safety were the same thing as salvation.

*Survive,* whispered the girl she used to be. *Take the deal. Dead heroes don't deliver anything.*

*Carry it home,* said the dead man.

Ashe began packing the folio. "You need until dawn. That's standard. But Ms. Dunmore—" he paused, pen hovering, "—the hunters are twelve hours behind me, and they don't take signatures. Dawn is generous. Don't spend it all."

He drove west into the dark, taillights shrinking to embers, and Felix stood alone between the trucks until the cold worked through her jacket and Priya came to fetch her in.

* * *

They voted around the camp stove, and it wasn't close. Priya spoke of the towns, of names she knew from her ruined career—families she'd tried to warn about contamination years ago and been discredited for it. Theo said nothing at all, just looked at Felix with a trust so naked it hurt to hold.

But Felix had spent the vote already thinking past it. Because here was the thing she'd realized while Ashe talked, watching his careful hands: he'd assumed the ledgers were singular. He'd assumed the choice was binary—surrender the truth or die holding it. And men who assume binaries miss the third option, the same way storm chasers who fixate on the funnel miss the flanking line building on their six.

So while Priya slept and Theo kept watch, Felix worked.

By lantern light, in the cab of the lead truck, she forged. She'd learned it young—forged chase permits, forged insurance claims, forged her mother's signature on school forms when the drinking got bad—and her hands remembered the way hands remember anything done often enough in desperation. She copied the ledgers page by page onto the blank survey paper they'd salvaged from Meridian, matching the watermarks by holding pages to the flame and studying the ghost-images, aging the ink with tea and engine heat, even duplicating the coffee ring August had left on the most damning page fifty years of habit had worn into it. She copied the map. She copied August's dictated coordinates, his final notes in his own failing hand. Everything. Two hours of the truest forgery of her life: a lie built entirely out of truth.

At 3 a.m., she wrapped the originals in oilcloth and sewed them into the lining of her jacket, against her ribs, where the compass rode. Then she placed the copies in the Meridian document case, exactly as they'd lived, and set it on the seat where any searcher would find it first.

If the Syndicate took the case, they'd take ash and tea-stains and her very best work. If they burned it, they'd burn nothing but paper she could reproduce. And if it came to blood—if the hunters caught them—then the truth would be stitched against her heartbeat, and they would have to go through her to get it.

She sat back in the dark and laughed once, quietly, at herself. Fifty thousand dollars of debt forgiven, and she'd spent the night committing fraud against the only institution that could afford to forgive it. August would have loved her for it. Or hated her. With August it had always been both.

Dawn came up gray and thin. On the eastern horizon, where the land flattened into the coastal plain, the sky had begun to organize—high cirrus streaking out like pulled wool, the barometer falling in her wrist gauge with a steadiness that made her teeth ache. The season's big one. The landfall. Coming down the coast like a debt collector.

Priya climbed into the passenger seat, saw the document case, and looked at Felix with a question in her face.

Felix started the engine. "We move now," she said. "Before the hunters, and ahead of the storm. There's a canyon route south—flooded by now, probably, but flood cuts both ways. It slows them worse than it slows us." She glanced at the falling glass, then at the kid asleep in the truck bed, then at the weight against her ribs. "We're going to outrun a hurricane carrying the evidence that breaks the Vane Syndicate. Anybody want to reconsider their life choices?"

From the truck bed, without opening his eyes: "Drive, Felix."

She drove. Behind them, far to the east, the first black towers of the eyewall stood up against the dawn like something rising out of the sea to collect what it was owed.
