## 9. Landfall

The storm had a name before it was anything at all—*Seraphine*, some clerk in a coastal office christening a smudge of cloud the way you'd name a child, with no idea what it would grow into. By the time it turned inland it had outgrown its name entirely. Priya watched it on the convoy's battered radar display and felt something old and superstitious move in her chest, the way she imagined sailors must have felt three hundred years ago, reading a black line on the horizon and understanding that the sea had decided to come for them.

"It's not supposed to track there," she said. "The models put landfall two hundred kilometers south."

"The models," Felix said from the driver's seat, "assume the land is behaving."

They were stopped on a ridge of rust-colored rock above a canyon country that looked, in the pre-storm light, like the skeleton of a drowned world. The air had gone strange—thick, greenish, tasting of ozone and wet stone. Somewhere behind them, thirty or forty kilometers back along their own trail, the Syndicate's trackers were closing. Priya could feel them the way you felt a toothache: not seen, but insistently present.

Ahead of them, between here and the federal investigator in Meridian Flats, lay ninety kilometers of slot canyons and dry washes that were about to stop being either.

"Every route out crosses the drainage," Theo said. He'd spread August's map across the hood of his truck, weighting the corners with lug nuts. His hands were still bandaged from the firefight at the cache; he moved them carefully, like a pianist with a sprain. "We go north, we hit the main channel where it funnels. We go south, we're driving toward the eyewall. There's no clean line."

"There's never a clean line," Felix said. "There's only lines you've studied harder than the storm has."

Priya had heard Felix talk like this before—the flat, almost liturgical calm she dropped into when the sky went bad—and she had always assumed it was performance, a chaser's theater. Watching her now, bent over the map with the wind pulling at her jacket, Priya understood it was something else. It was prayer. It was the only kind Felix Dunmore knew how to say.

* * *

They ran the canyon road as the light failed.

It was not rain yet—not properly—but the leading bands came through in long gray curtains that erased the world every forty seconds, wipers useless, headlights turning the water into a solid white wall. Felix drove by memory and by the shape of things: the lean of the canyon walls, the sound the tires made on different stone. Twice she stopped the convoy dead in the downpour, listening, then took a fork that wasn't marked on any map.

"That wash is a trap," she said, when Priya asked. "It drains the whole eastern mesa. In an hour it'll be a river. We take the high bench instead—it's longer, but longer is fine if the water hasn't arrived yet."

*Longer is fine if the water hasn't arrived yet.* Priya wrote it down in her head, in the ledger she kept of things she'd learned since leaving her ruined career: that speed was sometimes the coward's choice. That the desert forgave distance more readily than it forgave depth.

August's body rode in the bed of Felix's truck, wrapped in the tarp they'd used to cover the ledgers before they copied them. They hadn't been able to bury him. There hadn't been time, and the ground would have been mud by morning anyway. Priya found herself glancing back at the shape of him at every stop, half expecting—irrationally, shamefully—that he might sit up and tell them which way the water would run. He'd known this country fifty years. He'd known it better than the rain did.

In the third hour the rain stopped being weather and became geography.

It fell in ropes. It fell in sheets that hit the windshield hard enough to ring the glass. And in the canyon bottoms, where all the water of the eastern mesas was gathering with nowhere to go, the dry washes began to speak—a low, grinding roar, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"Flood stage," Priya said, watching a side channel fill in the space between one lightning flash and the next. What had been a crack in the earth was now a brown torrent carrying whole creosote bushes, fence posts, a sheet of corrugated roofing, spinning past like toys in a bathtub. "Felix. That channel rose four feet in under a minute."

"I know." Felix's voice didn't change. Her hands didn't change. But Priya saw her jaw set, saw the small muscle there working like a man chewing on a decision. "Main channel's ahead. If we can cross at the narrows before the surge comes down from the mesa, we're through. After that it's high ground all the way to the flats."

"And if the surge gets there first?"

"Then we wait it out on the bench and let the Syndicate catch us sitting still." She glanced at the mirror, at the darkness behind them. "I'd rather gamble with the water. Water doesn't hold grudges."

* * *

The narrows appeared out of the rain like a wound—a place where the canyon pinched down to barely two truck-widths, the crossing a shelf of slickrock just above the ordinary waterline. Except nothing was ordinary anymore. Even in the lightning flashes Priya could see the channel below the crossing running fast and heavy, brown and muscular, already licking at the edges of the shelf.

"We cross single file," Felix said over the radio. "No stopping on the shelf. Theo first—he's lightest, his truck will tell us if the rock holds. Then Priya. I'm last."

"You're last because you don't trust anyone else's judgment on the far side," Theo said.

"I'm last because I'm the best driver," Felix said, "and the last truck across a failing crossing needs the best driver. Go."

Theo went. Priya watched his taillights crawl out onto the shelf, watched the water reach up for his wheels and miss by inches, watched him make the far side and climb onto the gravel bar beyond, and she let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the fuel cache. Then it was her turn, and there was no breath left for anything but the crossing itself—the wheel fighting her, the flood's spray breaking over the hood, the sickening knowledge that the entire weight of her truck rested on sixty centimeters of slickrock above a current that could pick up a vehicle like a leaf.

She made it. She was still shaking when Felix's headlights swung out onto the shelf.

That was when the surge came.

Priya heard it before she understood it—a deep, rising bellow from upstream, the sound of a canyon being filled. In the strobe of the lightning she saw it: a standing wave of brown water coming down the channel like a moving wall, higher than the trucks, higher than the canyon rim in the low places, carrying a front of foam and debris.

And Theo's truck was still on the gravel bar.

He'd stopped—why had he stopped? Later she would learn: a boulder had come down in the flood and blocked the exit track, and he'd been backing up to find another line when the surge hit. The water took the truck broadside and shoved it, sliding, toward the mouth of a side channel—a surge channel, a drain, a throat. The taillights tilted. The radio filled with Theo's voice, very young and very controlled, saying, *"I'm in the water. I'm in the water, I can't—"*

Felix didn't hesitate. That was the thing Priya would carry for the rest of her life, the thing she would tell the investigators and the assemblies and finally, years later, her own students: Felix Dunmore did not hesitate. Every instinct she had built in twenty years of surviving—every rule that had kept her alive when everyone around her was taken—said *drive for high ground, save yourself, you are one person and the water does not negotiate.* Priya saw all of that happen in her face in a single lightning flash, the whole war of it.

Then Felix spun her truck downstream along the rim, found the point where the surge channel shallowed against a rock fin, and drove off the edge into the flood.

*"Felix!"* Priya was screaming into the radio and did not remember deciding to. *"Felix, the water—"*

The truck landed half-submerged, engine screaming, headlights carving cones through the brown chaos. Through the window Priya saw Felix clip her harness to the roll cage, lash a tow line to the frame with movements so practiced they looked calm, and play out the cable toward Theo's tilting truck while the current hammered her vehicle like a fist on a door. The water came over her hood. It came over her roof. For ten seconds—one, two, five, ten—there was nothing on the radio but static and the roar, and Priya stood on the gravel bar in the pouring rain screaming a name into a dead channel, and the world contained nothing but water.

Then the winch engaged. Then Theo's truck shuddered, tilted back from the throat of the channel, and began, inch by groaning inch, to come home. And out of the flood rose Felix's truck like a surfacing animal, streaming, dented, one headlight blind, and Felix's voice on the radio, hoarse and shaking and absolutely level:

*"Both vehicles mobile. Get off the bar. Now."*

* * *

They climbed out of the canyon system in the small hours, up a switchback track that Felix took at speeds Priya refused to watch, and came out onto the high plain east of the belt just as the eyewall made its full landfall behind them. From the ridge they stopped on, Priya turned and looked back at the storm they had outrun—or rather, threaded, the way a needle threads cloth—and saw it whole for the first time: a vast slow spiral of cloud lit from within by constant silent lightning, an eye forty kilometers wide, walking ashore over the exact country where the Syndicate had spent fifty years teaching the rain to stay away.

The aquifer beneath them was taking the first of it. Somewhere under the mud and the roar, a sea was beginning, drop by drop, to refill.

Theo sat in the cab of his rescued truck with the heater running, wrapped in every blanket they owned, gray-faced and grinning. "You came back for me," he said to Felix, when she leaned in his window to check on him. "You drove *into* it."

"Don't make it strange," Felix said.

But Priya saw her hands on the wheel afterward, when they rolled on toward Meridian Flats and the dawn and whatever waited for them there. They weren't quite steady. Twenty years of staying invisible, of measuring life in miles, of never once betting herself on another human being—and she had driven into a flood for a stowaway kid with bandaged hands, without a single heartbeat of hesitation.

Behind them, far back down the trail they'd come by, headlights flickered through the rain. Three sets. Four. The Syndicate had reached the flooded narrows and found the crossing destroyed, the trail washed to bedrock—and were turning, patient and unhurried, toward the only other road out.

Toward Meridian Flats. Toward the assembly. Toward them.

"They know where we're going," Priya said quietly.

Felix watched the headlights in the mirror for a long moment. Then she reached over and turned off the radar, and the cab went dark except for the road ahead.

"Good," she said. "Let them come to the hearing. Let them watch me say it out loud."
