## 10. Homecoming Rain

The rain found us before the town did.

It came in off the flats in long gray curtains, soft as breath, and I stood on the running board of the lead truck with my face turned up into it like a fool, like a woman who had never felt water fall from the sky before. Maybe I hadn't. Not like this. Not as a gift instead of a threat.

Behind me, Theo leaned on the horn until Priya smacked his arm, and then he was laughing—hoarse, bandaged at the throat where the surge channel had nearly taken him, laughing anyway—and the sound carried over the wet rattle of the convoy rolling toward Harrow's Bend with its lights burning through the rain.

We had driven two days out of the flooded canyons. Two days of mud to the axles, of Priya navigating by memory and stubbornness because half our instruments were dead, of Theo sleeping propped upright against the ledgers so that no one could take them without waking him. The copies rode in my coat, sewn into the lining by lamplight while the others slept, my hands still unsteady from the eyewall. The originals rode in an ammunition box between August's folded coat and his compass, which I had not yet been able to make myself touch.

Harrow's Bend rose out of the rain like something remembered rather than seen: a water tower rusted through in three places, a main street of shuttered storefronts, and the community hall at the end of it all with its windows lit yellow against the gray—the first light I'd seen in weeks that wasn't lightning or fire or the cold glow of a gauge telling me how little fuel we had left.

There were people waiting under the hall's awning. Dozens of them. Word travels fast in drought country; it has to, the way roots travel fast through cracked soil looking for anything.

I killed the engine and sat for a moment in the ticking quiet, watching the rain run down the windshield in braided streams.

"Last chance to run," I said, though I didn't mean it, and they knew I didn't mean it.

"You'd never make it without us," Theo said.

"She means she's scared," Priya said gently. "That's different."

"It is not," I said, and opened the door, and let the rain have me.

* * *

The assembly hall smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and old coffee, and it was fuller than any room I had ever stood in. Farmers in their good coats. Well-drillers with hands like tree bark. Women who ran the co-ops, men who'd lost orchards their grandfathers planted, kids perched on radiators with the wide-eyed silence of children who have learned that water talk is serious talk.

At the front, beside a folding table draped in a flag that had seen better decades, sat a federal investigator named Okafor—a compact woman with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and eyes that went through you like a core sample. She'd come up from the capital on four hours' notice when Priya's transmission reached a journalist who reached a senator who reached her. That chain of luck still made me uneasy. Luck, in my experience, was just disaster that hadn't finished arriving.

I laid the ledgers on the table one at a time, and the sound each made—wet leather, cracked binding, paper gone soft as cloth—was the loudest thing in the room.

"Survey Station Meridian," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Fifty years ago the Vane Syndicate's founding partners ran a watershed program across this whole basin. These are their field books." I opened the first ledger to the page I'd marked with a strip of Theo's electrical tape. "They didn't fail to find water. They found all of it. And then they seeded the recharge zones with a chemical stabilizer—bentonite slurry laced with polymer binders—to seal the aquifer's intake formations. To stop the land from drinking. So the only water left would be the water they sold."

The room did not gasp. Drought towns don't gasp. They went very still, the way a field goes still before hail, and a man in the third row said, quietly, "My father always said the wells died from the top down," and had to stop talking for a while.

Okafor read. She read for a long time, her finger moving down columns of figures, and once she took off her glasses and pressed the bridge of her nose and put them back on. Then she looked up at me.

"Where's the surveyor?" she asked. "These notes are signed. A. Reyes, junior field assistant, Meridian Station, 1974."

"Dead," I said. "Three weeks ago. In the station infirmary, with the rain coming in the broken roof." I set August's compass on the table beside the ledgers, its brass face clouded, its needle swinging slow and restless like something that hadn't decided where north was anymore. "He spent fifty years running from what he helped bury. He spent his last month driving back toward it. He wanted this—" I touched the ledger, "—to come home more than he wanted to."

"And you're Felix Dunmore," Okafor said. Not a question. "Chase pilot. Contracted to the Vane Syndicate. Outstanding debts in excess of—"

"I know what I owe them," I said. "Every digit."

"That's going to matter," she said. "Your testimony will be attacked. Everything about you will be attacked. If you stand up in those hearings, you do it under your own name, in the open, where every lawyer the Syndicate can hire will take you apart looking for something to discredit."

I thought about saying something clever. What came out instead was the truth, plain as gravel: "I've spent eleven years being nobody. It didn't keep anything safe. It just kept everything small." I looked back at the crowd—at Priya standing straight with her arms crossed, at Theo sitting on the radiator grinning at me like I'd already won. "My name's Felix Dunmore. Say it however you need to."

* * *

The fixer arrived that night, of course. His name was Corben Marsh, and he came alone, no escort, walking up the muddy main street through the rain with his coat collar up and his briefcase held like a shield, and I met him in the doorway of the hall with the whole town pretending not to watch through the windows.

"Dunmore." He looked older than he had across the bargaining table in the canyon camp—or maybe I was just seeing him clearly for the first time. "I'm authorized to offer full debt forgiveness. All of it. Clean slate. For the ledgers and your silence."

"I know," I said. "You offered before."

"The number's better now."

"There isn't one." I stepped out into the rain with him, because some conversations shouldn't happen under a warm roof. "But I'll tell you what I'll give you, Marsh. A seat in that hearing room. Because these ledgers name the people who trained you, promoted you, taught you to call debt a form of weather. You've spent twenty years making their storms for them. Here's your one chance to be something else."

He stared at me. Rain ran off his chin. Somewhere behind us a kid laughed, quick and startled, catching rain in a tin cup.

"They'll destroy you," he said finally.

"Probably," I said. "But they'll do it in daylight, and daylight's the one thing they can't buy back." I turned to go inside, then stopped. "August Reyes signed those field books, Marsh. Junior field assistant. He was nineteen years old and they owned him the way they own everyone, and he carried it for fifty years until it killed him on a cot in a ruined station. Don't carry it fifty years. Carry it three days. That's all the hearings need."

He didn't answer. But he didn't leave either, and in the morning he was sitting in the second row of the assembly hall with his briefcase on his knees, and when Okafor called the first witness he stood up, walked to the front, and said, "I'll corroborate all of it. I processed the payments myself," and the sound he made after—half exhale, half surrender—was the sound of a man setting down something heavy he'd forgotten he was holding.

* * *

They buried August on the ridge above Harrow's Bend, in the small cemetery where the drought towns put their dead, on a rise of red earth that the rain had turned the color of rust and new beginnings. The whole town came. Priya spoke about the hydrology—about how a nineteen-year-old surveyor's careful field notes would now rewrite the water future of an entire region. Theo nailed the repaired compass case shut over the ledgers' shipping manifest, because somebody had to build things even on a day of endings.

And I stayed after everyone else had gone down the hill, the way I always stay after, except that this time staying didn't feel like hiding.

The grave was simple. AUGUST REYES, 1955–2024. HE CAME HOME. Priya had cut the letters herself, and I hadn't corrected the last line, because it was true in every way that mattered.

I knelt in the wet grass and took out his compass. I'd carried it since the station, through the firefights and the flood, through the hearing and the nights I couldn't sleep, and its brass face was warm from my pocket, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled—not north, but northwest, toward the badlands, toward Meridian, toward the buried sea.

"He'd have hated this," I said aloud. "All the attention. All the names in the papers." I smiled, and my eyes stung, and I let them. "Good thing he's not here to see it."

I dug a small place in the red earth with my fingers and set the compass in it, face up, needle steady, and covered it over, and pressed the ground flat with both palms like sealing a letter.

Then I stood up, and the rain kept falling—soft, unhurried, falling on the badlands for the first time in fifty years with somewhere to go, sinking into the cracked ground, finding the sealed doors of the aquifer far below, knocking, patient, certain that sooner or later the land would drink again.

Down the hill, the lights of the hall were coming on, and Priya was waving at me from the porch, and Theo was shouting something about dinner, and Okafor's car waited with its headlights on to drive me to the capital in the morning, where I would stand up under my own name and say everything I knew.

"Felix!" Theo yelled again. "You're missing it!"

I wiped my face—which needed doing anyway, because of the rain—and started down the hill toward them, visible to the whole valley, letting myself be known.
