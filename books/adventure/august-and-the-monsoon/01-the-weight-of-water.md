## 1. The Weight of Water

The storm announced itself the way storms always did on the Kilow Reach—not with thunder, but with a smell. Green rot and hot stone, ozone threading through it like a wire. Isandro Yarrow stood at the tiller of the *Maren's Grace* with his face tipped into that smell and did the arithmetic he'd been doing all morning: forty miles of river between him and the cell, the cell moving slower than he was, which meant he could beat it to Harrow Bend by mid-afternoon if the current held and his engine didn't quit.

The engine had been threatening to quit for three weeks.

"Come on," he told it, in the low coaxing voice he used on machines and creditors. "Just this one more run."

The Grace answered with a shudder that traveled up through the deck plates and into his boots, then settled back into its laboring rhythm. Good enough. Everything he owned was good enough, right up until the moment it wasn't.

He checked the sky again out of habit rather than need. The cloud over the western hills had gone the color of wet slate, its base flattened and dragging, and beneath it the river had already begun to change—current stiffening, surface roughening into small confused waves that ran against the wind instead of with it. Somewhere upstream, rain was falling on ground that couldn't hold any more of it. That water would come down the Kilow looking for things to take, and when a river went looking, it found.

Which was, in the ugly ledger of Isandro's life, the whole business model.

Salvage men made their money in the hours after a flood took something and before the owner gave up on getting it back. Livestock first, then boats, then the heavy sad inventory of other people's lives—wagons, tool chests, grain stores, once an entire smokehouse that had come off its foundations and floated four miles intact. You charged by the hour and by the risk, and you charged double if the work happened in rising water, triple at night. The fees were good. The fees were the only reason a man with his debts would point a boat at a storm instead of away from it.

And the Optimists were eleven days behind him now. Maybe twelve.

He'd counted carefully. The last letter had reached him at a fuel dock outside Marrow's Ferry, handed over by a dockhand who wouldn't meet his eyes, and the date stamp on the envelope had told Isandro exactly how long his paper trail lagged behind his wake. Twelve days of grace, minus however long it took some clerk in the Optimists' offices to notice that the Yarrow account had gone quiet in three towns running. They'd send someone soon. Not a letter-man next time. The Optimists didn't hire letter-men for what came after letters.

Isandro pushed the thought down where he kept it, below the waterline, and opened the throttle as far as the engine would tolerate.

* * *

Harrow Bend was a loading town built on the wrong side of its own levee, which meant half of it flooded every spring and the residents treated this as a scheduling matter rather than a catastrophe. By the time Isandro tied off at the public dock, the air had gone thick and bruised-looking, and the levee path was crowded with people watching the western sky the way you'd watch a slow animal cross a road.

The man he wanted was easy to find. Prentiss Cobb owned two grain barges and a reputation for caring about both more than about people, and he was standing at the head of the dock with a ledger under his arm, watching his moored barges rock in the stiffening chop.

"You're the salvage man." It wasn't a question. Cobb looked Isandro over—the salt-stiff jacket, the rope burns on his palms, the boat behind him that had seen better decades—and Isandro watched him do the same mental arithmetic Isandro did constantly: how much is this one worth, and can I get it cheaper.

"I'm the salvage man," Isandro agreed. "You've got cargo on those barges that's going to be underwater by dark."

"Levee'll hold."

"Your levee has a gap at the north culvert. I saw it coming in. When the surge hits, that culvert's going to fail, and the water's going to come through your own streets and hit these barges from the landward side, where they're not built to take it." He said it flatly, no theater. Theater cost extra and Cobb looked like a man who resented paying for anything twice. "I can move your grain to high ground before the surge arrives. Four hours' work, maybe five."

Cobb's jaw worked. "What's your rate?"

Isandro named it. Cobb flinched, named half of it back, and they settled somewhere in the middle that Isandro could live with and Cobb would complain about for a year. Contracts on the Kilow were sealed with a handshake and a witness, and the witness was a levee-keeper's widow who took her fee in whiskey and watched everything with eyes like a bank examiner.

By the time the handshake was done, the first fat drops were hitting the dock planks.

* * *

It went the way good salvage work went, which was to say badly in every way that didn't actually matter and precisely in every way that did.

They got the first barge lightened and poled up the ramp road to the granary platform while the rain came down in ropes. The second barge was heavier, sitting lower, and the culvert failed exactly when Isandro had said it would—a sound like a door being kicked open somewhere underground, and then brown water standing up out of the street grates as though the town itself had exhaled. Men shouted. A dog lost its mind on a porch roof. The water came down the landward side in a moving wall of debris, fence rails and barrels and one entire chicken coop sailing past with the chickens still aboard, squawking like passengers who'd missed their stop.

Isandro worked the second barge's lines with hands that knew the work so well his mind was free to wander, and his mind wandered, as it always did, to the counting.

Twelve days, minus travel time for whoever the Optimists sent. Say they sent someone within three days of noticing. Say the someone traveled fast, by the post road instead of the river. That put a hunter in Harrow Bend around day nine or ten, asking a smiling kind of question at fuel docks and levee offices: *have you seen a man answering to Yarrow, salvager, mid-thirties, scar on the left forearm?* And everyone Isandro had just spent five hours saving grain alongside would remember him warmly and describe him accurately, because gratitude, it turned out, had excellent recall.

So: leave tonight. Leave before dawn at the latest, upstream, past the bend where the river forked, and let the flood cover his tracks the way floods covered everything. The Optimists could read paperwork; they were famously good at paperwork. But even they couldn't read a river in spate.

"Yarrow!" Cobb bellowed through the rain. "The line! Watch the—"

Isandro was already watching it. The stern line had chafed against the bollard and parted with a crack, and the barge swung its nose toward the current like a animal remembering it was wild. He got a turn around the cleat, took the strain through his shoulders and hips rather than his back, and walked the barge's weight down inch by inch while Cobb screamed instructions that were worse than useless. Forty feet of loaded grain barge does not want to go where you want it to go. It wants to go where the water says. Your entire job is convincing the water to say something else.

He convinced it. The barge kissed the ramp road's edge, men swarmed onto the lines, and twenty minutes later the last of Cobb's grain sat on high ground under tarps while the flood took possession of the empty barges and the lower street and everything else that hadn't been argued out of its reach.

Cobb paid him on the granary platform, counting coins into Isandro's palm with the expression of a man undergoing surgery without anesthetic. It was a good fee. It was, Isandro admitted, doing the math against his mental ledger, almost enough to make a real payment—one that would buy him another month of distance, maybe six weeks if the Optimists' clerks were lazy.

Then he walked back down to the dock where the *Maren's Grace* rode at her lines, and looked at her, and knew.

She'd taken water during the barge work—over the gunwale in the surge, more than she should have—and her bilge pump had been running the whole time with the strained, arrhythmic sound of a machine negotiating its own resignation. He popped the engine hatch and held the lantern close.

Water in the fuel. Water in places water should never be, because a seal had gone, and the seal had been going since Marrow's Ferry, and he'd known it, and he'd run her anyway, because the fee was there and the debt was there and that was the whole shape of his life: spending tomorrow to pay for today.

The repair would take most of the fee. New seal, gaskets, a tow to a yard with a crane because the shaft alignment was beyond what he could do at anchor. He sat on the dock edge in the dying rain and let that fact sit on his chest like a stone.

Behind him, Cobb was already telling anyone who'd listen how *he* had saved his own grain, with the salvage man assisting. Isandro almost laughed. In twelve days, Cobb's warm, accurate memory would be worth nothing anyway.

* * *

The yard at Harrow Bend was run by a woman named Dree who chewed a pipe unlit and quoted prices like scripture. She looked at the Grace's engine, looked at Isandro, and named a figure that took the fee down to a thin ribbon of coin barely worth the pouch it sat in.

"That's robbery," Isandro said.

"That's a crane and a shaft pull and parts nobody stocks because boats this old aren't supposed to still be working." Dree knocked the unlit pipe against the rail. "You want cheap, I can cheap it. Seal'll hold six months, maybe. Or you want it done right and you stop worrying about this engine for five years."

Six months was longer than he'd have anywhere. Five years was a word for people with addresses.

"Cheap it," Isandro said.

While Dree's boy wrestled the seal into place, Isandro sat in the yard's lean-to and did what he always did with money that wasn't going to survive the week: he counted it, and then he counted the days.

Twelve days from the letter. Minus three for the Optimists to notice his silence. Minus four, generously, for their man to reach Harrow Bend. That left five days of margin, and he'd spent one of them here waiting on a seal. Four days. He'd be gone in three, upstream on the fork, into country where the maps went soft and the towns drowned regularly enough that nobody kept good records, which was the only kind of country a man in his condition could afford.

His condition. He turned the phrase over and found it fit too well. Debts that compounded like floodwater—that was the truth of the Optimists' lending, everyone knew it, everyone borrowed from them anyway, because when your brother died in a lock accident and the burial guild wanted its fees upfront, you didn't shop around. You signed what they put in front of you and you told yourself you'd pay it down fast, and then the interest did what interest does, which is rise faster than any honest man's wages, quiet and total, until the debt stopped being a number and became a weather system of its own. They called themselves Optimists because they believed every debt could be collected. It was, Isandro had to admit, a kind of faith. The kind that sent men upriver after you.

"Seal's in," Dree's boy said, wiping his hands. "Runs rough till she burns the water out of the fuel line. Don't idle her long."

Isandro paid, thanked, and cast off in the last grey light, taking the Grace upstream on the ebb of the flood with the engine coughing and clearing itself, coughing and clearing, like a man trying to speak.

* * *

He ran through the night because sleeping in Harrow Bend meant being remembered in Harrow Bend, and memory was the one cargo he couldn't afford to leave anywhere. The fork came at moonrise, the Kilow splitting around a wooded island, and he took the western branch where the charts thinned to guesswork and the banks grew tall and reedy and uninhabited.

By the smallest hours the storm had passed behind him and the sky had scrubbed itself down to hard stars. The river ran high and fast with the flood's leavings, carrying its usual midnight procession: branches, fence posts, a lantern still burning as it spun slowly downstream, some drowned household's last lit thing going wherever dead houses go. Isandro steered around the big stuff and let the small stuff bump the hull, and he did not think about the letter in his coat pocket, the latest one, which he had not yet burned because burning them had become a ritual he rationed, one fire per crisis, and this crisis wasn't ripe.

He thought instead about the fee. About how he'd won it fair, worked hard for it, and watched it evaporate into a boat part inside a single day, so that the whole violent beautiful day had netted him, in real terms, perhaps nine miles of distance and a dry coat. That was the arithmetic of his life and he was good at it, so good at it, restless competence stacked on restless competence, and none of it added up to anything except motion.

Somewhere below the waterline of his attention, in the place where he kept the exhaustion, a voice observed that he could not do this for thirty more years. He acknowledged the voice the way you acknowledge a leak—note it, monitor it, keep rowing.

Dawn came up grey-gold over the reed banks, and the river ahead bent out of sight around a bluff, toward country he didn't know, carrying on its swollen surface the storm's debris and, wedged among the reeds of a slack-water eddy where the current had set something gently aside—

Isandro cut the engine.

He drifted. Old instinct, older than the debt: when a river sets something aside for you, you drift to it before the river changes its mind.

It was a chest. River-dark, waterlogged, half-sunk in the reeds, and even at fifty feet he could see the mark burned into its lid, black on black: a sun with rays like spokes, rendered in a style no workshop on the Kilow used anymore, because no workshop on the Kilow had used it in forty years.

Vhaloria. A nation erased from the maps before Isandro was old enough to read them.

He should throw it back. Every instinct that had kept him alive for twelve days at a time for three years said *throw it back, drift on, don't touch what the flood gives you for free.* Free things from rivers were how debts started.

The Grace bumped the reeds. Isandro leaned out over the gunwale, hooked the chest with his boathook, and felt its weight come up through the pole—heavy, sealed, patient.

Fatal curiosity, his brother used to call it. His brother had been dead eight years, and still the phrase arrived on schedule.

Isandro hauled the chest aboard, and the current, having delivered it, carried him on.
