## 2. Optimists

The Grace ran light and mean down the morning current, and Isandro let her.

He had not opened the chest. That was the discipline of it—leave it lashed under the tarp amidships like a passenger who'd paid no fare, and pretend it wasn't there, the way you pretended a tooth didn't ache or a name didn't mean anything. He'd slept badly in the reeds with one hand on the lid, dreaming of doors, and at first gray light he'd poled out of the backwater without ceremony and pointed his bow downstream, because downstream was where fees were paid and upstream was where questions lived.

The Kilow Reach unspooled wide and brown-green under a sky the color of wet tin. Rain had come through in the night and gone again, leaving the air scrubbed and heavy, every smell turned up loud—mud, cut cane, the tar-and-rot sweetness of the flooded timber camps. Driftwood rode the current in long processions. A drowned fence post stood in what had been somebody's bean field three days ago, wearing a garland of river weed.

Isandro counted while he rowed. It was how he prayed, if he prayed, which he did not.

Four days' rowing to Marrow Bend, where the survey office paid out salvage fees on the first and third of the month. Two days for repairs—the Grace's port seam wept when he leaned on her, and she'd been leaning all night. That left, by his arithmetic, eleven days before the Optimists' next quarterly filing put his name back on the circuit riders' lists, and maybe six weeks before a collector actually caught up with the gap between where the lists said he was and where he was. Six weeks was generous. Collectors didn't read lists; they read rivers, taverns, and men's faces. He'd been careful with his face for three years.

He rowed, and the chest sat under the tarp, and the river carried all three of them toward Marrow Bend like it was nothing, like it was cargo, like it was ordinary.

* * *

The boy found him at noon, which was the first wrong thing.

Isandro had tied up at a rope-ferry landing to boil tea over a spirit stove, and the boy came walking down the ferry road with the unhurried gait of someone being paid by the hour rather than the errand. Ten, maybe twelve. Barefoot. A canvas satchel across his chest, and a smile already installed on his face, bright and professional, the kind of smile adults pay children to wear because nobody suspects it.

"You're the salvage man," the boy said. Not a question. "On the green boat."

"I'm nobody."

"You're Isandro Yarrow." The boy said the name the way you'd set a coin down on a table—flat, final, letting it ring. Then he dug into the satchel and produced an envelope, heavy paper, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and held it out across the water between them as though handing over something delicate and alive. "From the Office of Equitable Recovery," he said, and the words came out rounded and rehearsed, syllables a grown man had fed him and made him repeat until they shone. "They said you'd say you're nobody. They said give it to you anyway."

Isandro looked at the envelope. He looked at the boy's feet, caked white with ferry-road dust, and at the satchel, and at the road behind him, empty, and at the treeline beyond the road, also empty, which meant nothing. Empty treelines were the Optimists' favorite scenery.

"Who paid you?"

"Man at the toll bridge." The boy shrugged, cheerful as a bell. "Gave me your description off a circular. Green boat, scarred hull, man travels alone, sleeps aboard, buys flour not bread because flour keeps." The smile widened a degree. "You buy flour."

Three years of care, summarized in a grocery habit. Isandro felt something cold move through him that had nothing to do with the river wind. They weren't chasing him. Chasing was for debtors who ran. They were *farming* him—letting him move, letting him work, pruning the wild margins of his life down to a row of facts some clerk could harvest whenever the quarter turned. The letter in the boy's hand wasn't a demand. It was a courtesy notice. *We know where you are. We have always known where you are.*

"Take it," the boy said, waggling the envelope. "Or don't. I get paid either way. But they said tell you the interest updated on the first, and there's a new schedule attached, and—" he screwed up his face, retrieving the exact words, enjoying them, "—'Mr. Yarrow should understand that Equitable Recovery views flight not as evasion but as evidence of solvency.'"

Isandro took the envelope. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was floodwater rising.

The boy lingered, rocking on his heels. "That's it? You're not going to run?"

"Where would I run?" Isandro said. "It's all river."

The boy considered this, found it satisfactory, touched two fingers to his brow in a salute he'd clearly learned from someone disreputable, and walked back up the ferry road, whistling. Isandro watched him go until the trees took him, then watched the trees a while longer, reading them. Nothing moved. Birds kept up their idiot business. Either the treeline was genuinely empty or whoever stood in it was better than him, and both possibilities argued for the same response: tea, then rowing.

He boiled the water. He did not open the envelope. He sat with it on his knee through the whole cup, feeling its weight, the weight of paper being always ten times its mass, and thought about his brother.

* * *

Teo had died in the month of storms, eight years back, in a boating accident so stupid it barely qualified—one overloaded ferry, one drunk pilot, one crossing of the Sere estuary that four hundred crossings a day survived without comment. Teo hadn't even been going anywhere important. He'd been carrying a wedding gift.

Their mother was already gone. There was no family money, no guild fund, no parish charity that covered a proper burial in the capital district where Teo had lived and worked and been known. There was only Isandro, twenty-three years old, holding a bill for a plot and a stone and a coffin the funeral house assured him was the least his brother deserved—and the word *deserve* doing its quiet, terrible work, because grief makes you agreeable to anyone who offers you a way to feel less cheap.

And there was the Optimists.

Everyone knew their name by then. The Fellowship of Equitable Recovery, they called themselves formally—*Optimists* behind their backs, because of the founding doctrine printed on every contract in small merciful type: *All debts are recoverable.* Not collectable. Recoverable. As if the money were a lost child, a drowned watch, something the world wanted returned and only needed help finding. Their offices smelled of beeswax and ink. Their agents wore gray and smiled. Their interest rates were described, in writing, as *encouragements toward timely resolution*, and they lent to exactly the people who could never refuse: the grieving, the sick, the proud, the drowning. They called those categories *fertile ground*. Isandro learned that later, from another debtor, in a tavern, both of them laughing in the awful way people laugh when the alternative is screaming.

He signed for four hundred crowns. The funeral cost three hundred and eighty. He remembered sitting in the beeswax-smelling office with the pen in his hand and the agent—a soft, kind-faced man named Pellows—sliding the paper across with both hands, like a gift, like a plate of food, saying, "We're glad to help a family in mourning. That's the whole point of us." And Isandro signing, and the relief arriving first, enormous, sunlit, and the understanding arriving second, small and cold, standing at the far end of the room, waiting politely for the relief to finish so it could have its turn.

The encouragements compounded monthly. When he missed the first payment—because salvage work is feast and famine stitched together, and that month was famine—they offered him a *restructuring*, which meant borrowing more to service the old borrowing, the debt rolling forward like floodwater finding the next low ground, always the next low ground, patient, total. By year two he owed nine hundred. By year three, twelve hundred, and the circulars began: his description, his habits, his boat, distributed to a network of toll-keepers and ferrymen and smiling boys whose smiles were worth two bits apiece. Not bounty hunters. The Optimists didn't believe in force; force was inelegant, force created martyrs and magistrates. They believed in *presence*. In the letter that arrived everywhere before you did. In the slow, hydraulic certainty of paperwork, which never tires, never drowns, never dies in a stupid accident on the Sere estuary.

His brother had been dead eight years. The debt had outlived the reason for it by seven. Somewhere in a gray office a clerk maintained a ledger line with Isandro Yarrow's name on it the way a priest maintains a candle—for the sake of the principle, because the principle was the god, and the god was *all debts are recoverable*, and no one had ever explained to the god that some things you borrow against are gone forever, that a coffin is not an investment, that grief does not yield interest.

Isandro finished his tea. He tucked the envelope into his coat, unopened, because burning it here, in daylight, at a ferry landing with eyes in the treeline, would be a performance, and he refused to perform for them. Even his defiance he would keep private. It was the last property they hadn't lien'd.

Then he rowed.

* * *

He burned it that night, anchored in a willow stand with the lantern hooded to a coin of light.

Not in anger—that surprised him most. He'd expected anger, had been saving some up all afternoon, turning it over like a stone in his pocket. But when he finally broke the seal and unfolded the pages, what rose in him instead was a terrible, clarifying tiredness, the tiredness of a man who has been swimming for three years and has just seen, printed in neat columns, exactly how far the far shore is.

The letter was courteous. That was the genius of them; cruelty would have been easier to hate. *Dear Mr. Yarrow,* it began, in a clerk's round hand. *We trust this finds you well and gainfully employed.* It itemized his balance—twelve hundred and sixty crowns, principal and encouragement combined—with the serene precision of a tide table. It noted, with something almost like warmth, that his recent salvage activity suggested improving prospects, and that the Office of Equitable Recovery wished him *continued success in recovering value from the river*, since value recovered from the river was, after all, value available for recovery generally. Attached was the new schedule. Attached was a list of authorized collection agents currently active in the Kilow basin. Eleven names. Eleven men whose job was to be present in his future, indefinitely, until the number was paid or he was.

At the bottom, above the signature of a man he'd never met, a handwritten postscript:

*Mr. Yarrow—Pellows asks after you fondly. He remembers you as one of our more reasonable clients. We hope you remain so.*

One of our more reasonable clients. Isandro read it twice. Reasonable meant: pays. Reasonable meant: doesn't make noise, doesn't write petitions, doesn't turn up at the office with witnesses. Reasonable meant: still afraid in the correct proportion. And the fondness—Pellows remembering him fondly across eight years and twelve hundred crowns—was the worst thing in the whole packet, because it was probably sincere. That was the country the Optimists ruled: a country where kindness was a collections strategy, where everyone smiled, where the whole apparatus ran not on hatred but on pleasantness, on the gentle unstoppable pressure of decent-seeming people doing arithmetic with your life.

He read the letter once more, slowly, the way you look at a thing you're about to stop owing anything to.

Then he lit it off the lantern flame and held it over the bail bucket and watched it burn. The wax seal went first, bubbling red, then the clerk's round hand curling brown, then black, the columns of numbers glowing briefly like coals in a grate—twelve hundred and sixty little fires—and finally the postscript, Pellows's fond regards flaring up and gone. He stirred the ash into the river with two fingers and watched it scatter. The current took it downstream, toward Marrow Bend, toward the survey office, toward everything that was owed and everything that wasn't.

Here was the thing the burning changed—not the debt, which remained, compounding in some dry room he'd never see, indifferent as weather. What changed was the direction of his looking. For three years he had rowed *away*: away from collectors, away from the estuary, away from the grave with its bought-and-paid-for stone. Away was a direction that required constant effort and got you nowhere; the river proved that daily. But watching the ash go, Isandro felt the strange arithmetic of dread convert, drop by drop, into something harder and more useful. Dread says *run*. Resolve says *outrun, outearn, outlast—or find the one current they can't file a claim against.* He didn't know yet which shape that would take. He only knew that he was done being reasonable, and that the decision, made quietly in a willow stand with wet fingers, felt less like a choice than like a keel finally set straight.

He banked the lantern. He checked the mooring lines, the bilge, the weeping port seam. Under the tarp, the chest sat in the dark with its patience, and his hand rested on the lid a moment—wood, iron banding, that stamped seal he hadn't examined by daylight and wouldn't let himself examine now.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow, in the sun, with the survey fee in hand, he'd decide what the river had actually given him.

He slept, and dreamed of doors again, and woke before dawn to the sound of rain beginning far upstream—a long, low rushing in the dark, like a crowd turning to look at him.

The monsoon was coming early. And the chest, when he finally threw back the tarp in the gray first light, was not the only thing aboard the Grace that hadn't been there a week ago: there was a second boat, half a mile back, holding station in the rain with its sail down, patient as paperwork.

It had been following him since the ferry landing.
