## 3. What the River Keeps

The storm spent itself against the hills sometime before dawn, and Isandro woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath.

He'd tied off in a backwater eddy below the ruins of an old toll bridge, the *Kestrel's Tithe* nosed into a stand of drowned willows, and he'd slept badly through the worst of it—half-listening to the rain hammering the canvas deck cover, half-listening for the other sound, the one that had followed him upriver for three weeks now: engines, voices, the particular way men who are paid to find you move through a landscape. He heard neither. Just the river, running high and brown and fast, dragging whole trees past him like it was late for something.

He climbed out of the berth stiff-necked and cold, boiled water on the spirit stove, and drank his coffee standing at the rail, watching the Kilow Reach do what the Kilow Reach always did after a storm: give things back.

That was the strange arithmetic of this country. The river took—docks, livestock, occasionally people—and then, days later, when the water dropped, it returned its inventory in no particular order. A wedding chest on a sandbar. A church door forty miles from any church. Once, two seasons ago, he'd watched a piano come down the current intact, keys still white, as if it were being delivered.

Isandro had made his living off that arithmetic for six years. Salvage fees, mostly—farmers and ferrymen paid by the pound to have their property fished out before it rotted or was claimed by someone with faster hands. It wasn't honest work exactly, but it was work, and it kept him moving, and moving was the point. A man anchored in one place was a man who could be found.

This morning the river had receded maybe four feet overnight, peeling back from the reed banks in long gray sheets, leaving them slick and gleaming and hung with debris. And there, wedged in the reeds perhaps thirty yards downstream, sat a shape that did not belong to the ordinary catalog of flood-borne goods.

It was square. It was dark. And it was too deliberate-looking to be accident.

Isandro stood very still and looked at it for a long time, coffee cooling in his hands, because thirty years of instinct were telling him two contradictory things at once. The first was *salvage*—that was a chest, chests held value, value meant fees, and fees meant another week of distance between himself and the Optimists' ledger-clerks. The second instinct was older and quieter and had never once been wrong: *that is not yours, and the river left it where you would find it.*

He finished the coffee anyway. Then he got the grapnel line and the skiff hook and went to see what the water had kept.

* * *

The reeds closed around his boots to the knee, cold and grasping, and the mud sucked at every step like it resented the intrusion. Up close the object resolved into a chest of blackened wood bound in dark iron, sitting upright, wedged between two clumps of cane as though someone had planted it there. No scuffing. No water staining above the halfway mark. Which meant it hadn't tumbled downriver—it had floated level, protected somehow, or been set down.

Set down. In the middle of a night storm. By whom?

The seal stopped him with his hand already on the lid.

Stamped into a disc of lead the size of his palm, centered on the lid: a sun rendered in black, its rays straight and rigid as spokes, ringed by lettering in a script he didn't read but recognized the way you recognize a face from a wanted poster. He'd seen it twice in his life. Once on a coin his brother had carried as a boy's lucky piece—a coin too old to be legal anywhere, sold to a collector the winter they buried their mother. And once, years later, on a customs manifest in a port office, where a clerk had crossed it out with a single stroke of red ink and written *VOID — NO SUCH STATE* across the entry.

Vhaloria. Erased from the maps before Isandro was old enough to read them. Forty years gone—not conquered, not renamed, just *struck*, the borders dissolved and the name retired like a ship too unlucky to sail again. Nobody talked about why. That was the eerie part. Whole nations fell all the time; falling was common as weather. But Vhaloria hadn't fallen so much as been *filed away*, and even the word for that kind of ending had gone out of use.

His hand hovered over the lead seal. The mud pulled at his ankles. Somewhere upstream a heron complained about the morning.

"Salvage," he said aloud, to hear something human say it. "Flotsam. Found property, unclaimed, fee owed to finder." The words sounded thin over the water, the way lies always do when there's nobody to sell them to.

He worked the chest loose. It was heavier than it should have been—heavy the way a thing is heavy when it's built to survive being thrown away—and he hauled it into the skiff and poled back to the *Tithe* with his heart going harder than the effort justified.

* * *

On deck, in full daylight, with the storm-washed sky pale as tin above him, he knelt over the chest and told himself he was only assessing it. Value. Condition. Provenance, if it came to that—the Optimists hadn't taught him much worth keeping, but they had taught him that everything has a paper trail, and paper trails can be priced.

The iron bands were fitted without a single rivet showing, joined so cleanly the seams might have grown that way. There was no lock. There was a latch shaped like a folded wing, and when he pressed it, the lid came open on hinges that made no sound at all.

Inside, on a bed of oilcloth gone stiff with age:

Documents. A dozen of them, maybe more, wrapped in waxed linen—folios, letters, what looked like a register bound in gray leather. Ink so fine and even it looked printed.

A signet ring, gold, heavy, the black sun again cut deep into the face, sized for a large hand.

And beneath everything, alone in a shallow tray lined with green felt, a sealed letter. The wax unbroken. The address written across the front in the same precise hand as the documents:

*To the Keeper of the Line, wheresoever the line may keep itself.*

No name. No place. Addressed, Isandro thought, to no one alive—or to whoever had survived being no one alive, which was not the same thing and might be worse.

He sat back on his heels.

The wind came off the water carrying the smell of silt and broken willow, and somewhere in his chest a familiar mechanism began turning, the one that had gotten him into every bad situation of his adult life. His brother Teo used to call it the itch. *You've got the itch, Sandro,* he'd say, eleven years old and wise as a magistrate, watching Isandro pry open a crate of salvage that was clearly marked with someone else's name. *One day the itch opens something that bites you.* Teo had died at twenty-six with debts of his own and no brother close enough to help, which was how Isandro had come to owe the Optimists nine hundred crowns at compounding interest for a funeral plot and a stone with both their names on it, because grief makes men generous and lenders make grief expensive.

The itch said: *read them.*

The rest of him said: *put it back in the water. Right now. Before anyone knows it surfaced. Some things float downstream for a reason, and some seals get crossed out in red ink for a reason, and a man with creditors on his heels does not pick up royal luggage out of a flood.*

He picked up the signet instead.

It was warm. That was the detail that undid him, later—that the metal should have been river-cold, should have been the temperature of the mud it had slept in, and instead it lay in his palm warm as a held coin, warm as if it had recently been worn. He turned it in the light. The black sun caught the morning and threw it back wrong, the rays seeming to bend slightly at their tips, an optical trick of the cutting, probably, a flaw in the engraving, certainly nothing more.

He set it down carefully, the way you set down a sleeping animal.

Then he unwrapped the topmost document, because he was who he was, and the itch had never once lost an argument.

It was a certificate of registry, official in every particular, bearing the arms of a government that had not existed in forty years. It recorded the birth of a child. A girl. Name entered in the rolls of the House of the Black Sun, bloodline certified through seven generations, witnessed by three signatures and a state seal. The date was sixty-one years old. The child, if she lived, would be a woman of sixty-one now, somewhere, anywhere, her existence attested by a nation that had been deleted so thoroughly that owning proof of it might be worth more than the nation had been.

Or might be worth a bullet. Depends who's asking, Isandro thought. Documents like this didn't sit in flooded rivers by accident. Documents like this were carried by people running from other people, and the people doing the chasing were rarely the sentimental kind.

He thought of the red ink. *VOID — NO SUCH STATE.* He thought of clerks, and archives, and the particular temperament of men who believe paperwork is power, because Isandro had met those men—they were the Optimists, every one of them, smiling accountants of other people's ruin—and the thought arrived cold and complete: *somewhere there is a man whose entire life is the absence of this chest, and he is looking for it right now.*

He repacked the documents exactly as they'd lain. He set the signet back in its hollow. He closed the lid until the wing-latch clicked, and then he sat on the deck with his back against the rail and stared at the thing and did the arithmetic he was always doing, the survival arithmetic, the *how long before they catch me* arithmetic—but this time the numbers wouldn't behave. Every path led through the same question: a chest like this, surfacing in the Kilow Reach, in monsoon season, in the one week he happened to be salvaging these waters. Coincidence was possible. Isandro had survived six years on the assumption that coincidence was usually possible and almost never true.

Options. He counted them on his fingers like Brill-counts, like a bookkeeper, because counting calmed him.

One: throw it back. Let the river keep its secret. Cost: nothing, except that he'd know forever what he'd found and given up, and the itch did not forgive surrender. Also—honest admission—if the chest was being hunted, it would surface again somewhere, in someone else's skiff, and whatever came of it would come without him having profited or prepared.

Two: sell it. To a collector, to a broker, to the highest bidder in the river towns where old things changed hands quietly. Risk: selling meant showing, and showing meant witnesses, and witnesses meant the hunters learned his route. Six years of staying invisible undone for one payday.

Three: deliver it. *To the Keeper of the Line, wheresoever the line may keep itself.* As if such a person existed. As if finding them weren't a lifetime's work for a man with resources, and Isandro had a leaking boat and four days of food.

Four, whispered by the part of him that was tired, so tired, of running: keep it. Not to sell. Not to deliver. Keep it the way you keep a lit match in a dry field—because putting it down feels more dangerous than holding on.

"You should throw it back," he told the chest.

The chest said nothing. Chests are good at that.

* * *

He rowed out to mid-channel in the late afternoon, meaning to do it. He had the chest in the skiff, balanced on his knees, the current pulling strong and steady toward the lowlands, and he genuinely intended to tip it over the side and watch the river take back its loan. It was the clean solution. It was the smart solution. Teo would have told him—

Teo would have pried it open, actually. Teo would have read every page by candlelight and made up stories about the girl in the registry until the stories were better than the truth. Teo had died owing money, but he had never once died bored.

Isandro looked at the water, brown and patient, ready to receive it.

Then he looked at the seal, and the crossed-out name behind it, and the letter addressed to no one alive, and he understood—with the flat certainty of a man reading his own chart—that he could not do it. Not because of the fee. Not because of curiosity, even, or not only. Because somewhere upstream or downstream or across forty erased years there were people whose names were in those pages, people who had been struck from the world's ledgers the way he'd been struck from the Optimists' good graces, and Isandro Yarrow knew exactly what it cost to be an entry someone else gets to void.

He shipped the oars. He let the skiff drift.

"All right," he said to the river, to the chest, to the itch, to Teo, to nobody. "You're mine now. Whatever that costs."

He rowed back and stowed the chest under his berth, beneath the floorboard that lifted, wrapped in his spare sailcloth, weighted with the tool bag. Then he cooked rice with the last of the dried fish and ate it watching the banks slide past as he took the *Tithe* upriver, against his usual habit, away from the lowland markets and the easy fees, toward the hill country where the river narrowed and the towns got poorer and stranger and further from the roads the Optimists liked to travel.

He told himself it was strategy. Shelter ahead of the main monsoon body, a hull that needed patching, waters where a lone boat drew no attention. All true. None of it the reason.

The reason was asleep under his floorboards, wearing a black sun, addressed to no one alive.

Two days later, in the drowned town of Lowmark, a boatwright named Sefa would run her thumb along the iron binding of that chest and look at him with eyes that missed nothing, and the cost of his decision would begin arriving—first in installments, then all at once, the way floods do.

But that evening there was only the river, high and glittering, and a man alone on a small boat, heading upstream into weather, lighter of purse than ever and heavier of cargo than he knew.
