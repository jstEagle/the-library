## 5. Drowned Town

The barometer had been falling all morning, the needle sagging like a tired shoulder, and Isandro Yarrow read it the way other men read scripture: as a warning with his name in it.

The main body of the monsoon was still two days out—maybe three if the high country slowed it—but its outriders were already combing the sky into long gray braids, and the Kilow had come up a hand's width since noon, brown and muscular, carrying fence posts and a drowned dog and somebody's kitchen chair spinning past his bow like furniture fleeing a burning house.

He needed walls around him that weren't canvas. He needed a hull out of the current before the river decided to keep him.

The charts showed nothing worth stopping for between the Reach and the uplands—a blank stretch the mapmakers had abandoned along with everything else they couldn't tax. But charts were written by people who'd never needed shelter badly enough to look sideways, and sideways was where Lowmark lived.

He smelled it before he saw it: woodsmoke and tar and something frying in deep oil, a smell so aggressively domestic it made his stomach clench like a fist. Then the town swung into view around a bend gone soft with willow, and there it was—a town that had lost an argument with the river and refused to concede.

Half of Lowmark stood on ground. The other half stood on stilts above brown water, houses on legs like herons, connected by walkways that rose and fell with the season on counterweighted pilings. Someone had painted the waterline marks on every stilt in cheerful colors—green for ordinary years, yellow for bad ones, and up near the eaves of the oldest houses, a single red band that nobody talked about. Boats were moored where streets should be. A man fished off what had clearly once been a porch. Laundry snapped between two rooftops over open water, shirts and trousers swelling in the wind like men drowning and being resurrected, over and over.

Isandro brought the *Maren Voss* in slow, testing the channel with the pole, and docked at a low wharf built from three generations of salvaged planking, each board a different age and opinion about nails.

"You'll want to tie off twice," said a voice from under the wharf. "River's in one of her moods."

He looked down. A boy of maybe ten sat on a piling with a fishing line, utterly unbothered by the rising water or the stranger poling a scarred salvage boat into his afternoon.

"Twice," Isandro agreed, and tied off twice.

* * *

Lowmark took some getting used to. The dry half of town climbed a hill in crooked terraces—the market, the chapel, a tavern called the Drowned Rat whose sign depicted a rat wearing a tiny life vest, which told Isandro most of what he needed to know about local humor. The wet half went about its business over the water with the unhurried confidence of people who had stopped waiting for things to return to normal and had instead decided this *was* normal, and normal could go hang.

He walked the plank-walkways looking for a boatwright, because the *Maren Voss* needed one. The storm cell he'd chased upriver for salvage fees had taken its cut—always did—and she was shipping water at the port seam, slow but patient, the way debts shipped interest. He'd patched her twice since dawn with tarpaulin and prayer, and prayer had never once held at sea.

The third boathouse he came to was the biggest, built out over the water on thick black stilts stained with decades of tar, its doors tall enough to swallow a river barge whole. Inside, lamplight and the smell of hot pitch. Outside, stacked on a floating raft-dock, a graveyard of hulls in various states of resurrection.

A woman was working on one of them, upside down, more or less—she was lying inside the overturned shell of a skiff, driving caulking into a seam with short, vicious, economical strokes. She wore leather aprons over rolled sleeves, her forearms were ropey and scarred, and her gray-streaked hair was tied back with what appeared to be a piece of rigging.

She heard him before he said anything. They always did, the good ones.

"If you're here to sell me insurance," she said, without emerging from the skiff, "the last man who tried is holding up a bridge piling downstream."

"I'm here about my hull."

"What's wrong with it?"

"Port seam, weeping. Took weather two nights running."

"How slow?"

"Couple inches an hour in a blow. Less when calm."

She backed out of the skiff, sat up, and looked at him. She had a broad, sun-cured face, eyes the flat gray of river slate, and the particular stillness of someone who assessed everything and committed to very little. Her gaze went past him, through the open doors, to the *Maren Voss* tied at the wharf, and read her the way he read weather.

"That's a salvage boat," she said. "Old pattern. Kilow-built, forty years back if she's a day." She stood, wiping her hands on her apron. "You've been keeping her alive with stubbornness and string."

"Tarpaulin," Isandro said. "Mostly."

"Mm." She came toward him at a walk that wasn't hurrying and couldn't have been stopped by anything less than a landslide. "Sefa," she said. "This is my yard. You're standing on my raft, which floats, so mind your weight."

"Isandro Yarrow."

"Yarrow." She tried the name like biting a coin. "That a river family?"

"No."

"Where from, then?"

"Around."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile; more like the place a smile kept its tools. "Around," she repeated. "Well, Around. Let's look at your seam."

* * *

She hauled the *Maren Voss* onto the marine railway herself, hand-cranking the winch while he scrambled to help and was waved off with a look that suggested his help was worth approximately one-tenth of his weight in ballast. The boat rose dripping from the river into the lamplight, and Sefa walked her length once, slowly, trailing one hand along the planking like a physician taking a pulse.

Then she crouched at the port seam, pressed her thumb against the weeping joint, tasted the water that beaded there, and frowned.

"This isn't storm damage."

"It was a bad night."

"This is old work failing. Somebody patched this seam before you—badly—with the wrong wood and no bedding compound, then painted over it and hoped." She looked up at him. "How long have you had her?"

"Three years."

"And bought her how?"

"Salvage. She was half-sunk."

"Half-sunk," Sefa said, "and the man who sold her to you knew exactly why." She straightened, cracked her knuckles one hand against the other, and delivered the verdict with the gentleness of a falling anchor: "You haven't got a leak, Yarrow. You've got a lie that's been soaking for three years, and it's coming apart. I can fix it proper. Two days' work, real materials. It'll cost you."

"How much?"

She named a figure. It was fair. That was somehow worse—if she'd gouged him, he could have hated her and left. Instead he stood there doing arithmetic he didn't like, counting what remained of the salvage fee after repairs to the engine mount and the mast step, and watching the number shrink toward the thin red line below which lived the Optimists and their smiling arithmetic.

"Problem?" Sefa asked. She asked it neutrally, but her eyes weren't neutral at all. They were doing to his face what they'd done to his hull: finding the seams, pressing gently, noting what wept.

"Just money," he said. "Like everyone."

"Everyone says that. Most people mean different things by it." She pulled a chalk stub from her apron and began marking the hull with quick white strokes, mapping the rot. "Some mean they want a new sail. Some mean they gambled. Some mean they owe people who shouldn't be owed." Chalk. Chalk. Her voice stayed conversational, almost idle, the way a current stays calm right before the drop. "Which kind are you?"

"The kind that's leaving in two days either way."

"Uh-huh." She marked another seam. "You know what I notice about men who are leaving in two days? They always tell me. Unprompted. Like they're rehearsing it." She straightened and looked at him full-on, and there was nothing cruel in her face, which made it harder, not easier. "You've got the look of a man sleeping in shifts. One eye on the door. I've fed a dozen of you through this yard over the years. Runners, debtors, one poor fool hiding from a wife who could track a fox across open water. You all say 'around.' You all say 'two days.'"

"And do they all stay?"

"Some." She turned back to the hull. "The smart ones don't."

* * *

He should have paid for the patch job in advance, slept aboard, and slipped out before light. That was the playbook. Three years of playbook. Keep moving, keep small, keep unmemorable. A man the Optimists couldn't find was a man who didn't pay interest, and Isandro had gotten very good at being a rumor with a boat.

But the rain started that evening—not the monsoon itself, just its scouts—and the wind came up the valley with a sound like a freight train clearing its throat somewhere over the hills, and the *Maren Voss*, half-hauled in Sefa's yard with her guts opened to the lamplight, could not go anywhere at all.

So he stayed the night in the yard's loft, above the boathouse, in a room that smelled of cedar shavings and lamp oil, and listened to the first hard rain hammer the roof like impatient fingers.

Near midnight he gave up on sleep and came down into the workshop, thinking he'd be alone, and found Sefa there too, sitting on an upturned keel with a mug of something steaming, mending a net by lamplight with fingers that moved faster than seemed lawful.

"Can't sleep either?" she said. Not a question. She nodded at a second mug sitting on the bench beside her, already poured.

He looked at it. "You were expecting me?"

"I was expecting whoever couldn't sleep." She pushed it toward him with her foot. "Sit down before you drip on my floor."

He sat. The drink was tea cut with something sharper, and it landed in his chest like a small warm detonation. Rain drummed the great doors. Somewhere overhead a shutter banged and settled. In the flooded street outside, the river breathed against the pilings—*in, out, in, out*—and Isandro realized he was listening to it the way you listen to a sleeping animal in the room: warily, and then, despite yourself, comforted.

"Sefa," he said.

"Yarrow."

"Why do you live here? Half-drowned town, red lines on the stilts, monsoon coming. There's nothing here anyone would fight for."

She didn't look up from the net. "You've got it backwards. Nobody fights for places worth fighting for. Places become worth fighting for because somebody stayed and fought." A knot went through her fingers, tight and clean. "Lowmark flooded thirty years ago and the government wrote us off—sent a clerk with a map and a checkbook, offered everyone relocation money to abandon the wet half. Half took it. Other half burned the clerk's boat." She shrugged. "We've been here since. River takes a street every few years. We build the street back. It's not complicated. It's just not easy, and most people confuse the two."

Isandro drank his tea and said nothing, and the silence sat between them without demanding anything, which was so foreign an experience that it took him several minutes to recognize it.

"My brother drowned," he heard himself say.

It came out of him the way water comes through a failed seam—all at once, having been building pressure for years. He hadn't meant to say it. He hadn't said it aloud in three years, not to anyone, not even to the river.

Sefa's hands paused on the net. Just paused. She didn't turn to look at him, didn't soften her face into sympathy, didn't do any of the things that would have let him slam the hatch shut again.

"He was sick first," Isandro went on, staring into the mug. "Long sickness. I borrowed to bury him. Real money, from people who call themselves Optimists, which should have been warning enough. And the interest—" He laughed, short and ugly. "You'd appreciate it, actually. It compounds like floodwater. Every year the river rises a little higher on the debt, and every year I move upstream ahead of it, and every year it's a little closer behind."

"And the boat?"

"Hers. The *Maren*. We were going to run salvage together, after he got better." He turned the mug in his hands. "I keep her floating because sinking her feels like burying him twice."

The rain filled the pause. Sefa set down the net at last and looked at him, and whatever was in her face, it wasn't pity. It was recognition—the look of one person who has carried water a long way meeting another.

"You know what I think, Yarrow?" she said. "I think you've been so busy running from the flood behind you that you never noticed you're already wet."

He opened his mouth to answer—

And the lamplight caught something behind her, on the far wall of the workshop, where tools hung among a scatter of oddments nailed up like trophies: a broken oar, a horseshoe, a child's carved bird. And a scrap of cloth, faded almost to ghost-gray, embroidered with a symbol he'd seen only once before, four days ago, stitched in gold thread on documents he should have thrown back into the river and hadn't.

A black sun.

His tea went cold in his hands.

"You've traveled," he managed, nodding at the wall, keeping his voice level with an effort that cost him more than the confession had. "That piece. Where's it from?"

Sefa glanced at the cloth, and for just a moment—less than a moment—her hands went still in a way that had nothing to do with mending.

"Oh," she said lightly, too lightly, picking the net back up, "that's been in this yard longer than I have. Come on. Finish your tea. And tomorrow, Yarrow—you're helping me caulk. Two days means two days, and I put you to work."

Above them the rain thickened, and out in the dark the river rose another inch against the red line, and Isandro drank his tea and did not sleep, and wondered what else this drowned town was keeping below its waterline.
