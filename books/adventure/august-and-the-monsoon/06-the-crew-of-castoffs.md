## 6. The Crew of Castoffs

The smell reached him before he was fully awake—onions in fat, something with pepper, bread turning gold on a griddle—and for three disoriented seconds Isandro Yarrow lay in the loft above Sefa's boathouse and could not remember a single reason to move.

Then memory did its work. The chest under the floorboards. The seal stamped black as an eclipse. Corvane's name somewhere behind him like a splinter working toward the heart.

He sat up. The loft was built into the rafters above the boatwright's workshop, reachable by a ladder that groaned under his weight. Rain ticked against the shingles in a rhythm too gentle to be the monsoon proper—just the season clearing its throat. Through the one small window he could see Lowmark spread out below: stilt houses standing hip-deep in brown water, walkways connecting them like stitches, laundry strung between poles and refusing, out of pure spite, to dry. Somewhere a dog was barking at the river. The river ignored it.

He came down the ladder into warmth and noise.

The workshop's long table had been cleared of tools and laid with plates. A man stood at the iron stove with his back to the room—a big man, gray-shot beard, forearms like mooring posts, moving with a precision that didn't match his size. He cooked the way Isandro imagined siege engineers worked: everything measured, nothing wasted, total command of a small territory.

"You're up," the man said without turning. "Good. Eggs don't wait and neither do I."

"Sefa said there'd be food," Isandro managed. "She didn't say there'd be a garrison."

"Brill," the man said, still not turning. "Twenty-two years in the Duke of Maraval's Fourth Foot, which is a fancy way of saying I dug latrines and got shot at. Now I cook." He turned then, and Isandro saw the old burn scar wrapping his left wrist like a bracelet of melted wax. "Sit. You're the salvage man. Sefa says your hull was held together with prayer and tar."

"Mostly tar."

"Tar's honest." Brill set a plate down hard enough to rattle the table. Eggs, flatbread, onions gone dark and sweet, a smear of something red and vicious. "Prayer isn't. Eat."

Isandro ate. It was, without competition, the best meal he'd had in two years, and he resented how much it undid him. Food made by someone who expected you to come back tomorrow—that was a different substance entirely from food bought at a dockside stall with one eye on the road behind you. He kept his face neutral anyway. Old habit. You didn't let people see what fed you; they learned where to cut.

At the far end of the table, a boy of maybe fourteen was eating with the same focused silence. He had Sefa's jaw and her dark, level eyes, and when Isandro glanced over, the boy looked back without flinching, cataloguing him the way you'd catalogue a plank with a suspicious grain.

"That's Tam," Brill said. "Sefa's sister's boy. Doesn't talk. Never has, far as anyone knows. Don't let it fool you—he hears everything, and he'll have your measure before you've finished breakfast." Brill leaned in, lowering his voice to a stage whisper the boy could obviously hear perfectly. "First thing he told me about you—" Tam tapped the table twice and made a quick gesture, hand opening like a bird leaving a fist. Brill snorted. "He says you sleep like a man listening for hoofbeats. Accurate?"

Isandro looked at Tam. Tam looked back, unblinking, chewing.

"Accurate," Isandro admitted.

Tam nodded once, satisfied, and went back to his eggs.

* * *

Sefa appeared mid-morning and put him to work, which was, Isandro would come to understand, how she welcomed people. His hull patch had been done properly but she redid half of it anyway, narrating every mistake without cruelty, the way a surgeon might describe a wound while closing it. By afternoon he'd caulked two seams, replaced a strake she pronounced "an insult to the tree it came from," and learned that Lowmark's whole economy floated on the annual rhythm of high water: boats in summer, repairs in fall, levee work when the monsoon came, and a stubborn, unglamorous trade in ferrying goods along streets that were canals four months of the year.

"Everyone here came from somewhere else," Sefa said, driving a fastening with three efficient blows. "Town like this collects people the way a snag collects driftwood. Some of it's good wood. Most of it just needed somewhere to stop."

"And you're the snag."

"I'm the boatwright." She handed him the mallet. "Snags don't charge."

He worked until his shoulders burned and found, to his quiet alarm, that he'd stopped calculating escape routes. There was a version of himself that noticed things like that—the drifter's reflex, always auditing a place for exits—and that version had gone strangely quiet since the night before, since Sefa had looked at the chest's seal and said *that's not salvage* and then, instead of asking questions, had simply made up a bed.

He didn't trust it. Quiet wasn't safety. Quiet was just the storm drawing breath.

* * *

The clerk arrived at dusk.

Isandro heard the walkway boards first—quick light steps, someone who knew exactly which planks were sound—and then a young woman came through the door shaking rain off a hooded cloak, already talking.

"—and if Hask won't release the ferry permits before the water tops the second mark, the whole east quarter is going to be rowing to market like it's a festival, and I told him, I told him plainly, Odile, you say, but does anyone listen to a—"

She stopped.

She'd seen the chest.

It sat on the workbench where they'd left it, oilcloth thrown over it loosely, and Isandro watched something happen to her face in the space of a single breath. The color left it in a specific order—cheeks first, then lips, then the steady animation around her eyes—until she looked like a woman reading her own name on a list of the dead.

"Odile?" Brill was up off his stool fast for a big man.

She didn't answer him. She crossed the room like a sleepwalker and pulled the oilcloth back, and there it was in the lamplight: the black sun, its rays broken by the horizontal bar, pressed into the lead seal of the lid.

"Where," she said. Her voice had gone thin. "Where did this come from."

"River gave it up after the storm," Isandro said carefully. "Kilow Reach, upstream. Wedged in the reeds."

"Kilow Reach." She repeated it like she was testing whether the words held weight. Her fingers hovered over the seal without touching it, the way you'd hover over a stove you knew was hot. "This is Vhalorian. This is a state seal. Not a merchant's mark, not a family crest—the chancery seal of the Royal Archive of Vhaloria, third issue, and I know that because my grandmother had one exactly like it on the letters she burned the year I was born."

The room had gone very quiet. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"Your grandmother was Vhalorian," Sefa said. Not a question.

"Vhalorian the way everyone from there is now—which is to say from nowhere, because there is no there." Odile finally touched the seal, one fingertip, barely. "They took it off the maps before I could walk. My grandmother used to say the ink dried faster than the blood. She taught me the script anyway, in secret, on the backs of tax ledgers, so I grew up fluent in a language that officially never existed." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I ran away from a countinghouse in Pell's Crossing six months ago because a man asked me to verify some shipping credentials and the watermark was Vhalorian bond paper, and I've been running ever since, and now—" She turned and looked at Isandro, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified all at once. "Now the river has delivered the actual archive seal to a boathouse in Lowmark during monsoon season. Do you understand what this is? Do you have any idea who else will understand what this is?"

"I have some idea," Isandro said.

And he told them. Not all of it—he kept the Optimists' ledger numbers and the exact sum of his debt folded away, those were his wounds and not for the table—but he told them about the letter delivered by the smiling boy, about the word *salvage* written in a creditor's careful hand, about the feeling of being followed through three towns by men who never hurried because they never needed to. He told them about pulling the chest from the reeds and the voice in his head that said *throw it back*, and how he hadn't.

"So someone knows you have it," Sefa said.

"Someone always knows. That's the business model." He rubbed his eyes. "There's a man named Corvane. I haven't met him. I've met his weather—you feel the pressure change before the front arrives. If he's heard a salvage man pulled anything sealed from the Kilow Reach, he'll come himself or send people who ask very polite questions."

"Polite questions we can handle," Brill said.

"No," Odile said. "You can't. You don't understand what he'd be. If this seal is real, if these papers are real—" she gestured at the chest "—then this isn't treasure. It's a claim. Whoever holds proof of the royal line doesn't just own history. They own every Vhalorian descendant in every valley from here to the coast, because suddenly those people are heirs instead of refugees, and heirs can be crowned—" her voice dropped, "—or destroyed. My grandmother didn't burn her letters because she was ashamed. She burned them because keeping them was a death sentence with a delivery date."

Tam had come to stand beside her without anyone noticing. He looked at the seal for a long moment, then at Odile, and made a short series of gestures—fluid, certain, a language Isandro couldn't read but whose tone was unmistakable.

"He's right," Odile translated, blinking. "He says the river didn't bring it here by accident. Rivers bring things to the people who can carry them."

"That's either wisdom or superstition," Isandro said.

"It's both," Sefa said. "That's the best kind."

* * *

Supper was Brill's doing again—river fish baked with fennel, potatoes smashed with garlic, a jug of sharp cider that made even Tam's mouth pucker. And somewhere between the fish and the second jug, the evening changed shape without anyone deciding it should.

It started with Odile teaching them a Vhalorian grace, or trying to. "You don't say thanks before eating, you say *may this hold us together*—it's a wish, not a debt. Everything in their language is a wish. There's no word for 'owe.'" She wrote it in the air with her finger, tracing shapes Isandro couldn't follow, and Tam copied her instantly, his hands quick and sure, and then he invented a variation that made her laugh so hard she had to put down her cup.

"What did he say?" Isandro asked.

"He signed 'may this hold us together' and then added 'especially the cider.'"

Brill told the story of the worst meal he ever cooked—army story, forty men, rain, a mule named Corporal who ate the entire salt ration—and Sefa argued with him about the correct angle for scarfing a joint the way other families argue about politics, with heat and history and no real anger in it. Tam corrected Brill's knife grip twice and was ignored twice and glowed about it. The lamp burned low. Rain came and went against the roof like a tide breathing.

And Isandro sat in the middle of it with his plate empty and his hands quiet, and felt something he had no clean name for.

It wasn't happiness. Happiness was a harbor-town word, a paid-in-full word. This was smaller and stranger: it was the realization that no one at this table had asked him what he was worth. Brill had fed him before knowing his name. Sefa had patched his boat before knowing his debts. Tam had taken his measure and apparently decided the tally was acceptable. For one evening—for this one evening, he reminded himself, because evenings like this were rented, never owned—he was not a debtor, not a fugitive, not a man with a price on his time. He was just the fourth chair at the table.

He caught himself memorizing it. The way the lamp swung slightly with the wind. Odile's hands still tracing letters. Brill's laugh, low and rolling, like water over stones. Sefa watching all of them with an expression she thought was stern and wasn't.

That was the danger. He knew it the way he knew weather. A man who starts memorizing a room has already decided to stay in it, and staying was the one thing Isandro Yarrow had trained himself out of for two straight years. You didn't memorize rooms. You noted the exits.

But nobody at this table was watching the door. And slowly, treacherously, neither was he.

* * *

Later, when the others had gone up—Odile to the loft's corner curtain, Tam to his alcove, Brill snoring within minutes like a landslide settling—Isandro stayed at the table with the lamp and the chest.

He lifted the lid again. Documents in that secret flowing script, a signet heavy enough to anchor a line, and the sealed letter addressed to no one alive. He should throw it back. Every instinct he owned said so. Weight in the hull, weight on the trail, weight on everyone sleeping above him right now.

From the alcove, a soft sound. Tam stood there in the dark, watching him watch the chest. The boy raised one hand and made a gesture Isandro hadn't learned yet—slow, deliberate, making sure he saw it. Then he pointed at Isandro, pointed at the chest, and pointed upstream, toward the black rain-hung dark where the river came from.

Then he went back to bed, leaving Isandro alone with the lamp and the understanding that the mute boy had just read him more accurately than any creditor ever had.

Upstream. Someone up there was meant to carry this.

Outside, far down the valley, thunder muttered—not the storm arriving, just the storm thinking about it—and Isandro sat in the warm ruin of the evening and wondered how many days of this a man was allowed before the bill came due.
