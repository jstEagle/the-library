## 7. Price of Curiosity

Odile had stolen her first document at nine years old.

It was a shipping manifest in her grandmother's house in Vessany, back when Vessany still had a name on the maps and a customs office and a garrison that flew a flag nobody believed in anymore. Her grandmother kept it folded inside a book of psalms, and Odile had copied it out letter by letter onto the back of a school exercise because she wanted to know why her grandmother's hands shook when she touched it. The manifest listed cargo: forty-one souls, embarked from the Kilow Reach, destination unrecorded. Her grandmother had been one of them. Odile hadn't understood that yet. She'd only understood that paper could hold a person the way amber holds an insect, and that some people would burn the world before they let the amber be opened.

Twenty years later, crouched in the loft of a boatwright's boathouse with a stolen candle stub and a borrowed pen, Odile felt the old hunger rise in her again like floodwater.

The chest sat below her, wedged under Isandro Yarrow's cot where he thought no one would look. Everyone looked. That was the trouble with drifters — they hid things in the obvious places because they'd never stayed anywhere long enough to learn how people actually searched. Tam had found it within an hour of Isandro's arrival and told her with two flicks of his fingers: *box. under bed. locked.* Brill had found it by tripping over it in the dark and swearing loud enough to wake the fish. Sefa had found it by looking at Isandro's face when Brill swore, which was the fastest way to find anything in Lowmark.

Odile had found it because she recognized the seal, and recognizing things was what she did. It was the whole shape of her life now: a runaway clerk who read everything and belonged nowhere, keeping her eyes down and her hand moving, copying other people's secrets into her own cramped shorthand until the secrets stopped feeling dangerous and started feeling like company.

But this seal — the black sun, twelve rays on a field of midnight blue wax gone grey with river water — this seal made her mouth go dry the way it had when she was nine.

She waited three days. Three days of mending nets with Sefa, of hauling sawdust for Brill, of watching Isandro Yarrow pretend he wasn't counting the hours until he could leave. Three days of the chest sitting there like a held breath. On the third night, Brill drank himself to sleep over his accounts, Sefa closed up the workshop with the finality of a woman bolting a door against the century, and Tam — faithful, sharp-eyed Tam — took his blanket down to the water to sleep in the skiff, as he did on warm nights, because the river talked to him in a language he trusted more than speech.

Odile lit her candle stub, went down the ladder on silent feet, and lifted the chest's lid with a clerk's tools: a thin blade, patience, and no conscience about locks that weren't hers.

* * *

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, the documents were better preserved than they had any right to be. The chest had been sealed well — waxed seams, a double bottom, the work of someone who expected it to be submerged and searched. She laid them out on the floorboards in candlelight: a genealogical roll written in the flowing court-hand of the old chancery; a bundle of letters tied with wire; a signet ring heavy enough to anchor a small boat, its face bearing the black sun; and the sealed letter addressed to no one alive, its wax unbroken, its surface furred with mold at one corner.

Her grandmother had taught her the court-hand. Not formally — nothing so dangerous as a lesson — but by making Odile address envelopes for her, letters to cousins scattered across three borders, each address written in that beautiful archaic script *so they'll know it comes from family*. So Odile read the genealogical roll the way other people read their mother tongue, and that was the first mistake, or the first mercy; she'd never decide which.

It was a register of descent. The House of Vhalor, traced through the fall — through the Year of Creditors, when the banks foreclosed on a kingdom and called it reform — through the dispersal, the renaming of towns, the burning of parish records. Someone had maintained this line through forty years of erasure. Names, dates, places of refuge. And not all of them dead. Not all of them even far.

Odile's pen stopped moving.

There, in the fourth generation past the fall, written in a fresher hand, ink barely browned: *Maren Oduya, b. Vessany-sur-Kilow, m. Tomas Oduya, issue: one daughter, Odile.*

She read it four times. The candle guttered. Somewhere below the floorboards the river moved against the pilings with its long patient breathing, and Odile sat very still in the way she'd learned as a child, the stillness of a girl who had learned early that being noticed was the same as being taken.

Her name. Her actual name, in a dead language's living hand, in a register of royal descent, in a chest pulled from the river a mile downstream from the town where she slept.

*One daughter, Odile.* Not her grandmother's name — Maren, then, was her mother, whom Odile had lost at six and barely remembered except as a smell of river-mint and a voice singing in the old tongue. Her grandmother had never spoken of it. Had never once, in all those years of psalms and manifests and letters addressed in court-hand, said the word *royal*, or *Vhaloria*, or anything but *family*, *our people*, *those who remember*.

Those who remember. Odile had thought it meant recipes and songs and the proper way to fold a mourning cloth. She had copied documents her whole life without understanding she was copying her own inheritance.

She should stop. Every instinct trained into her by twenty years of surviving bureaucracies said stop: put it back, close the lid, forget the names, let the drifter carry his cursed box wherever drifters go. Knowledge was weight. Knowledge got people visited at night by polite men with clean boots. She had watched it happen in Vessany — the archivists' men, the ones who came asking after records with smiles like ledger lines, and afterward certain houses stood empty and certain names were never said again.

But her hand kept moving. That was the thing about her, the flaw at her center: she copied. She had never once in her life found a document she could leave unread. So she worked through the night, translating the court-hand into her private shorthand, page after page, and the night grew long and the candle burned to a coin, and the names accumulated in her notebook like silt.

And near dawn, in the last bundle of letters, she found the second thing.

* * *

The letters were correspondence between the register's keeper — a name rendered only as *the Under-Chancellor, in exile* — and various scattered households of the old blood. Most were formal, coded, dull: confirmations of safe arrival, requests for tithes to maintain the record, warnings against contact with "the Society of Optimists," which made Odile's skin crawl, because everyone knew the Optimists, everyone owed the Optimists, and no one had ever suggested they were anything but moneylenders.

Then a letter dated eleven years ago, to a household in the upper valley:

*You ask whether the record can be used to restore what was taken. I will answer plainly, since plainness has cost me everything else. The register is not a weapon. It is a witness. Any man who takes it up to crown himself will find it crowns others instead — every name in it is a claim equal to his own, and there are more of us than him. Keep your children ignorant of their names if you must, to keep them safe. But do not let any single hand gather these papers together. A kingdom can survive many heirs. It cannot survive one thief.*

Below it, in different ink, a later annotation, hurried, the handwriting deteriorating:

*Corvane has the chancery lists. He has been buying debts to buy men. If the chest reaches him he will not restore the line — he will edit it. Burn this if you cannot protect it.*

Odile set the letter down with fingers that did not feel like her own.

Corvane. She didn't know the name. But she knew the type the way a fisherman knows weather: the archivist-enforcer, the polite collector, the man whose power lived in paperwork. She had fled one of those. The Optimists' regional office in Corvane's style would have been a place of immaculate shelves and soft voices, where your entire life could be reduced to a figure and the figure could be sold.

And the chest — Isandro's chest, the drifter's salvage, his ticket out of debt or his death warrant — was exactly the thing the letter warned against letting any single hand gather. Except it already had been gathered. It was gathered right now under a cot ten feet from where she knelt, and the man who owned it didn't know what it was, and somewhere upstream or downstream a man named Corvane was already editing.

She looked at the genealogical roll again. At her own name. At the other names — the Oduyas of the upper valley, the Sarns, the Ilvese family at the weir settlement, names that mapped onto families she'd drunk cider with at the autumn market without knowing why the old women watched her when she laughed. People in this valley. Possibly people in this town. Possibly, though her mind shied from it like a horse from a snake, herself.

Whoever held the chest could crown them. Or destroy them. Or — worse than either — sell them, one name at a time, to a man who collected debts.

The candle died. She sat in the grey pre-dawn light and finished the last page anyway, because she was who she was.

* * *

She found Isandro at the water's edge just after sunrise, checking his hull caulking with the sour concentration of a man rehearsing his departure.

He was easier to talk to than most, she'd decided, precisely because he didn't want to talk. He listened the way exhausted people listen — completely, gratefully, relieved to be spared the performance of conversation. She'd watched him with Sefa: the boatwright needled him and he absorbed it like ballast, and something in his shoulders came down a quarter inch each day he stayed. He was going to leave anyway. They always left. But she had maybe a day before he went, and the truth couldn't stay in her notebook.

"Yarrow." She sat down on the piling beside him, close enough that he had to acknowledge her, far enough that she could stand quickly. "Your chest. The one you're hiding under your cot like a guilty dog hides a bone."

His hands stilled on the caulking iron. "I don't know what you—"

"I transcribed it. All of it. Last night." She kept her voice level, conversational, pitched under the sound of the river — habits from another life. "I'm telling you first because you own it, and because you seem like the only person here who doesn't already have a stake. That won't last."

He turned and looked at her properly then, and she gave him credit: fear came into his face and discipline took it back out, fast, like a tide. "You broke into my property."

"I broke open a national emergency." She pulled the notebook from her coat and held it up, shut. "Do you know what the black sun is?"

"A seal off a drowned country. I figured somebody rich lost it."

"Vhaloria wasn't rich. Vhaloria was *foreclosed*." She heard her own bitterness come through and didn't soften it. "Forty years ago the creditors' consortium dissolved the crown, redrew the maps, renamed the towns, and burned the records. My grandmother escaped the burning. She taught me the court-hand those documents are written in without ever telling me why. Do you understand? I read your salvage haul, Yarrow, and it's a register of the royal line of Vhaloria, maintained in secret since the fall, and it isn't finished history. There are living names in it. Descendants. Whole families, here in this valley, who may not know what they are." She paused. "My name is one of them."

The caulking iron hung motionless in his hand. Down the levee, Tam was mending a net, watching them with the frank attention he gave everything, too far away to hear.

"So sell it," Isandro said finally, and his voice was rough. "That's what it's worth, isn't it? To whoever wants it. I've got creditors who'd call it a miracle."

"That's the second thing." She opened the notebook to the marked page and held it out. He didn't take it, so she read aloud, quietly. "*If the chest reaches him he will not restore the line — he will edit it. Burn this if you cannot protect it.* The keeper of this record was afraid of a specific man. An archivist who's been buying debts to buy men. Named Corvane."

Something moved behind Isandro's eyes — recognition, quick and buried, the flinch of a man who knows a predator's name. Odile filed it away. Everyone on this river owed someone; she wasn't naive. But she pressed on.

"Here's what I've pieced together, and I've been piecing things together since I was nine years old, so believe me when I say the arithmetic is sound. Whoever holds this chest holds proof of a bloodline. Proof can crown. Proof can also destroy — every name in that register is a target, an heir, a rival, a hostage, depending on the ambition of the reader. Your box isn't treasure, Yarrow. It's a list of everyone in this valley who could be made king, and therefore everyone who could be made dead. And you dragged it into a town full of stilt houses one monsoon away from drowning, and you're planning to *leave*."

"I'm planning to survive," he said. "There's a difference."

"Not anymore. Not since you pulled it out of the reeds." She stood, brushing splinters from her coat, and looked down at him — the drifter with the competent hands and the haunted arithmetic, the man who'd been running so long he'd mistaken velocity for safety. "You should have thrown it back. We both know it. But you didn't, and neither did I, and now we both know what it is, and knowing is the burden. You don't get to set it down. You only get to choose who carries it with you."

He stared at the river for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was quieter. "Why tell me? Why not Sefa? She'd know what to do. She always knows what to do."

"Because Sefa will want to fight, and Brill will want to drink, and Tam will want to help, and none of them can read what's in that box." Odile tucked the notebook away, and felt the weight of it settle against her ribs like a second heart. "I'm the only one who knows exactly how much trouble you've salvaged. Which means I'm the one who gets to be afraid in detail. Consider this me sharing."

A ghost of something crossed his face — almost a smile, gone before it landed. "You always talk like a legal brief?"

"My grandmother said I argued before I walked." She turned to go, then stopped, because there was one more thing, the thing that had kept her awake past the candle, and it wouldn't keep past noon. "Yarrow. The letter mentioned the Society of Optimists. Buying debts. Buying men. If your creditors are who I think they are, then the man hunting you and the man hunting this chest might be the same man. And if they are—" she met his eyes, "—then running isn't escape. Running is delivery. You'd be carrying it straight to him."

She left him there with his caulking iron and his silence, and climbed back up toward the house, and did not look at the levee road, where, far off in the morning haze, she could just make out two figures walking side by side at the easy unhurried pace of men with clean boots and polite questions.

They were still a mile out. They'd reach Lowmark by midday.

Odile went to find Sefa.
