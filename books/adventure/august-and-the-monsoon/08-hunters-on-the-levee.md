## 8. Hunters on the Levee

Isandro saw them before they saw him—or thought he did, which amounted to the same thing in his trade. Two figures on the levee road, a mile out, walking with the particular economy of men who had never once needed to hurry because the world always came to them. Clean boots. Dry cloaks despite the drizzle. One carried a ledger case slung crosswise like a soldier's pack; the other carried nothing at all, which was worse.

He was under Sefa's boathouse, on his knees in the mud with a caulking iron, when he spotted them. He kept working. His hands knew how to look busy even when his mind was running arithmetic.

Two men. Walking, not riding—so they wanted to be underestimated, or they'd come up from the barge landing and horses were a luxury this far into the drowned country. The one with nothing in his hands would be the talker. The one with the ledger would be the memory. In Isandro's experience—and he had a decade of it, most of it bad—the memory was the dangerous one. Talkers you could feed. Memories you could only starve.

*They know something,* he told himself, driving oakum into a seam that didn't need it. *Or they know nothing and are fishing. Decide which, and decide fast.*

The chest was ten feet above him, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged behind Tam's disassembled skiff frames where only someone looking for a shape that wasn't a skiff would ever find it. He'd moved it twice since Odile went pale over the black sun stamped into its lid. Twice was habit. Three times would be panic.

He put the caulking iron down, wiped his hands on his thighs, and climbed the ladder into the grey morning.

* * *

Sefa met him at the top. She'd been watching the road too; he could tell by the way she stood, arms folded, weight on her back foot, the posture of a woman measuring distance without admitting it.

"Company," she said.

"Salesmen," Isandro said. "Probably selling insurance against the flood."

"Nobody sells insurance in Lowmark. Nothing here is worth insuring." She glanced at him sideways. "Except maybe what you pulled out of the river."

He said nothing, which was an answer, and she accepted it with a grunt and turned toward the house. "Brill's making breakfast. Eat. Whatever they're selling, don't sell it hungry."

Tam was already on the roof of the stilt house, perched above the shutters with a spyglass he'd made himself from two lengths of pipe and a lens salvaged off a dead man's spectacles. He signed down at Isandro—two fingers walking, then a flat hand held level. *Two men. Stopping.*

"Stopping where?" Isandro asked, then remembered Tam couldn't answer, and hated himself a little for the reflex. Tam, patient as driftwood, pointed down the road, then mimed knocking on a door. The Pell house. First stilt house on the levee. Old Marek Pell, who was deaf as a post and would invite anyone in for tea just to have someone to shout at.

"They'll work their way up the row," Odile said from the doorway. She had her clerk's satchel over her shoulder, as if she might need to take minutes. Her face was composed, but her knuckles were white around the strap. "Polite men always do. It makes the last door feel inevitable."

"Then we don't answer the last door."

"They'll knock anyway." She came out onto the walkway. Rain beaded on the railing between them. "Isandro. If they're who I think they are—if they're his—you should know that Corvane doesn't send collectors. Collectors want money. He sends readers. Men who read rooms, read faces, read what people almost say." She looked at him steadily. "Don't try to out-lie a reader. Just be boring. Boring is the one thing they can't use."

"I'm very good at boring," Isandro said. "I've been doing it my whole life."

"No," Odile said quietly. "You've been doing *running* your whole life. Boring is new to you. Try to learn it before midday."

* * *

They ate Brill's breakfast—smoked fish, river rice, bread gone dense from the humidity—and nobody talked much, and the not-talking had a shape to it, everyone listening past each other for boots on planking. Tam cleared the dishes. Brill sharpened a cleaver that was already sharp, the rhythmic shhk-shhk of it filling the silence like a second heartbeat. Odile sat by the window with a book she wasn't reading.

At half past eleven, the knock came.

It was a good knock. That was the first thing Isandro noticed. Three raps, evenly spaced, firm but apologetic—the knock of a man who had practiced until knocking sounded sincere. Brill answered the door with the cleaver still in his hand, openly, like a man who simply hadn't finished cooking, and Isandro decided in that instant that he liked Brill more than he'd admitted to himself.

There were two of them, as advertised. The tall one had a face like a closed ledger—long, ruled, nothing written on it that hadn't been approved. The shorter one smiled immediately and often, the way weather smiles before it turns.

"Morning," the smiler said. "Forgive the intrusion. We're traveling on behalf of certain commercial interests—recovering property, mostly debts, some collateral. Names are Vess and Halloway." He gestured at the tall one. "My associate handles the paperwork. You'd be the boatwright? Sefa Andrin?"

"You'd be wrong," Brill said. "She's out. I'm the cook."

"A cook with a cleaver and a soldier's stance," Vess said pleasantly. "This town is full of surprises. And you, sir?" His eyes found Isandro and settled there with the light, proprietary touch of a man appraising livestock. "You have the look of a working hand. Salvage?"

"Ferryman," Isandro said. "When there's water worth ferrying."

"And when there isn't?"

"There's always water. Sometimes it's just not going anywhere useful."

Vess laughed—a real laugh, or a copy so good it hardly mattered. "I like him, Halloway. May we come in out of the wet? Five minutes. We're asking after a colleague. A salvage man, works alone, goes by Yarrow. Last seen on the Kilow Reach, pulling things out of the flood that perhaps weren't his to pull."

Inside the house, Isandro felt the room shift around him—Odile's book turning a page that hadn't needed turning, Tam going very still by the stove, Brill's cleaver pausing for exactly one beat too long. Vess heard all of it. Isandro watched him hear it, watch the smile widen by a degree, and understood that the room had been the interview all along, and the questions were just decoration.

"Yarrow," Isandro said, frowning with the effort of recall. "Skinny fellow? Missing teeth?"

"Missing nothing. Sharp as a pike and twice as slippery."

"Don't know him." He shrugged, easy, bored. "Salvage men don't run in packs. Everyone's got their own stretch of river and they guard it like dogs. If there's a Yarrow working the Kilow, he wouldn't come down here. This stretch belongs to the Marlow brothers, and they'd bite him."

Halloway spoke for the first time. His voice was dry paper. "The Marlow brothers were arrested in Tarnholt six weeks ago. Debt fraud."

"Was that what it was?" Isandro scratched his jaw. "They told me they were going to visit family."

Something flickered behind Halloway's eyes—not amusement, but its cousin: the pleasure of a correct entry. "You knew them well enough to be lied to by them."

"I knew them well enough to lose forty crowns to them." Isandro let the bitterness show, real and unhidden, because it was true and true things make the best mortar for lies. "If you're collecting their debts, you're welcome to the list. Start with me."

That bought him something. He could see it land—Vess recalibrating, filing the ferryman under *grievance, financial, unrelated*. People who'd been cheated by the same machine rarely hid its cargo. It was a good lie built entirely out of truth, and Odile had been right about one thing: he wasn't lying so much as arranging.

"We may take you up on that," Vess said. "In fact—" he produced a card, heavy stock, embossed with a coiled rope-and-hook sigil, the Optimists' mark rendered harmless as stationery— "if your friend Yarrow comes through, word travels fast in towns like this. There's a finder's fee. Generous. Enough to settle a man's own small disappointments." His eyes touched Isandro's again, gently, like fingers checking a lock. "Forty crowns' worth, perhaps."

The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere below, the river breathed against the pilings.

"I'll listen for him," Isandro said.

"I'm sure you will." Vess set the card on the table, squared it neatly with the table's edge—Halloway's habit borrowed, or the other way around—and smiled his weather smile. "We'll be at the Anchorlight inn through tomorrow evening. Asking around. Towns like this always remember more than they think they do." He paused at the door, and turned, and for a moment the smile dropped away like a mask slipping, and underneath was something older and colder and terribly patient. "The river gives things back, Mr.—?"

"Fenn," Isandro said. "Aldous Fenn."

"The river gives things back, Mr. Fenn. It's the one honest creditor left." The smile returned, seamless. "Good day."

* * *

They watched from the shutters until the two figures dwindled down the levee road toward the Anchorlight's leaning sign. Then Sefa came in from the back, dripping, having circled the whole town on the walkways to see where else they knocked. They'd knocked everywhere. They'd paid old Marek Pell in coin for his tea and his gossip, and Pell, being deaf, had shouted at them happily about everything except anything useful.

"A day," Sefa said. "Maybe two. They'll drink at the Anchorlight, tip the ferryman, tip the kids, and by tomorrow night they'll know every stranger who's passed through since spring thaw. Which is you."

"Which is why I'm gone at first light," Isandro said. "Upstream, before they're awake. They'll figure it out eventually, but by then I'm a rumor on the Kilow and the chest is nowhere they can follow."

"Upstream." Sefa said the word like she was tasting it. "Toward the narrows. Alone. In a patched hull, ahead of the monsoon's main body."

"I've run ahead of storms before."

"You've run *from* them before. Different animal." She crossed her arms. "And the chest goes with you, I suppose. Back to the river. Or to whoever's paying you now."

"Nobody's paying me. That's rather the point."

"Then who's it *for*, Isandro?" And there it was, the question he'd been dodging since the reeds, since the black sun, since Odile went white as birch bark over a name in a dead language. Who was it for. He didn't know. He knew only that throwing it back felt like drowning something that had survived forty years of water on purpose.

"It's for whoever it says it's for," he said finally. "The letter inside. It's addressed to no one alive. But 'no one alive' has a way of turning out to mean *someone*."

Sefa studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her shake her head slowly, the way you shake your head at weather. "First light," she said. "Fine. But you'll eat before you go. Brill will insist, and arguing with Brill costs more time than breakfast does."

* * *

He spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation felt wrong in a way he couldn't name until dark.

It wasn't the boat. The boat was sound—Sefa's patchwork had made her better than she'd been when he stole—bought her, he corrected himself, *bought* her fair in Tarnholt, though the deed was probably in a ledger somewhere with his name mispronounced on it. It wasn't the route; he knew the Kilow's moods upstream of Lowmark better than most men knew their mothers. It wasn't fear of Vess and Halloway, or even of the man behind them, whose name Odile still wouldn't say aloud but whose reputation had arrived ahead of him like pressure before a squall.

It was the house.

All afternoon, the ordinary machinery of the place went on around him, indifferent to his leaving, and every piece of it snagged him like a nail in current-driftwood. Tam on the roof, re-tying a shutter line, glancing down to check whether Isandro had noticed his knot-work—and Isandro had noticed, had raised a hand, had gotten back the small fierce nod of a boy who had decided, silently and irreversibly, that the drifter belonged to him. Brill pressing a parcel of smoked fish into the boat "for the road, no arguments," gruff as a landslide. Odile sitting across from him at supper translating three lines of the Vhalorian letter by lamplight, her voice low, the old language moving through the room like water through reeds—*and to whichever hand finds this, know that a nation is not its throne, a nation is its remembering*—and stopping, embarrassed, as if she'd been caught praying.

He lay in the bunk Brill lent him and listened to the rain start up again on the roof, soft and steady, and did the arithmetic he was best at.

Leave at first light. Upstream alone. No debts aboard except the ones in his head. By week's end he'd be a hundred miles gone, chest delivered or sunk, name changed, trail cold. It was a good plan. It was the plan he'd have made any year before this one, and it would have cost him nothing, because there would have been nothing here to cost.

That was the difference. That was the whole terrible difference.

He got up in the black hours before dawn, dressed in silence, took the fish parcel because refusing it would have required a conversation, and went down the walkway to the boathouse with his bag over his shoulder, stepping light on the wet boards, past Tam's door, past Odile's window with its guttering lamp, past the kitchen where Brill slept sitting up like a man expecting siege.

The boathouse smelled of tar and rain. His boat rocked gently at her mooring, patient, ready, asking nothing.

He stood on the dock with one foot on the gunwale and found that he could not make the other foot follow.

It was absurd. He'd left a hundred places. He'd left a brother's grave with creditors circling it; he'd left a woman in Tarnholt with a note and half his gear; he'd left jobs, rivers, whole years, without a backward glance, because backward glances were ballast and ballast sinks boats. Saying goodbye was a skill like any other and he had been fluent once.

But goodbye meant climbing these stairs in daylight and telling Tam he was going, and watching the boy's face do the thing faces did when they learned that adults lie by leaving. Goodbye meant telling Odile to stop translating, that the letters were safer unread, and seeing her look at him with that clerk's precision that missed nothing and forgave less. Goodbye meant telling Sefa—

He didn't know what telling Sefa meant. That was the problem. Every other departure in his life had had a shape he recognized. This one didn't. This one felt less like leaving a place than like amputating something that had only just finished growing onto him.

The rain thickened. Downriver, faint through the grey, he could hear the Anchorlight's door, voices carrying over water the way voices did before storms—laughter, a bottle, Vess buying the town one round closer to his answers.

One day. Maybe two. And then they'd come back up the levee with clean boots and no smiles at all.

Isandro stood on the dock, one foot on the boat, one foot on the world, and the storm clock ticking in his chest, and discovered that the hardest water he'd ever had to cross was four feet of black river between a mooring post and a life.

Behind him, up the walkway, a lantern came alight.

Footsteps—unhurried, deliberate, unmistakably hers—came down through the rain toward the boathouse, and Isandro Yarrow, who had outrun debt and weather and his own history for ten unbroken years, stood perfectly still and waited to be caught.
