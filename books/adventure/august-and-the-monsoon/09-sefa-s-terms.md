## 9. Sefa's Terms

Sefa had not slept a full night in eleven years, and she'd stopped apologizing for it somewhere around year four. The body learned what the river taught: the worst things came in the dark, and the people who slept through them woke up poorer or drowned. So she kept the old habit of her mother's mother—rising at the black hour, walking the walkways of Lowmark with a lantern hooded to a coin's width of light, checking the pilings, the lashings, the waterline marks she'd cut into the boathouse posts herself with a chisel the year Tam was born.

The marks were high tonight. Higher than they should be for the season, and the rain hadn't even started properly yet. She stood at the edge of the main walkway and listened to the river breathe, and the river sounded like a man running upstairs.

That was when she saw the light.

Not a lantern—a spill of it, thin and gold, leaking from under the door of the boat shed where the drifter's hull sat on the cradles. Isandro Yarrow. Third night now he'd come down after midnight, thinking nobody watched. First night he'd oiled the seams. Second night he'd re-lashed the cradles and checked the rudder pintles twice, the way a man does when he's counting his exits. Tonight there was packing involved. She could hear it—the particular soft scrape of canvas being folded around something box-shaped, the clink of a bilge lantern being stowed, the careful arrangement of a man making ready to vanish.

Sefa leaned against the post and let him work. There was no hurry. There was never any hurry at this hour; the hour belonged to her.

She thought about what Odile had told her three days ago, in the kitchen, with the door shut and Brill's radio turned up loud enough to cover them: *That seal is Vhalorian. That chest is worth more than this whole town, and somebody out there knows it exists.* Odile's hands had been shaking around her tea. Sefa had put her own hands flat on the table until the girl steadied. That was the job. That had always been the job—since Rennick drowned, since Tam went quiet, since the flood took the lower street and half the town with it and the government sent a man with a clipboard instead of a crew. Somebody had to keep their hands flat on the table.

She'd known men like Yarrow her whole life. Good hands, bad anchors. They came up the river with storm-chasing rigs and salvage hooks and stories about how they were just passing through, and every one of them was running from something with teeth. Most of them left before the monsoon because leaving was the only skill they trusted. The ones who stayed were the ones who found something heavier than the thing chasing them.

She didn't know yet whether Yarrow was going to be one of those. But she knew the chest was in that shed, and she knew two riders had come down the levee road yesterday asking polite questions with hard eyes, and she knew the arithmetic of storms better than anyone alive.

She unhooded her lantern and walked toward the shed, boots ringing on the planks, giving him every chance to hear her coming.

* * *

He heard her, all right. By the time she slid the door open he'd killed his lamp and gone still behind the hull, which would have worked if the shed weren't hers, if she hadn't known every shadow in it since before he was born. She hung her lantern on the hook by the door and looked at him over the curve of the keel.

"You're provisioning for two weeks," she said. "Hardtack, oilskins, spare line. You're planning to run the upper channel in the dark."

From the dark beyond the hull: "Couldn't sleep either?"

"I sleep fine. I sleep in pieces." She came around the hull. He was crouched by the cradles, and he didn't get up, which she noted—he wasn't ashamed exactly, but he wasn't going to make it easy to look at him either. "You're taking that chest out into the pre-monsoon current, alone, at night, with two Optimist riders camped at the ferry landing."

"Was," he said. "Past tense. Thanks for the review."

"The ferry landing, Yarrow." She let that sit. "You know who's at the ferry landing."

He got up slowly, brushing off his knees like the question was dust. In the lantern light his face had the gray look of a man who'd been doing sums all day and hated the total. "I know. I was going to go up the mill race instead. It's shallow but it's dark, and nobody watches it."

"Nobody watches it because it eats boats. There's a strainer across it from the spring floods—two dead cedars tangled together about forty meters up. You'd hit it blind in the dark and the river would keep the chest and give you back to me as kindling."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. A man redoing his sums and finding the numbers worse.

"So you've been watching me," he said.

"Every night. You're not subtle. You oil your hinges and then you don't oil them again, so the third night the hinge squeaks and tells me where you are." She crossed her arms. "Sit down. Not on the boat. On the sawhorse."

He sat. It cost him something visible—she could see the pride working against the exhaustion, the way it does in men who've been their own only ally too long. Pride always lost to exhaustion eventually. Her job was to be standing there when it did.

"Here's what I know," Sefa said. "Two days ago, riders came asking about salvage men. Yesterday, Odile told me what's in that chest—or what the seal says, which is worse than knowing, because not-knowing makes men imaginative. Today, Brill heard at the fish market that the Optimists have posted paper in Harrow Bend. A bounty." She watched his face do the thing faces do when a name catches up with them. "Not for the chest. For you. Debt recovery, they call it. Two hundred crowns, standing offer, plus expenses. That's more than most men on this river make in three years, Yarrow. That's a price that turns neighbors into informants and ferrymen into traps."

"How much for the chest?" His voice was flat. Working the problem, not the fear.

"They don't know about the chest. Yet. Which means right now, tonight, you are worth two hundred crowns and the chest is worth nothing to anybody but you—and whoever sealed it, who is forty years dead." She crouched so they were eye level, an old woman's trick that made young men uncomfortable and honest. "In a week, if Corvane's people connect the salvage report from Kilow Reach to the drifter who docked at Lowmark, both numbers change. And this town changes with them. Because they won't send two riders then. They'll send twelve, and a fire, and a lawyer."

"You don't know that."

"I buried eleven people after the last flood," Sefa said. "Six of them died because a timber company upstream wanted the levee money for a road. I know exactly what men with paperwork will do to a town that has something they want. I've read that book. It doesn't end well and the ending takes years."

The rain started then—not the monsoon, just the leading spit of it, ticking on the shed roof like fingers testing a lock. Both of them glanced up. Both of them did the same math. She saw him do it.

"Then I leave tonight," he said. "Before it matters."

"Through the mill race. With the strainer."

"Through the main channel, then. Past the ferry landing."

"With the riders awake, because the bounty went up today and ferrymen read bounties faster than scripture."

He stood up. Paced. Three steps and turn—that was all the shed allowed, and she watched him wear the track smooth. "So what's your play, boatwright? You've clearly got one. You didn't come down here to recite my obituary."

Sefa took her time. She took the lantern off its hook and held it up so the light fell full on his face, and she studied him the way she'd study a hull hauled out for survey—looking for the rot, sure, but also looking for the sound wood, because there was always sound wood, and the whole trade was building around it.

"My play," she said, "is that you're the best hull-man I've seen work in twenty years. I watched you fair those seams without a batten. You read wood. That's not teachable, and I need it." She let that land, watched him flinch slightly, because praise from a stranger lands harder than insult. "The levee on the north arm is rotten above the third buttress. I've got six able people and a town of farmers. The monsoon comes in two days—maybe three—and when it comes it'll sit on this valley for a week and push the river up four meters past anything we've marked. If the north arm goes, Lowmark drowns. Not floods. Drowns."

"And you want me sandbagging."

"I want you caulking, bracing, driving piles, and teaching my people what your hands know. I want you here through the monsoon. All of it. No slipping away at the black hour, no running the race, no heroic midnight passages." She set the lantern down between them on the sawhorse, so its light sat on the table like a stake in a card game. "And in exchange, the chest comes out of this shed and into a place no rider, no archivist, no Optimist with a warrant will ever find it. I've had thirty years to learn the hiding places in this valley. There's a flooded cellar under the old customs house that only fills at peak stage, and the only way in is underwater, and the only people who know the entrance are me and a dead man. Your chest sits in an oilskin wrap behind the fall-line, dry as a sermon, until the river drops. Nobody finds it. Not because they can't search—but because searching it costs more than it's worth, and men like Corvane's people only pay what they must."

He was quiet a long moment. The rain thickened on the roof.

"Why?" he said finally. "You met me nine days ago. I lied to you about my name for the first four."

"You gave a false name to a woman who never asked for it," Sefa said. "That's not lying, that's breathing. Why? Because I've watched you for nine days. You fixed Tam's skiff without being asked and told him it was already broken when you found it, so he wouldn't owe you. You eat what Brill puts in front of you and you thank him like it matters. When the riders came you stepped between them and Odile without thinking, and then you apologized for it, which is the part I liked." She straightened up. "You're a runner, Yarrow. Running's a fine trade. But runners die tired, and I've decided I'm tired of watching good hands leave this valley."

"That's not a deal," he said. "That's a trap with a floor in it."

"It's a levee," Sefa said. "It holds back everything trying to take what's yours. Same principle."

He laughed—one short, cracked note, surprised out of him—and then killed it, because laughing meant wanting, and wanting meant staying, and staying meant the Optimists could find him. She watched the whole war happen in his face in the space of two seconds. She'd seen it before. She'd lost it before, too, with other drifters, other springs. She waited anyway.

"No," he said.

There it was. He said it gently, which was almost worse—gentle refusal, refusal out of courtesy, the way you decline a meal you're starving for. "No. I appreciate—I'm not going to pretend I don't see what you're offering. But those riders followed me here. Whatever's coming, it's coming for me, and the smart thing—for you, for the kid, for all of you—is for me to be gone before it arrives. Take the chest, hide it, I don't care. Keep it. Sell it. It's brought me nothing but attention. But I'm not going to stand on your levee and draw fire onto your town. I've done the math on me, Sefa. I'm bad cargo."

"You're wrong about the math."

"I'm never wrong about the math." He said it with such tired certainty that she understood, suddenly, that this was the true wound—not the debt, not the chase, but the arithmetic itself. Somewhere back upriver a brother had died and a loan had been signed, and ever since, every kindness had been entered in a ledger as a liability. She'd seen ledgers like that. They always balanced. They always ended at zero.

"Then hear the rest of the math," she said, "and then go, if you still want to."

She picked up the lantern and opened the shed door. Rain came in sideways, silver in the light. Down the walkway, past the boathouse, the river ran high and fast and loud, and beyond it the dark hills held the road to Harrow Bend, and the ferry landing, and the two hundred crowns with his name under it.

"Bounty's two hundred crowns," she said. "A man who runs tonight gets caught inside a month—you know it, I know it, the whole river knows it, because runners run on routes and routes have tolls. A man who runs gets caught, and a caught man gives up the chest to save his skin, because that's what caught men do, and I don't blame them. So if you run, understand what you're carrying. You're not carrying a chest. You're carrying the address of everyone who helped you. Me. Odile. Tam. Brill. Every plank in this boathouse. You run, and you run *through* us, whether you mean to or not."

He had gone very still. Rain dripped off the doorframe between them.

"And if I stay," he said quietly, "I'm still carrying it. Just slower."

"No," Sefa said. "If you stay, you stop carrying it alone. That's the entire difference. That's the whole deal. Everything else is carpentry."

The wind gusted, and the river answered it, rising a hand's width against the pilings—she could hear the change, the deeper note in the current, the monsoon clearing its throat two valleys away. Two days. Maybe three.

Isandro looked at the river. Then—at last, and she could see what it cost him—he looked at the boathouse, at the dark windows of the kitchen where Odile's lamp still burned, at the skiff rack where Tam's little boat sat repaired and waiting. Looking was a kind of answer. She'd built her life on reading what people looked at.

"The bounty," he said. "Two hundred crowns. Where did Brill hear it?"

"Harrow Bend market. Posted paper on the ferry house, and a crier paid to read it aloud twice a day." She paused, and gave him the rest, because he'd earned the rest: "Brill also heard the description. Six feet, dark hair, scar through the left eyebrow. Travels alone. Answers to Yarrow." She watched him touch the scar, unconscious, the way men do. "They're close, Isandro. Closer than two riders at a ferry landing. The crier's been paid to work the whole valley. Someone wants you found before the roads wash out—which means someone expects the roads to wash out, which means Corvane's people know the monsoon schedule better than we do, and they're timing the hunt to end before the weather closes the valley."

"Or after," Isandro said slowly. "If they can't catch me before the storm..."

"Then they wait for the storm to do their work," Sefa finished. "A flooded town is an easy search. A drowned one is easier." She let the rain speak for a moment. "So. The chest goes in the water tomorrow, into the customs house cellar, before the stage rises past the entrance. And you—" she held out her hand, palm open, an old woman's rough palm with fifty years of rope and chisel written on it, "—you pick up a caulking maul at dawn and you help me hold back the sky. Or you walk out that door and down the levee, and I wish you luck with my whole heart, and I tell Odile you said goodbye, and we all take our chances."

The rain came harder. Somewhere down the valley, thunder rolled—long and low, like furniture being dragged across the floor of the world.

Isandro Yarrow looked at her hand for a long time. Then he looked past her shoulder, at the lamp in the kitchen window, and Sefa saw the exact moment the arithmetic broke—not because she'd argued him into it, but because the ledger had finally shown him a column he couldn't ignore: the one with names in it.

"If I stay," he said, "it's through the whole monsoon. And after."

"After," Sefa agreed, and did not smile, because smiling would have made it negotiable.

He took her hand. His grip was strong and cold from the night air, and it shook, just slightly, the way a man shakes when he sets down something heavy and doesn't yet know what to do with empty hands.

"Dawn, then," he said.

"Dawn," she said—and then, because the river had risen another finger's width while they talked, and because the night had one more thing to say, she lifted her lantern toward the walkway, where small bare feet were slapping closer through the rain.

Tam stood at the edge of the light, soaked to the skin, breath ragged, holding up Brill's second-best lantern with both hands. He was signing fast, faster than Sefa had ever seen him sign, and she read it twice to be sure, and the bottom dropped out of her stomach.

*Riders,* Tam signed. *Three of them. Coming up the levee road. Now.*
