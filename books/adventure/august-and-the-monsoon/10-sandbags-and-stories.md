## 10. Sandbags and Stories

The levee work began before dawn on the third day, and by the fourth Isandro had stopped counting days at all.

He learned the rhythm of it the way he'd once learned the rhythm of rivers: by surrendering to it. The town of Lowmark woke in layers. First Brill, whose kitchen fire glowed orange through the fog before anyone else stirred, sending up smells that pulled men and women out of their beds like fish on lines. Then Sefa, moving through the boathouse with a lantern, tapping hulls and testing knots, her voice low and certain as a tide table. Then everyone else—fishers, net-menders, the ferryman Osk with his bad knee, a dozen others whose names Isandro was slowly, unwillingly memorizing.

The sandbags came from the clay pits east of town. Old men filled them, children dragged them to the loading sledges, and the able-bodied carried them up the levee in a chain that never quite stopped moving. Isandro took his place in the chain without being asked. That was the trick of it, he realized somewhere around the second hundred bags: nobody asked. They simply made room for him, the way water makes room for a stone, and after a while he couldn't remember not being there.

"Shoulder lower," said Tam.

Isandro glanced down at the boy. Tam stood beside him in the mud, small and dark-eyed, holding two sandbags nearly as big as himself. He set them down, then pointed at Isandro's shoulders, then made a slow pressing motion with both palms.

"I know how to carry a bag," Isandro said.

Tam's expression didn't change. He picked up one of his own bags, slung it with an economy of motion that made the weight look like nothing, and walked ten paces up the levee with his spine straight as a mast. Then he looked back at Isandro and raised one eyebrow.

Isandro adjusted his shoulder. The next fifty bags went easier.

By midmorning he'd collected six more of Tam's signs—a flat hand for *stop*, a circling finger for *again*, two fingers walking for *go*, a fist tapped twice against the chest that Tam used so often Isandro finally asked Odile about it over lunch.

"It means *enough*," she said. "Or sometimes *that's good*. He invented half his vocabulary himself. His mother started going deaf when he was four, so they built their own language between them. She's gone now, but the language stayed."

She said it lightly, the way people in Lowmark said heavy things, and went back to her ledger. Isandro watched Tam across the commons, showing a smaller child how to sign *water*—fingers rippling—and felt something in his chest shift like ballast settling.

He'd spent eleven years keeping his vocabulary to what could be shouted across open water. *Hard to port. Line off. Not today.* Words you could say to strangers because they committed you to nothing. Now he was learning a language designed for exactly one other person, and finding it fit him better than any of the words he'd brought with him.

* * *

The rain came in the afternoons now—short, warm, preliminary, like a musician tuning before the real performance. It turned the levee work to slop and made the clay slick, and it drove the whole crew into Brill's kitchen at dusk, where the man himself presided over a pot big enough to bathe in.

Brill had been a soldier once. Isandro saw it in the way he moved through his crowded kitchen—never hurried, never surprised, everything within reach of his scarred forearms. He'd been a soldier, and then something had happened that he never talked about, and now he cooked for forty people every night with the focused tenderness of a man performing penance or prayer, possibly both.

"You eat like a man avoiding a conversation," Brill observed on the fifth evening, dropping a second helping onto Isandro's plate unasked.

"I eat like a man who's rowed thirty miles today."

"You eat like my old sergeant. He talked constantly about anything except the thing that mattered." Brill sat down across from him, unhurried, filling the space the way he filled a plate. "Sefa says you're leaving after the monsoon."

"Sefa talks too much."

"She does. It's why we keep her." Brill leaned back. "I'm not asking you to stay. I'm telling you the pot's big enough. Whatever size you decide to be, there's room."

Isandro looked down at his plate. Around them the kitchen roared—Osk arguing tide tables with the ferry boys, net-menders singing something filthy and half-remembered, Tam signing stories to three children at once, his hands flying. A room full of castoffs making noise like a family.

"That's the trouble with pots," Isandro said finally. "You get used to eating from one, and then every other meal tastes wrong."

Brill smiled, slow as sunrise. "Now you're having a conversation."

* * *

The letters were read at night, in Sefa's front room, by lamplight, while the rain worked at the roof like a patient creditor.

It had become ritual without anyone deciding it should. After dinner, Odile would fetch the oilcloth bundle from its hiding place beneath the floorboards, unwrap the Vhalorian documents with hands that still trembled slightly, and read aloud—first in the original tongue, which sounded to Isandro like water running over stones, then in translation, her voice steadying as she went.

There were seventeen letters. Most were correspondence between a woman named Ilvane Corizan and her sister, written in the last year before the end. Ilvane had been, by Odile's careful reconstruction, a keeper of the royal archives—not a courtier but a clerk, someone who understood what paper could do because she spent her life watching it do it.

"The banks moved first," Odile read on the sixth night. Rain hammered the shutters; the lamp flame leaned and recovered. "'They did not send soldiers. Soldiers are expensive and make martyrs. They sent auditors. By the time the Crown understood what was happening, every road, every harbor, every granary in Vhaloria was collateral. The kingdom had borrowed against itself for a hundred years, and the debt was called all at once.'"

She paused. In the corner, Tam sat very still, watching her hands even though she wasn't signing.

"'My brother-in-law says we should have seen it coming,'" Odile continued. "'But who sees a flood coming? Everyone knows the river will rise someday. Everyone builds anyway. The Optimists' ledgers show the loans were structured to fail—they wanted the default. They wanted the collapse. A kingdom that owes you nothing is worth only its taxes. A kingdom that owes you everything is worth its whole future.'"

The word landed in the room like a dropped anchor.

*Optimists.*

Isandro had heard the name his whole adult life—the smiling moneylenders, the cheerful contracts, the interest that compounded like floodwater. He'd thought of them as weather. Random, impersonal, something that happened to people who were unlucky or foolish or both. He had buried his brother on borrowed Optimist coin and told himself the shame was his alone.

But here it was, forty years old, in ink: the same institution, the same method, the same cheerful arithmetic applied to an entire nation. They hadn't destroyed Vhaloria out of malice. They'd done it out of procedure. And the procedure hadn't stopped. It had just moved downstream, to smaller kingdoms, to towns, to a storm chaser with dead eyes and a dead brother and a debt that grew eight percent annually whether he ate or starved.

"They're still doing it," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

Odile looked up. "What?"

"This letter. It's not history. It's a manual." He stared at the lamplight. "The Optimists didn't die with Vhaloria. I've been running from them for eleven years. They're the same flood, just—" he gestured vaguely, "—smaller rivers."

The room was quiet except for the rain. Sefa sat by the window, mending a net she didn't need mended, and her hands had gone still.

"My grandmother left Vhaloria when she was nine," Odile said softly. "She never talked about why. She'd just go quiet whenever money was mentioned. Like it was a disease she'd survived." She smoothed the letter flat. "Ilvane writes that the last decree the king signed was an order to burn the archives. To destroy the records of the bloodline, so no one could ever be found and crowned again—or held hostage, or used. But Ilvane didn't burn hers. She hid them. She wrote this letter the same night, and sealed it, and addressed it—" Odile turned the document over, showing the blank face of it, "—to whoever found it. 'To the finder,' it says. 'May you be kinder than the times that made me hide.'"

Sefa spoke from the window, without looking up from her net. "And Corvane wants these."

"Corvane wants what he can use," Isandro said. "A bloodline he can resurrect. A crown he can rule through. These letters say the monarchy died owing everything to everyone. That's not a throne. That's a liability."

"Then why does he want them?"

"Because paper doesn't have to tell the truth," Isandro said. "It just has to be believed. He was an archivist. He knows better than anyone that a document is worth whatever people will pay for believing it."

Tam made a sound—small, sharp, deliberate. Every head turned. He signed rapidly, and Odile translated, her voice slowing as she went:

"He says: if the paper is a lie, then the truth is in the people. He says the settlers upstream are the truth. He says—" Tam's hands paused, then finished, and Odile's voice caught slightly, "—he says that's why the letter has no address. Because the address is us."

Nobody spoke for a long moment. The lamp burned steady. Outside, the river ran high and dark toward the sea, carrying whatever it carried.

* * *

On the seventh day the barometer began to fall, and Sefa doubled the sandbag shifts, and Isandro stopped thinking about leaving entirely—not because he decided to stay, but because there was no longer a version of the work that didn't include him. His hands knew the chain. His shoulders knew the rhythm. When a sledge bogged down in the clay, he was already moving before anyone called.

That evening, hauling the last load up the levee in gray failing light, he passed Osk, who clapped him on the back with a wet slap and said, "You carry like a Lowmark man now," and Isandro found, to his genuine surprise, that he didn't correct him.

He stood afterward at the top of the levee, looking downriver. The water was the color of strong tea and rising fast, carrying branches, fence posts, a drowned door, the debris of places upstream that had lost their argument with the rain. Somewhere down that brown current were the Optimists' hunters, and somewhere behind them, patient as paperwork, was Corvane.

Two of Sefa's neighbors had come into the tavern two nights ago asking after a salvage man with a scarred boat. Asking politely. Buying drinks. The questions had been soft, the kind that left no bruises—but Brill had told him, and Brill's face when he told him had been the face of a man reporting enemy movement.

They were close. Days, maybe less. And the monsoon's main body was coming up from the south like a debt called due.

Isandro watched the flood-borne door spin past in the current and thought about Ilvane Corizan, sealing her letter to no one alive, trusting the river to deliver it kindly. He thought about the blank address. *The address is us.*

Behind him, down the slope, Tam was signaling the evening chain to a halt—flat hand, *stop*, then fist to chest, *enough*, then the rippling fingers, *water*, pointing at the darkening sky.

More rain was coming. The biggest rain there was.

And tomorrow, Isandro was almost certain, someone would come asking questions with a smile.
