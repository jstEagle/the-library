## 11. Corvane Arrives

Corvane had always believed that a town could be read the way an archivist reads a ledger: not by its entries, but by what it had stopped recording.

Lowmark's ledger, as far as he could tell from the ferry landing, had stopped recording hope sometime around the second flood. The stilt houses leaned into each other like old men sharing a secret. The levee was a spine of packed earth and mismatched stone, patched in three different decades' worth of materials. The water sat high against it, brown and patient, the way debt sits against a man who has stopped opening his letters.

He stepped off the ferry in good shoes and let them get muddy immediately. That mattered. He'd learned it forty years ago, in the reading rooms of Vhaloria's Ministry of Records, before Vhaloria was a word that made people flinch: power enters a room best when it arrives looking slightly worse off than expected. A king in mud is trusted. A king in polished boots is counted.

He was not a king. Not yet. He was, on paper—and Corvane had spent his life learning that paper was the only place anything truly existed—a senior collections agent of the Optimist Compact, traveling under a name that belonged to a dead man whose estate he had settled personally, efficiently, and with no witnesses. He wore a plain gray coat, carried one leather case, and smiled at the ferryman until the ferryman smiled back.

"Business?" the ferryman asked.

"Salvage arbitration," Corvane said. "There's a dispute over recovered goods upriver. I'm told this town sees everything that floats."

"Everything the river doesn't keep," the ferryman said, and laughed at his own joke, and Corvane laughed too, warmly, because the first rule of acquisition was to laugh at their jokes.

* * *

By evening he had bought three rounds for the taproom of the Drowned Gull and paid for a new roof beam for the widow Hesta's house, casually, in passing, as though generosity were simply something his hands did while his mind was elsewhere. He listened more than he spoke. He learned that the boatwright Sefa employed four people and trusted two of them. He learned that Brill the cook had a soldier's way of standing near doors. He learned that the clerk girl, Odile, had gone quiet for two days after a stranger's boat came in with salvage, and that she'd been seen copying something by lamplight.

And he learned—this from a boy of perhaps twelve, paid in coppers and flattery—that Isandro Yarrow had been asking about water levels upstream, and about the old trade roads into the Kilow valley, and had bought rope. A lot of rope.

Rope meant a journey. Rope meant soon.

Corvane drank very little himself. He held his glass and turned it slowly and let the room come to him, the way water comes to a low place. When a levee worker complained about the Optimists—loudly, drunkenly, to general applause—Corvane raised his own glass to the man and said, "They're vultures. I only work for them because they pay me to find things, and finding things is honest work." The room laughed. The room liked him. Rooms always liked him, right up until they understood him, and by then it was always too late.

He took a room above the taproom. From its window he could see the boathouse, and the boat in its slip, and one lamp burning late in Sefa's kitchen where three silhouettes bent over a table, heads close together, talking about him or about the chest or about running.

It didn't matter which. All three answers led to the same conversation.

* * *

He sent the invitation in the morning, written by hand on good paper, delivered by the boy with the coppers:

*Mr. Yarrow — I represent certain interested parties regarding goods recovered from the Kilow Reach. No accusation intended; quite the opposite. I believe we may be of extraordinary use to one another. The Drowned Gull, back room, midday. I'll have lunch sent up. — C.*

Then he waited, and while he waited he arranged the back room himself: table squared to the wall, two chairs, the window shuttered to a single blade of light. He set out the lunch—cold fowl, bread, pickled things, a bottle of white wine from a region that no longer appeared on maps, purchased at ruinous cost from a smuggler who hadn't known what he was carrying. The label said *Vhaloria*, in gold script gone brown at the edges. Corvane had kept it for thirty years, waiting for an occasion. This was not the occasion he'd imagined. Occasions never were.

Isandro Yarrow came in like a man entering a cage he'd already measured for exits. Corvane noted the details with professional pleasure: weight on the balls of the feet, eyes doing the room in a half-second—window, door, the blade of light, Corvane's hands—before settling into a workingman's slouch that fooled no one who knew what work actually looked like. Storm-chaser's shoulders. Salvage man's hands, scarred along the rope lines. Tired around the eyes in the specific way of a man who has not slept without one ear open in years.

"Mr. Vasquez," Isandro said, reading the false name off the invitation. His voice was flat, careful.

"That's today's name," Corvane said pleasantly, gesturing to the chair. "Please. Sit. Eat. The bird's better than it looks."

"I'm not hungry."

"Of course you're not." Corvane poured two glasses of wine anyway and pushed one across. "But you'll drink, because refusing would be a statement, and you're too smart to make statements in a closed room with a man you haven't yet priced. Sit down, Mr. Yarrow. I promise you the most honest hour of your year."

A long moment. Then Isandro sat—not relaxing, but conserving, the way a man leans on a wall he doesn't trust. He didn't touch the wine.

Corvane beamed. "Good. Now. Let me show you I'm not wasting your time." He opened his leather case and laid items on the table one at a time, unhurried, like a dealer laying a winning hand face-up. "Your name is Isandro Yarrow. You hold salvage licenses in three districts, lapsed in two. Fourteen months ago you borrowed six hundred crowns from a lender operating under the Optimist Compact's umbrella—the loan was arranged through a broker named Pellam, whom you may remember as a fat man with kind eyes. The money went to bury your brother Tomas, who drowned in the Verren collapse. The funeral was modest. The plot has a view of water, which I understand your brother would have wanted."

He watched the color leave Isandro's face and return as something harder. He continued, gently, almost tenderly.

"The principal has compounded. You currently owe nine hundred and forty crowns, plus recovery fees, plus my time—which is expensive, Mr. Yarrow, more expensive than you can imagine, though by tonight you may have some sense of it. Two collectors preceded me. One is still looking. The other found you in Marrow Ford and you left through a window at night and he broke his ankle chasing you, and he holds a grudge, and grudges are transferable assets in my profession." Corvane folded his hands. "So. That is what I know about you. Now let me show you what I know about the river."

He set the last item on the table: a sketch, done in his own hand, precise as an engraving. A chest. Reeded edges, iron strapping, and on the lid, rendered in careful ink, a black sun with sixteen rays.

Isandro looked at it for exactly as long as a guilty man looks at nothing, which is to say a fraction too long, and Corvane could have wept with satisfaction.

"There was a flood on the night of the ninth," Corvane said. "The Kilow Reach shed its banks and gave back some of what it keeps. And a licensed salvage man pulled a chest out of the reeds below the bend at Harrow Point, and by law—salvage law, which you know better than I, since you hold the licenses—one-fifth of any such recovery is owed to the Crown authority of record." He paused delicately. "There is no Crown authority of record for the Kilow Reach. Which means the recovery belongs entirely to you. Entirely, Mr. Yarrow. Do you see the beautiful problem? The law gives you everything, and everything, in this case, is a thing that men will burn towns to possess."

"You've got the wrong man," Isandro said. "I pulled timber out of that flood. Rotten planking. Sold it for repairs."

"Yes," Corvane agreed warmly. "You did. You also bought rope."

Silence. The blade of light moved a finger's width across the floorboards. Somewhere beyond the shuttered window, Lowmark went about its morning—hammers on the levee, a dog, a woman calling a child home—and Corvane let the silence do its work, because silence was the one instrument he'd never needed training to play.

Then he reached for the wine bottle and turned the label toward Isandro.

"Do you know what this is?"

Isandro's jaw tightened. "Wine."

"Wine, yes. From a country that hasn't existed for forty years. My father bought a case the week before the end and drank one bottle a year until he died, and I inherited eleven bottles and drank ten of them alone in a rented room in a city that used to be a provincial capital, toasting a nation that everyone assured me had never really deserved to exist." Corvane smiled, and for one unguarded instant the smile was not warm at all—it was the smile of a man standing in the ashes of a burned library, counting what survived. "Vhaloria, Mr. Yarrow. The black sun. You've seen it now. Stamped in wax, or stamped in iron, it makes no difference. It's the seal of a royal house that my employers' grandparents helped erase, and that I—" he caught himself, smoothed it, "—that history has been unkind enough to misplace."

"I don't know anything about any of that."

"No," Corvane said. "You don't. That's what I like about you. You're not an idealist. Idealists lie badly and die loudly. You're a debtor. Debtors are the most honest men alive—they know exactly what everything costs." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into something almost confiding, almost kind. "So here is what I know about the chest, and then here is what I want, and then you can go back to your friends at the boathouse and decide whether I'm a monster or a solution. Fair?"

Isandro said nothing. Corvane took it as assent.

"What's in the chest is paper and metal. Documents. A signet. Letters sealed forty years ago by people who are now dead or scattered or hiding under other names in valleys like the one upstream from this charming swamp. To you it's ballast. To my employers it's contraband—old claims, old bloodlines, old embarrassments, and the Compact does not tolerate embarrassments. But to me—" and here Corvane allowed himself, deliberately, visibly, one breath of genuine feeling, because the best lies are load-bearing truths, "—to me it is the last administrative record of a country I loved, and I have spent forty years watching men like my employers decide what happened there, and I am tired of it."

He stood, walked to the shuttered window, and opened it a hand's width. Light fell across the table, across the sketch of the chest, across Isandro's guarded face.

"So here is my offer, and I will make it exactly once, and I will never insult you by pretending it isn't generous. Give me the chest—all of it, contents intact, seals unbroken—and I will buy your debt. Not forgive it. *Buy* it. Every crown of it, every fee, every grudge held by every man with a broken ankle. You will walk out of this room owing nothing to anyone alive. Clean ledger, Mr. Yarrow. Do you know how rare that is? Most men never see one. Most men die compounding."

"And if I say I don't have it?"

"Then I'll believe you eventually," Corvane said pleasantly, "after a process. The process is unpleasant and involves other people, some of whom live in this town, and I would genuinely prefer to skip it, because I've taken a liking to the place. The boatwright seems competent. The cook seasons well. The girl—" he paused, and watched Isandro's stillness sharpen into something with an edge, and catalogued it, and filed it away as leverage of the finest grade, "—the girl writes a fine hand, I'm told. It would be a shame if the process touched any of them. So I do hope you have it."

The light lay between them like a drawn line. Isandro rose slowly, and Corvane noted that he did not look at the wine, or the fowl, or the money that wasn't on the table but might as well have been. He looked at the window. At the town beyond it. At the smoke rising thin and domestic from Sefa's chimney.

"Clean slate," Isandro said quietly. "No debts. Nothing chasing me."

"Nothing," Corvane agreed. "You could be anywhere by August's end. Anywhere at all. Isn't that all you've ever wanted?"

And Isandro Yarrow looked at him for a long moment with an expression Corvane had seen a thousand times across a thousand tables—the expression of a man being offered exactly what he'd sworn he wanted—and Corvane smiled his warmest smile and waited, because he was very good at waiting, and because he already knew, from the rope and the lamp burning late and the girl copying papers by hand, that the answer was going to be no.

Which meant, he thought, with a small cold pleasure that felt almost like happiness, that the process would begin after all.

He just needed to know where the chest slept tonight.
