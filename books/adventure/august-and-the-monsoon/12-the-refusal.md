## 12. The Refusal

Corvane poured the wine himself.

That was the thing Isandro noticed first—not the quality of it, though it was better than anything that had ever crossed Lowmark's docks, not the cut of the man's coat or the way his two hired men had melted discreetly out of Sefa's front room at a flick of his fingers. It was that Corvane poured with an archivist's precision, filling each cup to exactly the same line, as if measure itself were a form of courtesy.

"Kilow Reach salvage," Corvane said, handing over the cup. "You pulled a chest from the reeds below the old ferry crossing. Third of last month. I'd like to hear how you found it."

Isandro took the wine because refusing it would have been its own answer. "I pull whatever the river gives me. Most of it's fence posts and dead livestock."

"And when it isn't?"

"Then I sell it to whoever pays."

Corvane smiled. He had a good smile—broad, warm at the corners, the kind of smile that had opened doors in government offices for thirty years before the government it belonged to stopped existing. He sat across from Isandro like a man settling into a chair he owned.

"You're lying competently," Corvane said. "I want you to know I can tell. It's rare, actually. Most people lie badly and don't know it. You lie the way a man rows against current—efficiently, with an eye on the far bank." He sipped. "So let me save us both the effort."

He reached into his coat and set something on the table between them: a sheet of paper, heavy stock, stamped with the seal of the Optimists' counting house. Isandro's debt, itemized. Principal, interest, penalties for flight, penalties for concealment. The number at the bottom had grown so large since the last letter that it no longer looked like money. It looked like weather.

"I can make this vanish," Corvane said. "Not defer it. Not restructure it. Vanish. The Optimists will never trouble you again—in fact, they'll owe *you*, if we structure this correctly, which I intend to." He folded his hands. "All I require is the chest. Unopened is preferable, but I'm told you've already looked inside. That's forgivable. Curiosity is human. What matters now is what you do with what you know."

The rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere outside, Tam was hauling planks up the levee ladder, and Brill was arguing with someone about sandbag rotation, and Odile was reading by lamplight the way she did every night, translating Vhalorian script aloud in a low voice while Sefa pretended not to listen from the workbench. The whole house was alive around them, and Isandro sat in the middle of it holding a cup of a dead nation's wine, being offered his life back.

"The chest isn't here," he said.

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But it's close. Things that matter stay close to the people who hide them. You strike me as a man who keeps his valuables within swimming distance." He rose, buttoned his coat, and paused at the door. "Three days. After that my offer expires, and my methods change. I've enjoyed Lowmark, Mr. Yarrow. I'd hate to remember it differently."

He left the bottle.

* * *

Isandro didn't sleep. He sat on the porch above the waterline until the sky went the color of wet slate, turning the empty cup in his hands, listening to the river breathe against the pilings.

Three days. And the storm coming behind Corvane like a debt of its own.

When the lamp finally moved in the front room—Odile, always awake before anyone—he went inside and put the cup down on her table harder than he meant to.

"I need to tell you both something," he said. "And then I need to leave tonight, and I need you to let me."

Sefa came down the ladder from the loft without being called. She must have been listening; she struck him as a woman who listened to everything and admitted to nothing. She stood with her arms crossed, sawdust still in her hair from yesterday's repairs, and waited.

"The chest," Isandro said. "You already know I didn't find it by accident. Here's all of it." And he gave it to them—the whole shape of it, laid out like a hull with the planking stripped away. The chest wedged in the reeds. The black sun stamped on the lid. The documents, the signet, the sealed letter addressed to no one living. The Optimists' letter burned on the water. The boy who'd followed him for coin. Corvane's men on the levee asking polite questions. And now Corvane himself, three days, and a debt that would drown him if he stayed and follow him if he ran.

"I should have told you the night I docked," he finished. "I told myself I was protecting you. That was half true. The other half is that I've spent six years learning that every place I stop becomes a place I have to flee, and I wanted—" His voice caught on something. He pushed through it. "I wanted one place where I hadn't lied yet."

Silence. Rain on the roof. Brill, who had appeared in the doorway at some point with a knife he was using to pare an apple, kept paring it, unhurried, giving the moment room.

Sefa spoke first. "Where's the chest now?"

"Sunk in the slough behind the boathouse, under Brill's spare timbers, wrapped in oilcloth. Weighted with chain."

"Good spot," Brill said mildly. "Bad habit, telling people about good spots."

"They were going to burn the town down around it eventually," Isandro said. "You deserved to know why."

He braced for anger. He'd earned anger. Instead Sefa pulled out a chair, sat, and said, "Show me what's in the documents. Not the story—the papers themselves. Odile, get them."

"We don't have them," Odile said. "We have copies. I copied everything the second week, page by page, in my own hand, and hid the originals somewhere even Isandro doesn't know." She met his eyes, unapologetic. "You weren't the only one who planned for fire."

Something loosened in Isandro's chest that he hadn't known was tight. He laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and Sefa almost smiled.

"Right," she said, all business now. "Sit down. All of you. If we're going to be ruined together, we'll do it organized."

* * *

They spread Odile's copies across the workbench by lamplight: letters in a flowing pre-Exodus hand, a genealogical register, shipping manifests bearing the black sun, and the sealed letter whose wax Isandro had broken in the reeds a lifetime ago.

"Here's what I couldn't figure," Isandro said. "Why does Corvane want this? He was Vhaloria's state archivist—I checked, Odile confirmed it. If anyone knows these records are real, it's him. So why buy them off a salvage man instead of just declaring them? Who's going to argue with the archivist?"

Odile's finger stopped on the genealogical register. She'd gone pale again, the way she had the first night she'd seen the seal, but this time the pallor had a different texture to it. Not fear. Arithmetic.

"Because he can't declare them," she said slowly. "Look at the register. Look at who's listed."

The register recorded the royal line of Vhaloria down to the fall—births, marriages, deaths, each entry countersigned by the archivist of the day. And there, in the final decades, in a cramped professional hand Isandro now recognized from Corvane's own correspondence, were additions. Deaths dated years apart, entered in the same ink. Marriages that postdated the nation's dissolution, witnessed by names that appeared nowhere else in forty years of records.

"He's been editing it," Odile whispered. "For years. Before any of this. Look—these entries are in his hand, but the dates are wrong. He wrote a death in 941 using ink formulas that weren't common until 953. I worked in a records office for four years; you learn to see this. He backdated. He killed people on paper who are probably still alive, and married other people into the line who have no business in it." She looked up, and her voice steadied into something hard and bright. "These documents aren't proof of a bloodline. They're proof that there *isn't* one—at least not the one he claims. He's not restoring the Vhalorian monarchy. He's inventing it, with himself holding the only authentic copy of the family tree, and every rival claimant erased in advance."

Brill set down the apple. "The descendants he hasn't erased. The ones the letters name."

"Some of them are alive," Odile said. "Some of them might be in this valley—that's what the settlement upstream is, isn't it? People who walked away rather than be part of whatever he was building. And if the original register surfaces, it doesn't just embarrass him. It proves his credentials are forged. It proves the whole restoration is a fraud with his signature on every page." She tapped the paper. "That's why he needs the originals destroyed or controlled. Not to crown a king. To keep himself from being uncrowned."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Outside, the river muttered against the levee, and far off, thunder rolled with the patience of something enormous still deciding.

"So we don't run," Sefa said. It wasn't a question, but Isandro heard the question underneath it, aimed at him.

"Running is what I do," Isandro said. "It's the one trade I'm certified in."

"And how's that worked out?" Sefa asked. "Six years of running, and the debt got bigger every mile. You want to know what I see? A man rowing from a flood by heading downstream." She stood, planted both hands on the workbench, and looked at him the way she looked at a hull she intended to save. "Corvane gave you three days because he thinks you're alone. Men like him price everything by whether anyone will stand next to it. So here's my counteroffer. We don't give him the chest. We don't give him the copies. We take the truth upstream to the people it belongs to, and we let *them* decide what it's worth—and if Corvane wants to argue with the last living Vhalorians about their own family tree, that's his funeral, possibly literally."

"It'll start a war," Isandro said.

"It started one the day he forged his first entry," Odile said. "We'd just be the first ones fighting on the right side."

Brill picked the apple back up. "I've fought for worse reasons than a boatwright's say-so. And better ones. This qualifies as both."

Isandro looked at the copies spread across the bench—the dead language, the edited line, the small neat treasons of a patient man's pen. Six years he'd believed the world ran on debts: everyone owed, everyone collecting, the only question being who held the ledger. But here was a different arithmetic entirely. A mute boy hauling planks. A cook feeding strangers. A clerk who'd copied a dead nation's secrets by lamplight because forgetting was the one theft she wouldn't allow.

Fear was still in him. It would be in him until the end. But fear, he realized, was just water—and water could be channeled.

"All right," he said. "We move it upstream. Tonight, before Corvane's watchers settle in for the night—but we go by water, quiet, and we need a route that doesn't pass the narrows, because if I were him, that's where I'd wait."

Odile was already pulling the valley chart toward her, tracing the river with one finger. "There's a side channel past the drowned orchard. Shallow, twisty, impassable in high water."

Sefa glanced at the barometer nailed to the doorframe, its needle sagging lower than any of them liked.

"Then we'd best hope," she said, "that the storm holds off three more days."

As if in answer, the thunder spoke again—closer now, and no longer deciding anything.
