## 13. Monsoon Warning

The barometer in Sefa's workshop had been falling for two days, and on the third morning it fell off the edge of the dial entirely.

Isandro noticed it first because he'd been watching it the way a man watches a wound he knows is going to open. The needle had crept down through the storm band overnight and now sat pressed against the stop pin at the bottom of the glass, trembling faintly, as if it wanted to go lower and the instrument simply refused to allow it. He'd seen that once before, three years ago, upriver past Harrow Bend—a summer cell that dropped nine inches of rain in six hours and took a ferry landing with it, horses and all.

That had been a squall. This was the main body of the monsoon, still a hundred miles out over the low country, gathering itself like something with intent.

"Tam." He tapped the boy's shoulder and pointed at the barometer, then made the hand-sign they'd worked out between them—flat palm pressing downward, again, again. *Down. Down.*

Tam looked at the needle, then at Isandro, and his face did something old and knowing that didn't belong on a fourteen-year-old. He signed back: *Sefa. Now.*

* * *

They gathered in the boathouse loft where the light came through gaps in the planking in long dusty blades. Sefa stood at the window with her arms crossed, looking out at the river, which had turned the color of strong tea and was running high and fast against the pilings. Brill occupied the doorway behind them all, arms folded across a chest like a barrel, radiating the particular patience of a man who had waited out sieges before. Odile sat on a workbench with the chest's documents spread around her, weighted down with spools of caulking cotton, and she was not reading them. She was watching the door.

"The narrows gauge will tell us by noon," Sefa said. "But I don't need the gauge. My knees have been telling me since yesterday."

"How bad?" Isandro asked.

"Worse than 'sixty-one. Worse than anything this valley's seen since before Tam was born." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she said everything, and somehow that made it worse than drama would have. "If we stay, the levee has to hold. If it doesn't hold, there's no second chance. No boats out in that. No swimming. Nothing."

"And if we go?"

"Downstream." She turned from the window. "Past the narrows, past the ferry crossing, down to the lowland towns. Which is exactly where Corvane's people are sitting. Two of them were at the trading post yesterday asking after you again, Isandro. Polite as church. Buying flour they don't need so they have an excuse to linger." Her eyes found his and held. "So that's the choice. We run the gauntlet of your creditors in open water ahead of a flood, or we stand behind a wall of dirt and pray it loves us back."

Nobody spoke. Rain hadn't started yet—the sky was still doing its terrible bright imitation of a normal August morning—but everyone in the room could feel it coming the way animals feel it, a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the air.

Brill broke the silence. "I've held worse walls than that levee," he said. "With fewer men."

"You've held walls against soldiers," Odile said quietly. "This one's against the sky."

"Sky breaks eventually," Brill said. "Soldiers don't."

Isandro looked at the documents on the bench. At the black sun stamped into the lid of the chest below them, patient as an eclipse. Four days ago he'd have already been gone. Four days ago the arithmetic was simple: boat, current, distance, done. He'd spent eleven years getting good at leaving—good enough that it barely cost him anything anymore, or so he'd told himself, right up until the morning he'd found himself nailing shutters onto Sefa's windows instead of packing his gear.

"I can be gone tonight," he said. "Alone, in the dark, upstream if I have to. They're watching downstream. If I pull their eyes with me, the rest of you can decide freely."

"No," Sefa said.

It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It landed in the loft like an anchor hitting bottom.

"They're not here for us," she went on. "They're here for what you pulled out of my river. And every day you're in this town, you make us part of it whether we consented or not—I know that, and I'm not pretending otherwise. But you don't get to carry it away alone either. That's not how this works anymore." She crossed the loft and put one work-roughened hand flat on the chest's lid. "You want to know what I think? I think whatever's in these papers matters more than any of us understand yet. Odile's been translating for a week. Ask her."

Odile's hands had gone still on the table. When she looked up, her face was pale in a way Isandro had learned to read: not fear of the storm. Fear of men.

"There are names in these letters," she said. "Living names. Families in this valley who never stopped being Vhalorian, even after the maps changed. And whoever holds this chest holds proof of who they are—and Corvane doesn't want proof to honor it. He wants it to own it." She swallowed. "If we run, we take that power with us and he follows it forever. If we scatter, he picks us off one household at a time. But if we're together—if the whole valley sees what he is and what he wants—"

"He can't burn all of us," Brill finished.

"He burned the archive," Odile said. "He'd try."

* * *

By noon the narrows gauge confirmed what Sefa's knees knew: the big body of the monsoon would reach Lowmark in roughly forty hours, and the river would crest somewhere between four and seven feet above the highest flood line anyone alive had witnessed. Word went out through the town the way word always did in Lowmark—not by crier but by visitation, house to stilt-house, neighbor to neighbor, along walkways built for exactly this kind of conversation.

Isandro walked it with Sefa. It was, he understood dimly, a test of him as much as an errand—he was the stranger, the drifter, the man whose trouble had come calling with torches—and the town would weigh him with every handshake.

At the Maren place, old Widow Maren listened to Sefa's summary with her chin up and said, "I buried a husband under that levee's shadow and I'll not die running from weather. I'm staying."

At the net-sheds, half a dozen fishermen argued in low voices and split three ways: some to stay, some to take their families downstream tonight while the water was still passable, some undecided. One of them, a broad-shouldered man named Ketch whose boat Isandro had helped re-caulk, looked at Isandro a long moment and said, "You're the reason those polite gentlemen keep coming round."

"Yes," Isandro said. There was no version of denial that survived contact with a man like Ketch.

Ketch nodded slowly. "And you're staying?"

"That's being decided."

"It's decided when you decide it," Ketch said. "Rest of us can smell a man about to run. We've got a whole town full of people who stayed when leaving was smarter. You'll fit fine or you won't." He spat over the rail into the brown water. "Levee crew meets at dawn. Bring gloves."

* * *

Evening came down heavy and windless, the sky bruising purple in the west, and the town made its choice the way rivers make theirs—all at once, everywhere, without a single commanding voice.

Of Lowmark's hundred-odd souls, perhaps thirty packed their boats and headed downstream in the last usable light, poling through the slack water toward the lowlands and whatever waited there. Isandro watched them go from the levee and felt no judgment; he'd been one of those boats his entire adult life. But most stayed. Most hauled their skiffs above the flood line, shuttered their windows with boards Sefa's workshop had been selling at cost for two days straight, carried grandmothers' chests and seed stores and one indignant goat up into lofts and attics, and then—this was the part that undid him—they started carrying sandbags.

Not because anyone ordered it. Because the levee needed height, and it needed it before the rain, and they were the kind of people who answered need with their backs.

Isandro filled bags until his palms blistered inside his gloves. Tam worked beside him in the failing light, small and tireless, passing bags hand to hand up the line with a rhythm that needed no words. Brill ran the top of the levee like a foreman who'd been born to it, positioning bags, shoring weak spots, his burned arm from the boathouse fire wrapped and ignored. Odile had claimed the schoolhouse and turned it into a supply point—lantern oil, rope, dried fish, the Vhalorian documents sealed in oilcloth and hidden twice over—and she moved through the dark between houses with a lantern, checking on the old ones, counting heads.

Near midnight, Sefa found Isandro at the levee's northern end, where the earthworks met the rock of the bluff and the river ran hardest. She handed him a cup of Brill's terrible coffee and stood beside him looking at the water.

"Corvane's men," she said. "Two more spotted at the ferry crossing this afternoon. That makes five, maybe six. They're not leaving."

"They're waiting to see if we evacuate," Isandro said. "If the town empties, they come in quiet and turn it over board by board. If we stay—" He drank the coffee. It tasted like boiled rope. "Then they wait for the storm to do their work, and move on whatever's left."

"So we're betting everything on dirt and each other."

"We're betting everything on dirt and each other," he agreed. And then, because the night was dark enough and the hour honest enough: "I've run from things my whole life, Sefa. Debts. Weather. My brother's grave. Running always felt like competence. Like I was the smart one, the one who saw the flood coming." He turned the cup in his hands. "Standing still feels like drowning. But I'm standing."

Sefa looked at him for a long moment, and in the lantern light her weathered face did something rare—it softened. "I know," she said. "I watched you nail my shutters like a man saying goodbye to every board. Nobody stays well the first time." She drained her own coffee and tossed the dregs into the current. "Get some sleep. Dawn crew. And Isandro—" she paused at the edge of the lantern light, "—if Corvane's men come before the rain does, you wake me first. Not Brill. Me. This is my town."

She walked back along the levee, small and straight-backed against the bruised sky, and Isandro understood that he had just been given something he'd never once been offered in eleven years of drifting: not shelter, not charity, but a post. A place in a line of defense. A reason to be somewhere that wasn't merely the absence of elsewhere.

* * *

He slept two hours in the boathouse loft and woke before dawn to a sound that stopped his heart before his mind caught up: voices outside, low and urgent.

He came down the ladder with a boat hook in his hands and found the yard full of neighbors—Ketch, the widow Maren's grandson, two of the net-shed men—and beyond them, at the tree line where the river road left town, movement. Figures. Lanterns doused. Men on foot, spreading out along the levee's flank with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the ground they stood on.

Six of them. Maybe seven. And at their center, unmistakable even at distance by his stillness, a tall figure in a long coat who did not carry a weapon and did not need to.

Corvane had not waited for the storm.

The eastern horizon had gone the color of a healing bruise, and beneath it, far off over the low country, Isandro could see it now—could *feel* it—a wall of cloud stacked to the heavens, flickering silently from within, coming on like the end of an argument the sky had been having with itself for a month.

First rain, Isandro thought. Then fire. Then flood.

He turned to Ketch. "Wake everyone. Quietly. And get Odile—tell her it's time to move the chest."
