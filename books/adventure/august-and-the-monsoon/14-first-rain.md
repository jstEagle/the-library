## 14. First Rain

Tam felt it before he heard it—a pressure change in his ears, the way the river went glassy and still, holding its breath. He was up on the walkway between Sefa's house and the boathouse, coiling rope that didn't need coiling, when the first drops came. Fat ones, slow, spaced like footsteps. One hit the back of his hand and stayed there, a cold coin.

He held his palm out and waited for the second one.

It came. Then a third. Then the sky stopped counting and simply opened, and the sound arrived all at once—soft at first, a whisper on the tin roof of the smokehouse, then a rush, then a roar, as if the whole valley had been tipped into the river instead of the other way around.

Tam stood in it. He always did, the first rain of the season. Sefa had stopped scolding him about it years ago. It was the one thing he did that nobody else could do quite the same way: stand under the opening sky with his arms loose and his face turned up, and listen to something that needed no voice from him. The rain spoke in a language with no words in it, which made it the easiest language he knew.

Down at the boathouse, somebody was shouting—not alarmed, just loud, because of the noise. Brill's voice, carrying over the drumming: *"Tarps! Get the tarps on the paint stores or we'll be rowing in soup!"*

Tam shook the water off his face and went to help.

* * *

The boathouse smelled of cedar shavings and hot pitch, and inside it the rain was only a rumor on the roof. Isandro was already there, up on a ladder, nailing shutters over the big windows that faced the water. He'd taken his shirt off to work and Tam could see the map of him now—the old scars, the new muscle put on since the levee work started, the way he'd stopped carrying himself like a man ready to bolt. Three weeks ago he'd stood like a heron, all balance and readiness, weight forward on his toes even standing still. Now his heels were down.

Tam noticed things like that. When you couldn't talk, you got good at reading everything else.

He hauled the tarps off their shelf and dragged them to Brill, who grunted thanks and threw one over the stack of paint tins, lashing it down with the quick, brutal efficiency of a man who had once secured artillery for a living. Brill worked like everything might explode. Tam had decided early on that this was not fear. It was just how Brill loved things—by defending them.

"Boy." Brill pointed at the woodstove. "Get her going. Wet work ahead."

Tam knelt and fed the stove kindling, struck the flint twice, and had a flame going by the third strike. Brill nodded approval, which from Brill was practically an embrace.

Above them, the hammering stopped. Isandro came down the ladder, took in the shuttered windows, the tarped stores, the stove already breathing heat, and said something Tam couldn't hear—but he saw the shape of it, the way Isandro looked around the boathouse afterward with an expression Tam had learned to name only recently.

Satisfaction. The look of a man surveying something that was *his*, in however small a way. A window he had shuttered. A wall he had braced. A job finished beside another man whose shoulder he'd brushed a hundred times without either of them making anything strange of it.

Isandro picked up the hammer again and moved to the next window. Tam watched him set the first nail, draw back—and pause. His eyes went to the doorway, to the gray sheeting of rain beyond it, to the road that climbed out of Lowmark toward the pass.

He was checking the road.

He always checked the road. That was the first thing Tam had ever understood about him, back when he was still *the drifter* and not yet anything else: the man watched the way out of every room he entered. Watched the water for current, the levee for gaps, the tree line for riders. Even laughing at Brill's terrible jokes, some part of him sat facing the door.

But now—he checked the road, and then he turned back to the window, and drove the nail home, and did not check again.

Tam kept watching, the way he watched everything, patient as driftwood. Isandro set another nail. Another. The rhythm of it settled into something almost like the rain itself, steady, unhurried, going nowhere but into the wood. Between nails he glanced sideways—at Brill cursing a knot out of a rope, at Tam feeding the stove—and there was nothing behind the glance except the glance. No measuring of exits. No arithmetic of distance-to-the-horizon.

The drifter had stopped watching the road.

Tam didn't know why that made his chest feel tight. He wasn't sure what to do with a feeling that size and no hands to shape it into words. So he did what he always did: he crossed the boathouse, tapped Isandro on the elbow, and when the man looked down, Tam held up the flint and striker and then mimed, with exaggerated care, striking a flame on the first try and then the second and then—fumbling, dropping it, scrambling—on the third.

Isandro laughed. Not the polite laugh he'd given in the first days, the one that never reached his eyes. This one came up from somewhere lower and surprised him on the way out, and he pointed at Tam and shook his head and mouthed something that was probably *one of these days*.

Tam grinned and went back to the stove.

Outside, the rain thickened. Inside, the small work of keeping a place safe went on, nail by nail, knot by knot, and nobody mentioned the storm still two days downstream of them, gathering its strength in the dark of the sea.

* * *

Midday, Odile came down through the rain with oilcloth wrapped around her shoulders and a ledger clutched inside it like a rescued kitten. She stamped water off her boots in the doorway and glared at the weather as if it were a personal insult she intended to have corrected.

"Barometer's falling faster than Sefa says is polite," she announced. "And I've been translating all morning. Come listen, both of you. Brill, you too—you're not too important for literature."

Brill muttered that he absolutely was, but he came anyway, wiping his hands on his apron and settling onto a crate near the stove with the groan of a man twice his age. Tam squeezed in beside him. Isandro hung the hammer on its peg—the peg Tam had carved for it, shaped like a fish, because every tool in Sefa's boathouse deserved to hang on something better than a bare nail—and sat down across from Odile with his forearms on his knees.

Odile opened the ledger. Her copies of the Vhalorian letters filled page after page in her small, precise clerk's hand, and where the original script had defeated her she'd left gaps marked with little asterisks, like stars waiting to be named.

"This one," she said, tapping a page. "This one isn't a decree or a land grant. It's a letter. A woman writing to her sister, the year after the fall. Listen." And she read aloud, her voice finding the cadence of it:

*"They tell us the country no longer exists, which is a strange thing to write to you, since here I am, existing, in the rain, missing you. Paper can be burned. Maps can be redrawn. But I have your face, and Mother's hands, and the recipe for honey bread that Grandmother swore we'd forget and we have not. Let the ministers keep their borders. We are the country now, you and I, and everyone who remembers the names of things."*

Silence, except the rain.

Tam looked around the circle. Brill had gone still in the way he did when something landed deep—jaw working slightly, eyes fixed on the middle distance. Isandro was staring at the floor, but not seeing it; Tam could tell because his thumb was moving against his forefinger, the way it did when he was turning something over that had no easy side to set it down on.

"That's the whole letter?" Isandro asked.

"There's more. Recipes. Names of dead people she refuses to let be dead. A complaint about her landlord." Odile smiled, small and crooked. "That's what gets me. Forty years ago, a kingdom drowned, and what she wrote about was the rent."

"She wrote what lasts," Brill said, surprising everyone including himself. He cleared his throat. "Kings come and go. Rent is forever."

They laughed, and the laugh loosened something, and Odile read two more letters—one funny, one sad—while the rain played its endless percussion on the roof. Tam listened with his eyes half-closed. He couldn't have said why the letters mattered to him the way they did. He'd never seen Vhaloria. Nobody had. But there was something in the woman's voice across forty years, something stubborn and warm, that sounded like Sefa. Like this whole town, maybe. People who stayed when staying was the hard thing.

When Odile packed up to go back to the house, Isandro walked her to the door and stood a moment looking out at the rain. Tam watched him from the stove. The drifter's shoulders had that set they got when a decision was being made somewhere inside him, slow as sap rising.

Then he pulled the door shut against the weather and came back to the fire, and whatever the decision was, it stayed made.

* * *

Afternoon faded into the gray dark of the storm's edge, and Sefa called them all up to the house for the evening meal, because in Lowmark you ate together when bad weather came—it was law, unwritten but enforced. The table was crowded and loud. Brill had made stew with the smoked fish and the last of the fresh peppers, and he stood over the pot like a general over a map, directing Odile to fetch bread, directing Tam to stir, directing Isandro to sit down and stop trying to help because he stirred like a man rowing.

Sefa presided from the head of the table, gray-streaked hair still damp from checking the roof lines, and she ate the way she did everything—efficiently, watchfully, with one ear always on the river.

"The gauge at the mill bridge was at four marks at noon," she said, halfway through. "It'll touch six by tomorrow night. Levee holds at seven if the sandbags do their work. After that we find out what we're made of."

"We're made of stubbornness and Brill's cooking," Odile said. "We'll be fine."

"Speak respectfully of the cooking," Brill said, "or I'll season nothing but regret come morning."

Tam signed something, fast, and Odile translated without missing a beat, because translating Tam had become her second trade: "He says if the levee breaks, we should all climb onto Brill, because he floats highest."

"I do not float," Brill said with dignity, "I displace."

The laughter ran around the table and came back doubled, and Tam sat in the middle of it with his bowl warming his hands and thought, not for the first time, that a person could drown in a room full of talking and never once feel wet—but he never did here. They always saved him a place in the current. Odile's voice carrying his jokes. Sefa's eyes finding his across the table to ask a question and read his answer. Even Isandro, who'd learned six of his hand-signs now and used them clumsily, proudly, like a man showing off coins from a foreign country.

*Good?* Isandro signed across the stew, waggling his spoon.

Tam signed back: *Slow. Like your rowing.*

Brill roared. Isandro pressed a hand to his chest as though shot, and Sefa told them all to behave, and the rain hammered the roof like applause.

Later, when the bowls were cleared and Brill dozed in his chair and Odile bent over her ledger by lamplight, Tam went out onto the covered gallery to look at the water. It was fully dark now, and the river had come up over the lowest walkway, black and muscular, carrying things past the town—branches, a fence rail, a barrel, a lantern that still burned, swinging, with nobody holding it. He watched the lantern go and wondered whose it was and where they were. The river gave and took and gave again, indifferent as weather, which was exactly what it was.

The gallery boards creaked behind him. Isandro came out and stood beside him, close enough to share warmth, far enough to leave a man his air. For a while neither of them did anything but watch the flood go by.

Then Isandro raised his hands and signed, slowly, carefully, getting each shape right: *You. Sleep. Soon.*

Tam nodded. Then, because the moment felt like it would hold it, he signed back the sign he'd invented himself, the one he'd only ever used for family: two fists pressed together over his heart, then opened outward, like something unfolding.

Isandro frowned at it. *What that?*

Tam considered how to explain. Then he simply took Isandro's hands—rough, scarred, capable—and folded the man's own fists together over his heart, and opened them outward, guiding the motion.

Isandro did it again, alone, slower. "What's it mean?" he asked aloud, quiet, under the rain.

Tam shrugged and smiled and didn't answer, because some meanings you couldn't hand over; people had to grow into them. He went inside to his cot.

Through the thin wall he heard Isandro stay out on the gallery a long time. And once—just once, faint under the rain—Tam heard the sound of two fists opening over a heart, the soft snap of knuckles, repeated, practiced, like a man learning the shape of a word he intended to keep.

* * *

Tam woke in the deepest part of the night to a silence he didn't trust.

The rain had changed—no longer the steady roar of the storm's leading edge but something harder, gustier, with a new note underneath it, a hiss off the water that meant wind coming upriver ahead of schedule. He lay listening. Down the hall, Sefa's bed creaked; she was awake too. Everyone in Lowmark was awake, probably, the whole town lying in the dark doing the same arithmetic: *too soon, too fast, too much.*

Boots on the gallery. Not Isandro's—heavier, hurried. Brill's voice, low and urgent, and Sefa's answer, sharp. Then running feet on the walkway planks, more than one pair, and a fist hammering on a door down the row, and a man's voice cracking with the effort of staying calm:

*"Fire on the south bank! The boathouse row—somebody's put torch to the Kell boathouse—"*

Tam was up before the sentence ended. Through the window, past the sheeted rain, he saw it: a bloom of orange in the black downriver, swelling, feeding, rain falling straight into it and losing. And silhouetted against that glow, figures on the levee path—riders' shapes, mounted, motionless, watching the town wake up and burn.

Not fleeing the storm, those men. Riding ahead of it.

Tam grabbed his coat and ran for the stairs, and behind him he heard Sefa say one word into the dark, quiet and terrible, a word he'd never heard her use about any weather:

*"Corvane."*
