## 15. Fire in the Flood

The storm's edge arrived like a rumor first—a smell of iron in the wind, a greenish bruise building over the western hills—and then it arrived like a verdict.

Isandro felt it before he saw it, the way he always did: a pressure behind his eyes, a tightening in his chest that twenty years of chasing weather had never taught him to ignore. He stood on Sefa's porch with a mug of Brill's chicory going cold in his hand and watched the sky change its mind about everything.

"That's not rain yet," he said.

Sefa came out beside him, wiping her hands on her apron. She was a broad woman with forearms like dock rope and a face that had been carved by forty years of river wind into something patient and unimpressed. She squinted westward and said nothing for a long moment.

"No," she agreed finally. "That's the herald. Rain's still a day out. Maybe less."

"Then who's that?"

He pointed. Downriver, past the last of Lowmark's stilt houses, where the levee curved away toward the drowned orchards, two figures were walking the crest. Not townsfolk—townsfolk walked the levee bent under sandbags or tools. These two walked like men taking inventory. One stopped at intervals, marking something in a book or on a slate. The other carried a long case slung across his back that Isandro's eye read as a tool chest until he saw how the man's hand rested on it.

Sefa saw them too. Her jaw set.

"Corvane's," she said. Not a question.

"Scouts." Isandro set down the mug. "Measuring approaches. Counting houses. Deciding where we're weakest."

"And what are you deciding?"

Isandro looked at her. That was the thing about Sefa—she never asked a question she didn't already know the shape of the answer to. She'd patched his hull with her own hands, fed him at her own table, let him sleep under her roof for eleven days while the monsoon built itself out west like an army assembling, and she had never once pretended to believe his story about salvaging scrap from the Kilow Reach.

"I'm deciding they'll come tonight," he said. "Or tomorrow, when the rain starts and nobody can see far enough to shoot straight. Corvane doesn't send scouts unless the main body's already moving."

"Then we've got work to do." She drained his cold chicory without ceremony and handed the mug back. "Get Tam. Tell him we're pulling the boats up past the second row of pilings. If fire comes off the water, I want nothing floating loose to carry it."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in air so thick you could chew it. The whole town moved with the grim economy of people who had done this before—Lowmark flooded most years, burned once a generation, and its people had inherited both calendars. Odile went door to door with her ledger, organizing watches. Brill hauled sandbags two at a time, grumbling cheerfully about civilians who stacked them "like a drunk stacking cups." Tam ran messages between the levee crews with his quick silent feet, his hand-signs getting faster and more elaborate as the afternoon wore on, and Isandro found himself reading them now almost without thinking—the flat palm for *wait*, the circling finger for *again*, the two fingers flicked toward the eyes that meant *watch*, always watch.

It was Tam who signed it first. They were dragging Sefa's skiff up the rollers when the boy stopped, turned toward the boathouse at the end of the main pier, and made the sign Isandro had learned only three days ago: fist closed, then opened slowly, fingers spreading like something blooming.

*Fire.*

Isandro followed his gaze. At the boathouse, one of the scouts stood on the pier, apparently admiring the construction. The other had vanished. And there was a third man now, down at the waterline where the reeds grew thick, doing something small and deliberate with his hands near the pilings.

The reeds were dry. Three weeks of low water before the monsoon, and the reed beds along the pilings had died standing, a hundred yards of brown tinder waiting for a reason.

"They're not scouting," Isandro said. His voice came out very flat. "Tam. Get Sefa. Get everyone. Now."

The boy was gone before Isandro finished the sentence.

Isandro ran for the pier himself, boots hammering planks slick with the first fat drops of the leading rain. The scout on the pier turned at the sound, and Isandro got a good look at him—young, well-fed, wearing the Optimists' gray traveling coat with the collar turned up against the damp. The man smiled the way creditors smile.

"Evening," the scout said. "Lovely town. Be a shame if anything happened to it."

"Get off the pier."

"Isandro Yarrow." The scout said the name like he'd been practicing it. "Mr. Corvane sends his regards. He says you're a reasonable man, and reasonable men don't let whole towns burn for the sake of one box of old paper. He says come find him at the narrows inn, any time before dawn tomorrow, and we all go home dry."

The smell hit then—oil, sharp and chemical under the petrichor—and Isandro understood that the conversation was already over, had been over since before he reached the pier, that the scout was only here to deliver terms to a man whose house was already burning.

Down at the waterline, flame stood up out of the reeds like something being born.

* * *

The rain saved them and nearly killed them in the same hour.

It came down the valley ahead of schedule, the storm's outriders arriving in ragged sheets that hammered sideways off the river, and the same deluge that soaked the burning reeds also drove the smoke low and blinding along the pier. Isandro went into it blind, coughing, guided by the orange glow through the murk, and found the boathouse already alight at its landward corner where the oil had been poured thickest.

The fire didn't behave like fire should have. In horizontal rain it should have drowned; instead the wind fed it, bending the flames flat along the roofline, throwing embers in long streaming arcs that landed hissing on the wet planks and flared again wherever the boathouse tar and paint gave them purchase. It was a fire that moved sideways, a fire that traveled, and Isandro stood in the stinging rain watching it eat the building where he'd slept for eleven nights and understood, with a clarity that felt almost calm, exactly what Corvane had bought.

Not the boathouse. The choice. Burn it or give up the chest—that was the arithmetic, written in flame for the whole town to read.

"Yarrow!" Brill's voice, huge over the wind. The big cook came down the pier carrying a coil of hose over one shoulder like a stole, four men behind him hauling buckets, and behind them half of Lowmark, pouring out of their houses into the rain because that was what Lowmark did—it stayed, and it fought, and it buried its dead after.

"The chest—" Isandro started.

"Is in my kitchen under the flour sacks where it's been for six days, you great fool, you think I sleep next to it for my health?" Brill shoved the hose end into his hands. "Pump's on the barge. Go."

The line formed itself the way lines form in places that have practiced disaster: a chain of neighbors passing buckets hand to hand, Brill commanding the pump barge with a voice that cut through the storm, Sefa appearing out of the smoke with her sleeves rolled and her hair plastered flat, taking the position at the worst corner without anyone assigning it to her. Odile organized a bucket relay from the cisterns. Tam ran the line, keeping count, signing corrections to the bucket rhythm when gaps opened.

And Isandro stood at the nozzle with the rain trying to tear the stream apart and the fire leaning into the wind like a living thing, and he fought it.

For an hour, maybe more—he lost time the way you lose time in storms, in chunks. The boathouse roof went, collapsing inward with a roar and a gout of steam that scalded the front rank. The pier caught twice and was beaten back twice. The wind swung, mercifully, pushing the fire back onto its own ruins, and inch by inch the town took the building away from the flames—not saving it, nothing could save it now, but containing it, starving it, killing it by degrees while the rain did the rest.

It was in the last hour that the wall came down.

The landward corner, the oiled corner, the corner Sefa had held from the start because someone had to and she'd decided it would be her—its charred studs gave way all at once, a curtain of burning timber folding outward, and Sefa was directly in its path with a bucket still in her hands.

Brill was closer.

Isandro saw it happen the way you see things in nightmares, complete and slow and unstoppable: Brill dropping the pump handle, crossing the distance in three strides, catching Sefa around the shoulders and turning with her, putting his own broad back between her and the collapse. The burning wall came down across him. Steam screamed up. For one horrible moment Isandro thought the big man had simply vanished into the fire, and then Brill staggered clear, still holding Sefa, his shirt smoking, the skin of his arms and neck gone wrong—shiny, blackening in patches, split open along the forearm where a nail of burning timber had raked him.

He set Sefa down gently, said something Isandro couldn't hear over the storm, and then his knees went and Odile and two others caught him before he hit the planks.

* * *

They laid him in the back room of the cookhouse, which smelled of every meal he'd ever made for them, and which now smelled of burn salve and scorched cloth. The town's midwife, a spare grey woman named Hesta who had stitched fishermen and pulled children and once, reportedly, sewn a man's ear back on, worked over him by lamplight while the storm outside climbed toward its full voice.

Brill's arms were wrapped to the elbow. The burns ran up the right side of his neck and along his jaw, and one ear was blistered past saving. He lay on the cot grinning through it, because Brill grinned through everything, but the grin cost him now—you could see it costing him, see the sweat on his forehead that wasn't fever but effort, the price of every smile.

"Held the line," he rasped when Isandro came in. "Tell everyone. Held the damn line."

"You idiot," Isandro said. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "You absolute—I was right there—"

"You were holding the hose." Brill's eyes found him, red-rimmed and steady. "Somebody has to hold the hose, Yarrow. Somebody has to hold the line. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked." He winced as Hesta probed the edge of the wrap. "You want to know the funny part? I spent thirty years holding a spear for men who paid me in promises. This is the first line I ever held for people who'd hold it back."

Hesta looked up. "Out," she said. "All of you. He needs sleep and so does everyone else, and there's a town to check."

In the cookhouse proper, the survivors of the night sat among the wreckage of the evening meal—cold stew, overturned chairs, a window shutter banging loose somewhere upstairs. Sefa sat with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, soot to the elbows, a raw scrape across one cheekbone where Brill's grip had saved her from worse. Odile had the chest's documents spread on the cleared half of the table, though why, Isandro couldn't imagine, until he saw her face.

She'd been reading them by lamplight while the town burned. Looking for something.

"Odile?"

"He won't stop." She didn't look up. Her finger rested on a line of Vhalorian script she'd translated weeks ago, but she wasn't seeing it. "I keep thinking there's a version of this where he gets what he wants and goes away. There isn't. I've been reading these letters for days and I finally understand what I'm reading. Corvane isn't a creditor's enforcer who happens to be obsessed with Vhaloria. He *is* Vhaloria—or he thinks he is. He's the last man who remembers it, and he's spent forty years building a kingdom out of that memory, and these papers are the only thing in the world that can prove his memory is a forgery." She looked up at last, and her face in the lamplight was older than her years. "You don't negotiate with a man whose entire self is at stake. You don't buy him off. Burning the boathouse wasn't leverage, Isandro. It was a message. He's telling us the price only goes up from here."

Outside, the rain intensified, the true body of the monsoon arriving at last, drumming on the roof like a thousand urgent fists. Somewhere down the levee a watchman called the hour and the state of the water, and the state of the water was rising.

Sefa spoke for the first time. Her voice was hoarse from smoke. "Brill nearly died tonight holding a line for a box of paper that isn't even in the box anymore." She turned her head slowly and looked at Isandro, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse than anger. "That's done. That's finished. Whatever you were protecting by staying quiet, whatever plan you had about slipping away upstream alone and drawing them off—it died in that fire, along with my boathouse. Because next time they won't burn an empty building, Isandro. Next time they'll burn the cookhouse. Or the schoolroom. Or they'll stop burning things and start burning people."

"I know."

"Do you?" She set down the untouched tea. "Because I watched you stand in that fire tonight, and I watched your face when Brill went down, and I'll tell you what I saw. I saw a man stop running. But stopping and choosing aren't the same thing, and Corvane will be at the narrows by dawn, and the river's coming up, and we are out of nights for you to make up your mind."

Isandro looked at the documents on the table—at the black sun stamped into the oldest letter, at Odile's careful translations filling the margins, at the names of the living written in a dead king's hand. He thought of Brill's ruined arms. Of Tam's quick signs. Of the roster on Sefa's wall with his name still absent from it.

"The settlement upstream," he said. "The Vhalorian elders. How fast can we move the chest?"

Odile and Sefa exchanged a look.

"In this?" Sefa nodded at the window, where the storm was reaching down into the bones of the world. "Only by water. Roads are mud by now. But the river's rising, and rising fast—which cuts both ways. It'll carry us quick as anything." Her eyes narrowed. "It'll also carry anyone following us quicker."

"Then we go tonight," Isandro said. "Before the peak. Before Corvane expects anyone mad enough to run the upper reach in this."

Odile was already gathering the documents, sliding them into oilcloth with hands that trembled only slightly. "There's a problem," she said quietly. "The narrows. It's on the way. It's the only way. And Corvane told you himself where he'd be waiting."

Isandro looked out at the dark, at the rain falling in ropes, at the river beyond the pilings already climbing its banks, brown and fast and hungry.

"Then we don't take the narrows," he said. "We take the rapids."

Sefa laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—but she was already reaching for her oilskins.
