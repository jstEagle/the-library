## 17. Running the Narrows

The rain came at them sideways, hard as thrown gravel, and the river had stopped being a river and become a moving wall.

Isandro crouched low in the stern of Odile's skiff—*her* skiff, because his own boat was ash and floating splinters somewhere behind them in Lowmark—and steered by feel more than sight. The lantern was useless. The storm had eaten the moon hours ago. All he had was the sound of the water ahead: a low, continuous growl that deepened as they went, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"That's it," Odile shouted from the bow, both hands locked on the chest between her knees. "That's the sound Tam described. The narrows."

Tam hadn't described anything. Tam didn't describe things. He'd drawn it—in charcoal, on the back of a shutter board, quick and certain—the channel splitting around a spine of black rock, the drop beyond it, the eddy on the left bank where a strong swimmer might live if the river was feeling generous. He'd pointed at the drawing, then at Isandro, then made the hand-sign they'd invented during the levee work: *you go, I stay.* Then he'd pressed the charcoal into Isandro's palm and closed Isandro's fingers over it, which was as close to a blessing as Tam gave anyone.

"Left side," Isandro called back. "We hug the left."

"The chest goes under if we ship water!"

"Then bail!"

She was already bailing. She'd been bailing since the second bend, when the old seams of the skiff started to weep in long silver threads that the rain instantly disguised. The boat was Sefa's third-best, which meant it was held together by stubbornness and paint, and it was carrying cargo worth more than every boat Sefa had ever built, if you believed what the letters said. Isandro didn't fully believe it yet. But Corvane believed it, and Corvane's belief had burned down a boathouse and put Brill in bandages from wrist to shoulder, so belief was enough.

The growl became a roar.

He felt the bottom change first—a sudden lightness in the way the current took the hull, the lazy grip of the deep channel releasing them into faster, shallower water. Then the banks began to close in, dark walls of dripping pine crowding both sides of the flood, and the river narrowed from forty yards to twenty to something that looked, in the lightning flashes, less like a passage than a throat.

"Hold on!" he roared, though there was nothing to hold but the gunwales and each other.

The first wave hit them broadside and lifted the whole skiff like a leaf off a gutter. For one impossible moment they were airborne—he felt it in his stomach, the weightless lurch—and then they slammed down into the trough and a ton of cold water broke across the bow. Odile vanished under it, reappeared coughing, arms still wrapped around the chest, still bailing with one hand while she held on with the other. He would think about that later, about how she never once let go, but in the moment there was no later. There was only the next rock.

It rose out of the dark dead ahead, a fang of wet stone streaming foam, and Isandro threw his whole body against the sweep oar. The skiff spun its nose downstream, missed the rock by an arm's length, and the backwash sucked at them, dragging them sideways toward the rock garden on the right where the white water churned itself into a boiling gray mess.

"No no no—" Odile saw it too.

"LEFT!" He hauled until his shoulders screamed. The oar bent. Something cracked—not the oar; the oarlock—and for a heartbeat the boat answered to nothing at all, spinning free in the flood like a coin dropped into a drain.

Lightning lit the narrows entire: the black spine of rock running down the middle, the two channels tearing themselves apart around it, the drop at the far end where the river fell three feet into chaos. And in that single flash Isandro understood the drawing completely, understood what Tam had been telling him without words: *the left is alive, the right is death, and the middle will eat you.*

They were in the middle.

He did the only thing there was to do. He shipped the broken oar, grabbed the spare paddle wedged under the thwart, and paddled like a man trying to outrun his own name—hard, ugly strokes, all shoulder and fury, dragging the skiff's nose leftward against the current's opinion. The river shoved back. The right-hand chaos reached for them with long fingers of suction. The gap between the spine and the left bank was maybe six feet wide and looked narrower every time the lightning came.

"We're not going to make it!" Odile screamed.

"Then scream louder, it'll help!"

And then—it shouldn't have worked, nothing that stupid should ever work—but the hull caught a seam of faster water along the spine's flank, a tongue of current running clean through the gap, and the river, capricious as any creditor, decided to let them live. They shot through the slot with stone scraping wood on both sides, a sound like teeth closing, and burst out the far side into the falling water below the drop.

The skiff dropped. His stomach stayed up.

They landed flat, miraculously, brutally flat, and the impact drove the breath out of him and drove a foot of gray water over the gunwales. When he could breathe again he realized two things at once: the roar behind them was fading, swallowed by rain, and the boat was sinking.

Not dramatically. Not all at once. Just steadily, the way debts grew, the way interest compounded—a little more water in the bilge every minute than Odile could throw out, the seams opening wider with every mile, the skiff settling lower and lower until the river was inside the boat with them, patient, inevitable.

"How far?" Odile gasped. Her hands were bleeding; she'd torn the skin bailing. "How far to the settlement?"

"Two bends. Maybe three." He could see almost nothing. The valley walls here were steep and tree-choked, and the rain had thickened into a solid gray curtain that turned the world into a smear. "Can the chest swim?"

"It better learn fast."

* * *

The second bend nearly killed them anyway.

A strainer—a full pine, rootball and all, torn loose from some hillside upstream—lay jammed across the channel, its branches combing the flood like the fingers of a drowned giant. Isandro saw it late, a darker darkness ahead, and swung hard for the gap beneath the trunk where the water funneled through. It was a bad choice and the only choice. The branches above would wrap them, break their backs, hold them under. Below was just water.

They went under the trunk so close he heard the bark whisper along the gunwale, and then a branch that had dipped with the flood snagged the stern and slewed them around, and for ten seconds the river played with them like a cat with something already half-dead—spinning the skiff, dragging it backward, pressing it down. Odile went over the side.

No—she didn't fall. She *went*, deliberately, shoving off the hull with both feet to keep its weight from capsizing them, one arm hooked through the chest rope, and suddenly Isandro was alone in a sinking boat watching a clerk from Lowmark get towed through a flood at the end of a line like bait on a hook.

"Odile!" He got the skiff turned, got alongside her, hauled her in by the collar with the last of his strength. She came over the gunwale in a tangle of rope and hair and rain, still holding the line, still holding the chest, coughing up half the Kilow Reach.

"I'm fine," she lied, teeth chattering.

"You're insane."

"I'm Vhalorian," she said, and something in the way she said it—half joke, half oath—made his chest hurt in a place that had nothing to do with rowing.

The skiff had perhaps a mile left in it. He knew it the way he knew weather, the way he knew when a hull was done talking and starting to confess. Every stroke now was borrowed money. So when the third bend opened up and he saw the eddy—Tam's eddy, the calm water behind the point, exactly where the charcoal drawing promised—he didn't hesitate. He rowed them out of the main current and into the slack, and the skiff settled to the gunwales and stopped, cradled in the quiet water, going down gently as a tired animal lying down.

"Out," Isandro said. "Take the rope. Get to shore."

"What about you?"

"I'm right behind you."

Which was true for about four seconds. Because when he turned to drag the chest free of the flooding hull, the movement shifted the load, and the skiff rolled.

It happened slowly enough to watch. The gunwale dipping, the river pouring in over the side with a sound like applause, the whole wreck sliding out from under him into the black water. And the chest—freed of its rope, buoyant as a coffin, riding high for one glorious instant before the eddy's edge caught it and began to pull it back toward the main current, back toward the strainer, back toward everything downstream that wanted it.

Corvane wanted it. That was the thought that moved Isandro's body before his mind agreed to anything. Forty years of erased country, living descendants, a man who burned towns to own a piece of paper—all of it bobbing away from him in the rain, gathering speed.

Isandro went after it.

He dove off the sinking skiff into water cold enough to stop his heart from surprise alone, surfaced, stroked. The chest was ten feet away, then eight, then six, the current doing most of its traveling and none of his. His fingers brushed the wet cedar. He got a hand over the lid's edge, got his other hand on the rope, and then the eddy released him into the main flow and the river took them both.

It was different, swimming in a flood. There was nothing to push against. The water didn't carry you so much as happen to you, tumbling him end over end, driving him under, spitting him up. He kept his fist locked in the rope and the rope locked in the chest and told himself the simple arithmetic: *don't let go, don't let go, don't let go.* Downstream the strainer waited, branches combing the dark. Fifty yards. Forty. He tried to angle for the bank and the river laughed and dragged him sideways instead, and he realized with a strange, floating clarity that he could not make the shore. He could keep the chest or he could make the shore, and even that was optimistic, because the shore was a rumor and the chest was real and heavy in his fist.

Thirty yards. He put his boots up to fend off a floating log. Twenty. Somewhere in the roaring dark he thought, absurdly, of the Optimists' letter, the smiling boy, the compounding interest—everything in his life that had ever pulled him downstream—and he thought: *this time I'm holding on to the right thing.*

Fifteen yards from the strainer, something hit the water beside him.

He heard it before he saw it—the flat slap of a hull driven hard, a paddle-stroke, deliberate as a signature. Then a shape resolved out of the rain: a flat-bottomed rescue skiff, shallow and wide, built for exactly this water, and standing in the stern with the paddle planted like a lance—

Tam.

Of course it was Tam. Of course the mute boy who drew rivers in charcoal had known the narrows better than any map, had slipped out of Lowmark after them or ahead of them, had run the bank in the dark until the eddy, then read the flood and read Isandro's line and put himself exactly where the river would deliver a drowning man. Tam didn't shout. Tam never shouted. He just extended the paddle shaft toward Isandro across the churning water, steady as a levee, and waited.

Isandro had one hand on the chest rope and one hand free, and the math was brutal and instant. Let go of the chest, grab the paddle, live. Hold the chest, miss the paddle, and the strainer would settle the account.

He grabbed the chest with both hands.

Later, Odile would tell him he was insane. Later, Sefa would say nothing at all, just look at him for a long moment with those eyes that saw through evasions, and that silence would be worse and better than any lecture. But in the moment, in the flood, with the strainer fifteen yards away and closing, Isandro Yarrow—who had spent eleven years letting go of everything: boats, towns, names, his brother's grave, his own reflection in shop windows—held on to the one thing he refused to lose, and trusted the rest to somebody else.

Tam didn't curse him. Tam adjusted.

The boy swept the paddle in one long, powerful arc, swung the skiff's nose downstream parallel to Isandro's line, and matched his speed—reading the current the way he read everything, silently, perfectly. Then he leaned out over the water, hip against the gunwale, and took a fistful of Isandro's collar in a grip that had hauled sandbags for two weeks and knew exactly what a drowning man weighed.

The strainer's branches raked past close enough to touch. A twig whipped across Isandro's knuckles. And then the skiff's momentum and Tam's shoulders and the river's own whim all agreed at once, and Isandro came up over the side in a rush of cold water and colder air, chest and all, crashing onto the boards with the cedar box pinned under him like a heart he'd finally learned to keep beating.

For a long moment nobody moved. The rain hammered the hull. The strainer slid away behind them, harmless now, just a tree.

Tam sat back on his haunches, breathing hard, and looked at Isandro with an expression that needed no translation. It said: *you idiot.* And underneath it, unmistakably, it said: *I came for you.*

Isandro lay on his back on the bottomboards, chest under his arms, rain hitting his face, and laughed—cracked, gasping laughter that had no humor in it and all of it, the laugh of a man whose ledger has just come due and been paid by someone else. Tam watched him laugh and did not smile, because Tam didn't smile easily, but he reached out and gripped Isandro's shoulder, once, hard, and let go.

Odile's voice came thin across the water. She'd made the shore somehow—he'd lost track of her entirely in the chaos—and she stood on the muddy bank with a branch torch sputtering in the rain, holding the light high, shouting his name into the storm. Behind her, faint but unmistakable, other lights were kindling up the valley slope. Lanterns. Dozens of them, strung along the hillside like a constellation brought down to earth, marking the terraces and walkways of a settlement that wasn't supposed to exist.

The black sun settlement. People coming out into the worst night of the monsoon with lights, because two boats were overdue and someone had counted heads and found them short.

Isandro sat up. Water ran out of his sleeves. Tam handed him the paddle without ceremony, pointing him to the yoke, and took up position in the bow, facing forward, watching the water for rocks the way he watched everything—completely, quietly, missing nothing.

They rowed for the lights together.

As the bank came closer, Isandro could make out figures among the lanterns—elders in oilskins, young men wading into the shallows to catch the painter, a woman with gray braids standing slightly apart with her arms crossed, taking their measure. And behind them all, limping down the path with her arm bound and her face gray with pain and absolutely refusing to be carried, a boatwright who had no business being out of bed, who had told him *stay through the monsoon* and meant it, and who had apparently decided that if he wouldn't stay put, she would come to the water and collect him herself.

Sefa stood at the floodline with the rain streaming off her chin, and she did not look relieved. She looked furious, and frightened, and like a woman counting her people and finding, this once, the number intact.

"Well," Isandro said to Tam, hauling on the paddle, voice raw. "I guess we're expected."

Upstream, faint beneath the rain, carried down the valley on the wind, came a sound that froze the blood in his arms mid-stroke: a horn. Long, low, deliberate. Blown twice.

The elders on the bank went still. The woman with the gray braids turned her head toward the sound, and her face in the lantern light was old stone.

"They've found the valley," she said.
