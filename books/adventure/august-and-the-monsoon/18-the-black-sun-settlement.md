## 18. The Black Sun Settlement

The river forgave them nothing on the last stretch.

Isandro poled the leaking skiff through a corridor of drowned alders, water sloshing over his boots with every stroke, and watched the walls of the valley close in around them like hands cupped to shield a candle flame. The storm had followed them upstream all night—rain in gray ropes, the current running brown and fast—but here the water went strangely quiet, as if the river itself had been asked to lower its voice.

"There," Odile said.

She stood at the bow, one hand locked on the painter line, the other holding her copied pages flat against her chest beneath an oilskin. She had not slept. None of them had. But when she pointed, her arm was steady.

Where the valley pinched between two shoulders of black rock, the water spread into a wide, slow pool, and at its head stood a weir of fitted stone so old that moss had eaten the chisel marks. Above it, half-hidden by terraced gardens and the hanging curtains of rain, rose buildings that did not belong to any map Isandro had ever salvaged from. Slate roofs. A bell tower with no bell. Carved lintels bearing a symbol he now knew better than his own name: a black sun, rays inward, like light falling into itself rather than out.

Vhaloria. Or what was left of it, hiding in a fold of the world for forty years.

"They'll have seen us already," Tam signed from the stern, hands quick even in the downpour. He'd appeared at the narrows like a miracle in a rescue skiff, and Isandro still hadn't found words adequate to that, so he'd said nothing, which Tam seemed to understand perfectly.

"Then we come in slow," Isandro said. "Hands visible. We're not raiders."

"You're soaked, bleeding, and carrying a chest full of dead kings," Odile said. "You look exactly like raiders."

"Slow anyway."

They grounded the skiff on a shingle beach below the weir, and Isandro lifted the chest onto his shoulder—the weight of it had become familiar now, almost companionable, forty pounds of other people's history riding his spine—and they climbed the stone stair cut into the bank. Rain hammered the terraces. Somewhere above, a dog began to bark, then stopped abruptly, silenced by a hand or a whistle.

Figures emerged from the misted gardens without hurry. Six of them, maybe eight, wrapped in oiled wool, and every one of them carried something: a boar spear, a billhook, a long fowling piece older than Isandro's boat. They arranged themselves across the path with the unhurried competence of people who had practiced being found and not wanting it.

An old woman stepped forward. She was small and straight-backed, white hair plastered to her skull by the rain, and her eyes went first—not to Isandro, not to the weapons—to the chest.

"You came upriver," she said. Her accent was old-fashioned, vowels rounded like river stones. "Through the narrows. In this."

"The alternative was waiting at the bottom for the man chasing us," Isandro said.

"And who is that man?"

Isandro hesitated. He'd rehearsed this speech somewhere in the dark hours between rapids—that he would say *Corvane*, that he would explain, that he would hand over the chest and be done with the whole cursed thing. But standing under the gaze of people whose grandparents had chosen exile over a comfortable lie, the rehearsed words felt thin.

"He calls himself Corvane," Odile said, stepping up beside him. "But my grandmother's letters say there was a state archivist named Corvanis, who signed the inventory of the royal seal the day the monarchy fell. He was young then. He'd be old enough now." She pulled her oilskin back and produced her copied pages, protected in waxed cloth. "He wants your chest. He says it proves a bloodline. I think he means to prove one that doesn't exist—with himself holding the crown's leash."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then at Isandro. Then at Tam, mute and steady at his side, and at the chest dripping river water onto sacred ground.

"I am Maren Osta-Vhalor," she said. "Keeper of the settlement. You will come inside. You will be fed. And you will tell me everything, in order, while my people decide whether you are guests or problems." A ghost of dry humor crossed her face. "I should warn you. We have had forty years of practice deciding about problems."

* * *

They took the chest into a long hall built of black timber, its rafters smoke-darkened, its walls hung with tapestries so faded their scenes had dissolved into suggestion—a hunt, a coronation, a flood. A fire burned in a central hearth, and around it perhaps sixty people had gathered, drawn from houses along the terrace paths: farmers, weavers, two women in the gray robes of some vanished order, children peering from behind legs. They spoke Vhalorian among themselves, that liquid, formal language Odile had been translating all week, and switched to trade-tongue only when addressing the newcomers, which made Isandro feel like a guest at a table set in a language he'd never learn fast enough.

Brill wasn't there. Brill was thirty miles downstream with burns down his arms and Sefa holding the levee, and the thought arrived in Isandro's chest like a fishhook every time the firelight reminded him of firelight in a burning boathouse. He put it away. There would be time to feel things later, if later existed.

Maren sat across the hearth from them, and beside her an ancient man with clouded eyes whom the others addressed as the Reader. The chest sat between them all on a trestle table, and nobody touched it.

"In order," Maren said.

So Isandro told it. The salvage fee upriver, the letter from the Optimists, the debt that had started with a coffin for his brother and compounded like floodwater ever since. The night storm. The receding river. The chest wedged in the reeds with the black sun stamped on its lid. He told it plainly, without embellishment, because these people would smell embellishment the way fish smelled blood.

Odile picked up the thread where his ended—the copying, the names in the documents, the living descendants scattered through the valley towns, the discovery that Corvane's own credentials were forged, that the man who claimed to restore a dynasty was inventing one from whole cloth.

When she finished, the hall was silent except for rain on slate and the pop of wet wood.

Maren turned to the old man. "Reader. Your judgment."

The Reader's blind eyes were fixed on the middle distance, but his hands moved over the table until they found the chest's iron hasps. He didn't open it. He rested his palms on the lid as though taking a pulse.

"This chest left Vhaloria the last night," he said. "I was a boy. I remember the carts going out in the rain, and my mother crying, and my father saying the records must survive even if the kingdom didn't." His voice was reedy but level. "There were three chests. Two were decoys. This—" his fingers traced the seal, "—this one is real. I would know it by its weight. Lies weigh less."

"Open it," Maren said.

Isandro did. Forty years of sealed air breathed out of it: paper, wax, cedar, time. Inside lay the documents he knew—the genealogies, the land grants, the correspondence of a court in its final desperate weeks—and beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth he'd never dared unwrap, the sealed letter addressed to no one alive.

The Reader's hands moved through the papers like a man reading braille, faster than Isandro thought possible. The hall waited. Even the children went still.

"Yes," the Reader said at last, and his voice cracked on it. "Yes. These are the true registers. The succession rolls. The letters of the last council." He lifted one page toward Maren though he could not see it. "And this letter—it is addressed to *the keepers of the memory*, whoever they would prove to be. To us. It has been addressed to us for forty years and we did not know if it still existed."

"What does it say?" someone called from the crowd.

The Reader smiled, and it transformed his ruined face into something luminous. "It says: *We are not the kingdom. We never were. The kingdom is whoever remembers.*"

Maren closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, they were wet, and her voice was iron again. "And Corvane? What do these papers give *him*?"

"Nothing," Odile said. "That's the point. There's no surviving legitimate heir named in them—or rather, there are dozens, descended through commoners, through exile lines, through families that don't know what they are. The documents can't crown anyone. They can only say who *was*. Memory, not throne." She hesitated. "Which means whatever he shows the world, he's showing them a forgery. And these papers are the only thing in existence that can prove it."

"Then he cannot permit these papers to exist," Maren said.

"No," Isandro said. "He can't."

The word settled over the hall like the rain outside. Maren looked around at her people—at the gray-robed women, the farmers with spear-calloused hands, the children—and Isandro saw her doing arithmetic that had nothing to do with numbers: how many lives against how much truth.

"You could burn it," Isandro said quietly. "The chest. Scatter the ashes in the weir pool. He'd have nothing to disprove, and you'd have nothing he wants. I've thought about it every night since I pulled it from the reeds. It would have been simpler. Safer." He met her eyes. "But I've been running from simple and safe for six years, and all it bought me was more running. So I won't recommend it. I'll just say it's yours to choose. It always was."

Maren studied him for a long moment. "A salvage man," she said, "who found a kingdom's heart in the reeds and carried it upstream through rapids instead of selling it. My grandmother would have said the river chooses its couriers." She turned to the hall. "We do not burn. We have burned nothing in forty years—not our books, not our names, not our dead. We will not start with the truth."

A sound went through the gathered people—not a cheer, nothing so loud, but a low collective exhale, like a hull taking a wave and shedding it.

"The papers stay here," Maren continued. "Copied tonight, by three hands, and the copies go to three cellars. The originals go into the deep archive under the bell tower, where they have lain before and can lie again. As for you three—" her gaze took in Isandro, Odile, Tam, "—you have earned rest, food, and dry clothes. In that order."

Tam signed something rapidly, and Odile laughed for the first time in days. "He says dry clothes first, and he'll fight anyone who disagrees."

"Dry clothes first," Maren agreed, and something in the hall loosened.

* * *

They ate in a smaller room off the main hall—barley soup with river fish, dark bread, tea brewed from something piney and fierce—and Isandro discovered he was hungry in a way that felt almost embarrassing, the hunger of a body that had finally been given permission to want things. Tam fell asleep sitting up halfway through his bowl, and an old weaver tucked a blanket around him without comment, and Isandro watched it happen and felt something in his chest he refused to examine yet.

Odile sat across from him, turning her teacup in circles.

"My grandmother used to sing," she said. "Songs she said were nothing, just lullabies from her mother. Tonight I heard four of them in the main hall. Same tunes. Different words—they kept the old language alive and we kept the melodies without knowing why." She looked up. "Forty years, Isandro. They held a whole country in their mouths for forty years, and nobody downstream ever noticed."

"That's what staying looks like, I guess."

"Don't get philosophical. You're terrible at it."

"I'm exhausted. It looks similar."

She almost smiled, then didn't. "Corvane's men breached the valley behind us. That's what the scouts said while you were eating. Three riders through the south pass, moving fast, and more behind them. Maren's people have watched that pass for decades—they say no one comes through it in monsoon weather unless they're willing to lose horses." Her cup stopped turning. "He's willing to lose horses."

Isandro set down his spoon. Through the window, the rain had changed character—heavier, steadier, the storm's main body arriving at last, the barometer falling the way Sefa had taught him to read in the way the eaves dripped. Somewhere below the settlement, the river was rising again, undoing everything it had surrendered.

"How many does Corvane have left?" he asked.

"After the narrows? After the fire?" Odile counted on her fingers. "Eight. Maybe ten. Enough."

"Enough for what? He can't burn a valley in this rain. Even he knows that."

"He doesn't need to burn anything." Odile's voice dropped. "Think like an archivist, Isandro. Think like paperwork. He doesn't need the chest anymore if he can make sure no one believes what's in it. One fire in the right archive. One accident involving the elders. A settlement of hidden foreigners harboring stolen property—how hard is it to make a story like that stick, downstream, where nobody loves Vhaloria's ghost?"

Isandro stared at her. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like furniture falling downstairs.

"So we don't let him have an accident."

"We're sixty people and a bell tower with no bell."

"We're sixty people," Isandro said slowly, "who just spent forty years learning how to hide, and he's coming to *us*, in *our* valley, in the worst storm in a decade, with men who've been awake as long as we have and are twice as scared." He stood, joints protesting, and looked at the sleeping shape of Tam, at the fire, at the faded tapestry of the coronation dissolving into suggestion on the wall. "Sefa once told me the difference between running and standing is mostly deciding which direction you're facing. I'm done facing downstream."

Odile rose too. "What's the plan?"

"First, we wake Maren. Second, we ask her how her people watch a pass in the dark. Third—" he glanced at the window, at the drowning world beyond it, and felt, absurdly, calm, "—third, we introduce Corvane's riders to the monsoon. Let the storm do some of the work for once. It owes me."

Down in the valley, faint beneath the rain, a horse screamed—short, distant, cut off. The scouts' warning bells, silent for forty years, stayed silent; they had never needed bells to know when trouble was climbing toward them.

But everyone in the room heard the horse.

Odile met Isandro's eyes. "They're early."

"They're early," he agreed, and reached for his coat.
