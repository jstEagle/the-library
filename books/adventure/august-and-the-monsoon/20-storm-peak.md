## 20. Storm Peak

The storm arrived like a debt collector—no negotiation, no warning shot, just the full weight of it coming down the valley at once.

Isandro stood on the levee at what Odile's barometer said was two in the morning, though the sky had no opinion on the matter. Rain came sideways in ropes thick as his wrist, and the river below had risen past every mark Sefa had cut into the pilings over thirty years. The water wasn't brown anymore. It was the color of wet iron, and it moved with the patience of something that had all night and all the mountains behind it.

"Sandbag line!" Brill's voice carried through the rain, hoarse from three days of shouting, from the burns that wrapped his left arm to the elbow under layers of greased linen. He refused to leave the levee. Sefa had stopped arguing with him somewhere around midnight of the second day.

The line was a hundred people deep now—Lowmark's stubborn remnant and the Vhalorian settlers who'd come down from the valley when Corvane's men breached it at dusk. Old Mereth walked the sandbag chain with a lantern she had no business carrying in this wind, calling out weak points in a language Isandro didn't speak and a tone he understood perfectly. Tam worked beside him without being asked, passing bags with those quick sure hands, their hand-signals worn smooth by weeks of practice: *here*, *faster*, *hold*.

Somewhere upstream, the weir groaned.

It was an old structure, older than Lowmark—a spine of timber and stone thrown across the narrows a century ago to tame the current, already cracked, already leaking, and now holding back a river that had decided it wanted its valley back. If the weir went, the flood would come down on the settlement and the town together, and everything they'd bled for these three days would be a story told by survivors.

And Corvane was up there.

---

They'd seen his torches cross the ridge an hour before full dark—two dozen men with pitch and oil, because fire still worked even in horizontal rain if you were determined enough. The settlers had met them at the tree line and turned them back once, but Corvane hadn't retreated. He'd done something worse. He'd gone to the weir.

"He knows," Odile had said, finding Isandro in the dark, her copied pages waterproofed in oilcloth against her chest. "If the weir breaks, the flood does his work for him. The settlement drowns, the records drown, and whatever washes downstream can't crown anyone." She'd paused. "Or he's up there because he thinks we'll bring the chest to him. To save the town."

Both things were true. That was the trouble with Corvane—he never gambled on one outcome when he could arrange three.

Sefa had put it more simply, tying off the last rope on her skiff: "He's a man who reads water, Isandro. So read it back."

Now Isandro stood at the base of the weir path, rain hammering the hood of his oilskin, and made the calculation he'd been avoiding for three days. The levee could hold if the weir held. The weir would not hold much longer—he could hear it, a low grinding complaint beneath the storm, timber shifting against stone. Someone had to go up there, and everyone else had a reason to stay: the line needed Brill's voice, Sefa's judgment, Odile's translations, Tam's hands.

He had no reason to stay. He'd spent six years being a man with no reason to stay. It was, at least, a skill.

He caught Tam's eye and signed: *watch the line*. Then, because the boy deserved more than orders: *I come back.*

Tam looked at him for a long moment, rain streaming down his face, and signed back something Isandro hadn't taught him—something slow and deliberate, borrowed maybe from Odile's books. *You are not alone anymore.* Then he turned and shouldered a sandbag like the conversation was finished.

Isandro climbed.

---

The weir path was a knife-edge of slick stone above churning water, and the wind up there had teeth. He went on hands and knees for the worst stretches, the chest strapped to his back—he'd realized halfway up the hill that leaving it below was leaving it for whoever won, and he was no longer willing to let the river or Corvane decide. If it went into the flood tonight, it would go by his hand, on his terms.

Corvane stood at the center of the weir where the old sluice gate had collapsed into a broken gap, directing his men with lantern signals. They'd wedged timbers into the breach—not to repair it, Isandro saw with a cold drop in his stomach, but to rig it. A trigger. Pull the right beam and the whole compromised span would let go at once, sending the backed-up flood down the valley in a single wall instead of a long drowning rise.

"Salvage man!" Corvane didn't turn around. The wind stole half his voice and gave it back distorted, almost gentle. "I wondered which you'd choose. The levee or me."

"You're standing on a collapsing weir in a monsoon," Isandro called back. "That's not a choice anyone's making. That's just where you are."

Corvane laughed—a real laugh, brief and almost warm—and turned. In the lightning-flash Isandro saw how bad he looked. The charm was still there, the archivist's neat precision, but underneath it the man was wrung out, eyes sunk in bruised sockets, forty years of patient paperwork finally failing him in the one arena paper couldn't rule. His hired men hung back along the path, and Isandro counted them without meaning to. Eight. Two of them wouldn't meet his eyes. One kept glancing downriver, toward the torches of the levee line, toward a hundred people holding a wall together with their hands.

"The documents," Corvane said. "Give them to me, and I pull my men off this valley tonight. Every debt you carry dies with my signature. You've seen what my signature does—it moves armies and accountants with equal ease." He stepped closer along the wet stone, sure-footed even now. "You could be gone by morning. Free. Isn't that all you've ever wanted?"

And here was the thing Isandro discovered, standing in the worst weather of his life: the answer was no, and it cost him nothing to say it.

"I buried my brother with borrowed money," he said. "Six years running from the interest. And you know what I learned on this levee? Debt isn't what you owe. It's what you'll give up to keep from losing anything else." He shrugged the chest off his shoulder and held it between them, rain sheeting off the black sun stamped into the lid. "You want this because it's proof. But proof only works on people who agree to be ruled by it. These people—" he nodded downriver, at the lights strung along the levee like a constellation someone had dropped— "they don't. They read your letters out loud at night like stories. That's all Vhaloria is now. Stories. And stories don't need your throne."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—the first honest thing Isandro had ever seen there. Not greed. Grief. Forty years of an archivist's faith, that if you preserved the record carefully enough the past would stand up out of it and make the present make sense.

"They erased us," Corvane said quietly. "Maps, archives, names. I was the last man who remembered the succession. Do you know what that is? Being the only grave?"

"Yes," Isandro said. "But you dug it facing the wrong way."

Corvane's hand went to the rigged timber.

Isandro was faster—not because he was quick, but because he'd already decided. He wrenched the chest open against the wind, tore out the armful of letters and ledgers and the signet wrapped in oilcloth, and hurled them into the gap in the weir, into the black roaring water below.

For one heartbeat nobody moved. Even the storm seemed to lean in.

Then Corvane screamed—a sound with no charm in it at all—and lunged, and Isandro caught him, and the two of them went down onto the wet stone together as the river took the papers.

---

They fought badly, the way men fight on slippery stone in a gale—all grappling and breath, no craft. Corvane was stronger than he looked and mad enough to be careless; he got Isandro's collar and slammed his head against the weir twice, and the world went white and ringing. Through the white, Isandro heard the timber creak, heard one of Corvane's own men shout, "The span's going! *The span's going!*"

He rolled clear as the rigged beam shifted. Below him the gap in the weir yawned wider, water tearing through it with a sound like the end of the world arriving early.

Corvane didn't run. That was the terrible thing. He crawled toward the edge, toward the flood that had swallowed his life's work, reaching into the water as if he could take it back—as if forty years of careful preservation could be scooped out of a river in spate. Isandro grabbed his ankle and hauled, and Corvane fought him, actually fought to get *into* the flood, and Isandro understood then that he'd already lost everything he came with and the body just hadn't been told.

"The settlement remembers!" Isandro shouted into his ear, over the grinding of the failing span. "Mereth remembers! Odile's grandmother's recipes remember! You're not the last grave, you fool—you never were. There's a whole valley of them upstream!"

Corvane went still.

And in that stillness, the weir died. The old span buckled with a crack Isandro felt through his knees, and the river came through the breach in earnest—not a wall, the timbers had bought them that much, but a rising fury, and the stone under them tilted, and Isandro did the arithmetic of a salvage man one final time: his grip, the slope, the water, the distance to the bank.

He let go of Corvane.

Not into the flood—never that. He shoved him, hard, up-slope, toward the path, toward his own waiting men, and Corvane sprawled onto solid stone as the section Isandro knelt on slid, tipped, and dropped him into the shallows at the weir's drowned foot.

The cold hit like a hammer. The current took him instantly, tumbling him past the broken span, past the torchlight, into the dark—and the last thing he saw before the water closed over his head was the chest's empty lid spinning away downstream, the black sun turning over and over like a coin flipped by a god who hadn't decided yet.

*Let it go,* he thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace. *Everything. Let it go.*

Then hands. Hands in the water, callused and certain, hauling him sideways out of the main current into an eddy, and a skiff's gunwale cracking against his ribs, and Tam's face above him, furious and terrified and lit by a lantern, pulling with both arms while Brill—Brill, bandaged and burned and supposed to be on the levee—paddled them clear of the wreckage with strokes that cost him a grunt apiece.

Isandro coughed half the river out of his lungs and gripped the gunwale and laughed, or sobbed, or some ruined combination of the two.

"You," he rasped at Tam, "were supposed to watch the line."

Tam signed one-handed, still holding the lantern: *You come back. I watched.*

---

The weir's failure should have killed them all. Instead, by some mercy of the broken span, the flood came down in three great pulses instead of one killing wave, and the levee—hundred people deep, sandbags and planks and bodies and prayer—took each pulse and bent and did not break. Isandro reached the line as dawn began as a rumor behind the rain clouds, and worked until his hands stopped registering as his own, and when the fourth hour passed and the river crested and began, grudgingly, to fall, nobody cheered. They just kept passing sandbags, because nobody quite believed it yet.

Sefa found him at midmorning, sitting in the mud against a piling, too tired to be wet anymore.

"The chest?" she asked.

"Gone. In the flood." He looked at his empty hands. "I threw it away myself. Papers, seal, all of it. Corvane's claim dies with it—and so does the proof."

Sefa lowered herself beside him, joints cracking, and was quiet for a while. Down the levee, Mereth was leading the settlers in something low and rhythmic, not quite a hymn, not quite work song.

"You think truth lives in paper?" she said finally. "Boy, I patch boats. I know what lasts and what doesn't. Wood rots, canvas rots, ink rots." She tapped his chest with one blunt finger. "Odile has every page memorized. Has since the night she copied them. Mereth knew those successions before Corvane ever found his first forgery. You didn't destroy anything tonight. You just made it lighter to carry."

Isandro stared at her. Then, downstream, near the drowned remains of the boathouse, a figure was picking its way along the wreckage—thin, mud-caked, moving with the careful dignity of a man whose entire world had just been renegotiated. Corvane's hired men were nowhere in sight. The enforcer of the Optimists walked alone through the ruins of his ambition, and ahead of him, waiting with her arms folded and the settlers gathering quietly behind her, stood old Mereth.

Corvane saw her. Stopped. And Isandro, watching from the levee, saw the exact moment the last archivist of Vhaloria realized he was about to be judged by the only law he'd never managed to forge.

Mereth raised one hand—not in anger. In summons.

"Vhalorian law," Odile said softly, appearing at Isandro's shoulder, her voice raw from the night. "They kept it. All these years. Kept it the way he kept his papers." She exhaled. "He's going to face the thing he claimed to serve."

The rain was ending. Above the valley, for the first time in four days, there was a seam of pale gold in the clouds, and the river, exhausted, carried the last of the black sun's papers out toward the sea.
