## 22. August, Ending

The boathouse smelled of cut timber and river mud, and Isandro had come to think of it as the best smell in the world.

He stood on the new joists at mid-morning, hammer balanced in his hand, and looked out over Lowmark the way a man looks at something he has decided to keep. The flood had retreated three weeks ago, leaving its signature everywhere: a brown tideline across every wall, silt in the gutters, reeds growing from the second-story window of the chandlery. But the town had not washed away. That was the thing about Lowmark that Isandro was only now, finally, letting himself understand. It never did. It sat in the water and waited, and when the water left, it was still there.

Sefa's boathouse had fared worse than most. Corvane's men had burned it twice—once in the first assault, once more out of spite as they fled upstream—and what remained after the storm peak was a black skeleton of charred posts and a foundation slab that Sefa had stood on the morning after, arms crossed, saying nothing for a long time. Then she'd said: "We build it wider."

So they were building it wider.

Isandro drove a spike into the joist, felt the good solid bite of it, and allowed himself the small luxury of thinking about nothing at all. His hands knew this work. There was a peace in work that asked nothing of him but the next board and the next nail, no calculations, no head starts at midnight, no counting the miles between himself and the people who wanted his money or his blood or, lately, a chest he no longer possessed. For years he had lived like a man playing cards with a marked deck, always aware of the cheat, always planning his exit before the hand was even dealt. Now there was no deck. No game. Just August, and lumber, and the sound of Tam whistling through his teeth down by the waterline where he was fitting the new skid beams.

Tam couldn't whistle words, so he whistled tunes instead, and lately the tune had been one Odile had dug out of the Vhalorian letters—a coronation song, she'd said, though the elders at the settlement laughed when they heard it and told her it was actually a tax collector's drinking ballad that some royal clerk had put grand words to afterward. Tam didn't care. He whistled it anyway, off-key, with the fierce loyalty of someone who had decided a song was his.

Isandro smiled and swung the hammer again.

* * *

The letter had come on Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were when the mail boat came up from the delta, and the mail boat was how the Optimists had always found him. He'd watched it dock with the old clench in his stomach—the one that used to arrive ahead of every harbor, every checkpoint, every smiling boy paid two coppers to follow a stranger through a strange town. Old habits. He'd stood at the rail of the repair dock and watched the postmaster sort her bags, and he'd made himself stay there, made himself watch, because Sefa had told him once that fear you run from gets bigger and fear you stand next to eventually gets bored.

The envelope was cream-colored. Heavy stock. The Optimists' seal pressed into wax: an open hand holding a coin, which was either honest advertising or the most cynical joke in commerce. His name was written on it in the same copperplate hand as all the others, going back six years, back to the week he'd signed the first note to bury Tomas.

Tomas. His brother. Dead at twenty-six of a fever that three days of proper medicine would have broken, if Isandro had been home instead of chasing a storm cell two provinces south for salvage fees that turned out to be half what was promised. He'd come back to a grave he hadn't dug and a bill he couldn't pay, and the funeral house had introduced him, gently, sympathetically, to a lender whose interest compounded like floodwater—which was to say quietly, then suddenly, then all at once, carrying everything before it.

Six years. Sixty-one letters. He'd memorized the arithmetic of his own ruin the way other men memorized psalms. Principal four hundred crowns. Interest compounding quarterly. Penalties assessed for flight, for concealment, for the mere fact of being difficult to find. By the third year the debt had doubled. By the fifth it had become less a number than a weather system, a pressure front that followed him across three provinces, bending his whole life around it. Every job taken short. Every boat bought cheap and sold cheaper. Every town left before anyone learned his face well enough to miss it.

He'd burned sixty of those letters. The sixty-first he'd burned too, back in chapter and verse of his running days, standing on the deck of his boat watching the ash scatter on the Kilow Reach. Burning them had never changed anything. The debt wasn't paper. It was arithmetic, and arithmetic doesn't burn.

But this envelope—he turned it over in his hands, standing in the sun outside the postmaster's office while she pretended very hard not to be curious—this envelope was thin. One page.

He opened it right there on the street, which was itself a kind of miracle. A year ago he'd have carried it unopened for a day, letting dread ripen.

*To the debtor known as Isandro Yarrow,* it began, in that same copperplate. *Be advised that the claim of record against you, being principal, accrued interest, penalties, and recovery costs, has been purchased in full by a party acting on your behalf, and is hereby discharged. Your account is closed. No further action will be taken against you by this office or any office acting in concert with it.*

There was no signature. There was a line item at the bottom, a settlement figure, and beside it a notation: *paid in full, cash, witnessed.*

Isandro read it three times. Then he folded it along its original creases and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where he could feel it like a coal.

He knew who had paid it. Of course he did. There was exactly one person in the world with both the means and the reason, and she would deny it to his face with total sincerity, and she had already prepared the denial, because Sefa prepared everything. He could hear it already: *Salvage money. The chest went to the settlement, but the reward for delivery came back downriver, and I don't gamble, so I spend.* And maybe that was even true. Maybe part of it was true. The Vhalorian elders had insisted on paying something for the letters—not for the documents themselves, which they said belonged to everyone who remembered, but for the risk, and risk had a price even among people who'd chosen exile over comfort.

It didn't matter which version was true. What mattered was the sensation, standing there in the August heat with a dead account in his pocket: nothing was chasing him anymore.

He waited for the terror to come. It always came before—the vertigo of an open road with no one behind him on it, the suspicion that stillness was just the trap closing slowly. He'd felt it at the settlement, the first morning he woke without checking the window. He'd felt it his first week in Lowmark, reaching for the exit in every conversation, mapping the routes out of town the way other men mapped their way in.

This time, standing in the street with the gulls arguing over the fish market behind him, the terror came small. Came like a dog he'd fed too often: present, familiar, easily waved off.

He went back to the boathouse and worked until his shoulders burned, and that night he slept nine hours without dreaming of rivers at all.

* * *

Odile found him at lunch, sitting on the new skid beam with his boots dangling over the water, eating Brill's stew out of a tin bowl. Brill's cooking had gotten stranger since the fire—his hands were still bandaged to the fingers, so Odile and Tam took turns feeding the pots while he shouted instructions from a chair positioned, by his own demand, close enough to smell everything. Today's stew involved river eel, two kinds of pepper, and something Tam had foraged that nobody could name and everybody ate anyway. It was the best thing Isandro had ever tasted, and he suspected that had less to do with the eel than with the company.

"You got a letter," Odile said, dropping down beside him. She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light. She'd seen the postmaster's face. In a town this size, the postmaster's face was a newspaper.

"Account's closed," Isandro said. "Paid in full."

Odile was quiet for a moment. She was quiet the way clerks are quiet—doing sums. "The settlement money wouldn't have covered it," she said. "I've seen the figures. I copy everything, you know that."

"I know that."

"So either the elders paid more than they showed us, or—" She stopped. Looked at him sideways. "Or somebody added their own coin and will lie about it beautifully."

"Sounds like someone we know."

"It does." Odile pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them. Below them, Tam had given up on the skid beams and was skipping stones, each one ringing off the water in a clean series, five skips, six, seven. The boy had a gift for making things travel across surfaces that should swallow them. Isandro thought about mentioning this observation to him, then thought better of it. Some gifts are better left unnamed; naming them makes them heavy.

"Does it frighten you?" Odile asked. "Being free?"

Isandro considered lying, considered deflection, considered the thousand small evasions that had kept him alive for six years and alone for six years, and found he was tired of all of them. "Yes," he said. "Less than it used to."

"That's the most honest thing you've said since you docked here." She bumped her shoulder against his. "I'm putting it in my ledger."

"Don't you dare."

"Too late. Already inked." She grinned, and for a second she looked fifteen instead of nineteen, and Isandro understood that this was what family was—not blood, not obligation, but people who had seen your worst arithmetic and stayed to help you carry the remainder.

* * *

The levee roster hung inside the door of the town hall, and it had hung there, in one form or another, for longer than anyone living could remember. It was a sheet of oiled canvas, ruled into columns, and every household in Lowmark that drew benefit from the levee owed labor to it—days per season, assigned by lot, recorded in a hand that had changed eleven times since the founding. Sefa's grandmother's name was on it, faded almost to legend. Sefa's name was on it in three columns. Tam's mark was there too, a small drawn wave, because Tam had refused to sign with letters and the town had learned to accept his signature on its own terms.

Isandro had worked the levee all season. Everyone knew it. He'd hauled sandbags through the worst night of the monsoon with the rest of them, had held the line at the weir when it seemed the river would simply take the town and be done with it, had stood hip-deep in brown water passing planks hand to hand while the rain came down like judgment. His labor was in those walls. Anyone could see it.

But his name was not on the roster.

That was the difference, and Sefa understood the difference, which was why she'd said nothing for three weeks, and why she finally cornered him on the last day of August with the roster under her arm and an expression he'd learned to recognize as the beginning of a negotiation he was going to lose.

"You've been avoiding the town hall," she said.

"I've been busy."

"You've walked past it eleven times and crossed the street twice." She unrolled the canvas on the boathouse workbench and weighted the corners with hammers. "The autumn assignments post tomorrow. Storm season ends, repair season begins. New levee sections, the weir rebuild, the winter anchorage. It's the biggest roster in years."

Isandro looked at the empty column where names went. He knew what she was doing. She knew he knew. This was how negotiations went between them now—slow, transparent, almost ceremonial, two people circling a thing they both wanted and neither would say plainly, because saying it plainly would make it fragile.

"I'm not a resident," he said. "I don't hold property here. I don't—"

"You hold a berth in my boathouse that you're rebuilding with your own hands. You hold a seat at Brill's table that he'll fight you for if you try to give it back. You hold a standing invitation from the Vhalorian elders that you keep finding reasons not to use." She tapped the canvas. "You hold a debt to this town that you paid in sandbags and burns, and the town knows it, and the town doesn't care about your paperwork. Only you do."

"The Optimists care about paperwork."

"The Optimists," Sefa said, "are a rumor with a seal. Their enforcer is facing Vhalorian law in a valley that doesn't appear on maps. Their claim on you died in a flood with everything else." She said it without triumph, which was her gift. She delivered hard truths the way she laid keels—level, patient, built to last. "Nobody is coming, Isandro. Not for the chest, not for the money, not for you. The question isn't whether you're safe here. The question is whether you're brave enough to find out what you are when you're not running."

He looked at the roster a long time. The afternoon light came through the open doors and lay across the canvas in long gold bars, and he could see the ghost-names of the dead under the fresh ones, generations of people who had signed their labor to a wall of dirt and called it home.

"My brother used to say I had anchor disease," Isandro said finally. "That I was born wanting to drop hook somewhere and too proud to admit it. He said I'd run until I ran out of river."

"And?"

"And he was right. He usually was. It's why I buried him badly and spent six years paying for it." He picked up the pen Sefa had brought—she'd brought a pen, of course she had, she'd planned this down to the nib—and found his hand was steadier than he expected.

He wrote his name in the autumn column. *Yarrow, I. — levee section four, weir crew, boathouse reconstruction, ongoing.*

His name. Staying put.

Sefa looked at it for a moment, and something moved through her face that she would deny later with total sincerity, the same sincerity she'd bring to denying the letter. Then she rolled up the canvas, businesslike again, and said the weir crew started at dawn and he'd better not be late, and Isandro said he'd never been late in his life, which was a lie, and they both let it stand.

* * *

Evening came the way it did now, unhurried, without anything in it to flee. Brill held court from his chair while Odile read aloud—not the Vhalorian letters tonight, those were copied and bound and bound again, three copies in three safe places, because the elders had taught them the lesson of the whole affair: truth survives because people carry it, not paper. Tonight she read a delta novel, badly translated, full of pirates, and Brill heckled the plot, and Tam fell asleep against Isandro's shoulder somewhere in the second act, and nobody moved to wake him.

Later, Isandro carried the boy to his bunk and stood a while in the doorway of the room Tam shared with the boat tools and the model ships he carved from storm-wrecked timber. On the shelf above the bed, Tam had arranged his fleet: a brigantine, a clipper, a little gunboat with a crooked mast. And newest, still smelling of fresh-cut pine, a salvage boat with a shallow draft and a scarred hull, unmistakably, lovingly, his.

Isandro touched the tiny hull once, lightly, the way you touch a thing you're afraid of waking.

Then he went out onto the new deck of the boathouse, into the August dark, and sat with his feet hanging over the black water, and listened to the river go by on its way to nowhere that needed him.

Tomorrow: dawn, the weir, section four. A season of work with his name attached to it, in ink, in a town that had learned his face well enough to miss him.

For the first time in six years, Isandro Yarrow was exactly where he meant to be—and the only thing chasing him was morning.
