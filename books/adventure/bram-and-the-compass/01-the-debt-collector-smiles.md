## 1. The Debt Collector Smiles

The trouble with Mr. Quill was that he never stopped smiling, and the smile never changed. Astrid had watched it over breakfast in Penzance, over the counter of the chandlery where she'd tried to pawn her father's sextant, and now through the rain-streaked window of a bus shelter as he stood on the opposite pavement, hands folded, coat immaculate, waiting for her to make a decision she'd already made three times.

He didn't chase. That was the horror of him. He simply arrived wherever you were going to be, slightly ahead of schedule, and waited with the patience of a man being paid by the hour and enjoying every minute.

She slipped out the back of the shelter into an alley that smelled of chip fat and brine, took the stairs down to the harbor two at a time, and cut along the sea wall with her collar up. Behind her, unhurried, came the sound of polished shoes on wet stone. Not running. Never running. Just *there*, like weather.

"Miss Montrose." His voice carried across the water without effort. "A moment, when you have one."

"I don't," she said, and meant it in every sense.

"The Syndicate is nothing if not flexible. We can discuss terms at your convenience." A pause, timed like a metronome. "Though I should mention your convenience appears to be expiring."

Fourteen thousand pounds. That was what her brother's funeral had cost, plus the boat she'd borrowed to scatter his ashes and wrecked doing it, plus interest calculated at a rate Astrid suspected had been invented specifically for people like her—people who owed money to an organization cheerful enough to call itself a family. The Vane Syndicate. Everyone in Cornwall knew the name. Nobody said it above the volume of a murmur.

She ducked into the fish market, through the hanging rows of haddock and the men who pretended not to see her, because everyone in Penzance knew whose debt collector smiled like that, and out the far side onto the quay where the storm-chasing crews were loading.

It wasn't glamorous work. It wasn't even legal work, some weeks. The contracts paid cash, up front, half on signature, and they always read the same: *driver wanted, vehicle provided, sign nothing you can't outrun.* The chasers went ahead of the storms along the coast road, measuring swell, photographing wave faces for insurers and lunatics and occasionally the navy, and when the storm caught up—and it always caught up—you either drove faster than the water or you learned something about yourself that couldn't be unlearned.

Astrid signed the first contract she found. The man holding it had a beard full of salt and a handshake like a mooring line snapping taut.

"Cove section, west of Porthgwarra," he said. "Big low coming off the Atlantic. Freak spring tide—they're saying it'll drain places nobody's seen since the war. You know that coast?"

"No," said Astrid, which was true, and also the only qualification that mattered: she didn't know anywhere well enough for it to hold her.

* * *

They drove west through rain that came sideways, wipers losing their argument with the sky, and Astrid sat in the passenger seat counting the money in her head. Half up front: seven hundred pounds. It wouldn't even dent fourteen thousand. But it was time. Time was all a debt really bought you—the hours between payments, the miles between smiles.

That was the exhaustion nobody warned you about. Not the fear. Fear was fine; fear was fuel. It was the *moving*. Three years since Tomas drowned, three years of towns that blurred together, harbors that all smelled the same, landlords who all asked the same questions. She could sleep in a moving car better than in a bed. She'd stopped unpacking anything but her toothbrush. Somewhere around year two she'd realized she hadn't looked at a horizon without calculating how fast she could reach it.

"You're quiet," said the driver, whose name was Fen and who drove the way other people prayed.

"I'm cheap," said Astrid. "Quiet comes free."

Fen laughed. "You'll want to see this tide, then. They say it's pulling back further than anyone's got records for. Sea's going to show us its cellar."

The coast road wound down toward a cove Astrid had never seen accessible—a slot of gray water between black headlands, marked on no map she'd trusted. And when they crested the last hill and the cove opened below them, she understood what Fen had meant, and then she understood something else entirely, something that made her forget Quill, forget the money, forget the three years of running.

The sea was gone.

Not gone—*withdrawn*, pulled back like a held breath, so far out that the seabed lay naked and gleaming under a bruised light: kelp forests flattened like combed hair, boulders furred with barnacle, pools stranded and trembling. And there, at the back of the cove, where the cliffs folded together into darkness, a cave mouth stood open to the air for the first time in living memory.

It was carved. That was the thing that stopped her heart. The rock around the entrance had been worked by human hands—glyphs cut deep and old, spirals and open palms and figures with arms raised, and beneath them, worn nearly smooth, a single repeated mark that might have been a warning or a welcome or both:

*A hand reaching. A hand letting go.*

"Nobody goes in there," Fen said, but he said it the way people say things they've heard rather than things they believe. "Fishermen's stories. Wreck lies inside. Old one. Peregrine, or some such."

"Peregrine," Astrid repeated. The word tasted like a key turning.

She was out of the car before she'd decided to be. Her boots hit the exposed seabed and the smell rose up around her—iodine and rot and cold stone, the ocean's private rooms opened to daylight. Behind her Fen called something about the tide, about six hours, about how the water would come back hungry. She waved without turning around.

This was the flicker, though she didn't name it yet. For three years every choice had been made by fear—fear of Quill, fear of the ledger, fear of standing still long enough for her life to catch up with her. But standing on the seabed with the cave mouth breathing cold air at her, Astrid felt something she hadn't felt since before Tomas died, and it took her a moment to recognize it because it had been so long.

Curiosity. Stronger than the fear. Pulling harder than the debt.

The glyphs got stranger near the entrance. Deeper cuts, steadier hands. One showed a compass rose with the north point broken off. Another showed a face with the eyes scraped away. And at the center of the arch, at exactly the height of a person's heart, someone had carved a sentence in letters small enough that you had to stop and lean close, and Astrid did, rain dripping off her chin onto stone older than any language she knew:

*IT SHOWS YOU WHAT YOU CANNOT AFFORD TO LOSE. DECIDE IF YOU CAN AFFORD TO SEE IT.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, too loud against the empty cove. "Everyone's a salesman," she told the cave, and stepped inside.

The dark swallowed her whole, and somewhere behind her, far away in Penzance, a phone was ringing in a neat office, and a man with an unchanging smile was writing down coordinates he'd been given by someone who should not have known them, and the tide, patient as a creditor, began its long return.
