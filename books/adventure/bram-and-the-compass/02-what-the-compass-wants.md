## 2. What the Compass Wants

The cave breathed.

That was Astrid's first thought, three steps in with her headlamp cutting a cone through the dark: the place was breathing. Air moved against her face in slow pulls and pushes, the lungs of some enormous sleeping thing, and every exhale smelled of salt and old iron and something else—something sweeter, like the inside of a locket worn against skin for a hundred years.

Her boots found stone worn smooth as river glass. The glyphs from the entrance repeated here on the walls, but closer now, legible in the lamplight: spirals within spirals, and that same phrase she'd photographed at the threshold, though down here it had been carved deeper, by hands that had wanted it to last.

*IT SHOWS YOU WHAT YOU CANNOT AFFORD TO LOSE.*

"Great sales pitch," she muttered, because talking kept the breathing-cave from feeling like breathing-*at*-her. Her voice came back flattened, swallowed. The acoustics were wrong for a cave this size—or right for one much bigger than it looked.

She followed the slope down. The floor tilted toward the sea the way a throat tilts toward a stomach, and after two hundred meters the walls opened out and her headlamp simply gave up, the beam dissolving into a black so complete it felt structural, load-bearing.

But there was light below. Faint, green-gold, pulsing at the rhythm of the tide.

Astrid stood at the lip of the Peregrine's hold.

The ship lay keeled over in a flooded basin like a whale that had come here to die, its hull split along the starboard side, timbers furred with white anemones. And through that split, rising from the cargo deck in slow columns, drifted the light: hundreds of small glass vessels, jar-shaped, stoppered with wax and brass, each one glowing with its own private dawn. They hung in the water on lines of netting, an orchard of lanterns, and the green-gold pulse of them moved across the cavern ceiling like weather.

Memory. She knew it before she had a word for it. You didn't salvage *cargo* off a wreck like this; you salvaged what people couldn't bear to carry home. The Syndicate's ledgers called it "recovered sentiment." Quill's smile flickered through her mind, cheerful and bottomless. This was what he'd have sold by the gram.

And at the center of the hold, on a pedestal of black coral that no wave should have left standing, sat the compass.

It was smaller than she'd imagined from the glyphs' reverence. Brass, palm-sized, its lid open, the needle swinging lazily—not north, not anywhere, just circling, like a dog looking for a door. It glowed faintly, the only thing in the cavern that wasn't green-gold.

Astrid told herself she was going to look. Photograph it, document it, get out before the tide turned. That was the contract: storm data, not treasure. She was a professional. She was also eleven thousand pounds in debt to people who smiled while they ruined you, and her hand was already reaching.

The brass was warm. Warmer than the water, warmer than the air. Like holding a bird.

The needle stopped circling.

It swung once, hard, and pointed—at her.

Then the world folded.

* * *

Rain. A lake in the Lake District, gray under a sky the color of dishwater, and Tomas laughing at the far end of the jetty with his arms out like he could balance on anything, like gravity was a rumor he'd chosen not to believe.

*Astrid.* His voice came from everywhere, from inside her own skull. *You remember this.*

"I don't," she said, and the rain took her words, and she was seventeen again whether she consented or not, standing on the stones in her brother's too-big jacket, watching him walk backward off the end of the jetty into water that was colder than any of them had planned for.

The memory did not blur. That was the horror of it. Every detail arrived at full resolution: the way his hair went flat when it got wet, the exact pitch of his shout—*race you to the buoy*—the sound the lake made when it closed over him, soft as a book shutting.

She watched herself dive. Watched her own arms cut the water. Watched the green dark swallow them both, and felt again—the compass made sure she felt it, pressed it into her chest like a brand—the moment her lungs began to burn and her fingers found his sleeve and her body, her stupid brilliant animal body, made the arithmetic without asking her: *one of us can make it up.*

He'd gone under first. He'd been heavier, tangled in weed, already past panic into that terrible calm. And she had kicked for the light. She had kicked for the light and her last sight of him was his face turning toward her through the murk, not angry, which would have been survivable, but *understanding*, which was not.

*Astrid.* The rain again, the jetty, the gray. The memory held her there, made her stand in it, made her watch the ripples spread and flatten until the lake forgot him entirely. *You never told anyone you heard him say your name. You never told anyone you hesitated. You've been running from this since you were seventeen, and you call it adventure.*

"Stop," she said. "Stop it."

*This is what I show,* said the compass, and its voice was the voice of the needle, small and precise and pitiless. *Not north. Not profit. I point at the thing you refuse to carry, and I feed on what you refuse to remember. Everyone who holds me pays. The question is only the currency.*

The lake dissolved. She was on her knees on wet stone in the dark, the compass still gripped in her fist, her face soaked—and she couldn't tell if she was crying or if the cave was. The green-gold jars pulsed around her, patient, and she understood suddenly what they were: other people's refusals. Other people's Tomas moments, stoppered and shelved and glowing softly forever, because the sea keeps what we throw away.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. She nearly screamed.

Signal, down here—one bar, ghosting through the cave mouth. A message. No sender she recognized, just coordinates and a time and a single line:

*We know what you found. Bring it topside and your debt dies tonight. — Q*

Below her, the tide was coming back. She could hear it now, filling the lower galleries, patient as a creditor.

Above her, on the gray surface of the sea, lights were appearing where no lights should be.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Rehn stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Answer* and watched the cove go gold-green beneath the waterline, like a lamp lit under a tablecloth.

"Quill says the girl's already inside," said her first mate, Okafor, at her elbow. He said the name the way sailors said *storm*. "Syndicate's given us till the sixth tide. After that they bring their own divers."

Rehn didn't answer. She was looking at the glow with an expression her crew had learned not to name. Twenty years ago she'd stood on a different deck and watched a different glow sink below different water, taking nine people whose names she still said aloud on the anniversary, alone, at night, like a woman keeping a grave weeded.

The compass had been theirs. Built by her navigator, Marta, who'd claimed it pointed at what you feared losing, and Rehn had laughed then, and then the *Peregrine* went down with all hands and the laugh had died somewhere in her chest and never fully restarted.

"Marta built it to find her son," Rehn said finally. "He drowned. She wanted to know where."

Okafor waited. He was good at waiting.

"And instead it found everyone's drowning." Rehn turned from the rail. "Wake the dredgers. All of them. If the Syndicate wants to sell other people's grief by the gram, they'll do it over my dead hull."

"And the girl?"

Rehn looked back at the cove, at the small light moving in the cave mouth like a firefly in a bottle.

"The girl's carrying something," she said. "I can see it from here. Bring her aboard gently. Grief makes people grabby, and I need what she's holding more than I need my own hands."

In the dark below, Astrid climbed toward the retreating tide with the compass burning in her fist like a small accusing heart, and did not yet know that the lights above belonged to someone who had lost everything too—and that in this story, the rescuers kept price lists.
