## 3. Six Tides

The first tide came in grey and patient, and Astrid spent it learning the compass.

She'd hauled it back to her rented room above the chandlery in Mousehole, and all night it sat on the table between a cold cup of tea and an unpaid electricity bill, its needle swinging slow arcs that had nothing to do with north. When she faced the window, the needle pointed out to sea. When she turned toward the door, toward Penzance, toward Quill, it swung too—then shuddered, as if disgusted, and dragged itself back to the water.

Toward Tomas. Always, underneath everything, toward Tomas.

She didn't sleep. She sat with her hands flat on the table like a woman at a séance and watched the needle do its slow, grieving arithmetic. Once, around three in the morning, she reached for it and stopped with her fingers a centimeter from the brass. She remembered what it felt like—the cold climbing her wrist, the water rising in her throat, her brother's hand slipping through hers—and she pulled back.

*It feeds on what you refuse to remember.* She'd written that in her notebook, underlined twice, the way she used to underline things when she still believed writing them down made them manageable.

The knock came at seven. Three polite raps, spaced like punctuation.

"Astrid." Mr. Quill's voice through the door, warm as butter on toast. "I know you're awake. The gulls told me."

She opened the door because there was no version of this where not opening it helped. Quill stood in the narrow stairwell in his immaculate raincoat, hands clasped behind his back, smiling the smile of a man who has never once raised his voice because he's never needed to. Behind him, at the bottom of the stairs, two of his colleagues examined the chandlery's window display with the idle interest of tourists.

"Mr. Quill."

"You've been busy." He tilted his head past her, into the room, though he didn't step forward. He never stepped forward. That was the horror of him; the world simply arranged itself around his patience. "A cave. A wreck. And something brass that's made three separate salvage crews reposition their boats overnight. You do attract weather, Astrid. In every sense."

"I don't have it."

"Of course you don't." He said it the way you'd humor a child claiming not to have eaten the cake. "Here's my thinking. Your debt stands at eleven thousand four hundred pounds, plus interest accruing at a rate I'd describe as *enthusiastic*. The Syndicate is prepared to consider the item we both know is sitting on your table as payment in full. Clean slate. Fresh start. You could stop running. Imagine that. Imagine waking up and the day being yours."

For one treacherous second, she did imagine it. Waking without the ledger in her chest. Walking into Penzance and buying coffee like a person whose name wasn't attached to a number.

"What does the Syndicate want with it?" she asked. "It's a compass. It doesn't point anywhere useful."

Quill's smile deepened by a single degree. "It points at what people can't bear to lose. Do you understand what that is, Astrid? It's a map of the human heart's real estate. Every person who holds it shows us where they keep their valuables." He finally leaned in, just slightly, and dropped his voice to a confider's hush. "And the wreck—the Peregrine's hold is full of them. Salvaged memories, pressed into glass like preserves. Griefs. Loves. Last words. There are collectors who will pay fortunes for a dead man's final thought of his wife. There are lonely people who will pay more to feel someone else's love than they ever paid to find their own. We're not debt collectors, Astrid. We're about to be *merchants of the interior*."

The room seemed to tilt. She thought of the hold, the rows of sealed jars glinting in her torchlight, which she'd taken for ballast or rum. Little ships inside bottles. Little lives inside glass.

"You're going to sell people's last moments."

"We're going to *liberate* them. They were drowning down there. Nobody was using them." Quill straightened his cuffs. "You have until the third tide turns. That's tomorrow evening, roughly six-forty. Bring the compass to the harbourmaster's office, and we'll call it even. Or don't, and we'll discuss alternative arrangements, which I promise will involve vocabulary neither of us enjoys." He touched two fingers to his hat brim. "Sleep well. You look tired."

He descended the stairs. The gulls, as promised, said nothing.

* * *

Captain Ilsa Rehn stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Answer* and watched the tide come for the cove like it owed her money.

Forty years on the water and she'd never seen a spring tide pull back so far, so fast—as if the sea were clearing its throat to show them something. Her dredgers worked the shallows below in yellow slickers, and beyond them the cave mouth yawned, glyphs carved around its lip in spirals that her grandmother would have called old religion and her grandfather would have called good sense: *turn back.*

She hadn't turned back in thirty years. Not since the Peregrine went down with half her family aboard.

"Compass is ashore," said Tam, her first mate, coming up beside her. "Girl's got it. Saw the light moving in the chandlery window all night. Needle-shaped shadow on the curtain."

"The Syndicate?"

"Quill's car passed through the village an hour ago. Smiling at everyone. Which means someone's about to be robbed politely."

Rehn grunted. Below, the water slid over the cave mouth, sealing it until the next drawdown. Six tides, the glyphs said—or so the fisherman's boy had translated, badly, for a fiver. Six tides before the spring tide failed and the cove kept its secret for another hundred years.

Three tides left. And a girl ashore holding the only key, with Quill's polite noose already around her neck.

Rehn had held that compass once. Had gripped it on the deck of her father's boat while the Peregrine's distress flares burned orange on the horizon, and the needle hadn't pointed at the flares—it had pointed at her brother Joren, somewhere under the black water, and she'd understood in one sick instant that it wasn't pointing at where he was. It was pointing at where *she* was most lost. She'd thrown it overboard rather than follow it down.

The sea had given it back. The sea always gave things back eventually, changed.

"Ready the skiff," she told Tam. "I'm going ashore alone."

"And if the girl runs?"

Rehn watched the water close over the cave, smooth as a lid on a jar. "Then I'll do what I should've done thirty years ago," she said. "Follow the needle down."

* * *

Rehn found her at the cove at slack water, sitting on a rock with her boots off and the compass in her lap like something between a pet and a bomb.

"You look terrible," Rehn said.

"You look like a debt collector with better boots."

"I look like a woman who buried nine crew and has spent three decades dredging their grave for spare change." Rehn lowered herself onto the rock beside her, uninvited, unhurried. "Quill offered you clean slate for the brass. Don't take it."

"Why not? Because you want it instead?"

"Because I know what the Syndicate does with cargo like that." Rehn's voice went flat and hard, slate over slate. "They don't sell memories whole. They strip them. Cut the grief out of a widow's memory and sell the grief to a collector, sell the love to someone lonelier, and the widow gets back a hole shaped like her husband and a receipt. I've seen their warehouses in Rotterdam. Rows of jars. People walking around missing pieces and calling it peace." She turned, and her eyes were the exact colour of the water over the wreck. "That compass belonged to my crew. My brother held it last. Everything in that hold is mine to bring home, and I'll pay you for the key—real coin, or partnership, your pick. But I won't let Quill harvest it."

Astrid looked at the compass. The needle lay still now, pointing at nothing visible, which meant it was pointing at the rock beneath them, at the drowned dark, at Tomas.

"Everyone who comes to rescue me has a price list," she said quietly.

"Yes," Rehn said. No apology in it. "Mine's just shorter than most."

Astrid almost laughed. Almost believed her. And then, over Rehn's shoulder, she saw it: Tam, up on the headland path, speaking into a phone, and further along the cliff road, parked with criminal neatness, a grey car she'd know anywhere. Quill hadn't come for the meeting. Quill had come to watch Rehn make the offer—because either answer was an answer. Either way, the Syndicate now knew exactly where the compass was, and exactly who wanted it, and exactly how much each of them was willing to pay.

There was no deal. There had never been a deal. There was only bait, and she was holding it.

"Partnership," Astrid said, standing, shaking sand from her feet, smiling at Rehn with everything she had. "Let me think on it till the third tide. Meet me at the harbourmaster's, six-forty."

Rehn studied her a moment too long—but grief makes everyone a bad reader, and she nodded once and walked up the path, past Tam, past the grey car, pretending not to see any of it.

Astrid waited until the footsteps faded. Then she walked, calm as Sunday, to the slipway where the fishermen moored their skiffs, chose the fastest one, and put the compass in her pocket, where it pressed against her hip like a small cold heart.

Behind her, the sea began its third withdrawal, dragging the water off the rocks with a sound like an indrawn breath.

Six tides. Three gone. She had until the water finished exhaling—and then the cove would open, and everyone who wanted her would know exactly where to point their needles.

She pushed the skiff off the slip and rowed for the cave mouth, into the space between every price ever put on her head.
