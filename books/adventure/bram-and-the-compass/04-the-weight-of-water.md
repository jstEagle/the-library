## 4. The Weight of Water

The storm came in off the Atlantic like something with a grudge, and Astrid stood on the Peregrine's tilted deck and let it hit her full in the face.

Below her feet, the wreck groaned against the reef. The sixth tide was rising. In an hour the sea would close over the hold for good—or over her, if she stayed. Quill's skiff bobbed at the cave mouth behind her, lantern swinging, and beyond it Rehn's dredger sat black against the lightning, its crane arm raised like a gallows waiting to be used.

"Miss Montrose." Quill's voice carried through the rain, warm as ever, polite as a knife laid on clean linen. "You've had six tides. That's more generosity than the Syndicate usually extends. The compass, please. And then we'll discuss the cargo."

"The cargo is memories," Astrid said. "People's lives. You can't put those in a crate and sell them."

"We can and we do," Quill said pleasantly. "Grief is the most valuable commodity on the coast. Everyone wants someone else's past. Nobody wants their own."

The compass burned cold in her fist. She'd held it for five tides now, and every hour it grew heavier—not the brass, but what it carried. Tomas's face, surfacing and sinking. Her own hands pushing off the capsized dinghy while he called her name. Rehn's crew, four men and a boy, going down in water the color of slate while she watched from a lifeboat because the sea took them and not her.

The compass didn't point north. It pointed at everything she refused to carry.

And she was so tired of running from it.

"I'm going to open it," she said.

Quill's smile flickered—the first crack in all that courtesy. "That would be unwise."

"You mean unprofitable."

She knelt at the rail where the storm could reach her, and she stopped fighting the compass, and she let it show her everything.

* * *

Tomas went under at 4:17 in the afternoon on a Tuesday in June, and the memory gave her every second.

The dinghy flipped. Cold shock. His hand finding hers in the churn—his grip stronger than hers, always stronger, he'd been the swimmer—and then his eyes above the waterline, wide and certain, saying what he never said aloud: *you first, you live, get out.*

And she had. She'd kicked for the light. She'd felt his fingers slide from hers and she had kept kicking, because her body wanted air more than it wanted him, and that was the truth she'd been drowning herself in ever since: not that she'd killed him, but that she'd chosen herself, instantly, without hesitation, the way living things do.

The compass held the moment without mercy and without judgment. It simply showed her. Here is your brother dying. Here is you surviving. Both things are true. Neither one cancels the other.

Astrid wept into the storm, and the storm didn't care, and somehow that helped.

Then the compass turned, and showed her Rehn.

* * *

Ilsa Rehn on the deck of the Peregrine, eleven years younger, hair plastered to her skull, screaming names into the wind. Four names. Then a fifth, smaller—*Pietro, Pietro, where are you*—and the boy's cap floating past her in the dark, and Rehn reaching for it and catching nothing but seawater.

Rehn who had ordered the run through the storm because the cargo was worth more than the risk. Rehn who had spent eleven years buying salvage contracts along this coast, dredging coves, chasing glyphs and rumors, not for profit—for penance. The compass had been hers. She'd thrown it into the hold when the ship went down, because she couldn't bear what it pointed at.

It pointed here. It had always pointed here.

Astrid surfaced from the memory gasping, salt on her lips that wasn't all rain. On the dredger's deck, a lantern moved—Rehn herself, come down in a launch, standing in the bow with a boathook like a harpoonist from some older century.

"Astrid!" Her voice tore across the water. "Don't give it to him!"

Quill had boarded. Of course he had; the man climbed wet rock in a storm as cheerfully as he delivered foreclosure notices. He stood ten feet away now, rain beading on his immaculate coat, one hand extended.

"Last chance," he said. "The debt dies with the delivery. Or you drown with it, and we take both anyway. The Syndicate always collects."

Astrid looked at the compass. At the sea boiling over the reef. At Rehn, who had lost everyone and kept sailing anyway, because grief isn't a thing you outrun—it's ballast. You carry it or you capsize.

Survival wasn't fleeing. Survival was choosing what to hold.

"No," Astrid said.

And she raised the compass high over the rail, over the black water, and brought it down against the iron.

* * *

The brass cracked like a bell.

Light poured out—not fire, not electricity, but something older: every memory the Peregrine had swallowed, every stolen past in the flooded hold, rising out of the wreck in ribbons of silver and streaming seaward like fish returning to deep water. Astrid felt them pass through her, hundreds of lives, and none of them stayed. They belonged to the sea now. Let the sea keep them.

The last ribbon was small and bright and it paused beside her, shaped almost like a boy, and she didn't flinch and she didn't reach for it.

"Bye, Tom," she said.

It went out with the tide.

Quill made a sound she'd never heard from him before—a small, involuntary noise, like a man watching money burn. He pulled out a ledger, thumbed it, and his shoulders dropped half an inch. "The claim was tied to the cargo," he said, almost to himself. "Cargo's gone. Debt's—" He closed the book. For one naked moment he looked almost human. "Well. The Syndicate thanks you for your business, Miss Montrose."

He tipped his hat into the rain and climbed back down toward his skiff, and Astrid laughed—a cracked, ugly, wonderful sound—because she had just paid off a fortune in memories and had never felt richer.

Rehn's launch bumped against the hull. The captain hauled herself up the ladder, old and soaked and steady, and stood looking at the empty place where the compass had been.

"Eleven years I looked for that," Rehn said.

"I know."

"It pointed at my crew. Every day. Did it point at yours?"

"My brother."

Rehn nodded slowly. She looked out at the water closing over the reef, and something in her face loosened—grief set down, just for a moment, like a heavy pack rested against a wall.

"Come aboard," she said. "There's tea on the dredger. Bad tea. You look like someone who needs bad tea."

They sat in the wheelhouse while the storm wore itself out, two women who had both survived things they shouldn't have, drinking tea that tasted of rust, saying nothing that needed saying.

At dawn the sea was flat and pale and ordinary. Rehn offered her a berth, a wage, a place in a crew that dredged nothing but honest salvage. Astrid thought about it for exactly as long as it took to finish the tea.

"I've got a boat," she said. "Small. Ugly. Mine."

Rehn almost smiled. "Where will you go?"

Astrid stepped out onto the deck and breathed the clean cold air, carrying everything—Tomas, the choice, the weight of it all—and found she could stand up straight beneath it.

"North," she said. "For once, actual north."

She cast off before the sun cleared the headland, lighter than she'd been in years, poorer than she'd been in her life, and free.
