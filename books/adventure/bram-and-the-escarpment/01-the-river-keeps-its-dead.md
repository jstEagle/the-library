## 1. The River Keeps Its Dead

The river gave up its dead slowly, and only on its own terms.

Elodie Ravensworth had learned that the way she learned everything—by staying under too long. The Kessarin moved like a living thing at this hour, before dawn, before the bargemen lit their cook-fires, before anyone could stand on the bank and ask what a woman was doing alone in black water with a hook and a rope. It breathed. She could feel it breathing against her chest as she hung ten fathoms down in the cold green dark, her lamp throwing a cone of amber through water thick with silt and old secrets.

Below her, half-buried in the riverbed's soft clay, lay the *Merrow's Daughter*, a grain barge that had gone down eleven years ago with a hold full of wheat and three men nobody had ever bothered to name. The insurance had been paid. The paperwork had been filed. As far as the world was concerned, the barge had ceased to exist, and so, in every way that mattered, had the men.

That was why she was here. Not for the wheat—wheat rotted into something like wet candle wax after a decade, and it stank of it, a sweetish corpse-smell that clung to skin for days. She was here because the *Merrow's Daughter* had carried an iron deck winch, brass fittings, a ship's bell of decent bronze. Salvage nobody claimed. A wreck nobody mourned. Exactly the kind of work that let a woman stay a week in a place and leave no more mark than the current did.

Her rule was simple, and she kept it the way other people kept faith: never stay anywhere long enough to be found.

Seven days was the outside limit. Six if the town had a garrison or a harbor office. Five if anyone asked her surname twice. She paid in coin, always coin, always counted out flat on tavern tables so the innkeeper could see there was no more where it came from. She slept in rooms with two exits when she could get them, one when she couldn't, and never—not once in four years—had she given anyone a true direction of departure. If someone asked whether she was headed upriver, she'd say down. If they asked about downriver, she'd mention the coast road. Lies of geography came easier to her now than truths. That was probably a thing worth grieving, but grief was another thing she'd learned to leave underwater, where it couldn't follow her up the ladder.

She worked the winch bolts by feel, mostly. Her gloves were seal-hide, patched at every finger, and the cold came through them anyway after twenty minutes until her hands went from ache to numbness to something beyond either—a strange, bloodless competence, fingers moving like they belonged to someone else. The bolt wrench fit. She braced her boots against the barge's hull, felt the iron shudder through the soles of her feet like a word trying to be spoken, and turned.

The first bolt came free with a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard. Down here, sound was a thing you absorbed through your bones. The second fought her. Rust had welded it to its seat, and she hung there in the dark, hauling against her own leverage, breath rasping through the regulator's worn valve in a rhythm she knew better than her own heartbeat—in, two-three-four, out, two-three-four—and thought, not for the first time, that this was the only conversation she had anymore that was entirely honest. Her lungs asked. The river answered. There was nothing to negotiate.

* * *

She'd been diving since she was nine years old, which meant she'd been diving longer than she'd been afraid of anything else, including the navy, including men, including the particular silence of a letter that never comes.

Tomas had taught her. Their father had been a mudlark on the lower Kessarin before the war took his lungs and then the rest of him, and Tomas, seven years older, had put the mask on Elodie's face himself, in the shallows off Corry Point, and told her: *The water can't hurt you if you don't fight it. Everything drowns that fights.* He'd held the back of her head in one big hand and she'd believed him absolutely, the way you believe anything at nine years old when the person saying it is the whole architecture of your world.

He'd joined the navy at nineteen because there was nothing left in Corry Point but rust and rumor, and because he still believed, in the uncomplicated way of boys who have never been lied to by institutions, that a uniform was a kind of promise. He'd written home twice a month. Then once a month. Then a letter had come that wasn't like the others—short, the handwriting cramped and slanting, as though he'd written it fast and hidden it fast, and all it said was:

*El. If anything happens to me, don't sign anything. Don't take their money. Ask questions until someone makes you stop asking.*

Then nothing. Eleven months of nothing. Then a naval clerk with a face like a closed ledger had handed her a folded paper and a small purse of coin and used the phrase *lost in the course of duty*, and when she'd asked what course, exactly, what duty, he'd looked at her with something that might have been pity or might have been warning, and said the file was sealed, and that the family was advised—*advised*, as though it were weather—to accept the settlement.

She hadn't signed. She hadn't taken the money. She had asked questions until someone made her stop asking, and the making-stop had involved two men in civilian coats standing at the end of her street for three nights running, and after that she'd understood the shape of her life going forward, which was long and narrow and always moving.

Four years now. Four years of wrecks nobody claimed, in rivers nobody patrolled, in towns where she was *the diver woman* and nothing more. She was good at it. She was, if she was honest—which she permitted herself only underwater, where honesty cost nothing—the best salvage diver working the inland waters, and she had made herself that way on purpose, because excellence was armor too. Nobody questioned the best. They just paid her, and watched her load her gear onto whatever leaky boat she'd bought that season, and forgot her face by the next market day.

That was the arrangement. That was the bargain she'd struck with the world: I will be useful and invisible, and in exchange I will not be found.

* * *

The third bolt sheared.

It happened the way these things happen—one moment resistance, the next a bright cracking report transmitted straight up her arm, and then she was falling, tumbling backward off the hull into the open water column, the wrench spinning away below her into depths the lamp couldn't reach, and the line she'd anchored to the barge rail above paying out through her harness with a shriek of friction.

She didn't panic. Panic was a luxury; she'd priced it out of her life years ago. But the line kept running, faster than it should, and when she grabbed it the weave burned through her glove and she understood, with the cold clarity that comes only in the last moments of things, that the anchor point above had shifted—that the barge's rail, rotten through with eleven years of rust, had given way, and that the line was now attached to nothing, and that she was sinking.

The silt cloud swallowed her lamp's beam. Black, then. Total black, the kind that has weight, that presses on the eyes like thumbs. She was falling through it with eighty pounds of gear and a lungful of air that wanted very badly to become the river's property.

*Tomas,* she thought, absurdly, uselessly, the way you think of a door in a house that burned down long ago. *You told me. Everything drowns that fights.*

So she didn't fight. She went loose. She dumped nothing, kicked nothing, let herself fall the last few fathoms in a slow corkscrew while her hands, moving with the calm of long rehearsal, found the knife at her belt, found the harness buckle, cut herself free of the dead line even as her boots touched bottom and sank ankle-deep into clay that gripped like a hand.

Which left the ascent. Eighty pounds of lead and iron, no line, no light worth the name, and maybe—she did the arithmetic the way drowning people do, fast and without mercy—maybe four minutes of air in the bottle, five if she cheated, and cheating meant the blood-bends later, meant waking at midnight feeling like her joints were full of broken glass. She'd cheated before. She'd pay again.

She dropped the weight belt. The river took it gladly, and the sudden lightness nearly lifted her off her feet, and she began to kick, up into the black, counting her breaths, rationing them, in-two-three-out-two-three, and somewhere around forty fathoms' worth of climbing she began to see the faint gray suggestion of surface overhead, and somewhere around the point where her vision started to tunnel and sparkle at the edges she broke through into air and rain and the enormous indifferent gray of dawn coming up over the Kessarin.

She hung on the float line for a long time, just breathing. Just letting the river breathe around her.

A man watching from the bank would have seen only a head in the water, dark hair slicked back, and might have thought: drowned girl, or otter, or trick of the morning mist. Nobody was watching. There was never anybody watching. That was the whole design of her life, and she had built it plank by plank with her own hands, and floating there with her heart slamming and her lungs full of honest air, Elodie Ravensworth admitted to the river what she admitted to no one else: that some mornings, surfacing alone like this, the first fully formed thought in her head was *I almost died and there is no one who would have known to look for me.* And the second thought, arriving right behind it like a shadow: *Tomas didn't even get that. No one looked for him at all.*

* * *

By midmorning she had the winch on deck, lashed down under oilcloth, and the *Magpie*—her current boat, a broad-beamed salvage punt she'd won at cards in Harlow's Bend and would abandon within the month, per the rule—was riding low but steady. She sat on the gunwale eating hard bread and salt fish and watching the channel traffic go by: coal barges, a navy patrol boat far off downstream, its gray hull catching the light like a blade laid on gray cloth.

She looked away from the patrol boat. She always looked away from the patrol boats. Looking invited noticing; noticing invited questions; questions were how people got found.

The near-drowning had left her hands unsteady, which annoyed her. She wrapped them in dry cloth, flexed the fingers until the trembling stopped, and tried to think about nothing at all, which was her other discipline, harder than diving. Nothing at all lasted about ninety seconds before Tomas arrived in it, as he always did, wearing the face he'd had at nineteen, standing on the Corry Point dock with his kit bag over his shoulder, saying, *Don't look so grim, El. I'll write every week.*

And he had. Until he hadn't. And the letter—the last letter, the strange one—she still had it. Eleven years, four boats, thirty towns, and she still carried it folded in oilskin inside her chest of tools, because she could not make herself throw it away and could not make herself stop reading it, and between those two impossibilities she had built a whole life, the way a river builds a delta: not by deciding anything, but by refusing, year after year, to stop moving.

*Ask questions until someone makes you stop asking.*

She had stopped asking. That was the truth she floated on, the way wreckage floats—barely, and only because it was hollow. She told herself it was prudence. She told herself the war was over and the files were sealed and the men in civilian coats had better things to do now, and that a woman alone survived by being small. All true. None of it the real reason. The real reason was simpler and worse: she was afraid of what the answers would cost. She had seen what asking had cost Tomas. Whatever he had found in his neat naval records, in his faithful monthly letters turning desperate—it had been heavy enough to pull him under, and she was not certain, even now, even armored, even excellent, that she was strong enough to carry the same weight and keep swimming.

So she dove for dead men's brass instead. She raised winches and bells and fittings from ships whose names meant nothing, and sold them to dealers who didn't ask, and moved on before the moving-on hurt, and called it a living.

The afternoon turned gold and then copper, and the wind swung around out of the southeast, which the old bargemen said meant rain inside three days, real rain, monsoon-edge weather pushing up from the coast. She noted it the way she noted everything—tide tables, patrol schedules, the moods of harbormasters—with a salvager's practical attention. Storms were bad for diving but good for business; storms buried wrecks and uncovered others, shifting the river's holdings like a dishonest banker.

She was coiling line when the dredge hook snagged.

Not the way hooks snagged, either—not the grab-and-tear of sunken timber, not the slither of cable over cable. This was different. The *Magpie* lurched, swung her stern halfway round, and the cable came taut with a deep, groaning reluctance, like something on the riverbed had been disturbed from a sleep it had been promised would be permanent. Elodie stood very still, coil of rope forgotten in her arms, and listened to the cable sing—that low, freighted harmonic of a load far heavier than anything the *Merrow's Daughter* should have been hiding.

She'd hooked coffins before. She'd hooked engine blocks, cannon, a church bell once, whole and beautiful, that she'd had to leave because raising it meant hiring hands and hiring hands meant witnesses. She knew the grammar of snags the way she knew the grammar of currents.

This was none of them. This was heavier than a coffin and more deliberate than debris. The cable ran down at an angle that spoke of something sitting proud of the riverbed, something with edges, something that had been *placed*.

Elodie walked to the stern, took the cable in both raw palms, and pulled, once, testing—and felt the answer come back up through the wire and into her bones, patient and immense.

Whatever lay down there in the dark had been waiting for her.

She should have slipped the hook. Every instinct trained into her across four years of running said *slip the hook, move downriver, be somewhere else when whatever this is surfaces*, because nothing good ever came up on a line that heavy, and heavy things drew crowds, and crowds drew the wrong kind of attention, the gray-hulled kind.

But her hands were already paying out the lift lines. Her hands had never learned the rule. Her hands, scarred and salt-cracked and steadier now than they'd been all day, belonged to the nine-year-old on the bottom of the shallows at Corry Point, and to the boy who taught her, and to the letter in the oilskin, and they were already working, rigging the lift, tying off, preparing to raise whatever the river had decided, after eleven years of keeping, to give back.

The wind out of the southeast smelled of rain and something else—coal smoke, far off, downstream. A gray smudge on the evening horizon that might have been weather.

She dove at slack water, just before dark, and followed the cable down into the green-black, and her lamp found it resting in the clay like a thing enshrined: an iron-bound chest, lead-sheathed, crusted with a decade of river growth—and stamped into the lead, clean as the day a die had struck it, a crest she did not recognize and would never afterward forget.

A crowned wave, breaking over a star.
