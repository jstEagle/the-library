## 2. A Chest Stamped in Salt

Dawn on the Serrin came the color of old pewter, and Elodie Ravensworth was already wet.

She had been wet since before the first light, since the hour when the river gave up its warmth and the mist lay on the water like a sheet drawn over a face. She worked the barge alone, as she always worked alone—the *Kestrel's Debt*, forty feet of patched iron and stubbornness that she'd won in a card game she'd cheated at, moored over the wreck of a grain barge that had gone down nine years ago with no one to claim her and no one to mourn her. Just the kind of dead Elodie liked best. The kind nobody was looking for.

The dredge winch groaned. She fed it cable hand over hand, her gloves gone soft and rotten from three years of river work, her shoulders burning in the way she'd come to think of as honest pain—the kind that asked nothing of her except that she keep going.

"Come on, then," she murmured to the water. "What have you got?"

The river didn't answer. The river never answered. That was the thing people who didn't work it never understood—the Serrin wasn't cruel and it wasn't kind, it was simply patient in a way no living thing could afford to be. It kept what it took. It kept the grain barges and the pleasure boats and the bodies of the drowned, kept them soft and silted and secret, and if you were patient too, if you learned to read the current the way other people read faces, sometimes it let a little of it back.

Never all of it. Never the ones you wanted.

She spat over the side, a habit that had more ritual in it than respect, and checked the depth gauge bolted to the rail. Nine fathoms. The wreck lay in the old channel scar below the escarpment's shadow, where the current ran slow and cold and the silt lay thick as wet flour. Her hook had been dragging through the barge's cargo hold since midnight, and for the last hour it had been snagging on something that didn't feel like grain, didn't feel like timber, didn't feel like anything that belonged to a dead ship hauling wheat to a market that no longer existed.

The winch cable went taut.

Not the taut of a normal snag—that forgiving, grumbling tautness of cable catching on rot. This was something else. The cable hummed. She felt it through her boots, through the deck, through her back teeth: a deep, absolute resistance, the kind of weight that didn't argue but simply refused. The *Kestrel's Debt* rolled gently toward the cable and stopped, and the river around the hook went strangely smooth, as if the water itself were holding its breath.

Elodie let go of the winch and stood very still.

She had raised a cannon once, off a wreck near Harrow Bend—a six-pounder crusted in rust and riverstone, and it had come up complaining but it had come up. She had raised an engine block the size of a coffin. She had raised, on one terrible night she did not let herself think about, a thing that was not cargo at all, and she had put it back down and never gone back to that stretch of water, and she had told herself that was mercy rather than cowardice, and she had almost believed it.

This was heavier than the cannon. This was heavier than the engine. And it was coming up clean—no tumbling, no dragging, no slow grind of something being levered loose from the mud. It was coming up the way a thing comes up when it has been *set down* carefully, by hands that wanted it found later, or wanted it never found at all.

"Right," she said aloud, because the sound of her own voice kept the other sounds away. "Right."

She threw the winch into low gear and let the engine do the arguing. The cable came up foot by foot, the barge listing, the river bulging around the rising weight in a slow dark boil. Ten feet down. Six. Three. The water went from pewter to black glass, and then the thing broke the surface with a sound like a whale breaching, and Elodie forgot, for a long moment, how to breathe.

It was a chest.

Not a sailor's sea chest, not a merchant's strongbox—those she knew the way she knew her own hands. This was something older and more deliberate. It was the size of a coffin, which her mind supplied unhelpfully and refused to retract, and it was sheathed in lead, the metal gone the soft grey-white of bone where the river had scoured it, furred with silt along every seam. Iron banding held it at the corners, and the banding had been riveted with a care that spoke of shipyards and state money, not salvage and desperation.

But it was the lid that stopped her heart.

Stamped into the lead, dead center, pressed so deep it had survived decades of silt and current, was a seal. A circle, and inside the circle a design she knew without knowing how she knew it—a tower above three waves, and around the tower's crown, a ring of nine stars. Beneath it, in lettering worn nearly smooth but still legible to a woman who had spent her childhood tracing the forbidden alphabet in the margins of her grandfather's smuggled books, a single word.

*Vessany.*

Elodie sat down on the winch housing without meaning to.

There was no Vessany. There had been no Vessany for twenty-two years. The maps said so—she had checked, once, in a chart room in Corvane's own port city, young and stupid and grieving, and the cartographer had looked at her with the flat patience of a man explaining weather to a child. *No such nation, miss. Never was. You're thinking of the Vessany Coast, the old name for the delta. Common confusion.* And the atlas pages where Vessany should have been were blank, or worse than blank—reprinted with new names, new borders, new harbors, as if the sea itself had been ordered to forget.

But the sea had not been ordered to forget, and neither had the river, and here was a chest stamped in salt and lead with the name of a drowned country, coming up out of a wreck that had no business carrying it.

Her hands were moving before her mind caught up. That was the diver's curse and the diver's gift: the body went to work while the mind was still afraid. She got a line around the chest's iron banding, hauled it against the gunwale with the deck winch, and got it aboard with a grinding crash that nearly took her over the side with it. Water poured from the seams. The lead was cold under her palms even through her gloves, cold in a way that felt less like temperature than like intention.

She found the lock—a mechanism, not a padlock, something with tumblers and a keyway shaped like nothing she'd seen outside of a bank vault. She got her knife out. She got her pry bar out. She knelt over the chest in the grey morning light with her breath coming fast and her pulse hammering in her throat, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *Tomas would have known what this is.*

She put the pry bar to the seam.

That was when she heard the engine.

* * *

It came up the channel from the north, and there was no mistaking it, not for her, not for anyone who had grown up on this water the way she had. Fishing boats coughed. Barges chuffed. The navy's patrol cutters *sang*—a clean, high, disciplined note from engines maintained by people who were paid to maintain them, running at the exact speed regulation prescribed and not one knot faster, because the men aboard had nothing to fear from anyone and wanted everyone on the river to know it.

Elodie went from kneeling to flat on her deck in the space between two heartbeats.

She lay in the shadow of the winch housing, cheek against cold iron, and watched the cutter come around the bend below the escarpment. It was a Harrow-class, grey hull, white water at the bow, the twin-mast silhouette that meant it carried both signal gear and a boarding party. It swept the channel the way the navy swept everything—slowly, thoroughly, and with the serene confidence of an institution that had never once been made to account for itself.

It was not, she told herself, here for her. The Serrin was patrolled the way a house was swept: routinely, without malice, without meaning. Grain runners, smugglers, unlicensed divers—she was technically the last of these, working a wreck with no salvage writ, but the navy had never cared about a lone woman raising scrap off a dead grain barge. There was no money in her. There was no politics in her.

She lay very still and made herself believe that.

The cutter slowed.

It was a small thing, the slowing—half a knot, maybe less, the kind of deceleration a helmsman might make to read a chart or light a pipe. But Elodie had spent eleven years on this river learning the difference between the movements that meant nothing and the movements that meant *someone is looking*, and this one meant someone was looking. The cutter's bow swung a degree. Its glass—she couldn't see it from flat on her deck, but she could feel it, the way you felt a hand on the back of your neck—its glass was sweeping the shoreline, the channel, the anchored boats.

The *Kestrel's Debt*.

She did the arithmetic the way she did everything now, fast and cold and without letting herself feel the cost. The chest was forty feet of open deck away from any cover. It was lead-grey on an iron-grey barge under a pewter sky, which was the only reason it hadn't been spotted already, and it was the single most incriminating object she had ever laid eyes on. If a boarding party came aboard and found it, she would not be fined for unlicensed salvage. She would not be fined at all. She would simply go aboard the cutter, and there would be a report, and the report would go up a chain, and somewhere at the top of that chain a man whose name she didn't yet know would read the word *Vessany* and make a quiet decision, and Elodie Ravensworth would become one more thing the river kept.

She had promised herself, eleven years ago, over a grave that had no body in it, that she would never again be something the river kept.

The cutter was two hundred yards out and closing at its lazy, ceremonial pace.

Elodie moved.

She didn't run—running was noise, and noise was death. She flowed, low and fast, the way she moved underwater, and got the deck line off the cleat and around the chest in three passes, and then she was at the stern winch and the sea valve of the ballast tank that she had never, in three years, had occasion to open. The chest was too heavy to hide. She'd known it the moment it broke the surface, the way you knew a storm was coming before the first cloud. There was only one place on the Serrin where nothing could be found, and she had spent her whole life learning its door.

The chest went over the side with barely a splash—she'd rigged the line to pay out slow, and it sank stern-first, lead and iron and drowned nation, down through the black glass water toward the silt it had been taken from. She paid out line to the length she'd marked, tied it off to the winch drum, and closed the tank valve, and by the time she straightened, her hands were shaking so badly she had to press them flat against the rail to make them stop.

The cutter was fifty yards out.

She made herself do the ordinary things. She got the kettle on the spirit stove, because a woman alone at anchor who was not making tea was a woman doing something worth watching. She got out her mending—net patches, always net patches, the universal costume of harmless river people. She sat on her stern bench with the kettle ticking and the net in her lap and her heart going like a drum in a parade, and she watched the grey cutter slide up the channel toward her.

It didn't stop.

It came abeam of the *Kestrel's Debt* at maybe forty yards, close enough that she could see the ensign snapping, close enough that she could see a young officer on the foredeck with a signal glass, and the glass swung across her barge, across her, across the empty deck where a lead-lined coffin of a chest had sat not ten minutes ago, and moved on. The cutter's wake came sideways a moment later, gentle and indifferent, rocking her hull. The engine note climbed as it accelerated, clean and disciplined, and it swept on up the channel toward the escarpment and out of sight around the bend, and the river closed behind it like water over a stone.

Elodie sat with her net and her tea and did not move for a long time.

* * *

The mist burned off by midmorning, and the sun came up pale and wintry over the escarpment, and Elodie told herself she was being a fool.

That was the argument she ran, all through the morning, while she worked the grain barge's hold with her hook and brought up rotted wheat and river glass and one intact brass lamp that would buy a week's food. It was a fool's chest. It was a fool's name. Vessany was a story old people told, a grief the war had buried under twenty years of official forgetting, and whatever her grandfather's smuggled books had said, whatever Tomas had once whispered to her across a kitchen table the last night she ever saw him—*Elodie, if I tell you something, you have to promise you'll forget it, because forgetting is how you stay safe*—whatever all of that had been, it was twenty-two years dead, and the navy's cutter had proven only that the navy patrolled its own channel the way navies did.

But she didn't believe it, and she knew she didn't believe it, because she hadn't gone back to work after the first hour. She'd sat on her stern bench with her tea going cold and her eyes on the water, and she'd listened to the river, and the river had said nothing, and the nothing had been deafening.

A grain barge did not carry lead-lined chests. Grain barges carried grain, and rats, and the slow despair of their crews. A lead-lined chest stamped with the seal of a nation that had been erased from every map in the empire did not end up in the channel scar below the escarpment by accident. It had been put there. Deliberately. Weighted to stay down. And someone, somewhere, had either meant for it to stay down forever—or had meant, with the terrible patience of people who hid things in rivers, for it to be found by exactly the wrong person at exactly the wrong time.

Or the right one. She couldn't decide which frightened her more.

She went down herself in the early afternoon, when the light was best, because there were questions a hook couldn't answer. She dropped through water gone green-gold with the sun, down past the thermocline where the cold took her like a hand closing around her ribs, down the shot line to the wreck's shadow. The grain barge lay heeled over in the silt, her hold gaping, her timbers furred with weed. And forty feet off her stern, exactly where Elodie had sunk it, the chest sat on the channel floor, settled into the silt up to its banding, patient as everything else the river kept.

She hung above it in the cold green silence and looked at the seal.

Up close, in her own dive light, the detail came back. The tower. The three waves. The nine stars. Her grandfather's books had called it the Crest of the Nine Harbors, and had said that when the last harbor closed, the name would close with it, and she had thought, as a child, that this was poetry. It was not poetry. It was a promise. Somebody had stamped that seal on this chest knowing what it would cost anyone who found it, and had weighted it with lead—lead, the diver's oldest enemy, the metal that ate light and lied to magnets and made salvage gear read *nothing here*—and had sent it down into the one place in the world where a thing could be lost on purpose.

She put her gloved hand flat on the lid, over the word, and the cold of it came through the glove and into her palm and up her arm, and she thought about Tomas again, the way she tried not to, the way she always did eventually, the tide of it coming in whether she willed it or no.

Tomas, who had joined the navy at nineteen because he believed in things, which was his tragedy and had very nearly become hers. Tomas, who had written her one letter from a posting she could never find on any chart, a letter the censor had cut so full of holes it read like lace, and the only whole sentence in it was the one that had kept her awake for eleven years: *If anything happens to me, look to the water, Elodie. Whatever they tell you, look to the water.*

She had looked to the water. She had looked for eleven years, working wrecks up and down the Serrin, telling herself it was salvage, telling herself it was a living, telling herself every lie a grieving woman needs to keep her hands busy. And now a chest stamped with a drowned nation's seal had come up off a wreck in the exact channel where her brother's last posting should have been, and she was beginning to understand, with a sick slow vertigo, that it might not have come up by chance at all.

The river kept its dead. But somebody had to put them there.

Her air gauge said she had ten minutes of bottom time left. She spent four of them clearing silt from the chest's seaward side, looking for a maker's mark, a date, a second stamp—anything. She found it on the banding, small and neat, stamped into the iron by a shipyard hand: a serial number, and before it, a two-letter code she recognized from the navy surplus that came through every port market in the delta.

*NV.* Naval stores.

The cold stopped feeling like cold. It went through her like current, like the moment on a dive when the silt clouds and you understand that the wreck you're inside is not resting, it is *falling*, and you have perhaps a minute to decide which way to swim. The navy had packed this chest. The navy had weighted it and sunk it in a naval channel, under the guns of its own patrols, in the water where her brother had last been alive. And this morning, a navy cutter had slowed as it passed her barge—not stopped, not boarded, just *slowed*, the way a man slows when he passes a grave he knows is there.

Or the way a man slows when he checks on something he buried.

Elodie kicked for the shot line and went up faster than was safe, the green light falling away beneath her, the pressure easing off her ears and chest, and she broke the surface into the grey winter air gasping as if she'd been the one drowned for twenty-two years. She got her fins off, got over the gunwale, got the line up hand over hand until the chest broke the surface again and came aboard, streaming, heavier than sin, and she stood over it on her empty deck with the water running off it and her breath tearing in and out.

The lock. The mechanism with the shaped keyway. She knelt and looked at it properly for the first time, and her blood, already cold, went colder.

It was a navy pattern. She'd seen it on ordnance crates in port markets—seen it, and seen the way sellers handled those crates, which was to say not at all, which was to say they resold them sealed and unopened and never asked. It was a lock that took a naval key, and she did not have a naval key, and every instinct she owned was telling her that the chest was not empty, that a chest this heavy, this armored, this carefully drowned, did not come all this way through twenty-two years of river to hold nothing but its own lead.

She wanted it open. That was the truth under everything, the truth she'd been outrunning since dawn: she wanted it open the way she'd wanted her brother's letter to make sense, the way she'd wanted the navy's file on his disappearance to be more than a form and a signature, the way she'd wanted, for eleven years, one single thing in this world that was not a lie. The chest was a lock and she was a key that didn't fit, and the wanting was going to make her stupid.

She was still kneeling there, her pry bar in her hand and the lock gleaming its small brass arrogance at her, when she heard the engine again.

Different engine. Slower, lower, a working boat's note, not the navy's disciplined song. It came down the channel from the north, from the direction of the escarpment towns, and it did not pass her by. It slowed. It came about. It crept toward her anchorage with the unhurried certainty of a boat whose pilot already knew what he would find there, and Elodie rose from the chest with the pry bar still in her hand and watched a small, shabby, single-masted craft swing into the current off her stern and begin, with great deliberation, to drop anchor.

There was one man at its tiller. He was watching her across the water, and he did not look away when she looked back, which almost no one on this river ever did.

The sun was going down behind the escarpment, and the channel was filling with shadow, and the chest sat between her feet like a heart that had started beating again after twenty-two years. Elodie tightened her grip on the pry bar and waited to find out whether the man in the small boat had come to help her open it—or to make sure she never did.
