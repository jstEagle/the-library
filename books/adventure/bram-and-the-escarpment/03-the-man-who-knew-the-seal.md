## 3. The Man Who Knew the Seal

The tavern was called the Drowned Gull, which Bram Ashgrave considered either an act of remarkable honesty or remarkable stupidity on the part of its owner. It sat at the end of the Saltmere quay like something that had given up trying to stay upright, listing toward the water on pilings gone soft as old cheese, and it smelled of tar, fried fish, and the particular sourness of men who had spent the day arguing with a river and losing.

Bram had been sitting in the corner booth for six hours, and he had ordered exactly two drinks in all that time, both of them tea, because a man who drank while he worked was a man who made mistakes, and Bram Ashgrave had not survived eleven years of running by making mistakes.

He watched the woman through the salt-fogged window, and his hands would not stop shaking.

Not fear. Not yet. He told himself it was the cold—that the river damp got into everything this far north, into the joints, into the teeth, into the spaces between thoughts where a man kept the things he didn't want to look at directly. But that was a lie, and Bram knew lies the way a priest knew scripture: intimately, professionally, from long years of handling other people's.

He was afraid because he had seen the crest.

* * *

He had been on the quay at dawn, doing what he did everywhere he went—learning the place, cataloguing it, filing it away in the vast disordered archive behind his eyes. The fishing boats going out with their lamps still lit. The customs officer who took bribes small enough to be affordable and large enough to be worth it. The navy patrol schedule, which was wrong, which had changed three days ago, which meant someone upstream had radioed down about something, and Bram had spent the whole morning turning that fact over like a stone looking for what lived underneath.

And then she had come out of the water.

He'd seen salvage divers before. Half the river towns had one—a lean, sunburnt person who could hold their breath too long and charge too much for it. But this woman worked alone, which was unusual, and her gear was patched and mended and maintained with a ferocity that suggested she trusted no one else to touch it, and when she came up the ladder onto her barge with the first load of scrap, she moved like the water hadn't beaten her but merely argued with her and lost.

Her name he learned within the hour, because names were cheap in harbor towns if you bought enough rounds for other people. Elodie Ravensworth. Salvage diver. No family, no partner, no fixed berth—she'd been working downriver at Wrenholt the month before, and before that somewhere past the escarpment, and nobody could say where she'd be next month, and everybody said it in the same tone they used for weather. *She comes and goes.*

A rootless woman. A careful one. The kind of person, Bram thought, who had taught herself never to be found, which meant she had once been found by something she couldn't survive finding her again.

He understood that. He had written the file on people like her, back when he still wrote files.

Then the dredge had hooked the chest, and Bram had set down his cup so fast it cracked against the saucer, and the world had narrowed to a single point of light and noise.

* * *

Because he knew that chest. He knew it the way you know a face from a photograph you were never supposed to see.

Lead-lined, iron-banded, stamped on every face with the seal of Vessany: the triple wave beneath a broken crown, pressed deep enough into the lead to have survived twenty-two years of silt and salt. He had catalogued forty-one objects bearing that seal during his service in the Naval Archive at Corvane's own headquarters. He had held Vessany's shipping manifests in white-gloved hands. He had read the trade ledgers of a nation that officially, legally, cartographically, had never existed—and he had done it in a reading room with no windows, under a regulation that classified the very name as a archival error, a clerical ghost, a thing that had slipped through and must on no account be acknowledged.

*Vessany does not appear in the historical record,* the standing order said, in Admiral Corvane's own hand, or in the hand of whoever drafted such things for him. *Any document bearing the Vessany crest is to be treated as misfiled material of no provenance and destroyed without entry.*

Destroyed without entry. Bram had recatalogued eleven thousand items in his seven years there, and he could count on one hand the ones marked for destruction without entry, and every single one of them bore the broken crown and the triple wave.

So when the chest came up out of the river on Elodie Ravensworth's dredge line, dripping and gleaming and stamped with the mark of a drowned country, Bram Ashgrave felt the fear arrive in his body before his mind could dress it up in anything respectable. It came up through the floorboards of the tavern, through the soles of his boots, into the roots of his teeth. Because he knew what a sealed Vessany chest meant. He knew what the navy had done to keep the contents of the last one from ever seeing daylight. And he knew—he knew with the flat, cold certainty of a man who had read the internal correspondence—that whatever was inside this one, someone had already died for it once, and more people would die for it again, starting, quite possibly, with the woman on the barge.

And then, an hour later, the patrol boat had come sweeping down the channel with its searchlight playing over the water like a finger pointing, and Bram had watched Elodie Ravensworth lie to three sailors with a smoothness that impressed him even as it frightened him, and he had thought: *she doesn't know what she has.* She thought it was treasure. She thought it was salvage rights and a buyer in some port city and money enough to stop moving for a season.

She didn't know that the navy didn't send patrol boats for treasure.

* * *

By full dark, Bram had talked himself into it and out of it four times.

Out of it: the sensible argument, the one that had kept him alive across eleven years and nine assumed names. Walk away. Take the morning packet boat south. Whatever was in that chest, it wasn't his problem; he had carried enough of other people's secrets to fill a warehouse, and every one of them had cost him. He owed this stranger nothing. He owed himself the quiet life he'd been pretending to build, the little archive job in the provincial capital, the rented room with the crooked shutters, the whole careful architecture of nothing.

Into it: the memory of a document.

It came to him now, sitting in the corner of the Drowned Gull with his cold tea, the way it came to him most nights—the way it had come to him every night for eleven years, patient as tide. A single page. He had pulled it himself from a restricted drawer, cataloguing number 7734-V, and he had stood in that windowless room holding it and feeling the paper seem to warm in his hands as though it were alive, as though it had been waiting eleven years in the dark for someone to pick it up.

An order. Dated the fourteenth of the Flood Month, twenty-two years ago. Signed with a cipher he had never been able to forget. *Sink the vessel at position. No survivors to reach shore. No record to remain.*

No survivors to reach shore. No record to remain.

He had copied it. Of course he had copied it—he was an archivist, copying was the only prayer he knew—and he had hidden the copy inside the binding of a hymnal in the chapel of the naval academy, because it was the only place he could think of where no one would ever look, and then he had reported the original as misfiled, per regulation, and watched it go into the destruction furnace, and he had told himself that the copy was enough, that someday, somehow, it would surface, that the truth was safe now, sleeping in a hymnal, waiting.

Three weeks later the academy chapel burned. Officially: faulty wiring. Bram had stood in the crowd of cadets watching the smoke and understood, with a clarity that had never left him, that someone had known. That someone always knew. That the fire had not been about the hymnal at all—it had been about *him*, a message written in ash: *we know what you know, and we are patient, and we are everywhere.*

He had run that night. He had been running since.

And now a chest bearing the seal he had catalogued and burned and dreamed about had come up out of the river into the hands of a woman who didn't know what it was, and the patrol boats were already changing their schedules, and Bram Ashgrave sat in a tavern corner and understood that the choice he had been avoiding for eleven years had finally, inevitably, found him anyway.

You could run from the navy. You could not run from a document.

* * *

He watched her come ashore just after full dark.

She tied her skiff at the far end of the quay, well away from the tavern lights, and he noted that too—the instinct for shadow, the habit of arriving and leaving by the edges of things. She carried a canvas bag over one shoulder and walked with the pry bar still in her right hand, not concealed, not brandished, just *there*, the way some women carried umbrellas. Her hair was still wet from the evening dive. The lamplight caught the salt drying on her forearms.

She stopped at the tavern door. He saw her hesitate—saw her do the arithmetic every careful person does entering a room full of strangers: exits, faces, hands. Then she came in, and the room's noise bent around her the way water bends around a piling, and she took a seat at the bar with her back to the wall and ordered stew and bread and ate like someone who had learned that meals might be interrupted.

Bram finished his tea. He checked, without moving, the three things he always checked: the false bottom of his satchel, where the forged papers lived; the knife at his belt, which he hoped never to use; and the door, which was thirty feet away and which he was about to walk away from, deliberately, toward the most dangerous conversation of his life.

*Charm,* he reminded himself. *Charm and a lie. You're good at both. God help you, you've had practice.*

He crossed the room carrying his cup like a prop, and he let his shoulders loosen and his mouth find the easy half-smile that had opened a hundred doors in a hundred towns, and he said:

"That's Ravensworth salvage moored at the east quay, isn't it? I've been admiring her rigging all day. I don't think I've seen a dredge winch reeved that way outside the southern yards."

She looked up at him. Her eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and they went through him like sonar—one pass, quick and total, taking his measure, his hands, his accent, his boots, the cut of his coat. He felt catalogued. He almost laughed. If she only knew.

"Southern yards," she said. Her voice was flat, unhurried, giving nothing. "Where's south?"

"Far enough." He set his cup on the bar, an offering, a surrender of territory. "Bram Ferrow. I'm a records man—shipping registers, cargo manifests, wreck claims. Boring work, but somebody has to know where everything sank." A lie, folded neatly inside a truth; the best lies were tailored from honest cloth. "I'm down here tracing a claim for an insurer in the capital. A barge lost off the escarpment, years back. Nobody's ever claimed the wreck."

"Nobody claims most wrecks," she said. "That's rather the point of them."

"True enough." He smiled, and let the smile carry a little self-deprecation, a little weariness—the portrait of a tired clerk far from home. "May I? The bench is free and my booth has developed opinions about my spine."

She considered him for a long moment. He let her. Charm, he had learned long ago, was mostly the discipline of silence—of letting the other person's suspicion exhaust itself against your patience.

Finally she shifted an inch along the bench, which from her was evidently a signed treaty. He sat.

"You watch the water a lot," she said. Not a question. "You were on the quay this morning. You were at the window all afternoon."

"I watch everything," Bram said pleasantly. "Occupational hazard. Records men who don't watch end up insuring ghosts." He turned the cup slowly on the bar. "Though I'll confess the afternoon's entertainment exceeded expectations. I don't often see a dredge bring up anything worth the navy sending a boat."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, controlled, gone. There it is, he thought. There's the dread. You've already started wondering, haven't you, why a patrol boat cares about your scrap.

"Navy looks at everything on this channel," she said. "It's a military waterway."

"It is," Bram agreed mildly. "Though in eleven years of this work I've noticed they only sweep the channel twice a day unless something's come up that shouldn't have." He let that sit. Then, lightly, as if the thought had only just occurred to him, as if he were a bored clerk making conversation: "Odd thing, though. The old wrecks along this stretch. Most of them predate the war, but there's a cluster of hulls off the escarpment shelf that don't match any registry I've ever searched. Ships that were never built, sunk in waters where no ship was ever reported lost. You spend enough nights in archives and you start collecting those little holes. Ships that aren't in the books." He looked at her, and let one thread of truth show through the weave, just one, because a lie with no truth in it wouldn't hold her. "I've always wondered what else isn't in the books."

She was silent. Around them the tavern roared on—dice, arguments, a fiddle tuning itself badly—but between the two of them there was a small, dense quiet, like the pressure change before a storm.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked at last.

"Because you look like a woman who collects things that aren't in the books," Bram said.

*Careful,* he told himself. *Too close.* But it was done, and watching her face he understood that it had needed to be done—that Elodie Ravensworth would smell flattery and small talk forever, but she would listen to a warning, because warnings were the currency of people who lived by their wits, and she recognized the coin.

"The chest," she said quietly. Not touching it, not looking at it, her eyes steady on his. "You saw it come up."

"I saw the seal."

"And?"

And now, Bram thought. Now you choose how much of yourself to spend.

He leaned in slightly, and dropped his voice beneath the fiddle, and gave her the second lie—the necessary one, the one he would carry like a stone in his boot from this night forward.

"The broken crown and three waves," he said. "It's a merchant house mark. Vessany & Sons, out of the old southern trade—they ran grain and refugees up this coast before the war. The house collapsed when the war came; the family scattered; the mark survives on maybe a few hundred sealed strongboxes, most of them still underwater. Collectors pay for them. Serious collectors." He shrugged, a small shrug, a clerk's shrug. "If that's what you've got, it's worth money. Real money. But—" and here he hesitated, and let the hesitation show, because a good lie needed seams, "—it's also the kind of money that brings out bad men. If the navy saw that crest today, they weren't looking for scrap. They were checking whether anyone had found something they'd prefer stayed found-not."

Elodie Ravensworth looked at him for a long time. The fiddle scraped. The stew steamed untouched in front of her.

"You knew the mark on sight," she said. "From across the water. At dusk."

"I have a memory for marks," Bram said. "It's why I'm good at the boring work."

It was, at least, entirely true. He had recognized the crest of Vessany from two hundred yards in failing light, and he would recognize it in his sleep, in his grave, in whatever came after, because he had spent eleven years trying to forget it and failing, and because the last time he had seen it, it had been stamped on an order to drown a shipload of people whose names he had once read aloud to himself, alone, in a locked room, like a prayer for the unforgivable.

"Tomorrow," Elodie said finally. "At low tide. If you want to look at it, come to the barge. If you're lying to me, Bram Ferrow, I'll know."

"Yes," Bram said. "You will."

He paid for her stew before she could object, tipped his head, and walked out into the dark with his heart going like a drum in a procession.

Behind him, through the window, he could see her still sitting at the bar, motionless, thinking. Ahead of him the quay stretched out black and wet under a sky the color of wet slate, and far down the channel the navy's patrol light swept the water once, twice, patient as a lighthouse, patient as a promise.

Eleven years, Bram thought, and the document has finally come looking for me.

Low tide was at dawn. He had until then to decide how much of the truth a woman could bear—and how much longer he could survive telling her less than all of it.
