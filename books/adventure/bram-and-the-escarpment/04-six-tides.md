## 4. Six Tides

The chest sat between them on the floor of the boathouse like a thing that had drowned and been given back.

Elodie had not wanted to bring it here. Every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her alive through nine years of rivers, wrecks, and borrowed harbors—had screamed at her to drop it back into the channel, let the silt have it, let the river keep its secrets the way it kept its dead. But her hands had already been working the padlock before her mind finished the argument, and by then it was too late. Curiosity was a current, and she had never once beaten one by standing still in it.

Bram knelt opposite her, sleeves rolled, his long fingers hovering over the lead lining where it curled away from the oak like old bark peeling from new wood. He hadn't touched it yet. He'd spent ten minutes just looking, and Elodie had spent those same ten minutes deciding whether that made him careful or afraid.

"You said you recognized the seal," she said.

"I said I recognized *of* the seal." He glanced up. Lamplight caught the gray at his temples, the salt-and-pepper stubble he never quite shaved clean. "There's a difference between knowing a crest and knowing what it cost."

"Then tell me what it cost."

He exhaled slowly, the way men do when they're buying time with breath. Outside, the river moved against the pilings in its endless low conversation—*hush, hush, hush*—and somewhere down the dock a mooring line creaked like a door in an empty house.

"Vessany," Bram said, "was a coastal nation about the size of a bad joke. Two ports, a fishing fleet, a language nobody bothered to learn because everyone who mattered spoke ours. When the war ended, the treaties redrew the coast and Vessany stopped existing. Not conquered. Not annexed. *Erased.* The maps were reprinted within the year. The archives were sealed. If you asked a schoolchild today where Vessany was, they'd point at open water." His jaw tightened. "Nations don't drown quietly, Elodie. Somebody holds them under."

"And this is theirs?"

"This is theirs." He finally laid his palm flat on the lid, reverent as a man touching a coffin. "This seal was stamped on state documents. Naval correspondence. Things that were supposed to burn when the country did."

Elodie looked at the crest pressed into the lead—a tower above three waves, worn shallow but unmistakable—and felt the cold of the channel still clinging to her own skin, the memory of the patrol boat's searchlight sweeping past her snorkel like the eye of something blind and hungry. She'd hidden under a hull for forty minutes while that light passed. She'd thought, in the black and the cold, *this is what Tomas felt.*

She hadn't let herself think his name in weeks. Now it came up like a body off the bottom.

"Open it," she said.

* * *

Bram worked the lock with two thin picks and no commentary, which told her he'd done this before and didn't want to discuss when or why. The mechanism surrendered with a click that sounded obscenely loud in the boathouse quiet. Then the lid came up, and the smell rolled out—brine and rot and, beneath it, something older and drier. Paper. Wax. Ink that had waited.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as shell, lay documents. Dozens of them. Bundles tied with tarred cord, wax seals intact, water-stained at the edges but whole. Lead-lined chests don't leak. Whatever had sunk this chest had meant for it to survive.

Bram lifted the first bundle as if it might bite. Unwrapped it. Read.

Elodie watched his face change. She'd known him four days—four days since he'd appeared at her elbow outside the harbor office with a lie so smooth she'd almost believed it, four days of him being useful and charming and carefully, deliberately vague—and she had catalogued his expressions the way she catalogued currents. But she had nothing for this. His face went white in patches, like frost forming, and his hand began to shake, and he set the papers down on the oilcloth with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something explosive.

"What," Elodie said. "What is it."

"Shipping manifest." His voice came out wrong. Flat. "Dated eleven years ago. Vessany's Pride, out of the last free port, bound for resettlement across the strait. Passengers listed by family." He swallowed. "Refugees. Four hundred and six souls."

"The ship that sank."

"The ship they say sank. Storm loss, structural failure, tragic accident—all of it entered into the record by a navy board of inquiry that never interviewed a single survivor, because officially there weren't any." He picked up the second bundle. This one he read in silence, lips moving, and Elodie watched the frost spread until his whole face was gray. "These are requisition orders. Demolition stores. Scuttle charges, signed out of a naval armory three days before the Pride sailed, authorized by—" He stopped.

"By who."

He turned the page toward her without looking at it himself, as though he couldn't bear to be the one reading it aloud. The signature was a scrawl, but the name printed beneath it was crisp as an engraving: **R. CORVANE, CDR., ORDNANCE & SALVAGE DIVISION.**

Elodie stared at it. The lamplight hissed. Somewhere out on the black water, a night bird called once and got no answer.

"That's not possible," she heard herself say. "Corvane was a commander then. He wasn't anywhere near—"

"He was exactly near enough." Bram's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Elodie. These aren't copies. These are originals. Signed orders to sink a refugee ship full of civilians and call it weather. Someone in the ordnance division put them in a lead chest and weighted it into the sea rather than let them burn." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet, and he didn't wipe them. "Someone wanted this found. Eventually. By someone who could use it."

The boathouse seemed to tilt. Elodie gripped the edge of the chest to steady herself, and her fingers found the third bundle, the one Bram hadn't reached yet, tied with a different knot—sloppier, hurried, human. She tore the cord.

A witness list. Names in a clerk's hand, columns of them, headed by a title that made her blood stop: **PERSONS HAVING KNOWLEDGE OF PRIDE DISPOSITION — DO NOT ENGAGE — REPORT TO DIVISION.**

*Do not engage. Report to division.* Like vermin. Like contamination.

Her eyes went down the column. Names blurred. And then one name stood up off the page and walked toward her, and she couldn't breathe.

**RAVENSWORTH, T. — ENSIGN, RN SURVEY CORPS.**

*Tomas.*

Not dead-at-sea. Not lost-with-all-hands. A witness. Listed, filed, flagged. Her brother had been twenty-two years old, a survey officer with good marks and a mother's photograph in his kit, and he had *known*—he had seen something aboard or ashore, something that put him on this list, and eleven days after the Pride went down, Ensign T. Ravensworth had vanished from every record the navy would show her, and the letter they sent his sister had used the phrase *regret to inform* four times in two paragraphs.

"They killed him." The words came out of her sounding like someone else's voice, low and even and terribly calm. "He saw what they did and they killed him for it."

"Elodie—"

"They didn't lose him. They didn't misplace him. They put him on a list and they *erased* him, the way they erased Vessany, the way they were going to erase this chest if I hadn't—" Her hand closed around the manifest hard enough to crease it, and Bram's fingers came over hers, gentle, prying, and she let him take it because otherwise she would tear it, and some animal part of her knew that these papers were the only bones of her brother left in the world.

"Breathe," Bram said quietly. "Breathe, and listen to me, because there's worse and you need to hear it now, tonight, while we can still think."

"Worse than my brother's murder receipt?"

"Worse than finding out." He drew the last document from the chest—a single sheet, heavier stock, folded around something that clinked faintly. Inside was a navy hydrographic notice, official channels and depths marked in red, and clipped to it a small brass tide-table marker stamped with the escarpment station's code. Bram spread it flat on the floorboards between them. "This is a dive window. Marked by whoever packed this chest. The wreck of the Pride isn't scattered on the channel bottom, Elodie. It's wedged against the escarpment, below the shelf, in the one place the navy's own charts say can't be salvaged except—" his finger traced the red line, the numbers beside it, "—except now. Except in the next six tides."

"Six tides."

"After that, the monsoon comes up the coast and the surge buries the site under thirty feet of shifted sand until next spring. Whoever left this knew the schedule. Knew the navy would come eventually, too—they always come—but bet the finder could beat them if the finder moved fast." He met her eyes. "And the navy knows the window too. Corvane built his career on that wreck. When word gets out that a salvage diver pulled a Vessany-sealed chest out of the channel—and it will get out; the patrol boat logged your position before you dove the second time—there will be a fleet on that escarpment inside of two days. Salvage ships. Cutting crews. Men whose job is to make sure the hold of that vessel reaches the surface in pieces small enough to feed into a furnace."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft. Elodie became aware, distantly, of her own heartbeat—not fast, strangely, but deep and slow, like something surfacing from very far down.

Six tides. Six chances for the river to give up her brother's truth before the storm took it, and the navy burned it, and the world went on not remembering a country or a ship or a boy named Tomas who wrote one desperate letter and then became a line in a file.

She thought of the letter. She had it memorized the way other people memorized prayers. *If anything happens to me, Ellie, look at the water. Don't trust the boards. Look at the water.* She'd been fifteen. She'd thought grief had made him strange.

He'd been trying to hand her a map.

"Then we don't beat them to the wreck," Elodie said.

Bram frowned. "We—"

"We beat them to the *hold*. There's a difference." She was already moving, pulling her gear locker open, counting tanks by touch in the dimness. Her hands were steady now. Steadier than they'd been in nine years. "Whatever's in that sealed compartment—logs, orders, the captain's copy of something—it stays down there until I can carry it up myself. And when I do, it doesn't go to a furnace and it doesn't go to a court that buried it once. It goes into daylight. All of it. Every name."

"Elodie." Bram rose too, and for a moment they faced each other across the open chest, across the paper ghosts of four hundred and six people and one erased nation. "You should understand what you're saying. Once we move on this, there's no more running for you. No more anonymous harbors, no more working wrecks nobody claims. Corvane doesn't chase divers. He chases *stories*, and he ends them."

For a long moment she said nothing. Outside, the tide was turning; she could feel it in the pilings, in the groan of the dock, the whole river reversing itself with a patience older than any navy.

"Tomas ran," she said finally. "He ran, and he wrote one letter, and he died alone in a file cabinet. I'm done running." She looked down at the manifest, at the neat clerk's hand that had reduced her brother to a flag and a directive, and something in her chest that had been drowning for eleven years finally touched bottom and pushed off. "Six tides, Bram. Count them with me."

He held her gaze a moment longer—something moving behind his eyes that looked almost like guilt, though she was too lit with purpose to read it properly—and then he nodded once, sharp, and reached for the charts.

"One," he said.

* * *

They worked through the dark hours while the first tide drained.

Bram spread the hydrographic chart across the workbench and weighted its corners with wrenches, tracing the red-marked approach to the escarpment site with a pencil stub, muttering depths and current windows half to himself in a way that told Elodie, again, that his archive career had involved far more than filing. She loaded gear. Checked regulators. Wrapped the documents from the chest in fresh oilcloth and waterproof rolls and hid them in three separate places, because a lesson lived in her marrow now: never let the whole truth ride in one hull.

It was near dawn, gray light seeping under the boathouse door like water finding a crack, when Bram went still over the chart.

"What?" Elodie said.

He didn't answer immediately. His pencil had stopped on the escarpment approach, on a notation he'd made himself an hour ago, and he was staring at it as though the ink might rearrange itself.

"The window's tighter than I thought," he said at last. "The dive window above the hold—there are maybe ninety minutes of slack water per tide where a diver can work the shelf without the current pinning her. And the monsoon's leading squall line..." He rubbed his eyes. "Elodie, the navy won't need six tides. Corvane runs his salvage operations like blockades. If he has even a rumor of the chest—if that patrol boat's log crossed the right desk—we'll see masts on the horizon by tide two. Maybe sooner."

"Then we leave with this tide."

"With this—" He looked up sharply. "You've been diving all night. You haven't slept. The escarpment run is twelve hours upriver through channels that eat careless boats, and you want to leave *now*?"

"I want to leave before the masts." She slung the last tank into the skiff and turned to face him, and the dawn light through the door's gap fell across the open chest between them, across the manifest with her brother's name on it. "Every hour we wait is an hour Corvane gains. You said it yourself—he chases stories. Well, this story leaves port in twenty minutes, and he can chase it all he likes." She paused, and her voice softened despite her, despite everything. "Come with me or don't. But decide fast."

Something flickered across Bram's face—that guilt again, deeper this time, a shadow under the skin—and then he was moving, gathering his notes, rolling the charts, and the moment passed unexamined, the way so many of his moments did.

Outside, the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the wind had shifted, and it smelled—faintly, prematurely, impossibly—of rain coming up from the sea.

Elodie cast off the lines herself. As the skiff swung into the current and the escarpment rose ahead of them, blue-gray and patient as a sleeping animal, she counted the tide going out beneath her hull.

One.

Five remained.
