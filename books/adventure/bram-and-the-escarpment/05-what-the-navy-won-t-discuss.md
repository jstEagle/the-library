## 5. What the Navy Won't Discuss

The chest sat between us on the floor of the boathouse like a third person who had come in from the rain and refused to leave.

Bram had spread the documents across an upturned skiff, weighting the corners with brass fittings he'd scavenged from my junk drawer without asking. He handled each page the way a priest handles relics—two fingers, no hurry, as though the paper might bruise. Outside, the river worked at its endless arithmetic, adding and subtracting water against the pilings, and I tried to read his face instead of the pages because I already knew what the pages said.

I'd known since the moment I saw the manifest. Some knowledge arrives like that—not as discovery but as recognition, the way you recognize your own name called across a crowded harbor.

*Witnesses interviewed aboard HMS Vantage, prior to scuttling of refugee vessel Vessany's Pride.* And there, fourth from the top, in a clerk's careful hand: *T. Ravensworth, Able Seaman.*

"Tomas," I said aloud, because saying it made it a fact instead of a wound. "He was on the Vantage."

Bram didn't look up. "I know."

"You knew before we opened it."

"I suspected." He smoothed a corner flat with his thumb. "The Vantage was the escort ship. Whatever happened to the Pride, someone on that deck saw it happen. The navy would have taken statements." A pause, delicate as a diver's equalization. "I didn't know your brother was one of them. I'm sorry."

Sorry. The word sat in the boathouse air with all the weight of a soap bubble. I turned away from him and looked out through the warped planks at the gray water, and the gray water looked back, and somewhere beneath it lay the barge I'd been stripping for three weeks, and beyond the barge, deeper, past where my lines reached, the escarpment dropped away into cold black nothing—and I understood, all at once, with a vertigo so complete it felt physical, that I had never found any of this by accident.

* * *

Tomas joined the navy the spring after our mother died, which was not a coincidence, though he pretended it was.

I remember the recruiting office in Saltmere Quay—the way the flag hung motionless in the windless morning, the recruiter's boots too shiny for a man whose job was convincing boys to get them muddy. Tomas was nineteen and grief had made him porous; every promise poured straight through. *See the coast,* the recruiter said. *Learn a trade. Be somebody the parish has to salute.* I stood behind him in the queue wanting to grab his collar and drag him home, and I didn't, because I was seventeen and proud and half-convinced by the shiny boots myself.

He wrote to me every month for two years. Ordinary letters—about bad coffee, about a mate who could juggle knives, about the strange luxury of a hammock after years of sleeping in a house that still smelled of illness. He sent me a shell once, spiral-cut by some dockside artisan, and I kept it on my windowsill and told myself the navy was fine, the navy was just a job, the navy was a thousand boys doing push-ups on decks while gulls mocked them.

Then the letters changed.

It was the eleventh month of his third year. His handwriting, always square and patient, went jagged. He wrote less about coffee and more about silence—about how certain questions got you extra watches, how certain names were not said aboard his ship, how a man he'd shared a mess table with for eight months had been transferred overnight and no one would say where. *Some things are classified that shouldn't be, El. That's all I'll say in writing. Don't write back asking. Burn this if you're smart.*

I did not burn it. I am not smart in the ways that matter.

Six weeks later, one more letter came. One page. The ink was smeared, as if written in haste or damp or both, and I have read it so many times the creases have gone soft as cloth. I can recite it the way other people recite prayers:

*El — if anything happens to me it wasn't an accident. There's a ship they don't talk about. Ask about Vessany. Ask anyone from the old coast towns what happened to the refugees and watch their faces. I've put something where the river can keep it safe. Rivers keep things better than archives do. Archives burn. If I go quiet, don't look for me in the places they tell you to look. Look where they tell you not to. — T.*

That was the last. No death notice, no discharge papers, no returned effects box with his watch and his cut shell. Just a gap in the correspondence, clean as a missing tooth, and when I finally scraped together passage to the naval administration offices in Corvane's district, a lieutenant with kind eyes and dead voice informed me that Able Seaman T. Ravensworth had been *transferred to a posting not subject to family notification*, and that further inquiries would be *noted*.

Noted. As if my grief were a logbook entry. As if they would file it away in some archive and it might, someday, be useful to them.

I left that office shaking with a rage I had nowhere to put, and I did the only thing I could think to do with it. I went to the river. I bought a dredge with Tomas's savings. I started pulling up everything the water had swallowed—because he had told me rivers keep things better than archives do, and I believed him, and I hated him a little for making me believe him, and I loved him completely for knowing I would.

* * *

"Elodie."

Bram's voice pulled me back to the boathouse, to the smell of tar and wet rope, to the documents fanned across the skiff like a hand of terrible cards. He was watching me with that careful archivist's attention, the look of a man cataloguing damage.

"You've gone somewhere," he said.

"The recruiting office in Saltmere." I sat down on an overturned crate because my legs had opinions of their own. "There was a flag that wouldn't move. I've been thinking about that flag for ten years and I only just realized it never moved because the wind never comes into those offices. They build them that way. Dead air. Nothing stirs."

"That's not why you've gone pale."

"No." I picked up the manifest again, my thumb under Tomas's name. "Read me the coordinates again. The wreck site. Where you think the Pride went down."

He hesitated. Then he read them: latitude, longitude, the bearing off the escarpment's northern shoulder, the depth soundings copied from a chart he should not have owned.

I closed my eyes and saw my own charts—the ones I'd drawn over three seasons of diving this stretch of river, inked and re-inked, annotated with depths and currents and the locations of every hull I'd ever touched. The barge. The drowned pilot boat. The scatter of ballast stone two hundred yards east. And at the center of it all, the place where my best lines always went slack and my compass needle wandered and the water went cold enough to ache even in summer: the deep hole below the escarpment shelf, the one the local fishermen wouldn't trawl, the one I'd been circling for months like a moth around a lamp I couldn't see.

The coordinates matched. Not approximately. Exactly. The navy's own survey numbers, from whatever classified chart Bram had copied them from, fell inside the boundaries of the ground I had been working alone for three years.

"They led me here," I said.

Bram said nothing, which was its own confirmation.

"Think about it." My voice came out level, which surprised me; underneath the levelness something was screaming. "A woman with no family, no berth, no ties—just a salvage rig and a reputation for diving wrecks nobody claims. And suddenly the best unclaimed ground on the whole river opens up. Permits nobody contests. Patrols that sweep past and don't stop me, when they stop everyone else. I thought I was lucky. I thought—" I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Three years I thought I was clever. Finding ground nobody else wanted. And it wasn't luck. It wasn't cleverness. It was bait on a hook, and I took it, and I've been swimming in circles on the end of their line ever since."

"You can't be sure of the mechanism," Bram said carefully. "Corvane's fleet doesn't micromanage river permits. It could be—"

"It could be coincidence. Yes. Let's talk about coincidence." I stood, and the boathouse seemed to tilt. "Coincidence is that the one stretch of river I work happens to sit over the wreck of the ship my brother died trying to expose. Coincidence is that the chest with the proof comes up on *my* dredge, in *my* nets, after three years of me combing this ground like a woman searching a house for a thing she's lost. Coincidence is that Tomas wrote *look where they tell you not to look*—and the navy, whether they meant to or not, spent three years telling me exactly where to look by making sure I was the only one looking there." I pressed my palms flat against the skiff's hull to steady myself. "They didn't hide the evidence from me, Bram. They *planted* me on top of it. Either they wanted it found by someone deniable—or they wanted to know the moment anyone came near it. Maybe both. Maybe I've been a tripwire this whole time. Every dive I made, every line I ran, I was ringing a bell for them."

The words hung in the dead air of the boathouse, and I thought of the recruiting office, the motionless flag, the architecture of stillness, and I felt the full sick weight of it settle into my bones: ten years of running, of never sleeping twice in the same bed, of wearing loneliness like armor plate—and none of it had mattered, because I had never been running *from* them. I had been swimming *in* their current the entire time. They hadn't needed to chase me. They had only needed to wait.

And the worst of it—the part I couldn't say out loud, the part that sat in my chest like floodwater—was the small, shameful relief underneath the horror. Because if they had led me here, then Tomas's letter had been true. *There's a ship they don't talk about.* He hadn't drowned himself in paranoia. He hadn't abandoned me. He had seen something, and he had hidden it where the river could keep it, and he had trusted me—me, specifically, across all that distance and all those years—to be the kind of person who would dig until she found it.

They had used me as bait. But I had also, without knowing it, been following his map. Both things were true, and I could not yet tell which one I was drowning in.

* * *

Bram gathered the documents with those relic-handler's fingers, squaring the edges, and I watched him and thought: *he knew the seal on sight. He knew the Vantage's role. He owns charts he shouldn't own.* Everyone in this story had been placed, it seemed—every piece on the board set down by some hand I couldn't see. The question that had been forming in me all evening finally climbed into my throat.

"Why are you here, Bram? Really. Not the tavern story, not the defector's pension, not any of it. The navy erased a nation and murdered a shipload of refugees and buried my brother's name—and you, an archivist, a paper-man, a man who smells of ink—I can *smell* it on you, under the salt—you turn up in a dockside tavern the same week the chest comes out of the river." I stepped closer. "Who told you to find me?"

Something moved behind his eyes—fear, or its well-dressed cousin, calculation—and outside, far downstream, a light appeared on the water. Small. Moving. The running lamp of a boat coming up the channel against the ebb, where no honest fisherman worked at this hour.

Bram blew out the lantern.

"Bram—"

"Later," he whispered. "I swear to you, later, everything. But right now we need to decide what we do with a chest that six tides from now will be under twenty feet of monsoon swell—and whether we're still here when whoever owns that light comes to collect what they planted."

The light grew. The river kept its dead, and apparently its appointments.

I put my hand on the chest's cold lead lid and waited.
