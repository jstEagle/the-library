## 6. Harbor of Small Betrayals

The harbor at Saltmere was the kind of place that smelled like an apology—tar and rot and old fish, all of it trying to cover something worse underneath. Bram had known harbors like it his whole life. He'd grown up in one, catalogued a hundred more in naval survey charts, and learned long ago that every port town kept its sins the way the river kept its dead: submerged, patient, waiting for someone careless enough to go looking.

He left Elodie mending the dredge net in the boathouse they'd rented under a false name, her hands moving with that fierce, unthinking competence he was beginning to find dangerous to watch, and walked into Saltmere alone.

The tavern he wanted sat at the end of the fish-quay, leaning toward the water like a drunk leaning on a friend's shoulder. Its sign had once depicted a gull; now it was a gray smear of wood that might have been anything. Inside, the air was thick enough to chew—pipe smoke, spilled ale, the wet-wool smell of men who worked the water and lied about what they found there.

Bram ordered nothing. He simply stood in the doorway until the room noticed him, which took about as long as it takes a candle to gutter.

In the corner booth, half-shadowed behind a hanging lantern of green glass, sat the man he'd come to find. Oskar Vell. Smuggler, chart-broker, occasional informant for whoever paid best that week. Bram had dealt with him twice before, in another life, when Bram had still carried navy credentials and Vell had still pretended to carry scruples.

Vell looked up, and his face did something complicated—recognition first, then recalculation, then a smile that arrived late and stayed too long.

"Well," Vell said. "Dead men do walk."

"Only on business." Bram slid into the booth opposite him. The table between them was scarred with knife-marks and ring-stains, a document of small violences. "You look prosperous."

"And you look like a man who needs something." Vell turned his cup slowly on its saucer, a habit Bram remembered—the man thought better with his hands busy. "Which is interesting, because the last time I saw you, you were leaving the Archives with nothing but your coat and your reputation for being difficult."

"My reputation travels faster than I do."

"It does. That's part of the problem." Vell leaned forward, lowering his voice beneath the tavern's noise. "There are people asking after you, archivist. Not navy—not exactly. Contractors. The kind who don't wear uniforms because uniforms can be identified. They came through here four days ago asking about a woman diving wrecks alone in the lower channel. And about anyone who might be traveling with her."

Bram kept his face still. It was a skill he'd perfected over years of sitting across from admirals' aides while they explained, gently, why certain documents could not exist. *Stillness is a language*, his old instructor used to say. *Learn to speak it fluently and you will survive rooms that kill honest men.*

"And what did you tell them?" Bram asked.

"What was there to tell? A diver works alone. Divers are common as gulls." Vell shrugged. "But I'll be honest with you, since honesty is apparently the fashion this evening—I don't enjoy having contractors ask me questions. It makes me want to have answers ready. Answers that keep me breathing."

"That's why I'm here. To give you some."

Bram set a folded paper on the table between them. He didn't open it. Vell's eyes went to it anyway, hungry and quick, the way a man looks at a purse dropped in the street.

"What is it?"

"A list. Archive designations, mostly. Records from the eastern annex that were never catalogued properly—deliberately misfiled, actually, by clerks who were paid to lose them. Shipping manifests. Customs waivers. Cargo declarations for vessels that officially never made port." Bram let that sit. "There are collectors who would pay dearly for proof that certain merchant houses built their fortunes on smuggling during the blockade years. Certain houses that are now very respectable and very litigious."

Vell's fingers had stopped turning his cup. "You're selling me ammunition."

"I'm selling you leverage. There's a difference. Ammunition gets fired; leverage gets hoarded." Bram tapped the paper once, lightly. "In exchange, I need charts. Escarpment currents, the deep channels along the ridge, tide tables for the coves above the drowned shelf. Old surveys if you have them—pre-war, before the navy redrew everything."

Something flickered across Vell's face—too fast for Bram to name it, but slow enough for him to notice, and noticing was the whole of his trade now. He had spent eleven years in the naval archives learning to read documents, and three years since learning to read men, and the second skill was harder because men rewrote themselves constantly, amending their own records without leaving a trace.

"The escarpment," Vell said carefully. "That's Corvane water."

"The escarpment is nobody's water. That's rather the point of it."

"The escarpment is where ships go to stop existing." Vell's voice had dropped further. "You know what's up there. Everyone who reads charts knows something's up there. The navy closed the shelf after the war, posted exclusion notices, and then quietly paid off every salvage crew that ever got curious. And now a defector archivist walks into my tavern asking for current tables for exactly that stretch of coast, traveling with a woman who pulls wrecks out of rivers for a living." He shook his head slowly. "I'm not stupid, Bram. Whatever you two have found—or think you've found—it's worth killing for. I can smell it. Killing-for-it has a smell."

Bram held his gaze. "Then you understand why I'm offering what I'm offering. This stays between us."

"Between us." Vell smiled again, and this time the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Of course. Between us."

They settled terms in the low, ritual language of such transactions—Bram would send the full list within the day; Vell would deliver the charts by dawn, wrapped in oilcloth, left in the hull of the derelict skiff moored at the third quay post. No names written down. No promises spoken aloud beyond the ones neither of them believed.

Bram rose to leave, and Vell caught his sleeve.

"For what it's worth," the smuggler said, and for one strange moment his voice lost its oily smoothness and sounded almost human, almost tired, "whatever you're diving for up there—some things stay sunk for a reason. Not because of guilt. Because of peace. You pull up the wrong thing, and it isn't just you that drowns."

"I know," Bram said. "I've already drowned once. Everything since is just swimming."

* * *

He walked back along the quay with the fog coming in off the water, thinking not about Vell but about Elodie.

She was in the boathouse when he returned, still working the net, lamplight catching the salt-dried rope burns on her knuckles. She didn't look up when he entered, but she knew—he could tell by the smallest pause in her hands, a hitch in the rhythm like a skipped heartbeat.

"You were gone two hours," she said.

"I found us a way through the escarpment currents. Charts. Pre-war surveys."

"From whom?"

"A broker. He deals in old documents." Bram hung his coat on the peg by the door, arranging his movements into the shape of casualness. Lying well required choreography; he'd learned that too. "It cost me, but not money."

Elodie looked up then. Her eyes were gray-green, the color of the channel in bad weather, and they held his the way a current holds a swimmer—not violently, but absolutely.

"He deals in old documents," she repeated. "And you knew him already."

"I knew *of* him. From my time in the service." True, technically. He was getting better at truths that bent without breaking. "Elodie—these charts matter. The currents above the shelf are vicious. Without them, we dive blind into water that moves like a millrace."

"I believe you." She returned to the net. "I just notice you didn't answer the question I actually asked."

He stood there a moment, coat half-hung, caught in the small dishonesty of the gesture. Then he finished hanging it and crossed to the workbench, busying himself with the lamp wick.

"I answered enough," he said. "Some doors, Elodie, are better left closed until we're past them."

"Mm." She tied off a knot with unnecessary force. "My brother used to say that. Right before he joined the navy."

The words landed harder than she probably intended—or perhaps exactly as hard as she intended; he was still learning the calibrations of her cruelty, which was never casual and always precise. Bram said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't add another plank to the floor he was building between them, plank by plank, telling himself it was scaffolding.

That night he lay awake in the loft above the boathouse listening to rain start against the roof, and told himself the same story he'd been telling himself since the archive: *I am protecting her. What she doesn't know can't be pried out of her.* It was even true. It was also true that Vell had looked at him for one moment too long, and that the word *contractors* had been spoken aloud in a tavern where anyone might have been listening, and that Bram had walked away from that table feeling like a man who had just signed something without reading it.

Sleep came eventually, thin and unsatisfying, like broth made from nothing.

* * *

The charts were waiting at dawn, exactly as promised, wrapped in oilcloth in the derelict skiff's hull. Bram collected them before the fishing crews launched, walking the quay in the gray light with gulls screaming overhead like the world's most cynical audience.

He didn't open the package until he was back in the boathouse. Elodie was still asleep in the loft; he heard her breathing change when the door opened, the way sleepers surface halfway when a familiar sound enters a room, then sink again.

At the workbench, he unwrapped the oilcloth.

Three charts, rolled tight. Two were exactly what he'd asked for—pre-war hydrographic surveys of the escarpment shelf, annotated in a dead cartographer's careful hand, showing the deep channels and the treacherous counter-currents that ran along the ridge like veins. Worth the price. Worth double.

The third chart was newer. Much newer. Naval issue, by the watermark—the kind of thing that should not have left a chartroom, let alone surfaced in a smuggler's package. And across its upper margin, in fresh ink, someone had written coordinates. Six sets of them, bracketed together, each annotated with a date.

Tide dates. The next six tides.

Bram stared at it for a long moment while the rain ticked against the boathouse roof and the river breathed against the pilings below.

It wasn't an accident. Nothing Vell did was an accident. The old smuggler had included it deliberately—a message, or a warning, or a confession. *This is what's coming for you. This is where they'll be. Now you know, and knowing makes you complicit in whatever you choose to do next.*

Or—and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome—it was bait. Vell selling him reassurance wrapped around a hook. *Look how helpful I am. Look how I warn you. Trust me further.*

Either way, the meaning was unmistakable: Corvane's fleet had schedules. The fleet had coordinates. And somewhere between the tavern booth and this oilcloth, word of two travelers asking after the escarpment had traveled faster than any boat could carry it.

Bram sat down slowly on the workbench stool.

He should wake her. That was the honest arithmetic—wake her, show her the chart, say *we've been sold, we may already be followed, we need to move now and assume every plan is compromised.* She deserved that. She'd earned it with rope-burned hands and a brother's ghost and the way she'd looked at him last night and said *I just notice you didn't answer the question I actually asked.*

But if he woke her with this, she would want to abandon the dive window entirely. Six tides, and the monsoon behind them like a closing door. If they ran, they ran forever—if they ran at all. And if they stayed and the fleet came early, they needed every hour of preparation, every advantage, and the advantage began with knowing the enemy's timetable while the enemy didn't know theirs.

*What she doesn't know can't be pried out of her.*

The old lie, worn smooth as river stone from handling.

Bram folded the naval chart closed, hiding the fresh ink inside the older paper, and rolled all three charts together. He would tell her about the currents, the channels, the dive windows marked in the cartographer's hand. All true. All useful. And the rest—the coordinates, the dates, the sold secret sitting in his chest like swallowed lead—he would carry alone, because carrying things alone was the one skill he had never managed to unlearn from the archives, where a man's whole career could depend on what he chose not to file.

Outside, the rain thickened. Downriver, invisible beyond the fog, the escarpment waited with its drowned secrets, and somewhere on gray water a fleet was already turning toward them with the patience of tide itself.

Bram rolled the charts, tucked them under his arm, and climbed the ladder to wake Elodie with the truth he'd chosen—which was to say, with most of it.

Above him, her breathing had already changed. She was awake. He couldn't see her face in the dark of the loft, only the shape of her stillness, and he wondered—with the first real cold settling into his stomach—how long she had been lying there listening, and how much of his silence she had already learned to read the way he read documents: not for what they said, but for what they were careful not to.
