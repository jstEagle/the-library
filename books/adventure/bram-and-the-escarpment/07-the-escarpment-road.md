## 7. The Escarpment Road

The escarpment road was older than the war and meaner than either of us.

It climbed out of the river country in switchbacks cut into red clay, the kind of track that carters avoided and smugglers memorized, and it went up through country that smelled of crushed fennel and hot stone. Below us the river unspooled silver-brown, wide as a lake in places, hiding everything it had ever taken. I kept my eyes on it longer than I meant to. Somewhere down there, under forty feet of green water and silt, lay the sealed hold of the *Vessany's Pride*, and somewhere inside that hold lay the last word of a story that had already cost me one brother.

Six tides. That was what Bram had said, counting them off on his fingers like a man who had learned to measure time in water. Four left now. The monsoon's leading edge was still out over the open sea, a bruise-colored wall we could see from the high ground, too far to fear yet, too close to forget.

"You're limping," Bram said behind me.

"I'm favoring," I said. "Limping is when you complain about it."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if he'd let it finish. I'd noticed that about him in three days of travel: he started every honest thing—a laugh, an answer, an apology—and then caught it halfway and swallowed it, like a diver who keeps testing the surface with one hand before he'll come all the way up.

We walked. The mules we'd bought in Harrow's Ferry were carrying the chest instead of us, and they were bitter about it, rolling their eyes at every switchback as if the lead-lined weight on their backs were a personal insult. I didn't blame them. I'd carried it two miles myself the first night, before I understood what it weighed—not the metal, but the names inside it. Tomas Ravensworth, witness. My brother's name in navy ink, in a manifest for a ship the maps said had never existed.

The escarpment rose around us in long shelves of limestone, terraced by hands dead a thousand years, planted now with olive trees gone half-wild. Wind came up off the lowlands and moved through them so the whole hillside whispered. It was beautiful country. I hated it a little for that. Beautiful country was where people stopped running.

I hadn't stopped running in nine years. I wasn't going to start because the light was nice.

* * *

We made the ridge by late afternoon and found the shrine.

It was one of those roadside things the old Vessany folk had built before the war—before there was no such thing as Vessany folk anymore, officially speaking. A hollowed niche in the rock face, a carved lintel worn smooth, and inside, sheltered from a century of weather, a small stone figure of a woman holding a lamp out over water. Someone had painted her lips blue once, generations ago; the color had faded to a ghost of itself, like breath on glass.

Bram stopped in front of it the way a man stops when he's walked into a room and forgotten why.

"You know her," I said.

"Everyone from the coast knows her." His voice had gone careful, the way it did whenever the past reached up and touched him. "The Lampmother. She guides the drowned home. Or she guides the living away from where the drowned are. Depends which prayer you're saying." He touched the stone lintel with two fingers, briefly, almost guiltily, then took his hand back and shoved it in his pocket like he'd been caught stealing.

"You crossed yourself just now."

"I did not."

"With your fingers. On the stone."

"That was checking for weathering." He was already walking again, fast, up the shelf of road. "For structural purposes. Shrines fall on people."

I looked at the little woman with her ghost-blue lips, holding her lamp out over nothing, and I followed him, and I did not say what I was thinking, which was that he prayed like a man with something specific to be forgiven for.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the war's leftovers, its stones tumbled into a shape like a broken tooth. Bram built a small fire—smaller than he wanted, bigger than I liked—and cooked rice with dried fish and a fistful of the wild fennel that grew everywhere on the slopes, and it was, annoyingly, good. He cooked the way he did everything else: competently, quietly, with the air of a man performing a task he'd done in worse places for worse company.

I sat with my back against a tower stone and cleaned the rust off my dive knife and watched him over the fire.

Three days ago I'd pulled a knife on him in a tavern doorway, and he'd talked me down with facts and flattery in equal measure, and I still wasn't sure which of the two had actually worked. Three days ago I hadn't known his real name was Bramwell Ashe, or that he'd served eleven years in the naval archives at Corvane's own headquarters, or that the reason he knew the Vessany seal on sight was that he'd spent a decade cataloguing the paperwork of a country the navy had erased. He'd told me all of it in fragments, on the road, doling out his history like a miser paying a debt—one coin at a time, wincing each time.

He owed me more than coins. He owed me the whole truth, and I could feel the shape of what he was still holding back, the way you feel a hole in the dark water before your lantern finds it.

"Bram."

"Mm."

"What page are you not reading me?"

He didn't look up from the fire. "I've shown you everything relevant."

"Relevant according to whom?"

"According to me." He stirred the rice. "Which is the only authority I have left, so I guard it jealously."

That was the other thing about him. He answered honestly enough to make you stop asking. It was a skill. I recognized it because it was mine.

I went back to my knife. The wind moved through the olives below us, whispering, and far off to the east the monsoon's bruise sat on the horizon, patient as a creditor.

* * *

I woke once in the black hours, the way I always woke—fast, silent, hand on the knife—and heard the fire down to coals and Bram's bedroll empty.

For a moment I lay still and listened. No footsteps. No voices. Just wind, and the small sounds of cooling stone, and somewhere downslope the nervous complaint of one of the mules.

I got up without noise and went along the terrace of road toward where the mule had smelled him. The moon was down; the stars were hard and close; the escarpment dropped away to my left into a darkness so complete it felt solid, a cliff falling into night instead of sea. And there, twenty paces along the path, crouched against the rock face with his back to me, was Bram—and he was burning something.

A page. Small, torn from a notebook, cupped in both hands against the wind, feeding itself to a match flame in slow orange bites. I saw his face lit from below, and what was on it was not the calm archivist mask. It was naked. Grief, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. His lips were moving. Not words I could catch. A name, maybe. Maybe several.

Then the flame reached his fingers and he hissed and crumbled the last corner to ash between his palms, and ground it into the dirt with his boot heel, thorough, methodical, destroying even the ash—

—and turned, and found me standing there.

Neither of us moved for a long moment. The wind pushed between us, cold off the drop.

"What was it?" I said.

"Nothing." Too fast. Too smooth. He was rebuilding the mask even as I watched, laying the calm back over his face plank by plank. "An old note. A supply list. It's nothing, Elodie."

"Supply lists don't make men pray."

"I wasn't praying."

"You were." I came closer. Close enough to see the powder of ash still gray on his fingertips, worked into the creases of his knuckles like something that had been there a long time. "You were praying over a supply list at three in the morning, alone, on a cliff, and then you burned it and ground the ashes into the road. Show me another page from the same notebook, then. If it's nothing, it's nothing."

His jaw tightened. For a second—the length of a match flame—I thought he'd do it. Thought he'd hand me the notebook and let me read whatever he was carrying, whatever name he'd been saying into the fire. I saw the want move through him like current under ice.

Then it froze over.

"It's nothing," he said, and his voice was level and pleasant and completely closed, and I understood suddenly that this was the most dangerous thing about him. Not the archive knowledge, not the navy protocols, not whatever skills had let him defect from Corvane's own citadel alive. This. The door that shut without a sound.

"Fine," I said.

"Elodie—"

"No. Fine. It's nothing." I turned back toward camp. "But hear me, Bramwell. When we get to that hold—if we get to that hold—you will not be keeping anything from me that touches my brother. Not a page, not a name, not a guess. You can keep your secrets about yourself. Keep them all. But Tomas is mine, and anything you know about why he died comes to me whole, or you can walk back down this road alone and explain to Admiral Corvane's fleet what you're doing on it."

Behind me, silence. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats against it.

"There were names on the page," he said finally, very quietly. "That's all I'll say tonight. Names. And one of them was not your brother's, and I thank God for it, and I burned it because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because the last time I failed to destroy a piece of paper with names on it, everyone whose name was on it died. So I've developed habits."

The wind came up the escarpment face and moved through us both.

"And the other names?" I asked. "The ones that weren't spared?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. The ash on his knuckles answered for him, ground in deep, worked into the skin.

"We walk at first light," he said. "The dive window above the hold opens at slack tide tomorrow evening, and after that the squalls come early. We'll want the descent rigged before dusk." He picked up his bedroll and walked past me toward the fire, and paused there, half-turned, his face unreadable in the coal-light. "I'm not lying to you, Elodie. I'm rationing the truth. There's a difference, and someday—maybe—I'll show you the ledger."

"Someday's a tide too far," I said. "Four left."

Something moved across his face then—something that wasn't the mask, wasn't grief either. Closer to wonder, unwilling, like a man surprised by warmth in a room he'd braced himself to find cold.

"Four left," he agreed softly. "Get some sleep. I'll take the watch."

I didn't sleep. I lay on my bedroll watching the coals breathe and listening to him sit the watch with his back straight against the broken tower, and I hated how much steadier the camp felt with him in it. Nine years I'd traveled alone because alone was safe, because alone couldn't be betrayed, couldn't be sold to a fleet, couldn't burn pages full of names behind your back. Alone was armor, and I had worn it so long I'd forgotten it was heavy.

Now there was a man ten feet away who knew the way through Corvane's front gate, who cooked fennel rice like a gift, who prayed over the dead in secret and lied badly and beautifully about it—and I caught myself, in the dark, doing arithmetic I had no business doing. How many days to the coast, after. Whether the salvage season ran long that year. Whether a person could anchor anywhere at all and still be herself.

I rolled over and faced the stone wall and told myself it was the exhaustion talking.

Below the escarpment, far off across the black water, a single light appeared where no light should be—out past the river mouth, past the shoals, steady and white and wrong. A ship's lantern, riding at anchor where no honest ship anchored. Then a second. Then a third, strung out along the dark like beads on a wire.

They were two days ahead of where Bram's charts said the fleet could possibly be.

I sat up. "Bram."

"I see them." His voice had gone flat and quick, all the softness scoured out of it, and he was already pulling on his boots. "Count them for me. Slowly."

"One. Two. Three. There—four, further south, half-hidden by the headland."

"Four." He stood, staring down at the lights, and I heard something in his breathing I'd only heard once before, the night he first saw the seal on the chest: fear with a calculation running underneath it. "Corvane doesn't run four ships to catch a diver and a defector. Four ships means he thinks the evidence is worth a blockade." He turned to me, coal-light carving his face into halves. "Someone told him exactly where we're going. Not that we exist—where. To the hour."

"Who?"

And I watched him arrive at the same name I was arriving at, watched it land on him like cold water, and he said it anyway, quiet, like a confession:

"The smuggler. Harrow's Ferry. The charts I traded for." He closed his eyes. "God help me. I paid him in archive secrets, Elodie. I paid him in *names*."

Down on the black water, the four lights burned on, patient as the tide, and the first thin edge of dawn was coming up behind the monsoon like a blade being drawn.
