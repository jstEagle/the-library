## 8. Corvane

The flagship *Vigilant* rode at anchor off Harrow's Ferry with her running lights doused, and Admiral Edran Corvane stood at the stern rail watching the river do what rivers always did, which was carry things away and pretend they had never existed.

He had been awake since the third bell. Sleep had become, over the years, a country he could visit but no longer live in. He poured tea from the pot his steward left under a wool cozy—still hot, still bitter, still correct—and drank it standing up, because sitting down invited thought, and thought at this hour went where he could not afford to follow.

Below him, in the chartroom, the fleet was waking. Six hulls. Two salvage cranes, a dredge barge, an escort pair, and the *Vigilant* herself, old enough to remember the war and refitted enough to pretend she didn't. On paper, this was a routine recovery operation: unclaimed wreck, hazard to navigation, standard clearance. Corvane had signed the paperwork himself, in his own hand, because there were documents he would not ask another man to touch.

The wreck in question lay against the escarpment shelf in forty-one fathoms of cold brown water, and on every surviving chart of that stretch of river she appeared as nothing at all—a blank space, a surveyor's omission, one of the war's small polite silences.

She had been called the *Vessany's Pride*.

Corvane set down his cup and looked east, where the sky over the escarpment was going the color of old pewter. The monsoon was six days out. The barometers aboard three ships agreed on four; his own shoulder, which had been predicting weather since a shell fragment lodged near the joint twenty years ago, said five. It hardly mattered. What mattered was that the storm would come, and the storm would move silt, and the silt would close over whatever the dredge woman had found like a hand closing over a mouth.

If only the sea were as discreet as the silt.

"Admiral." Lieutenant Sess, his flag lieutenant, materialized at the chartroom door with the particular carefulness of a man who has learned to read his superior's moods by the set of his shoulders. "Signals. Priority traffic from the Ministry."

"Read it."

Sess read it. Corvane listened to words like *salvage rights*, *contested claim*, and *civilian diver*, and felt the familiar tightening behind his eyes that he had privately named, years ago, the headache of other people's honesty.

"The diver," Sess finished. "One Elodie Ravensworth. She works the unclaimed wrecks. No guild registration, no salvage license, no fixed address. The Harbor Office describes her as—" he consulted the flimsy, "—*transient*."

Transient. Corvane almost smiled. He had been transient once himself, in the way of young men who have not yet found the thing they will spend their lives defending or burying.

"And the chest?"

"Recovered, sir. We believe so. Our source at Harrow's Ferry confirms she raised something lead-lined off the barge site two nights ago."

Our source. Corvane turned the phrase over with distaste. The whole apparatus ran on sources now—harbor clerks, tavern keepers, smugglers paid in small mercies—and every one of them believed they served the navy out of patriotism or fear, when in truth they served it out of the same hunger that moved everyone: to matter. He had learned long ago that you could buy anything in this country except silence, and silence was the only thing worth having.

"Course for the cove," he said. "Slow ahead through the narrows. I want the escort pair ahead of us and the dredge barge aft. And Lieutenant—no signals in the clear. Nothing that names the site."

"Aye, sir." Sess hesitated. "The Ministry asks again whether we intend to attempt full recovery of the, ah, cargo."

Corvane looked at him. Sess was twenty-six, clever, ambitious in the bloodless way of clever young men, and entirely innocent of what the word *cargo* meant when applied to that particular hull. Corvane envied him the innocence the way one envies a man his last good year.

"There is no cargo," Corvane said. "There is a wreck. And wrecks are cleared, not recovered. Make our intentions *clearance operations* in all correspondence. The storm will be cited as the urgency. That much, at least, is true."

It was true. That was the thing nobody understood about lies—the durable ones were built on true bones. The monsoon *was* coming. The wreck *was* a hazard. The orders *had* been issued by a government that no longer existed, in a war that everyone agreed must never be repeated, to a captain who had obeyed them and then drowned with his ship and his conscience together. Every fact in the file was accurate. Only the shape of them, laid end to end, made a murder.

* * *

In the great cabin, after the fleet got underway, Corvane unlocked the drawer he kept locked even from himself, in the sense that he opened it perhaps twice a year and hated it each time.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, was a copy of the order. Not the original—the original had burned, officially, along with the archive wing, in a fire whose cause was investigated for eleven days and concluded upon with impressive speed. This copy was in his own hand, taken down from dictation thirty-one years ago, when he had been a commander with steady hands and a superior named Vell who had said, in the flat voice men used for weather reports:

*The Pride cannot be allowed to make port. There are eleven hundred souls aboard who saw what happened at the Vessany landing, and eleven hundred witnesses are eleven hundred futures for a war we have just paid everything to end. You will see to it.*

Corvane had been thirty-four. He had asked—one question, one, he had earned that much—*And the crew, sir?*

And Vell had looked at him with something that was not quite pity and said, *The crew knew their duty, Commander. See that you know yours.*

He had seen to it. He had drafted the routing that put the *Pride*'s escort exactly where it needed to fail to be. He had certified the convoy schedule that gave the scuttle party their window. He had not planted the charges—he had never needed to be the kind of man who planted charges; he was the kind of man who made sure the right hands were available—and when the ship went down in the dark with all hands and all those eleven hundred futures, he had sat at his desk until dawn and then written the incident report that closed the matter for a generation.

Eleven hundred and forty-two souls. He knew the number the way other men knew their wedding anniversary. Some mornings it was a weight. Most mornings it was arithmetic. He had never decided which was worse, and suspected the question itself was a luxury.

Because here was the thing—the thing he would say aloud, if there were anyone alive he could say it to: it had worked.

The war ended. The peace held. The maps were redrawn and Vessany was erased—not out of cruelty but out of surgery; a nation is a wound that keeps bleeding unless you close it, and closing it meant pretending it had never been open. Four million people had died in that war. Corvane had stood at the armistice table and watched the signatures go down and understood, with a clarity that had never left him, that the peace was not built on justice. It was built on exhaustion. And exhaustion is a foundation that holds only so long as no one digs beneath it.

The *Pride* was buried beneath it. So were the eleven hundred and forty-two. So was Tomas Ravensworth—a name he had learned only recently, from a manifest extract, a junior signals officer who had seen too much of the scuttle operation's paperwork and had written one letter home and then vanished into the navy's machinery like a coin into deep water. Corvane had read the letter's intercept summary himself. *They're lying about the ship. I can prove it. Don't trust anyone in uniform.* The boy had been twenty-three. He had trusted someone anyway.

Corvane closed the drawer.

He did not feel guilt, precisely. Guilt was for acts; his had been omissions, certifications, routings—the white-collar sin of the well-ordered mind. What he felt was closer to stewardship. Someone had to carry this. Someone had to stand watch over the lie the way a lighthouse keeper stands watch over a dangerous coast, knowing the light misleads but saves more than it kills. If he laid it down, who would pick it up? The ministers who had inherited the peace without paying for it? The correspondents who would print the truth in bold type and go home to their suppers while the treaties burned?

No. The truth was a luxury of stable times. These were not stable times. Half a dozen powers across the water still remembered Vessany fondly, the way one remembers a weapon. Let the wreck speak, and you did not get a memorial—you got a casus belli. You got the border provinces aflame within a season. You got the war again, with better guns, and this time no one would sign anything, because this time both sides would know exactly what the other was capable of.

He had done the arithmetic a thousand times. Eleven hundred and forty-two against four million. It was not even a calculation. It was a rounding error with a conscience.

The trouble—the eternal trouble—was that the dead did not consent to being rounded.

* * *

At midday the fleet passed the ferry crossing, and Corvane came up to the forebridge to look at the town.

Harrow's Ferry clung to the riverbank the way such towns did: crooked piers, tarred roofs, drying nets strung like laundry between poles. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin straight lines, which told him the wind was light and the monsoon still offshore. Children waved from the seawall, because children always waved at warships, having not yet learned what warships were for.

His flag lieutenant joined him at the rail. "The diver, sir. Ravensworth. Our source says she's traveling inland with a companion. A man. Description matches—" Sess consulted his folder with the delicacy of a priest handling relics, "—the archivist. The defector."

Corvane went very still.

Bram. He had not heard that name spoken in nine years, and hearing it now was like hearing a floorboard creak in a house he had emptied decades ago. The archivist. The junior cataloguer who had pulled the wrong file—or the right one—and who had tried, with the touching incompetence of honest men, to save a copy of the sinking order. Who had cost the service two loyal officers, one fire, and a decade of quiet vigilance, and who had then walked out of the country with nothing but his memory and his shame.

So. The diver had found the chest, and the archivist had found the diver. Corvane assembled it the way he assembled all intelligence, patiently, without flinching: the chest bore the seal; the seal would mean nothing to a scavenger and everything to a cataloguer; and somewhere in the drowned dark of that hull lay the sealed hold, and in the sealed hold lay the captain's log and the original orders, and if those reached daylight—

He made himself finish the sentence. He always made himself finish the sentence. Cowardice, in his experience, was mostly the habit of leaving sentences unfinished.

—if those reached daylight, the peace died. Not weakened. Died. And the four million became a number that had bought nothing.

"Sir?" Sess said. "Your orders regarding the defector?"

Corvane watched the town slide past. A gull hung motionless over the ferry pier, riding air he couldn't see.

"The same orders as ever, Lieutenant. Recovery of naval property. Apprehension of persons interfering with naval operations." He paused. "I want it understood throughout the fleet that no harm comes to the civilian. Ravensworth. She is a scavenger who has stumbled into deep water; that is a rescue, not an execution. Is that clear?"

"Perfectly, sir." A beat. "And the defector?"

Corvane looked south, toward the escarpment, toward the blank space on the chart where a ship full of witnesses had gone down obediently, on schedule, in the dark.

"The defector is a different matter," he said. "He has already chosen his side. Men who choose are responsible for the choosing." He turned back to the chartroom. "Signal the dredge barge. When we reach the site, I want demolition charges placed against the hull—not salvage cuts, Lieutenant. Demolition. The wreck comes down, the hold collapses, and the monsoon does the rest. We will log it as storm-preventive clearance."

Sess's pen had stopped moving. "Sir—with respect. If there are documents aboard—"

"There are no documents aboard," Corvane said mildly. "There is a wreck. You have my wording."

But the young man lingered, and Corvane, who had spent thirty years reading junior officers the way farmers read weather, saw the question forming behind his eyes like a squall line. Sess was not stupid. Stupid men never asked. It was the clever ones, the ones who added columns correctly, who eventually arrived at the sum and stood holding it with nowhere to put it down.

"Sir," Sess said carefully, "may I ask—what is it we're keeping down there? At the bottom?"

Corvane considered him for a long moment. Outside, the river glittered, indifferent and beautiful, carrying its secrets seaward with the patience of all water.

"You may ask," he said. "You may not have the answer. The answer is the one thing on this ship I am not authorized to give—even to myself, some days." He picked up his cup, found the tea cold, drank it anyway. "What you need to know is this: there is a peace above your head, Lieutenant, and it rests on a floor. Your job, my job, the fleet's job, is to make sure no one pries up the boards to see what's underneath. Because underneath is not treasure. Underneath is the reason the treasure was buried."

He turned to the chart table and began, in his own hand, drafting the demolition order.

"And when the storm comes," he added, without looking up, "we will be able to tell ourselves—we will even believe—that the sea took it. That is the arrangement. That has always been the arrangement."

The pen scratched. The fleet steamed on toward the escarpment, six hulls against the current, and behind them Harrow's Ferry shrank to a smudge of smoke on the southern shore, and ahead, waiting in the brown cold water of the cove, lay a door that Corvane had spent half his life pretending was not there.

Two tides, his shoulder said. Perhaps three.

He wrote faster.
