## 9. Tide One: The Drowned Bell

The tide turned at dawn without ceremony. It never did anything with ceremony; that was the river's oldest trick, to make its great movements look like accidents.

Elodie stood at the rail of the *Magpie* and watched the water change color beneath her—black going to green going to a bruised silver as the light came up behind the escarpment. The cove where the Vessany's Pride lay was not on any chart she'd ever bought or stolen. Bram had found it in the smuggler's charts, a penciled annotation in a dead man's hand: *deep hole, old iron, don't anchor*. Sixty fathoms of cold, still water cupped in the elbow of the escarpment like something the land had swallowed and tried to forget.

She checked her gauges for the fourth time. Air, reserve, the little brass depth gauge whose needle trembled when she breathed on it. The suit was old but honest. The knife at her calf was older and more honest.

"You don't have to go down first," Bram said behind her. He'd been awake all night; she could hear it in his voice, that particular sandpaper quality of a man who had argued with the dark and lost. "We could send the line down empty. See what comes back."

"And tell it what? *Please surface if you're a war grave?*" She pulled her hood up. "I've been diving wrecks since I was fifteen, Bram. This is what I'm for."

"That's what worries me."

She looked at him then—at the archivist's hands wringing each other, at the shadows under his eyes, at the way he kept glancing at the mouth of the cove as though the horizon might grow ships out of it. Two tides until Corvane's fleet, by the smuggler's arithmetic. Maybe less. There wasn't time for comfort, so she gave him honesty instead, which was the only currency she'd ever had enough of.

"Tomas is down there," she said. "Or the reason he's dead is. Either way, I'm going down to meet it."

* * *

The water took her the way it always did—a cold hand closing over the back of the neck, the world shrinking to the size of her own breathing. Down here there was no fleet, no chest, no six tides. There was only the rope in her fist and the slow rain of silt through her lamp beam, each particle catching the light like ash from a fire no one had put out.

She descended along the shot line they'd dropped at slack water yesterday, counting fathoms by feel because the gauge fogged past thirty. At forty the light died entirely and her world became a sphere ten feet across, blue-white at the center, black at the edges, her own heartbeat the loudest thing in creation.

At fifty-two fathoms her lamp found iron.

It came out of the dark gradually, the way large things do underwater—not appearing so much as being revealed, as though it had been waiting for her specifically. First a rail, furred with river rust the color of dried blood. Then a hull plate, riveted, enormous, sloping away beyond the reach of her beam. Then the name.

She knew it would be there. She'd read it in Bram's documents a dozen times. But knowing is one thing and seeing is another, and when her light crossed the letters—*VESSANY'S PRIDE*, each one taller than her hand, softened by sixty years of silt but still proud, still legible—Elodie made a sound into her regulator that was not a word and could not have been mistaken for one.

The ship lay on its starboard side, settled into the mud at a gentle angle, like a woman lying down to sleep who had simply never gotten up. No list of battle damage. No torn plating, no shell scars, none of the ragged violence she'd seen on war wrecks from here to the Salt Coast. The hull was whole. The hull was *intact*.

Ships that die in battle are wounded. Ships that die any other way are murdered.

Her hands were steady on the rail. Her hands were always steady; it was the rest of her that shook. She swam the length of the exposed flank, lamp sweeping, reading the iron the way Bram read paper. Cargo ports closed. Lifeboat davits swung out—empty, but swung out, meaning someone had tried. Meaning someone had been given time to try, or had taken it.

Then she reached the stern section, and stopped.

There, low on the port side, just above the buried rail, was a rupture. Not torn outward. Blown *inward*—no. She corrected herself, drifting closer, running her gloved fingers along the metal's curled lips. Blown from inside. The plates bent away from the hull, petals of a flower made of iron, and around them the rivets hadn't sheared—they'd been pushed, punched outward by a force that began within.

A diver knows what a mine looks like from outside. Elodie had spent her whole adult life reading wounds in steel, and this wound said one thing in the plain language of metal: the explosion had started *here*. Inside. Below the waterline, against the engine room bulkhead, in a place no shell fired from another ship could reach.

Scuttle charges. Placed by hand. By someone who had walked these decks with a satchel and a set of orders.

She hung in the black water beside the wound in the hull and felt the awe drain out of her body like warmth, replaced by something colder and slower moving. She had come down expecting a battle. She had braced herself for the evidence of war—the excuse, the tragedy, the terrible arithmetic of two navies meeting in the wrong water. That, at least, would have had a shape she understood.

This was not a shape. This was a signature.

They hadn't sunk her. They'd *opened* her. And then let six hundred people drown in the dark, and written *lost with all hands, cause unknown* in a ledger somewhere, and erased a nation off the maps so thoroughly that even the sea forgot which country owned the wreck.

Elodie pressed her palm flat against the hull, over the place where the name used to be legible, and held it there. The iron was cold as a coin left all winter on a windowsill. Somewhere under her glove, under sixty years of silt, were the last fingerprints of people who had believed the navy meant rescue.

"I see you," she said into her mask. The words came out as bubbles, meaningless, silver. She said them anyway.

* * *

She worked the wreck for an hour, because grief without work is just drowning in slow motion. She tagged the scuttle breach with a wax pencil on her slate, sketched the charge pattern—three points, she found, tracing the bulkhead lines, a professional's triangle, placed by someone who understood ships well enough to kill one cleanly. She photographed what could be photographed in the murk. She collected a sample of the hull steel near the breach, sawing free a piece the size of her palm while some deep animal part of her brain screamed that she was taking flesh from a corpse.

And it was during this work, half-distracted, her mind running ahead to the sealed hold aft where the captain's papers would be, that she heard the bell.

At first she thought it was her own air. Regulators sing sometimes—a thin harmonic when the diaphragm wears, a sound divers learn to ignore. She paused, thumb on the valve, listening to her own breathing. Steady. Clean.

The sound came again.

One note. Low, round, bronze-deep, swelling out of the dark beyond her lamp beam and passing *through* her—she felt it in her teeth, in the bones of her inner ear, in the fluid of her spine—and fading away downstream into the black.

Elodie went absolutely still.

Sixty fathoms down. In water cold enough to kill an unprotected man in minutes. On a ship that had been dead longer than she'd been alive.

Bells do not ring themselves. Bells need a hand, or a wind, or a mechanism, and all three require a world with air in it. Every rational instrument she owned told her what she was hearing: silt shift, hull flex, gas venting from the sediment, the ship settling by fractions as the river rearranged its grip. She had catalogued a hundred such sounds in a hundred wrecks. She knew their names.

But knowing the name of a thing has never once stopped it from being true.

The bell rang again—closer now, or louder, forward, toward the bow—and this time she caught the rhythm. Not random. Not the arrhythmic groan of settling iron. Three strokes. A pause, long enough to hold a breath. Three strokes again.

She knew that pattern. Every child raised on this river knew it, the way children raised near mountains know avalanche weather. Three strokes and a silence was the dead-march toll, the ring for a soul departing, rung by harbor chapels from the escarpment to the sea. Her grandmother had described it once, describing a funeral Elodie had been too young to attend: *three and a rest, three and a rest, until everyone who loved them has counted themselves among the mourners.*

Her lamp beam shook. She steadied it with both hands and made herself swim forward, toward the sound, because the alternative was to swim up, and she had built her entire life on the principle that she did not run from water. Water had taken Tomas. Water did not get to take her nerve too.

The forecastle deck rose out of the silt ahead, and there, bolted to its broken frame, hung the ship's bell.

It was green with age, crusted white with mineral bloom, tilted on its bracket like a hanged man's head. As her light touched it, it was still. Perfectly, insultingly still. No motion. No vibration she could see, though she drifted close enough to touch it—did touch it, her glove meeting cold bronze, and the contact sent a shiver up her arm that had nothing to do with temperature.

From somewhere below her—below the deck, below the mud, from a depth that should not contain anything but darkness and pressure—the bell rang a third time. Three strokes. The silence after stretched out long and full, a held breath, a room waiting for mourners to finish counting.

Elodie backed away. She was not ashamed of it. Some part of her, the part that had been diving since she was fifteen, the part that respected the water the way you respect a sleeping animal, understood that she had just been *noticed*.

She turned for the shot line.

And that was when her lamp caught the porthole.

It was a foot from her face, set in the superstructure above the forecastle, its glass miraculously unbroken behind a skin of silt. Through the glass—dim, distorted, green-gold in her beam—she could see the interior of a cabin. A bunk frame. A shelf. And pinned to the bulkhead inside, preserved from the rot by sixty years of cold and dark, a square of paper.

White. Still white. After everything, still white.

Her heart slammed against her ribs hard enough to fog her mask. Paper didn't survive like that. Paper dissolved, disintegrated, became silt like everything else. Unless it had been sealed. Unless someone had put it somewhere the water couldn't reach, and the porthole glass had held, and—

*Tomas wrote one letter,* she thought. *One desperate letter, and then he vanished.*

Her fingers were on the porthole before she'd decided to move them, testing the frame, finding it dogged shut from the inside. Of course it was. Sealed cabins kept their dead's secrets; that was the whole point of sealing them. She'd need tools, light, time—and time was the one thing the tide would not sell her. Above her, far above, the first gray suggestion of dawn was already bleeding into the surface, and every minute down here was a minute off Bram's count, off the fleet's approach, off the six tides the monsoon had promised to bury everything under.

She pressed her glove to the glass one last time, over the white square of paper, committing its position to memory the way she committed everything—by feel, by angle, by the fixed stars of the wreck around it. Aft of the foremast step. Starboard side, upper cabin. Third porthole from the break.

Then the bell rang again, and this time she did not flinch, because she finally understood what it was doing.

Three strokes. A rest. It wasn't haunting her. It was *counting* her. The way her grandmother had said—until everyone who loved them has counted themselves among the mourners. Sixty years the bell had waited in the dark for someone to come down and grieve properly, and now here she was, and the bell knew it, and the river knew it, and somewhere in the black water all around her the Vessany's Pride was holding its breath.

"Six tides," Elodie whispered to the drowned ship, to the bell, to the white paper behind the glass. "Give me six tides, and I'll read every name out loud."

She kicked for the line and rose through the black water with the bell's last stroke following her up like a hand on her ankle—three rings, and a silence, and in the silence, faint but unmistakable, carried on a current from the open channel behind her, the sound she had been dreading since the cove: engines. Diesel engines. More than one. Running slow and dark, coming upriver with the flood tide.

Corvane's fleet was not two tides out.

It was already here.
