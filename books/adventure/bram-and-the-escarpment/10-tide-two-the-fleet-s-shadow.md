## 10. Tide Two: The Fleet's Shadow

Bram heard the engines before Elodie surfaced.

He had built a fire in the lee of the wrecked barge—small, disciplined, fed with driftwood that burned hot and clean—and he was crouched over it warming his hands when the sound came up the channel on the flood tide. Not one engine. A chord of them, low and arrhythmic, the way a fleet sounds when it is trying not to be heard and failing at it, because no navy ever built has learned to whisper.

He killed the fire with a kick of wet sand and was moving before the last ember died.

Elodie broke the surface thirty feet out, black water sheeting off her shoulders, and he read her face in the half-light the way he had once read dispatches—fast, complete, no second pass needed. She'd heard them too. She swam for the skiff with the flat, economical strokes of a woman who had already stopped being afraid and started being furious, which was worse, and which Bram was beginning to understand was simply how she was built.

"Skiffs," she said, hauling herself over the gunwale, mask pushed up, voice stripped to its wire. "Two of them, maybe three. Sweep pattern—they're fanning out from the point." She wrung her hair with hands that did not shake. "That's not a patrol. That's a search line."

"It's a screen," Bram said. "Skiffs go first. Always."

She looked at him. In the dark her eyes caught what little light there was and held it, and he felt the question arrive in her before she spoke it—the same question that had been gathering in her for days now, tide by tide, every time he knew something he shouldn't.

"How do you know that?"

He gave her the answer he had prepared, the one that cost nothing: "Anyone who's read enough naval correspondence knows how they move."

It was a good lie. It had been a good lie for eleven days. And it was dying right in front of him, because Elodie Ravensworth did not blink at answers like that; she filed them, and he could feel himself accumulating in whatever ledger she kept behind those grey eyes, page by page, and ledgers like hers always got audited in the end.

* * *

They ran the skiff up a creek mouth that didn't exist on any chart worth trusting—a slot between two drowned cedars where the water went tea-dark and still—and Bram shipped the oars and listened. The engine-chord had changed. Deeper now. Slower. Behind the skiffs' high, searching whine, something heavy was making way upriver, something that displaced enough water to push a swell ahead of itself into the shallows, so that their hidden boat rocked gently against the cedar roots as if the river itself were flinching.

"That's not possible," Elodie said quietly. "You said six tides. The fleet was six tides out—I did the arithmetic myself off your charts."

"The charts were right." Bram kept his voice level and hated how easily it stayed level, how smooth the machinery of his composure still ran after all these years of disuse. "Someone sold us."

He watched her arrive at it. He watched her think of exactly one person who had handled charts recently, who had traded anything at all lately, whose fingers had been too quick with the rolled oilskin in the smuggler's back room at Harbor of Small Betrayals—and he watched her decide, deliberately, visibly, not to say the name yet. It was a mercy. He would have preferred the accusation. Accusations you could fight.

"Corvane doesn't run screens at night unless he expects contact," she said instead. "And he doesn't bring the heavy hulls up a channel this shallow unless he already knows where he's going." Her jaw worked. "He knows about the hold. He knows where we are. Two tides early, and he knows *where*."

Bram said nothing. The swell rocked the skiff. Somewhere upstream, faint as a memory, the drowned bell rang again—or he imagined it did; he had been imagining it since Elodie first described it, the way a man who has spent his life among paper begins to hear paper everywhere, rustling at the edge of things.

"We can't dive tomorrow," Elodie said. "Not with a search line working the cove. We've lost Tide Three."

"No," Bram said. "We haven't."

* * *

Here is what Bram Halloway knew that frightened him more than Corvane's fleet: he knew fleets.

Not from books. Books had been his alibi, his cover story, the soft scholarly shape he wore like a borrowed coat—*archivist, defector, man of paper*. But before the archives there had been four years in the naval signals service, four years of learning how a fleet thinks, and then six more years of learning how a fleet hunts, because after he tried to save the copy of the sinking order and failed, they had taught him the rest from the wrong side of it. He had spent six years being the thing a search line was designed to find. He knew its appetite. He knew its rhythm. He knew that Admiral Corvane, methodical as a tide table, always anchored his screens at first light and always—*always*, because the man believed order was a form of virtue—lit his command ship's recognition lamps at dusk, a double amber pulse repeated down the line, so that every vessel in his formation could find its neighbors in the dark.

Corvane called it discipline. Bram called it a signature. And a signature, once known, could be forged.

"There's a fishing station downstream," Bram said. "Past the point, on the far shore. Abandoned since the war—drying racks, a stone hearth, pitch-pine pilings." He was already pulling the chart roll from under the thwart, unrolling it across Elodie's knees, his finger finding the notch of coastline without hunting for it. "The whole point sits inside Corvane's sweep arc. When his skiffs work the cove tomorrow, they'll be looking north and east, toward us, toward the wreck. Their backs will be to that station."

Elodie studied the chart, then him. "So we run downstream tonight. While the screen's still forming."

"No." He heard his own voice drop into a register he hadn't used in years, clipped, certain, the voice of a man reading an order aloud, and it startled him, how close to the surface all of it still lived. "We stay. You dive Tide Three as planned, because the search line will pull *away* from the cove at dawn—that's the pattern, they expand outward from the anchor point. But they'll only pull away if they believe there's something out here worth finding *elsewhere*. Something warm. Something lit."

Understanding moved through her face slowly, like cold through deep water. "A signal fire."

"A signal fire," Bram agreed. "Lit at the fishing station, timed to the hour when their lookouts come on watch. Big enough to read at four miles. Wrong enough to be interesting." He met her eyes. "Corvane's skiffs will investigate. His picket will shift to cover the investigation. And for roughly five hours, the cove belongs to you."

"And when they get there," Elodie said, "and find an old hearth and some burnt wood—"

"They'll find evidence someone signaled a rendezvous. Ash, wick-stubs, maybe a scrap of oiled canvas I'll leave behind. They'll report contact-signs, not contact. Corvane doesn't ignore contact-signs. He'll widen the search, re-task his pickets, spend the whole day combing the far shore." Bram allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "He'll be thorough. Thank God he's thorough."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. The swell rocked them against the cedars. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever she was approaching something she'd decided not to say directly.

"You've done this before."

It wasn't a question. Questions have upward inflections; this had none. It landed flat and heavy as an anchor, and Bram felt the truth of the last eleven days shift beneath him, all the small competent tellings finally coming due—the way he'd read the patrol's sweep pattern at the chest site, the way he'd known which side of the escarpment the monsoon would hit first, the way he'd walked them through a naval checkpoint at Grayharbor with forged transit papers filled out in a hand so perfectly regulation that the corporal had actually complimented it.

"I've done this before," Bram said.

"Running from the navy."

"Yes."

"Not toward it. From it. For years." Her eyes hadn't left his face. "You weren't just an archivist who stole a document and fled. Archivists don't know how to build decoy fires. Archivists don't know watch rotations and recognition lamps and how a search line breathes." She leaned forward slightly, and the lantern-light between them made her look, for a moment, like someone interrogating a wreck through glass. "What were you, Bram?"

And there it was—the audit, arriving exactly on schedule—and Bram Halloway sat in a dark skiff with the fleet's swell rocking him gently and understood that he had perhaps an hour to decide how much of himself to surrender, and that every hour he delayed would cost him more than the confession itself.

But not yet. Not with Tide Three coming. Not with her needing to sleep before dawn and trust being a thing that, like a cutting charge, had to be placed precisely or it shattered everything around it.

"After tomorrow's dive," he said quietly. "I'll tell you everything. What I was. What I took. What it cost." He held her gaze because looking away would have been another lie. "You have my word. And I know what my word has been worth lately. But I'm asking you to spend one more night on credit."

Elodie looked at him for a long time. Then she picked up the chart, rolled it, and handed it back.

"Dawn, then," she said. "Build your fire."

* * *

They went downstream separately—he in the skiff, hugging the shadowed bank; she overland along the deer track above the tideline, carrying the coil of slow-match and the lamp oil they'd bought in the fishing village, because if the skiff was taken she should not be taken with it. That had been her condition, stated flatly while they loaded the boat: *if it goes wrong, you're the one they catch.* He had agreed. He had not told her that being the one they catch was a thing he had rehearsed for six years, that he kept a set of false papers sewn into his coat lining and a story prepared for three different ranks of interrogator, and that the preparation was the part of himself he most despised—not the running, but the readiness. The ease of it. The way fear had become, in him, a kind of craft.

The fishing station rose out of the dark like the ribs of something that had lain down and died facing the sea. Drying racks in broken rows, bleached bone-grey. A stone hearth taller than a man, built by people who had expected to keep lighting it forever. The smell of old salt and old smoke lived in the stones themselves.

Bram worked by feel and by the thin starlight, and his hands remembered everything.

He stacked the pitch-pine pilings he'd pried loose in a cone against the hearth's windward face—dense at the base, hollow-cored, a structure that would burn fast and tall and throw embers a hundred feet into the sky. He soaked the base with lamp oil but left the top third dry, so the flame would climb rather than gutter. He laid the slow-match in a damp trench thirty feet downwind, weighted with stones, its burning end crawling toward the pyre at a speed he had calculated twice: ignition at approximately the third hour of the middle watch, when Corvane's lookouts came fresh on duty, eyes adjusted, bored, hungry for anything at all to report.

Then, kneeling in the cold sand, he added the details that separated a signal from a bonfire.

A scatter of wick-stubs, cut from candle-ends, half-burned—evidence of waiting. A scrap of oiled canvas, torn, not cut, tucked under a rack leg. Boot prints in the soft ground below the tideline, made with his own boots but worn on the wrong feet for ten minutes to alter the stride, a trick from a man he had known in the signals service who had defected two years before Bram did and vanished so completely that Bram sometimes wondered if he'd invented him. He hesitated over the last item—a strip of cloth, dyed the particular faded blue of Vessany's merchant marine, knotted to the rack—and placed it anyway, because ambiguity was the whole art. Too clear a message and Corvane would know it was staged. Too vague and the skiffs would row past. The fire had to say exactly this: *someone was here, someone was signaling, someone may still be near.*

It had to say: *look here, not there.*

He struck the match at the appointed minute, touched it to the slow-match, and walked backward down the beach without hurrying, because hurrying was how men left sign, and he climbed the bluff behind the station and lay flat in the bracken with the wind in his face and waited.

At the third hour of the middle watch, the fire took.

It went up with a sound like a held breath released—a roar, a column of orange light standing on the dark water, sparks streaming landward on the wind like a flight of startled birds. Four miles up the channel, Bram knew, that light would bloom on a hundred retinas at once. Bells would ring. Voices would sharpen. Somewhere aboard Corvane's command ship, a man trained all his life to believe that order was virtue would receive a report of an unauthorized signal fire on his blind shore, and his orderly mind would begin, immediately and inevitably, to organize itself around it.

Bram lay in the bracken and watched the skiffs turn. He saw their running lights come up—one, two, three, then a fourth from farther out, the picket detaching from the line exactly as the pattern demanded—and he felt the old terrible pleasure rise in him, the clean joy of a machine doing what he'd built it to do, and underneath the pleasure, as always, the shame, because the machine was himself, and the skill was real, and the skill had been paid for.

Paid for by Tomas Ravensworth, among others. Paid for by a copy of an order he had catalogued and failed to save. Paid for in six years of sleeping in boots.

*You weren't just an archivist,* she had said. No. He had been the archive's ghost, the man who knew where every body was filed, and the navy had chased him across three coastlines for it, and somewhere in those six years of running he had become very, very good at exactly one thing: making large, powerful, certain organizations look in the wrong direction.

The fire collapsed inward with a shower of gold. Downstream, the skiffs' lights converged on the fishing station like moths, and the search line breathed outward, away from the cove, away from the drowned bell, away from the sealed hold where a captain's log waited in the dark with all its names.

Five hours. He had bought her five hours, maybe six.

Bram got up out of the bracken and turned toward the cove, and that was when he saw, far up the channel beyond the turning skiffs, past the screen, at the very limit of what the night would show: the command ship's recognition lamps, double amber, pulsing patiently down the length of the fleet like a heartbeat.

And beside them, brief and unmistakable, a third light. White. Steady. Burning on the flagship's signal bridge in direct violation of blackout discipline, visible for one full second before it went dark.

A heliograph flash. Aimed—not at the fleet, not at the burning station downstream.

At the bluff. At him.

Someone aboard that ship knew precisely where Bram Halloway was lying in the wet grass. Someone wanted him to know they knew. And the message, when his mind finished translating the flash-pattern he had hoped never to see again—the old archival distress code, the one reserved for recovered assets, the one that meant *we can wait, we know you'll come home*—curdled his blood colder than the wind off the water.

Because only three people alive had ever been taught that code.

One was dead. One was himself.

Which meant Admiral Corvane had found the third—and had brought him to the cove to make the offer Bram had been dreading since the day he stole the first page.
