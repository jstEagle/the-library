## 12. Tide Three: The Hold Door

The third tide came in gray and mean, the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

Elodie felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her eyes, a taste of iron in the water that hadn't been there at dawn. The river knew things before the sky did. It always had. Her father used to say the water was older than weather and remembered every storm it had ever swallowed, and that a diver who didn't listen to it was already drowned, just waiting for her body to catch up.

She listened now, kneeling on the deck of their borrowed skiff while Bram cinched the last buckle of her harness with hands that had learned tether work in six days flat. Six days since the archive beneath. Six days since he'd laid his shame out on the planks between them like a gutted fish—*I catalogued the order, Elodie. I touched the paper and I put it back*—and she had not forgiven him, but she had not left either, and some mornings that felt like the same thing.

"Air's full," he said. "Tether's clean. Knife's sharp." He looked up at her, and in the low light his face was all angles and worry. "Window's four hours if the barometer holds. Less if it doesn't."

"It won't hold." She said it plainly, because lying to Bram was a habit she'd never managed to build. "I can feel it. But I don't need four hours. I need forty minutes."

"Forty minutes to cut through a hold door that's been sealed under sixty feet of river for eleven years."

"Forty minutes to try."

He wanted to argue. She could see the argument assembling itself behind his eyes—the same argument they'd had twice already, worn smooth from handling. Instead he reached up and tugged the strap at her shoulder one final time, a small unnecessary adjustment, the kind of gesture people make when what they actually want to say is *come back*.

"Forty minutes," he said. "Then you're on the line whether you've found God down there or not."

"I'm not looking for God."

"No," he said quietly. "You're looking for your brother."

* * *

The descent was different on the third dive. Familiarity had done its slow work; where the Vessany's Pride had once been a shape of dread rising out of the green dark, she was now almost a place—a wreck with rooms, with corridors Elodie had mapped in her head the way other people memorized streets. She moved down the port side past the torn cargo netting, past the galley skylight furred with mussels, past the place where the scuttle charge had blown the hull open like a flower made of shrapnel. The bell was silent today. It had rung for her on the first dive, impossibly, one clear bronze note rolling up the current into her bones, and she had decided—not entirely rationally—that it would ring again when she deserved to hear it.

The hold door waited aft, below the waterline, tucked into the shadow of the collapsed superstructure.

She'd seen it twice now and both times it had stopped her cold. Not rusted, not burst, not buckled by the sinking or gnawed by eleven years of river. Sealed. A steel door set flush into the frame, edges still true, the wheel-lock stiff with concretion but whole. Someone had shut this door deliberately before the ship went down—or after—and whatever was inside it, someone had wanted kept.

Today she came at it with tools instead of awe.

The cutting charge was supposed to have come from the depot raid. That was the plan, the good plan, the plan that had died two nights ago when the monsoon's leading edge made landfall early and turned their careful timetable into smoke. No depot raid meant no shaped charge, no clean breach, no neat circle of steel peeled back like a tin lid. It meant Elodie, forty feet down, with a pry bar, a sledge, a tank of oxy-acetylene she'd rigged from salvage parts and did not trust, and forty minutes.

She clipped her tether to the rail beside the door and got to work.

The first hour of any hard job is the hour that tells you what the job really is. She'd learned that on the barge wrecks, working alone in channels nobody claimed, hauling up other people's losses for other people's ledgers. So she let the first ten minutes be honest: she hammered the wheel-lock free, link by crusted link, and the wheel turned three inches and stopped, and the door did not open, and the river pressed against her ears like a held breath.

*Sealed from inside,* she thought, and the thought went through her colder than the water. *Someone closed it from inside.*

No. Maybe not. Maybe the mechanism was jammed, warped, welded by corrosion into a single mass pretending to be a lock. She told herself that version. She worked the pry bar into the seam anyway, braced her boots against the frame, and pulled until her shoulders screamed and the bar sang a high thin note through the water and the door gave exactly nothing.

The oxy-acetylene rig was worse. She'd built it from a salvaged torch head and a regulator she'd filed down herself, and underwater it burned with a sullen orange flame that ate air faster than she could feed it. She got eight inches of cut into the hinge side before the flame guttered, coughed, and died, and she hung there in the sudden silence listening to her own breathing go ragged, watching the cut glow dull red and then fade to nothing, eight inches of progress out of the six feet she needed.

Her gauge read twenty-one hundred psi. Forty minutes gone, give or take.

She should have gone up. Every rule she lived by said go up: the window closing, the air burning down, the squall she could feel gathering somewhere above her like a fist. But the door was right there. Eleven years of dead men were right there, and Tomas's name was on a manifest that said *witness*, and witnesses testify, and the only courtroom left was this sealed room at the bottom of a river the navy had chosen for its grave.

She went back to the pry bar.

This time she worked it lower, into the seam near the sill, where the frame had taken the worst of the sinking's stress. She could feel something different there—a softness in the metal, a fatigue crack running along the weld like a hairline fracture in old porcelain. She wedged the bar deep, wrapped her tether arm around the rail, and threw everything she had against it—back, legs, lungs, grief, six tides of fury compressed into one long animal pull.

The crack ran. She heard it as much as felt it, a sound like ice letting go on a spring river, and the door shifted—a quarter inch, maybe less, enough to exhale a thread of black silt from the gap like breath from a mouth. Enough to know the hold behind it was not empty. Enough to know it was full.

Enough to know she couldn't get through it. Not today. Not with a pry bar and a dying torch and a body running on fury and half a tank of air.

The squall arrived while she was still hanging there, hating the door.

She felt it first as a change in the light—a dimming far above, the surface going from silver to slate. Then the current changed, the lazy river-current replaced by something with intent, dragging at her tether lines, pushing her sideways against the hull. Silt lifted off the bottom in sheets. The wreck groaned around her, a long structural complaint, and somewhere above, faint through forty feet of thickening water, she heard thunder the way divers hear thunder: not as sound but as pressure, a fist knocking on the ceiling of the world.

Her gauge read fourteen hundred. The visibility was collapsing—ten feet, then six, then the green dark closing in like a hand.

Elodie put her palm flat against the hold door one last time. Steel, cold and absolute, keeping its secrets one more tide.

"Hold," she told it, and didn't bother pretending she meant anything else. "I'm coming back."

The ascent was the worst of her life. The current fought her the whole way up the tether, and by thirty feet the surface above her was a churning gray chaos shot with rain, and by fifteen she could see the skiff—or rather she could see the place the skiff should have been, an empty patch of whitecaps with a tether line vanishing into it. For three seconds, three full seconds, she hung in the water believing the boat was gone, believing Bram was gone, believing the river had finally collected on a debt it had been patient about for eleven years—

—and then the skiff surged out of the rain wall, Bram hauling the tether hand over hand with the line wrapped twice around his waist, his mouth moving around words the wind shredded before they reached her.

* * *

She came over the gunwale like something landed, not landed gracefully—Bram caught her harness and dragged her the rest of the way, and for a moment they just crouched together in the bilge water while the rain hit the deck like gravel thrown by an angry god.

"The door?" he shouted over the storm.

"Cracked. Quarter inch. Can't breach without a charge." She ripped her mask off, and the air above the water tasted like salt and ozone and coming ruin. "There's something in there, Bram. The hold's full—I saw silt move, I *felt* it move, like the room exhaled. Whatever Corvane buried is sitting twelve feet behind that steel."

"Then we come back with a charge."

"When? The monsoon's here. The fleet's—" She stopped. He was looking past her, toward the mouth of the cove, and his face had gone the particular bloodless color she'd learned to read as *navy*.

She turned.

Three skiffs were sweeping the cove's entrance, running dark, riding the squall's leading edge the way wolves ride a herd's panic. Gray hulls, gray canvas, searchlights off but not stowed—she could see the shapes of the mounts, ready. Behind them, out beyond the point where the river widened, the loom of something bigger sat on the horizon like a held verdict. A corvette, maybe two, holding station outside the storm line, waiting for the weather to do their work for them.

"They're not searching," Elodie said, and her voice came out flat and strange, stripped of everything but arithmetic. "Look at their pattern. They're not sweeping for us—they're *fencing*. They've closed the cove. They're making sure nothing leaves."

Bram was already coiling the tether, already moving, six days of defector instincts turning him toward the oars, the tarp, the lee of the headland. "Storm cover works both ways," he said. "They can't see us either. We run for the fishing village, we—"

"No."

The word came out of her before she'd decided to say it, and once it was out she couldn't have taken it back with both hands.

Bram stopped. Turned. Rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away, just watched her with those archivist's eyes that catalogued everything and surrendered nothing.

"We don't run," she said. "That's what they expect. That's what everyone does—runs, hides, drowns quiet. Tomas ran. He wrote one letter and then he ran and they caught him anyway, because running is just drowning with extra steps." She stood up in the pitching skiff, boots wide on the wet planks, and pointed back through the rain at the gray shapes fencing the cove. "Six tides, Bram. This is tide three. We have a cracked door, half a map, and a navy that thinks the storm is doing its killing for it. And I am done—*done*—being the thing that runs."

"Elodie—"

"They led me here!" It tore out of her louder than the thunder, and she let it. "Eleven years I worked these waters thinking I chose them, and it was a trail of breadcrumbs laid by men in dress uniforms. They used my own grief as bait and I swam straight into the hook and I *still* found it, I still raised the chest, I still cracked the door—and now they think a little rain will finish the job?" She was shaking, and it wasn't cold. "Let them sweep their skiffs. Let them fence their cove. Tomorrow we find a cutting charge if we have to take it off one of those boats ourselves, and at tide five I go back down that line, and at tide six that door comes off its hinges if I have to tear it loose with my teeth."

The rain hammered down. Somewhere across the cove, a searchlight flickered on, swept the whitecaps once, and died again, blind.

Bram looked at her for a long moment. And then something in his face changed—not softening exactly, more like a decision arriving, the look of a man who has spent years calculating escape routes and has just, deliberately, burned the map.

"There's a supply barge anchored off the point," he said slowly. "Corvane's forward depot. Demolition stores, cutting charges, the works. I know its watch rotation." A pause, and the ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "Because I helped write it."

Elodie stared at him through the rain.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I'm planning," he said, reaching for the oars. "It looks similar from the outside."

Behind them, at the mouth of the cove, the nearest navy skiff turned its bow toward the headland—and began, unhurried, to close the distance.
