## 13. Justice Outside the Law

The village was called Sallow's Rest, though no one living could say who Sallow had been or what he was resting from. It clung to the coast like a barnacle—forty-odd houses of grey timber, a drying yard hung with nets that smelled of brine and rot, and a single crooked street that ended at a staithe where fishing boats knocked their hulls together in the swell. The kind of place, Elodie thought, where strangers were noticed by morning and forgotten only if they bought enough beer.

They had come in after dark, poling a borrowed skiff up the tidal creek with their lamp shuttered, and taken a room above the Salt House tavern under Bram's forged papers. He'd produced them from somewhere inside his coat without comment—the way he produced everything, charts and ciphers and small necessary lies—and Elodie had decided, for the moment, not to ask.

Now she sat at the room's one table with the chest open before her, its lead lining dull as old moonlight, and watched the candle flame bend each time the wind found a gap in the shutters. Outside, the monsoon was still a day south, but you could feel it gathering—a pressure behind the ears, a restlessness in the gulls, the sea working itself into a longer, meaner rhythm against the breakwater.

Bram stood at the window with the shutter cracked an inch, watching the street. He had been watching the street for an hour. He was very good at watching streets, Elodie had learned. It was the being watched back that made him flinch.

"We should go over it again," she said. "All of it. Before we decide anything."

He didn't turn. "You've already decided."

"I want you to hear it anyway."

That got him. He let the shutter fall and came to the table, pulling the second stool out with the careful economy of a man who had spent years not taking up space. In the candlelight his face looked older than it had on the dock at Corren's Landing, when he'd first approached her with his rehearsed smile and his lie about researching river trade. The lie was long dead between them now. What remained was harder to look at.

Elodie lifted the documents out one by one and laid them in a row across the scarred wood, the way she'd seen him do it twice already, his hands reverent as a priest's. The scuttle order, countersigned in three hands. The fuel requisition that proved the *Vessany's Pride* had been loaded with demolition charges before she ever left harbor. The manifest—one hundred and twelve names, and among them, in the column marked *witnesses transferred*, a line that read **T. Ravensworth, engineman's mate**.

And beneath all of it, the captain's preliminary log fragment they'd raised from the outer wreck, water-blurred but legible: *orders received. God forgive us.*

"There," Elodie said, and put her finger on the manifest. Not on Tomas's name. She couldn't touch that yet. On the names around it. "A hundred and twelve people. And those are just the ones they listed. Maren says there were more below decks they never counted." She looked up. "There are families, Bram. Sons and daughters and widows who've spent twenty years believing their people drowned in a storm. Some of them are still alive. I've found four already—wrote to them from Corren's Landing before I knew what any of this meant. They wrote back. They *remember*."

"You wrote to them." His voice was flat. "Under your own name."

"Under my own name."

He pressed both hands flat on the table, and the candle guttered. "Elodie. Do you understand what you've done? If Corvane's people intercept one letter—"

"They won't."

"You don't know that. You can't know that." He wasn't shouting. That was the terrible thing about Bram; he never shouted. His anger came out cold and precise, like water finding cracks. "Four families. Four addresses sitting in some postal clerk's satchel right now, each one a thread, and every thread leads back to you, and through you to that chest, and through that chest to—" He stopped himself. Took a breath. When he spoke again it was quieter, which was worse. "I have watched this navy bury things before. I know how they do it. They don't send a hundred men with guns. They send one man with a lantern and a story about an accident, and by the time anyone asks questions, the house is burned down and the fire inspector has already signed the report."

"So we don't give them time to burn anything."

"And how do you propose—"

"The press." She said it plainly, because she'd turned the thing over all day while they walked the coastal path, and it had only grown more solid with handling. "Not the courts. I'm not a fool. I know what the courts did the first time—Maren told me about the inquiry, how it sat for eleven days and called three witnesses and closed with a verdict of navigational error. The courts are where truth goes to be embalmed." She tapped the scuttle order. "But there's a correspondent in Vellmark. Halvard Sten. He ran the stories about the munitions barges two years ago, the ones that got a port director jailed. He doesn't answer to the Admiralty. He doesn't answer to anybody. You get him the documents, he gets them printed in four capitals inside a week, and then it doesn't matter what Corvane does—because you can't drown a story that's already swimming."

Bram was shaking his head before she finished. "You can't drown it, no. But you can burn down everyone who ever touched it." He pulled the manifest toward him and turned it so the witness column faced her. "Look at this list. Every name here is a death warrant waiting to be countersigned. The moment this goes public, Corvane doesn't need to destroy paper anymore—he needs to destroy *people*. Witnesses. Survivors. Anyone who can stand up and say *I remember*. You think he'll hesitate? He scuttled a ship full of refugees because they'd seen something. What do you think he does to a printer in Vellmark?"

"He can't reach a printer in Vellmark."

"He reached an archivist in the capital," Bram said, "who had a locked office and a sworn commission and twenty years of service." His voice did something strange on the last words—not breaking, but bending, like a spar under load. "Me, Elodie. I was untouchable. I was the most careful man in that building. And the night I copied the sink order, they knew about it before my ink was dry."

* * *

Bram had not meant to tell her that part. Not tonight, not in this voice, not with the wind rising outside like something gathering courage.

But it was out now, hanging in the candle-smoke between them, and Elodie had gone very still in the way she went still underwater—he'd seen her do it through the viewing glass off the escarpment, that absolute suspension, as if she could stop even her blood moving until the danger passed.

"Before your ink was dry," she repeated.

He should have lied. He was good at lying; it had kept him alive through six years of exile, through dockside taverns and forged papers and a name he'd worn so long he sometimes answered to it in dreams. But she was looking at him across the candle with Tomas's stubbornness written all over her face—the same jaw, the same refusal to look away—and Bram found, wearily, that he was tired of surviving by halves.

"I told you I catalogued the order," he said. "That part was true. What I didn't tell you is that I copied it. All of it. Three pages, verbatim, hidden in a folio of shipping manifests where no auditor would ever look. I told myself I was building a case. That someday the war would end, or the ministry would change, or someone brave would ask me the right question, and I would hand it over and the truth would come out and everything I'd lost would be worth it." He laughed, and hated the sound. "Someone did ask, eventually. A junior minister with reformist ideas. I gave him the copy. Within a month he was reassigned to a colonial survey post. Within two, I was dismissed—for irregularities in my filing, nothing that could be argued with, nothing that mentioned Vessany at all. And within the year, two of the clerks who'd handled the original file were dead. One fell from a bridge. One simply stopped coming to work, and his rooms were cleared, and no one would say where he'd gone."

The candle flame leaned hard, righted itself.

"So when you say the press," Bram said, "when you say *four capitals inside a week*—I believe you. That's the worst part. I believe it would work. The story would swim, exactly as you say." He met her eyes. "And I also believe that by the time it reached the second capital, everyone whose name is attached to these pages would be dead, and Corvane would stand at the inquiry—because there would be an inquiry, there always is—and testify with tears in his eyes about the tragic excesses of wartime, and retire on a pension to write his memoirs. I have seen the machine work, Elodie. From the inside. I catalogued its gears."

"Then what?" Her voice cracked across the word. "Then we sit on it? We bury it deeper than they buried the ship? We let Maren light her lamp over a lie until she dies?" She was on her feet now, and the chest's contents trembled in the draft. "My brother is *in* that column, Bram. T. Ravensworth. He saw what they did, and they took him, and for twenty years I thought—I thought maybe he'd deserted, maybe he'd run, maybe he just didn't want to find me. And he didn't run. He was *transferred*. He was a loose end, same as your clerks, same as every name on that page, and the least I owe him is to make his name a weapon instead of a wound."

"It will be a wound," Bram said quietly. "It will be a wound with a body count."

"Then whose fault is that? Theirs or ours?"

"That question," he said, "is exactly what a man like Corvane is counting on. Because he knows that people like us ask it, and hesitate, and hesitate, and meanwhile the tide comes in." He rose too, slowly, and for a moment they stood on opposite sides of the little table with the dead nation's secrets spread between them like a border neither would cross. "I'm not saying do nothing. I'm saying that going loud, alone, with no protection, gets exactly one outcome: the documents seized, the witnesses buried, and us in a cell or the river. There has to be a way that protects the families *first*. Safe houses, false names, passage out—before a single word goes to print. That takes time. Time we may not have, but—"

"Time." Elodie laughed, short and bitter. "Corvane's fleet is a day behind us and the monsoon is half a day behind *him*, and you're talking about safe houses."

"I'm talking about not getting people killed."

"You're talking about being afraid," she said, and immediately regretted it, because she saw it land—not as an insult but as a confirmation, his face closing like a door.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The sea worked at the breakwater. Somewhere below, in the tavern, a fiddle started up and stopped again, as if even the music couldn't decide.

"I was afraid for twenty years," Bram said finally. "It kept me alive. It's not a flaw in me, Elodie, it's a load-bearing wall." He began, with hands that were not quite steady, to gather the documents back into their oilcloth sleeves. "We'll decide at dawn. Both of us. No one decides anything tonight."

She wanted to fight. Every muscle in her wanted to keep swinging at him until one of them broke open. But the candle was burning low, and she was suddenly, bone-deeply tired, and underneath her fury there was another thing she had no armor for: that when he said *both of us*, some starving part of her had gone quiet and warm, like a hand cupped around a flame.

Because that was the trap of it, wasn't it. She had built her whole life on leaving—never staying anywhere long enough to be found, never wanting anything enough that losing it could kill her. And now she stood in a rented room above a stranger's tavern, arguing by candlelight with a man who catalogued his fears like archive entries, and the argument mattered less than the fact of him standing there. Love against duty, Maren might have called it, if Maren were here to name things. But it didn't feel like two forces opposed. It felt like one force tearing down the middle.

"Dawn," she agreed.

He nodded, rolled the oilcloth closed, and paused with his hand on the chest lid. "Elodie. For what it's worth—if I believed shouting this from the rooftops would save more lives than it costs, I'd climb the roof myself." His mouth twisted. "I'd probably fall off. But I'd climb it."

"I know," she said. And then, because the candle was almost out and the truth was easier in the dark: "That's why I haven't left yet."

Something moved through his face—relief, or grief, or both wearing the same coat—and he closed the chest and turned down the lamp.

* * *

She woke past midnight to the sound of the fiddle again, faint through the floorboards, and to an absence beside the window.

Bram was dressed, coat on, standing where the shutter gap let in a knife of moonless dark. He didn't hear her at first—or pretended not to—and she lay still and watched him take something from his breast pocket: a folded page, small, soft at the creases from much handling. He held it toward the gap as if reading it by starlight, and his lips moved once, soundlessly, and she saw his shoulders do the thing shoulders do when a man swallows something whole.

Then he folded it away, and stood a moment longer looking out at the black harbor, and Elodie understood with cold certainty that whatever was written on that page, it was the reason he'd really come to Corren's Landing. The reason under the lie. And that dawn was coming, and one of them was going to have to ask.

Outside, far down the coast, a light appeared where no light should be—low, steady, sweeping the headland in slow arcs. Not the lighthouse. Not Maren's beam, which stood white and faithful to the north.

This light was green. Navy green. And it was getting closer.
