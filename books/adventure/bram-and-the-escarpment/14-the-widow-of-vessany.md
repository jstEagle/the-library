## 14. The Widow of Vessany

The lighthouse stood at the end of the world, or close enough to it that the difference hardly mattered.

Elodie saw it first as a smudge against the rain-hazed headland—a white finger rising from the escarpment's last shoulder, where the land gave up and the sea began its long argument with the rocks. The fishing village below it was barely a village: a dozen houses the color of driftwood, nets hung like funeral shrouds, boats hauled up on rollers and left to rot in the salt wind. The people here had the look of those who had chosen to be forgotten, or who had been forgotten so long they'd stopped minding.

She'd walked half the night from the cove where they'd hidden the skiff, Bram silent beside her, both of them soaked to the marrow. The name had come to them in fragments over three days—Maren, Maren of Vessany, the widow who kept the light—passed along by an oyster-woman who wouldn't meet their eyes, by a boy mending traps who pointed up the cliff path without a word. As if everyone in this coast knew what lived at the top of it and had agreed, collectively, not to speak of her.

"She'll know we're coming," Bram said at the base of the tower. He said it quietly, the way he'd been saying everything since the village, since the argument about courts and correspondents that neither of them had won. "Women like her always know."

"Women like her," Elodie repeated. "You say that like you've met one."

"I've read about them." A pause. The rain ticked against his hood. "It isn't the same thing."

The door was iron, older than anything else on the headland, and before Elodie could knock it opened.

* * *

Maren Vessany was small. That was the first thing—the shock of it. Elodie had built her into something enormous in her mind, the last survivor of a drowned nation, keeper of thirty years of grief, and instead there stood a woman scarcely taller than Elodie herself, bent slightly at the spine, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin gone gray with age. Her face was a map of weather. Her eyes were pale as shallow water and just as clear, and they went from Elodie to Bram and back again with the unhurried attention of someone who had spent decades watching the horizon for things that did not want to be seen.

"You're the diver," she said. Not a question.

"How did you—"

"The whole coast knows you now, girl. Two strangers asking after the Pride." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the storm decides for you."

Inside, the lighthouse was all circles. A spiral stair wound up through the center of everything, and around it the rooms stacked like rings: a kitchen, a parlor, a sleeping loft, each one curved to fit the tower's bones. It smelled of lamp oil and dried kelp and, beneath it, something older—paper, maybe, or memory. Every wall was covered in names.

That was the second thing. Elodie stopped dead just inside the parlor and stared.

Hundreds of names, inked directly onto the plaster in a fine, careful hand, some faded to ghosts, some fresh enough to glisten. They climbed the walls, circled the windows, marched up the stairwell into the dark above. Not random. Ordered. Alphabetical within families, she realized, the way manifests are written.

"You've been reading them," Maren said, watching her. "Good. Most visitors pretend not to see."

"What is it?"

"It's the list. All of them. Everyone who was on the Pride when she went down." The old woman moved past her to the stove, unhurried, feeding it split wood with hands knotted like roots. "I write them when I remember new ones. Sometimes I get a name wrong and have to scrape it off and start again. That's worse than forgetting, getting one wrong. You've insulted the dead twice then."

"There were four hundred and eleven," Bram said softly. He was standing before the wall near the window, his face lit by lamplight, reading. His voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice he used when feeling got too close to the surface. "I catalogued the manifest. Four hundred and eleven souls aboard, two hundred and six accounted for by the navy's inquiry. The rest were listed as 'unrecoverable.'"

"Unrecoverable." Maren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a gull's cry. "That's what they called my daughters. Unrecoverable. Like cargo that slipped its lashings." She turned, and her pale eyes fixed on Elodie with sudden, piercing weight. "But you didn't climb my cliff to talk about numbers. You found the chest."

Elodie's hand went, without thinking, to the oilskin satchel beneath her coat.

"I know what's in it," Maren said. "Or I know what should be in it. Sit down, girl. Both of you. This will take as long as it takes, and the lamp doesn't need me till dusk."

* * *

She told it plainly, the way old soldiers tell battles. No ornament, no self-pity. She had been twenty-three when Vessany's Pride made her last crossing, a refugee ship flying no flag because Vessany itself had already been struck from every chart—erased by treaty, its people scattered, its harbors given new names. The Pride carried the ones who wouldn't abandon the drowned country entirely: families, elders, a hold full of documents the new authorities wanted burned. Maren had two daughters asleep in the steerage berths when the charges went.

"Not torpedoes," she said. "Not mines. Charges. Set from inside, at the seams, where the hull was thinnest. I was on deck. I heard them—I heard them *before* the water came, that double-thump through the plates, and I knew. My father was a shipwright. You don't forget what breaking steel sounds like from the inside." Her hands lay folded in her lap, perfectly still. "And then the navy ships that had been shadowing us for two days turned away. Turned *away*, do you understand? They watched us go down and they steamed off toward the horizon like men leaving a funeral early."

"You survived," Bram said. "How?"

"A fishing boat. Out of this very village. Old Tomas Vessany—not your Tomas, girl, though I wondered about the name when I heard it—old Tomas pulled me out of the water along with nine others. Of the ten of us, I'm the last." She said it the way she might report the tide tables. "The inquiry took my statement. Then the statement disappeared. Then they offered me money, and when I refused it, they offered me silence, and silence was the only thing they gave me for free."

Elodie opened the satchel then. She laid the documents out on Maren's table—the scuttle order, the fleet positioning logs, the manifest—and watched the old woman read them with fingers that trembled only once, only briefly, when she reached the page bearing the seal.

"So it wasn't lost," Maren murmured. "All these years I told myself it sank with the ship, that the truth was unrecoverable too. And it was sitting in the mud the whole time, waiting for someone stubborn enough to go down and get it."

"There's a name on the manifest," Elodie said. Her voice came out rough. "A witness listed for deposition. Tomas Ravensworth. He was—" She stopped. Started again. "He was my brother. He joined the navy at seventeen. He wrote me one letter, and then nothing. Eleven years of nothing. And now his name is on this paper, tied to this ship, and I need to know—"

"I know why you're here," Maren said gently. "I've known since you walked through my door wearing your brother's face around your eyes."

She rose, slow, joints cracking like kindling, and climbed the spiral stair. Elodie started to follow but Bram caught her arm—*wait*—and so she waited, heart hammering, while footsteps creaked overhead, paused, creaked again. When Maren came back down she was holding a tin box, rust-bloomed at the corners, and inside it a letter gone soft and yellow at the folds.

"He sent it to me," Maren said. "Eleven years ago. Through channels I never learned and never asked about. There was a note with it, unsigned: *Keep this until someone comes asking about the Pride.*" She held it out. "No one ever came. Until you."

Elodie took the letter the way you take something that might burn.

The handwriting was Tomas's. She would have known it anywhere—that slight forward lean to the letters, like a man always walking uphill. She'd seen it last on a birthday card when she was fourteen. She read it standing, then sitting, then standing again, because her legs wouldn't decide.

*Sister. If you're reading this then either I'm dead or I'm gone, and I've promised myself not to tell you which, because either way you'll come looking, and looking is dangerous. But you never listened to me about anything, so I'll say it once: the Pride was not lost. It was spent. There are papers in the sealed hold—captain's log, original orders—and I put my name on the witness list to make someone account for them someday. If a woman named Maren still keeps a light on the eastern headland, trust her past reason. I'm sorry about the birthday I missed. I'm sorry about all of them. Don't dive angry. —T.*

*Don't dive angry.* She laughed, and it broke in the middle, and then she was crying, hard and ugly, into her sleeve while the lamp above them turned its slow eternal circle and the storm pressed its shoulder against the glass. Bram put a hand on her back and left it there, saying nothing, which was the only right thing anyone could have done.

* * *

Dusk came early under the storm. Maren climbed the tower to light the lamp, and Elodie followed her up the spiral stairs, past the names on the walls, into the lantern room where the great lens sat in its cage of brass like a captured piece of the sun.

"You're coming with us," Elodie said. It wasn't a question either. "When we run. Corvane knows the coast is warm. He'll sweep every headland between here and the capital. You can't stay."

Maren trimmed the wick with hands that didn't shake at all anymore. Outside, the sea was going black and ragged; the monsoon's leading edge had made landfall far to the south, and even here the swells were beginning to stand up like startled animals.

"Girl," she said, "do you know how many nights I have kept this light? Nine thousand, four hundred and six. I count. It passes the time." She closed the lens housing and wiped her fingerprints from the brass. "Every night I light it, and every night I think: somewhere out there is the place where my daughters lie, and this beam touches that water, and for as long as it burns, nobody gets to pretend they were never there. That's not a job. That's the only prayer I have left."

"The navy could come for you."

"They've had thirty years to come for me. I'm more useful to them as a madwoman on a cliff." She smiled, and it transformed her face—made her young for one impossible moment, a bride, a mother, a woman stepping aboard a ship with her whole life in two trunks. "Besides. Someone has to show you the way out tomorrow night. There's a channel through the outer shoals that no chart marks honestly. Corvane's captains won't risk their hulls in it, but a skiff can fly through, if there's a light to steer by." She tapped the lens with one knuckle, gently, the way you'd tap a faithful horse. "I'll be your beacon. That's my rescue. Let me keep it."

Elodie looked at her—at the small, bent, unbreakable shape of her—and understood that grief, given thirty years and a tower, becomes something else. Not softer. *Load-bearing.* Maren had taken the worst thing that ever happened to her and built a structure out of it, and people navigated by it whether they knew it or not.

"Then take this," Elodie said, and pressed the manifest copy into her hands. "If we don't make it—if Corvane takes the documents—you're the backup. You know the names better than any archive."

Maren weighed the pages in her palm, then nodded once, solemn as a christening. "Then we're all anchored to each other now. That's how it works. That's how it always worked, on the Pride. Nobody drowns alone unless everybody lets go."

They came down the stairs together. Bram waited at the bottom, and Maren studied him for a long moment with those shallow-water eyes.

"You were archives," she said. "Navy. Before."

Bram went very still. "How—"

"You flinch at the word *inquiry*. Only archivists flinch at words." She patted his cheek, once, almost fondly. "Whatever you failed to save, young man, you're saving it now. That's all the ledger cares about."

Outside, the wind had risen to a sustained moan, and far to the south the sky flickered with the storm's true arrival. Elodie stood a moment on the gallery looking down at the darkening water, Tomas's letter folded against her heart, and felt the strange new weight of purpose settle over her like ballast—no longer running, no longer drifting, but *steering*.

Tomorrow night they would run the shoal channel with a widow's light to guide them, straight into whatever Corvane had waiting.

Above them, right on schedule, the lamp began to turn.
