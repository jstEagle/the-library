## 15. Tide Four: What Bram Took

The letter had changed her. Elodie could feel it in the way she moved through Maren's lighthouse that last morning—slower, heavier, but no longer hollow. Tomas's words sat folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and for the first time since the river took her brother's name and gave back nothing, she knew exactly what she was carrying and where she was taking it.

She woke before dawn to rain ticking against the glass of the lamp room, found Bram's cot empty, and thought nothing of it.

That was the thing she would carry longest, she decided later. Not the betrayal itself—betrayal she understood, betrayal was just the tide doing what tides did. It was the *not thinking*. She had spent eleven days learning to sleep with this man ten feet away, learning the rhythm of his breathing through the dark, learning which creak of the stair was his weight and which was the tower settling. And when his cot lay empty at first light, her mind, so finely tuned to danger it could read a patrol boat's wake from three miles off, simply said: *he's gone to check the lines.*

He'd left his coat. That should have been the second warning. Bram without his coat was like the lamp without its wick—a thing missing its whole purpose. The coat hung on the peg by the door, and in its inside pocket, weighted down like an apology, was a folded sheet of paper he had copied out in his small, precise archivist's hand.

Elodie stood over the peg for a long moment before she touched it. Rain on the glass. The lamp turning above her, sweeping its slow blade of light across the water, across the shoals, across the grey line where Corvane's fleet would be.

Then she took the paper out and read it by lamplight, though the sky was lightening, because her hands wanted something to hold while they shook.

It was a copy of terms. *Terms*—the word itself was a knife, formal as a court summons. Written in Bram's hand but addressed in the language of the navy, clause after clause, each one initialed:

*I, Bram Voss, formerly Second Archivist of the Naval Records Office, do offer myself into custody, together with full testimony regarding the disposition of Order 114-K and all documents pertaining thereto...*

*In exchange, the Admiralty will guarantee safe passage and permanent immunity for one Elodie Ravensworth, civilian, who shall bear no liability for the recovery or possession of materials...*

*The vessel and remaining contents of the wreck site are to be destroyed by demolition following my surrender, per standard protocol.*

She read it three times. The third time was not because the words were difficult but because her body kept trying to make them mean something else, some other arrangement, some other woman's name in that second clause. But it was her name. Her name, spelled correctly, protected, *purchased*—with him as the coin.

And the third clause. The vessel destroyed. The hold breached or not, the captain's log recovered or not—Corvane got Bram, and the Pride went to the charges anyway, and everything they had bled for these six tides came apart behind them like foam on a wave.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty lamp room, to the turning light, to him wherever he was. "No, you don't get to buy me."

* * *

She found the skiff gone from its mooring below the lighthouse and stood on the wet rocks doing arithmetic. Tide tables in her head, wind direction, the pull of the current past Widow's Point—and then she stopped doing arithmetic and started doing what she actually knew how to do, which was think like a diver. Like a salvager. Like someone whose whole life had been reading water for what it hid.

If Bram had gone to Corvane, he hadn't gone to the fleet. The fleet lay south, beyond the shoals; no skiff made that run in this weather. He'd gone to the picket—the forward outpost. She'd seen its mast-light two nights ago from the gallery, a single star that didn't belong to the sky, anchored in the lee of Gull Rock where the channel narrowed. A signal station. A place where a man might come ashore under a white rag and ask, quietly, to be taken to someone with authority.

A place a man might go if he wanted to be caught.

She took Maren's other boat—the old one, the one the widow kept for hauling lamp oil, broad-beamed and slow and utterly unremarkable, the kind of boat that could pass a navy picket without raising an eyebrow because it raised nothing at all. She was halfway down the cliff stair before she let herself feel anything, because feeling came after, feeling was for afterward, and right now there was only water and weather and the fact that Bram Voss had tried to spend himself on her behalf without asking whether she could afford the price.

The rain came sideways off the sea. She ran the old boat with the mizzen reefed double and her hood streaming, and the whole way across the grey mile of water between the point and Gull Rock she rehearsed what she would say to him, and every version ended with her hands around his throat, and none of them ended with what she was actually afraid of saying, which was: *you were supposed to ask me. You were supposed to stand in the rain and argue with me until we hated each other, but you were supposed to ask.*

* * *

The picket ship was a corvette, lean and mean, riding at anchor in the lee with its signal lamps dark against the storm-grey morning. Bram's skiff bobbed alongside, empty, tied off with a navy hitch she recognized because he'd taught it to her—of course he had, of course he'd taught her the knots of the service that was about to eat him.

She brought Maren's boat in close under the corvette's quarter, where the hull's shadow made a pocket of dead water, and shipped her oars, and listened.

Voices came down through the open stern gallery above her. Corvane wasn't aboard—she'd have known, the whole sea would have known—but someone with authority was, because the voices had the particular flatness of men conducting a transaction they intended to deny later.

"—the order stands regardless," one voice was saying. "You understand that. Your testimony is a curiosity. The site is a problem."

"The site is evidence," Bram said, and Elodie closed her eyes, because she knew that voice, knew the precise calm he put over fear like varnish over rot. "Which is why your admiral wants me more than he wants you to admit. I catalogued 114-K. I can authenticate it. Without me, whatever your divers raise is paper. With me—" a pause, and she heard him choose the word, heard him lay it down like a card he'd been holding face-down for eleven days, "—with me it's a verdict."

Silence. Then laughter—not cruel, exactly. Worse than cruel. Amused.

"You've been out of the service too long, Voss. You think the Admiralty trades. The Admiralty *takes*." A boot scraped on the gallery deck. "But the admiral says bring him in, so bring him in. The woman—"

"The woman is nothing," Bram said. "She's a river salvager who stumbled onto a chest. She has no documents. I have everything."

Elodie's hand found the boathook.

Not because she'd decided anything. Because her body had decided, the way it decided things underwater, below argument, below thought—instantly, totally, with the whole animal intelligence of a person who had spent her life keeping herself alive in places built to kill her. The papers were in the oilskin wrap beneath the thwart of Maren's boat, six inches from her knee. Everything. The manifest, the copies, Maren's confirmation, the chart of the sealed hold. Everything he'd just told them didn't exist.

They were going to come down and search the boats. Of course they were. *The Admiralty takes.*

She slipped the oilskin bundle inside her coat, against Tomas's letter, against her heart, and began, silently, to move Maren's heavy old boat away from the corvette's shadow—one oar, feathered, barely breaking the skin of the water—when the rain thickened all at once into a white roaring curtain, the squall line's leading edge arriving early, exactly as the widow had promised it would, and the world above the gunwale dissolved into noise.

In that noise, a shape came over the corvette's side. Down the ladder. Into the boarding skiff.

Bram.

His hands were bound in front of him—not roughly, she noted, not yet, they still thought he was a bargain—and a young lieutenant sat across from him with a lantern, and the coxswain bent to the oars, and the boarding skiff swung out into the rain heading for the shore station on Gull Rock where a launch waited to carry him south to the fleet, to Corvane, to the charges and the silence and the end of everything.

Elodie did the mathematics of the water in four seconds, the way she'd done it ten thousand times: current setting northeast along the rock, wind backing, the skiff's course crossing hers at a closing angle of maybe thirty degrees, forty yards, and closing.

She had no plan. She had a boathook, a boat full of ballast stones, and a fury so complete it had passed through rage and come out the other side into something cold and clear and absolutely steady.

She let the current take her. Drifted, dead in the water, just another piece of flotsam in the squall, until the boarding skiff's bow crossed ten yards off her starboard quarter—and then she shipped the boathook, stood up in Maren's wallowing old tub, and hooked the skiff's gunwale with both hands on the shaft.

The lieutenant looked up. He was twenty-three years old and he saw, rising out of the rain like something the sea had sent, a woman with her hood blown back and her eyes fixed not on him but on the bound man beside him, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach for a whistle he never got to blow, because at that moment Bram Voss threw himself sideways against the skiff's gunwale with his whole body, both bound hands slamming down on the far rail, and the little boat rolled, and the lieutenant went into the black water with a splash and a strangled shout, and the coxswain dropped his oars to grab for him, and Elodie hauled.

She hauled the way she'd hauled the chest up from forty fathoms, with her legs and her back and every ounce of the stubbornness that had kept her alive when staying alive had been the only thing she had. Bram came over the gunwale of Maren's boat in a tangle of rope and rain and landed against the thwart, gasping, and she cut his bindings with the diver's knife off her belt—one stroke, no ceremony—and shoved him down flat among the ballast stones, and put her own body between him and the corvette, and pulled for the shore with everything the storm allowed.

Behind them, shouts. A whistle, finally, shrill and useless in the wind. The corvette's silhouette sprouting activity like a kicked nest. And ahead, the grey loom of Gull Rock, and beyond it the open channel, and beyond that the lighthouse, and Maren's lamp already beginning to turn in the dying light.

* * *

They ran the boat up into the sea cave below the point—the widow's secret berth, dry-floored at low tide, invisible from the water—and Elodie shipped the oars and stood in the dripping dark with her chest heaving and did not look at him.

Bram sat among the ballast stones, wrists raw, hair plastered to his skull, breathing in the ragged way of a man who has very recently believed he was going to die. When he spoke, his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Elodie—"

"Don't." The word cracked off the cave walls. "Don't you dare say my name like we're still—we're not. Not right now. You don't get to be familiar with me right now."

"Listen to me—"

"*Listen to you?*" She turned on him then, and the lantern light caught her face, and he flinched—actually flinched—at what was in it. "I read your terms, Bram. Your beautiful, careful, initialed terms. You sold yourself to the man who killed my brother's ship, and you bought me immunity like I'm some—some cargo you could ship somewhere safe. Clause two. My name in clause two. Did you even read what you wrote? 'Permanent immunity for one Elodie Ravensworth.' You wrote me into a treaty. You wrote me out of my own life!"

"Yes." He said it simply. No defense in it, no excuse. Just the bare fact, laid down between them like a body. "Yes, I did."

"And the third clause!" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated that he could hear it break. "The vessel destroyed. You gave them the wreck, Bram. You gave them the hold, the log, the captain's orders—everything Tomas died for, everything Maren survived for—you handed it to them wrapped in your own confession, and called it saving me!"

"Because it WAS saving you!" He was on his feet now, and for the first time since she'd known him his composure was gone entirely, stripped away, and underneath it she could see the thing he'd been hiding since the dockside tavern: not calculation. Terror. Terror for her, worn so long it had calcified into strategy. "Do you know what Corvane does to people who cross him? Do you know what happened to the last archivist who tried to keep a copy of 114-K? I do. I attended his funeral. There wasn't enough of him to bury. You want to run the shoal channel tomorrow night with a widow's lamp and a boatload of paper? They'll sink you in the first hundred yards and call it weather, and no one will ever find enough of you to bury either, and I—" His voice failed. He stood in the dripping dark with his ruined wrists and his drowned-man's hair and finished, barely audible: "I have already lost everything once, Elodie. I could not lose you too. So I made it so I wouldn't have to."

The cave was silent except for the sea working at the entrance, and the rain, and their breathing.

"That's not sacrifice," she said at last, and her voice had gone quiet, which was worse, and they both knew it was worse. "Sacrifice is giving something up. You didn't give yourself up. You gave up *us*. You decided for both of us. You looked at everything I've become in eleven days—everything I chose, on the escarpment road, in the hold door, at Maren's table—and you decided it was yours to overrule. That's not love, Bram. Love doesn't purchase people. Even—" her breath caught, "—even beautifully. Even with good clauses."

"I was trying to keep you alive."

"I was trying to make the truth *survive*," she said. "There's a difference. Tomas knew it. Maren knows it. The whole drowned nation of Vessany knew it, and the water knows it, and the only person standing in this cave who doesn't seem to know it is the man who catalogued the order and ran." She stepped closer. Close enough to see the rain on his lashes, close enough that he couldn't look anywhere else. "So here's what happens now. Tomorrow night we run the channel together—all the way, both of us, or I go alone and you can spend the rest of your life being Corvane's well-preserved archivist. Choose."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere above them, faint through thirty feet of stone, the lighthouse beam turned, and turned, and turned.

Then Bram Voss, former Second Archivist of the Naval Records Office, defector, liar, betrayer, and the only man she had ever let anchor herself to, reached into his shirt and drew out the one thing she hadn't known he carried: a thin brass tube, waterproofed, sealed with wax, hanging on a cord around his neck.

"My original notes," he said hoarsely. "The ones I told you I burned on the road. I burned a decoy page. These are real—dates, cipher keys, the location of the duplicate file in the capital archive. If we're going to do this, if we're going to do it *together*, then Corvane shouldn't just lose the wreck." Something new kindled in his face, something she'd never seen there before, wild and bright and entirely unhiding. "He should lose the war."

Elodie looked at the tube, and at him, and felt the fury in her chest shift and settle into something harder and more durable—not forgiveness, not yet, forgiveness would take longer than one tide—but alliance. Ballast. The thing you build a boat on.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"You never decide for me again. Not to save me. Not to spare me. Never." She held out her hand for the tube. "We sink or swim as crew. Both of us. Or not at all."

He placed the brass tube in her palm and closed her fingers over it with both of his bound-raw hands, and outside the cave mouth the rain came down like judgment, and the tide turned, and the fifth tide began.
