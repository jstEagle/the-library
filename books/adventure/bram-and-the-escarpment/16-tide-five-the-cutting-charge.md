## 16. Tide Five: The Cutting Charge

The rain had not yet arrived in earnest, but the air had begun to taste of it—that green metallic tang that meant the monsoon was gathering itself beyond the escarpment like a debt come due. Bram felt it on his tongue as he stood at the window of the room above the chandlery, watching Elodie pack.

She packed the way she did everything: without ceremony, without sentiment, each item given exactly the space it required and no more. The manifest went into an oilskin sleeve against her sternum. Tomas's letter—Maren's letter, Tomas's last words carried twenty years in a lighthouse keeper's hands before she passed them on—went beside it. Everything else was tools: line, knife, spare regulator parts, a slate and chalk for underwater communication. She moved through the small room like water finding its level, and Bram understood, watching her, that he had been forgiven but not absolved, and that those were two different countries, and she lived in only one of them.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm memorizing," he said. "In case."

"In case of what?"

He didn't answer, because the honest answer—in case the next time I betray you is the last one—was not something either of them could afford to carry into what came next. Instead he said, "There's a supply depot at Harrow Point. Corvane's forward station for the salvage fleet. If they're staging demolition work on the wreck—and they will be, they'll want to bury it rather than raise it—they'll have cutting charges there. Proper ones. Not the fishing explosives Maren's nephew can get us."

Elodie stopped packing. "You want to walk into a navy depot."

"I want to walk *through* one. There's a difference." He crossed to the table where his old notes lay, the ones he'd let her read after the rain—the whole archive of his shame spread out like a confession written in his own hand. He tapped a page. "Depot protocol hasn't changed since I catalogued it. I know the requisition forms. I know the countersign rotation—I helped design the archive side of it. I know which officer signs off on demolition stores when the fleet's at sea, and I know that officer has never once verified a courier's credentials, because in eleven years nobody has ever been stupid enough to walk in the front gate of a naval installation and ask for high explosives."

"And if they have changed things?"

"Then we'll find out very quickly, and very loudly."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, a fishing boat ran for shelter with its sail half-furled, and the light coming through the window had gone the color of pewter. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its edge and gained something worse: tenderness.

"Last night you offered yourself to Corvane to buy my freedom. Today you're offering to walk into his guns. Do you understand how that looks from where I'm standing?"

"It looks like a man who keeps trying to die for you instead of live with you." He said it before he could stop himself, and watched her flinch, and made himself keep going. "I know. I'm working on it. But this isn't sacrifice, Elodie. This is arithmetic. We need charges. I know the door. That's all this is."

"That's never all anything is, with you."

"No," he admitted. "But it's enough to start."

She shouldered her pack. For a moment her hand hovered near his—not touching, but near, the way a compass needle hovers before it settles—and then she pulled open the door and the wind came in off the river carrying the first spit of rain, and there was no more time for hovering.

"Front gate, then," she said. "Since we're being stupid."

* * *

Harrow Point sat on a finger of land where the river widened toward the sea, a sprawl of corrugated sheds and coaling cranes behind a fence that had been built to stop fishermen, not archivists. The fleet was gone from its anchorage—all of it, every hull, standing out toward the cove where the Vessany's Pride lay waiting under six tides of patience. That was the gift hidden inside the disaster of the smuggler's betrayal: Corvane had taken his ships to the wreck, which meant the depot behind him was running on a skeleton crew of clerks and sentries who had spent the last three days watching the horizon and wishing they were anywhere else.

Bram wore the uniform he'd kept folded in the bottom of his bag for eight years, the way other men kept letters from dead lovers. It still fit. That was its own kind of obscenity. The braid at his cuff marked him as a records officer second class—a rank so bureaucratic, so deliberately dull, that no sentry in the fleet's service would look twice at it, because looking twice at records officers was how men got assigned to inventory coal in the rain.

Elodie walked three paces behind him, in a borrowed coat, playing the part of a hired porter hauling their single crate of "survey instruments." Inside the crate, wrapped in sacking, lay the empty space where the cutting charges would go, and a coil of fuse, and Elodie's diving knife, and neither of them mentioned any of it.

The sentry at the gate was nineteen at most, and the rain had reached him properly now, drumming on his helmet, and he wanted this interaction over with the pure and holy desperation of the young and wet.

"Purpose at Harrow Point?"

"Requisition escort, demolition stores, transit to fleet anchorage." Bram produced the folder he'd spent the previous evening forging—requisition form 44-C, the countersign current through tomorrow's dawn, the signature of Lieutenant Vasquez, ordnance officer, reproduced from memory down to the little ink blot where the man's pen always caught on the downstroke of the V. Bram had catalogued four hundred of Vasquez's signatures once. He could have drawn them blindfolded. He sometimes thought he could have drawn them in his sleep, and perhaps he had; his dreams in those years had been nothing but filing cabinets.

The sentry looked at the folder. Looked at Bram's face. Looked at the folder again.

"Vasquez signed this himself?"

"Do I look like a man who forges documents?" Bram said, and put just enough weary offense into it, the exact note of a functionary whose morning has already been ruined by lesser men. It was a performance, and it was also true, and the truth of it settled over him strangely. Eight years ago he had walked out of the archives with nothing, because he'd tried to take the order whole, all at once, a single heroic theft. He'd learned since then that lies worked better when they were load-bearing walls built out of real bricks.

The sentry waved them through. "Ordnance shed's the third on the left. Sign the ledger."

They signed the ledger. That was the thing about navies, Bram thought, walking across the yard with the rain flattening his hair and Elodie's breath steady behind him—they trusted paper the way other institutions trusted God. Every step of their passage was being recorded, neatly, in triplicate, by men who believed that a record was the same thing as a wall. Somewhere in another life, Bram had been one of those men. He signed *R. Calloway, records escort*, in a hand that was almost his own, and felt the old self dissolve a little further into the new one with every stroke of the pen.

The ordnance shed smelled of grease and cordite and the particular institutional loneliness of buildings that exist to be ignored. A storeman with a wheeze and a clipboard met them inside, and Bram recited the stock numbers for marine cutting charges—K-series, waterproof casing, ten-minute delay fuses—from memory, and the storeman squinted at the requisition, and squinted at Bram, and then did what every storeman in every navy in history has done when confronted with correct paperwork on a miserable afternoon: he decided it was someone else's problem.

"Ten charges," the storeman said. "That's fleet-level authorization."

"Fleet's at sea. Vasquez signed transit authority." Bram tapped the folder. "You want to hold up the admiral's demolition schedule over a signature you can verify by runner in an hour? Be my guest. I'll wait. I've got nowhere to be except here, in this shed, with you, forever."

The storeman looked at the rain hammering the roof. He looked at the ledger. Something in him quietly surrendered, the way it does in all of us, and he began pulling crates.

It was while Elodie was loading them—her hands quick and sure, checking each casing the way she checked her dive gear, with the intimacy of long practice—that Bram heard the boots.

Not one pair. Several. Moving with purpose, moving toward the shed, and beneath them the sound that turned his blood to ice water: a signal whistle, three short notes, the fleet's call for security alert. Someone at the gate had finally gotten curious. Someone had sent a runner to the anchorage, or found Vasquez asleep in his bunk forty yards away, or simply remembered that records officers second class did not, as a rule, collect high explosives in person during a rising storm.

"Elodie."

"I hear it." She didn't panic. That was the miracle he would remember afterward, in all the years that followed—she didn't panic, didn't freeze, didn't even look up from the crate. She finished seating the last charge, closed the lid, and said, "How many ways out of this shed?"

"One door, one skylight, one ventilation grate too small for a cat." He was already doing the arithmetic, the cold fast arithmetic of a man who had spent his career mapping rooms he hoped never to be trapped in. "Back wall. There's a maintenance yard behind the ordnance row, and past that the coal loading ramp runs down to the water. There'll be a boat."

"There's always a boat," Elodie said, "if you're willing to swim for it," and something in her voice was almost laughing, and Bram realized with a jolt that she was afraid and exhilarated in equal measure, and that he was too, and that fear shared evenly between two people stops being fear and becomes something with teeth and wings.

The boots reached the door. A voice—Vasquez himself, awake now, furious now—shouted the question that ends all paperwork: "*Who's in my shed?*"

Bram picked up his end of the crate. Elodie picked up hers. They moved.

* * *

Later, Bram would try to reconstruct those minutes and find that memory had broken them into bright fragments, the way storms break light. The back wall of the shed giving way—not breached but *opened*, because Elodie had scouted it in the first thirty seconds and found the boards rotten behind the shelving, because of course she had, because she read structures the way he read ledgers. The maintenance yard, all mud and puddles shining like hammered tin. The shout going up behind them, and then the whistle, and then—God help them—the crack of a rifle, the bullet slapping into a coal pile close enough to sting their faces with grit.

"They're firing *warning* shots," Elodie panted, running. "They want us alive."

"Corvane wants the chest. Sentries want to avoid a tribunal." Bram's lungs burned. His legs, which had spent eight years sitting in reading rooms and tavern corners, found a rhythm they had no business remembering, some old drill buried in the muscle. "Keep to the ramp shadow. The floodlight won't reach—"

Light bloomed overhead anyway, the yard's great lamp swinging on, and for one naked instant they were lit like specimens—two figures, one crate, forty yards of open ground between them and the coal ramp and the black water below it. Bram heard boots converging from the left, from the gate, from everywhere, the navy's whole slow body waking up around them, and he heard himself say, "Drop the crate."

"We don't drop the crate."

"The charges are replaceable. You are not—"

"*Move.*"

And then she did the thing he would carry with him for the rest of his life, the image that would surface unbidden in quiet rooms years later: she shifted her grip, got low, and *ran harder*, hauling the crate through the mud with the same terrible economy she brought to everything, and instead of leaving her Bram matched her stride, and the two of them crossed that killing ground together, lit and exposed and utterly committed, and the rifles cracked again and again and the bullets went wide because the shooters were young and the rain was heavy and because—Bram would swear this later, would swear it on anything she asked him to swear it on—the storm itself leaned down that night and put its shoulder in front of them.

They went off the end of the coal ramp together, crate and all, into water black as ink and warm as blood.

The boat was where boats always are if you're willing to swim for it: a navy launch, moored, keys in the ignition because navies trust locks the way they trust paper and are wrong about both. Elodie came up gasping, shoved the crate ahead of her, and Bram got the engine turning on the third pull while the first searchlight swept the ramp above them and found only rain.

Then the river took them.

* * *

They ran dark, engine throttled low, hugging the eastern shore where the current ran fastest and the patrol boats wouldn't risk their drafts. Behind them Harrow Point dissolved into glare and shouting, and then into weather, because the monsoon chose that moment to make landfall.

It came down the escarpment like something released rather than arrived—the leading edge of it visible even at night, a gray wall advancing on the water, swallowing the far bank, swallowing the lights, swallowing the world. The first gust hit the launch broadside and laid it over, and Elodie laughed, actually laughed, a wild unarmored sound Bram had never heard from her, and hauled the tiller over to meet it.

"There it is!" she shouted over the roar. "Right on schedule! Six tides, and this is the sixth!"

"You find this funny!"

"I find it *honest*!" Rain streamed down her face; she didn't wipe it away. "Everything else lies! The navy lies, the courts lie, you lied—" she caught herself, and grinned, fierce and soaked and alive—"you *used* to lie. But a storm tells you exactly what it is. Come on, archivist. Race it."

And Bram, who had spent his entire adult life being careful, being hidden, being a man who survived by staying small and unreadable—he took the tiller from her side and set their course straight down the throat of the squall line, and felt the reckless joy of it rise in him like water in a lock, unstoppable, lifting them both. They were not careful. They were not hidden. They were two people in a stolen boat with ten stolen charges and a dead nation's proof against their hearts, running before the biggest storm in a decade toward a wreck the entire might of the fleet was converging on, and they were moving as one, oar and tiller, breath and breath, and he could not remember ever having been so completely, so catastrophically happy.

The cove opened before them just as the sky split open above them, lightning nailing the water silver, and in that flash Bram saw it: the fleet. Not arriving. *Arrived.* Six hulls riding at anchor across the mouth of the cove, lights burning, boats in the water, a ring of steel closing on the Vessany's Pride—and between the fleet and the shore, threading the gap where no warship dared follow, one small dark channel of deep water, unguarded, impossible, theirs.

Elodie saw it in the same instant. Their eyes met in the lightning's afterglow.

"One tide left," she said. "One dive."

Bram looked at the wall of storm, and the wall of steel, and the woman beside him, and found that none of it frightened him anymore.

"Then we'd better make it count," he said, and drove the bow into the dark.
