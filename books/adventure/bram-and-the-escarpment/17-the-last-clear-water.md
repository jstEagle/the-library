## 17. The Last Clear Water

The monsoon did not arrive like weather. It arrived like a decision.

Elodie felt it before she saw it—a pressure change in her ears that had nothing to do with depth, a taste of iron on the wind coming off the escarpment, the gulls gone silent and inland as if they'd read the same ledger she had and knew which column their names fell under. The cove that had been green glass for three days now shivered with a swell running out of the southeast, long and low and patient, the kind of sea that was only clearing its throat.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Debt*—Bram's borrowed ketch, patched hull, honest rust—and checked her gauges a fourth time. Two tanks lashed to the frame. The cutting charge from the depot raid, wrapped in oilcloth and strapped to her harness like a second heart. Her knife. Her slate. A wax-sealed tube for the documents, because paper drowned as easily as people.

Bram was already on deck, working the tether drum with hands that had learned knots in places he still didn't talk about. He'd run the line three times along its full length, testing every splice, every thimble, every turn of the winch. She had watched him do it without saying anything, because she understood what it was. Not fussing. Confession. Every knot tied twice was an apology for something.

"Wind's backing," he said. "We have maybe ninety minutes before the squall line makes the surface work untenable. After that you're diving blind in a washing machine."

"Ninety minutes is generous." She pulled her hood back, let the spray hit her face. "The hold door took a partial charge yesterday. Another one set right and it comes off like a lid."

"And if it doesn't come off like a lid?"

"Then I come up, we argue about it, and I go back down."

He looked at her then—the look she was learning to read, the archivist's look, cataloguing her the way he'd once catalogued orders he couldn't stop. Fear in him, yes. But underneath it something steadier, something that had survived a burning archive and a decade of running and a betrayal she hadn't fully forgiven and wasn't sure she wanted to, because forgiving it would mean admitting how much of her had already rearranged itself around him.

"Tether signals," he said, all business now. "One pull, okay. Two pulls, coming up. Three pulls, emergency—I haul regardless of what you're doing. Four pulls—" his voice caught on it, just slightly, "—four pulls means cut everything and I'm coming down after you."

"Four pulls means you're a fool."

"Four pulls means I'm not doing this again," he said quietly. "Standing on a dock while someone I—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Tomas lived in that unfinished sentence between them like a third diver, always below them, always pulling.

* * *

The water took her the way it always did: completely.

At ten meters the storm above became a rumor, a gray flicker, a god turning over in sleep. At twenty it was nothing at all. The Vessany's Pride lay on the shelf below the escarpment wall where the river met the old sea channel, and in the failing light she was less a wreck than a cathedral—one hundred and forty meters of refugee ship settled into the silt with her masts broken but her bones proud, railings furred with white anemones, the great hatch coamings standing open like mouths mid-word.

Elodie had dived her four times now. The first time, she had heard the bell.

It was ringing again today. Faint, arrhythmic, more suggestion than sound—the ship's bell torn loose in the sinking, hung now from a stanchion near the bow, swinging in the surge with a voice too soft for living ears. Divers' lore said drowned bells rang for the dead. Elodie had stopped believing in lore somewhere around the navy chapel where they'd given her an empty coffin and a folded flag and a lie with gold braid on it. But she heard it anyway, and this time, instead of horror, she felt something closer to acknowledgment. *You're here. We know. Go get it.*

The hold door waited aft, beneath the collapsed boat deck. Yesterday's charge had split the seam along its lower edge; the plate stood proud of the frame by the width of her hand, exhaling silt in slow breaths whenever the surge pushed through the wreck. She worked herself into position against the hull, boots braced on a stanchion, and unpacked the cutting charge with fingers made clumsy by cold and by the weight of what waited behind the steel.

Captain's log. Sealed orders. Names.

Somewhere behind those bolts was the document that would make Corvane's reasonable face finally crack—orders signed, dated, filed in a hand that had never expected daylight. Bram had described them from memory a dozen times: he'd catalogued the copy eleven years ago, logged it, shelved it, and then tried to steal it back, and the trying had cost him his rank, his country, and nearly his life. He had never seen the original. He had spent ten years not knowing whether the thing he'd ruined himself for even existed outside his memory.

She was about to find out for both of them.

The charge went on low, against the hinge-side seam, taped and wedged and double-checked the way Bram checked knots. She backed off to twenty meters, put the wreck's engine casing between herself and the door, covered her ears, and closed the circuit.

The explosion underwater was not sound. It was a fist through the whole world—a concussion she felt in her teeth, her sternum, the fluid of her eyes. Silt erupted in a brown galaxy. Somewhere above, faintly, she imagined Bram's hands going tight on the drum.

She counted to thirty. Then she swam into the cloud.

* * *

Visibility inside the cloud was arm's length, then half that. She found the door by touch, by memory, by the shape of the gap where solid steel had been. It had worked. The plate hung inward like a jaw knocked loose, and beyond it the hold breathed out cold black water that had been sealed since the night the Pride went down.

Her lamp found the first of them almost immediately.

Bones. Not scattered—arranged. Lashed into the hold when the ship sank, the refugees had died in the dark together, and the silt had taken them gently, and now they lay in rows and clusters with their belongings around them: a child's shoe fused to the sediment. A wooden bird, carved, still clutched. A wedding band grown into bone. Elodie hung in the doorway of their tomb and made herself look, because looking was the whole point, because Corvane's peace was built on never looking, and someone had to be the eyes.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, and it wasn't enough, and she sent it anyway. *I'm sorry. I'm here to take your names out of the dark.*

The captain's cabin annex was aft of the main hold, a small steel room that had flooded slow—the log cabinet's brass fittings were barely tarnished. She pried it open with her knife. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against a flooding the captain had clearly foreseen: the log, thick as a brick, its pages swollen but legible. And beneath it, in a lead sleeve stamped with the seal she now knew better than her own name—the seal of Vessany, the nation that no longer existed except in wrecks and widows and one old woman's lighthouse—the sealed orders.

She pulled the sleeve free. Through the lead she could feel the shape of folded paper, official weight, the crisp authority of a thing typed by a man at a desk while a ship full of families sailed toward the hole he'd ordered blown in the water beneath them.

Her gauge said fifty bar. Plenty. Ninety minutes, Bram had said, and she'd been down maybe twenty-five.

She turned for the door.

And the squall line, ahead of schedule the way everything in this story was ahead of schedule, reached down into the water and took hold of the wreck.

* * *

She felt it first as a change in the bell—that soft arrhythmic tolling suddenly quickening, urgent, the surge arriving through the escarpment channel in pulses strong enough to move her body sideways even inside the hold. Then the silt came back, a second eruption, this one from the seabed itself as the swell ground the wreck against the shelf. Her lamp beam shrank to a brown smear. The door plate she'd blasted free shifted with a groan she felt in her molars, and something in the overhead—the boat deck, the davits, a hundred tons of loose history—settled with a sound like the ship clearing her throat.

Move. She moved.

The exit was where she'd left it. That much was true. What wasn't true anymore was the way out beyond it—the guide line she'd run from the hold to the shot line to the surface had parted somewhere aft; she found the end of it whipping in the current like a severed nerve, and beyond it the water was the color of strong tea and getting worse.

Panic is a diver who arrives early and stays late. Elodie knew its voice. *You're lost. You're blind. You're fifty meters from a wreck that wants to keep you.* She put her back against the hull, planted her fins, and did the arithmetic instead of the fear. Shot line was north-northeast of the hold, fifteen meters, maybe eighteen. Current running south-southeast. So: swim north, hug the hull, count kick cycles.

She counted. Ten kicks. Twenty. The hull ended under her hand and there was open water, tea-brown, featureless, the escarpment wall somewhere to her right like a cliff face in a dream. Thirty kicks. Forty. Nothing. No shot line, no anchor chain, no light from above—the surface had become a ceiling of moving smoke.

Fifty bar. Forty-five. The cold was in her joints now, and the cold was also fear wearing a coat.

She stopped. Hung in the brown nothing. And for the first time since Tomas's funeral, since the empty coffin, since the first night she'd slept on a dredge barge because stopping meant being findable—for the first time in eleven years, Elodie Ravensworth admitted she could not do this alone.

She reached for the tether.

Gone. Cut or parted—she'd known it, some animal part of her had known it since the second eruption, and she'd refused to add it to the arithmetic because adding it made the sum unbearable. The line that connected her to the surface, to the ketch, to Bram—to the one person on this earth who had chosen to be her line—was gone.

Forty bar. The gauge needle seemed to be moving faster now, though she knew that was impossible. Everything was impossible. The bell was ringing somewhere behind her, faster, frantic, and she thought: *that's not the surge. That's not the surge at all.*

Then the line hit her across the chest.

Not the tether—a new line, thrown, paid out fast, and it wrapped her forearm and went taut with a jerk that nearly took her arm from its socket. She grabbed it with both hands. It hauled. Steady, powerful, rhythmic hauls—not panic-pulling, not dragging, but the measured, practiced work of someone who had done this, who knew exactly how hard to pull a diver through zero visibility without tearing her mask off or ripping her regulator from her mouth. Haul, pause for her to kick, haul again.

She followed it. Kicked when it paused, tucked when it turned, and let herself be reeled through the brown dark like something precious being brought home. Once, the line drew her past a looming mass—the shot line's anchor block, she realized later—and once it turned hard and she understood Bram was reading the current off the far end, feeling the ketch's drift through his palms, navigating her by tension alone.

Thirty-five bar when her head broke surface.

The world above was rain like gravel and a sky the color of wet slate, and the *Kestrel's Debt* was heaving alongside, and Bram was on his knees at the rail with the line wrapped twice around his forearms, hauling her the last meters hand over hand, and his face—

His face was the thing she would keep. Not fear. Something past fear, stripped to the studs, a man watching the water give back the only thing he had decided he couldn't lose. He got her over the gunwale and onto the deck and his hands were everywhere at once—mask, tank valve, the pulse at her throat—as if he needed to verify with his own fingers that she was real and breathing and his.

"I lost the tether," she rasped. Rain hammered the deck. "Second surge. It parted."

"I know. I felt it go." His voice was wrecked. "I felt it go and I threw the spare over the side and swam the end out on the heaving line until I found you. Blind. In that." He laughed, or sobbed, or both. "I haven't been in open water in eleven years, Elodie. I swore I never would be again."

"You were on the deck."

"The last eight meters, I wasn't." He said it simply, the way he said the hardest things, and she understood: he had gone into the water for her. The defector who feared drowning more than firing squads, the man who ran from navies for a decade—he had gone over the rail into a monsoon sea on a hope and a heaving line.

She looked at him, kneeling in the rain, soaked through, shaking, holding her wrist like a man holding a mooring line in a hurricane, and something in her chest that had been armored for eleven years did not come off so much as simply stop being there.

"I have it," she said. "Bram. I have both of them."

She got the lead sleeve out of her harness with numb fingers. He took it in both hands like communion, and even through the rain and the dark and the rising scream of the squall line she watched him read the stamp through the lead, watched ten years of shame and exile and running come to a single point in his face.

"It's real," he whispered. "The original. It exists."

"It exists." She gripped his collar, made him look at her. "And so does the fleet. Look astern."

Through the rain curtain, out where the cove opened to the channel, lights were appearing. Running lights, masthead lights, the white shapes of hulls assembling out of the murk one by one, patient as a verdict. Six tides had become five, had become this—the fleet arriving with the storm, closing the mouth of the cove like a hand closing around a coin.

Bram pulled her down behind the bulwark as a searchlight swept the water where they'd surfaced seconds before.

"They're between us and the open sea," he said.

Elodie looked at the lights, then at the lead sleeve in her lap, then at the distant point past the fleet's western edge where—impossibly, stubbornly, faithfully—a single white beam was sweeping the rain: Maren's lighthouse, burning on schedule, marking the one road through the escarpment waters that no warship's chart dared follow.

"No," Elodie said, and felt the truth of it settle through her like ballast. "They're between us and *their* sea."

She started the engine.
