## 21. Names Read Aloud

The escarpment in autumn smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and Elodie stood at its edge in a borrowed coat that didn't fit her shoulders, looking down at the river she had spent eleven years learning to disappear into.

Below, the water ran the color of hammered pewter, the same water that had given her the chest, the bell, the hold, the names. Above her, on the flat shelf of grass where the old signal station had stood before the war took even the memory of what it signaled, someone had built a platform of rough pine planks. On it stood a table, and on the table lay a book.

Not a grand book. Not a ledger bound in navy leather or an archive folio with wax seals and ribbons. It was a schoolteacher's notebook, the kind sold for three coppers in any river town, its cover soft with handling, because Maren had insisted it be ordinary. *They were ordinary people,* the old woman had said, when Bram offered to have the list transcribed by a stationer in the capital. *Ordinary people drowned like ordinary people. Let them be read from something that looks like it came out of a kitchen drawer.*

So the notebook sat there in the wind, open to a page of Maren's careful, upright hand, four hundred and six names in columns, and the wind kept trying to turn the pages as if it too wanted to read them first.

* * *

Elodie had not slept. She had told herself it was the preparations—the correspondenceents arriving under foreign flags sewn into their coat linings, the families' coalition delegates coming up the escarpment road in carts and on foot, the small army of witnesses Bram had spent two months assembling with the patience of a man who had once catalogued ten thousand documents and knew how to make a paper trail walk uphill. There had been arrangements. There had been arguments about arrangements. There had been, for three nights running, Bram pacing the lighthouse floor below Maren's lamp while Elodie pretended to sleep and listened to his footsteps wear a groove in the boards.

But that was not why she hadn't slept, and she knew it.

She hadn't slept because tomorrow—today, now, this gray-gold morning—the name would be said aloud. *Tomas Ravensworth.* Out loud. In front of people. In a country that had spent eleven years pretending he had never existed, that had returned his effects to her in a box marked with a file number instead of a name, that had sent her a form letter signed by a clerk who spelled Ravensworth two different ways on the same page.

She had carried the sound of his name inside her mouth for eleven years like a stone she couldn't swallow and couldn't spit out. Said only once, at the graveside that wasn't a graveside, over a coffin that wasn't a coffin, because the navy had buried an empty box and called it closure. She had whispered it since, in the dark of rented rooms, in the hush between dives, testing whether it still hurt. It always did. But whispering was not saying. Whispering was still hiding.

Today the whole escarpment would hear it.

* * *

The crowd gathered the way weather gathers—slowly, then all at once.

By midmorning there were hundreds. Elodie watched them come up the road and tried to count them the way she counted things underwater, in fathoms and masses, because actual numbers made her hands shake. Old men in coats too heavy for the season. Women holding photographs the way you hold something already broken, so no further piece falls off. Children who didn't understand yet, wearing their grandparents' grief like oversized clothing. A delegation from the Vessany diaspora in their inland colors, muted blues and river-grays, the colors of a nation that existed now only in fabric and memory and, as of nine days ago, in every newspaper from here to the far capitals.

Nine days. The story had broken in four cities in a single morning—Bram had planned it that way, coordinated to the hour, so that no single government could smother its copy—and Corvane had been recalled before the week was out. Recalled in disgrace, though Elodie suspected disgrace would sit lightly on a man who had never believed he was wrong. That was the thing about reasonable monsters. They retired to their gardens and called it peace.

She hadn't gone to watch him leave. Bram had asked if she wanted to. She'd said no, and then, after a moment, she'd said why: *"Because if I stand in a crowd and watch him walk past, I'll be watching him again. And I'm done giving him my eyes."*

Bram had looked at her then with an expression she was still learning to read—not pity, not admiration, something quieter than both—and said, "Then look at this instead," and handed her the notebook, and Maren's columns of names, and let her stand there holding four hundred and six lives in her two hands until her arms stopped shaking.

* * *

Maren spoke first.

The old woman climbed the platform steps slowly, one hand on the rail, refusing all offers of help with a flick of her fingers that had probably ended conversations aboard a sinking ship sixty years ago. The crowd went silent for her the way water goes still around a keel. She was tiny at that distance, a scrap of black wool and white hair, and her voice, when she began, was thin—but it carried. Wind off the river helped. Or maybe grief carries, Elodie thought. Maybe it has always been able to carry; we just rarely give it room.

"I was nineteen," Maren said. "I was in the third berth, port side, and I remember the water coming in cold as a hand. Four hundred and five people went into the river that night, and I am the one who came back up, and for sixty years I have asked myself why." She paused. Her hands found each other at her waist and held on. "I stopped asking last month. Because the answer is standing behind me. All of you. You are why. Somebody had to live long enough to say the names."

And then she turned to the table, and opened the notebook, and began.

* * *

Elodie had thought she was prepared. She had rehearsed this moment against herself for weeks the way a diver rehearses a blackout—what she would do, where she would put her eyes, how she would breathe. She had decided she would stand at the back. She had decided she would not cry in public; she had spent eleven years perfecting the art of crying nowhere, invisibly, underwater where the salt had somewhere honest to go, and she had assumed today would be more of the same, just with an audience.

She was wrong about nearly all of it.

She could not stay at the back. Bram found her there twenty minutes in—she felt him before she saw him, the way she always did now, some current-sense she couldn't explain and had stopped trying to—and he didn't say anything, just stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and after a while she realized she had drifted forward without deciding to, drawn up through the crowd like something rising through darkening water toward a light it had been told to fear.

And she could not keep the tears down. They came at the fourth name, or the ninth, she lost count—a woman's name, *Ilse Varrow*, and something about the shape of it, the ordinary domestic music of it, broke something loose that all her armor had been built specifically to hold. She cried the way the rain was starting to fall, quietly at first and then without any interest in dignity, standing in a crowd of strangers who were all crying too, and nobody looked at her, and that was the miracle of it. Nobody looked. For eleven years she had hidden her face because being seen meant being found, and found meant taken, and taken meant Tomas died twice. Here, among these people, being seen was the whole point. Being seen was the ceremony.

The names went on. Maren read for an hour, and then her voice failed her, and a delegate from the coalition took the book and read, and then a fisherman's daughter, and then a boy of maybe twelve who stood on his toes to see over the pages and pronounced each name with the enormous care of someone learning that words can be heavy.

The sky lowered. The first real rain of the season came in off the west, soft and steady, and nobody left. Umbrellas bloomed and were shared with strangers. Water ran down the escarpment shelf in bright threads and dripped off the platform's edge, and Elodie thought: *the river is listening. The river brought them home and now it's come up the hill to hear.*

* * *

*Tomas Ravensworth.*

The reader—a coalition woman with a voice like a cello—said it plainly, without ceremony, the way Maren had instructed them all: *say the names as if introducing friends.* And the world did not end. The ground did not open. No patrol boat swept the channel, no admiral's men came over the hill, no clerk's pen struck him from existence a second time. The name simply went out into the rain and the air and the ears of several hundred living people, and stayed there, and became true in a way it had never been allowed to be.

He had been real. He was real. He had joined the navy at seventeen because he believed in it, had written her one desperate letter from a posting he wasn't supposed to describe, had died trying to tell the truth about four hundred and six people, and had waited eleven years in the dark of the river for his sister to stop running long enough to find him.

*I found you,* she thought, standing in the rain with her face bare and her arms useless at her sides. *It took me eleven years and a chest stamped in salt and a man who burned his own life down to help me do it, but I found you. You're not mine to carry anymore. You're theirs now. Everyone's.*

She heard herself make a sound she didn't recognize—half sob, half laugh, the sound of a load-bearing wall coming down—and Bram's arm came around her shoulders, and she let it. She let it the way she had learned, in the last clear water, to let his tether take her weight. Not because she was weak. Because she was finally, deliberately, attached.

* * *

Afterward, the crowd thinned the way crowds do, reluctantly, in knots of twos and threes, people touching the table as they passed, some leaving flowers, one old man leaving a child's shoe, which no one moved. The rain settled in for the afternoon. The notebook was closed, wrapped in oilcloth by the delegate's careful hands—it would go to the new national archive, the one being built, the one Bram had been asked to consult on, a sentence that still made him laugh in a startled, disbelieving way whenever anyone said it aloud.

Elodie stayed until the platform was empty except for her and the rain and the view of the river.

"You're supposed to come inside," Bram said from behind her. He had two cups of something hot and steaming and was holding one out like a peace offering, which, she supposed, after everything, it still occasionally was. "Maren will send the lighthouse cat after you otherwise. She's threatening it seriously."

"In a minute."

He didn't push. He came and stood beside her instead, and they watched the water together, the pewter river unspooling below the escarpment, carrying its rain and its silt and its dead and, lately, some of them back.

"Eleven years," she said. "I had a rule. Never stay anywhere long enough to be found. Never sleep two nights in the same bed, never dive the same wreck twice, never—" She stopped. Started again, lower. "I thought if I kept moving, nothing could catch me. And the whole time, the thing I was running from was just... a name I wouldn't say out loud. I could outrun any fleet in the world. I couldn't outrun that."

"And now?"

She turned the cup in her hands. Below them, far downstream, she could just make out the masts of the salvage barge at its mooring—their barge, bought with money she still didn't entirely trust herself to own, fitted out for the slow, patient work of raising the Pride's remains as a documented grave site. Permits posted publicly. Manifests filed openly. A work schedule nailed to the harbor board where anyone could read it. Everything she had spent her adult life avoiding: visibility, permanence, a fixed point on a chart.

"Now I'm going to stay in one place," she said, "and let the whole world know exactly where that is." She looked at him. Rain dripped off her chin and she didn't wipe it away. "Terrifying. Absolutely terrifying. I've fought admirals, Bram. This is worse."

He laughed—that startled laugh she loved—and put his arm around her again, and this time she leaned into it fully, letting it hold some of her weight, the way she would for the rest of her life, if he'd let her, which the way his hand tightened on her shoulder suggested he very much intended to.

"The barge sails on the morning tide," he said. "First job on the manifest. Want to hear it?"

"Tell me."

"Recovery of remains, section seven, near the forward hold." His voice gentled. "There's a marker buoy to place. We get to choose what goes on it."

Elodie looked out at the river—at the gray water, the falling rain, the far shore where the escarpment ran down to meet it—and found that the answer was already in her mouth, waiting, light as a lifted anchor.

"Tomas Ravensworth," she said. "And four hundred and five others. Beloved. Found."

The rain fell softer, as if agreeing, and down on the mooring the barge lanterns came alight one by one against the dusk—small, stubborn, and lit on purpose.
