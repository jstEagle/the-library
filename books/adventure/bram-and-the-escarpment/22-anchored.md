## 22. Anchored

### Elodie

The river had changed its mind about winter.

Elodie felt it first in the water itself—clearer now, colder, holding light longer into the morning than it ever had during the monsoon months. She stood at the rail of the *Honest Work*, a name Bram had insisted on and she had pretended to hate, and watched the sun come up over the escarpment the way she used to watch horizons: looking for what was coming.

Nothing was coming. That was the strange part. That was the part she still hadn't learned to trust.

Below her, marked by four white buoys they'd set themselves, lay the grave of the *Vessany's Pride*. Not a wreck anymore. They'd stopped calling it that in the third month, after the families' coalition had argued the word into something gentler. A grave site. Documented, surveyed, photographed from every angle, its coordinates published in three languages so that anyone who wanted to could come and stand on the shore above it and know exactly where their dead were sleeping.

She'd spent eleven years diving wrecks nobody claimed. Now she dove one everybody did, and it was harder work and better work, and some mornings she woke before dawn not because she was running from something but because she wanted to be the first one in the water.

"Tea," Bram said behind her, and pressed a tin mug into her hands. Steam curled up past her chin. He'd learned how she took it—too strong, too sweet, the way Tomas had taught her when they were children drinking from chipped cups on their mother's step—and he never commented on it, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"You're up early," she said.

"I'm always up early."

"You're up early *nervously*." She turned. He had his archive face on, the one he thought was neutral and wasn't—the slight tightening around the eyes, the jaw held like a man carrying something breakable in his mouth. "What is it?"

He hesitated. She watched him decide, the way she'd watched him decide a hundred times since the storm, since Corvane's boat had slid away into the rain and left them alive for reasons neither of them fully understood. He decided to tell her. He almost always decided to tell her now. It had taken months. It would probably take years. She had decided that was all right.

"The coalition sent the final list," he said. "Of names recovered from the hold. Cross-referenced against Maren's testimony and the captain's log." He handed her a folded paper, soft already at the creases from being handled. "There are forty-one confirmed. And there's one more they're asking about. A deckhand listed only as R. Voss. Maren thinks—" He stopped. "Maren thinks he might have been from her village. She wants to dive. Or at least go down in the bell. She's seventy-eight, Elodie. The doctors say no. She says the doctors weren't on the Pride."

Elodie looked out at the buoys bobbing in the gold water.

"Set up the bell," she said. "I'll take her down myself."

* * *

The diving bell was old navy surplus, bought cheap after the scandal broke and half the salvage fleet was decommissioned in disgrace. It hung from the *Honest Work*'s crane like a brass heart on a chain, and inside it, wrapped in blankets with her hands folded in her lap like a woman going to church, sat Maren Ostrand, last living survivor of the *Vessany's Pride*, watching the river close over the porthole the way other people watch a door open onto home.

"I was nineteen," Maren said. Her voice came through the speaking tube slightly warped by water and age, but steady. "The water came in through the aft hold and I went up the ladder and I didn't look back. You understand? I climbed and I climbed and I never once looked back to see who was behind me."

"Forty-one people made it because you lit that lamp afterward," Elodie said. "Every ship that found this coast safe in sixty years found it because of you."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Elodie agreed. "It isn't. But it's something."

They descended through the clear cold layers of the river. Elodie worked the winch controls with gloved hands, easing them down along the guide line to the main deck, which lay at forty meters with its rails furred in white silt and its masts gone to coral-colored stone. Through the porthole, the *Pride* spread out below them—not a horror anymore, not even really a mystery. Just a ship. Just the place where it happened.

"There," Maren whispered. "By the foremast. That's where Ida Kessler stood. She was singing. Did you know that? When the charges went, she was singing, and I have thought about that every day of my life, whether she finished the song."

The bell drifted to a stop. Silt rose in a slow ghost around them. Elodie put her hand flat against the porthole glass, and on the other side, inches away, the river pressed back—patient, enormous, keeping what it kept.

"We can read the names down here," Elodie said. "If you want. We brought the list."

Maren was quiet for a long time. Down here, quiet had weight; it settled on your shoulders like the water did.

"Yes," she said finally. "Start with the ones nobody knew. Start with R. Voss."

So Elodie read them, all forty-two, her voice low through the tube while the river listened, and when she reached the last name—the deckhand, the unknown, the one the record had nearly swallowed whole—Maren closed her eyes and said, "Rurik. His name was Rurik Voss. He carried my trunk aboard. Forty-three names, then. Write it down. Somebody should write it down."

And somebody did. Bram wrote it down.

* * *

### Bram

Bram had been an archivist for nine years before he became a fugitive, and he had discovered, in the months since, that he was still an archivist—that the instinct ran deeper than fear or loyalty or anything else. He catalogued everything now. The survey photographs of the *Pride*, numbered and cross-indexed. The letters from the families, each one copied twice, one copy sealed against fire. The transcript of the tribunal, all six hundred pages of it, though everyone knew the verdict before it opened. And now, in a ledger bound in blue cloth that Elodie had bought him at the harbor market, the master list of the dead, forty-two names long, waiting for the forty-third.

He wrote *Rurik Voss* in careful ink and sat back and looked at the page, and felt the thing he felt most days now, which was not peace exactly—he wasn't sure he'd recognize peace—but something adjacent to it. Something like standing on solid ground after years of believing the ground was a story told to children.

The door of the boathouse opened and let in the smell of the river and the sound of Elodie stamping snow-melt off her boots.

"She's asleep in the cabin," Elodie said. "Took the tea I offered and the blanket and told me my knots were an insult to the profession. I think that means she likes me."

"It does. That's how she talked about the lighthouse keepers she trained. Highest praise available." He turned the ledger toward her. "Forty-three."

Elodie leaned over the desk. Her hair was still damp from the bell, and she smelled of cold water and lamp oil, and Bram had learned—slowly, clumsily, the way he learned everything that mattered—that he was allowed to notice these things. That noticing was not the same as taking. That she noticed him back.

"Rurik Voss," she read aloud. "Carried Maren's trunk aboard." She straightened. "You know what's strange? Eleven years I dove wrecks nobody claimed, and I told myself it was because the pay was better and the questions were fewer. But it was this. It was always this. Somebody has to write down the name."

"Somebody did," Bram said. "It just took us too long."

"Then we won't take long again."

She said it simply, without drama, the way she said most true things, and Bram understood that she was not making a vow so much as describing a fact about herself, the way you might describe the current of a river you intended to live beside forever. He picked up the pen and added a line beneath the name—a provenance note, archivist's habit: *Identified by Maren Ostrand, survivor, 14th of Frostmonth. Witnessed by E. Ravensworth and B. Calloway.*

Calloway. His real name, the one he'd buried with the archives, the one he'd given back to himself the day the tribunal published it in exoneration. It still startled him sometimes to hear it spoken aloud, to sign it on salvage permits, to see it stenciled on the hull of the *Honest Work*. For years his name had been the most dangerous thing he owned. Now it was just his.

"Corvane's letter came," Elodie said, and he looked up sharply.

She held it out. He hadn't seen her take it from the mail crate. The envelope was heavy paper, official, the seal broken. Admiral Corvane—no, not admiral anymore; the recall had stripped him of rank along with everything else—wrote in a small, precise hand.

"He's asking permission," Bram said slowly, reading. "To visit the site. To stand on the shore. He says—" He paused. "He says he has no right to ask. He asks anyway."

"What do you want to do?"

Bram set the letter down. Outside, the river moved the way it always moved, indifferent and generous at once, carrying silt and light and the occasional recovered thing toward the sea. He thought about the man Corvane had been in the eye of the storm—reasonable, terrible, sincere—and the man he must be now, an old soldier with nothing left to protect except the shape of his own guilt.

"I think," Bram said, "that the river gives some things back. Maybe it can give him back whatever he lost by coming here. Maybe seeing the buoys is the sentence the courts couldn't write." He met her eyes. "But it's not my shore alone anymore. What do you want?"

Elodie was quiet for a moment. Then she took the pen from his hand, turned Corvane's letter over, and wrote on the back in her blunt diver's script:

*Come at low tide. Bring nothing. Stand where the families stand.*

She signed both their names.

* * *

### Elodie

He came on a gray morning two weeks later, alone, in a hired skiff, wearing civilian clothes that fit him like a disguise he'd finally earned the right to wear badly. Elodie watched him from the deck of the *Honest Work* as he stepped onto the dock, and she felt—nothing she'd expected. Not rage. Not satisfaction. Just the strange human arithmetic of watching a large man become small.

Corvane walked to the shore marker—the granite stone the coalition had raised, engraved with all forty-three names now, Rurik Voss last and newest—and he stood there for a long time. Long enough that Elodie's tea went cold in her hands. Long enough that gulls landed near him and decided he was furniture.

When he finally turned and saw her, he did not approach. He inclined his head, the smallest possible degree, and mouthed something she couldn't hear across the distance, and she understood that he wasn't asking to be heard. He was asking to be permitted the gesture at all.

She raised her mug to him. Just that. Then she turned back to the work—coiling lines, checking the bell's air valves, the small honest labor of a small honest operation—and when she glanced back ten minutes later, the skiff was gone and the shore was empty except for the stone and the wind.

Bram appeared beside her, following her gaze.

"Well?" he asked.

"He stood there," Elodie said. "That's all he did. That's all he could do." She shrugged. "Maybe that's what justice looks like from the outside. Not thunder. Just a man standing where he put the dead, knowing he can't leave."

Bram's hand found hers. His fingers were ink-stained and cold and certain.

"Tomas would have hated that he got off so easy," she said.

"Tomas would have hated it for exactly one day," Bram said, "and then he'd have gotten drunk with the man and argued philosophy until dawn. From everything you've told me."

Elodie laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising her, rising out of her like a bubble from deep water, breaking clean at the surface. She realized she did that more now. Realized she'd stopped flinching at her own brother's name, that grief had become less like a wound and more like a scar: tender, permanent, hers.

"Come on," she said. "Tide's turning. We've got a mast section to raise before dark, and Maren will inspect our knots."

* * *

### Bram

They raised the foremast on the evening tide, slow and careful, cradled in canvas slings, and laid it on the dock under lamplight for the conservators who would come in the spring. Barnacles had colonized it in patterns like old handwriting. Elodie ran her palm along the wood and said nothing, and Bram had learned that her silences underwater-adjacent were not empty—they were full, the way the hold of the *Pride* had been full, with things worth keeping.

Later, when Maren was asleep in the cabin and the lamps were trimmed low, they sat on the roof of the wheelhouse with blankets around their shoulders, watching the moon come up over the escarpment. Below them the buoys knocked gently against their moorings, keeping time like patient bells.

"Do you ever think about leaving?" Elodie asked. "Really leaving. Somewhere the river doesn't reach."

Bram considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid dearly to collect it.

"I think about places," he said. "Mountains. Cities with good libraries. But I don't think about leaving anymore. There's a difference." He turned to look at her, her face silvered and sharp and calm. "For nine years I was an archivist of other people's lives, and then for three more I was an archivist of my own lies. This is the first thing I've built that's actually mine. Us. The boat. The ledger with forty-three names in it. I'm not anchored because I'm afraid to drift, Elodie. I'm anchored because this is where I want to be."

She was quiet a moment. Then she leaned her head against his shoulder, and he felt the weight of her settle—not leaning, exactly. Trusting.

"My rule used to be never stay anywhere long enough to be found," she said. "You know what I figured out? I was never hiding from the navy. I was hiding from the day someone would find me and I'd have to decide whether to stay." She lifted her head. "You found me. I stayed. Turns out that was the whole decision, and I made it in a tavern over bad wine before I even liked you."

"You liked me immediately."

"I tolerated you with unusual patience."

"Elodie Ravensworth," Bram said gravely, "you fell into my eyes across a crowded tavern."

She laughed again, and shoved him, and he caught her wrist and kissed her knuckles where the rope burns had faded to pale threads, and above them the stars came out over the escarpment in their ancient, indifferent multitudes, and none of it mattered except this: the warmth, the water, the work waiting in the morning, the ledger open on the desk below with room in it for however many names the river chose, in its own time, to give back.

The river still kept its dead. But it had learned, in the way of rivers, to loosen its grip—to surrender, name by name, what truth demanded returned. And on the shore above the grave of the *Vessany's Pride*, the granite stone stood in the wind with its forty-three names facing the water, and every morning the light came up the channel and touched them, first thing, before it touched anything else.

Elodie watched it happen the next dawn from the deck of the *Honest Work*, tea in her hands, Bram's shoulder warm against hers, and for the first time in eleven years she did not check the horizon for what was coming.

She checked it for what was already here.

It was enough. It was, at last, home.
