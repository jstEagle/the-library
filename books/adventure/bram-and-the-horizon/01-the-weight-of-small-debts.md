## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The rain in Serem never fell so much as it hung, a grey suspension that turned the whole town the color of wet slate. Jonah Blackwood had lived inside that grey for eleven months now, long enough to know which of the market awnings leaked, which of the tea stalls watered their brew, and which of the porters' lines would take him on without asking for papers he could not produce.

He went by Jompa these days. It was a common enough name in the border country, common as river stones, and it slid off the tongue of anyone who used it without catching on anything. *Jompa, take this load up to the checkpoint.* *Jompa, you've got strong shoulders for a man who eats so little.* *Jompa, where are you from?* And he would answer, the way he always answered, with a shrug and a half-smile: *Around. Here and there.*

Here and there was a lie with a mountain range inside it.

This morning the load was roofing tin — forty sheets of it, bundled and strapped to a wicker frame that bit into his shoulders through two layers of sacking. The delivery was for a government sub-office at the top of the town's single steep street, and Jonah climbed it slowly, not from weakness but from habit, because a porter who moved too well invited questions, and a man named Jompa could not afford questions. He kept his eyes down, counting the cracked flagstones the way other men counted sheep, and let the rain bead and run off the brim of his hat until the street became nothing but rhythm: step, breath, step, breath, the tin shifting its weight with each stride like a living thing riding him.

At the top of the street, the clerk signing for the tin looked at him a moment too long.

"You're new," the clerk said. "I haven't seen you before."

"Been here since spring," Jonah said. His voice came out flat and low, pitched to be forgettable. "Portering mostly."

"Since spring." The clerk made a note in a ledger whose pages had gone soft and wavy with damp. "There was a guide, years back. Blackwood. Worked these ranges before they closed the upper valley. You'd have been too young for that, I suppose."

Jonah felt the name land somewhere beneath his ribs, the way a falling stone lands in still water — one clean plunge, then rings spreading outward through everything.

"Never heard of him," he said.

"No," the clerk said, still looking at him. "Nobody has, really. That's rather the point." He signed the chit, tore off the carbon copy, and held it out without quite meeting Jonah's eye, and Jonah understood that the man knew exactly what he was doing — that the whole exchange had been a small cruelty performed with bureaucratic precision, or perhaps only a small curiosity, which in a border town amounted to the same thing.

He took the chit and went back down into the rain.

* * *

The porters' line gathered under the corrugated awning behind the grain shop, where the men stood in a loose hierarchy determined by seniority, strength, and how much trouble they were willing to make. Jonah had learned his place near the middle — visible enough to get work, unremarkable enough to be left alone. He drank salt tea and watched the mist come down off the ridgeline above the town like something being poured.

"Jompa." It was Pemba, the line boss, a squat man with a voice like gravel poured slowly. "Two loads tomorrow. Rice and kerosene up to Namche La. Good pay."

"I'll take it."

"Everyone will take it. That's why it's good pay." Pemba squinted at him. "You don't complain much. I notice that about you."

"It doesn't help."

Pemba laughed, a short bark. "No. It doesn't. But most men complain anyway. It's how they prove they're alive." He moved off down the line, allocating suffering, and Jonah stood with his tea going cold and thought about what it meant that a line boss had noticed his silence as a distinguishing feature. Eleven months in Serem, and the sum total of what anyone knew about him was that he didn't complain.

That was invisibility done properly, he told himself. That was the craft of it. A blacklisted guide did not simply lose his license; he lost his name from every register in three districts, lost the right to lead anyone above the snowline, lost the letters of reference that might have carried him over into some other trade. What remained was the body — shoulders, lungs, the knowledge in his feet — and the body could work as a porter anywhere, because porters were interchangeable by design. Porters were furniture. Furniture didn't need a history.

And yet there were mornings, like this one, when the knowledge sat in him like ballast and would not stay quiet: the way a route reads itself to a trained eye, the angle of scree, the sound a snowbridge makes an hour before it fails, the exact weight of weather coming down a valley. All of it still there, all of it useless, a language spoken fluently in a country where no one would let him talk. Invisibility, he was beginning to understand, was not peace. It was a life half-lived, a man standing forever in the doorway of his own existence, holding a coat he was never allowed to put on.

He finished the tea and took another load anyway, because the alternative was thinking.

* * *

The moneylender's shop sat at the bend where the main street narrowed, between a tailor's and a shuttered photo studio, and it announced itself by smell before sight — old paper, lamp oil, and the particular sourness of lentils stored too long. Tsering Norbu had run it for thirty years, they said, and had the face of a man who had spent those thirty years watching other people's misfortunes ripen.

Jonah owed him four hundred rupees. It had begun as eighty, advanced against a bad week the previous winter when the portering work dried up and he'd needed medicine for a chest infection, and it had grown the way such debts grow, with interest calculated in a hand so fine and patient it seemed almost tender. He had paid back sixty in installments. The remainder had sat there for months, a small stone in his shoe, tolerable.

Today, when he pushed open the door, Tsering Norbu looked up from his ledger with an expression Jonah had learned to dread more than anger: mild, regretful, immovable.

"Jompa." He gestured to the stool across the desk. "Sit. Have some tea."

"I can't stay long. There's a load—"

"The load will wait. Tea first." The old man poured it himself, which was worse than sending a boy, because it meant ceremony, and ceremony meant business. He set the cup down and folded his hands. "A letter came for me last month. From Kalimpong."

"All right."

"A man I used to lend to. Before your time here." Tsering Norbu's eyes were pale brown, unhurried, the eyes of someone reading a document written on the borrower's face. "He owed me a great deal. He wrote to say he could settle it if I pressed a certain claim of mine elsewhere. A transfer of obligation, you understand. Very old custom. Very fair."

Jonah's hands were steady around the cup. He had learned steadiness the hard way, on ledges where steadiness was the difference between a story and a grave. "What claim?"

"Four hundred rupees. Yours." The old man opened his ledger to a page marked with a ribbon and turned it around. There was Jonah's false name in the fine patient hand, and beside it, newly inked, a date. Three days hence. "Your friend in Kalimpong says you're good for it. He seems very confident about you. Almost as though he knows you."

"I don't have friends in Kalimpong."

"Then perhaps it's a coincidence, and coincidences are cheap. Four hundred rupees is not." Tsering Norbu closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a lid. "I don't wish you harm, Jompa. You carry loads well and you don't drink and you don't gamble, which in my experience makes you nearly unique among porters. But a debt is a debt, and mine has been bought by a man who wants it collected promptly. If it isn't—" He spread his hands, an entire philosophy contained in the gesture. "Then I must sell the paper. And the men who buy such paper are not gentle the way I am gentle."

The rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"Three days," Jonah said.

"Three days." The old man refilled his cup unasked, an odd courtesy, almost kind. "You know, in thirty years, I have found that men who owe money divide into two kinds. Those who run, and those who suddenly remember resources they didn't know they had. Running costs more, in the end. It always costs more." He smiled, showing teeth stained the color of the ledger paper. "But then, you would know about running. A man carries himself the way you do — like luggage he's tired of — he's usually running from something heavier than rupees."

Jonah set down the cup, untouched, and stood. "I'll have it."

"I believe you will," said Tsering Norbu, and returned to his ledger, and that was somehow the most frightening thing of all — that he believed it, that everyone in this damp grey town seemed to believe things about Jompa that Jompa himself could no longer verify.

* * *

Evening came down the valley like water filling a basin. Jonah ate alone at the Everest Lodge, the cheapest of the three teahouses clustered at the lower end of town, where the dal was thin and the benches were scarred with the initials of trekkers who had passed through in better years, before the upper valley closed and the trade routes thinned to a trickle. He sat in the corner with his back to the wall — an old habit, professional, the posture of a man who had once been responsible for other people's lives and had never fully handed back the weight.

Four hundred rupees. He counted what he had, mentally, the way he counted anchors on a pitch: ninety-one rupees in a cloth roll under the floorboard of his rented room. Sixty more coming from the rice run. It was not close. It was not close in any currency or any arithmetic.

Which left the options laid out with terrible clarity, the way a route lays itself out when you stand at the base of it. Run — north, east, over some pass with a false name and a lighter name behind it, starting the whole grey ritual again in some other slate-colored town, some other awning, some other line boss noticing that he didn't complain. Or stay and find four hundred rupees in three days, which for a porter meant months, and months meant the paper sold, and the men who bought such paper were not gentle.

Or.

His eyes went, without permission, to the far wall of the teahouse, where a faded government poster curled away from the plaster. It showed the upper Kalyani valley in stylized greens and blues — a tourist poster from before the closure, promising *PRISTINE TRAILS AND GLACIAL SPLENDOR* — and across it, stamped diagonally in red, the words RESTRICTED AREA. PERMIT REQUIRED. NO ENTRY.

Eleven months, and he had never once let himself look at it directly. Now he looked. Beyond that red stamp, beyond the passes at the head of it, lay the ranges he had guided for nine years. Lay the routes he knew better than the streets of any town he'd lived in. Lay, if the rumors that drifted down with the mule caravans were true, syndicate survey camps and company patrols and a valley emptied of the people who had lived in it for centuries — sealed twice over, by governments and by money, closed the way a wound is closed, badly, and over something.

There was work in forbidden places. There was always work in forbidden places, for a man with his skills and nothing left to lose but a name that wasn't even his.

Jonah looked at the poster until the lamplight guttered, then finished his cold dal, paid with exact change, and walked out into the hanging rain. Above the rooftops, the ridgeline had cleared for the first time in days, and the last light caught the high snowfields and turned them the color of old bone. He stood in the empty street and watched them, and felt the old language stirring in his feet, in his lungs, in the deep professional quiet of his hands.

Behind him, from the direction of the bridge road, came the sound of a mule train arriving late — bells, curses, and voices raised in the particular excitement of travelers bringing news. He did not turn around. Not yet.

But later, much later, lying awake under the floorboard with his ninety-one rupees, Jonah would remember that he had heard them coming — the bells in the dark, and beneath them, fainter, a sound he had not heard in eleven months and recognized instantly even through the rain:

Someone calling for a guide.
