## 2. A Stranger at the Last Teahouse

The rain came to Serem the way debts came—slowly, then all at once. Jonah Blackwood stood under the sagging awning of the last teahouse before the high passes and watched the first drops strike the dust of the road, each one raising a small dark crater that vanished as quickly as it was made. Above the town, the clouds had been building all afternoon, stacking themselves against the shoulders of the mountains like creditors waiting outside a door.

He had a name for himself here, but it wasn't his. In Serem he was *Karma*, porter, available for loads up to forty kilos, no questions asked and none answered. The name had come to him the way false things come—easily, cheaply, from a moneylender's clerk who hadn't bothered to look twice at the man signing for his first sack of rice. Three months now. Long enough that when someone called out "Karma!" in the market, his body turned before his mind could object, and that small betrayal of reflex shamed him more than any of the larger ones.

The teahouse was called the Drukpa's Rest, though everyone just called it Pemba's, after the woman who ran it—a broad-shouldered widow with forearms like dock rope and a memory for faces that Jonah had tested carefully in his first weeks and found flawless. She knew he wasn't Karma. He was almost certain she knew. But she poured his tea without ceremony and never used his name at all, which was its own kind of mercy, and he had built a fragile loyalty around her for it.

Inside, the room smelled of butter tea and wet wool and juniper smoke. Four low tables, two occupied: a pair of muleteers arguing over freight rates in low voices, and an old monk dozing over a newspaper gone soft with handling. Jonah took his usual corner, back to the wall, sightline to the door—not paranoia anymore, just architecture of habit, the way a man who has slept in prison learns to sleep facing the bars.

Pemba brought his tea without being asked. She set it down and paused, her eyes going past him to the window, to the road.

"You expecting anyone?" she said.

"No one expects me," Jonah said. It came out more bitter than he'd intended, and Pemba looked at him with that flat, unhurried appraisal she reserved for men who said too much or too little.

"Then you'll want to see this," she said, and went back to her stove.

Jonah turned to the window.

The man coming up the road was wrong in every particular. He walked like a man walking on a ship—weight lurching, correcting, lurching again—and his clothes were the clothes of someone who had dressed well once, decades ago, and had since worn the outfit through every season of every year without surrendering it. A canvas coat gone the color of weak tea. Boots resoled so many times they were mostly patch. On his back, a leather pack old enough to have its own history, cinched down tight, guarded even now by the way the man's hand kept drifting back to touch it, checking, reassuring, checking again.

But it was the face that held Jonah. Not the fever-bright eyes, not the grey stubble, not the cracked lips moving around some private word. It was the way the man read the road ahead of him—the small automatic flicks of his gaze to the ridgelines, to the scree slopes, to the cloud ceiling pressing down on the passes. Jonah knew that scanning. He'd done it ten thousand times. It was the look of a man who had spent his life reading mountains the way other men read newspapers, and who could not stop reading them even now, even half-dead on his feet.

*Prospector,* Jonah thought. Or surveyor. One of those men the companies sent up into the blank spaces with a theodolite and a contract and no retirement plan.

The stranger reached the door, failed to find the handle, found it, and came inside in a rush of cold air and the smell of stone dust and something else beneath it—sickness, sweetish and wrong, the smell Jonah had learned to dread in high camps, the smell of a body burning through its own reserves.

The muleteers stopped talking. The monk opened one eye and closed it again.

The old man stood in the middle of the room, swaying slightly, and looked at each of them in turn with an expression Jonah couldn't place at first. Then he had it: the man was counting. Counting exits, counting hands, counting who sat where. A professional assessment performed by a body that could barely hold itself upright.

"Pemba," the man said, and his voice was rough as river gravel. "Tea. Strong. And whatever you have that isn't tea."

"Sit down before you fall down," Pemba said, already moving. "I have tsampa and I have eggs and I have opinions about men who travel sick."

"I'm not sick." He lowered himself onto the bench across from Jonah—not beside the muleteers, not near the door, but directly opposite Jonah, and Jonah understood a moment later that this too had been chosen. "I'm acclimatizing badly. There's a difference. Sick is what happens next if I stop."

"Poetic," said Pemba, setting down tea and a bowl of tsampa. "Eat."

The man ate the way dying men eat—mechanically, with grim discipline, as though food were a task on a list and the list was longer than the time remaining. Between spoonfuls he drank the tea in long pulls. His hands shook, but his eyes didn't. They stayed on Jonah with a directness that would have been rude in anyone else and was, in this man, simply honest. He was looking at Jonah the way Jonah had seen clients look at maps—as though trying to determine whether the thing in front of him showed the terrain he needed.

"You're a guide," the old man said. Not a question.

"I'm a porter."

"You've got guide's hands." The man nodded at Jonah's, wrapped around the cup. "Rope calluses in the wrong places for carrying. And you sit facing the door, and you checked the roof beams when you came in—load-bearing habit—and when I stumbled on the threshold your weight shifted forward before your mind caught up. Porters learn to keep their own feet. Guides learn to watch everyone's." He took another pull of tea. "You're a guide who's pretending to be a porter, which means either you're hiding from something or you're saving for something, and your boots say hiding."

Jonah set down the cup slowly. Under the table, his right hand had drifted, without his permission, toward the knife at his belt—an old habit from a bad year—and he stilled it.

"You're observant for a man who can barely stand."

"Fever sharpens some things and rots others." The old man smiled, and there was something in the smile that snagged at Jonah, some familiar geometry in the way the cheekbones moved, and he pushed the recognition away before it formed. Strangers reminded him of people constantly now; three months of self-erasure made ghosts of everyone. "My name is Dorje."

"All right, Dorje."

"That's all? Most people ask which Dorje. There are many."

"There are many Karmas too," Jonah said. "We manage."

Something flickered across the old man's face at that—amusement, or pain, or both—and he laughed, a short dry sound that turned into coughing that bent him double. Pemba appeared with water and stood over him until the coughing passed, and when she straightened up she caught Jonah's eye and gave him a look that said, plainly: *this man is going to die, and soon, and you know it as well as I do.*

Jonah did know it. He'd seen the climb from the valley—the man had covered ground that should have taken a healthy walker six hours in what must have been nine or ten, in weather, at altitude, with a fever cooking him from inside. Whatever was in that leather pack, Dorje had decided it was worth spending the last of himself to carry.

And that was the moment Jonah noticed the other thing. The thing that had been sitting at the edge of his attention since the old man walked in.

The window.

Beyond the rain-streaked glass, across the road, under the eave of the closed provision shop, a man stood smoking. He had been there when Jonah arrived—he'd registered him the way he registered everything, filed under *unimportant*—but the man hadn't moved in forty minutes. Nobody stood under an eave for forty minutes in falling rain unless they were being paid for it. He was big through the shoulders, and he smoked with a patience that wasn't natural patience, the kind of stillness that had to be practiced. When Dorje had come up the road, the smoker had watched him the whole way—had tracked him, Jonah realized now, the way a hawk tracks, head turning in small precise increments.

And when the smoker's gaze left Dorje, it came to the window of the teahouse. To the corner table. To Jonah.

Held there for three seconds. Then away.

Jonah's blood went quiet and cold, the way it did before rockfall. He kept his eyes on his tea and let his mind run the arithmetic. Did the watcher follow Dorje, or was he watching the teahouse? Was he watching *him*? Three months of careful nothing, of portering rice and keeping his mouth shut, and the walls he'd built felt suddenly thin as tent silk.

He looked up. Dorje was watching him watch the window, and the old man's expression had changed—hardened, sharpened, the fever receding behind something colder and more awake.

"You see him too," Dorje said quietly. "Good. I was starting to think the fever had put him there."

"Who is he?"

"A man whose employers believe I am carrying something that belongs to them." Dorje turned his cup slowly on the table, once, twice. "They are correct. That is the tragedy of my life—I have finally stopped lying to myself about exactly one week too late for it to be useful."

"What do they think you're carrying?"

"A map." The old man said it lightly, tossing it away, but his hand had stopped moving on the cup. "An old map. Of a place they sealed years ago and would prefer the world forget exists. They want it destroyed. Not sold, not studied—destroyed. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Jonah heard himself say, "Which place?" and hated the hunger in his own voice. Because he knew. Of course he knew. There was only one sealed place above these passes, only one valley both governments had closed with matching decrees and matching silence, only one river whose source appeared on no map printed in twenty years. Every porter in Serem joked about it in the way people joke about things that frighten them. *Going up the Kalyani? Better bring two passports and a prayer.*

Dorje looked at him for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Across the road, the smoker lit another cigarette from the ember of the last, unhurried, a man with nowhere to be because his work had come to him.

"The source of the Kalyani," Dorje said. "Yes."

"And you want me to take you there." Jonah shook his head before the sentence finished leaving his mouth. "No. Whatever you're paying, no. I carry sacks. I don't carry people into closed valleys. That's—" *That's how I lost everything the first time,* he didn't say. "Find someone else."

"There is no one else." For the first time, the old man's voice cracked, and underneath the gravel Jonah heard something raw. "Look at me, porter. Look at me properly. I have perhaps four days of walking in this body, and the mountain will take three of them. I need a guide who knows the gorge routes, the ones that don't appear on patrol schedules. I need someone who moves like a man with no name, because named men get remembered and remembered men get reported. And I need someone who, when I offer him everything I have left, will understand that I'm not offering money." He leaned forward, and the lamplight fell into the hollows of his face, and again that terrible flicker of familiarity, that sense of a face seen before someplace Jonah couldn't reach. "I'm asking you to guide me one final distance. After that, what happens to what I carry won't be my problem. Or yours. Unless you make it yours."

"And why would I? Why me? You walked into this room and picked my table out of four tables."

Dorje smiled again, and this time the smile was sad, and it was the sadness that broke something loose in Jonah's chest, some old rusted bolt turning for the first time in years.

"Because you looked at the window before I told you to," the old man said. "Because your hands are guide's hands. And because when I said the Kalyani, you didn't ask where it was." He rose, unsteady, gathering his pack against his chest like a child, like cargo, like the only thing in the world he trusted. "Rest tonight, porter. Think about it. I'll be leaving at first light, with you or without you. The fever doesn't negotiate, and neither, anymore, do I."

He counted out coins for Pemba—too many coins—and climbed the stairs to the rooms above, each step a negotiation, his hand white-knuckled on the rail.

Jonah sat alone in the corner with his cooling tea and the sound of the rain thickening outside, and he told himself the sensible things. Told himself about Voss—no, he didn't have the name yet, wouldn't have it for days—but told himself about the syndicate, about blacklists, about what happened to men who touched sealed valleys. Told himself that invisibility was safety, that a porter with a false name could live a long unremarkable life, that the dead prospector upstairs was not his problem and the map was not his problem and the man under the eave was not his problem.

Across the road, the smoker dropped his cigarette, ground it under his heel, and looked up at the teahouse windows one more time—at Dorje's light, and then, deliberately, at Jonah's corner.

Then he turned up his collar and walked away into the rain, unhurried, a man whose errand could wait until morning.

Jonah sat in the emptying room and understood, with the clarity that comes only at altitude or at the bottom of things, that his invisibility had just ended. Whatever he chose at first light, the man with the cigarette had already chosen for him. He had been seen. Seen men had only two directions left to move—toward the danger or away from it—and only one of those directions was upstream.

Above him, through the floorboards, he heard the old man coughing, stopping, coughing again, and between the fits a sound so faint Jonah might have imagined it: a voice, low and ragged, saying a name over and over like a prayer or an apology.

A name that sounded, impossibly, like his own.
