## 3. The Trade

The trail out of Serem climbed through terraced barley fields gone wild at the edges, and Jonah took the switchbacks at the pace of a man who had somewhere to be and nowhere to be, which was its own particular torment. Dorje rode ahead of him on a borrowed mule, swaying in the saddle like a man asleep upright, and every hundred paces or so he would lift one hand—not waving, not signaling, just lifting it, as if testing whether the air was still there.

Jonah had told himself this was a job. Two days' guiding to the shrine at Lhakpa La for a fee that would cover his debt to the moneylender with a little left over. That was the arithmetic he'd done in the teahouse while the old man's fever burned behind his eyes and the stranger in the corner watched them both over the rim of an untouched cup. Simple arithmetic. A body carried uphill, a coin purse lightened.

But arithmetic didn't explain why he kept looking at Dorje's hands.

They were a guide's hands, or had been once—knuckles swollen from cold, nails cracked and darkened by years of rope work, the left index finger shortened at the second joint in the way fingers shorten when they've been frostbitten and kept working anyway. Jonah knew that hand. He'd seen it on a dozen old men in a dozen mountain towns, men who had outlived their usefulness and their friends and sometimes their minds, but never their habit of reaching for terrain that wasn't there.

"You climb," Dorje said suddenly, without turning around.

"I porter."

"Porters carry." The old man's voice was thin as wind through a crack. "You *climb*. I watch you walk. You read the ground before your feet touch it. Like a man reading a letter he already knows by heart."

Jonah said nothing. Above them the barley gave way to juniper scrub, and the juniper to bare grey scree, and the air began to taste of altitude—the metallic edge of it, the way it scraped the back of the throat clean.

They camped that night below the tree line, in the lee of a boulder field that caught the last of the sun and held it like a coal. Jonah pitched the tent, boiled snow for tea, and did all of it efficiently, wordlessly, the way he did everything now—a man performing competence without presence, a ghost doing chores.

Dorje sat propped against his pack, which he had not let out of arm's reach since Serem. It was an ordinary pack, oilcloth over wicker, patched at the straps. But the old man held it the way other men held children.

"Your name," Dorje said.

"Dorje, you hired me to carry you up a mountain, not to talk."

"That is not your name."

Jonah looked at him. In the blue dusk the old man's face was all planes and hollows, the fever having burned away whatever softness age had left. His eyes were clear, though. That was the terrible thing about dying mountaineers; the body failed long before the attention did.

"It's the name I go by," Jonah said.

Dorje nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something rather than contradicted it. "In Serem, the moneylender's boy asked about you. I heard. They ask about a guide who lost people on the Kalyani crossing. Four years ago." He paused. "I said I knew nothing."

Something cold moved down Jonah's spine, unrelated to the altitude. "Why?"

"Because a man should be able to choose when he is found." Dorje closed his eyes. "Sleep. Tomorrow is hard walking, and I have less tomorrow than you think."

* * *

He was right about that, though neither of them knew how right until the middle of the next afternoon.

The trail to Lhakpa La followed a lateral moraine above a stream valley, easy going by mountain standards, brutal by any others. By noon Dorje could no longer stay in the saddle. Jonah walked the mule while the old man leaned against him, half-carried, his breath coming in wet rattles that Jonah recognized from high-altitude clinics and rescue camps—the sound of fluid where fluid should not be. Pulmonary edema, probably. Maybe the fever was typhoid on top of it. Either way, the diagnosis was the same one the mountains always delivered: too high, too far, too late.

"We turn back," Jonah said. "There's a herder's hut two hours down. I can get you there, get you warm—"

"No."

"Dorje—"

"*No.*" The old man's hand closed on Jonah's sleeve with shocking strength. "Listen to me. Listen. I am not going down. I have been going down my whole life. Every year further from—" He coughed, a long tearing cough, and when he spat there was blood in it, bright as prayer flags. "Upstream. Take me upstream. As far as I can go."

"There is no upstream. The valley's sealed. Both governments, the whole length of it. You know that. Everyone knows that."

"I know what is *said*." Dorje's eyes fixed on him, and there was something in them now beyond fever—something urgent and ancient and terribly lucid. "I know what is true. And so will you, if you do one thing for me. One thing, and I will pay you more than the moneylender ever dreamed you owed."

"I don't want your money."

"Everyone wants money. Money is how we say sorry without saying sorry." He laughed, and the laugh became another cough. "Not money then. A trade. My trade for yours."

"What trade?"

Dorje fumbled at the pack, and Jonah moved to help him because the old man's hands were shaking too badly to manage the buckles. Out of the oilcloth came a bundle wrapped in a woolen shirt that had been washed so many times it was more memory than fabric. Inside the shirt was a tin box, tobacco-colored with rust, and inside the tin—

A map.

Hand-drawn on waterproofed linen, dense with ink that had faded to the brown of dried blood. Jonah had seen a thousand maps in his life; he'd drawn half of them himself. But he had never seen anything like this. It was the Kalyani's upper course—the gorge, the ghost villages, the whole forbidden spine of the sealed valley—rendered in lines so fine and so confident they could only have come from years of walking, years of measuring, years of loving. Springs marked with tiny stars. Shrines marked with smaller ones. Margins crowded with notes in three scripts, some of them clearly added decades apart, the ink changing generations of color like tree rings.

And at the very top, past the last named place, past everything: the source. Marked not with an X but with a small, careful circle, and beside it, in letters almost too fine to read:

*Where the water remembers.*

"The source of the Kalyani," Jonah said. His voice came out strange. "This is—Dorje, this is worth more than either of us will ever see again. This is worth killing for. There are men in Serem who would cut a throat for a rumor of this valley."

"Yes," Dorje said simply. "That is why I chose you."

"Chose me? You fell into the teahouse half-dead. You didn't choose me, you collapsed at my table."

"I collapsed at *your* table." The old man smiled, and it transformed his face into something younger and sadder than it had any right to be. "Out of every teahouse on the frontier. Out of every town. Do you believe in that kind of luck? I don't. Luck is a story lazy people tell."

The wind came up the valley then, carrying the smell of snow from heights they couldn't see, and Dorje shivered violently and pulled the map against his chest.

"Here is the trade," he said. "Carry it upstream. Don't sell it. Don't give it to soldiers, don't give it to the syndicate men, don't give it to priests or policemen or anyone who asks nicely. Carry it to the source, and there—" his voice caught, steadied— "there, you will understand what to do. Not before. Before, you will only want to be rid of it. Everyone wants to be rid of a debt. But a debt isn't a weight, boy. A debt is a *direction*."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will die here on this moraine with my map, and you will walk down to Serem and pay your moneylender and disappear a little more each year until there is nothing left to disappear." Dorje said it without cruelty, the way a doctor reads a chart. "I am offering you the opposite of that. I am offering you upstream."

Jonah looked at the map. He wanted, with a physical ache, to put it back in the tin and step away from it. Everything in him that had survived four years of exile screamed to do exactly that—invisibility was a discipline, and he had become very good at it, and this map was the end of all of it. This map was eyes on him forever. This map was Voss-shaped trouble, though he didn't yet have a name to put to that shape.

But he looked at Dorje's hands holding it. The frostbitten finger. The rope-scarred knuckles. The hands of a man who had spent his life going toward things everyone else went around.

"Why me?" Jonah asked quietly. "Really. Not luck. Not stories. Why?"

Dorje studied him for a long moment. The wind pulled at his white hair. Somewhere below them, the mule shifted and stamped, impatient with the business of dying.

"Because you look like someone I owe," the old man said at last. "And because you walk like someone who owes himself."

He held out the tin.

And Jonah—who had refused gifts for four years, who had built a whole gray life on the principle of taking nothing from anyone that could later be called a debt—took it.

The linen was lighter than it should have been. That was his first thought. All that distance, all those years of ink, and it weighed almost nothing. His second thought was that this was how the worst burdens always arrived.

"I'll carry it," he said. "I'm not promising anything else. I don't know what's at your source, and I don't know what you think I'll understand there, and I'm telling you now I probably won't. I'll carry it upstream as far as I can, and after that—"

"After that, the river decides." Dorje settled back against the rock, exhausted by the transaction, satisfied with it. "Good. Good. That is enough. That is more than I—" He stopped. His gaze had drifted past Jonah's shoulder, up-valley, to where the gorge walls narrowed into shadow, and something moved across his face that was not pain.

"Dorje?"

"She used to sing," the old man said softly. "On the crossing days. When the water was high and the porters were frightened, she would sing so they wouldn't hear the river." His eyes were still fixed on the distance, seeing something Jonah couldn't. "I told her the syndicate would take care of us. I signed their papers. I measured their tunnels. I thought a map was just a map." His breath hitched. "I never drew the part where she—"

He coughed again, longer this time, and Jonah moved to support him, and the old man's weight sagged into his arms, light as dry timber.

"Her name," Dorje whispered. His lips were turning grey. "Say her name. So the mountain hears it said properly, one more time. Mi—"

The wind took the rest.

Jonah bent close. "Mi-what? Say it again. *Dorje.*"

But the old man's eyes had gone fixed on the peaks, and his chest barely moved, and when he spoke again it was one word, barely voiced, shaped more by the last habit of his mouth than by any intention in it:

"...son..."

Then nothing. Just the wind, and the mule stamping, and the enormous indifferent silence of the mountains coming down like a lid.

* * *

Jonah buried him at dawn, above the tree line, in ground he had to break with an ice axe because the earth was still frozen. He stacked stones over the grave in the traveler's fashion, working methodically, refusing to think, because thinking led to the word *son*, and the word *son* led nowhere good—it was a common enough last word, men died calling for mothers and sons and wives and gods, it meant nothing, it was coincidence stacked on coincidence, and Jonah Blackwood had learned that coincidences were how the mountains lured you off the safe route.

But his hands remembered things his mind refused. His hands remembered being seven years old, watching a broad-backed man draw maps at a kitchen table by lamplight, the ink-brown lines of rivers appearing under a short-fingered pencil. His hands remembered that man leaving one autumn with a syndicate contract folded in his coat, and his mother not crying until the door closed, and the smell of the lamp oil lingering in the room for weeks.

His hands shook now, setting the last stone, and he let them.

When the cairn was finished he stood before it in the early light, the tin heavy in his jacket, and spoke aloud because the silence demanded it.

"I don't know who you were," he said. "But I'll carry it upstream. That much I promised, and that much I keep."

Below him the trail wound on toward the pass, and beyond the pass, invisible behind walls of law and stone and armed men, the forbidden valley waited with its drowned villages and its crossed-out names. Jonah shouldered his pack. He did not look back at the cairn again, because looking back was a luxury of men with futures.

Two hours later, from a ridge above the lateral moraine, he saw dust on the trail behind him—riders, three of them, moving fast, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly whom they were looking for.

Jonah turned off the path and started climbing.
