## 4. The Man with the Grey Gloves

The rain came to Serem three days after Dorje died, and it came the way bad news always came to that town—sideways, without warning, and smelling of somewhere else.

Jonah felt it first in his knees, the old fracture aching like a struck bell, and he stood in the doorway of the room he rented above the tannery and watched the first fat drops darken the dust of the lane below. The monsoon was early this year. Everyone said so. The old women at the well said it with their chins lifted toward the mountains, reading clouds the way other people read letters from home, and there was something in their faces he hadn't seen before—something between dread and satisfaction, as if the sky had finally agreed to say out loud what they'd been whispering for years.

He should have left already. He knew that. He'd known it since the night Dorje's hand closed around his wrist on the trail above the last teahouse, since the weight of the oilskin packet settled into the inside pocket of his jacket where it had lived ever since, warm as a second heartbeat. Every morning he told himself: today I go. Every evening he found a reason—the money wasn't enough yet, the passes would be swollen, the mule trains weren't moving—and every reason was a lie wearing another lie's coat.

The truth was simpler and uglier. The truth was that he had spent four years learning how to disappear, and disappearing was a skill that worked in only one direction. You could vanish into a town like Serem, become a porter with a false name and a bent back, become nobody. But leaving meant becoming someone again, and someone was exactly what he could not afford to be.

So he stayed, and he portered, and he waited for courage or catastrophe, whichever arrived first.

Catastrophe wore grey gloves.

* * *

Jonah saw him for the first time outside the moneylender's house on Tsering Lane, and even before he understood why the man mattered, his body understood. It was the old instinct, the one that had kept clients alive through whiteouts and rockfall for eleven years before the blacklist took everything: the sense of a wrongness in a landscape, a boulder sitting where no boulder should sit.

The man stood beneath the eave of the shuttered carpet shop across from the moneylender's gate, out of the rain, doing nothing. That was the wrongness. Nobody stood still in Serem during the rains. Porters hurried, traders bargained, children ran shrieking between the puddles. This man simply stood, hands folded behind his back, watching the moneylender's door with the patience of a man who had nowhere else in the world to be.

He was tall, narrow through the shoulders, dressed in a travelling suit of dark grey wool that no one in Serem owned and no one who passed through Serem could afford. His hair was cropped close and going silver at the temples. And when he raised one hand—not waving, just turning his palm up to test the rain—Jonah saw the gloves. Grey leather, fine-grained, buttoned at the wrist. Gloves in this heat, in this wet. Gloves like a man who never wanted to leave fingerprints on anything again.

Jonah crossed the lane without hurrying, took the covered stairway down past the dye-works, and let the crowd of the market absorb him. His heart was going hard and low, a drumbeat in his ears, and he made himself count stalls as he passed—onions, dried fish, rope, salt tea—because counting was an anchor, and anchors were what you used when the ground started to move.

He didn't know the man. He was almost certain he didn't know the man. But he knew the *type*, and knowing the type was worse. Eleven years in the high country had taught him every species of danger the mountains bred, and the rarest and worst of them was the patient one. Storms you could read. Rockfall you could hear coming. But a man who stood under an eave and watched a door—that was a creature of a different range entirely, one that hunted by waiting, and Jonah had only ever met its kind once before, years ago, in the office of the syndicate's liaison in Kalpa, the day they took his certification and his name off every register in the district. The men from the company had worn suits too. They had smiled too little and too late.

He told himself it was coincidence. A traveller, a creditor's agent, a government man—Serem saw all three. He told himself that all the way back to the tannery, up the outside stair, into his room, where he locked the door and sat on the edge of the cot with his elbows on his knees and did not believe a single word of it.

The packet was inside his jacket, hanging on the nail by the door. He took it down and unwrapped it for perhaps the hundredth time.

Dorje's map lay open on the blanket: the Kalyani drawn in a prospector's careful hand, ink gone brown with age, the river rendered as a living thing—a spine of blue running from the drowned valley up through gorges Jonah knew by other names, past villages marked with small crosses instead of names, crosses like graves, up and up to the source itself, circled twice and annotated in a script grown shaky near the end: *still flowing. they lied about this too.*

Below the map, folded separately, the papers. Jonah had read them a dozen times and understood maybe half. Survey reports. Compensation schedules with names missing where names should have been. A letter fragment on syndicate letterhead, unsigned, discussing "the resettlement question" in language so clean it might have been about drainage. And one page that stopped him every time, a list of accident dates with figures beside them—payments, he thought, or hush sums—he couldn't tell. Somewhere in those pages, Jonah suspected, was the answer to a question he'd been carrying since the night a dying stranger gripped his wrist and whispered a name Jonah almost recognized.

*Carry it upstream,* Dorje had said. *Don't sell it.* As if selling had been a live possibility. As if Jonah were the kind of man who sold things now, who had anything left worth the paper it was written on.

He wrapped the packet again, and that was when he heard the voices below—the tannery owner's wife, shrill with the particular excitement Serem reserved for strangers with money, saying something about the gentleman, the gentleman asking questions, the gentleman wanting to know—

Jonah went very still.

Then he moved to the window, the one that looked down over the yard, and eased the shutter wide enough to see.

Two men stood talking in the rain under the tannery's dripping eave. One was the tannery owner himself, wringing his cap in his hands, bowing his head at intervals. The other was the man with the grey gloves.

He hadn't put up an umbrella. Rain ran off the brim of his hat and darkened the shoulders of his grey suit, and he didn't seem to notice or care, and somehow that frightened Jonah more than anything else—the absolute indifference of him, the way he stood in the weather as though weather were a problem other people had. He was speaking quietly. The tannery owner kept nodding, kept glancing up at the building, kept gesturing with his chin toward the stairs, toward the rooms above, toward—

Toward Jonah's window. Jonah stepped back from the shutter as if it had burned him.

He couldn't hear the words. He didn't need to. He watched the gloved hand rise, unhurried, and press something into the tannery owner's palm—a fold of notes, by the way the man's shoulders jumped—and then the grey man turned, surveyed the yard once, slowly, the way a surveyor measures ground he intends to own, and walked out through the gate into the rain.

Jonah stood in the middle of his rented room with the packet against his chest and did the arithmetic of his situation, and the sum came out very simple: the man was asking after a dead prospector's belongings, and there was exactly one dead prospector's belongings in Serem worth a stranger's money, and they were hanging on a nail inside a jacket six feet above the yard where the grey gloves had just been standing.

Dorje had been watched. Jonah had known it that night in the teahouse without letting himself know it—the shape at the far table, the way the old man's eyes had flicked to the door. Someone had followed Dorje out of the high country, or been sent after him, and now that Dorje was dirt in the burial ground above the town, the following had transferred, the way a debt transfers, to whatever the dead man had been carrying.

To whoever the dead man had given it to.

*Don't sell it.* No, Jonah thought, standing in the failing light while the rain thickened against the roof. Selling was the least of it. The man downstairs hadn't come to buy.

* * *

He gave himself until full dark, because panic was a luxury and haste killed more guides than avalanches ever had. He ate—forced down cold rice and lentils, because a body climbing out of the Kalyani gorge needed fuel and fear was not a substitute—and while he ate he planned, laying the route out in his head the way he'd once laid out routes for paying clients, back when his judgment was worth money.

Upstream. There was no other direction. Downstream lay Serem, the roads, the checkpoints, the world the grey gloves owned; upstream lay the gorge, the ghost villages, the sealed valley, and every kind of death the monsoon could invent. Four days ago he'd have said the choice between them wasn't a choice at all. Now he packed his kit by feel in the dark—rope, stove, grain, the canvas roll of tools he'd carried through three ranges—and understood that the choice had been made for him the moment Dorje pressed the map into his hands and died with a name on his lips.

There was one stop he had to make first.

The moneylender's house was dark, but the old man kept late hours the way misers keep everything, and when Jonah knocked softly at the side door, a shutter opened a hand's width and one eye appeared in the gap.

"You're paid through the month," the eye said.

"I'm leaving tonight." Jonah kept his voice level. "I've brought what I owe besides."

The silence stretched long enough that rain filled it. Then the door opened, and the moneylender—small, dry, wrapped in a blanket against the damp—counted the notes Jonah held out with two fingers, quick as a bird pecking, and tucked them away, and looked up at him with an expression Jonah had never seen on that face before. Not greed. Something older and wearier.

"The gentleman asked about you," the old man said. "This afternoon. At my house."

Jonah's stomach dropped through the floorboards. "What did you tell him?"

"That I had a porter named Karmapa who owed me money." The moneylender shrugged inside his blanket. "It is true. You are Karmapa. You owe me money. Or you owed me. What a man tells a truth is his own affair." He studied Jonah a moment longer. "He showed me a paper. A drawing of a face. Old face, mountain face. He asked if such a man had passed through. I said the mountains send us many faces."

"Dorje," Jonah said quietly. "You knew him."

"I knew a prospector who drank too much chang and talked too much about a river." The old man's eye glittered. "I also know that men who ask questions in grey gloves do not pay me, and men who pay their debts in the night and leave before dawn—" he paused, and something almost like kindness crossed the seamed face, "—leave nothing behind for anyone to find. Go upstream, Karmapa. And whatever you carry, carry it better than he carried his drinking."

The door closed. Jonah stood a moment in the rain, absurdly moved by a moneylender's discretion, then turned and went.

* * *

He left Serem by the drovers' path behind the cremation ground, the one no official map acknowledged and every local boy used for smuggling, and the town fell away below him in stages—first the lamplight, then the dogs' noise, then the smell of woodsmoke and wet hides, until there was only the path, the rain, and the black shoulder of the mountain rising ahead.

At the last bend he stopped and looked back once. Serem lay in its bowl of darkness, a scatter of embers under the rain, and somewhere down there a man in grey gloves was learning, or would learn by morning, that the porter called Karmapa had settled his accounts and vanished. By noon tomorrow the teahouse keeper would remember. By evening the trail registers would be checked, the ferryman questioned, the drovers' path noticed. Voss—Jonah didn't know the name yet, but he already knew the man would have one, a short cold syllable of a name—would follow. Men like that always followed. It was the only prayer they knew.

Let him come, Jonah thought, and was surprised to find the thought carried no bravado in it at all. It was simply true, the way a forecast is true. The storm was coming whether he climbed into it or not. The only question the mountains ever asked was whether you'd meet it standing or running, and he had spent four years running, and running had gotten him a false name and a cot above a tannery and a life so small he could hold all of it in one hand.

He touched the packet inside his jacket. Under his palm, the map's oiled surface was slick with rain-damp, and he thought of Dorje's grip on his wrist, the strength still left in a dying man's fingers, spent all at once on a stranger.

*I'm sorry,* the old man had whispered, or something that sounded like it, and then the name, the almost-familiar name, swallowed by the wind off the pass.

"Whoever you were," Jonah said aloud, to the rain, to the dark, to the dead man whose blood might or might not have run in his own veins, "I'll get it there. Upstream. All the way."

The words went out of him and did not come back, and he found that he felt lighter for having said them, which frightened him more than the grey gloves had, because it meant the promise had become part of him, load-bearing, and there was no unbuilding it now.

He turned north, into the teeth of the coming monsoon, and began to climb.

Behind him, far below at the edge of the lamplight, a figure in a dark suit stood at the mouth of the drovers' path, motionless in the rain, gloved hands folded, watching the mountain take Jonah back.
