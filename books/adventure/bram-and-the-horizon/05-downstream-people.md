## 5. Downstream People

The rain in Serem came the way bad news did—sideways, without warning, and sticking to everything it touched.

Voss stood under the eave of the last teahouse before the high passes and watched the water run off the corrugated roof in a beaded curtain. He did not step inside immediately. That was a mistake amateurs made: bursting through doors, announcing themselves, letting the room arrange its face before they'd had a chance to see it unarranged. He had learned—over nineteen years, over three governments and one corporation that outlived all of them—that a room told you everything in the first four seconds if you were patient enough to let it lie to you first.

Through the fogged window he counted: six bodies. A truck driver asleep against his own duffel bag. Two women mending nets by the stove, their hands moving with the automatic rhythm of people who had done nothing else for thirty years. An old man reading a newspaper two days out of date. A boy stacking tin cups. And behind the counter, the keeper—a broad-shouldered woman with grey shot through her braid, wiping down wood that was already clean.

No body matching the description of a dying prospector. No body matching the description of Jonah Blackwood, though Voss had long ago stopped trusting descriptions of Blackwood. Photographs aged badly; men under false names aged worse.

He pushed open the door.

The room's temperature changed. It always did. Not the heat—the attention. Conversations thinned to threads, eyes slid toward him and away again, and somewhere in the corner of the room a cup was set down a half-second too carefully. Voss wore plain clothes, good ones, the kind that said money without saying where it came from. Grey gloves, folded over his belt because his hands sweated in the lowland humidity and wet palms left prints on things he might need to touch later. People remembered gloves. He had decided years ago that being remembered was more useful than being forgotten, provided you controlled what they remembered: a patient man, a polite man, a man who asked questions as if the answers were a formality.

"Tea," he said to the keeper, and sat at the counter where she could not avoid looking at him.

She poured without speaking. The tea was over-steeped and bitter, the way it got when a household expected no guests worth impressing. He drank it anyway, and set the cup down, and turned it slowly on its saucer, once, twice, watching her watch it.

"You've had a hard week," he said.

"We have hard weeks."

"A man died on your floor." He said it flatly, without accusation, the way one remarks on weather. "Old. Feverish. Came in from the lower trail sometime in the last ten days. You carried him up to the loft, or he slept by the stove—I don't much care which. I care about who he spoke to."

The rag in the woman's hand stopped moving. Resumed. Stopped again.

"I don't remember every sick man who stumbles through here," she said. "This is the last teahouse before the passes. Half the valley's sick men end up on my floor."

"Of course." Voss smiled, and it reached his eyes, because he had practiced until it did. "Then let me make this easy. I'm not police. If I were police, there would be paperwork, and paperwork means your name goes into a system, and systems are read by clerks, and clerks talk. I'm from the Kalyani Development Consortium. We're conducting an internal review of a former employee who went missing with company property. If you help me quietly, this stays quiet. If you make me go to the district office instead—" he turned the cup again, "—then everyone who served him tea becomes a line in someone else's report. You understand how reports work. They don't have to accuse anyone. They only have to exist."

The keeper looked at him for a long moment. She had the face of a woman who had buried a husband in a mine collapse or a flash flood—one of the two, in this country always one of the two—and had gone on pouring tea afterward because the alternative was stopping.

"He came in nine days ago," she said finally. "Couldn't stand. We put him in the back room. He had a fever like a furnace. Kept talking about water. About the river being higher than it should be, about a survey line that was wrong, wrong by forty meters, he said, and nobody would listen." Her jaw tightened. "I've heard dying men say stranger things."

"And who did he speak to?"

"A porter. Young man. Quiet. Carried loads down at Serem for whoever paid. He sat with the old man half the night."

"What did they talk about?"

"I wasn't in the room."

Voss waited. Silence was a tool with an edge on both sides, and he knew exactly how long he could hold it before it cut him instead. The women by the stove had stopped pretending to mend. The boy stacking cups had gone entirely still, a boy learning early what men like this sounded like.

"The porter took something," the keeper said at last. "A packet. The old man made him swear on it—I heard that much through the wall. Made him swear to carry it upstream and not sell it. Then the old man was dead by morning, and the porter was gone before light, and I'll tell you what else, since we're being honest with each other." She leaned across the counter, and her voice dropped to something that was not fear but its close cousin. "I've kept this teahouse twenty-two years. In that time I have seen surveyors come through with your consortium's badge on their packs, and I have seen soldiers come through behind them, and I have seen whole villages walk down this road carrying their doors on their backs because they weren't allowed to take anything bigger. So whatever the company lost, whatever the dead man stole—" she straightened, "—I hope the porter runs fast, and I hope he never stops."

Voss considered her. There was a version of this conversation where he noted her name, her license number, her political sympathies, and filed them for a subordinate to handle. But a hostile witness in a border town was worth less than a cooperative one, and besides—he found, in the small dry ledger of his private judgments—that he didn't entirely dislike her. It was a weakness. He catalogued it with the rest.

"Describe the porter," he said.

"Tall. Careful with his feet—you could tell he'd walked heavy country his whole life. Hands like a guide's." She paused, and something flickered behind her eyes, some recalculation, some small alarm arriving late. "There was a scar. Left forearm. Old burn, long one."

Voss felt the particular stillness that came over him at moments like this—not excitement, nothing so warm, but the click of a lock finding its tumblers. A burn scar on the left forearm. He had read the personnel file eleven years ago, and then read it again after the incident, and then burned his own annotated copy when the order came down that the file was to cease existing. Some things the hands remembered even when the archives were cleansed.

"Thank you," he said. "You've been very helpful."

He left money on the counter—too much, deliberately, because overpayment bought silence better than threats did, and cost him nothing—and stepped back out into the rain.

* * *

The map of the region lived in his head, which was safer than paper. He stood at the edge of the town square under a leaking awning and laid it out mentally, the way he'd been trained to do in a different life, a government life, before the consortium had made him an offer he'd been smart enough to accept and foolish enough to think he understood.

Nine days. The porter—Blackwood, almost certainly Blackwood, the burn scar settled it—had left Serem nine days ago, ahead of any pursuit, with a dead prospector's packet and a sworn promise. Dorje. Voss turned the name over. Yes. Dorje Wangchuk-Tsering, field surveyor, contracted 1998 through 2011, one of the original triangulation teams on the upper Kalyani. A good employee for thirteen years. A problem for the last three. The kind of problem who started asking why a reservoir's planned floodline matched so precisely the boundaries of the mineral concessions, and not the boundaries of the villages. The kind of problem who kept copies.

They had retired him gently—severance, a pension, a quiet understanding that certain valleys were closed to him now, sealed by joint ministerial decree on both sides of the border, environmental protection, everyone signed, everyone satisfied. And Dorje had taken the pension and disappeared into the lowlands for five years, and everyone had assumed that was the end of it, because everyone always assumed that was the end of it.

Until the old man surfaced at the last teahouse with a fever and a grudge and, apparently, a map.

Voss walked the town. This too was method: a route reconstructed from the ground told you things a map never would. At the porters' stand by the grain depot, a foreman with a clipboard told him—after the appropriate amount of money—that yes, there'd been a quiet one working loads all spring, called himself something forgettable, took the jobs nobody wanted, the steep ones, the wet ones. Left suddenly, eight nights back. Left owing nothing, which the foreman found suspicious in itself. Nobody left Serem owing nothing unless they were running.

At the trailhead above the town, Voss crouched and read the mud the way other men read letters. Rain had degraded most of it, but the lower gorge path held boot prints well in the clay seams, and there—two days' travel up, at the junction where the trail split between the pilgrim route and the river gorge—had been a choice. The pilgrim route was faster, safer, patrolled by monks who asked no questions. The gorge was slower, crueler, and closed.

Blackwood had taken the gorge.

Voss straightened, and for a moment allowed himself the luxury of admiration. Because the gorge was closed, and a closed gorge meant checkpoints, and checkpoints meant records, and a blacklisted guide with a false name would never survive a checkpoint. But the gorge also meant patrols from the other direction—consortium security rotations, thin ones, sloppy ones, because no one in eleven years had ever actually tried to go upstream. Blackwood wasn't avoiding the law. He was avoiding *them*. Which meant he already suspected what the packet was worth, and to whom.

Which meant the old man had told him something.

Voss opened his phone—satellite-linked, encrypted, one of three he carried—and composed a message to the regional office in careful, deniable language: *Retrieving misplaced archival material. Requesting rotation schedule for Gorge sector, past 30 days. Requesting that no other assets be tasked. This is contained.*

The reply came in four minutes. *Rotation schedule attached. Director asks timeline.*

*Fourteen days,* Voss typed. *Possibly ten.* He hesitated, then added: *It is contained. Do not escalate. Escalation is how the last one leaked.*

He put the phone away and looked up the gorge, where the rain fell into green dark and the river below ran brown and swollen with the season's first meltwater. Somewhere up there, a dead man's son—or no, not his son, that was sentiment, the file said Blackwood's father was a schoolteacher from the south who died of liver failure—somewhere up there, a disgraced guide was climbing toward a valley that three governments and one corporation had spent a decade agreeing did not exist.

Dorje had worked for them. That was the part the teahouse keeper hadn't understood, the part nobody downstream ever understood: the syndicate had not conquered the Kalyani valley. It had *built* the maps of it. Every contour line, every surveyed crossing, every "unsafe terrain" designation that kept hikers and journalists and inspectors out—drawn by their own contractors, certified by their own engineers, filed with ministries that cashed their consulting fees. When you owned the map, you owned the territory's truth, and the territory itself could burn, flood, or simply vanish into a reservoir with no one the wiser. Dorje had drawn those lines himself for thirteen years. And then, near the end, he had apparently drawn one more—a map to the source itself, the one place the consortium's cartography went deliberately blank—and given it to a stranger with a burn scar and a debt.

Voss had read enough of Dorje's flagged memos, back when purging them, to know the old man's style. Precise. Obsessive. Moralizing in the margins. If the map was in Dorje's hand, it would not merely show a route. It would explain. It would accuse. It would be, in effect, a confession with contour lines.

And confessions, in Voss's experience, were the only documents more dangerous than ledgers.

He checked his watch. Fourteen hours of daylight left, if the rain held off, which it wouldn't. The gorge entrance was two days' hard walk. Blackwood had nine days on him—but nine days spent dragging a packet up closed country alone, without local knowledge, without contacts, probably without enough food. Voss had money, radios, a rotation schedule, and the considerable advantage of knowing exactly what the prize was and therefore exactly where it had to go.

There was only one place upstream that mattered. Everything converged there eventually—the river, the roads that weren't roads, the truth, the dead. The source.

Voss pulled on his gloves, settled his pack, and started up the gorge trail at a pace he could hold for twelve hours.

Behind him, in the teahouse window, the keeper watched him go, and he saw her turn and speak quickly to the net-mending women, and he noted it, and filed it, and did not stop. Let her send word if she liked. Word traveled by foot in these mountains, a day at a time, and he traveled faster than foot.

Ahead, the rain thickened into the grey curtain of the coming monsoon, and the gorge swallowed the trail, and somewhere above the clouds a river was rising that would carry everything—maps, ledgers, lies—down to the sea if it had to.

Voss walked into it without hurrying. He had burned one valley already. He could climb to this one.
