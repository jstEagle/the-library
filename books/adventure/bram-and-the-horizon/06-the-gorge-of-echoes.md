## 6. The Gorge of Echoes

The gorge announced itself before Jonah saw it—a low, grinding roar that rose through the pine forest like the breathing of some enormous animal asleep beneath the earth. He had been walking since before dawn, following the river's sound the way Dorje's map told him to: *keep the water on your left, always your left, and when the roar grows too loud to think over, you will know you have arrived.*

He stopped at the lip of the descent and looked down.

The Kalyani dropped away below him in a series of white staircases, wedged between walls of grey granite so sheer they seemed planed by hand. Mist hung in the depths like smoke trapped under glass. Somewhere down there, the river was doing its ancient work, chewing through the mountain's spine a grain at a time, and the sound of it filled the whole world—no birdsong, no wind, nothing but that vast percussive patience.

Jonah crouched and studied the trailhead. A cairn of stacked stones, old and lichen-crusted, marked the way down. Beside it, pressed into the mud, were boot prints. Military tread, deep-heeled, no more than a day old. Two men, maybe three, moving downhill at an unhurried pace.

They weren't hurrying because they didn't know yet what they were looking for. That would change. Voss would have reached Serem by now; Voss would have talked to the teahouse keeper, who had watched Jonah carry Dorje out with the eyes of a woman who missed nothing. It might be hours before the description traveled upriver. It might already be traveling.

Jonah touched the map where it lay against his chest, inside his jacket, wrapped in oilcloth. He had caught himself doing this all morning—the same gesture, again and again, the way a man touches a wound to confirm it still hurts. The map was there. The promise was there. *Carry it upstream. Don't sell it.* He still didn't fully understand what he'd agreed to, standing in the rain while an old man died with Jonah's own name—almost his own name—dissolving on his lips. But he understood this much: the boots in the mud meant someone else wanted it badly enough to send soldiers into a sealed valley for it.

He started down.

* * *

The gorge swallowed him whole.

The trail was a rumor more than a path—a shelf of broken rock stitched along the canyon wall, sometimes wide enough for two abreast, sometimes narrowing to a hand's breadth of stone above three hundred meters of churning white water. Jonah moved the way he hadn't moved in years: reading the rock before he committed his weight, testing holds without looking at them, letting his body remember what his mind had spent four years trying to bury. This was the work he had been born to, the work that had been taken from him, and his hands knew it the way a drowned man's lungs know water.

It should have hurt more than it did, how good it felt.

By midday the walls closed overhead into a slot of blue sky, and the light came down in shafts that turned the mist to gold. The roar changed pitch around every bend—sometimes a deep organ note, sometimes a shriek—and beneath it, if he stood still long enough, he could hear the echoes layering on each other, the gorge repeating itself a dozen times over until a single footfall became a small avalanche of sound.

That was why they called it the Gorge of Echoes, he supposed. And that was why it would kill him if the patrols got close. In here, sound carried both ways.

He ate cold tsampa paste from a cloth bag and drank straight from a side seep, cupping the icy water in his hands. Then he heard it—or felt it first, a wrongness in the rhythm of the river, a human voice shredded thin by distance and spray.

He froze. Listened. There it was again, upstream and below, high-pitched, cut off, then rising again. Not a shout for help exactly. Something more desperate than that. A scream being spent carefully, the way you spend a match when you're not sure you have another.

Jonah stood at the junction of two instincts. The old one said: *keep moving, keep invisible, every hour you stop is an hour Voss gains.* Four years of invisibility had built that instinct into something load-bearing. But underneath it, older still, was the man he used to be—the guide whose job, whose whole identity, had been to hear that sound and go toward it. He had left that man behind in a courtroom and a newspaper headline and a blacklisted name. He had thought the leaving was permanent.

His feet made the decision before his pride could veto it. He went toward the sound.

* * *

She was stranded on a rock island in the middle of a side channel, and she was furious about it.

That was Jonah's first impression—not fear, not tears, but a scowling, arms-crossed fury, as though the river had personally insulted her. She couldn't have been more than fifteen or sixteen, small-boned, sun-darkened, her hair braided tight against the wind. A scatter of goats bleated hysterically from the far bank, penned behind a low wall of stones. Her crossing stones—the flat stepping rocks that locals used at low water—were submerged now, slick and trembling under the current, and the channel between her island and either bank ran fast and waist-deep and full of rolling gravel.

"Hey!" Jonah shouted over the water. "Hey—you!"

She turned, saw him, and her expression did something complicated: relief and suspicion arriving at the same moment and refusing to yield to each other.

"The water's rising," he called. "Storm upstream. You can't wait it out."

"I know the water rises!" she shouted back, with the contempt only a teenager can achieve while drowning-adjacent. "I'm not stupid!"

"Then why are you still on the rock?"

"Because I was waiting for someone less stupid to walk past!"

Despite everything—the patrols, the map burning against his ribs, the four years of practiced solitude—Jonah felt his mouth twitch. He looked at the channel. Looked at the rock. Measured the current with his eyes the way he'd measured a thousand crossings, and found the line: a diagonal ferry-angle downstream to a submerged shelf just below the island, then a short, violent push across the throat of the channel to the near bank where the gravel bar fanned out.

"I'm coming across," he called. "When I reach the rock, give me your hand. Do exactly what I say. Can you do that?"

She hesitated. He watched her weigh it—stranger, lowlander, unknown quantity—against the rising water licking at her feet. Pride lost.

"Do exactly what I say," she repeated, mocking his accent slightly. But she nodded.

He went in.

The cold hit him like a fist. Monsoon-fed snowmelt, the kind of cold that stops the breath and turns muscle to wood in thirty seconds. He angled downstream as he waded, leaning on the current instead of fighting it, feeling for the shelf with his boots. Found it. Two steps. Then the throat of the channel took him to the chest and tried to peel him off the bottom, and for one long gray moment the world was nothing but roar and pressure and the map's weight dragging at his chest like an anchor—

—and then his boot found the far seam of gravel and he hauled himself up onto the island, gasping, soaked to the collarbones.

The girl was staring at him. "You're bad at that," she said.

"I'm *fine* at that." He shook water off his hands. "Give me your hand. We cross together, me on the upstream side. When I say lean, you lean into me. If we go down, don't fight to stand up—float on your back, feet downstream. Understood?"

"You talk like a schoolteacher."

"And you talk like someone whose goats are going to eat her tent. Hand."

She gave him her hand. Small, calloused, rope-burned at the palm. They crossed with the water hammering at them, and she leaned when he said lean, and fought when her body told her to even though he'd told her not to, and in the end it didn't matter because the line held and they came up the gravel bar together, streaming, alive.

On solid ground she pulled her hand free immediately and stepped back, wringing out her braid, reassembling her dignity piece by piece. Up close he could see she wasn't local to the lowlands—the high-valley cut of her cheekbones, the turquoise bead at her ear, the wool coat woven in patterns he'd only ever seen in photographs of villages that officially no longer existed.

"Thank you," she said, in the tone of someone paying a debt she resented.

"You're welcome. What's your name?"

A pause. Long enough that he recognized the calculation in it, because he performed the same calculation every time someone asked him the same question.

"Tashi," she said finally.

"Jonah," he said, and then, out of habit so deep it bypassed thought, "—Karma. People call me Karma."

Her eyes narrowed, just slightly. Filed away. She'd heard the hitch; he could tell she'd heard it. But she let it go, which told him she had her own reasons for knowing what a false name was for.

"Your goats," he said, nodding across the channel. "Can they swim?"

"They can walk across at the shallows, half a mile down. I was going to do that." She glanced upstream, at the darkening sky pressing down into the gorge, and her bravado flickered. "I was going to do it an hour ago. The rock seemed safer. Then the water came up faster than—" She stopped, jaw tightening, refusing to finish the sentence in front of a stranger.

"Storm's early this year," Jonah said. "Monsoon's coming up the valley ahead of schedule. You should get down off the river."

"I live up the river."

He looked at her. She looked back, chin up, daring him to make something of it. Everyone official said there was no one living up the Kalyani. Everyone official said the upper valley had been evacuated years ago, resettled, compensated, closed. Everyone official said a great many things.

"You're from one of the villages," he said carefully. "Upstream."

Something shut behind her eyes. "You ask a lot of questions for a man who almost drowned in a knee-deep stream."

"It was chest-deep."

"It was knee-deep where I would have crossed it." She whistled sharply, twice, and began working her way down the bank toward the shallows, and after a moment's exasperation with himself, Jonah followed.

* * *

They moved the goats along the lower path together, because the alternative was admitting he wanted her company, and neither of them was willing to admit anything. The animals flowed around their legs like gray water themselves, and Tashi handled them with a fluency that needed no words—a touch here, a stone tossed there, the flock reading her the way weather reads a ridge.

She talked. Not to him, exactly—around him, the way people talk when silence has become more dangerous than speech.

"There were eleven families on our side of the bend," she said, apropos of nothing. "Before. Now my grandmother says there are four, and two of those are just old men who refused to leave and are too stubborn to die." She picked her way over a fallen slab. "The soldiers came when I was seven. I remember the registers most. They burned the school records in a barrel in the courtyard. My teacher cried. I didn't understand why paper was worth crying over." She glanced at him sideways. "I understand now."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you asked about the villages. Nobody asks about the villages. Lowlanders pretend we're ghosts." She shrugged. "Ghosts don't need goats rescued."

"I didn't rescue your goats."

"No. You rescued me, which was stupider." But there was no venom in it now. Something else had entered her voice, quieter. "Why are you going upstream, Karma-who-is-not-really-called-Karma? Nobody goes upstream. The passes are closed. The patrols shoot at shadows after the first storm."

"I'm delivering something," Jonah said. "For a man who died."

"What kind of thing?"

"A debt."

She considered this with the gravity of someone raised on stories where debts were living things. "To whom?"

"That's the part I don't know yet."

Tashi nodded slowly, as if this were the most sensible answer she'd heard all year. Ahead, the gorge bent north and the light failed all at once, evening falling into the canyon like a stone into a well. And then she stopped, abruptly, one hand raised, and every goat stopped with her, and the silence that fell had a different texture than the silence before.

Boots on rock. Above them, on the high trail along the opposite wall—two figures, maybe three, headlamps just kindling in the blue dusk, moving upstream with the unhurried confidence of men searching rather than fleeing.

Patrols. The first ones. Closer than he'd calculated.

Tashi looked at the soldiers, then at Jonah, and read everything she needed to read in his face. "You're the shadow," she breathed. "The one they're hunting. The teahouse woman sent word up with the salt caravan—*a tall lowlander with a dead man's bag.*"

Jonah's stomach dropped. Word had outrun him. Of course it had; gossip moved faster than any patrol, and Serem was a town built entirely out of gossip.

"They'll reach the bend before us," he said quietly. "Is there another way? A shepherd's path, anything?"

Tashi was already turning, already moving, the goats wheeling with her like a flock of thoughts. "There's a way," she said. "My grandfather's way. It isn't on any map, and it isn't kind to lowlanders." She looked back at him, and in the last gray light her face held something that wasn't quite trust and wasn't quite judgment—something being decided, in real time, between the two.

"But you saved me from the water," she said. "So now the water owes you, and I collect its debts. Come on. And whatever you're carrying—" her eyes flicked to his chest, to the shape under the wet jacket, "—you'd better hold on to it. The gorge echoes everything. Even secrets."
