## 7. What Tashi Remembers

The fire had burned down to a red heart, and Tashi fed it one more branch of juniper because the smoke kept the midges away and because she liked the smell. It was the smell of her grandmother's kitchen, of wool drying on stones, of every evening before the soldiers came. Above them the gorge walls rose black against a sky salted with stars, and somewhere below, the Kalyani went on talking to itself in its gravel voice, the way it always had, the way it would after everyone who remembered its old names was dead.

Jonah sat across the flames with the map rolled in his hands like something wounded. He hadn't eaten much. He kept looking at her the way strangers did when they'd decided she was a child and then caught her doing something that made them unsure.

"You asked about the village," she said. "Upstream. You asked where the people went."

"I asked if anyone still lived there."

"No one lives there." She turned the branch so the green end caught. "That's not the same as no one being from there."

He waited. He was good at waiting; she'd noticed that about him already. Most men filled silence like water filling a boot. This one let it sit.

"Tell me what you know," she said. "About why we left. The official reason."

He shrugged, uncomfortable. "I've heard versions. That the valley was declared unstable after a seismic survey. Landslide risk above the reservoir zone. That the government relocated everyone for their own safety. Compensation was paid—there were papers, I saw some once, years ago, when I still worked these ranges. Resettlement certificates. There was even talk of a model settlement downstream. New housing."

"New housing," Tashi repeated.

"That's the account."

She nodded slowly, and fed the fire another stick, though it didn't need one.

* * *

Here is what Tashi remembered, and she had spent six years deciding whether to stop remembering it, and had failed.

She was nine. It was the end of the barley harvest, the week when her mother hung the first dried cheese from the rafters and the whole village smelled like smoke and milk. The soldiers came in trucks that couldn't properly climb the last switchbacks, so they walked up from the river road in a long grey line, and their boots made a sound on the stone path that Tashi could still hear in her body, a sound like the mountain itself clearing its throat before it spoke.

There were two kinds of men. Soldiers in grey, and other men in plain clothes with clipboards and soft voices, and it was the soft-voiced ones you had to watch. Her grandmother said so afterward, many times: *the loud ones only want your fear, but the quiet ones want your name.*

They gathered everyone in the courtyard below the chorten, all forty-one households, and a man with a clipboard read out an order in accented local speech, the words stiff as frozen rope. The valley was unstable. The mountain above the glacier had shown signs of movement. For their own protection, the villages of the upper Kalyani were to be relocated within nine days.

Her father stood up. He was a big man, slow to anger, the kind who carried doubled loads without complaint, and he asked the question everyone wanted asked: *unstable by whose measurement?*

The clipboard man smiled and said the measurements had been done by experts.

"What experts?" her father said. "When? Show us the instruments. Let our own people walk the slopes with them."

"There are documents," the man said.

"Then show us the documents."

And here was the thing—the thing Tashi had turned over ten thousand times like a stone in her pocket, looking for the crack in it—the man never showed anything. Not that day, not in the days after. Men came back twice more, and each time there were papers they could not display, signatures they could not produce, offices they could not name. And each time there were more soldiers, and fewer questions, until the ninth day came and the trucks idled at the bend below the village and the choice was no longer a choice.

But it wasn't the soldiers Tashi remembered most. It was the burning.

On the eighth day, three men in plain clothes went building to building with a metal box, collecting paper. School records. Land registries. The herding ledgers that said which family had grazed which meadow since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. The temple's donation book, a hundred years of names. They called it *verification*, and they took everything into the yard behind the schoolhouse and burned it in a steel drum, and the ash came down over the barley fields for two days like dirty snow.

Tashi had stood at the edge of the yard and watched the corner of a page curl and go. She recognized the handwriting on it. It was the teacher's, the round patient letters he used when he wrote out the alphabet for the small children, and she understood, in the way children understand things sideways and whole at once, that they weren't destroying paper. They were destroying proof. Proof that the village existed. Proof that anyone had ever been born there, learned there, buried their dead in its cold high ground.

After that, the valley could be called anything they wanted. Unstable. Empty. Uninhabited since ancient times. A place that had never held anyone worth compensating.

Her grandmother's sister refused to leave. Old Yudon, who was blind and said the mountain had been her eyes for eighty years and would see her out. They carried her to the truck anyway, wrapped in blankets, and she died the following spring in the resettlement blocks, in a concrete room where you couldn't tell morning from evening except by the generator's hum. Before they took her, Yudon had gripped Tashi's wrist with surprising strength and said: *Remember the names. Paper burns. You don't.*

So Tashi remembered names. Forty-one households. She could recite them still, lying awake in whatever shelter the season gave her, a litany against forgetting. Norbu the blacksmith. The two Dolkar families, elder and younger, who shared the high pasture rights. Pema Wangchuk, who kept the temple key. Three hundred and six souls, and she knew almost every one, because that was what remained when the registers burned—a girl with a good memory and a grudge shaped exactly like a valley.

* * *

"And the compensation," Jonah said quietly, when she finished. "Did anyone receive—"

"Some signed." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way hurt too much. "People were tired and frightened and the trucks were leaving either way. Some took the resettlement money. My father wouldn't touch it. He said taking payment for a lie makes the lie yours forever. He died four years ago in the lowlands, working construction, in a town where nobody knew his name meant anything." She looked up. "What did your papers say about the evacuation?"

He hesitated, and she watched him weigh truth against comfort and choose, this time, truth.

"That it was orderly. Voluntary. That the risk assessment was peer-reviewed." His mouth twisted. "I believed it. I was young, and it wasn't my valley, and believing official accounts is easier than carrying the weight of doubting them. I guided survey teams through these gorges after the relocation. We mapped routes for equipment. Nobody ever told me what the equipment was for."

"And now?"

"Now I'm carrying a dead man's map upstream through a valley both governments pretend doesn't exist." He turned the roll over in his fingers. "Tashi—who burned the records? The soldiers, or the others?"

"The others. The quiet men." She studied him across the fire. "Why does it matter?"

"Because soldiers follow orders and forget them. Quiet men with clipboards work for someone who pays them to make things disappear." He was silent a moment. "There's a company. A syndicate. They ruined me once—I thought for reasons of my own failure. Lately I've begun to wonder if my failure was also a document somebody wrote."

The fire cracked. Down in the dark the river rose and rose in its bed, louder than it had been that morning, feeding on the distant rain.

"My grandmother said the mountain keeps accounts," Tashi said. "She said rivers carry everything downhill eventually—silt, bones, secrets. The Kalyani has been carrying ours for six years." She poked the embers flat. "You're going upstream. Whatever you're carrying—it's going the right direction."

Jonah didn't answer, but his hand closed tighter around the map, and Tashi, watching him, felt the first small dangerous warmth of something she had sworn off feeling for strangers: hope that he might actually get there.

Then he unrolled the map a little, angling it toward the firelight to check tomorrow's route, and she saw his face change. Not fear exactly. Something older and stranger than fear—the look of a man reading his own name in a place it should not be.

"Tashi," he said slowly. "This village of yours. The school register that burned. Was there another copy? Anything written down—names, dates—"

"The teacher kept a private roll. Hidden, maybe. Why?"

He didn't answer at once. His thumb rested on the map's margin, on a cluster of tiny handwritten characters near the ghost village's mark, characters she couldn't read at that distance but that had clearly stopped his breath.

"Because I think," he said, "someone wrote it down again."
