## 8. Ghost Village

They came down out of the moraine at first light, and the village rose to meet them the way drowned things rise—slowly, unwillingly, out of a fog that clung to the valley floor like milk poured into water.

Jonah had seen abandoned places before. He had guided clients past empty herding camps and through villages emptied by landslide and by the slow arithmetic of young people leaving for towns that offered electricity and wages. Abandonment usually looked like neglect: doors ajar, roofs sagging, a wheelbarrow rusting mid-task as if its owner had simply stepped away to answer a call.

This was different. This had been *stopped*.

The houses stood whole. The slate roofs were intact, the dry-stone walls true, the prayer flags on the ridgepole of the largest house bleached to the color of bone but still knotted, still tied by hands that had expected to come back and replace them. Cooking pots sat on hearths. A loom stood in the open doorway of one house, the warp still stretched, the shuttle resting in its cradle of thread. Whoever had left had left in a hurry, but not in panic—or rather, in a panic so total and so controlled that it had frozen everything mid-breath, the way a flash flood freezes a herd of goats mid-stride in the stone of a canyon wall.

"Chorten," Tashi said quietly, behind him. Not the village's real name—he understood that at once. She was giving him the word for what it was: a shrine, a place of the dead. She would not say the village's name aloud, and he understood that too, though he didn't yet understand why. "My mother's mother lived in the blue-shuttered house. Third from the shrine wall."

"You don't have to—"

"I know I don't." She pushed past him, her staff striking the cobbles with a sound too loud for the silence. "Walk."

* * *

The fog thinned as the sun climbed, and the village emerged in detail that made Jonah's chest ache. A stone water channel still ran, fed by a side-stream, water sliding clean and cold over its mossy bed as if the village were merely sleeping in. A dog—no. He started at a shape in the shadow of a doorway, then saw it was a woven basket, shaped by years of use into the ghost of a resting animal. Children's chalk marks on a wall by the well: a game board, half finished, the counters still in play. He counted them without meaning to. Eleven stones. Someone had been winning.

"They came in the fourth month," Tashi said. She had stopped at the well and was looking down into it as if the water might answer. "I was eleven. Soldiers first—two lorries, then four. They said there had been a survey. They said the dam surveys had shown the valley would flood within two years, that the government had already signed the order, that anyone who stayed would lose compensation. They had papers. Stamped papers, with ribbons and seals, the kind that make old men bow." Her voice was flat, reciting. "My grandfather asked to see the survey itself. An officer hit him with the flat of his baton and told him papers were not for farmers. The next day the records office burned. Not the whole building—just the room. Just the shelves."

"The school register," Jonah said.

"The school register. The land rolls. The birth records. Everything written in the valley's own hand." She turned and looked at him, and in the grey morning light her face was very young and very old at once. "They burned what we wrote about ourselves. Then they wrote about us what they wanted. That's how you kill a place. You don't have to kill the people. You just have to kill the record."

Jonah stood very still. The map was against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath his jacket, and he was suddenly, acutely aware of its weight—not the weight of paper, but the weight of exactly this: a record. A thing written in someone's own hand. Dorje's hand.

"Where was the school?" he asked.

Tashi's eyes narrowed. "Why?"

"Because you said the teacher kept a private roll. If I were a teacher in a valley being erased, and I had one night and one tin box, I would hide it somewhere the soldiers would never think to look. Somewhere that wasn't the school." He was thinking aloud now, the old guide's mind moving the way it used to move over a route, testing holds. "Somewhere that looked like nothing."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she jerked her chin toward the upper end of the village, where the houses gave way to terraced fields gone to seed, and above the fields a low stone building with a rusted iron roof.

"The shrine-house," she said. "Where the dead are kept before burning. Nobody loots a charnel house. Even soldiers are afraid of ghosts." A pause, and something bitter moved through her voice like a fish under ice. "They were right to be. This place is full of them."

* * *

They went up through the terraces in single file, Tashi leading now, and Jonah let her. It was her village. Whatever he was looking for, he had no right to lead here.

The fields had gone wild in the years of emptiness—buckwheat escaped and self-sown, standing in ragged gold drifts that whispered against their knees. Bees worked the gone-to-seed flowers in a low, constant drone, the only voices the valley had left. Jonah found himself cataloguing, the way he always did: the water channel still flowing, which meant the source stream above was healthy; the absence of fresh hoofprints; a marmot colony whistling on the wall by the third terrace, which meant no hunters had passed recently, which meant no patrols either, or none in weeks. The village was not only abandoned. It was *unwatched*. The syndicate's men, whoever and however many they were, had decided this stretch of the river was empty.

He thought of Voss—the name Tashi's teahouse keeper had whispered, the man in the grey gloves asking after a dead prospector's belongings—and put the thought away like a knife into a sheath. Not now. Now there was only the shrine-house and whatever waited in it.

The building sat at the head of the terraces with the mountain rising sheer behind it. Low, windowless on three sides, its single door facing east to catch the dawn. Tashi stopped before it and did not touch the door.

"My uncle was laid out here for three days before they came," she said. "We were going to burn him on the fourth. The soldiers arrived on the second." She looked at Jonah. "He's still in there, isn't he. Under the stones. They never let us come back for the dead."

"Tashi—"

"Open the door. I'll wait here."

He understood. Some thresholds a person cannot cross twice. He put his hand on the iron latch, which was cold and furred with lichen, and lifted it, and went in alone.

* * *

The interior was dim, lit by one small smoke-hole and the light leaking under the eaves. Rows of stone cairns filled the floor, each roughly man-length, each marked with a wooden tablet whose painted letters had faded to shadows of shadows. The air was dry and mineral and old, and beneath it, faintly, something else—incense, maybe, or the memory of incense, sunk so deep into the stone that the stone itself had become a slow-burning stick.

Jonah stood a moment and let his eyes adjust, and made himself breathe, and made himself be respectful. He was not a religious man. His father had made sure of that, in the way of men who believe in nothing and punish their children for hoping otherwise. But there are rooms that command a certain posture from anyone, and this was one. He felt the way he felt on a ridge in high wind: small, watched, and precisely, usefully awake.

The teacher's hiding place, if it existed, would be low and ordinary. He knelt by the nearest cairn and looked beneath it. Nothing but dust and a dead moth. He worked his way down the row, checking the bases of the tablets, the gaps in the wall stones, the hollow beneath the smoke-hole. His hands were steady. His heart was not.

It was the third cairn from the eastern wall. The stones there were seated on a slab that sat a finger's width proud of the floor, and when he shifted the lowest stone aside and got his fingers under the slab's edge, it came up with a grinding whisper, and beneath it, in a cavity lined with oilcloth gone brittle, lay a tin box.

He lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a injured bird.

The box was a tea tin, its painted lettering flaked, its lid rust-welded in places but hinged at one corner where someone had carefully, patiently worked it free over what must have been many nights. Inside: a bundle of papers wrapped in waxed cloth, tied with cord. And on top of the bundle, a single loose sheet, folded once, addressed in a careful hand.

Jonah's breath stopped.

Not because of what was written. Because of the hand itself. He knew that hand. He had spent the first nineteen years of his life watching it move across the pages of ledger books and field notebooks and the margins of maps, a narrow, forward-slanting script with a peculiar hooked *k* and a habit of crossing letters in a single unbroken stroke. He had hated that hand. He had tried, in boarding school, to make his own handwriting nothing like it, and had failed, because a hand is not a choice; it is a spine.

The sheet read, in that hand, in the trade tongue:

*To the teacher, with respect. I could not stop what was done. I have written down what I know. When it is safe, or when it is never safe, this should go back to the valley. Forgive an old man's cowardice. — D.*

*D.*

Dorje.

Jonah sat back on his heels in the charnel house among the dead of Chorten, and the floor seemed to tilt beneath him, gently, the way a bridge moves when someone else has stepped onto it.

He untied the cord with fingers that did not feel like his own fingers.

* * *

The register was a school exercise book, its cover soft with handling, its pages filled with the same hooked script. It was not only a register. The teacher had copied what she could remember of the burned records before the burning, or Dorje had dictated it to her afterward—Jonah couldn't tell, and the distinction hardly mattered. Names. Birth dates. Land holdings, field by field, terrace by terrace, with the old boundaries drawn in a careful cartographer's line. A census of a valley, reconstructed from memory by two people who understood that memory, written down, becomes a weapon.

And then, on the fourth page, a list of the school's pupils, the year of Tashi's eleventh summer. The names ran down the margin in a child's uneven hand—copied by the teacher from her own memory of her students—and beside each name, in the hooked adult script, a note. *With aunt in the south. Family remains whole. Father taken for questioning, returned.* Small, terrible annotations. A ledger of what the erasure had cost, family by family.

Jonah read down the list of children. Tashi's name was there—her true name, the name she would not say in her own village, and he looked away from it quickly, ashamed, as if he had seen her without clothes.

And then, near the bottom of the page, in the column where the teacher had noted which children had relatives outside the valley, which families had kin in the low towns who might take them in:

*J. Blackwood — guide, foreign-born — no kin in valley — present at survey, spring of the dam year.*

Jonah read it three times.

The fog in his head did not lift; it thickened, and something moved inside it, vast and slow, the way a mountain moves when it is not a mountain at all but a glacier pretending to be one.

*J. Blackwood.* His own name, in a dead village, in a dead teacher's book, in a hand he knew better than his own. *Present at survey.* He had never been here. He had never— his mind reached for the memory and found nothing, a clean white wall of nothing, and the nothing was worse than a memory would have been, because it meant either that the book lied or that he did not know the shape of his own life.

The survey. The spring of the dam year. He had been twenty-two. He had been, that spring, exactly where his father had told his mother he was: *surveying a river in the eastern ranges, good money, steady work, don't ask questions.* He had spent that season, he realized now, with a sick, slow drop, doing something—carrying instruments, driving stakes, walking lines through someone's fields. He had thought of it, when he thought of it at all, as a job. A season of good pay. He had never asked what the instruments were measuring, or for whom, or what the stakes were stakes *in*.

And his father had been there. His father had been here, in this valley, and Jonah had been here too, and neither of them had known—or one of them had known, and had said nothing, and had watched his own son walk lines through fields that were about to be stolen, and had written the boy's name into a dead teacher's book as a witness. *Present at survey.* Not an accusation. A fact. His father had recorded him the way he recorded everything else—carefully, completely, in that hooked hand—as if Jonah too were part of the valley's stolen history. As if Jonah had been taken from the valley along with the fields and the records and the names, without ever knowing he had belonged to it.

He became aware that he was shaking. Not cold. Something older than cold.

* * *

When he came out, the sun had cleared the ridge and the terraces were burning gold. Tashi stood where he had left her, but she was not looking at him. She was looking at the tin box under his arm, and her face had gone through surprise into something harder.

"You found it."

"The teacher hid it under the cairns. Your uncle's, I think." He held it out to her. "It's yours. It was always meant to come back to the valley."

She took it, but she didn't open it. She was watching his face with the flat, unblinking attention she gave to unstable ground. "You've read it."

"Yes."

"And?" And then she saw it—whatever had happened to his face while he read, the vertigo, the unraveling—and her voice changed. "Jonah. What's in it?"

He opened his mouth to say *nothing*, the reflex of a man who had spent six years being no one, and found he couldn't. Not here. Not among the cairns of her dead, with her village's stolen census in her hands.

"My name is in it," he said. "J. Blackwood. In the pupil list. In the column for outsiders present at the survey." His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like a voice heard across a gorge. "Tashi, I was here. That spring. I was part of the survey crew and I didn't even know— I carried their instruments. I helped them measure the fields they were going to drown." He heard his own breathing, too fast. "The man who wrote the notes. The man who wrote the map. Dorje. It's my father's handwriting. It's been his handwriting my whole life, and I knew it the moment I saw it, and I told myself it was a coincidence, and it was never a coincidence. He knew me. He knew me when he handed me that map on the trail, and he knew what I'd done here, and he gave it to me anyway."

Tashi was silent for a long time. Below them the village lay in the sun, its chalk game still unfinished, its loom still threaded, its eleven counters waiting in play. Somewhere in the wild buckwheat a pheasant called, three notes, like a question asked twice and then abandoned.

"So the man who burned the valley," she said at last, quietly, "sent his son to carry back what was burned."

"I don't know what he sent me to do. I don't know what I am in this anymore." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I've spent six years being a false name in a border town, and it turns out the false name was the honest one. I don't know who J. Blackwood is. I never did."

Tashi looked down at the tin in her hands. When she spoke again, her voice was the voice she used on the trail, when the route was bad and there was no choice but to take it.

"Upstream," she said. "Whatever you are, you're it upstream. Because I heard water in the night, Jonah. The main river. It's up." She turned her face toward the gorge below the village, and he heard it too now, beneath the bees and the pheasant—a deep, changed sound, the sound of a river that had begun to remember it was a monsoon river. "And there's a crossing at the foot of the terraces that won't exist by noon."

He looked at the shrine-house, at the cairns, at the tin box in the girl's hands, at the whole frozen, breath-held valley that his own feet had once helped measure for drowning.

Then he shouldered his pack, and they started down toward the water.
