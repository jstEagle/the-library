## 10. The Bridge at Kalpa Bend

The rain had followed Voss for three days, and he had begun to think of it as a colleague.

It worked the way he worked—quietly at first, then all at once, then without any interest in what it destroyed. It had softened the trail behind the girl and the guide until the mountain itself seemed to be lying to him. It had turned the porters' village at Serem into a swamp of rumor. And now it stood on the far side of Kalpa Bend with him, drumming on the hood of his oilskin, patient as a debt collector, while he studied the suspension bridge that was about to become the most important object in his professional life.

It was not much of a bridge. Two cables of twisted wire rope, sagging under their own history, anchored into boulders on either bank and slung across a gorge where the Kalyani ran brown and swollen, forty feet down, loud enough that Voss had stopped trying to hold conversations an hour ago. The planking between the cables had been laid by someone with more faith than lumber; half of it was missing, half of what remained was rotten, and the whole structure swayed in the wind like something dreaming. A sign nailed to the near post read, in faded Devanagari and cruder English: *CROSS AT OWN RISK. MAX 2 PERSONS.*

Voss appreciated the honesty. Most things in this country lied to you pleasantly.

He crouched at the anchor boulder and touched the wire where it wrapped around the iron ring. Wet, but the wrap was tight, recently tensioned—someone had come through here within the last two days and taken the trouble to make the crossing safe. That was the guide's work. He'd been reading the man's habits for a week now, the way a tracker reads spoor: the careful camp sites, the fire ash buried twice, the pace that was fast but never reckless. A professional. A blacklisted one, which meant a desperate one, but desperation only made men faster, not smarter.

And the girl. Tashi. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, goat-herder, knew every goat path in the middle gorge according to the teahouse keeper's widow, who had told Voss everything she knew and several things she invented before he'd finished his first cup of tea. The girl was the variable. Guides could be predicted. Children could not.

He straightened, flexed his hands inside the grey gloves—he wore them even in the rain, especially in the rain, because wet skin left prints and prints were confessions—and looked downstream the way he'd come.

His trackers were strung out along the bank below him like beads on a wet string. Three of them, ex-soldiers from the lowland garrisons, good men for following and poor men for thinking, which was exactly what he'd asked the syndicate's man in Serem for. They were cold, soaked through, and beginning to look at him the way hired men look at employers when the work stops being easy. He would need to give them something soon. Money, or fear. Fear kept longer.

*There,* he thought, watching the far bank. *There they are.*

Movement above the far anchorage—a flicker of color against grey rock, gone as quickly as it came. Then again, lower, closer to the bridge head. Two figures working their way down through the scrub: one small and quick, one taller, moving with the deliberate economy of a man carrying weight he didn't want seen. The map. Or whatever the old prospector had given him. Voss had never actually confirmed what Dorje Lhakpa had been carrying when he walked out of the high country to die in a border-town teahouse. He only knew what the syndicate's records said Dorje had *taken*, years ago, when he left their employ: survey notes, compensation figures, accident reports—the paper skeleton of the Kalyani valley project. Enough paper to hang a dozen men if it ever reached a court or a newspaper or, worse, the villagers themselves.

Voss had been sent to make sure it never did. The instruction had been elegant in its simplicity: *recover or destroy.* He had done this kind of work before. He was good at it. What he had not expected, seven days ago in Serem, was to find himself hunting Jonah Blackwood.

He'd known the name, of course. Every man in the syndicate's orbit knew it, though most of them had forgotten why. Blackwood had been the guide on the Upper Kalyani survey eight years back—the young one, the talented one, the one whose incident report Voss himself had drafted at a desk in the company office while the real story sat in a filing cabinet two rooms away. Voss remembered writing it. He remembered the specific pleasure of it, the craft: a sentence here implying the guide had ignored protocol, a clause there suggesting equipment negligence, the whole document assembled so carefully that even Blackwood's own union rep had read it and shaken his head. Ruin by paperwork. No blood, no noise, no body. Just a man erased from a profession the way a name gets erased from a school register.

He had been proud of that report. He could admit that now, standing in the rain at the edge of a gorge, watching the man he'd ruined descend toward the only bridge for twenty miles. Pride was a thing you could admit once it no longer cost you anything.

The two figures reached the far bridge head. The girl went first onto the planks—of course she did, she'd have crossed this thing since she could walk—and the guide came after, moving with that maddening steadiness, one hand on the cable, testing each board before trusting it.

Voss stepped out of the cover of the rocks and walked to the near end of the bridge, making no effort to hide. There was no point. In thirty seconds they would see him anyway, and there was a kind of leverage in being seen first—in choosing the moment instead of having it chosen for you.

The wind carried his voice badly across the gorge. He pitched it low and flat, the way you speak to horses and frightened men.

"Mr. Blackwood."

The guide stopped. Even at forty feet, even through rain, Voss saw the stillness take him—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. Men always recognized the voice of the person who had ended their life, even if they'd never heard it spoken aloud.

"You know who I am," Voss called. "That saves time. I'd like to talk. Just talk. The girl can go on ahead—I've got no business with her."

It was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and that was fine. Lies at this distance weren't meant to convince. They were meant to occupy. Every second the guide spent deciding whether to believe him was a second Voss used to study the bridge, the anchors, the angles.

The girl had stopped too, halfway out over the void, one small hand locked around the cable. She was looking at Voss with an expression he recognized from villages he'd helped empty: not fear yet. Calculation. Children who had lost things learned early how to measure a threat.

"Your name is Voss," the guide called back. His voice was rougher than Voss remembered from the deposition transcripts. Living rough did that. "You wrote the report."

So he knew. Interesting. The old man must have told him—or the papers had. Either way, the fiction of ignorance was off the table, and Voss adjusted accordingly. With men who knew, you offered less and implied more.

"I wrote a lot of reports," Voss said. "Most of them true. That one paid for your silence for eight years, whether you signed it or not. I'm here to close the account." He let the pause stretch. "Walk back across. Give me what Dorje gave you. You walk away clean—new papers, new name if you want it, money enough to stop portering sacks in Serem. The girl keeps her valley. Nobody upstream ever hears my name."

Above them, thunder rolled down the gorge like a barrel dropped on stone. The river answered, rising, and Voss felt rather than saw the bridge shudder—a long slow oscillation, the cables singing a low note under the wind.

He watched the guide's face do its arithmetic. It was a good offer, or it sounded like one, and that was the point. But the guide had spent a week running upstream through ghost villages with a dead man's map, and men don't spend a week doing that for money. Voss knew it even as he made the offer. The offer wasn't for the guide. It was for the girl—to plant the seed, to make her the pressure point later, if it came to that.

"He's lying," the girl said. Not loudly. The river almost took it, but the wind turned at the right moment and carried it. "They burned our records. My mother went to the office in Serem with her grandfather's land papers and the man threw them in a stove while she watched." She turned her head and looked at the guide, and Voss saw something pass between them that he hadn't planned for and couldn't price. "You know what he is. Why are you listening?"

Because, Voss thought, answering her silently, he's listening because I am offering him the one thing he wants more than justice, and you're fifteen and don't yet know that everyone has such a thing. He's listening because I built the cage he lives in and I'm the only one holding the key, and even a man who hates the keyholder will listen to the sound of it turning.

But the guide didn't answer her. Instead he did something Voss found genuinely unsettling, in the small cold way that competent people are unsettling: he smiled. Briefly. Without humor.

"No," Blackwood called. "I had a life once. You taught me what it costs to buy it back." He turned to the girl. "Go. Cross. I'm right behind you."

And that should have been the end of it—the bridge swinging, two figures retreating up the far bank into the rain, Voss left to find another crossing and lose another day. Voss had already begun composing the fallback in his head when the girl moved.

She didn't cross. She crouched.

Voss's eyes went to her hands, then to the cable she held, then—too late, a full second too late, a lifetime too late—to the coil of light rope hanging at her hip, the kind goat-herders carry for tethering animals, with a sickle-bladed knife clipped beside it.

"Tashi," the guide said sharply.

"The bridge is the only way," she said. To the guide, not to Voss. Her voice had gone very steady, the way voices do when the person inside them has already decided and is just informing the world. "If we cross, he follows. If we cut it, he stops." She was already unwinding the rope, looping it around the main cable above the anchorage, tying herself a harness with fingers that moved like she'd done it ten thousand times—which, Voss understood, she probably had, on fences and gates and goat pens, in a village that no longer existed except in her memory and in the ashes of a stove in Serem.

"If you cut it, there's no way back," the guide said. "Not for us either. Not for anyone. The monsoon's coming—the fords below will be gone in a day. We seal ourselves upstream with nowhere to retreat."

"I know," the girl said.

And Voss, watching her, felt the first genuine emotion of the entire pursuit rise in his chest—not anger, not yet, but a kind of appalled respect, the feeling of a chess player watching a child sacrifice her own queen to break open a position he'd spent years building. She wasn't threatening him. She was threatening *herself*. She was burning her own retreat to deny him his, and there was no countermove to that, because you cannot outbid a person for something they have already decided to give up.

"Girl," he called, and heard his own voice change, the flatness cracking into something almost human. "Think. Upstream is a drowned valley and a winter that kills goats. You cut that bridge and you're sealing yourself in with him and a dead man's papers, and when the snow comes—"

"When the snow comes," she said, "everyone will know what your company did, because he'll have told us. That's the difference between us, sahib. You're afraid of paper. We're not."

The knife came out. Small, curved, honest.

"Tashi—" The guide's voice again, and this time Voss heard something in it that the transcripts had never contained: alarm. Real alarm, for her, not for the mission. "Wait—"

She waited exactly long enough to finish the knot.

Then she swung out over the water, hanging from the looped rope beneath the main cable, and began cutting the hangers—the short vertical wires that held the planks to the spine of the bridge. One. Two. The severed ends whipped away into the gorge. The bridge lurched, groaned, dipped at the far quarter like a wounded animal shifting its weight.

Voss did the math faster than he'd ever done anything in his life. Hanger wires, maybe forty of them; she was cutting the ones nearest the far anchorage; the span would fail from the far end, collapse toward the center, drop everything into the gorge including—

Including the near anchorage's tension, if the failure torqued the cable. Which it would. Which meant the boulder, the ring, the whole arrangement on *his* side might hold or might not, and either way the bridge would be a pair of dead ropes hanging into white water, useless to him, useless to anyone, for weeks, until a crew came with new wire, and no crew was coming, because the monsoon was landing tonight and the syndicate's reach ended where the roads drowned.

"Stop her," he shouted—at his trackers, at God, at no one—and the nearest tracker scrambled up the bank toward the anchor boulder as if three men could run across a collapsing bridge.

Three hangers. Four. The planking peeled away section by section and fell, spinning, into the brown torrent, swallowed without a ripple worth the name. The girl hauled herself hand over hand along the main cable toward solid rock with the knife still in her teeth, and the guide stood at the far bridge head and did not run for cover, did not move at all, just stood in the rain watching her with an expression Voss recognized because he had seen it in his own mirror once, years ago, the night he signed the last page of the false report and understood what he had become: the look of a man watching the door close behind him and finding, to his own astonishment, that he is glad.

The cable parted at the far anchorage with a sound like a rifle shot.

The whole span dropped. The near cable whipped, tore its wrapping loose from the boulder in a spray of rust and water, and lashed backward past Voss's shoulder close enough to part the fabric of his sleeve—he felt the wind of it, the heat of friction through the glove—and then the bridge was gone, all of it, the wire snaking down into the gorge and the river taking it the way it took everything, patiently, without comment.

Silence, except for the rain and the water.

Voss stood at the edge of the near anchorage and looked across forty feet of roaring void at the place where the bridge had been. On the far side, the girl crouched on solid rock, coiling her rope, unhurt, methodical, already packing. Beside her the guide raised one hand—not a wave, not quite. An acknowledgment. The gesture a professional gives another professional across a finished job.

Then they turned and climbed, up through the scrub, into the treeline, into the rain, going up. Always up. Toward the source. Toward the drowned valley and the cairns and whatever the old man had wanted delivered there.

Behind Voss, the trackers had gathered at a respectful distance, saying nothing, waiting to be told what happened next, and what happened next was nothing, because there was nothing to tell. The road back was a day behind him and closing as the rivers rose. The road forward did not exist. Somewhere above him, in weather that was about to become a catastrophe, were two people who had just demonstrated—with a knife and a length of goat rope—that they wanted their future more than he wanted their past.

He looked down at his gloves. Grey wool, grey leather, immaculate. His one vanity, his one constant, the uniform of a man who handled other people's lives without leaving marks.

The rain fell on them and darkened them, and for a long moment Voss let it.

Then he turned upstream, to where the gorge narrowed and the goat paths began, and started to climb.

* * *

They found the first path an hour later, barely a ledge scratched into the cliff face, marked with a cairn of stones that someone had rebuilt recently—within the season, Voss judged, kneeling to examine it. Fresh-moved rock. Someone still came this way. Someone still remembered the route well enough to maintain it, in a valley that officially held no one.

He stood, and looked up at the wall of the gorge disappearing into cloud, and understood, with the clarity that sometimes arrives at the exact moment it is least useful, that he was no longer pursuing anyone.

The bridge was cut. The retreat was sealed. There was only upstream now—for all of them.

Higher on the ridge, half-lost in the rain, something glinted: a scrap of bright cloth snagged on thornbrush, left deliberately, he was sure of it, a marker or a taunt or simply a girl's practical habit of marking the way for whoever followed. The wind took the sound of the river and lifted it, and beneath the water's roar Voss thought he could hear, faint and impossible, the far-off first thunder of the monsoon proper arriving over the high peaks—coming down the valley like a verdict, meeting him on the path, going where he was going.

He pulled his gloves tighter and climbed.
