## 12. Tashi's Judgment

Tashi woke to the sound of Jonah reading.

Not aloud—she could tell by the stillness of his lips—but the kind of reading that wanted to be aloud. His mouth moved around the words like a man chewing stones. He sat hunched in the cave's mouth with the map spread across his knees and a bundle of folded papers beside him, papers she had not seen before, soft with handling, their edges furred like old cloth. The storm outside had settled into a steady grey drumming on the rock, and the light that came through the cave mouth was the color of dishwater.

She had slept badly. She always slept badly now, but last night worse than most, because of what she had seen in the firelight before sleep took her: Jonah's face when he read those letters. She had watched him from under her blanket the way you watch a stranger at a well—carefully, without seeming to—and what she had seen was a man being unmade and remade in the same hour. Grief, yes. But underneath the grief something else, something that looked almost like relief, and relief was the thing she did not forgive easily. Relief was for people whose villages had not been burned.

She sat up. The wool blanket slid from her shoulders and the cold came in at once, the high cold of the cave, smelling of wet stone and juniper smoke from their small fire.

"You found more papers," she said.

Jonah flinched. She noted it—the flinch of a man caught doing something private—and felt her chest tighten with an old, familiar anger that had no proper home yet.

"Letters," he said. "Drafts. Dorje wrote them and never sent them." He hesitated, and she watched him decide whether to lie to her. She watched him decide against it, or maybe just fail to find a lie fast enough. "They were addressed to his son."

"The son who never came."

"Yes."

Tashi rose, folded her blanket with the sharp economy her grandmother had taught her—corners square, no wasted motion—and came to sit across the fire from him. Not beside him. Across. She wanted the fire between them this morning, though she could not yet have said why, only that some instinct older than thought told her to keep a line drawn in the ash.

"Read me one," she said.

"Tashi—"

"You read them all night. I heard you turning the pages. You read them for yourself. Now read one for me." She held out her hand, palm up, the way beggars do, and hated herself a little for the gesture and did not take it back. "You carry his words into my mountains, Jonah Blackwood. I have a right to hear them."

He looked at her for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face. Then he unfolded the letter on top of the pile—a thin one, water-stained along one edge—and read.

*My son. Today I signed the compensation schedules for the upper valley. The numbers are lies stacked on lies; I know because I made some of them myself. The company calls it resettlement. I walked through Serku yesterday after the trucks left. There was a shoe in the road. One shoe, small, a child's. I stood there a long time. I am fifty-one years old and I stood in a road holding a child's shoe and I could not make my legs move. If you ever read this, know that your father was a coward who did his courage too late, and know also that I am going back up there, and that whatever I bring back will not be enough, but it is what I have.*

The rain filled the silence after. Tashi sat very still.

Serku. Her grandmother's village. The shoe could have been anyone's shoe. It could have been Lhamo's little brother's shoe; Lhamo had lost a brother's sandal in the evacuation and cried about the sandal for a year, about the sandal and not the brother, because grief finds the strangest handles. It could have been any of four hundred shoes walking down that road ahead of the soldiers.

"He was there," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using it. "Your father was there. When they emptied us out."

"I think he helped them do it," Jonah said quietly. "The letters go back years. At first he's proud of the company. Proud of the surveys, the roads, the money coming in. Then the numbers start bothering him. Then the accidents—" Jonah stopped. He looked down at the letters as if they might burn his hands. "There are things in here about accident reports. About men who died in the lower galleries and were written down as drunk, as careless, as their own killers. And about a guide who asked too many questions."

Tashi looked at him. The fire cracked a knot of juniper and threw a spark upward that died before it reached the roof of the cave.

"What guide?" she asked, though she already knew. She had known since the school register in the ghost village, since she'd seen his face when he found the name crossed out. She had spent two days not saying it, carrying the unsaid thing like a stone in her cheek, telling herself there were other explanations, that Blackwood was a common name even among outsiders, that coincidence was a river with many tributaries.

But rivers run downhill, and so does truth.

"My father," Jonah said. "Jonah Blackwood senior. He worked the Kalyani valley for the syndicate as a route consultant. And then he started asking questions, and then he was blacklisted—ruined, named in falsified reports, blamed for a collapse that killed six porters. A collapse the company caused by cutting the shoring budgets. They needed him discredited before he could talk. So they made him the disaster." Jonah's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against his knees. "I grew up in the shadow of that lie. I lost everything to it. My license, my name, my—"

"Your village didn't burn," Tashi said.

The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she did not want them back.

Jonah stopped talking. The rain went on. Somewhere above them, water was finding its way through cracks in the mountain, gathering, patient, the way water always is.

"No," he said finally. "It didn't."

"My grandmother walked out of Serku in the rain with three children and a cooking pot. My grandfather stayed to argue with the surveyors and they took him away and we never saw him again. Four hundred people. The records burned, the graves plowed, the names struck from the district rolls so that officially no one had ever lived there at all. Officially, Jonah, *we never existed.*" She heard her own voice rising and could not stop it, did not entirely want to. "And you sit there with your ruined career and your stolen name and your father's sad letters, and you want me to grieve with you. You want me to say, poor Jonah, how they wronged you."

"I don't—"

"*They erased us.*" Now she was on her feet, and the fire was between them and she was glad of it, glad of anything that stood between her and the look on his face. "Your father helped hold the pen. Your father drew the maps that brought the trucks. And you—you come upstream carrying his bones and his paper, and I feed you, I show you the paths, I cut a bridge rope and strand myself in these mountains for you, and the whole time you are the son of the man who signed my grandmother's world away."

"Tashi." Jonah stood too, slowly, the way you stand when a large animal is near. "I didn't know. Until last night I didn't know any of it."

"And until last night I didn't know you were his blood." She spat the word. "Blood goes somewhere, Jonah. Blood carries things. You've been climbing toward that source like a man going to confession, and I thought—I thought you carried it for *us.* For the people downstream. That's why I stayed. That's why I'm still here." Her voice broke on the last word and she despised it, despised the crack, despised the heat behind her eyes. "Was I wrong? Is this all just yours? Your father, your name, your redemption? Are we just the scenery on your way to becoming an honest man again?"

The question hung in the wet air between them, and she watched him try to answer it, and watched him fail, and understood that his failure was itself an answer.

"That's what I thought," she said.

She turned and went to the cave mouth.

* * *

The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist. Below the cave the mountainside fell away in long grey folds, and through the mist she could see the Kalyani far beneath, swollen and brown, dragging whole trees in its current like a man dragging furniture he has decided to throw away. Upstream, past the shoulder of the ridge, lay the drowned depot where they had found the ledger. Beyond that, three hard days' climb, the source. Beyond that, everything.

Her pack sat against the wall where she'd left it. It would take ten minutes to load. The goat paths were still there; the mountain had not moved; she knew a dozen ways down off this ridge that Jonah Blackwood would never find alone, and Voss would find even less easily. By nightfall she could be below the snowline. By the day after, at her aunt's summer shelter above Kalpa Bend, drinking salt tea, telling herself the story of the outsider she had guided and abandoned, and how it had been the sensible thing, the necessary thing, the thing any reasonable person would have done.

She stood with her hand on the pack strap for a long time.

The mist drifted. The river roared far below, patient as arithmetic. And Tashi stood in the cave mouth of a mountain that had swallowed her village's history, and she found that she could not make her hands lift the strap.

Because here was the thing she could not argue her way around: if she left, the ledger went wherever Jonah went, and Jonah, she now understood, was a man who could be talked into burning things. She had seen it in him already, in the cave at Kalpa Bend, in the way he spoke of clearing his name, wiping the slate, starting clean. Burning was what the syndicate did. Burning was the family trade. And if the ledger burned, then Serku burned a second time—not the houses this time but the fact of the houses, the proof, the four hundred names, and this time the match would be held by a girl from Serku herself.

No. She would not carry that down the mountain. She would not spend sixty years carrying it the way her grandmother carried the cooking pot, uselessly, symbolically, the pot that had cooked nothing since.

So it was not forgiveness that turned her around. Let that be clear, she told the mist, let it be clear to him, let it be carved somewhere in this cave: it was not forgiveness. It was arithmetic. He owed a debt and dead men pay nothing and she was not fool enough to release the debtor.

She turned back. Jonah had not moved. He stood where she'd left him, the letters gathered against his chest like something wounded, and his face—she studied it with a cold new attention, the attention she gave to weather, to ice bridges, to anything that might kill her—his face was wrecked and open and waiting.

"You're staying," he said. Not a question. He had seen her turn.

"I'm staying." She picked up the fire poker and fed two sticks of juniper to the coals, busying her hands because her voice needed steadying. "Understand what that means. I am not staying for you. I am staying because of what you carry. The ledger, the letters, the map—all of it goes to the source, and then it goes to the elders, to the people, and every copy that can be made will be made, and it will be kept in houses, in three places at least, so that no fire and no flood and no syndicate ever takes it from us again. That is what happens next. Say you understand."

"I understand."

"And you." She pointed the poker at him, and the coal-light ran red along its length. "You are not the victim in this story anymore. Do you hear me? You were wronged, fine, it's true, they broke you like they broke everything else. But you are also his son. You inherited something from that man besides his eyes. You inherited the debt. And debts in the mountains are not paid with tears." She set the poker down. Her hand was trembling now that there was nothing in it. "An apology is a stone you toss across a river. What you owe cannot be tossed. It has to be carried, upstream, in the rain, on your back, all the way to the top. And then it has to be given away. All of it. Even the parts that would clear your name. Especially those."

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"You're asking me to give up my own justice."

"I'm telling you what the price is. Whether you pay it is your business." She met his eyes and held them, and it cost her, because looking at him hurt in a way she hadn't expected—hurt because he was not a monster, that was the whole rotten trouble of it, he was just a man made of other men's choices, standing in the wreckage of a father he'd never known, and pity kept rising in her like groundwater no matter how many stones she piled on top of it. "My grandfather used to say the river doesn't accept apologies. It accepts what you put into it. Everything you put into it. Forever."

Outside, the mist was lifting off the ridgeline in long ragged banners, and through the tearing veil Tashi saw something that stopped her breath.

A light. Far below and across the gorge, on the trail they had abandoned two days ago—a small yellow point, bobbing, moving. Then a second. Then a third, strung out along the switchbacks like beads on a black string.

Torches. Or lanterns. Moving upstream. Toward the depot. Toward them.

"Jonah," she said, very quietly, and he turned and followed her gaze, and she watched the color leave his face. "How much farther to the source?"

"Three days. Two, if the weather breaks and we push."

"Then we don't wait for the weather." She was already rolling her blanket, her hands moving fast now, the anger in her transmuted at once into motion, into purpose, into the clean cold clarity of the hunted. "Pack everything. The letters stay inside your coat, against your skin. If they take you, they take the ledger over my body—no. No, listen to me." She grabbed his sleeve, made him look at her. "If they take you, you give them the ledger. You hear me? You give it up and you run. Dead men deliver nothing. The copies are what matter, and there are no copies yet, which means right now, today, on this mountain, you are the only archive my people have. So you stay alive. Whatever it costs. Promise me."

Jonah looked at her—at the torches, at her face, at the fire where his father's letters lay—and something shifted in him, some final settling, like scree finding its angle of rest.

"I promise," he said.

Tashi shouldered her pack and stepped out of the cave into the thinning rain, and above the gorge the clouds were beginning, at last, to tear.
