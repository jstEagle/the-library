## 13. The Syndicate's Ledger

The depot announced itself before they saw it—a smell of rust and drowned timber rising off the water like a confession.

Jonah felt it first through his boots, standing on the shelf of slate above the flooded hollow where the Kalyani's old supply line had once run. The monsoon had been at work here for days, and the river had climbed into the low ground with the patience of something that remembered every wrong done to it. What had been a yard was now a brown lake, lapping at the eaves of a long, low building that sagged into the water on its western side, its corrugated roof buckled into a wave of its own.

"That's a syndicate store," Tashi said. She said it flatly, the way she said most things now, but Jonah had learned to read the undertow in her voice. "My father carried loads there. Before."

"Before," Jonah repeated. The word had become a country between them.

She didn't look at him. She hadn't looked at him properly since the cave, since the letters, since *my son*—and he couldn't blame her for it. He had spent two days carrying the knowledge like a stone under his ribs: Dorje had been his father. Dorje had written letters and never sent them. Dorje had chosen the syndicate's money over a wife and a boy in a school register, and then, decades too late, had tried to buy back what could not be bought with a map and a dying breath.

And Tashi had watched him learn it. Had watched the son of the man who erased her village weep over drafts addressed to *my son*, and had made her own arithmetic about what grief was worth.

"The water's going to keep rising," she said instead. "If there's anything in there worth finding, it's today or never."

Jonah looked at the depot a long moment. He should have wanted to walk past it. Every instinct that had kept him alive for six years in Serem—the instinct to be small, to be invisible, to touch nothing that belonged to powerful men—told him to put his head down and climb. But the letters had broken something open in him, and what lay underneath was not grief. It was older than grief. It was the feeling he'd had at nineteen, reading his first accident report, knowing the numbers were wrong and being told by everyone whose opinion mattered that he was too young to know what he knew.

"I want to see inside," he heard himself say.

Tashi finally turned. Her eyes were narrow, unreadable as scree slopes. "Why?"

Because my name is in there somewhere, he almost said. Because I have spent six years believing I failed, and if there is paper in that building that says otherwise, I want to hold it in my hands until my fingers go numb.

"Because Dorje sent me upstream," he said. "Not just to the source. He sent me *through* all this. Past the gorge, past your village, past Kalpa Bend." He nodded at the drowning building. "He knew what was in these places. I think he wanted me to find it."

"He wanted you to carry his guilt up a mountain," Tashi said. "That's not the same as wanting you to find things."

"No," Jonah agreed quietly. "It isn't. But I'm going in anyway."

* * *

They went in at dawn, when the water was lowest, wading chest-deep along the wall where the current ran weakest. The cold took Jonah's breath and held it. Below the surface his feet found concrete, then shelving, then the soft collapse of rotted cardboard. The interior was a cathedral of half-drowned industry: cable drums bobbing against the ceiling beams like lily pads, crates stacked to the rafters on the dry side, their stenciled markings still legible—KALYANI CONSOLIDATED MINING & HYDRO, SUPPLY DEPOT 4, PROPERTY OF THE SYNDICATE. A calendar from eleven years ago hung curled on a nail, its photograph a smiling engineer giving a thumbs-up in front of a drill rig.

Tashi moved through it like a ghost moving through her own house. Jonah watched her touch the edge of a workbench, lightly, with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure would wake friendly.

"There was a weighbridge out front," she murmured. "Trucks came at night sometimes. My father said the ledgers lived here. Real ones." She glanced at him. "The ones they showed the government were printed in the capital. Pretty books. Clean columns."

"And the real ones stayed here."

"Until the evacuation. Then the soldiers burned the office in our village, and everyone assumed—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Everyone assumed everything burned."

Jonah hauled himself onto the dry shelving and began working along the row of crates nearest the old office partition, prying lids, shaking out silt and dead insects. Rope. Detonator wire, fused into one grey mass. Tin plates gone to lace. And then, wedged behind a false panel in the third crate—a panel so well made he'd have missed it entirely if his hand hadn't caught the hairline seam—a metal document case, olive-drab, military surplus, its latch crusted but intact.

His heart began doing something he didn't authorize.

"Tashi."

She came. Together they worked the latch free, and the lid came up with a sigh of trapped air that smelled, impossibly, of paper and machine oil and another decade.

Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas, lay books.

Not one book. Seven of them. Waterproof stock, the pages slick and yellowed, bound in oilcloth covers stamped with the syndicate's wheel-and-mountain insignia. Jonah lifted the top one and opened it, and the world tilted slightly on its axis.

Columns. Dates. Names.

*Accident Report — Reconciliation Ledger,* the header read. *Quarter 3.*

He turned pages with hands that had crossed flooded rivers without trembling and now would not stay still. The ledger cross-referenced incident reports against internal memos, against payment vouchers, against a rotating set of initials that appeared wherever the numbers changed. And there—in an entry dated the spring of the year everything ended—was a name he knew better than his own heartbeat.

*J. BLACKWOOD — GUIDE OF RECORD.*

Beneath it, in a clerk's careful hand: *Report amended per V. Original filed. Original retained.*

Per V.

Voss.

Jonah sat down on the shelving without deciding to. The tin was cold through his trousers. He read the entry again, then the next, then the next, following the thread backward through the quarters like a man following a rope down into a well. His blacklisting had not begun with the coroner's inquiry, as he'd believed for six years. It had begun here, months earlier, in this depot, in these columns. The rockfall that killed three porters on the lower Kalyani road—an accident he'd been blamed for assessing falsely, the error that stripped him of his certification, his name, his right to stand on any mountain in two countries—that rockfall had been *amended*. The original report, his report, the one that cited overloaded trucks and a haulage schedule no sane guide would have signed, was marked *retained*. Kept. Filed away in a waterproof box in a drowned building because someone named V. had understood, even then, that paper was the only thing in these mountains more dangerous than the river.

"They kept the originals," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened, as though someone else were speaking through him. "All these years. They kept the evidence of what they did."

"Why?" Tashi asked. But he could see she'd answered herself; her eyes had gone distant, calculating. "Insurance," she said. "In case one of them needed leverage against another. Men like that don't trust each other. They just share crimes."

Jonah turned another page and stopped breathing.

A memo, folded into the binding, typed on syndicate letterhead. *Re: D. Blackwood — field surveyor. Subject continues to press for publication of haulage figures. Recommend severance of contract and standard containment of associated parties. The son is a guide of record on the affected route. See attached.*

Attached was a list. On the list was his name, his certification number, and beside it, in the margin, a single handwritten word:

*Managed.*

Six years. Six years of eating other men's leftovers in Serem, of answering to a false name, of flinching whenever a well-dressed stranger looked at him twice—and it had never been failure. It had been policy. His ruin was not a consequence of his mistakes; it was a *deliverable*. Somewhere in a glass office in the capital, a man had ticked a box, and Jonah Blackwood had ceased to exist, and the reason was that his father had loved numbers more than lies.

The anger came up through him slowly, which surprised him. He'd expected it to arrive like the flood—sudden, total, sweeping. Instead it rose like groundwater, filling every space in him, silent and complete, until he realized he was gripping the ledger hard enough to crease the waterproof pages and Tashi's hand was on his wrist.

"Jonah."

"They wrote me down," he said. "Like a line item. *Managed.*"

"Yes." She didn't soften it. She never softened anything anymore, and he understood that this, too, was a kind of justice—she refused to let him be the only wounded party in the room. "Now you know how it feels to be a column in their book. We've known it our whole lives."

He looked at her. Her face was thin from the trail, wind-scoured, and there was mud dried along her jaw, and she was sixteen years old and steadier than any adult he had ever guided. Something in his chest cracked—not painfully. More like ice going out of a river in spring, the first groan before the movement.

"You're right," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm not—this isn't only about me." He lifted the ledger. "But Tashi—look at it. Look at what's in here. The falsified reports. The payments. The evacuation orders dated *before* the 'accident' that justified them. This isn't just my name they buried. It's your village. It's the whole valley. This book is the crime scene. Not the mountain. Not the river. *This.*"

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she reached into the case and drew out the second ledger, and the third, turning pages with the same terrible care she'd used on the workbench, and Jonah watched her find her own history in the columns—the dates of the soldiers' arrival, the requisition for the burning of records, a voucher authorizing compensation of four hundred rupees per household, *disbursement incomplete*.

"My grandmother signed nothing," Tashi said quietly. "They wrote *refused* next to her name. And then they wrote *resolved* next to it anyway." She closed the book with a snap that echoed off the tin roof. "Take them. Take all of them. We're not leaving these for the water."

* * *

They wrapped the ledgers in the waxed canvas and then in Jonah's spare shirt and then in the oilskin that had protected the map, layering them the way you'd wrap a climber with hypothermia, and Jonah carried the bundle against his chest in the pack frame, where he could feel its weight with every step. It weighed less than a coil of rope. It weighed more than the mountain.

They climbed out of the hollow as the river took the last of the yard, water sliding through the doorway they'd entered by, closing over the calendar, over the smiling engineer and his thumbs-up, over the weighbridge where Tashi's father had carried loads in the years when the valley still belonged to itself. Jonah stood on the slate shelf and looked back once. The depot's roof broke the surface like a drowned animal's spine, and then the rain thickened and it was gone.

They walked upstream through the growing storm, and Jonah's mind moved the way water moves—finding every channel, cutting deeper everywhere it passed. If the ledgers existed, copies might exist. If Voss had come all this way hunting a map, Voss knew—or feared—what else might have survived the burning. *Standard containment of associated parties.* Voss had contained him for six years without ever needing to show his face. Which meant Voss had been young when it started, or ambitious, or both. Which meant the initials in the margin—*per V.*, cool as a signature on a cheque—belonged to the same man who was somewhere behind them in this rain, climbing toward the source with hired trackers and a syndicate's patience.

"Tashi." He raised his voice over the rain. "When we reach the source—if we reach it—what do we do with these? Courts are downstream. Courts are *their* country. Every judge between here and the capital has shaken hands with men whose initials are in these books."

She walked three paces in silence, head down against the weather, and when she answered she didn't turn around.

"Then we don't take them to courts," she said. "Dorje didn't send you downstream, did he? He sent you up. Everything that matters in this valley goes up." She pointed her chin toward the white blur of the high country, where the river was born. "There are people below the source. People who came back when the patrols thinned. Elders. My mother's people. Let them read it first. Let them decide. Not lawyers. Not soldiers. Them."

Jonah thought about it the rest of the afternoon, through the rain and the scree and the long grinding traverse above the swollen river. Every practical instinct said she was wrong—evidence needed institutions, institutions needed procedure, procedure needed the low country and its paper palaces. But every practical instinct he owned had been trained by the very system these books exposed, and he was done trusting it. Dorje had understood that. It had taken the old man a lifetime and a death to understand it, but he had understood: some truths can't be delivered. They can only be *returned*—carried home and given back to the people they were stolen from.

That night they sheltered under an overhang above the tree line, and Jonah took out the ledgers and read by candle stub while Tashi slept, or pretended to. Page after page. Name after name. And near midnight, wedged in the back of the final volume, he found a single loose sheet in a hand he now knew intimately—Dorje's hand, younger, harder, the letters struck rather than written.

It was a list of the ledgers themselves. Seven volumes. Locations. And beneath the seventh entry, a line that stopped Jonah's breath:

*If I die before delivery, my son will finish it. He always finishes what he carries.*

Jonah sat in the dark with the candle guttering, and the rain hammered the mountain above them, and far downstream—days downstream, but coming, the way the monsoon comes—he imagined he could hear the sound of a man in grey gloves, climbing.
