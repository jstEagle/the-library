## 14. Monsoon Landfall

The rain came in from the south like an army that had been marching for weeks and was tired of waiting.

Voss stood under the corrugated awning of the Kalpa Bend checkpoint—abandoned now, its tin roof singing under the first real assault of the monsoon—and watched the river he had failed to cross three days ago. The Kalyani had doubled in width overnight. Where the suspension bridge's anchor posts still jutted from the far bank, brown water boiled and folded over itself, carrying whole trees downstream with a patience that looked almost deliberate. Somewhere up there, past the gorge, past the ghost villages, a dead man's map was climbing toward the source in the hands of a guide who should have stayed buried.

He flexed his fingers inside his grey gloves. The left one had stiffened since the bridge—the cold water, the long walk back downriver to the ford at Serem, the humiliation of arriving wet and empty-handed at his own rendezvous. He did not think of it as humiliation. He thought of it as arithmetic. Three days lost. Two hundred millimeters of rain forecast in the next forty-eight. One man ahead of him on a trail that would soon be underwater.

Behind him, the trackers were packing in silence. He had hired them out of Namche with cash and a story about recovering stolen company property, which was true in the way that most useful lies are true. There were four of them: two brothers from the Tamang side of the range who moved over wet rock like it was dry, a heavy silent man called Pemba who carried more weight than any two porters Voss had ever employed, and a young one, Dawa, who had not yet learned to keep his eyes off Voss's gloves.

"You'll want to leave the rope coils," Voss said, without turning around. "Weight is time. Time is everything now."

Pemba said something low in Tamang. The brothers laughed—not loudly, but enough. Voss let it go. Men could laugh at him all they liked as long as they walked where he pointed. Laughter was cheap. Obedience was the currency that mattered, and he paid for it in certainty, which he had never once run short of.

Except once.

* * *

Eleven years ago, in an office in Serem that smelled of ink and damp paper, Voss had written a report that killed a man's career without touching his body.

He remembered the desk. That was the strange thing, the thing that surfaced now, at odd hours, like a body rising in floodwater: not the man's face, not the hearing, but the desk—a green baize surface worn pale in a crescent where some clerk before him had rested his forearm for thirty years. Voss's own report lay on it in draft, four pages, typed except for the corrections, which were his. *Guide error. Rope anchors inspected post-incident and found unserviceable. Recommend permanent removal from regional register.*

All of it false. Every line.

The accident itself had been real enough—two porters dead below the icefall, a client maimed—but the cause had been what Dorje had tried to tell the inquiry it was: a syndicate survey team working the moraine above the route without permits, dislodging the serac that came down on them. Dorje had evidence. Photographs. A supply manifest with the company's own stamp on it, dated the day of the collapse. And so the company had needed a counterweight, and Voss—young then, hungry, already good at being the instrument other men pointed—had been given the assignment the way you hand a man a tool: here, this is what the report will say, make it clean.

He had made it clean. He had even been proud of it, in the way of craftsmen. Four pages, no adjectives, no anger, nothing that could be called a lie if you squinted—only omissions arranged with the precision of load-bearing stones. The register board had read it for eleven minutes. Jonah Blackwood's name had come off the rolls by the end of the week, and Dorje had stopped writing letters to his son, and the valley upstream had been sealed the following spring under an emergency conservation order that Voss himself had drafted the annexes for.

He had told himself, then, that no one died of a report. People died of seracs, of rivers, of bad luck. Paper only decided who got to keep working afterward.

It had taken him years to understand that this was the whole trick—that the men above him had never needed killers because they had clerks, and clerks were better, because a killing ends a life while a report rewrites it, and rewritten lives go on suffering in full view of everyone, unavenged, unbelieved.

Now the son of the man he had ruined was climbing toward the source with a ledger's worth of truth in a waterproof roll, and Voss had been sent to make sure it never arrived.

*Sent*, he reminded himself, as the rain hammered the tin roof. Not sent—drawn. There was a difference, and the difference was the only dignity he had left. No one had ordered him personally. He had volunteered. When the telex came through about the old prospector dying in Serem with company property on him, Voss had read it twice and felt something move in his chest that he refused, even alone, to call fear. It was more like recognition. The past does not chase you, he had learned. It simply waits upstream, and sooner or later you have to climb toward it.

"Boss." Dawa, beside him, pointing downvalley. "Riders."

Two of them, coming up the flooded track from Serem, ponies steaming. Voss watched them approach with the flat attention he gave everything now. They were the syndicate's own men—company ponies, company rain capes—and the lead rider dismounted and handed him a oilskin packet without a word.

Inside: a photograph, grainy, taken from the teahouse across the yard. A tall man in a porter's jacket, face half-turned, jaw set. And beneath it, in the director's handwriting, four words:

*No courtrooms. No bodies.*

Voss read it twice and put it away. No bodies meant no witnesses either, eventually, and it meant the trackers he'd hired were temporary arrangements. He had assumed as much. It changed nothing about tonight. It only clarified the shape of the country he was moving through—one in which every man walking with him was both instrument and liability, including himself.

"We move at first light," he said. "Before the river comes up again."

Pemba looked at the sky, then at Voss, and said, in careful trade-tongue: "In this rain, the gorge paths are gone. The goat trails. Everything above the second gorge floods."

"I know what floods," Voss said. "I wrote the hydrology annex."

That silenced them, which was not the same as convincing them. He turned back to the river and let them mutter.

* * *

That night, in the checkpoint's one intact room, Voss sat with a candle and the map he had reconstructed from the teahouse keeper's testimony, and he drew the route he believed Blackwood would take. It was good work, and he knew it was good work, because he had spent eleven years learning how this valley lied. The maps the government published showed trails that had been washed away in '09. The maps the syndicate kept internal showed the real ones, and Voss had memorized those, because drafting the closure order had required knowing exactly what you were closing.

Which meant he knew something the boy probably didn't: there was exactly one viable crossing above Kalpa Bend once the monsoon landed, a granite shelf above the third gorge where the river split around a midstream island, and it would be passable for perhaps six hours between storms. If Blackwood took it, Voss would take it behind him. If Blackwood missed it, the river would do the work, and Voss would only need to find the body—or not find it, which served just as well.

He should have felt satisfaction. Instead he felt the candle's heat on his face and heard, beneath the rain, the sound of that office again: the tick of the wall clock, the scratch of his own pen making the world smaller.

There had been a moment—he had never told anyone, had barely admitted it to himself—when the draft was finished and he had read it through one last time before signing, and his pen had hovered over the recommendation line. *Permanent removal.* He had thought: this is a man's whole life, and I am holding the eraser. He had thought: I could write *suspension pending review* instead, and no one above him would ever check, because no one above him read anything carefully; that was the whole architecture of the place, truth surviving only in the gaps of other people's laziness.

Then he had remembered the bonus attached to the closure file, and the flat in Serem he wanted, and the way his superior had clapped him on the shoulder and said *you're the kind of man this company is built on*—and he had written *permanent*, and signed, and gone out into the evening feeling light, feeling promoted, feeling nothing at all, which was the feeling he had spent eleven years mistaking for peace.

The candle guttered. Outside, the rain found a new register, deeper, as if the sky had shifted its whole weight onto this one valley.

Dawa appeared in the doorway, dripping. "The brothers say the storm is a bad one. They say the mountain is angry this year. They want double pay to go above the second gorge."

"Give it to them," Voss said. "From my share."

Dawa stared. Voss did not explain. It was not generosity. It was inventory management: he was spending money he might not live to spend, on men whose fear was the only honest thing in this operation, and honesty was worth its price. Besides, there was a colder reason, one he kept below the waterline of his own thinking: if the trackers quit, he would go on alone, and some part of him—the part that had hovered the pen over that page eleven years ago—wanted to know whether he would.

* * *

They left at grey dawn, into rain that fell not in drops but in sheets, in walls, in a continuous drowning descent that turned the world to a single colour. Voss led. The trackers followed. The track along the river vanished within the hour, and after that they climbed on goat paths that existed mostly as suggestions—here a hoof-worn ledge, there a root system holding a shelf of soil together out of habit—and Voss moved over them with a sureness that surprised even himself. His body remembered this country. His body had surveyed half of it, years ago, before the sealing, when he had walked the valley with a theodolite and a notebook, measuring a place in order to help erase it.

He had liked it then. That was the worst of it. He had stood on the moraine above the future reservoir site and looked at the terraced fields and the prayer flags snapping in the wind and the children running the irrigation channels, and he had felt the ordinary pleasure of a professional in beautiful country, and none of it—not one field, not one flag—had seemed real to him as anything but data. The people had been numbers in a resettlement table. He had written the table too.

Now, wading a side-stream that had become a brown torrent, hauling himself up a slope that poured water, he passed a stone cairn at the path's bend, waist-high, flags long rotted off it, and he knew without asking what it was. Memorial. Someone had died here, or near here, or had lived here and been taken elsewhere, and someone who loved them had stacked these stones one on another because stacking stones was the only sentence grief could write that weather couldn't erase.

He did not stop. But he counted it. By nightfall he had counted nine.

* * *

On the third day of the storm, at the mouth of the second gorge, they found the sign.

One of the brothers—Tenzin, the sharper-eyed—whistled twice from a ledge above, and Voss climbed to him and looked where he pointed: a patch of gravel beside a boulder, sheltered from the worst of the rain, bearing boot prints. Two sets. A large man's, a small one's—small and light, a child's or a woman's, quick strides.

"They camped here," Tenzin said. "Night before last. Before the big water."

Voss crouched. The prints were deep at the toe, shallow at the heel: a loaded man, climbing hard. Beside them, pressed into the mud at the boulder's base, a single mark he recognized with a jolt that went through him like cold water finding a seam—a boot heel with a cracked lateral edge, the print slightly rolled outward.

He had seen that print before. Eleven years ago, on the moraine below the icefall, in the mud of the accident site, photographed forty times for the inquiry. Guide's boots, well-worn, right heel cracked. He had described them in his report—*the guide's footwear showed wear inconsistent with professional standards*—one more small true thing deployed in service of one great false thing.

Jonah Blackwood was still wearing the same boots. Or boots exactly like them. Either way, the man was two days ahead, climbing with a child, straight up the throat of the gorge, into country that was trying to drown them both.

Voss stood. The rain ran off his hat brim in a solid curtain. Below him the Kalyani roared through the gorge mouth with a sound like sustained thunder, and somewhere above, faint through the noise, he thought he heard—no. Nothing. Rockfall, maybe. Or wind. But he stood listening a moment longer than the trackers understood, listening the way a man listens outside a door behind which his own name is being spoken.

"Boss?" Dawa.

"He'll take the granite shelf at the third gorge," Voss said. "When the river drops between storms. We'll be there first." He turned downvalley of the ledge, back the way they'd come, and corrected himself. "Above it. We'll be above it, waiting."

Pemba exchanged a look with the brothers. Voss saw it and named it for what it was: the look men give a leader who has stopped calculating and started wanting. He had seen it before, on other expeditions, in other men's faces aimed at other men, and he had always despised it as weakness.

Now he wore it. He could feel it on his own face like the rain.

Because here was the thing he had not written in any report, to anyone, ever: he did not want the map destroyed anymore. The map was paper; paper was nothing; he had spent his life proving how little paper resisted. What he wanted—what had pulled him up this drowned valley day after day, past the cairns, through the roaring water, with hired men watching him come apart at the seams—was to stand in front of Jonah Blackwood and see the son of the man whose life he had erased, and to learn whether the looking would kill him or finally, finally finish something.

*No courtrooms,* the director had written. *No bodies.*

"There's a storm coming behind this one," Voss said to the trackers, and heard his own voice come out level, professional, the voice of a man who writes reports. "We move now, while the river's high. Nobody crosses water ahead of us. Nobody waits for us."

They shouldered their loads. The rain thickened until the gorge walls dissolved and the world was grey water falling forever, and Voss climbed into it, upward, toward the source of the river and the source of everything else, counting cairns.
