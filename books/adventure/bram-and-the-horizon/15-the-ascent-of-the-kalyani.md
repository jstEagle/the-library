## 15. The Ascent of the Kalyani

The rain had stopped sometime in the night, but the river hadn't noticed.

Jonah stood at the base of the gorge where the Kalyani dropped out of the high country in a series of falls that no map he'd ever seen had bothered to name—because no mapmaker was supposed to be here, and the people who lived here named things for use, not for sale. In monsoon season the falls were not waterfalls at all. They were vertical rivers, columns of white violence taller than any building Jonah had ever guided a client past, and the spray rose off them like breath off a living thing, soaking the rock walls fifty meters to either side until every handhold wept.

Above the first fall, the gorge narrowed to a slot barely wider than a man's shoulders. Above the second, according to Tashi, there was a route—a goat path her grandfather had shown her, cut into the face itself, traversing behind the falling water on ledges no wider than a boot.

"You're sure," Jonah said. It wasn't a question and they both knew it wasn't.

Tashi didn't answer right away. She stood with her weight on her good leg, the other still stiff from the cold river days ago, and looked up at the column of water the way she looked at everything now—measuring it, testing it against what she remembered of before. She'd been doing that more since Kalpa Bend. Since the bridge ropes sang and parted under her knife and Voss's figure shrank on the far bank into something small enough to hate without fear.

"My grandmother took goats up this way when I was small," she said finally. "Before. She said the mountain keeps one road for those who have nothing left to lose." She glanced at him sideways, and there was the old edge in it, but underneath the edge something else had been growing since the cave, since the letters, since she'd learned whose blood ran in his veins and decided—without ever saying so—that she would stay anyway. "I used to think she meant it as a warning."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's an invitation."

* * *

They climbed out of the gorge floor before dawn, while the rock was still cold and the falls were at their lowest flow—which was to say, still thunderous, still lethal, but a fraction less than they would be by noon when the sun hit the glaciers above and melted another day's worth of mountain into the channel. Jonah went first through the lower section because his hands knew this work even if his name didn't: the blacklisting had taken his permits, his reputation, his place in the registers, but it had never found a way to take the ten thousand hours of stone and rope and ice that lived in his body. That was the bitter joke of it. The syndicate had made him unemployable and in doing so had made him exactly the kind of climber willing to attempt a route no employed guide would touch.

He set the pitch above the second fall himself, a rising traverse across wet schist with the whole roaring weight of the Kalyani at his back, its spray hitting him like thrown gravel. He placed gear where gear could be placed and prayed where it couldn't, which on this rock was roughly a fifty-fifty arrangement. Twice his feet skated on algae-slick stone and twice the rope came tight and held, and both times he heard Tashi below him at the stance, calling the beta she could see from beneath—"crack left! No, your *other* left!"—in a voice pitched to carry over the water, sharp and certain and entirely unafraid of him.

That was new. That was the thing he kept noticing. Everyone else in his life had watched him with either hope or suspicion—the clients hoping he'd get them down alive, the syndicate's men suspecting he'd steal from them, the officials in Serem suspecting everything. Tashi watched him the way you watch weather. Without flattery, without doubt. If he fell, she would be sorry, and then she would keep climbing.

It was the most restful thing anyone had ever done for him.

They reached the ledge behind the falls in mid-morning, and it was worse and better than she'd promised. Worse because the water came down the rock face beside them in a solid green-grey sheet close enough to touch, and the wind it drove ahead of itself tried constantly to peel them off the wall. Better because the ledge was real—continuous, sloped just enough to drain, worn smooth in places by decades or centuries of hooves and feet. Her grandmother's road. The mountain's one road for those with nothing left to lose.

Halfway along it, Tashi stopped and put her palm flat against the rock, over a cluster of small drilled holes plugged with wooden pegs gone black with age.

"Look," she said.

He looked. Below the pegs, carved shallow and nearly erased by lichen, was a line of characters in the valley script—the script he'd seen on the memorial cairns in Dorje's marginal sketches, the script the burned records would have carried. He couldn't read it. But Tashi could, and she read it aloud once, quietly, and then translated, though he hadn't asked her to:

"'The water remembers what the papers forget.'"

She said it without ceremony, the way you'd read a signpost. Then she kept walking. But Jonah stayed a moment longer with his hand near the carved words, feeling the vibration of the fall come up through the stone into his bones, and thought about Dorje sitting somewhere with a pen, drafting letters to a son he couldn't send them to, writing *the water remembers* in margins instead of saying it out loud to anyone. A whole life of confession compressed into map notations. His father had been talking to him for years. He just hadn't known the language.

"Blackwood." Tashi's voice, ahead, flat with urgency. "Come see where the road ends."

* * *

The road ended where the ledge simply stopped, sheared away by some ancient collapse, leaving a gap of perhaps four meters above a drop that went down into the white throat of the falls themselves. Beyond the gap the ledge resumed, and beyond that it climbed toward the notch Tashi called the goat gate—the way through to the upper valley, to the source, to whatever waited there that Dorje had spent his last feverish days pointing Jonah toward.

Jonah studied the gap for a long time. There were no holds across it that he trusted. The rock on the far side overhung slightly. The spray here was constant, blinding, and the roar made thought difficult.

"We go around," he said. "There has to be—"

"There isn't. I've been thinking about it all morning." Tashi had already uncoiled the rope from her shoulder. She began tying a loop with quick, economical movements, the knots of someone raised around pack animals and steep ground rather than guide schools. "My grandmother's story. When I was eight she told me how they got the goats across here. You throw a line across, weighted. Someone crosses on the line. Then you haul the loads."

"Someone crosses on the line." He looked at the gap, at the drop, at the girl who was fifteen years old and had already lost a village, a family's graves, and a history to the machinery that had also lost him his name. "Across that."

"You're lighter than me," she said, which was true, and which was also not the reason she'd decided, and they both knew that too. The reason was written in the set of her jaw. She had spent the whole journey watching him almost drown for a piece of paper; she was not going to let him volunteer for the crossing and pretend it was arithmetic.

"Tashi."

"It's my road," she said. "My grandmother's. My village's. You're carrying their ledger and their truth, but you're still a guest on this mountain, Jonah Blackwood, and guests don't get to do the dangerous part while the family watches." She held out the looped rope. "Throw it. I'll cross."

For a moment neither of them moved, and the falls filled the silence with their argument. And Jonah understood, standing there, that this was the thing he'd been climbing toward as much as the source—not the glacial spring, not the cairns, but this: a moment where the debt between them, the whole tangled inheritance of what his father's company had done to her people and what her people's knowledge was doing to save his life, could become something else. Not debt. Not guilt repaid in risk. Partnership. Two people on a mountain, each holding half the rope.

So he threw it.

The weighted end arced through the spray and caught on the far side on the second try—he'd weighted it with a carabiner wrapped in sling, and it snagged behind a horn of rock and held when he tested it with his full weight, and then tested it again, and then a third time because some habits are older than shame. Tashi clipped in, backed to the lip, and looked at him once.

"If I fall," she said, "don't you dare let go of that rope."

"I won't."

"I mean it. Whatever happens. Whatever you see. You hold the rope and you finish the climb and you deliver the ledger, and you don't you dare turn into a man who stands at gaps looking guilty." And she stepped off the edge before he could answer, and crossed.

She crossed fast and clean, hand over hand, boots walking the sagging line, body angled into the spray, and the whole time the falls tried to pluck her off like a leaf and the whole time she moved as if she'd done it a hundred times, as if her grandmother's arms were around her, and when her feet hit the far ledge she turned and shouted something at him through the roar that he couldn't hear and didn't need to. He hauled the loads across—both packs, the waterproof roll with the ledger inside it—and then he crossed last, on a line a teenage goat-herder had rigged, and pulled the rope through after him, and cut it, because there was no going back this way regardless and a rope left hanging was a gift to whoever followed.

Voss, if Voss was still coming. Jonah tried not to think about the far side of the gap, the long detour the enforcer would have to find or fail to find, the storm-swollen river between. He failed a little. But less than he would have a month ago.

* * *

Above the goat gate the character of the mountain changed. The gorge walls fell away and the country opened into a hanging valley of grey scree and old snow, and the Kalyani, diminished up here to a stream you could step across, ran bright and cold through grass the color of jade. After days in the roar of the lower river the silence was enormous. Jonah's ears rang with the absence of sound.

They made camp early, in the lee of a boulder split by frost, and Tashi cooked tsampa paste on the small stove while Jonah studied the map one more time by the last light. Dorje's ink lines converged here, above the goat gate, on a fold of the valley marked with a symbol Jonah had learned to read as well as any word: a circle of small stones. Cairns. The map ended at the cairns. Everything after that, Dorje had left blank—as if the paper itself understood that some destinations are not places.

"Eat," Tashi said, and pushed the bowl at him.

He ate. They sat shoulder to shoulder against the boulder while the temperature fell and the first stars came out over the ridge lines, hard and close. Somewhere below and behind them, days of storm-country away, a man with grey gloves was finding his own route or losing it. Ahead, a morning's walk up, the water began.

"Jonah." She said his name rarely, and always deliberately, as if re-issuing a decision each time. "Tomorrow. When we get there. What happens?"

He turned the question over honestly, because she deserved honesty and had paid for it in rope-lengths. "I don't know. Dorje wanted it delivered here, not to a court. I think—I think we read it. All of it. Every name. Out loud." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "And then it belongs to whoever comes back for it. Not me. Not the law. Them."

"The elders will want to know why you brought it and not him."

"He died trying."

"I know." She poked the coals. Sparks rose straight up in the windless air. "I'm not asking what they'll say. I'm asking what *you'll* say. When they ask who you are."

Jonah looked at his hands, scarred and rope-burned and steady, and thought about the school register in the ghost village with *J. Blackwood* crossed out in a stranger's ink, and the letters addressed to *my son*, and the false papers in his pocket bearing a name that had never fit him anyway. For years he had answered questions about himself with silence, and silence had felt like safety and been a kind of drowning.

"I'll say I'm the son of the man who helped break this valley," he said, "and that I carried his confession home because he couldn't. And then I'll shut up and listen."

Tashi considered this for a long moment, firelight moving on her face. Then she nodded, once, the smallest nod, and lay back against the boulder, and said, "Good. Sleep. Tomorrow we climb the last of it, and the glacier doesn't wait for speeches."

But Jonah lay awake a while longer, listening to the stream that would become the river that would become the falls, and above it all, faint and rhythmic, a sound he couldn't place at first—until he realized it was coming from the high snowfields, a deep shifting groan, the sound of a glacier calving in the warm night air. The mountain was moving. The source was not still.

And with the sound, fainter, carried down on the wind from the pass they would cross at first light, came something else: a thin thread of woodsmoke, where no smoke should be.

Someone was camped above them.
