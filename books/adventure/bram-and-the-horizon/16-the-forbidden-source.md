## 16. The Forbidden Source

The last day of the climb began in the blue hour before dawn, when the storm finally loosened its grip and the mountain showed them what it had been hiding all along.

Jonah woke with his cheek against wet stone and his hands so cold they had stopped registering as his own. The cave mouth framed a sky scrubbed clean by three days of rain, and above it—impossibly close, impossibly white—the peak stood against the fading stars like something that had been waiting for them to look up.

"Tashi." His voice came out as gravel. "Tashi, wake up."

She was already awake. She was always already awake now, as if sleep had become something she did out of courtesy rather than need. She sat at the edge of the cave with her knees drawn up, watching the light come into the world, and when she turned to him her face held none of the wonder he felt. Only attention. Only the fierce, practical attention of someone who had grown up under this mountain and knew it was not a postcard.

"It's clear," she said. "We go today."

They went.

* * *

The route above the last camp was not on Dorje's map. Tashi had told him this two nights ago, in the flat voice she used for facts that hurt: *the map ends where my grandmother's stories begin.* What remained was a scramble through a hanging valley of scree and old ice, then a wall of rock split by a chimney that no sane guide would have attempted in any season, let alone one still shaking off a monsoon's first fury. Water ran down it in threads, silvering the stone, finding every crack Jonah's fingers needed.

He led. It was the first time since Kalpa Bend that he had led, and the strangeness of it moved through him like warmth returning to frozen limbs. For days Tashi had taken them over ground she knew from stories and instinct—ground that existed nowhere on paper—and he had followed, humbled, learning to read the mountain through her instead of through instruments and habit. But here the mountain spoke his language again. Edges and friction. Weight distributed. The old arithmetic of the body that had once made him famous and then, when the syndicate finished with him, made him a liar in every register book from Serem to the coast.

His ruin lived in his hands too, he thought, jamming his fingers into a crack slick with meltwater. They remembered everything. That was the thing no one warned you about—that disgrace didn't unlearn itself. You could hide in a border town under a false name for years, portering rice and kerosene for men who would never know your name because you gave them another one, and still your hands stayed honest. Still they knew how to move on stone.

"Left," Tashi called from below, her voice thin in the thin air. "There's a flake—it moves. Go right of the stain."

He went right of the stain.

Above the chimney the angle eased, and then eased again, and then there was no more climbing at all—only walking, which after four days of vertical work felt like a kind of hallucination. They crossed a plateau of grey moraine where the storm's wreckage lay strewn: branches torn from stunted birches far below, carried up and deposited like offerings; a sheet of blue plastic, half-buried; the small bleached ribcage of an animal no larger than a hare. The air smelled of stone and snowmelt and something green and improbable, and Jonah's breath came in long ragged pulls that hurt in a way he had missed without knowing he'd missed it.

Tashi caught up to him on the flat and walked beside him, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the ground wasn't already saying better.

Then the plateau tilted, and rose, and ended—

—and the source was there.

* * *

It was smaller than anything deserved to be that had fed a river the length of the Kalyani. A spring, rising from beneath a shelf of glacier-scoured rock: a pool perhaps four paces across, so clear that the eye fell straight through it to the pale stones of its floor, so still that the sky lay in it entire, doubled and perfect. From its lower edge the water spilled over a lip of rock no higher than Jonah's knee and began, in a thread no wider than his wrist, the long journey downstream—through gorges and drowned valleys and ghost villages and the border town where a man named Jonah Blackwood had spent three years trying to stop existing—all the way to the plains, to the sea, to everywhere.

The glacier hung above it, blue-veined and ancient, dripping its slow metronome into the pool. And around the pool, ringing it in a broken circle, were the cairns.

Dozens of them. Hundreds.

Small ones, knee-high, built of flat river stones stacked with care. Larger ones, waist-high, crowned with slabs. Some were ancient, their stones furred with lichen the color of tarnished bronze; some were new enough that the mortar of mud between their rocks had not yet washed away. Each one faced the water. Each one had been built by hands that had come here—or sent something of themselves here—to mark a place that could not be visited, in a valley that had been sealed, by people who had been told to forget.

"Oh," Tashi said. Just that. One syllable, and then her hand went to her mouth, and Jonah saw that she was crying without any of the noise or drama of crying, the tears simply falling, as if her face had decided this on its own.

"What is it?" he asked, though he knew. He had known since the school register in the ghost village, since the name crossed out in faded ink. But knowing a thing and standing inside it were different countries.

"They're ours," she said. "The cairns. My mother told me—when they evacuated us, when they burned the records—she said the old people walked up here first. Before the soldiers came. Before all of it. They climbed to the source and they built these, one for each family, so that even if the valley was taken, the water would remember where everyone had lived." Her voice cracked and steadied. "I thought they were stories. I thought she was telling me stories to make me sleep."

Jonah looked at the cairns and understood what he was looking at: a census written in stone. An archive the syndicate hadn't known to burn. Every family displaced from the valley below—every household erased from the official record, scattered to resettlement camps and border towns and silence—had left its signature here, at the headwaters, where no patrol bothered to climb and no ledger thought to look.

And Dorje had known.

* * *

Jonah took off his pack with hands that shook, and not from the cold.

The map had ridden against his back for weeks now, wrapped in oilcloth, sweated over, slept beside, nearly drowned for. He unwrapped it at the pool's edge, kneeling on the wet stone, and spread it open in the full light—and for the first time he saw it whole, because for the first time he was standing in the thing it described.

Dorje had drawn the valley from memory, and the memory was exact. Every bend of the gorge. Every crossing. The ghost village rendered in fine, loving strokes, each roofline labeled in a script that had grown shakier toward the margins. And here, at the top of the page, above the final contour lines, a small circle inked in blue, and beside it words Jonah had read a dozen times without understanding:

*Where the water begins, give it back.*

He had thought it meant the map. Carry it upstream, deliver it somewhere, hand it to someone—authorities, journalists, lawyers, anyone with the power to make the truth bite. He had spent the whole climb assuming the destination was a person. A court. A newspaper office in some city where men in clean shirts could turn paper into justice.

But there was no one here. There was only the water, and the cairns, and the mountain holding its breath.

"He didn't want it filed," Jonah said slowly, hearing the understanding assemble itself aloud. "He didn't want it won. He wanted it—" He stopped. Started again. "The confession wasn't addressed to the law, Tashi. It was addressed to here. To this. He wanted the truth brought home to the people it was stolen from, at the place they'd marked so they wouldn't be forgotten. Not evidence. A returning."

Tashi knelt beside him. She reached out and touched one of the marginal letters—the drafts addressed to *my son*, the pages Jonah had read by lamplight in the cave while fury and grief fought each other in his chest—and her fingers rested there a moment before withdrawing.

"You said he whispered a name," she said. "When he died. You almost recognized it."

"My mother's name," Jonah said. It still cost him something to say it here, in this thin holy air. "Or half of it. I told myself I imagined it. I told myself a lot of things about him." He looked out over the ring of cairns, at the lichen and the new mud and the patient stones. "He worked for them. He helped seal this valley. And then he spent—I don't know how many years—drawing this map, writing those letters, carrying all of it toward the one place he knew it would mean something. And when he couldn't carry it anymore, he gave it to a stranger in a teahouse. To me. Without telling me. Because if he'd told me, I'd have said no."

"Would you?"

Jonah considered the question honestly, the way the altitude forced you to consider everything honestly, stripped of the usual padding. Three months ago—a lifetime ago—he would have said yes without hesitation. He had been a man whose whole discipline was refusal: refusing hope, refusing attachment, refusing the past. He had rescued the map from the river before himself and told himself it was about survival, about leverage, about the ledger's promise of clearing his name. Even after the ledger, even after reading the falsified reports that had manufactured his disgrace to silence his father, he had clung to the idea that this journey ended in his vindication. That the story was about him.

But standing here, at the circle's center, with the spring murmuring its first syllables of the long journey down, he found the old answer had quietly died somewhere on the trail, and he couldn't remember exactly when.

"No," he said. "Not anymore. Maybe not even then. Maybe I just needed to pretend the debt was mine so I'd have a reason to keep going." He folded the map along its worn creases, gently, the way you handle something that has outlived its maker. "He chose this place over a courtroom. Over me, maybe. And I think—" His throat closed. He waited it out. "I think he was right. Courts can be bought. He watched them get bought once already. But this—" he gestured at the cairns, at the water, at the girl kneeling beside him who had lost a village and gained, somewhere between Kalpa Bend and here, an unwilling partner— "this can't be bought. This just has to be told."

Below them, far below, beyond the shoulder of the ridge, there was a sound. Faint. Almost nothing. But Jonah's ears had been trained by twenty years of mountains to catch the sounds that didn't belong, and this one didn't belong: the knock of stone on stone, rhythmic, deliberate, ascending.

Someone was climbing toward the source.

Tashi heard it too. She went still, head cocked, and then her eyes found his, and in them was no fear—only a hard, bright readiness, the look of someone who had cut a bridge rope behind her and meant it.

"How long?" she whispered.

Jonah listened. Counted the intervals. Judged the rhythm of the steps against the grade of the slope.

"An hour," he said. "Maybe less, if he's got trackers with him." He looked down at the map in his hands, at the ledger wrapped safe in its oilcloth at the bottom of his pack, at the ring of stones that held the names of everyone the syndicate had tried to erase. "Voss found another way across."

* * *

They had an hour. Perhaps less. And the truth, Jonah understood with a clarity that felt almost like calm, had traveled three thousand miles and thirty years and one dying man's last request to arrive at this pool of sky-colored water—and now it had to decide, in the space of sixty minutes, whether it would reach the people it belonged to, or die here among the cairns with the man who'd carried it.

The spring went on spilling over its lip of rock, patient as stone, beginning again the journey it had begun ten thousand times before.

Jonah closed his hand around the map and stood up.
