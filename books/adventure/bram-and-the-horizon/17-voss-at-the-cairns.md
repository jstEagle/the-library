## 17. Voss at the Cairns

The storm had been trying to kill him for three days, and Voss had begun to suspect it was winning.

He came up the last pitch of the Kalyani's headwall with his hands bare because he had lost his gloves somewhere below—the grey ones, the ones he'd worn for eleven years without once thinking about them—and the rock was so cold it burned. Water ran down every surface. The mountain had stopped being stone and become a kind of falling, a vertical river wearing the disguise of a route, and he climbed through it because there was nothing left in him that knew how to stop.

He had hired four trackers in Kalpa Bend. He had two now. One had turned back at the gorge with a look of such contempt that Voss still carried it like a stone in his chest; the other, a silent Sherkoti man named Pemba, had gone into the flood two nights ago trying to hold a rope across a side-channel, and the river had taken him with the indifference of something that had never been paid to care.

So: one man, alone, climbing toward a place that did not officially exist, to retrieve a book from a guide who had already died once and apparently hadn't enjoyed it.

Voss laughed at that—heard himself laugh, a thin cracked sound torn away instantly by wind—and understood, in the detached way of a man observing his own deterioration from a slight distance, that he had crossed some line. Not the line of exhaustion. He had been exhausted before, in the early years, when the work was physical and the syndicate still pretended to be a company. This was another line entirely. The storm had stripped things off him the way it stripped soil off the slopes, and underneath, where he had always assumed there would be competence and purpose, there was instead a memory.

He climbed anyway. That was the horror of it. He knew what waited at the top and he climbed anyway, because the alternative was to be a man who had done all of it for nothing.

* * *

He had written the report himself.

That was the thing no one knew—not Blackwood, not the syndicate's board, not the courts that had blacklisted a guide on the strength of a document with Voss's own handwriting in its margins. Eleven years ago, in a prefab office at the lower depot, with rain hammering the tin roof and Dorje sitting across the desk from him looking older than his years, Voss had taken a true accident and made it false.

There had been an accident. That was the part he clung to, even now, even here. A winch cable had parted on the upper haulage; two men had died; the maintenance logs showed what they showed. The valley's displacement was supposed to be a separate matter—a land acquisition, compensation schedules, the ordinary machinery of progress. But the dead men's families were valley families, and their grief had teeth, and the teeth were aimed at the syndicate, and so the accident had to become something else. It had to become the fault of the guide who had signed off on the cable inspection. It had to become negligence, individual, nameable, prosecutable. Jonah Blackwood. A young guide with a good reputation and no powerful friends.

Dorje had sat across the desk and said, quietly, "He's my son."

And Voss—who was thirty-one years old and ambitious and already half-owned by the men above him—had said, "Then you understand how important it is that we get this right."

He remembered the exact quality of the silence after that. Not outrage. Something worse. Dorje had looked at him the way a man looks at weather—as at a force too large to argue with, only to survive or not survive—and then he had asked for paper, and begun drafting the confession the syndicate wanted the son to have written, and Voss had watched him do it, and corrected the dates.

*You understand how important it is that we get this right.*

He had thought, for eleven years, that the sentence was the worst thing about him. He had built his whole life on the assumption that the worst thing about a man is knowable, containable, a single load-bearing sin around which everything else could be organized. He had been wrong. The worst thing was that he had slept well afterward. That he had eaten dinner. That the rain on the tin roof had sounded, to him, like money.

* * *

The headwall ended the way all things end—abruptly, without ceremony—and Voss pulled himself over the lip into a world that stopped his breath.

Not the glacier. Not the spring. The cairns.

They covered the shelf like a field of standing stones, hundreds of them, thousands, small stacks of river rock and slate rising out of the snow in rows and clusters and long patient lines, each one the height of a man's knee or shin, each one placed by hands. Some were old enough to be furred with lichen. Some were new—days old, weeks—and someone had wedged scraps of cloth into their crevices, faded reds and blues gone ghost-pale, prayer flags or mourning flags or both, snapping in the wind like small insistent voices.

Beyond them, the source: a spring bursting from beneath blue ice, gathering itself into a stream no wider than a stride, which would become, in its descent, the river that watered everything he had spent his life helping to take.

And beside the largest cairn, wrapped in a tarp against the rain, sat Jonah Blackwood, watching him come.

There was a girl too—young, goat-herder's build, standing slightly in front of Blackwood with a rock in her hand and no intention whatsoever of using it as anything but a last resort. Voss catalogued her and dismissed her. His eyes went back to Blackwood and stayed there, because he had imagined this meeting for eleven years and the imagination had never once gotten the details right.

He had imagined hatred. What he saw instead was worse: recognition. Blackwood looked at him the way you look at a man whose face you've seen in a photograph you studied too long.

"Voss," Blackwood said. Not a question.

"You know me." It came out ragged. The climb had taken his voice and given him someone else's.

"I found your signature." Blackwood's hand moved, briefly, toward the tarp. "In the ledger. The accident reports. You wrote the one that has my name in it."

The wind came up the shelf and moved through the cairns, and the flags all leaned together, and for a moment the whole field of stones seemed to lean with them, the entire accumulated weight of the displaced dead tilting toward him, and Voss stood in it and did not fall down, though he could not have told anyone why not.

"The ledger," he said. "Where is it."

"Here." Blackwood didn't move. "It goes nowhere until I decide where it goes."

"You don't get to decide." Voss took a step and his legs nearly refused him; he locked them and stood swaying like something staked out. "You think this is about you? Your name? Your redemption? There are men in Serem who read a ledger like that the way a doctor reads an X-ray, and then people start disappearing—not you, you're nobody, you're a portering job with a fake name—*other* people. Villagers. Witnesses. The girl's family, if she has any. You want to carry that book downstream, fine, but understand what you're carrying."

"I do understand," Blackwood said. "I've had nine hundred meters of climbing to understand it."

"Then give it to me and go home."

"No."

The word was quiet and absolute, and it landed in Voss like a blade finding the gap in armor, because he had known it was coming—he had known since Kalpa Bend, since the gorge, since the bridge where the girl cut the ropes with a face like flint—and still hearing it undid something he had spent a decade welding shut.

* * *

What happened next, Voss would never fully be able to reconstruct. Later, lying awake in whatever bed or cell or borrowed room held him, he would try to arrange it into sequence and fail, because it had not happened in sequence. It happened all at once, the way a dam goes.

He heard himself talking. That was the only way to describe it—the sensation of speech leaving him without passing first through decision, a lifetime of containment failing at a single seam.

"I wrote it," he said. "The report. Not the falsified copy—the original. I was there. I inspected the winch housing six weeks before the cable parted, and I wrote in my own log that the wear pattern exceeded tolerance, and my supervisor told me to amend it, and I amended it, and when it parted I amended everything else too. Two men. Tsering Norbu and a boy of nineteen whose name I had to look up afterward because I'd never learned it while he was alive. And the company needed a culprit who wasn't the company, and your father sat across my desk and told me you were his son, and I used it. I used it like a tool. Because it worked."

The girl had gone very still. Blackwood had not moved at all. The rain fell on the cairns and made dark continents of the snow between them.

"He tried to fix it," Voss went on, and his voice broke on *fix*, splintering into something younger. "Did you find the letters? Of course you found the letters. Eleven years he wrote to you and sent none of them, and collected evidence instead—documents, copies, the real maintenance logs, testimony from the widows—and he hid it all in plain sight in a depot we drowned ourselves, because he knew we'd never look in our own wreckage. And I knew. I always knew he was building something. I let him, because I thought an old man's guilt was harmless, and then he walked into a teahouse and handed the whole thing to *you*, and I have spent a month chasing a dying man's conscience up a river."

"Why?" The girl spoke—Tashi, the name surfaced from Voss's files, daughter of a village called Ruksum, evacuated, records burned. Her voice was steady in a way that frightened him more than shouting would have. "Why chase it? Why not let it reach whoever it reaches?"

"Because—" And here it was, the true answer, the one he had outrun for a thousand kilometers of river, and it came out of him small and naked as a man stripped by floodwater. "Because if it reaches anyone, I am finished. And because if it burns, I am finished differently. Don't you see? There is no version where I survive this. There is only the version where I choose how."

He took another step. His boot crunched against slate, and he looked down and saw that the cairn at his feet had a stone on top that was different from the rest—smoother, whiter, carried up from somewhere far below—and scratched into its face with a knife point, crude but legible, a single name.

He knew the name. He had looked it up nineteen years ago in a payroll ledger. He had written it, later, into a compensation schedule at forty percent of its proper value.

"They're all names," he said. His voice had gone strange to his own ears. "Every stone. They brought the names up here because there was nowhere else to put them."

"Yes," Blackwood said.

"Burn it." Voss heard himself beg—it was begging, there was no other word, the great enforcer of the Kalyani syndicate standing in the rain asking a disgraced guide for mercy. "Burn the ledger. Burn the letters. Burn the map. Here, at the source, where the river can take the ash and no one will ever—Blackwood. Jonah. Listen to me. If you burn it, no one ever knows what I did, and no one ever knows what your father did either. Your father sold you. Do you understand? He sat in that chair and he sold his own son to save himself, and the ledger will tell you that in his own handwriting, and you will carry that knowledge for the rest of your life. Burn it and he stays a stranger who gave you a map. Burn it and we both walk down out of these mountains as free men. I'll sign anything. I'll swear the blacklist was fraud, I'll testify, I'll—"

"You'll perjure yourself," Blackwood said, "to protect yourself. It's the same skill. You've just changed employers."

The words hit like the cold. Voss opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"That's the temptation, isn't it," Blackwood went on, and his voice was not cruel; that was the unbearable part, it was almost gentle, the gentleness of a man who had already drowned once and knew the water. "You're offering me my father back. The innocent version. And you're offering yourself a clean grave. And the price is those." He nodded at the cairns—at the hundreds of names, at the girl standing among them, at the burned school register Voss knew was in his pack because Voss had read the manifest of everything Dorje carried. "The price is that they stay unnamed forever. That the valley stays a lie. That she—" he touched the girl's shoulder, lightly, "—goes on being a rumor in her own homeland."

"She's one girl," Voss whispered.

"She's everyone," Blackwood said. "That's what you've never understood. That's why you lost your gloves and your trackers and your way, and you still climbed, because you thought the mountain was the obstacle. The mountain isn't the obstacle, Voss. The mountain is the witness."

Wind again. The flags snapped. Somewhere under the ice the spring pulsed onward, indifferent, ancient, feeding the river that fed everything.

Voss stood among the cairns and felt the last structure of his life settle and crack, and he understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the very end of a thing—that he had climbed all this way not to destroy the ledger but to be stopped. To meet, at the top of the world, one man who would refuse him. Because if Blackwood refused him, then the choice was out of his hands, and a man can live with anything except his own choice.

"Then do it," he said hoarsely. "Whatever you're going to do. Read it, burn it, drown it, give it to them—I can't—" His knees went. He caught himself on the cairn, on the white stone with the name, and held on. "I can't carry it anymore. Eleven years. I can't."

Below them, faint through the rain, a sound rose from the valley: voices. Many voices. The returning villagers, coming up the trail toward the source, toward the cairns, toward the truth that had been walking toward them all monsoon long.

Blackwood turned toward the sound. So did the girl. And Voss, kneeling among the names of the dead with the rain running down his face like a verdict, heard Blackwood say quietly:

"They're here. It's time you met the people you wrote about."
