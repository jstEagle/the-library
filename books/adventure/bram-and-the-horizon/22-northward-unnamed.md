## 22. Northward, Unnamed

The letter had been in his breast pocket for eleven days, and Jonah had read it so many times the creases were going soft, the ink at the folds beginning to blur like a river seen through fog.

*Dear Jonah,* it began, in Tashi's careful, upright hand—the hand of a girl who had learned her letters in a school that no longer existed, from a teacher whose name was on a register he still dreamed about sometimes. *We finished the first roof today. It took four days because the timber came down from the high forest on sledges, and old Pemba insisted on cutting the ridgepole himself even though his hands shake. When they raised it, everyone shouted the way you shout when something is born. Ama put butter on the beam for the mountain spirits. I thought of you standing under it, and I decided you would have laughed, so I laughed for you.*

He could hear her voice in every line—dry, precise, unwilling to be sentimental until she chose to be, and then devastating.

*The valley is greener than I remembered. Or maybe I am seeing it differently now. The elders copied the ledger three times before the rains ended. One copy stays in the temple, one goes to the district office when we are ready, and one is being read aloud every winter at the cairns, so the children know their own history from the mouths of their own people instead of from papers written by men with grey gloves. Voss's trial is not a trial. It is slower and stranger than that. He works. He carries stone. Some of the women will not look at him. Some of the men talk to him as if nothing happened, which is its own kind of punishment, though I don't think they mean it as one. He asked me once if you ever wrote. I said no. Then I felt bad lying, so I am writing, and you can tell him yourself someday if you ever come back, which you should, but not yet. Not until the second roof is done. A man shouldn't see a place half-built.*

Jonah smiled at that, alone on the trail, and folded the letter away.

Ahead of him, his party was strung out along the ridge path like beads on a frayed cord: the two botanists from the university, arguing happily about lichen; the young surveyor who talked too much and carried too little; and Mrs. Ansel, sixty-one years old and tougher than any of them, who had hired him in Serem with a handshake and no questions about his name, because his name was in the guiding register now, signed plainly, *J. Blackwood*, where anyone could read it.

That had taken some getting used to. For years he had been other men—porters called Norbu or Karim or nothing at all, a face that slid past teahouse windows without catching on anything. Now clerks looked at his signature and looked up at him and matched the two together, and the world did not end. The world, in fact, had barely noticed. That was the strange mercy of it: identity, once surrendered and reclaimed, turned out to weigh almost nothing at all. Like an old coat finally hung up, he found he didn't miss the weight of it, only occasionally remembered the shape of the shoulders it had given him.

They were five days out from the last road, climbing into a range that appeared on none of his old maps—or rather, appeared on them as a blank space above the tree line, annotated in some cartographer's tired hand: *unsurveyed; local reports unreliable.* Jonah had come to love those words. Unreliable local reports had saved his life more times than he could count.

The morning was cold and rinsed clean, the kind of post-monsoon light that made distances honest. Below them the Kalyani country fell away southward—a green smear of gorge and memory—and ahead, north, the new range stood up white against a sky so blue it looked deliberate. Somewhere in that whiteness was a pass the surveyor swore existed and Mrs. Ansel doubted, and somewhere beyond the pass, valleys with names Jonah didn't know yet, rivers that fed some other sea.

He caught up to the party at the lunch halt, a shelf of granite above a stream gone glassy with autumn. The botanists were photographing a flower the size of a thumbnail. The surveyor was eating rice with his fingers and talking with his mouth full about contour intervals. Mrs. Ansel sat slightly apart, as she always did, drinking tea she'd made herself on a stove small enough to fit in a cupped hand.

"You're doing it again," she said, without looking up.

"Doing what?"

"Standing there looking north like a man reading a letter."

Jonah huffed. "Maybe I am."

"Your letter?"

"Tashi's." He lowered himself onto the rock beside her. "First roof's done. She says I can't visit until the second one's finished. She's very strict."

"She sounds like a good guide."

"The best I've trained." He let that sit a moment, because it was true and because saying true things had become, over these months, a pleasure rather than a risk. "She runs the whole rebuild. The elders listen to her. She told them the ledger should be read aloud every winter and they agreed, and then she told me about it in a letter of maybe two hundred words, most of which were about a ridgepole."

Mrs. Ansel smiled into her tea. "And what did you tell her?"

"That I'd bring timber tools when I come. And that Pemba should let someone else cut the next ridgepole before he cuts off a thumb."

"And after that?" Mrs. Ansel turned her head then, and her eyes were sharp and kind in the way of people who have buried enough to stop pretending otherwise. "After the second roof, Blackwood. What then? You're not a man who sits still. I've watched you for three weeks. You sleep like a sentry. You check the weather like it owes you money. There's a next thing in you. I can hear it ticking."

Jonah looked north again.

The truth was he had been feeling it for days—not restlessness, exactly. Restlessness was the old disease, the shame-driven itch that had kept him moving through Serem like a ghost through walls. This was quieter. This was the feeling of a compass needle settling after long spinning, pointing at something not because it was fleeing anything, but because it wanted to go.

"I don't know," he said, and found that he meant it peacefully. "For years I always knew. I knew what I was running from, then I knew what I was carrying, then I knew what I owed. Now—" He spread his hands. "Now the debts are paid. The map's delivered. The ledger's where it belongs. My name's my own. And it turns out nobody has assigned me a heading."

"Terrifying," Mrs. Ansel said dryly.

"Completely."

"Good," she said. "Nothing worth walking toward comes pre-assigned." She screwed the cap onto her thermos and stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Break's over. Move your party, guide."

* * *

They made the high camp by midafternoon, a hollow below the pass sheltered by a wall of boulders some ancient glacier had dumped and forgotten. Jonah got the tents up while the light went long and gold, and cooked dal and rice while the surveyor measured things that didn't need measuring and the botanists pressed flowers and argued about whether a particular moss was one species or two, a debate Jonah suspected would outlive them all.

It was good work. That was the thing he kept rediscovering: guiding was good work, when you did it clean. Reading weather. Watching feet. Knowing when to push and when to call the halt. All those skills the syndicate had tried to bury under a falsified accident report—*Blackwood, J., negligence contributing; license revoked*—had never actually died. They had just waited underground, like seed in a burned field, and Tashi's village had taught him that burned fields grow back greener, given rain and time and people stubborn enough to carry water.

After dinner he left the others to their stoves and climbed the twenty minutes to the ridge crest alone, the way he always did on the last night before a pass. It wasn't superstition anymore. It was just habit now, and a good one: go up, look, and decide.

The wind up there came straight off the northern ice, thin and bright and smelling of nothing at all. Below him the camp glowed faintly, two tents and three cook-fires, small and human against all that darkening stone. Behind him, south, the last of the sunset was burning along the edge of the Kalyani watershed, and he imagined he could almost see it—the drowned valley, the spring, the ring of cairns where he had read a dead man's ledger aloud to the mountain while the monsoon broke overhead like a verdict.

He had not gone back. Tashi had told him not to, and he obeyed her instructions better than he had ever obeyed any employer's. But he thought of it the way you think of a healed bone: aware of it in changing weather, grateful for it in every step.

He took out the compass.

It was Dorje's compass—he understood that now, understood everything now, or as much as a son ever understands a father. A cheap brass thing, the needle housing scratched, the back engraved with initials that weren't Dorje's, because it had never been Dorje's either; it had been bought or won or stolen decades ago and passed hand to hand down a chain of men who all used it to find their way toward the same forbidden valley, each of them certain the finding would fix them. Jonah had carried it upstream through gorges and ghost villages and a river in flood. He had nearly drowned holding onto it. At the source, he had meant to leave it on the cairns, an offering—but Tashi had pushed it back into his hand without a word, and he had understood that too. Some inheritances aren't meant to be buried. They're meant to be used.

The needle swung, wobbled, settled. South-southwest, toward home, toward the valley, toward the second roof and the winter readings and Voss carrying stone under the eyes of women who would not look at him. Every pull of gravity in his life pointed that way now.

Jonah turned the compass over in his palm and looked north.

Beyond the pass the range descended into a chaos of ridgelines the maps gave up on, and beyond those, faint in the last violet light, another wall of peaks that no one in his party could name. The surveyor claimed there was a hot spring up some tributary. The botanists had heard rumors of a forest of birch at an altitude where birch shouldn't grow. Local reports, presumably unreliable. Jonah felt the old familiar lift in his chest—the one he'd spent years drowning in shame and then years mistaking for obligation—and recognized it at last, cleanly, for what it had always been underneath everything else.

Not flight. Not debt. Just want. A man looking at unnamed country and wanting to walk into it.

He thought about what he would tell Tashi, in his next letter. *I'm heading north for a while. Nothing's wrong. That's the strange part.* She would write back something dry and perfect, probably about how the roof beams needed him more than the glaciers did, and she'd be right, and he'd go anyway, and she'd understand that too, because she was the one person alive who fully grasped the difference between running from a place and being free to leave it. You could only have the second, he'd learned, if you first belonged somewhere enough to leave it properly. He belonged to the Kalyani now, whatever the government's seals and the syndicate's lawyers said. Belonging traveled with you. It didn't chain you to one valley. It gave you a self to carry into all the others.

The stars were coming out over the ice. He checked the compass one last time, mostly for the ritual of it, and watched the needle settle toward a heading no client had requested, no map had marked, no debt demanded, no ghost had whispered in a dying man's breath.

North by northeast. Into the blank space above the tree line.

He closed the compass, put it back in his pocket beside Tashi's softening letter, and stood a moment longer in the wind, letting the silence have him. Somewhere far below, a fire popped. Somewhere farther, in a valley he couldn't see, a woman was laughing under a new roof beam, and an old man's shaking hands were already eyeing the next ridgepole.

Jonah Blackwood turned and walked down toward the campfire light, unhurried, unhaunted, unnamed by anyone but himself—

—and at the edge of the firelight he stopped, because Mrs. Ansel was waiting up, holding out a scrap of paper the surveyor had pulled from his pack an hour ago: a traveler's note, months old, passed hand to hand up the trail. Rumors, it said, of a lost party in the northern range. Last seen heading for the birch forest that shouldn't exist. Families asking after guides willing to look.

She raised an eyebrow. "Well, Blackwood? We're going that way anyway."

He took the paper, and read it twice, and felt the horizon open like a door.
