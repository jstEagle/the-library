## 1. Dead Air

The Vessel River had a mood for every hour of the day, and by six in the morning it was sulking.

Mirabel Iversen stood at the rail of the *Bram* with a tin mug of coffee going cold in her hands, watching the fog lift off the water in slow gray rags. Below her, the engine ticked as it cooled from yesterday's run. The dock at Corven's Landing smelled of diesel and wet rope and the particular green rot of pilings that had been standing in the river longer than anyone could remember. It was, she thought, the smell of home, if home were something you could smell. She'd been running this stretch of water for eleven years, and she knew its moods the way other people knew their own tempers: when it would hold a sandbar steady and when it would move one overnight just to be contrary; where the current ran lazy and where it hid teeth under a smiling surface.

The radio crackled behind her, and she turned toward it with the small, automatic hope she'd been carrying for three days now.

"—calling the *Bram*, calling the *Bram*. Mirabel, you copy?"

She set down the mug and keyed the handset. "Iversen here. Go ahead, Dispatch."

"Morning check-in. Nothing from the Halloway party again. That's day three."

Day three. She let the number sit in her chest for a moment before answering. "Copy that. Their schedule?"

"Check-ins at oh-six-hundred and eighteen-hundred, daily, per contract. Last transmission was oh-six-hundred three days ago. Dr. Halloway's voice, routine report, all nominal. Then nothing." A pause, the sound of paper shuffling two hundred miles away in an office where the river was only a line on a map. "Sponsor's been notified."

"Sponsor." Mirabel said the word flatly. Everyone on the upper Vessel knew who sponsored the Halloway survey—knew of Aldous Wren the way fishermen know of weather fronts, as a pressure you feel coming before you see it. Wren Consolidated had bought up half the salvage rights on the river system in the last five years, quietly, through lawyers and shell companies, the way a man buys up land around a spring he intends to charge for. "And what does the sponsor want done about his silent scientists?"

"That's not for me to say. But he's asked for you specifically. He'll be at your berth at eight."

Mirabel looked downriver, toward the headwaters she couldn't see—the unmapped country past the last chart, where the Vessel shed its wide, barge-friendly body and went narrow and cold and opinionated. Three days of dead air. In her experience there were a dozen innocent explanations for a missed check-in: a flooded generator, a cracked antenna, a radio operator who'd rather fish than file reports. Scientists were notorious for all three. But twelve hours of silence was equipment trouble. Twenty-four was carelessness. Seventy-two, in country like that, was something else—not necessarily bad, but *unknown*, and unknown on the Vessel had a way of staying unknown.

"Tell him eight o'clock works," she said, and signed off.

* * *

Aldous Wren arrived at seven fifty-eight, which told Mirabel he wanted her to notice his punctuality. He came down the dock alone—no assistants, no security—and that too was a performance, though she couldn't yet read what it was performing. He was a lean man somewhere north of sixty, dressed in field clothes too new to have ever touched actual mud, and he walked the planks like a man who had studied how dockworkers walked and practiced it in front of a mirror. His eyes, though, were the honest part of him: pale, quick, and cold as water under ice. They went over the *Bram* the way an appraiser goes over jewelry.

"Captain Iversen." He offered a hand. His grip was dry and precise. "I appreciate you making time."

"You bought the time," she said. "I hear I'm expensive this week."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but a receipt for one. "May we talk aboard?"

"It's my boat. We can talk anywhere on it."

They sat in the wheelhouse, Wren folding himself onto the bench across from the helm with the careful economy of a man accustomed to chairs that cost more than boats. Through the glass behind him the river went on doing its morning work, sliding south, indifferent.

"I'll be direct," Wren said. "My survey team is overdue on communications. I want a supply run made today, and I want you to make it."

"Dispatch already told me they're dark. Three days." She kept her voice level, professional. "You're not worried?"

"I'm concerned, naturally. But concern isn't evidence. Equipment fails. Weather interferes." He said it smoothly, but she caught the seam in it—the faint over-rehearsal, like a man reciting a comfort he didn't feel. Or didn't want her to think he felt. "What I need is someone who knows the upper river well enough to get there fast and get back with answers. Your name came up in every conversation I had."

"Whose conversations?"

"Harbormasters. Freight agents. A deckhand named Sef Kettering, who speaks of you the way sailors speak of good luck." He produced a folder—not thick, but dense—and laid it on the chart table between them. "Triple your standard charter rate. Half up front. Full bonus on return with either contact reestablished or personnel recovered."

Mirabel didn't touch the folder. Triple rate was not a payment; it was a confession. Nobody paid triple for a milk run. Triple meant the buyer believed the job might cost more than money could cover, and was hoping cash would do the covering anyway.

"Mr. Wren," she said, "when a client offers triple, I start asking why the job scares him."

"It doesn't scare me." For the first time something flickered behind the pale eyes—irritation, quickly banked. "It inconveniences me. There's a difference, and the difference is worth triple to whoever can resolve it. My team is five scientists with two years of funding behind them and a window that closes when the seasonal flood comes. If they've lost their radio, I need to know now. If they've lost anything else—" he spread his hands, a gesture of reasonable men everywhere, "—then time matters even more."

"And if they're dead?"

"Then I'll need that known precisely, and documented, and I'll pay the same bonus for certainty as for rescue." He said it without flinching, and she gave him credit for that; most men would have performed grief. Wren simply declined to pretend. It was almost refreshing. Almost.

She opened the folder at last. Inside: the survey's coordinates, deeper into the headwaters than any charter she'd run—past the last charted bend, into the gorge country where the maps went blank and the guidebooks stopped pretending. Supply manifest. Team roster. Dr. Emeric Halloway, team lead; four names beneath his she didn't know. And a clause, printed in smaller type than the rest, assigning to Wren Consolidated all rights to "materials, specimens, and findings of whatever nature" encountered on site.

"Your contract gives you everything they find up there," she said.

"My contract funds everything they find up there. Ownership follows investment. That's not sinister, Captain, it's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." She closed the folder. Outside, the fog had burned off entirely, and the river lay bright and hard in the sun, running south without comment. Somewhere up in that blank space past the last chart, five people hadn't spoken in seventy-two hours, and the only man who seemed eager to reach them was a man who talked about them in the language of invoices.

Her instincts were speaking clearly, and they were saying what they always said, in the old river idiom: *when the water looks calmest, check your depth.*

But there was rent due on the boat slip. There was Sef's wages, and the new impeller the *Bram* needed, and the slow, grinding arithmetic of a woman alone with a boat and a reputation that only stayed good as long as she kept saying yes. Triple rate. Half up front. And underneath the money, quieter but real: five people out there who had trusted the river to carry help to them if they needed it, and she was the help the river had.

"I'll take the contract," she said. "Standard safety terms apply. I decide what the *Bram* carries and how she runs. And if I judge the situation beyond my capacity, I turn around, and you still pay the first half."

Wren considered her for a moment, and she had the odd sensation of being measured for something—a coat, or a coffin.

"Agreed," he said. "Load at noon. I'll have supplies delivered to your berth." He rose, smoothed trousers that didn't need smoothing, and paused at the wheelhouse door. "Captain. One thing."

"Mr. Wren."

"The upper river past the gorge—it does strange things to people. So I'm told. Whatever you find, try to find it quickly." He smiled then, fully, and it was the worst expression she'd seen on him, because it looked practiced too. "Sentiment costs time, and time up there is the one commodity nobody controls."

He went down the plank and away along the dock, and Mirabel stood at the wheelhouse window watching him go, feeling the trap close around her so gently she could almost believe she'd walked in on her own.

Which, she supposed, was how traps worked.

* * *

By noon the supplies were stacked on the dock in neat, shrink-wrapped pallets, and Sef Kettering had arrived with his duffel and his doubts, in that order.

"Triple rate," he said, dropping the duffel on deck with a thud. "Word travels. What's wrong with the money, skipper?"

"Nothing's wrong with it. That's what worries me." She nodded at the pallets. "Help me load. I want to see every crate before it comes aboard."

They worked through the afternoon, the *Bram*'s hold swallowing cargo in its patient way—dry goods, fuel drums, medical kits, spare parts, all of it ordinary, all of it exactly what a survey camp six days upriver would need. Mirabel checked manifests against contents with the thoroughness of a customs officer, and found nothing amiss, and was beginning to feel foolish about her own suspicion when they came to the last four crates.

They were sealed. Not shrink-wrapped like the others but crated in hardwood, strapped in steel banding, stenciled with handling codes she didn't recognize—and marked, in block letters, with a set of coordinates.

She stood over them a long moment, coffee mug forgotten, the dock sounds fading around her. She knew coordinate formats the way she knew channel markers. These weren't the survey camp's. They weren't anything she'd seen on a chart. The numbers put them somewhere past the last mapped bend—deeper than the camp, deeper than anything with a name.

"Wren's people delivered these separate," Sef said, coming to stand beside her. He was a broad, quiet man with a beard going gray at the chin and twenty years of river in his shoulders. "Fella dropped 'em off an hour before the pallets. Didn't talk. Paid cash for the handling."

"Did he say what's in them?"

"Fragile scientific equipment, he says." Sef spat over the rail, an old habit the river had never cured. "Equipment don't usually come sealed like a coffin, skipper. And it don't usually get addressed to a place that ain't on any map."

Mirabel crouched and ran her thumb along the steel banding. Cold under her skin, colder than the morning air justified. Hardwood, steel, precision stenciling—this was not expedition gear. This was cargo somebody expected to survive rough handling and didn't mind admitting it.

"We could refuse them," Sef said. "Contract says you decide what the *Bram* carries."

"She did decide. That was this morning, before I saw these." She straightened. The river slid past below them, bright and busy and keeping its own counsel. Somewhere upstream, in the blank space past the last chart, five people had gone quiet, and the man paying triple to reach them had sent along four boxes addressed to nowhere.

"Skipper," Sef said, and his voice had dropped into the register he used for serious things, the register of storms and dead engines. "I've run the headwaters with you six years. You know what I'm going to say."

"Say it anyway."

"The water up there has swallowed better boats than this one. Better crews. Men who knew the channels better than their own backyards." He looked at the crates, then at her. "Whatever Wren's after, it ain't worth what that country takes as toll. It never has been."

Mirabel looked at the four sealed crates, and at the wide brown water, and at the last of the afternoon light coming down the valley like something being poured. Her instincts were still talking. They had been talking since dawn. But the first half of the fee was already in her account, and five people were quiet in the high country, and she was, whatever else, the help the river had.

"Bunk down," she said finally. "We cast off at first light."

Sef held her eyes a moment longer, then nodded once and went below. Mirabel stayed on deck until dark, listening to the river run south, telling itself whatever stories rivers tell. Above the last charted bend, the headwaters waited with their mouth shut.

She slept badly, and dreamed of water rising over stones that spelled a name she almost recognized.
