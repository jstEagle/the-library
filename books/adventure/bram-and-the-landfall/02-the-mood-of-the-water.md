## 2. The Mood of the Water

The Bram sat low in the water before Mirabel had loaded a single crate of her own, and that was the first thing wrong with the morning.

She stood on the dock at Corren's Landing with her hands in her coat pockets, watching Wren's men work. There were six of them, hired muscle by the look of them—broad through the shoulders and narrow through the imagination—and they moved the sponsor's cargo aboard with a efficiency that had nothing to do with care. The crates came off a canvas-topped truck in a steady rhythm, passed hand to hand down the gangplank, and disappeared into the Bram's hold as though they'd been weighed and measured for the space.

They hadn't been. She'd built that hold herself, or rebuilt it, caulking every seam with her own hands one bitter winter when work was thin and pride thinner. She knew its capacity the way she knew the span of her own arms. Whatever arithmetic Wren's people were running, it didn't include her boat so much as use it.

"Morning, Captain." One of the men touched his cap brim without quite removing it. He had a clipboard against his hip and the particular blankness of a man who has been told not to answer questions. "We'll be out of your way directly."

"You're already in my way," Mirabel said pleasantly. "That's my deck you're standing on."

He smiled the way a wall smiles and went back to work.

The crates themselves bothered her more than the men did. They were sealed—not roped, not latched, but *sealed*, the lids screwed down and the seams run over with tar until each one looked like something exhumed rather than shipped. And stenciled on every side, in block letters fresh enough to smell of paint, were coordinates.

Mirabel read coordinates the way other people read street signs. She'd spent nineteen years on the Vessel and its tributaries, and she could place most numbers above the Last Chart within ten miles. These were past that. Well past. These were numbers from the headwater gorge, the drowned country where the river came down out of mountains no survey party had ever mapped to government satisfaction—the kind of coordinates that appeared on no claim, fell under no license, and belonged to nobody because nobody had ever proven they existed.

Except, apparently, Aldous Wren.

She crouched beside the last crate waiting at the dock's edge and ran her thumb along the stencil. The numbers were precise. Not region-precise—*point*-precise, down to minutes and seconds, the sort of fix you got from an instrument sitting on the ground looking at something specific. You didn't stamp coordinates like that on supplies going *to* a site. You stamped them on cargo coming *from* one.

"Captain Iversen."

Wren stood at the head of the gangplank in a gray coat too good for the river, hands clasped behind him like a man touring an estate he already owned. He'd arrived an hour after dawn without warning, which she suspected was his habit everywhere: arrive after the decision has been made, then stand near it looking inevitable.

"Mr. Wren."

"I trust the loading is proceeding satisfactorily." His eyes went over the crates, the deck, the water, with the unhurried appetite of a man taking inventory of everything he intended to have. "I confess I don't know boats. But I'm told this one is the best on the upper river, and I paid accordingly."

"You paid for passage and resupply," Mirabel said. "The contract's clear. My boat goes up, delivers to the survey camp, comes back. That's all it does."

"All?" He smiled faintly. "Captain, nothing is ever all anything. But yes. Passage and resupply." He turned toward the rail, looking downriver toward the gray-brown water sliding past the pilings. "You've been up past the Last Chart before, I understand."

"Once. Years ago. Ran a geological team as far as the narrows and back."

"And?"

"And what?"

"What did you think of it?"

Mirabel considered him. There was a question under the question; there always was with moneyed men, who treated conversation as dredging. "It's water," she said. "Fast, cold, and it doesn't care what you think of it. Same as any water. Just less forgiving about it."

"Mm." Wren nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd written down already. "The headwaters have a reputation. Swallowed boats, swallowed men. Superstition mostly, I imagine."

"Mostly," Mirabel said, and left the rest of the sentence where it lay.

* * *

Sef found her in the hold an hour later, wedged between two of the sealed crates, checking lashings she'd already checked. Her old deckhand had come down at first light without being asked, the way he always did when something smelled wrong—he claimed he could feel her bad decisions through the floorboards of his room above the chandlery.

He was sixty-one and built like a mooring post, with a face the river had been working on for decades and hands that had forgotten how to be fully clean. He looked at the crates. He looked at her. He looked at the crates again.

"No," he said.

"They're paying triple."

"No," Sef said again, with the patience of a man explaining weather to a child. "Triple doesn't buy what this is. Triple buys risk. This isn't risk. This is a boat full of boxes that don't want to be looked at, going to a place that don't want to be gone to, for a man who looks at you like you're a receipt." He spat over the side, ceremonially. "I've watched you take bad contracts for nineteen years, Mirabel. This is the worst one, and you know it, which makes it worse than the worst one."

"The survey team's gone quiet. Three days of dead air. If they're hurt up there—"

"If they're hurt, it's a rescue, and rescues pay double and come with glory. This pays triple and comes with *him*." Sef jerked his chin upward, toward the dock, where Wren stood talking quietly with the clipboard man. "Men like that don't send triple pay up a river for science. Science rides in open crates with labels on. These—" he laid one palm flat against the tarred seam of the nearest box, and Mirabel noticed he took his hand away quickly, as if the wood had been colder than the morning justified "—these ride like evidence."

"They're marked with survey coordinates."

"I saw."

"Headwater coordinates. Past anything filed."

"I saw that too." Sef lowered himself onto a crate that wasn't sealed and looked at her, and beneath the gruffness his face did the thing it only did rarely, the thing that reminded her he'd pulled her out of the Vessel once, in flood season, when she'd been twenty-six and certain she couldn't drown. "Girl. The headwaters have swallowed better boats than yours. Better captains too. Boats with steel hulls and engines worth more than everything you own. The old pilots wouldn't go up there past midsummer, and the old pilots weren't superstitious—they were *observant*. There's a mood to that water. It changes its mind."

"It's a river, Sef."

"It's a river *there*," he said. "Here it's just water doing what water does. Up there—" He shook his head. "My father ran timber rafts off the lower gorge before the war. Came back once with half his crew and none of his logs, and he never talked about it except once, drunk, and what he said was: *the river up there knows what it wants and it lies to get it.*" He shrugged. "Drunk talk. Probably."

"Probably," Mirabel agreed.

They sat a moment in the dim of the hold, listening to the dock sounds above—the scuff of boots, the gull argument, the truck engine idling. Between them lay the sealed crates with their impossible coordinates, breathing their tar-and-something-else smell into the dark.

"Walk away," Sef said quietly. "Give him back his money. We'll take the mail run to Tarrow Bend and eat plain for a month, and in a month we'll both still know our own names."

It was the last part that caught her, though she couldn't have said why. *Still know our own names.* A strange way to put it. She turned it over once and set it down.

"I can't," she said. "Not because of the money. Because three days ago five people stopped answering their radio, and everyone else on this river is willing to wait for a fourth day, and a fifth, until it's official enough to be someone else's problem." She stood, brushed the dust from her knees. "If I walk away and they're dead, I'll have walked away. That's a thing I'd have to carry, and I've got enough ballast."

"And if they're not dead? If they're something else?"

"Then I'll know that too."

Sef looked at her a long time. Then he sighed, the whole-body sigh of a man surrendering to a battle fought and lost many times before, and hauled himself up off the crate.

"Well," he said. "Then I suppose I'm coming, since somebody's got to keep you from drowning out of pure stubbornness. Again." He jabbed a finger at the sealed crates. "But we do it my way below the narrows. Slow water, daylight hours, and if the river starts lying to us, we believe the lie and turn around. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Agreed means agreed. Not agreed-until-I-decide-differently-at-two-in-the-morning."

"Sef."

"Because you decide differently at two in the morning. It's documented."

Mirabel almost laughed. It felt good in her chest, brief and warm, like a match struck in a cold room. "Agreed means agreed."

* * *

They cast off at noon with the tide, such as it was on the upper Vessel—a slow breathing of the current that old pilots called the river's inhale. Wren did not come aboard. He stood on the dock with his clipboard man and watched them go, and just before the gap between the Bram and the pilings widened past jumping distance, he raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More a man releasing something he now considered accounted for.

The sealed crates rode in the hold, lashed and silent. Twice during the first hour Mirabel went below to check the lashings, and twice she stood among them in the dim light feeling like a woman locked in a room with sleeping strangers. The coordinates stared out from every side. *Past the chart. Past the claims. Past everything filed or fenced or owned.*

Point-precise. Instrument-fixed. The numbers of something seen.

She climbed back into the daylight, took the wheel from Sef, and pointed the Bram's nose upriver, into the long green corridor of the channel. Ahead the water ran smooth and brown-gold in the afternoon light, docile as a dog, and the banks slid by with the mild indifference she'd known all her life.

But she kept thinking of Sef's father, and the sentence he'd said drunk: *the river up there knows what it wants.*

And she kept thinking of the crates, and the one detail she hadn't mentioned to Sef, because saying it aloud would have made it real: the coordinates stenciled on those boxes were not the survey camp's coordinates. Halloway's expedition had filed its position weeks ago; she had it in her contract papers, memorized.

The crates were marked for somewhere else. Somewhere four miles farther up, past the camp, past the narrows—to a point in the middle of the gorge where, according to every map ever drawn, there was nothing at all but white water and stone.

Something down there had already been found. And Aldous Wren wanted it moved before anyone else could see it.

Mirabel opened the throttle, and the Bram began to climb the river.
