## 3. Past the Last Chart

The last chart ended at a place called Two Sisters Bend, where the cartographer's ink simply gave up—a faded annotation reading *character of river unknown beyond this point*, and then nothing but the cream-colored blankness of paper that had never been touched by a sounding line. Mirabel had always liked that blankness. It was the honest part of the map, the part that admitted what every other inch pretended not to know.

By noon of the second day past the bend, she had stopped liking it.

The Vessel narrowed the way a throat narrows before speech. The walls closed in—first forested bluffs, then raw clay cutbanks veined with roots, then shelves of grey stone that leaned over the channel like old men conferring. The water darkened from tea-brown to something nearer to iron, and it moved differently too: no longer the broad, patient drift of a lowland river but a gathered, muscular current that pulled at the Bram's hull as though testing her weight.

"Soundings are lying," Sef said. He stood at the bow with the lead line coiled in one fist, and there was a flatness in his voice she'd learned to read across twenty years of shared water. "I've dropped three times in a hundred yards. Bottom says six feet under the port bank, nine mid-channel, four off the starboard—and none of it agrees with what the boat's telling me under the keel."

"Boats exaggerate," Mirabel said.

"Not this one." He swung the lead and let it fly again, a smooth silver arc, and read the wet line with his thumb before he even hauled it in. "Seven feet. And I'll tell you something else. The bar we crossed this morning—the one that nearly took our paint off? It wasn't on my memory of the run up, and I've been up this far once before, eight years back. There was open water there. I'd have sworn it on my mother's grave."

"You'd have sworn anything on your mother's grave if the price was right."

He grinned at that, briefly, but the grin didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and when he turned back to the bow the line between his shoulders stayed knotted.

Mirabel held the wheel and watched the banks. The crates in her hold rode quiet beneath the tarps—Wren's sealed boxes with their survey coordinates stamped in black, coordinates that put them miles beyond any claim Wren's company had filed, beyond any claim a man could legally file at all. She had told herself they were instruments. She had told herself a great many things since taking his contract, and each one had required slightly more effort than the last, like wading into deeper water.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel shelf below a leaning cottonwood, and Mirabel slept badly in the way she slept badly only on rivers that felt wrong—not from noise but from a silence with texture to it, a silence that seemed to be listening back. In the grey hour before dawn she came awake all at once, certain she had heard something, and lay listening to the water slide past the hull.

It wasn't the sound that troubled her. It was the rhythm. Rivers have rhythms the way hearts do, and a woman who has spent her life on them reads them without thinking—the long exhale of a straight run, the stutter of water over gravel, the deep even pulse of a channel that knows its own mind. This river's pulse kept changing. Not fast, not dramatically. Just enough. A beat held a half-second too long, as if the current were considering its options.

She got up and walked the bank in the half-light, coffee cooling in her hand, and found what she would spend the rest of the day refusing to say aloud: the sandbar that had scraped their keel yesterday morning was gone. Not moved. Gone. The gravel of it lay redistributed along the near shore in a neat windrow, as though something had picked the bar up and set it aside, deliberately, the way a housekeeper moves a chair.

Rivers move their bars. Every deckhand knows it; it's the first lesson and the last. Bars travel downstream in freshets, build in dry years, drown in wet ones. But bars travel slowly, and they leave evidence, and they do not tidy up after themselves overnight.

She stood looking at the clean-swept channel until Sef's footsteps crunched behind her.

"You see it too," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I see a river doing what rivers do."

"Yeah." He spat into the water, an old habit, an offering to whatever ran the currents. "And I see a dog walking on its hind legs. Doesn't mean I stop calling it strange."

* * *

The cache was marked on Wren's manifest as Station Four—a supply depot the survey team had established on their way up, stocked against the return journey. The coordinates put it on the inside of a horseshoe bend where a creek mouth fanned out into a shallow delta, and by mid-afternoon they were working the Bram through the shallows toward it, Mirabel at the wheel, Sef poling off the bow, the water so clear over the gravel shoals that they could count the stones beneath.

The depot hut stood on stilts above the flood line, a prefabricated box of corrugated steel that should have gleamed dully in the afternoon sun. It did not gleam. It looked, from two hundred yards out, like something that had been wept on—streaked dark, sagging slightly, though the structure itself was sound.

No smoke. No movement. That was expected; the survey team was weeks upstream by now. What was not expected was the door.

It hung open. And the opening was inward.

Mirabel brought the Bram alongside the stilt platform and made fast without speaking, and Sef came aft without being asked, and they stood together at the foot of the ladder for a moment the way men stand at the door of a house where something has died.

"Could be bears," Sef offered.

"Bears don't shut doors behind them."

"That's my point. Door's open."

"The latch is on the outside, Sef. Look at it. Something opened that door from the inside and didn't bother closing it, which means whatever was in there wanted out, or—" She stopped. Or wanted whoever came next to look inside. The thought completed itself without her permission.

They went up. The ladder rungs rang hollow under their boots.

Inside, the depot was a ransacking done by someone with all the time in the world. Crates of canned goods sat stacked in a perfect pyramid in the center of the floor—neatly, almost lovingly arranged—but every can had been opened. Not eaten. Opened. Lids peeled back in tidy curls, contents untouched, sixty cans of beans and peaches and condensed milk standing open to the air like small silver mouths. Rice sacks had been slit and their contents poured out in careful spirals on the floorboards, patterns that might have been decorative or might have been writing in a script that hurt to try to follow. The medical kit was intact. The rifles were intact, racked and oiled. The radio was intact, its dial glowing faintly, tuned—as far as Mirabel could tell—to a frequency that wasn't a frequency, down at the very bottom of the band where the scale ran out of numbers.

"It's the inside-out of a robbery," Sef said quietly. He hadn't touched anything. Neither had she. "Thieves take. This is—somebody left everything, but broke it first. Broke the *usefulness* out of it."

Mirabel crouched by the rice spirals. Up close they resolved into lines, hundreds of them, laid grain by grain with a patience that made her skin crawl worse than violence would have. Violence has heat in it. This was cold work. Deliberate work. Work done by someone—or something—that had wanted the supplies ruined and wanted the ruin to be *read*.

In the corner, half under the radio table, she found the logbook the survey team should have kept at the station. Its pages after the third day were filled edge to edge with a single sentence, repeated in Halloway's handwriting, growing progressively larger until the letters sprawled off the margins and down the sides:

*The water remembers the shape of what stood in it.*

The last entry was different. Smaller. Written carefully, in the top corner of the final page, as if the writer had been saving space:

*Mirabel—Iversen—is that your name? It will be soon. Come quickly. Bring nothing you love.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings refused to stay in her head, sliding away like water off waxed canvas. Her own name, in a stranger's handwriting, in a depot she had never visited, on a route she had never run, weeks before Wren had ever offered her triple pay to make this trip.

Outside, the light was going coppery, and the river's voice had changed again. She could hear it through the open door: that held-beat pulse, longer now, more deliberate, like something counting.

Sef appeared beside her. He had seen the logbook. His face had gone the color of the clay banks.

"Boss," he said. "Tell me we're turning around."

Mirabel looked at the open cans, the grain-script, the page with her name on it. Somewhere upstream, five scientists were waiting for supplies they had already known she'd bring. Somewhere behind her, a sponsor waited for cargo she was increasingly afraid to deliver. And the river ran on between them, rearranging its bars in the dark like a man moving furniture in a room he doesn't want searched.

"We're going up," she said. "But we're going up armed, and we're going up fast, and Sef—if I start writing things in rice, you put me over the side."

He managed half a laugh. It died young.

They cast off into the gathering dusk, and behind them the depot door swung slowly on its hinge, open inward, patient as a held breath.
