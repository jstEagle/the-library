## 4. The Camp That Waits

The gorge opened at dusk the way a hand opens—slowly, reluctantly, showing you something you weren't sure you wanted held.

Mirabel brought the *Bram* around the last bend with her engine idled to a murmur and her eyes doing the work her instruments had given up on three days ago. The chart in her wheelhouse showed open water here, a wide braided channel running north toward the survey coordinates Wren had burned into her memory like a brand. The river disagreed. It had been disagreeing for two days now, sliding its sandbars into places no map admitted existed, rearranging itself overnight the way a sleeper turns in bed. She had stopped trusting the paper and started trusting the water's mood, which was a thing you learned after twenty years on the Vessel: rivers had moods, and this one was *attentive*.

"There." Sef stood at the rail, one hand shading his eyes against the last copper light. "Smoke."

There wasn't smoke. There had been smoke once—the gray ghost of it still hung above the bluff in a thin, unbroken line that hadn't moved in hours, maybe days, a plume frozen mid-thought. Below it, on a shelf of gravel where the headwater current slowed to think, five tents stood in a neat crescent. Guy-lines taut. Rain flies buttoned. A wind gauge turned lazily on its mast. A solar array caught the dying light and threw it back in orange panes.

And around a fire pit of blackened stone sat five figures.

"Cut the engine," Mirabel said quietly.

The silence that followed was worse than the noise. On the Vessel, silence meant shallows, or deadfall, or something holding its breath. She let the *Bram* drift the last hundred yards, stern-first, the way she'd approach anything that might be wounded—or waiting—and threw a line over a drowned alder without ever quite deciding to do it.

The camp smelled wrong before it looked wrong. Woodsmoke, yes, but underneath it something mineral and cold, like the air off a cave mouth, like wet slate. The fire pit held ash so fine and pale it might have been flour. No heat came off it when Mirabel passed her palm above it. Whatever had burned there had burned out days ago, and nobody had fed it since.

The five scientists sat in a half-circle facing the fire as if it were still burning. As if they were still warm.

They were alive. That was the first thing her body understood, before her mind caught up—a rise and fall of shoulders, the slow blink, the small animal movements of breathing people. But they sat the way furniture sits. A woman in a field jacket with her hands folded in her lap, thumbs moving against each other in an endless, absent circuit. A young man with a notebook open on his knee, pen resting on the page mid-word, though when Mirabel tilted her head she saw the page was blank. Another man had let his cup tip in his fingers and had not noticed the tea—or whatever it was—soaking darkly into the gravel between his boots.

None of them looked up. None of them looked at anything.

"Sef," Mirabel said, and didn't finish the sentence. Sef had already gone back to the boat. She heard him there, moving, staying close to the gunwale, keeping the *Bram* between himself and the camp the way you'd keep a door between yourself and a strange dog.

She walked the crescent slowly. Professional. That was the word she gave it. A supply captain assessing her clients. Her boots crunched on gravel and not one of the five flinched.

Up close they looked *used*. Not ill, not starved—they'd been eating; ration wrappers lay tidily folded by the fire pit, arranged in a stack too neat for any camp she'd ever run—but worn in some way that had nothing to do with labor. Their eyes had the flat shine of river stones. The woman with the folded hands had a smear of pale dust along her sleeve, the same chalky gray as the ash, and Mirabel thought: *they've been digging.* Or the ground had been giving something up to them.

Instruments ran everywhere. A seismograph ticked softly inside a tent whose flap stood open, its needle drawing a line so steady it looked printed. A water-quality monitor blinked green beside the river. A laptop screen glowed in another tent, a spreadsheet open, cells filled with numbers down to row four hundred and something—as if someone had been entering data hour after hour, diligent as a monk, right up until they'd walked out to sit by a dead fire and stop.

"Hello," Mirabel said, to all of them, to none of them.

Nothing. The thumbs circled. The tea soaked.

She crouched by the woman in the field jacket—close enough now to see the expedition patch on her shoulder, the stylized river-fork logo of the Wren Foundation, and beneath it a name tape: HALLOWAY. Dr. E. Halloway. The team lead. The voice on the radio checks that had come through crisp and cheerful for the first week and then thinner, then slower, then not at all.

"Halloway. Mirabel Iversen. I've got your resupply aboard. Forty days of provisions, fuel, medical kit. You want to tell me what's going on here?"

Halloway's eyes came up.

It should have been a relief. It was the opposite. The eyes rose to hers with a smoothness that felt rehearsed by something that had watched humans blink for a long time and gotten very good at it, and behind them was—not emptiness. Emptiness would have been simpler. Behind them was *attention*, patient and courteous, like a clerk at a counter who will wait all day because the day belongs to him.

"You're late," Halloway said. Her voice was pleasant. Reasonable. "But that's all right. We knew you'd come."

"We?"

"The river told us you would." A small smile, warm as a lamp. "It tells us most things now."

A prickle walked up Mirabel's spine, scalp to tailbone. She kept her voice level, kept her hands loose, kept the captain on her face like a mask fitted over a face. "Your radio checks stopped three days ago. Wren sent me up with triple pay and a hold full of crates I wasn't allowed to open. So before we talk about the river telling you things, you're going to tell me why five trained scientists are sitting around a cold fire at dusk like it's church."

Halloway considered her. The consideration lasted too long, stretched like taffy, and somewhere in the middle of it Mirabel realized the other four had turned their heads—all together, all at the same speed—to look at her. Five pairs of stone-shine eyes. The young man's pen finally touched the blank page and began to move, writing nothing, or writing something only he could see.

"You came upriver," Halloway said gently, "past the last chart. Past the cache at Miller's Bend—that was ours, I'm sorry about the mess, we needed what was in it—or rather, it needed to be found. Past the bar that moves. You read the water the whole way, didn't you? You didn't use your maps after the second day."

"I used my judgment."

"Yes." Halloway nodded, pleased, as if Mirabel had answered a question on an exam. "That's how it chooses who to keep. Not the ones who follow lines on paper. The ones who listen." She unfolded her hands for the first time and laid them palm-up on her knees, and Mirabel saw that the palms were gray with the same pale dust, worked deep into the whorls like ink into a printer's fingers. "We've been listening for eleven days. Do you know what's under the bluff?"

"Rock."

"Stone," Halloway agreed, with a tiny correction of the head, as if Mirabel had said something charmingly naive. "Carved stone. A city, or the bones of one. Drowned when the river changed its mind, centuries ago. We found the tops of the walls at low water and we thought, wonderful, a discovery, a career. And then we stayed. And then—" she paused, and for just a moment something human flickered across her face, something frightened and drowning, there and gone like a fish turning under the surface, "—and then it stayed in us."

Mirabel straightened slowly. Every instinct she owned was telling her to walk back to the boat, cast off, and let the foundation find its own lost sheep. But forty days of provisions don't unload themselves, and more than instinct—older than instinct—was the simple stubborn arithmetic of the river: you don't leave people. Even people who have stopped being entirely people. Especially them.

"I'm mooring for the night," she said. "Light's gone, the bars shift after dark, and I'm not breaking my hull on your account. In the morning you'll tell me everything, starting with what's in those crates I hauled two hundred miles, and then we'll talk about who's fit to travel."

"Of course," Halloway said, agreeable as Sunday. "You'll want supper. We have plenty. We haven't been hungry lately—it's strange how little the body wants, once the mind is full."

That sentence followed Mirabel down to the waterline like a dog.

Sef met her at the gangplank, lantern in hand, his broad face tight. "Well?"

"They're alive. Fed. Polite." She took the lantern from him because her hands wanted something to hold. "Polite is the worst part. They're polite the way a spider's web is polite."

"And the team lead?"

"She greeted me like she'd been expecting me." Mirabel looked back up the bank. In the blue last light the five figures sat unmoved, and the frozen smoke still hung above the bluff, and beneath the bluff—she could feel it now, the way you feel weather coming in a bad tooth—something old and patient lay under the water, wearing the river like a coat.

"One more thing," she said, and heard her own voice come out lower than she intended. "She called me by name. Before I gave it."

"That's on the manifest, Captain. Wren's office would've radioed ahead—"

"She didn't say Iversen." Mirabel watched the camp, watching her. Five heads, five stillnesses, one attention. "She called me Ansa. Said it like she'd known me all my life."

Sef frowned. "Who's Ansa?"

"I don't know." The lantern flame bent in the river wind. "That's what frightens me. When she said it, I almost answered."

* * *

She did not sleep so much as stand watch over herself.

The *Bram* rode at her mooring with the current combing her hull, and Mirabel lay in her bunk in the dark running the day backward like film, looking for the frame where things had gone wrong, and could not find it, and that was its own kind of terror. Because the truth was nothing had gone wrong. The camp was intact. The scientists were alive. The contract was fulfilled. If she squinted—if she was tired, and she was—everything was fine.

Except that lying in her bunk she could hear, faint and rhythmic under the river sound, a ticking from the shore. The seismograph. Steady as a clock. And she caught herself keeping time with it, her heartbeat leaning toward it, wanting to match.

And except that when she closed her eyes she saw a harbor she had never visited: white walls stepping down to green water, boats with eyes painted on their bows, a woman on a quay calling a name across the water—

*Ansa.*

Mirabel's eyes snapped open. Her heart slammed. The bunk boards pressed cold against her back and the Vessel River ran black past her windows and none of it—none of it—was a harbor with painted eyes.

Above the bank, the camp's lanterns had gone out one by one, politely, in order, as if the dark had been requested.

She lay awake until the sky went gray, and in the first true light she went up the bank alone, because morning was for work and work was for keeping your shape, and found Dr. Halloway standing at the water's edge with her gray-dusted palms turned upward, waiting for her with a smile of perfect, terrible kindness.

"Good morning, Ansa," Halloway said. "We've written down everything you asked for. And there's something below the bluff we'd like to show you."
