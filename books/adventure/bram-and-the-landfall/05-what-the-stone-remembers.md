## 5. What the Stone Remembers

Mirabel woke in a tent that was not hers, under a blanket that smelled of someone else's woodsmoke, and for three full seconds she could not remember her own name.

Then it came—*Mirabel Iversen*—arriving like a boat coming through fog, and she lay still beneath the canvas and let the relief of it wash through her. Outside, the Vessel ran high and green with glacier milk, and the camp made its small sounds: rope creaking against a ridgepole, the tick of cooling instruments, Dr. Halloway's voice somewhere near the fire, low and even, talking to no one.

She had meant to leave yesterday. She had said so aloud, standing at the rail of the Bram with Sef beside her, both of them watching the five scientists come down to the water's edge to meet them like a delegation from some country that had never been mapped. She had said, *We'll offload and run back down before dark.* And Halloway had smiled at her with such open, reasonable warmth and said, *Of course. But stay the night. The channel shifts after dusk here—you'd ground on the bar below the bluff and lose your hull.* It had sounded like seamanship. It had sounded like sense.

Now, in the grey of morning, Mirabel lay in the borrowed tent and tried to reconstruct why she had agreed, and found the memory soft at its edges, like bread left out in rain. She remembered saying yes. She could not remember wanting to.

That frightened her more than anything else the camp had shown her—the cold fire, the motionless figures, the instruments logging data no one had read. She dressed quickly, boots on wet grass, and stepped out into a morning so beautiful it felt staged: mist lifting off the river in ropes, the bluff across the water going gold at its crown, five scientists already up and moving among their tasks with the unhurried competence of people who had done these same motions ten thousand times.

"Sleep well?" Halloway asked, handing her a tin mug. The coffee was terrible and real, and she clung to it.

"Well enough."

"You dreamed," Halloway said. Not a question.

"Everyone dreams."

"Not like this." Halloway looked out at the water, at the place where the current bent around the bluff and went strange and slow, as if reluctant. "You dreamed in rooms you've never stood in. You knew where the doors were." A pause. "I did too, my first week."

Mirabel set the mug down on a crate with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it. "Dr. Halloway. I came to resupply you. I'd like to do that today, and then I'd like to take my boat home."

"Of course." Halloway smiled again, and there was nothing sinister in it—that was the worst part, the utter absence of menace, only a vast tired sorrow worn like a coat. "But first I want to show you what we found. Because you're going to dream about it either way, Captain. Better to see it awake."

* * *

**From the recovered journal of Dr. Edda Halloway, expedition lead — entry dated the ninth day after landfall:**

*We have stopped pretending we are surveying. I should write that plainly, because plainness is becoming scarce here.*

*The ruin lies below the bluff, submerged since the river changed course—Okafor estimates four centuries, maybe six. When the water is low you can see the tops of the walls through eight feet of current, laid out in streets, in squares, in a pattern too deliberate to be anything but a city. Wren's satellite imagery flagged it two years ago. He told us it was a mineral anomaly. He told us a great many things.*

*The stones are carved. Not decorated—carved the way a page is written. Okafor says the script resembles nothing in any corpus, but that is not the remarkable thing. The remarkable thing is what happens when you touch them. When you dive down and put your bare hand flat against the stone and hold it there.*

*It gives you something back.*

*I don't have better language yet. Vashti went first, on the third day, before we understood. She came up from her dive weeping and could not say why, and then over dinner she began describing her grandmother's kitchen—and Vashti has no grandmother living, and never had one who kept a kitchen like that, with the blue tiles and the copper pot and the smell of cardamom and smoke. She described it in detail. She described the woman who worked there, whose name was Ilse, and how Ilse hummed when she kneaded bread, and how the winter the river froze she'd—*

*Vashti remembers all of it. Every day. She says it is warmer than her own childhood, which she now has to work to recall. She says Ilse feels more real to her than her mother does.*

*We thought it was suggestion. Hypoxia, nitrogen, group hysteria—I wrote all the boring explanations in my field notes because I needed them to be true. Then I dove myself.*

*I held the stone for perhaps forty seconds. What came back was a harbor. Not this river—a harbor, salt wind, masts knocking, a man named Tomas shouting numbers down to me from a deck, and I was laughing, and my hands were young and scarred in ways mine are not. I lived an hour of his afternoon. I know how he took his coffee. I know that he was afraid of deep water and went to sea anyway, and I know the exact quality of his fear because for forty seconds it was mine.*

*Tomas has been dead four hundred years. I carry his afternoon now like a stone in my chest, and it does not fade. That is the thing no one tells you: it does not fade. Memory is supposed to decay. This doesn't. It sits in you whole and bright while your own life goes grey at the edges, and I am beginning to understand the mechanism—not chemically, not yet, but morally. The stones remember better than we do. They are greedy for it. Perhaps they were built to be. Perhaps the city drowned itself on purpose, the way a man might burn a library rather than let it be looted.*

*And we are the looters. We keep diving. God help me, we keep diving, because each memory is more vivid than our own lives, and we are all of us homesick for places we've never been.*

*Today Vashti called me by her sister's name and did not correct herself. Okafor has started keeping a list of things he is certain happened to him, and the list gets shorter every time he reviews it. Renn doesn't write anymore; he says he can't tell which hand is writing.*

*I have begun to be afraid not of losing my mind but of trading it away piece by piece, gladly, for other people's treasures. That is what this is. Not haunting. Commerce.*

*Tomorrow I will seal the site and radio for extraction. I swear it here, in ink, so that tomorrow-me cannot pretend today-me didn't insist.*

*—E.H.*

* * *

Mirabel stood at the edge of the bluff with the wind pulling at her collar, and looked down through the clear green water at the roofs of a dead city.

They were close—closer than any ruin had a right to be, eight feet down in a river that should have silted them over centuries ago. But the current bent around the site in that slow, deliberate way, and she understood suddenly that the water wasn't covering the city. It was keeping it. Polishing it. Holding it clean for whoever came next.

Halloway stood beside her, hands in pockets, letting her look.

"How long," Mirabel said, "have you been down here?"

"We arrived in spring." Halloway's voice was flat with honesty. "It's nearly autumn."

"And the radio?"

"Works fine." A long breath. "We stopped calling in because every report we drafted was a lie, and then we stopped being able to tell it was a lie, and then—" Halloway turned, and Mirabel saw that the scientist's eyes were red-rimmed, exhausted, human. "Captain Iversen. Do you know how you lose yourself? Not all at once. You lose yourself the way a river takes a bank. Grain by grain. One day you look down and the shore you stood on is a hundred miles downstream and you never heard a single grain go."

Below them, through the water, a carved lintel caught the sun and threw it back up in letters no alphabet claimed.

"Come down to the water," Halloway said gently. "Touch nothing. Just stand where we stand when we come up from a dive, and listen to us talk about what we've seen. Then decide whether you're taking my team anywhere—or whether they'd only carry it with them."

"They're your team, Doctor. Don't you get to order them home?"

"I ordered them home eleven days ago." Halloway laughed, and it was the worst sound Mirabel had heard yet—dry, rueful, entirely sane. "Nobody moved. Including me. I gave the order and then I went down and touched the harbor wall again, because I wanted to see Tomas's daughter's wedding. I *needed* to. Do you understand? It isn't addiction. It's worse. It's homesickness for a life that was never yours, and it fits better than your own."

Mirabel looked at the water a long time. Somewhere behind her, one of the scientists was humming a tune she almost recognized, and the almost was unbearable.

"What did you touch last?" she asked.

"A doorframe." Halloway's face went distant. "A woman named Amara was setting fish to dry on it, four hundred years ago, and she was thinking about leaving. About walking east until the river forgot her. She never did. But I know exactly how badly she wanted to, and Captain—" Halloway's voice cracked, just once, and reassembled itself. "I think about her wanting to leave every day. More than I think about my own daughter. I had a daughter. I'm fairly sure. There's a photograph in my kit and I recognize the love, but the face—" Halloway pressed the heels of both hands briefly against closed eyes. "The face is becoming a stranger wearing my feeling."

* * *

**From the recovered journal of Dr. Edda Halloway — final dated entry:**

*Sealed the site today. Or I wrote that I sealed it. Renn says I spent the morning on the bluff doing nothing, and I believe him, which means one of us is wrong and I can no longer determine which. This is what I feared most: not madness, but the loss of the referee. The part of you that rules on disputed memories. Mine has gone quiet, or been bought.*

*The supply boat came. Good. Someone from outside, someone with ballast—someone whose self hasn't been soaked in this water yet. If anyone can get us out, it's a person who still knows what she wants.*

*Except she hesitated at the gangplank tonight, and I watched her look at the water, and I saw the expression I see in my own mirror. Curiosity. The first grain off the bank.*

*Wren will come soon. I know it the way you know weather in a bad knee. He didn't send us to survey. He sent us to confirm, and confirmation is all he needs, and then he'll bring machines and men who don't ask questions, and they'll cut this city out of the river slab by slab and sell other people's souls by the kilogram.*

*I could sink the samples. I could end it. But I find I can't make my hands do it, and I no longer trust that the reluctance is mine.*

*If anyone reads this: the stones give, and giving is how they take. Whatever you loved, write it down now, in detail, and hide the paper, and check it daily against your heart. That's all the defense there is. Inventory. Vigilance. Grief.*

*I miss my daughter. I am choosing to write that sentence even though I can no longer fully picture her, because the missing is mine. The missing is mine. Say it until it holds.*

*—E.H.*

* * *

That evening Mirabel sat in the Bram's cabin with her logbook open and forced herself to do what Halloway's journal advised. She wrote down everything she could verify: the Bram's engine hours, Sef's real name and his mother's village, the color of her own front door, the debt on the boat, the route home in three legs with distances.

Then she turned to a fresh page and wrote: *My daughter's name is June. Her hair is—*

Her pen stopped.

Her hair is brown. Her hair is black. There was a gap between her front teeth, or there wasn't. She was born in a house with a blue door, or she was born in a hospital, and Mirabel could feel both versions sitting in her chest with equal weight, equal warmth, equal *certainty*, and one of them belonged to a woman four hundred years dead who had also loved a child named something like June.

Mirabel shut the logbook. Her hands were steady, which frightened her most of all—steady hands, a calm heart, the smooth surface of water over something drowning.

On deck, Sef was coiling line in the dark, and beyond him the scientists moved through their evening rituals around a fire they had finally lit, patient and polite, waiting for nothing, prepared to wait forever.

"Cap'n," Sef said quietly, without looking up. "How long we staying?"

Mirabel opened her mouth to say *we leave at dawn*—and heard, instead, in the pause before the words, a small treacherous voice asking what the water would show her if she went down to it, just once, just to look.

"Dawn," she said.

Behind them, far upriver, past the gorge, thunder rolled—though the sky held not a single cloud.
