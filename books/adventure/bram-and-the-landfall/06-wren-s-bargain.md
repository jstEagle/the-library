## 6. Wren's Bargain

The floatplane came out of the north on the fourth morning, and Mirabel heard it before she saw it—a droning wasp-sound swelling over the gorge until the water itself seemed to flinch beneath it.

She had been on the bluff with Halloway, watching the mist burn off the drowned city below. The stones down there had been quiet all night, or as quiet as they ever were; she had slept badly, dreaming in a voice that wasn't hers, a woman's voice counting children in a language that predated every alphabet Mirabel knew. She woke with salt on her lips and the taste of someone else's grief.

Now the plane banked over the gorge, twin floats flashing, and dropped low enough that its shadow crossed the camp like a hand passing over a candle.

"That's his," Halloway said. He said it without surprise. That was the worst part—how little surprised any of them were anymore. "He said he'd come when we found the deep chamber."

"You told him about the deep chamber." Mirabel kept her eyes on the plane. It was circling, hunting for water flat enough to land.

"I told him everything. That was the arrangement." Halloway turned to her, and his face did something complicated—the old academic courtesy stretched thin over whatever the stones had left of him. "Mirabel. You should understand. The arrangement was never about surveying."

The floatplane touched down on the long pool above the gorge with a violence of spray, taxied to the shingle beach, and shut down. Six men came off it in quick order, and they were not scientists. They wore gray field kit and carried carbines slung across their chests with the casualness of men who had carried them so long the weapons had become furniture. Behind them came Wren himself, stepping down onto the floats and then the wet stones as if the whole wilderness were a lobby he'd been shown into.

He looked better than he had at the charter office in Corvane. Rested. Fed. His coat was dry, his boots were dry, and there was not one thing about him that the river had touched.

"Captain Iversen." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had closed contracts for thirty years. "You found them. I never doubted you would. Though I confess I hoped you'd take longer about it—I'd have paid for the suspense."

"The check-ins stopped," Mirabel said. "Three days of dead air. I came up because your people went silent."

"They didn't go silent." Wren walked past her toward the camp, boots crunching, contractors fanning out along the treeline with the smooth economy of a machine engaging. "They went *deep*. There's a difference. Ask Dr. Halloway—he'll tell you the silence was the most productive period of his career."

Halloway said nothing. Around the cold fire pit, the other four sat where they always sat, motionless, faces turned toward the bluff as though listening to something under the earth. One of them, the young geologist whose name Mirabel could no longer be sure she'd ever learned, turned her head slowly to watch the contractors pass, and her eyes were full of a patience that had nothing human in it.

Wren stopped at the edge of the bluff and looked down at the water, clear as glass over the pale geometry of the ruins, and Mirabel watched his face while he looked. She had expected greed. What she saw was worse: recognition. The look of a man greeting an old idea finally made flesh.

"It's real," he said softly. "Fifty years my family poured money into rumors—drowned temples, memory-stones, river-cults that carved their dead into the bedrock so the flood couldn't take them twice. Every expedition came back with nothing, or came back wrong, or didn't come back." He glanced at her. "Do you know why I hired a supply captain instead of a salvage crew, Captain? Because a salvage crew would have known what to look for. You just wanted to get paid. Clean hands. No priors in the archive."

"You knew what this place does," Mirabel said. It wasn't a question. Her voice came out flat and strange, like someone else reporting weather.

"I knew what it *did*." Wren crouched, laid his palm flat against the bluff-top stone, almost tenderly. "My grandfather's journals called them witness-stones. The carvers believed memory was a substance—like water. It pools. It can be drawn off, stored, transferred. These aren't monuments, Captain. They're cisterns. And a cistern is only worth what you can do with what's inside."

* * *

They set up on the shingle by noon: generators, winches, a crane arm bolted to the floatplane's spare pontoon raft, dive lights strung along the shallows like festival lanterns. The contractors worked in silence, and the silence had a discipline to it that made Mirabel's skin crawl worse than shouting would have.

Wren walked the site with Halloway at his elbow, pointing, directing, and the orders came fast and certain—a man executing a plan drafted decades before anyone here was born.

"The core chamber first. The twelve seated figures. Cradle them, don't cut them—fracture degrades the imprint density, we learned that in '09." He caught Mirabel watching him and smiled again, that contract-closing smile. "Yes, '09. There was a team before yours, Captain. Before Halloway's. They got three stones out. Two arrived intact. My father spent forty years trying to read them."

"And?"

"And he died trying." Wren said it lightly, the way a man mentions bad weather. "The imprints don't transfer cleanly through stone alone. They need proximity, repetition, immersion—that's why the site works, that's why the river matters. But we've refined the method since. A fragment held against the temple for eleven minutes, sustained concentration—" he tapped his own skull, twice, playfully, "—and you can pour a life in. Or pour one out. Imagine it, Captain. Education in an afternoon. Confession without interrogation. Skill without apprenticeship. Grief, sold back to the grieving at a fair market price. Or the reverse of all of it. Emptying a man of everything he is and shipping the container wherever we like."

He said *we*, and Mirabel understood that the word included her, the way a net includes the fish.

"You're going to strip the site," she said.

"I'm going to *harvest* it. Stripping implies loss." He gestured at the rising waterline, the brown stain on the bluff marking how far the river had climbed since her arrival. "The landfall flood comes early this year—you've seen the rain, you've felt the barometer. When it seals this gorge, the site drowns for another century and my family waits another generation. Not acceptable. We have nine days, maybe ten. Everything load-bearing comes out before then."

"And these five?" She nodded toward the scientists around the fire. "Are they load-bearing?"

Wren considered her with genuine curiosity, the way a man looks at an animal that has spoken.

"They're proof of concept, Captain. They're also employees, and their contracts cover occupational hazard." He softened, or performed softening. "We'll bring them home. Best care. Whatever's left of them will want for nothing."

* * *

It happened that evening, at the edge of the dive lights, when the contractors' generator noise had settled into its drone and the camp had become two camps—one working, one waiting.

Mirabel went to her bunk in the Bram's cabin to escape it, and lay down, and did what she had done every night since Corvane: she took out the memory of her daughter and held it up against the dark.

This was her discipline. Her private liturgy. Lira, seven years old, standing on the dock at Tarrow's Bend in the yellow rain slicker with the torn pocket, squinting into the sun, missing front tooth, hair escaping its braid—

The braid.

Mirabel lay still in the dark and felt the vertigo open beneath her like water giving way.

Lira had never worn braids. Lira had refused braids from the age of four—screamed about them, cried about them, worn her hair loose and wild as a protest she maintained to the end of her short list of certainties. Mirabel had fought that battle a hundred mornings and lost it a hundred times, and loved losing it.

But the memory showed a braid. Neatly done, tied with blue ribbon. And behind the image, attached to it now like a burr, came details she had never lived: the smell of the ribbon dye between Mirabel's fingers as she tied it; the specific weight of the child's head tilted trustingly against her knee; a whole small morning of compliance that had never, ever happened.

Someone else's daughter. Someone else's morning. Some woman centuries drowned who had braided her child's hair by this river, and whose tenderness had soaked into the stone, and which had seeped up through the bluff, through the camp, through the hull of the Bram, and settled into the holiest room Mirabel owned—the room behind her eyes where Lira lived.

They had put a stranger in her daughter's face.

Mirabel rolled off the bunk and made it to the rail before she was sick, hanging over the black water, gripping the steel until her knuckles burned. Below her, faint through the hull, she could feel the river moving against the stones, patient as breath.

When she straightened, Wren was standing on the shingle below, looking up at her, and in the dive light his expression was not unkind.

"Now you understand," he called quietly. "That's what it costs to stand this close. That's what it *earns*." He spread his hands, salesman and priest both. "Everything you are, Captain—every love, every loss—it's all just cargo. And cargo can be moved."

Mirabel looked down at him, and the fury that rose in her was so clean and so total that it frightened her more than the vertigo had—because underneath it, steady as ballast, she could already hear herself planning. The retaining wall on the gorge's south flank, old and cracked, holding back the high channel. The flood coming days early. Nine days, Wren had said.

She gave him three.

Upstream, thunder spoke in the mountains, and the river rose a finger's width against the Bram's hull, as if it had been listening too.
