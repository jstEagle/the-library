## 7. The Rising

The rain began in the night, and by morning it had stopped being weather and become a fact.

Mirabel stood on the Bram's deck with her collar up, watching the river climb the bank in slow, greedy inches. The Vessel had turned the color of strong tea, thick with silt torn from a hundred unseen gullies, and it moved now with a muscular purpose that hadn't been there two days ago. Debris rode the current—branches, whole trees, a section of fence post that meant someone's pasture was underwater somewhere far downstream, in a country that still believed in fences.

"Early," Sef said beside her. He didn't say more than that. He never did.

"The landfall's not due for another week." She'd been running this river long enough to feel its calendar in her bones—the dry weeks when the headwaters dozed, then the first storms of the season, then the great swelling that came down the gorge like a held breath finally released. The landfall flood was as reliable as grief. It did not come early.

"It's coming early." Sef spat over the rail. "Everything up here comes early or late or sideways. Nothing here keeps honest time."

She thought of Halloway sitting at the cold fire, greeting her with a name that fit better than her own. She thought of the stone under the bluff, and the way her memory of her daughter's face had begun to accumulate details she had never lived—a scar on the left eyebrow she knew wasn't there, a birthday in a month that was wrong, wrong, and yet so firmly seated now that arguing with it felt like arguing with a wall.

The rain fell. The river rose. Somewhere below them, Wren's contractors were loading stones onto a barge as if the water would wait for their schedule.

* * *

By noon the camp had dissolved into organized chaos. Wren ran his operation the way he did everything—quietly, precisely, and with the assumption that everything within reach was already his. The barge sat low against the bank below the bluff, its deck stacked with crated fragments wrapped in canvas, and a winch line ran down into the flooded shallows where divers worked the submerged ruin in shifts.

Mirabel watched from the Bram's wheelhouse, ostensibly checking her mooring lines. Actually counting.

Four crates an hour. At that rate they'd have the core stones—Halloway called them the *keystones*, said they were older than the rest, said *older* in a voice that made the word sound like a wound—in three days. The flood, if it kept its new pace, would arrive in two.

Wren appeared at the rail of the barge, dry under an umbrella held by one of his men, and looked across the water at her boat with the mild expression of a man checking inventory. He raised one hand—not quite a wave, not quite a summons. An acknowledgment that she existed, and that existing, for the moment, suited him.

She raised two fingers back and turned away before her face could say what it wanted to say.

* * *

It was Voss who cracked first.

He came down to the Bram in the late afternoon, wading the last stretch through water that had been dry ground that morning, and he was shaking—not from cold, though the rain had a knife-edge to it, but from something deeper. He was the youngest of the five, a hydrologist who'd signed on because the headwaters were unmapped and unmapped things were, to him, holy.

"Take me out," he said. "Please. Take me and Okafor."

Mirabel stood in the wheelhouse doorway and studied him. His eyes were red-rimmed. His hands wouldn't stay still.

"You know what I can carry," she said gently. "You, Okafor, Sef, me. That's all the Bram is, with the fuel we've got."

"I don't care about the equipment. I don't care about—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I don't know what I care about anymore. That's the thing. That's the whole thing, Mirabel. I sat down at that fire six weeks ago and I have been trying ever since to find the seam between what happened to me and what happened to somebody else four hundred years ago, and there isn't one. There isn't a seam. It's all just—it's all just *in* me now, and some of it is drowning, and I can't tell whose drowning it is."

The rain drummed on the wheelhouse roof. Downriver, faint through the gray, came the sound of the winch, patient as a heartbeat.

"Okafor too?"

"Okafor worse. Okafor's been standing at the waterline every morning saying goodbye to people she never met. Real goodbyes. Crying over them." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "And the worst part—the absolute worst part—is that I remember her mother. Her dead mother, from three centuries back, like she was mine. I grieve her at breakfast. Every day. I grieve a woman I never saw because a rock told me to."

Mirabel reached out and gripped his shoulder. Under her hand he was vibrating like a struck wire.

"Be aboard before dawn," she said. "Quietly. Don't tell Halloway. Don't tell anyone."

"And the others?"

She looked upriver, toward the bluff, toward the ruin beneath its skin of brown water, and found she couldn't answer honestly, because the honest answer was: *I don't know yet whether I'm saving anyone or just choosing who gets to forget.*

"Dawn," she said again.

* * *

Sef found her that evening in the stern, coiling line that didn't need coiling. He leaned against the rail and watched the river a while before he spoke.

"Barge grounded."

She went still. "Grounded where?"

"Half a mile down. Bar came up overnight—right in the channel, right where the chart says twelve feet of nothing." He tapped the rail. "I sounded it myself this morning, before the rain got serious. Wasn't there. Now it's holding forty tons of Wren's ambition on top of it like a fly in amber."

"A bar that wasn't there yesterday."

"A bar that wasn't there *ever*." He turned to look at her, and in the failing light his face was hard to read, which was unusual; Sef's face was usually the easiest thing on the boat. "You've seen what this river does up here. You've seen what those rocks do. And you're telling me you believe in coincidence."

"I'm not telling you anything."

"You're not telling me a lot of things very loudly." He shifted, and his voice dropped. "I loaded crates for that man, Mirabel. Crates marked with coordinates past the last legal claim, way back in chapter two of this bad idea. I told myself it was pay. I told myself it was your call and my job was to keep the bilge dry. But I've been watching those divers come up out of that water looking like men who've been robbed, and I've been listening to that scientist cry about mothers she never had, and I'm done telling myself things."

"Sef—"

"I'm not asking for a speech. I'm asking which side of this boat you're standing on. Because tomorrow morning, when you take Voss and Okafor downriver—and you are going to take them, I can see it sitting behind your eyes like ballast—somebody's going to have to be on this deck, and somebody's going to have to decide what happens to that barge full of stolen dead people's heads."

The rain came harder. Out on the black water, lightning walked silently along the far ridge, counting the hills.

Mirabel looked at her old deckhand—her father's deckhand before that, a man who had taught her to read water before she could read words—and understood that whatever she said next would be the truest thing either of them heard all week.

"There's a retaining wall under the bluff," she said. "Old stone. Halloway showed me on the sonar—it's what's keeping the ruin from silting over completely. It's also what's keeping the deep channel pinned against the east bank." She let that sit. "If the wall goes, the flood takes the site. All of it. Buries it under twenty feet of gravel and moving water before Wren's crane could lift another crate."

Sef was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at a channel marker that confirmed what he'd already read from the current.

"That bar," he said, "that wasn't there yesterday."

"What about it?"

"I think the river's on your side." He pushed off the rail and headed for the engine hatch. "Get some sleep, Captain. Dawn comes early, and everything up here comes early."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and the rain hammering the glass, and she took stock of herself the way she'd take stock of a hold—item by item, testing each thing for weight and honesty.

Her name. Still hers, mostly. Her hands, her scars, the ache in her left knee from a fall on the Kettle Rapids fifteen years ago—that was hers, verified, load-bearing. Her daughter—

Her daughter's face surfaced unbidden, and she examined it like a suspicious bill. The scar on the left eyebrow: not hers, not real, planted. The birthday in the wrong month: planted. But underneath the false details, the true face persisted, dimmed but present, the way a coin persists under silt. She could still find it if she dug. For now.

For how long? Another week of this water, this air, these stones? Would she stand on her own deck one morning and mourn a stranger's mother at breakfast, unable to say why?

Outside, the river rose another visible inch against the pilings, and she felt something in her chest shift—from dread into something harder. The flood was coming early. Wren's schedule was built on a flood that would arrive politely, on time, after the loading was done. But rivers did not keep appointments, and neither, tonight, would she.

Two scientists at dawn. A barge aground on a lie. A wall of old stone waiting under brown water for someone to give it permission to fail.

Mirabel turned up the lamp, checked her fuel gauge, and began, quietly and without ceremony, to plan the destruction of the only proof that any of them had ever been who they were.

Downriver, through the rain, the barge groaned against its sandbar—a long, wooden complaint, like something being warned.
