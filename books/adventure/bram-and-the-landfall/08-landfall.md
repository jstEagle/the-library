## 8. Landfall

The rain came down like a verdict.

Mirabel stood at the wheel of the *Bram* with both hands locked on the spokes and the gorge roaring ahead of her, a black seam in the drowning world where the Vessel River squeezed itself between walls of wet stone. The storm had arrived three days early, the way the old-timers said it sometimes did when the landfall flood came angry. The water had risen a fathom since dawn. It moved now with a sound she had never heard from a river before—not the rush of current but something closer to breathing, long and deep and patient, as if the whole valley had filled its lungs.

Behind her, the deck was chaos. Sef had the scientists lashed to the cargo ring—Halloway among them, hollow-eyed, his journal strapped to his chest like a life vest made of paper. Two of Wren's contractors had come aboard at gunpoint's distance and then thought better of it when the barge grounded; they crouched now near the stern winch, hands white on the rail, no longer men with orders but men with a river.

"Bar's moving!" Sef shouted over the rain. "The one that took their barge—it's shifted! I can see it breaking up!"

Of course it is, Mirabel thought. The river was rearranging itself again, the way it had all the way up, sandbars walking like animals, channels closing and opening as though the water were a hand closing around something it meant to keep. She had spent thirty years reading this river's moods, and every instinct she owned told her the same thing: the gorge would not stay open much longer. When the surge peaked, the whole headwater country would go under—the camp, the bluff, and everything beneath the bluff.

Everything beneath the bluff.

She looked back once, only once, toward the ruin.

* * *

They had gone down to it in the grey hour before the storm broke, Mirabel and Halloway, while Wren's men hauled crates up the switchback trail. The retaining wall stood at the waterline below the bluff—a curve of fitted stone older than any nation, slick with rain, holding back the flood-stage river from the drowned galleries behind it. Through gaps in the masonry she could see carved faces under the water, serene and terrible, their mouths open as if mid-word. The stones that remembered. The stones that stole.

Wren wanted the core stones. Twelve of them, each taller than a man, set into the wall itself like teeth in a jaw. His contractors had rigged slings and a crane barge, and even now, even with the sky falling, they were working—chains groaning, floodlights stuttering against the rain, the first core stone half-lifted and swinging above the water like a hanged man.

"He'll get them out," Halloway had said beside her, shouting against the wind. "If the wall holds through the surge, he'll have them on the barge by morning. And then everyone who ever stands near them—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Mirabel had watched him try to remember his mother's face for an hour the night before, and watched him fail, and watched him weep without knowing what he grieved for.

She had put her hand flat against the wet retaining wall, feeling the cold of it climb her arm, feeling—she could not deny it anymore—faint pressures behind her eyes, images that were not hers pressing at the edges of her mind like faces at a window. A woman braiding rope on a dock that existed nowhere on any chart. A child's voice calling a name in a language dead a thousand years.

Her daughter's face. Even that. Even that had details in it now she had never lived—a scar on the left cheek that Ada did not have, a house behind her that Mirabel had never entered. The ruin had gotten into her memories the way floodwater gets into a hull: silently, everywhere, rising.

She had looked at Halloway, and he had looked at her, and neither of them had needed to say it aloud.

Some things should be buried.

* * *

Now she ran the *Bram* at the gorge with the throttle wide open.

The channel was a rumor. Where the chart showed forty feet of depth there were boils and whirls; where it showed rock walls there was nothing visible at all, just brown violence and rain. She steered by feel, by the tilt of the hull, by the way the spray blew off the standing waves—and by something else, something new, some borrowed knowledge whispering turn here, trust me, and she hated herself a little for trusting it, and turned anyway.

The boat leapt the first standing wave and slammed down so hard the deck plates rang. Sef whooped—not joy, exactly, but the sound a man makes when fear gets too large to hold quietly.

"Wren's still loading!" one of the contractors screamed from the stern. "He won't stop! He says the surge will pass!"

"The surge won't pass," Mirabel said to the wheel. "The surge is the point."

Through the driving rain astern she could see the work lights of the crane barge, see the first core stone swaying in its sling, see Aldous Wren standing on the barge deck in his ruined good coat, pointing, directing, insistent as a man arguing with God. He had tripled her pay once. He had smiled at her across a chart table and called the headwaters an opportunity. Now he stood in the mouth of the thing he'd tried to buy, and he still believed he owned it.

The second wave took the *Bram* broadside and threw her sideways twenty feet. Mirabel rode it, corrected, held her. The gorge walls narrowed. Debris came downriver—whole trees, tent canvas, a section of walkway from the camp—and she threaded them the way a needle threads cloth, because stopping was dying and hesitation was stopping.

"Sef!" she roared. "When we clear the narrows, hard about! We're going back along the bluff!"

Sef stared at her through the rain. "Back? Captain, that water's—"

"I know what it is." Her voice cracked like the thunder overhead. "There's a wall down there holding back the ruin, and if it's standing when the flood crests, Wren strips the site and carries it out piece by piece, and everything those stones took from us goes out into the world to take from everyone else. Forever." She met his eyes. "I'm breaching it."

For a heartbeat Sef said nothing. Then he grinned—wild, terrified, loyal—and spat over the side for luck. "Thirty years I've waited to watch you do something insane," he shouted. "Hard about it is."

* * *

They came around the bluff's shoulder into the flooded shallows where the survey camp had been, and found it already going under—tents collapsed into brown water, instruments blinking their last beneath the surface, the cold fire pit dissolved into river. The crane barge lay aground on the bar that hadn't been there yesterday, listing hard, its crane arm bucking against the load. The first core stone hung in its sling above the flood, turning slowly, its carved face catching the lightning: serene, patient, watching.

Wren saw the *Bram* coming and waved his arms, mistaking rescue for arrival.

"Captain!" he bellowed across the water. "Take the stone aboard! There's bonus money in it—triple again, do you hear me? We lift it clear and the rest can drown for all I care!"

Mirabel brought the *Bram* alongside the grounded barge, close enough to shout, close enough to see the rain running off his face, close enough to see that his eyes had changed. Somewhere in these weeks among the stones, Aldous Wren had taken memories too—or bought them, or traded for them—and what looked out of him now was not entirely a living man's hunger. It was older than that. It was the hunger the carvers had built into the rock.

"You knew," she shouted back. "You always knew what they do."

"They're worth more than you'll earn in ten lifetimes!" He was climbing onto the barge rail, reaching for the sling cable, one hand out toward the turning stone. "One touch, captain—one touch and you'd understand—"

The surge hit.

It came down the gorge like a train, a wall of water two boats high, and it struck the grounded barge amidships. Timbers shrieked. The crane arm whipped. Men went down screaming, and the sling cable parted with a crack like a rifle shot, and the first core stone dropped into the flood and vanished, taking its serenity down into the dark.

Wren went to his knees on the tilting deck. A lifeline had come loose and trailed in the water beside him—orange rope, bright, alive, the single most ordinary object in the world. Mirabel saw it. Sef saw it. Any man in his right mind would have grabbed it.

Aldous Wren reached instead for the second core stone, still chained to the barge's deck, and pressed both palms flat against its carved face.

Mirabel would remember that image for the rest of her life, whatever remained of it: a rich man kneeling in the flood, embracing the thing that was eating him, smiling as the water closed over his shoulders—as if he finally understood what ownership meant, and it was the stones that owned him.

"Bram!" Sef screamed. "The wall! Go!"

* * *

She drove the *Bram*'s bow straight at the retaining wall.

The old masonry had held for centuries against ordinary floods, but it had never faced the *Bram* at full throttle with a landfall surge behind her. The impact was enormous—a detonation of timber and stone and spray that threw Mirabel against the wheel and knocked the breath out of the world. The hull ground forward through collapsing rock, and behind the breach the drowned galleries opened their mouths, and the river poured in.

The ruin drank the flood.

Water thundered through the gap, filling the galleries, toppling pillars, silencing the carved faces one by one as the brown flood rose over them. The pressure wave sucked at the *Bram*, dragging her stern toward the breach, and for one endless moment Mirabel fought the wheel with everything her arms had, hauling the boat off the rocks as the masonry folded down around them like a jaw closing.

Then they were free, swept out into the main channel, spinning, shipping water, alive.

Astern, the gorge swallowed itself. The crane barge went under whole, chains and stones and all. The last thing visible above the flood was the tip of the highest carved pillar, a single dark finger pointing at the sky—and then the river took that too, and the headwater country was only water, featureless and final, hiding what it meant to hide.

Sef stood beside her, streaming, laughing and crying at once. "It's done," he kept saying. "Captain, it's done, it's done."

Mirabel looked at the boiling water where Wren had gone down, and felt—she searched herself for grief, for horror, for triumph—and found instead a strange, quiet emptiness, like a room after the furniture's been carried out. She had chosen oblivion over ownership. She had fed her own stolen memories to the flood along with everyone else's, the false daughter, the borrowed dead, the whole haunted archive of the place. What was hers would remain. What was theirs was buried.

That was the bargain. That was the price. She paid it and did not look back.

Except—

Her hand, without her asking it, had already gone to her coat pocket, where a fragment the size of a fist sat wrapped in oilcloth. Broken loose from the wall in the collision, or taken—she honestly could not remember which. Small. Heavy. Warm, even in the rain.

The *Bram* limped downriver into the dark, riding the crest of the landfall flood, carrying five passengers who no longer remembered why they had come, and one secret in the bilge that Mirabel Iversen had not yet decided whether she owned, or it owned her.
