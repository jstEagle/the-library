## 9. Passengers Without Luggage

The Bram ran south with the flood at her back, and the river that had fought them going up seemed almost eager to be rid of them now. Mirabel stood at the wheel with her hands light on the spokes and let the current do the arguing, because she had no strength left in her arms for anything but steering. Behind her, the deck was crowded with people who moved like passengers on a train through country they'd never chosen—sitting where they were put, eating when food appeared, watching the banks slide past with expressions of polite, unfocused attention.

Seven of them. Herself, Sef, Dr. Halloway, and four of the five scientists. The fifth had stayed behind with Wren's drowned ambition, buried under forty feet of brown water, and no one spoke about him because no one could remember his face.

That was the thing Mirabel kept circling back to as the gorge fell away astern and the river widened into its middle reaches: they had come upriver as a crew of eleven, and now they were seven, and not one of them could say what had happened to the other four. Not one of them could say much of anything.

She tried it herself, quietly, running the voyage backward like a ledger. She had left Corvane Landing on a Tuesday—she was sure of the Tuesday, sure of the smell of tar and diesel on the dock, sure of Sef's grin when she'd told him triple pay. After that the ledger went soft. There was a cache ransacked from the inside, or maybe she'd only heard about a cache. There was a camp at dusk, a cold fire, a woman named Halloway saying a name that fit her like a coat. There was rain, and a barge aground, and a wall of old stone giving way under her crowbar while the flood came down the gorge like God clearing His throat. Those pieces existed. She could hold each one up to the light. But between them lay gaps where days should have been, whole stretches of river unaccounted for, and when she reached into those gaps her memory came back smooth as water over a dropped stone—as if something had skimmed the surface clean rather than torn anything out.

She knew what tearing felt like. That was the terrible part. She knew there had been things taken, because she could feel the shapes of them, the way you feel a tooth that isn't there anymore. Her daughter's face—she had thought about her daughter's face every day for six years, and now when she reached for it she found a portrait painted by someone else's hand. The eyes were right. The gap in the front teeth was right. But the girl in the memory was standing in a garden Mirabel had never owned, wearing a dress she'd never bought, and when Mirabel tried to move the scene—to turn the girl around, to see the yard behind her—the image resisted, fixed and finished, like a photograph instead of a life.

*Whose garden?* she wondered, for the hundredth time since the gorge. *Whose daughter is standing in your head, Iversen?*

"Sandbar," Sef said beside her, not looking up from the depth line he was coiling. "Port side, fifty yards. She'll want to swing wide."

Mirabel swung wide. She hadn't seen the bar yet herself; the water over it was the same tea-brown as everything else. But Sef called bars the way other men called weather, and she trusted him, and she put the Bram where he said and felt the faint drag of shallows slide past her hull without touching.

It wasn't until an hour later that it occurred to her to wonder how Sef had known. He'd been below decks when they passed that stretch going up. He'd never seen that bar. Neither had she.

She looked at him. He looked at the river. Neither of them said anything, and the silence between them had the weight of a shared confession neither dared to make aloud.

* *

They made their first night's anchorage in the lee of Cutter's Bluff, sixty miles down, where the channel ran deep and predictable and a body could almost believe the world was still mapped. Mirabel got a stove going in the pilothouse and put coffee and tinned stew in front of everyone, and they ate in a silence that wasn't hostile, exactly. It was the silence of strangers thrown together by a wreck, except none of them could remember the wreck.

One of the scientists—a young man named Pell, who had a burn scar on his forearm that he kept touching as though checking whether it still belonged to him—finally set down his tin cup and said, "Does anyone else—" and stopped.

"Say it," Halloway said. She sat nearest the stove, wrapped in a blanket Mirabel had found in the forward locker, and she looked older than the woman who had greeted Mirabel by another name at dusk a lifetime ago. Her hair had gone fully gray in the space of days. Her hands were steady, though. Whatever else the stones had done to her, they had left her hands steady.

"I can't account for the trip," Pell said. "Any of it. I know we went up the Vessel. I know we were surveying. I have—I have notes." He patted a waterproof satchel at his hip the way a child pats a pocket holding something precious. "But I don't remember writing them. And some of them aren't in my handwriting."

"Show me," Halloway said.

He spread the notebooks on the deck plates, weighted against the breeze off the water. Mirabel leaned over them with the rest. The pages were dense, meticulous, dated in sequence—and wrong. The handwriting shifted mid-entry, sometimes mid-sentence, looping into a slanted script none of them recognized, then back again. One entry described taking soundings at a bend of the river that Mirabel knew did not exist. Another, dated three days after they'd all been at the camp, read simply, in the stranger's hand: *the water remembers being sky. ask the pilot. she'll understand.*

Nobody asked the pilot anything. Mirabel kept her eyes on the page and her face very still.

"It's like luggage," said the woman scientist, Okafor, speaking for the first time all evening. She was a hydrologist, Mirabel remembered—or someone remembered, and had left the fact lying around where Okafor could find it. "We're traveling without our luggage. We arrived with nothing but what we're wearing." She turned her cup slowly on its base. "I keep waiting for it to come back. The missing parts. Like jet lag, like waking from anesthesia. But it doesn't come back, does it. It's just gone, and we're just—we're carrying these holes around, and the holes don't even hurt. That's the worst part. They don't hurt."

"They will," Halloway said quietly. "Later. When you reach for something and find it's not there. It hasn't started hurting yet because you haven't needed any of it." She looked up, and her eyes found Mirabel's across the pilothouse. "Some of us already have."

Mirabel held the look for a moment, then turned away and busied herself with the stove. She did not want to talk about holes. She had a hole shaped like a garden, shaped like a girl's laugh, shaped like six years of Sundays, and she had learned in the last two days that if she let herself stand at the edge of it and look down, she would start screaming, and she could not afford to scream. There were seven souls on this boat and sixty miles of floodwater behind them and a sponsor's company that would send more planes, more men, more questions. Screaming was a luxury.

* *

On the third day downriver, with the banks gone familiar and the current lazy, Halloway came to the pilothouse and stood beside Mirabel at the wheel for a long time without speaking. The others were asleep or pretending to be. The river ran gold with late afternoon.

"You scuttled it," Halloway said at last. It wasn't quite a question.

"I sank a wall," Mirabel said. "The flood did the rest."

"You know what I mean." Halloway watched the water. "The site. The stones. Everything Wren wanted. You ended it."

"The flood ended it. I just declined to stop it."

Halloway smiled faintly at that, and for a moment Mirabel saw the woman she must have been before the headwaters—sharp, funny, the kind of scientist who'd have argued cheerfully with God about the design of a coastline. Then the smile faded and something rawer came through underneath.

"I've been trying to reconstruct it," Halloway said. "What we found. What it was. I have fragments. Impressions. I remember kneeling on wet stone in the dark, and I remember weeping—not sadness, exactly, more like recognition. Like meeting someone I'd been mourning my whole life without knowing it." She shook her head slowly. "And I don't know whose grief that was, Mirabel. Mine? Some mason's who cut those blocks four thousand years ago? A king's? I stood in that place and I was full of other people, and now that it's gone I'm empty of everybody, including myself. So I'm asking you, because you were the last clear mind on that bluff." She turned and faced her directly. "What did we find?"

The question hung in the warm air of the pilothouse. Mirabel gripped the wheel and felt the river answer through the wood, the small patient shudder of the current talking to the hull.

And she heard herself say, in a voice flat and easy as a dockhand's: "Nothing worth the trip."

It came out smoothly. It came out rehearsed, though she had never rehearsed it. It came out like a story she'd decided on somewhere below the level of deciding, and she understood, with a cold sliding feeling in her chest, that some part of her had built this sentence days ago and fitted it to her mouth like a key to a lock—because it was the sentence that would end the conversation, protect the boat, protect the crew, protect whatever it was she was protecting.

Halloway studied her for a long moment. She was not a fool; she had spent her life reading strata, reading what lay beneath surfaces. "Nothing worth the trip," she repeated.

"That's right."

"And the four who didn't come back?"

"Flood took them. I couldn't reach them." Also smooth. Also possibly true. She genuinely did not know.

Halloway nodded slowly, and Mirabel saw her decide to accept it—not because she believed it, but because she was too hollowed out to fight, and because some instinct in her understood that the pilot was holding something shut the way you hold a door closed against a wind. She gathered her blanket around her shoulders and went aft to the others, and Mirabel stood alone at the wheel with the lie sitting in her mouth like a coin.

Below her feet, under the deck plates, wedged in the bilge stringer where she'd wrapped it in oilcloth and lashed it with wire, was a piece of the bluff. A fragment of carved stone no bigger than a prayer book, heavy as sin, cool even in the engine heat. She had taken it in the last minutes on the collapsing wall—taken it without letting herself think, the way a drowning woman grabs at driftwood—and she had told herself it was evidence, leverage, insurance against Wren's company coming after them with lawyers and worse.

She had known, even then, that she was lying to herself.

It was a memory. That was all the stones were—that was the whole obscene miracle of them—and the fragment in her bilge was a memory pressed into mineral like a flower in a book. She hadn't dared touch the carving since the gorge. She hadn't dared find out whose life slept in it, or what it would do to hers when it woke. But every time she walked over that section of deck she felt it, the way you feel a letter unopened in your coat pocket. A hole in her, shaped like truth, and the truth folded up small and hidden in the dark where the water sloshed.

Sef came up the companionway with two cups of coffee and handed her one. He glanced at the deck plates, just once, in the place where nothing was visible, and then out at the river, and said nothing at all.

Which meant he knew. Or suspected. Or had his own fragment hidden somewhere she hadn't found.

The Bram ran on toward Corvane Landing, seven souls lighter than she'd come, carrying cargo no manifest would ever list—and in the bilge, riding quiet as ballast, the one stone the flood had failed to take.
