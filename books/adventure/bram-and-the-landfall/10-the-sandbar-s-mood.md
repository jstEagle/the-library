## 10. The Sandbar's Mood

The Vessel River below the gorge was a different animal than the one that had swallowed her summer, and Mirabel Iversen had spent six weeks learning to trust it again—or pretending to.

She stood at the wheel of the *Bram* in the gray light before dawn, one hand on the spoke, the other around a tin cup of coffee gone cold an hour ago. The river ran wide and brown and patient here, the way it always did above Kettering Bend, carrying silt from a hundred nameless creeks toward the delta. A heron lifted off the shallows on legs like folded umbrellas. Somewhere upstream a log jam groaned as it settled. Ordinary sounds. River sounds. She catalogued them the way she'd done for twenty years, the way her father had taught her before he taught her anything else: read the water like a face, and it will tell you what it's thinking.

But lately the water told her things she hadn't asked to know.

It had started small, three days after they'd come down out of the headwaters with a boat full of people who couldn't say why they'd gone. She'd been running the Narrows at Kessel Point with the morning fog still on the water when she'd put the wheel over hard to starboard without knowing why—and thirty seconds later the channel had swung left where every chart in her cabin swore it ran right, and a sandbar the color of wet bone had broken the surface exactly where she would have been. Sef had looked at her like she'd grown a second set of eyes. She'd looked at herself the same way.

It kept happening. Not often. Once or twice a week, maybe. A feeling in her hands before her eyes confirmed it—the river gathering itself for a turn, a bar building where none had been, a snag rolling off a bank into the fairway. Knowledge arriving whole, unearned, like remembering something she'd never learned. And each time it came, she felt the same cold finger trace down her spine, because she knew where knowledge like that came from. She knew what kind of stone pressed its memories into whoever lingered too long.

*You're clean,* she told herself most mornings, standing right here at this wheel. *You got out. You scuttled it. Whatever's in you is just—residue.*

Residue. That was the word she'd settled on, because the alternatives were worse.

* * *

Sef came up from the engine room at midmorning wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime last year. He didn't ask if he could take the wheel beside her; he never did anymore. Since the landfall he'd taken to standing near her the way men stand near a stove in winter—not for warmth exactly, but for the proof of it.

"Fuel's good," he said. "Bilge is—" He stopped. She watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, and watched him lose. "Bilge is fine."

"Bilge is fine," she agreed.

They both knew what lived in the bilge. They'd known since the second week home, when she'd gone down with a lantern to chase a leak that wasn't there and found it instead: a fragment of carved stone no bigger than a loaf of bread, wedged behind the fuel tank where she had no memory of putting it. Grey-green, dense as sin, its surface crawling with lines of script that seemed to rearrange themselves when she wasn't looking directly at them—though she'd learned by then that *not looking directly* was a skill you lost faster than you gained it.

She should have dropped it overboard that first night. She knew that. Instead she'd sat on the engine room sole with the thing in her lap for two hours, turning it, letting its weight argue with her fear, and in the end she'd wrapped it in canvas and put it back. Because holding it was the only time the hole inside her had edges. Because somewhere under all that alien script was a single memory that wasn't hers and wasn't anyone's—a woman's voice singing in a language with no living speakers, a song about a river, about waiting, about a door that opens only underwater—and it was the closest thing she had to proof that what happened in the gorge had happened at all. That Wren had drowned reaching for beauty instead of a rope. That she had chosen, deliberately, with open eyes, to drown a city rather than let it be owned.

Everyone else had come home hollowed. Halloway and his four survivors sat in a boarding house in Kettering, polite and puzzled, unable to account for eleven weeks of their lives, asking her gently recurring questions—*what did we find, Mirabel? was there anything worth the trip?*—and hearing her say, each time, *nothing worth the trip,* and watching them nod and drift back to their tea. The sponsors' lawyers had come sniffing; there was nothing to find. No coordinates worth the paper. No stones. No Aldous Wren—the company had disowned him so fast his name was already coming off the letterheads. An expedition that vanished into rumor. Five scientists who remembered nothing. A charter captain who told the same flat story until everyone stopped asking.

Only the stone remembered. Only the stone knew she existed as she had been.

And that, she thought now, watching the current fold around the Bram's bow, was the trap she'd been living in for six weeks: keep the fragment, and keep the proof, and keep the leak in her hull through which the drowned city's knowing seeped into her blood. Throw it away, and be free—but free of what? Free of the evidence. Free of the choice. Free to become someone who might, someday, half-believe she'd dreamed the whole thing.

Her father's voice, in memory or in residue, she honestly couldn't tell anymore: *A river doesn't forgive, girl. It only forgets. Learn the difference.*

* * *

The sandbar came into view a little past noon.

She'd have known it blindfolded. It lay at the mouth of the first unnamed creek above Kessel Point, a long pale tongue of sand that shifted with every flood season, and it was here—right here, in charted water, in water she'd run a hundred times—that the river had lied to her for the first time. Chapter three of the voyage, though she no longer counted chapters; counting required a self doing the counting, and hers had developed gaps. But she remembered this bar. Here the channel had run two fathoms deep on Monday and six inches on Tuesday, and here she'd grounded the Bram for half a day while Sef cursed and poled and the wilderness had felt, for the first time, not indifferent but *attentive*. Deliberate. A river rearranging itself to keep something hidden.

Now the bar lay low and golden in the autumn light, gulls standing along its spine like punctuation, and the channel bent around it in a long clean curve that her hands knew without consulting her eyes.

She brought the Bram in slow and dropped anchor in the lee, where the current slackened into a brown eddy full of drifting leaves. Sef appeared at her elbow without being called.

"This is it," he said. Not a question.

"This is it."

He looked at the bar a long moment. "You know," he said, "I've been meaning to tell you. I don't remember the headwaters. Not any of it. I remember loading crates in Kettering and I remember coming downriver after, and between the two there's just—" he made a shape in the air with his rag, something torn, "—a hole. And I've decided I can live with the hole. What I can't live with is wondering what else you're keeping down in that bilge besides ballast."

"I know."

"You're a good captain, Mirabel. Best I've shipped with. But you've been carrying something since the gorge, and it's been carrying you right back." He folded the rag over his belt, precise as a man closing a ledger. "Whatever it is—it doesn't get to steer."

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

He touched the wheel housing once, lightly, the way he touched everything he respected, and went forward to make fast the anchor line, and did not watch her go below. That was Sef's particular kindness: he gave her privacy the way other men gave alms, grudgingly and exactly.

* * *

The engine room was warm and smelled of diesel and old paint, and the fragment waited behind the fuel tank where it had waited for six weeks, wrapped in canvas like something sleeping. She knelt and drew it out and unwrapped it, and there it was in the lantern light: grey-green, dense, its script shifting at the edge of vision like fish under ice.

She held it in both hands and let herself feel its weight one last time. Two pounds, maybe three. Lighter than an anchor. Heavier than a life.

And it gave her what it always gave her—the edges of the hole, the shape of what she'd lost. Her daughter's face, which she could still see, though she no longer trusted a single detail of it: were her eyes really that grey? Had that scar really been on the left brow, or had the stone put it there, borrowed from some drowned mother centuries dead who'd also loved a grey-eyed child? Mirabel had chosen, in the gorge, in the storm, with the flood coming down like the end of the world—to bury the city and everything in it, including the certainty of her own past. She'd chosen oblivion over ownership. She'd told herself it was the only honest trade.

But she'd kept this. One stolen song. One secret. And the keeping had cost her more than the losing would have, because every day since, the stone had paid her back in small counterfeit coin: knowledge of channels she'd never sounded, moods of water she'd never read, a river's thoughts leaking into hers like dye into clear water. She was becoming a map of a place that no longer existed, drawn by a hand that wasn't hers.

*A river doesn't forgive. It only forgets.*

Mirabel carried the fragment up on deck. The afternoon light lay gold on the sandbar, and the eddy turned slow and brown beside the hull, and the gulls had not moved from their stations, and the whole scene was so ordinary it hurt—this was the world that had taken her back without questions, and she was about to give it one last piece of the truth and keep none of it for herself.

She said the words aloud, because some things need air to be real. "I was there. I found it. I ended it. I chose." Her voice cracked on *chose*, and she let it. "That's mine. The rest was never mine to carry."

She dropped the stone over the leeward rail.

It went into the brown water without a splash worth the name—one soft sound, a ring of ripples, gone. The river closed over it the way rivers close over everything: completely, indifferently, forever. For a moment she thought she heard, faint and far down, a woman singing in a language with no living speakers, and then the current took that too, and the eddy smoothed, and the sandbar lay golden and silent in the light, keeping whatever it kept.

Sef came aft and stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a while. The Bram swung gently on her hook, pointing downriver, the way boats do when they're ready.

"Well," Sef said finally. "Feel different?"

Mirabel considered it honestly, the way she read water: patiently, with both hands on the wheel. The hole was still there. It would always be there—her daughter's face with its uncertain details, eleven weeks of her own history she could never fully account for, a summer that had happened to someone very much like her. But the hole had stopped leaking. The knowledge that wasn't hers had gone quiet, or gone home, or drowned with the rest of it. What remained was hers: the choosing, the losing, the standing here now with salt wind in her hair and a boat that needed painting and a crew that no longer remembered why they'd gone but had come home anyway, to her, because she'd brought them back.

"Not different," she said. "Just mine."

She weighed anchor. The Bram came round and took the current, and Mirabel Iversen read the river ahead of her the old way, the earned way—by eye, by instinct, by twenty years of listening—and where the channel bent at Kessel Point she saw the turn with her own eyes before her hands made it, and it was enough. It would be enough. She'd see to it.

Behind them, at the mouth of the first unnamed creek, the sandbar lay quiet in the long gold light—and if the river had a mood about giving up what it had swallowed, it kept it to itself, the way it keeps everything, all the way down to the sea.
