## 1. The Weight of Nothing

The salt flats did not forgive, and they did not forget. They simply waited.

Isandro Sterling had learned that about them years ago, back when she was still learning everything—how to read a horizon, how to ration water by the weight of the canteen against her hip, how to walk so that her boots left nothing behind but heat. The flats taught patience the way a debt collector teaches arithmetic: slowly, and with interest.

She crouched now at the edge of a cracked pan, one knee pressed to ground hot enough to sear through canvas, and studied the print in front of her. A boot heel. Left foot. The edge was crumbling already, grains of salt sloughing into the depression like snow deciding it would rather be sand. An hour old, maybe less. Maybe more. Out here, time moved like syrup poured on glass, and every tracker's guess was a wager against the wind.

The thief had come this way. Odrik Vane, if the Guild's papers were to be believed—a name too elegant for the man described in the warrant, which said he was small, quick-fingered, and fond of dyed scarves that trailed behind him like banners announcing his own vanity. He'd lifted something from a Guild warehouse in Callow's Rest: a strongbox, contents unspecified, which meant valuable, which meant Isandro's problem.

*Your problem,* she corrected herself. Not the Guild's. Not anymore. The Guild had made very sure, over six years, that every problem she carried became hers alone, while every solution belonged to them.

She rose, shading her eyes against the white glare, and looked at the trail the way a gambler looks at cards she can't afford to fold. Boot prints, spaced wide—he'd been running, or hurrying, or both. A smear where he'd slipped on the salt crust. And there, thirty paces on, the ghost of a campfire ring, stones arranged with the fussy neatness of a man who liked order even when he was stealing from people who dealt in nothing else.

Isandro unslung her canteen, weighed it, and drank two swallows when she wanted ten. Then she walked on.

* * *

The debt lived in her like a second heartbeat.

Six years ago it had been a number on a page: four hundred crowns, lent against her ranger's badge and her future wages, at an interest rate dressed up in words like *reasonable* and *standard practice*. She had signed because her sister Lira was coughing blood into a rag in a rented room in Callow's Rest, and the Guild's physician charged what the Guild's physician charged, and the Saltwater Guild did not extend charity to rangers any more than the sea extended mercy to drowning men.

Lira had died anyway. Eleven months later, in the spring, when the acacias were blooming and the whole world smelled sweet enough to choke on.

But the debt had not died. Debts, Isandro had learned, were the only things in the Saltwater Territories that never died. They grew. Four hundred crowns had become six hundred, then nine hundred, then a figure so large that when Maren Voss recited it to her each quarter, in that voice like a ledger being read aloud, Isandro stopped hearing numbers and heard only the shape of her own captivity. *Twelve hundred and forty crowns,* Voss had said last month, tapping the page with one lacquered nail. *And you've been slow, Sterling. Slow costs extra.*

Slow. As if she hadn't spent six years tracking thieves and deserters and runaway debtors across half the known map, bringing them back so the Guild could do to them what it was doing to her. As if she hadn't surrendered every commission, every bounty, every scrap of salvage money, straight into the Guild's open mouth.

There had been a year—she didn't let herself think of it often—in which she'd almost found a way out. A rich vein of silver in the Ashfall hills, staked legal and clean, worth three times her debt. She'd filed the claim, hired a surveyor, begun to believe. Then the Guild had invoked a clause she'd never seen, buried in the loan contract like a scorpion under a stone: all discoveries made by the debtor, of any kind, escheat to the Saltwater Guild until satisfaction of principal. The vein had become theirs. Her year had become theirs. After that, she had stopped looking for ways out and started looking only for ways through—one day, one trail, one payment at a time.

That was the trick of it, she thought now, walking the white immensity with her shadow shrunk to nothing beneath her. The debt didn't chain her legs. It chained her attention. It sat on her shoulder like a carrion bird and turned every horizon into a question: *How much is that worth? How much closer does that bring you? How long?*

Even the compass at her belt had become part of the arithmetic.

She touched it without meaning to—the way you touch a scar—and felt its familiar weight: brass casing worn smooth at the edges, glass scratched in one corner where Lira had dropped it down a well and laughed at her own clumsiness. Their father's compass. The only thing their father had left besides debts of his own, scattered across three territories like seed he'd never stayed to harvest. Isandro had tried to pawn it twice. Twice she had stood in the pawnbroker's dim shop in Callow's Rest, laid it on the counter, watched the man's thumb trace the engraving—*For my girls, who will always find their way*—and twice she had picked it up again before he could name a price.

It wasn't worth much. That wasn't the point. It was worth exactly as much as the last thing anyone had ever given her expecting nothing back, and she could no more sell it than she could sell the memory of Lira's laugh, thin and startled, echoing up out of that well.

So she carried it. North by northwest, following a thief across the end of the world.

* * *

By late afternoon the light began to change, and Isandro knew the storm was coming before she saw it.

The flats had a language, and she spoke it fluently. The air went still in a way that felt held, like breath before a word. The horizon, usually knife-sharp, softened at its edges. And the salt itself—the endless cracked pans, the polygons of dried earth like the scales of some sleeping leviathan—began to whisper, a fine hiss as static built in the dry air and lifted the first grains loose.

Dust storms out here weren't weather. They were erasures.

Isandro checked her trail one last time. Odrik Vane's prints led toward a line of low mesas to the north, dark smudges on the white like ink drying on paper. Between her and the mesas lay perhaps four miles of open pan, and beyond them, according to the survey maps she'd memorized and the ones she'd merely survived, the flats gave way to the flooded lowlands—channels and marshes and drowned orchards where the monsoon rains pooled for months and boats mattered more than boots. If the thief reached the lowlands ahead of her, she might as well be chasing a fish through the sea.

She lengthened her stride.

The wall of dust came off the western horizon like a decision the sky had been postponing. One moment the world ended in clean blue; the next, a brown-gray rampart stood there, miles high and miles wide, rolling toward her with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been argued with. The wind arrived first, hot and strange, carrying the smell of places far away—red clay, crushed sage, the mineral tang of country she'd never see.

Isandro wrapped her scarf over her nose and mouth, pulled her goggles up, and kept walking. There was no shelter. There was never shelter on the flats; that was rather the point of them. You hunkered down, you protected your eyes and your water, and you waited to find out how much of the world would still exist afterward.

The storm took the sun first. Light drained from the day as though someone had pulled a plug, and then the dust was on her, around her, inside everything—a grinding, gritty blindness, the wind screaming in a register just below pain. She turned her shoulder into it and counted her steps, because counting was what you did instead of panicking. Forty steps north-northwest. Rest. Forty more. The compass was useless in the murk; she navigated by dead reckoning and stubbornness, the two supplies she never ran short of.

Somewhere in the third hour—or the fifth; time dissolved in the brown roar—she lost the trail.

Not gradually. Not the way trails usually faded, print by print, hope by hope. It simply ceased, between one step and the next, as though Odrik Vane had walked off the edge of the world and taken his boot prints with him. Perhaps the storm had buried him. Perhaps he'd cut sideways. Perhaps he'd never existed at all, and she'd spent two days pursuing a story the Guild had told itself.

Isandro stopped. The wind pushed her, patient as a hand on her back. Sand hissed against her coat, against her goggles, seeking entry the way the Guild sought entry—through every seam, every gap, every place she'd failed to seal herself shut.

She should keep moving. Movement was survival; standing still on the flats during a storm was how they found your bones in spring, posed mid-stride like monuments to poor judgment. She knew this. She had known it for twenty years.

And yet she stood there, in the roaring brown nothing, and let the emptiness look at her.

This was the thing she never said aloud, not to Voss, not to the other trackers, not to the empty room she rented by the week in Callow's Rest: that some part of her had stopped believing there was anything on the other side of the debt. That she tracked thieves across the salt not to earn her freedom but because the tracking was the only hour of any day when she knew exactly who she was. *Isandro Sterling, ranger, finder of the lost.* Strip away the badge and the commission and the canteen, strip away the number Voss recited each quarter, and what remained? A woman with a compass she couldn't pawn and a promise she'd never made to anyone, standing in a storm, owned down to her next breath by men who had never once been thirsty in their lives.

The flats mirrored her perfectly. That was their cruelty and, in a way she'd never admit to anyone, their comfort. No landmarks. No edges. Just white going gray going brown going black as the storm thickened, an emptiness so complete it felt less like a place than like an absence wearing the costume of one. Somewhere out there was a mesa line, a thief, a strongbox, a number with her name attached. Somewhere out there was a life she'd been promised would resume once the debt was paid—the lie every debtor tells themselves, that freedom is a door at the end of a corridor, and you only have to keep walking.

She had been walking six years. The corridor did not end. The corridor was the world.

Isandro unclipped the compass, cupped it in both hands beneath her scarf, and opened the lid by feel. The needle swung, wild, useless, spinning like a thing possessed—which was, she supposed, honest of it. Even the instrument of finding couldn't find anything here. Even north had given up.

"For my girls," her father had written, "who will always find their way."

Lira had found her way out. Six feet down, in the spring, with the acacias blooming. Isandro had found her way into a ledger. And their father—well. Their father had found his way onto the next boat, as he always did, leaving his compass and his daughters and his promises scattered behind him like ballast he no longer needed.

She closed the lid. Clipped it back on her belt. Felt its small weight settle against her hip, familiar as grief.

"Keep moving," she told herself, and her voice came out flattened by the wind, stripped of everything but instruction. "Forty steps."

She walked. The storm walked with her. Hours passed, or didn't. Once, she thought she saw a figure in the murk—tall, still, watching—and blinked grit from her eyes, and it was gone, and she decided she had imagined it, because the alternative was that the flats had started showing her things, and trackers who started seeing company in the empty places did not stay trackers long.

Toward what might have been dawn, the wind began to die. It went reluctantly, in gusts and grudging sullen lulls, and the dust thinned from soup to haze to a fine suspended veil that turned the early light the color of old brass. Isandro halted, unwound her scarf, and spat grit. Then she looked around at the world the storm had left her.

Everything was gone.

Not hidden—gone. The mesas she'd been steering for had vanished, either swallowed by distance or erased by the drifts of fresh sand that now sculpted the flats into new shapes, dunes where pans had been, valleys where dunes had been. The sky was pale and featureless. The horizon was a rumor. Her own tracks had filled in behind her within minutes, so that she appeared to have arrived from nowhere, walking on nothing, bound nowhere.

She turned a slow circle. In every direction: the same white silence, vast and absolute, broken only by the soft tick of settling salt and her own breathing.

Anyone else would have despaired. Isandro felt something worse: recognition. This was her life, rendered in landscape. This was the interior country she'd been crossing for six years, since the day she signed the contract and the day after, when Lira's fever broke for the last false time and Isandro let herself believe. An emptiness with no landmarks. A debt with no visible edge. A self worn smooth as river stone, shaped entirely by what had been done to it.

"Well," she said to the silence, because talking to yourself was the first symptom and also the only cure. "You're lost."

The silence agreed with her.

She got her bearings the hard way—sun's angle, wind's memory, the faint geological lean of the land toward the distant lowlands—and set out north by northwest once more, toward where the mesas had been, toward where the thief had been, toward where the strongbox and the number and the rest of her captivity waited like a destination on a map drawn by creditors.

She had gone perhaps a mile when she saw it.

A thread of smoke, thin and gray, rising from behind a drift of new sand at the base of a shattered ridge. Small. Careful. The smoke of someone who did not want to be found—which meant, on these flats, someone worth finding.

Isandro sank into a crouch, hand drifting to the short rifle slung across her back, and studied the smoke with the flat patience the desert had taught her. A thief with a fire was a thief who believed himself safe. A thief who believed himself safe was a thief about to have a very bad morning.

She began to move, low and silent, along the ridge line, her shadow long and thin in the young light, her boots finding the hard crust where they'd make no sound. The compass tapped gently against her hip with each stride, keeping its small faithful time, pointing forever at a north that had nothing in it.

Behind her, the storm's wreckage stretched to every horizon. Ahead, the smoke curled upward into the clean morning air, and beneath it, though she couldn't have known it yet, a dying man was waiting with a map and a promise that would cost her everything she had left—

which was, when she counted it honestly, almost nothing at all.
