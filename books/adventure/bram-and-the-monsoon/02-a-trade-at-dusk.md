## 2. A Trade at Dusk

The smoke led her down off the ridge and into a country of broken stone, where the flats gave way to low hills the color of old bone. Isandro moved with the patience the desert had taught her—three steps, stop, listen; three steps, stop, look. The wind had died with the morning, and in that stillness every sound carried for half a mile: the tick of cooling rock, the dry rattle of a thornbush, her own breath going in and out of her like tide through a sea cave.

The camp sat in the lee of a shelf of sandstone, tucked against it the way a wounded animal tucks itself against a wall. She saw it first as an arrangement of wrongness—the glint of a cookfire burned down to ash, a bedroll flung open, a kettle tipped on its side with its handle pointing at the sky like a broken finger. No one tidied a camp into ruin. Ruin happened to camps.

She crouched at the edge of the shelf and watched it for a long time. Ten minutes. Fifteen. The sun climbed and her shadow shortened beneath her, pooling under her boots like spilled ink. Somewhere above, a hawk turned in slow circles, patient as arithmetic.

No movement. No second watcher. Only the fire's ash lifting now and then in small gray ghosts when the air remembered how to move.

She went in low, knife already loose in her hand, though she told herself she wouldn't need it. The bedroll was still warm. That was the first thing she noticed, and it troubled her more than the blood did—the blood was everywhere, soaked into the sand in a wide dark fan, flies already at work at its edges. But the bedroll warm meant someone had left in a hurry, recently, alive. And the blood meant someone else had not.

She followed the blood around the shoulder of the sandstone shelf, and there he was.

He was propped against the rock with his legs stretched out in front of him, one boot on, one boot lying a yard away as if it had been removed with great care or fallen off without any care at all. He was older than she'd expected—sixty, maybe more—with a face seamed deep as cracked mud and a white beard gone yellow at the mouth. His hands were a prospector's hands, scarred and stained, and they were pressed flat against his belly as if he could hold himself together by will alone. Between his fingers, blood seeped up through the gaps, black in the shade, bright where the light found it.

Gut-shot. She'd seen enough of them to know what it meant: hours, not days. Pain, then quiet, then nothing.

His eyes found her before she decided whether to announce herself. They were pale eyes, almost colorless, and they were terribly clear. Dying men's eyes were usually clouded, swimming. These were focused on her like a surveyor's instruments.

"You're not Odrik," he said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel.

"No."

"He said he'd come back." A wet sound that might have been a laugh. "They always say that."

Isandro stayed where she was, ten feet off, weight on her back foot. "Who shot you?"

"The same people who were paying him, I expect. Or their cousins. It gets complicated out here." He shifted, and the shift cost him something; she saw it go through him like a current through wire. "You're a ranger. I can see the badge."

She didn't look down at it. She never looked down at it anymore. "Retired," she said, which was the word she used instead of *sold*.

"Badges don't retire. They just change owners." He coughed, and the cough brought red to his lips, and he wiped it away with the back of one ruined hand as if it were an embarrassment, a social failing, rather than the end of him arriving early. "Water," he said. "If you're carrying any."

She was carrying very little. That was the way she traveled now—light, fast, unencumbered, everything she owned arranged so it could be abandoned in under a minute. But there was water. She came forward slowly, keeping her eyes on the rocks around them, and knelt beside him, and held the skin to his mouth while he drank in small, disciplined sips, the way a man drinks who has learned not to waste anything, even at the end.

"Thank you," he said. He looked at her for a long moment. "Iversen. Your badge says Iversen."

"It says a lot of things."

"I knew an Iversen once. Good tracker. Terrible at cards." He smiled, and the smile cracked the dried blood at the corner of his mouth. "Halloway. Tovan Halloway. Though I suppose by tomorrow it won't matter what either of us was called."

"Tovan Halloway." She turned the name over. It wasn't famous, but it wasn't nothing. Prospectors talked about prospectors the way sailors talked about weather—half rumor, half reverence. Halloway had spent thirty years looking for drowned Vess. Everyone in the trade knew that. Everyone in the trade also knew that everyone in the trade said that, about themselves, usually after their third drink.

"Vess," she said, because he was waiting for her to say it.

"Vess." He closed his eyes briefly, and she thought for a moment she'd lost him, but no—he was gathering himself, arranging his last hour the way he'd arranged his camps, everything in its place. "You know the stories. Every fool with a pan knows the stories. The city under the floodwater. The gates that sealed it when the monsoon came and never opened again. Gold in the granaries, they say. Relics in the temples. All of it sitting under forty feet of green water for two hundred years, waiting for some clever bastard to come along."

"And you're the clever bastard."

"I'm the dying one." He said it without self-pity, which she noticed, and which she would remember. "The clever ones are the ones who shot me. Or hired the man who did. Corvane. You've heard the name?"

Everyone had heard the name. The Corvane syndicate ran the river tolls from Callow's Rest to the delta, moved cargo that no honest barge would carry, and had lately developed an interest in things pulled out of the flooded country. She said none of this. She said, "Why would Corvane shoot the man who found what they wanted?"

"Because I wouldn't sell." He laughed again, that terrible wet laugh. "Thirty years, ranger. Thirty years of dry holes and dead ends and winters eating boiled leather. And then, eight months ago, I found it. Not the city—I'm not a liar, even dying—but the way in. The old survey road. The gatehouse. And underneath the gatehouse—" His hand moved toward the breast of his coat, and stopped, trembling. "Underneath, I found the map."

"What map?"

"The map my own father helped draw, before the Guild bought it and buried it and let the man who drew it die poor." His voice had risen, and the effort of raising it cost him; he sagged against the stone, breathing in shallow pulls. When he spoke again it was quieter. "A survey of the floodgates. All of them. Where they stand, how they're built, how they open. Do you understand what that means? Not treasure. Gates. Gates that could turn the floodwaters away from the lowland villages, or drown them all in a single season, depending on whose hand is on the wheel. Corvane wants it because whoever holds those gates holds the whole delta by the throat. And I—" another cough, longer this time, and when it passed he was grayer than before, "—I just wanted to give it to my daughter."

The word landed in the space between them and sat there.

"Bram," he said. "Bram Halloway. She lives in Callow's Rest, or near it—a village called Marrow's Bend, upriver. She mends nets. She hates me." He said this last part plainly, the way a man states the depth of a well he fell into long ago. "And she has every reason to. I was gone more than I was home. I chose the desert over her mother's grave, over her school fees, over every single thing that mattered, because I believed—God help me, I *believed*—that Vess would make it all right in the end. That I'd come home rich and forgiven." He looked down at his hands, at the blood between his fingers. "This is how I came home."

Isandro said nothing. The wind had begun again, faintly, moving the heat around without lessening it. The flies droned. Somewhere behind the sandstone shelf, a pebble clicked down a slope, and both of them went still until it settled.

"Odrik took a copy," Halloway said at last. "I thought he was a friend. He was a friend, I think, right up until Corvane's money got bigger than our friendship. They'll kill him for it too, when they've wrung him dry—or worse, they'll let him lead them somewhere wrong, and then kill him. But the original—" his hand pressed his coat again, "—the original is mine. Ours. My father's lines. My thirty years. And Bram's, if she wants it, which she won't, but that's her right too."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the first person in three days who isn't holding a knife on me." He smiled faintly. "Well. You are holding a knife on me. But you haven't used it, and you gave me your water, and your eyes keep going to the horizon the way a decent person's do—checking whether trouble's coming for me, not coming for you."

She hadn't known she was doing that. She put the awareness away carefully, the way she put away all evidence of her own softness.

"I'm tracking a thief," she said. "That's all this is. I have business in Callow's Rest and no time for—"

"For dying men?" He said it gently. "Nobody ever has time for dying men. That's rather the point of us."

He tried to sit forward, and failed, and she found herself—without deciding to—putting a hand behind his shoulders and helping him up. His back was hot through the cloth. Fever had arrived, then. The clock was running faster now.

"In the coat," he breathed. "Oilskin. Left side."

She hesitated one heartbeat longer than she was proud of. Then she reached into his coat, past the smell of blood and sweat and the sweetish stink of a wound going bad, and drew out a packet wrapped in oilskin and tied with waxed cord. It was heavier than she expected. Older. The oilskin had been replaced at least twice—she could see the newer stitching over the older—and beneath her fingers the shape of it was rigid, board-backed, deliberate. Something made to last.

"Don't open it here," he said quickly. "Please. If anyone's watching—and someone is always watching—let them see you take nothing. Let them think you robbed me of my boots and moved on." His colorless eyes fixed on hers. "Open it when you're alone. Open it when you've decided."

"Decided what?"

"Whether you're the kind of woman who keeps a promise." He managed the ghost of a grin. "It's a short decision, usually. Most people already know."

"I don't make promises," Isandro said. "Promises are debts with better manners."

Something moved across his face—not anger, not disappointment. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his younger self speak from someone else's mouth.

"Then hear the terms," he said, "and refuse them properly, out loud, so we both know where we stand." He gathered breath the way a drowning man gathers water, in desperate pulls. "Deliver the map to Bram Halloway, in Marrow's Bend, before the dry season ends. Don't read it. Don't sell it. Don't let the Guild have it, don't let Corvane have it, don't let anyone have it but her. That's all. One delivery. One promise. To a man who has nothing else left to offer you except—" he paused, and the pause cost him, and he pushed through it, "—except the knowledge that you did the one clean thing available to you in a dirty year."

"I have debts," she heard herself say. "Real ones. The kind with ledgers and enforcers attached. I can't go wandering upriver doing favors for corpses."

"No," he agreed. "You can't. So don't do it for me." His hand rose—shaking, heavy, unstoppable—and closed around her wrist with a grip that astonished her, that seemed impossible from the ruin of him. "Do it for her. She's twenty-six years old and she thinks her father forgot her. Let her learn he didn't. Let her learn it from something other than a stranger's apology." His grip tightened, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, to the register of a man speaking from the far shore. "I know what it is to be owned, ranger. I sold myself to Vess the day her mother died. You've sold yourself to something too—I can see its chain on you, the same as mine. But this—" he tapped the oilskin packet against his chest, once, twice, "—this is the opposite of a debt. This is the one thing in this world you can carry that nobody owns. Not the Guild. Not Corvane. Not the dead man who gives it to you. Yours only while you choose it. That's what a promise is. That's why it's worth more than gold."

The hawk cried out overhead, once, and wheeled away east.

Isandro knelt there in the blood-soaked sand with a dying stranger's hand on her wrist, and she felt the trap close around her—not his trap, he was too honest a man to build traps, but her own, the one she'd been walking toward since the dust storm, since the compass, since the day she'd signed her name in the Guild's ledger and called it survival. She could stand up. She could walk away. The flats were empty in every direction and no one would ever know.

That was exactly the problem. No one would ever know. Except her. Except the compass tapping at her hip, keeping its faithful time, pointing north at nothing.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "it means nothing. You understand that. I'll carry it because it's on my way. Because Callow's Rest is upriver anyway. Because it costs me nothing."

"Of course," Tovan Halloway said, and smiled with bloody teeth like a man who had just won a card game. "Nothing at all."

She took the packet from his coat. It was warm from his body, and she hated that, and she took it anyway.

He talked while she worked—giving her Bram's description, the bend of the river, the blue door on the net-mender's hut, the way Bram would scowl, the way her scowl was her mother's scowl, the way she should be told *last*, at dusk, when the light was kind. He talked about the gates, a little, in fragments that made less sense the harder she listened, numbers and depths and seasons, and she understood that he was delirious now, giving the map's secrets to the air. She checked his wound once, knowing what she'd find, and found it. There was nothing to be done that hadn't been undone already.

Near the end he asked her to turn him so he could see the west, where the sun was going down huge and orange behind the hills, and she did, propping him against the stone with his face to the light. He watched it for a long time.

"She'll be angry," he said. "At first. Maybe always. That's fair. Anger is just love with nowhere to go, and I gave it nowhere to go for twenty years." His breathing had slowed, spaced out, each one a little farther from the last. "Tell her the corner of the map—the bottom left, the little drawing in the margin—that one's hers. I drew it for her when she was six. She'll know."

"I'll tell her," Isandro said. And then, because the lie needed finishing, because she was already committed to the shape of it: "Before the dry season ends."

He didn't answer. She waited, watching his chest, watching the light leave the sky in bands of copper and violet, watching the first stars arrive over the eastern hills like a slow audience taking their seats. When she finally touched his shoulder, he was gone, and his face in the last of the light looked younger by thirty years, and mildly surprised, and at peace.

She sat with him until full dark. Not out of sentiment—she told herself it was caution, making sure no one returned to the camp, making sure the trail was cold. Then she dug, because the desert deserved better than carrion birds and because some habits outlive belief, and the ground was hard and her hands were blistered by the time she finished, and she laid him in facing west, the way he'd chosen, and stacked stones over him until the mound looked like a hundred other mounds scattered across these hills, anonymous and permanent.

Then she stood, and brushed the dirt from her knees, and took out the oilskin packet, and held it under the rising moon without opening it.

It weighed almost nothing. That was the strange thing. All evening she'd felt it dragging at her like an anchor, and here in her palm it weighed less than her compass, less than her knife, less than the badge she couldn't pawn. A dead man's paper. A daughter who hated him. A city under green water. Nothing. It meant nothing.

She tied it inside her coat, against her back, where a knife wouldn't find it easily, and told herself that was only sensible storage and not the beginning of anything.

By midnight she was walking north, toward Callow's Rest, toward the Guild's ledger and Maren Voss's patient, hungry eyes, with a stolen boat ticket in her pocket and a dead man's promise riding between her shoulder blades like a second spine.

It means nothing, she told the empty country, and the empty country, being empty, agreed with her completely.

But three days later, when she stood in the Guild hall in Callow's Rest and Maren Voss asked her, smiling, what she'd found out on the flats—

Isandro lied.
