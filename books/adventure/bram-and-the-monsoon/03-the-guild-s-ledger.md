## 3. The Guild's Ledger

Callow's Rest smelled of tar and drowned things.

Isandro came down off the flats with the dust of three days still in her teeth and the map pressed flat against her ribs, sewn into the lining of her coat where a woman might once have kept a love letter. The town rose out of the river haze the way it always did—crooked pilings, crooked roofs, crooked promises—leaning over the brown water like old men arguing at a bar. Barges sat low against their moorings. Gulls screamed over the fish market. Somewhere a bell was ringing the hour, though no one in Callow's Rest had ever been known to arrive anywhere on time.

She had told herself, all across the empty miles between the prospector's grave and the riverport, that she was coming here for supplies. Rope. Pitch. A new water skin to replace the one the dust storm had scoured raw. That was true, in the way that most comfortable lies are true: it simply wasn't the whole of it.

The whole of it was that Callow's Rest had a Guild house, and the Guild house had a ledger, and Isandro Sterling's name had been written in that ledger for six years now in ink that never dried.

She stopped first at the chandler's on Tidewalk Row, because if she was going to lie to Maren Voss she wanted to do it standing in a room where the lie had already been paid for. She bought rope she didn't strictly need. She counted out coin from the pouch at her belt—the Guild's coin, technically, advanced against her debt and clawed back twice over—and let the chandler's boy wrap the coil in burlap like a purchase worth carrying home. Then she stood a moment in the doorway looking down the long curve of the waterfront, at the masts and the cranes and the gray stone bulk of the Saltwater Guild house crouched at the end of the quay like a spider that had grown tired of pretending to be furniture.

The compass hung at her hip on its worn lanyard. Brass case gone green at the seams, glass scratched to frost, needle still true as scripture. Six years she'd carried it. Six years she'd walked past pawnshops in four towns with it hanging right there, and never once gone in, because pawning it would mean admitting what it was: the last thing her sister had ever given her, and the only thing in the world Isandro owned outright, free and clear, that the Guild couldn't touch.

They could touch everything else. That was the point of a debt.

* * *

The Guild house smelled of lamp oil and wet stone, and beneath both, faintly, of money—the particular dry, mineral smell of strongboxes kept in damp country, iron sweating its slow rust into the air. A junior clerk took her name without looking up. Another led her up a stair worn hollow in the middle by decades of boots, all of them climbing to report and none of them climbing to be free.

Maren Voss received her in a room with one window and no chair for guests.

That was deliberate. Isandro knew it was deliberate, and stood anyway, hands loose at her sides, the rope-and-pitch parcel under one arm like a shield made of errands. Voss sat behind a desk of black riverwood, writing, and did not look up for the length of ten full heartbeats. It was an old trick and Isandro had watched her use it on debtors, on smugglers, on a Corvane factor once who'd come in swaggering and left gray-faced. It worked less well on Isandro every year, which Voss surely knew, which meant the trick wasn't for Isandro at all. It was for whoever else might be watching through the door.

"Three days overdue," Voss said at last, setting down her pen. She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice. Her voice was the sound the river made under ice—patient, and moving whether you liked it or not. "Your check-in was due at the Saltwater station on the flats road. Instead I get word from a drover that you were seen walking north out of the dust storm like a woman chasing something."

"I was chasing something." Isandro set the parcel on the desk, unhurried. "A thief. Guild property, taken from the Waystation vault at Harrow Bend. You'll find the report filed."

"I've read the report." Voss folded her hands. She was not a large woman, but she occupied space the way a keel occupies water—everything around her arranged itself in relation to her whether it wished to or not. Gray at the temples now, more than last year. The same flat eyes, the color of a ledger page before anything is written on it. "It says you lost him."

"The storm lost him. There's a difference."

"There is," Voss agreed, "and it doesn't matter. The item was recovered from the thief's body two days ago by our own people. He's dead, Sterling. Washed up in a culvert south of the flats with his pockets turned out and his throat opened. So whatever you were doing out there for three days, it wasn't recovering Guild property."

The words went through Isandro like cold water through a cracked hull. *Dead.* Odrik, dead—not by her hand, not by the storm, but opened up and emptied somewhere in the dark while she slept behind a dune with a dying man's promise sewn against her ribs. Which meant someone else had found him first. Someone who hadn't wanted what he stole so much as they'd wanted him silent about having stolen it.

She kept her face still. Six years of reporting to Maren Voss had taught her that much, at least: the ledger did not pay interest on fear.

"Then the matter's closed," Isandro said. "I'll file my expenses."

"You'll stand there a moment longer." Voss leaned back. Through the single window, behind her, the river slid past, fat and brown with the first melt-swells of the coming monsoon, carrying a dead branch and half a fence post and somebody's unmoored skiff, all of it moving downstream toward the lowlands with the terrible patience of water. "You were seen, Sterling."

"Seen."

"At the Harrow Bend waystation. Before the theft. Talking to the man who later turned up dead." Voss's voice didn't change at all, and that was how Isandro knew this had been prepared—that there were papers in some drawer, statements from some clerk, a little architecture of fact built brick by brick while Isandro was out on the flats learning what a gut-shot prospector's last hour smelled like. "And you came back three days late with no thief and no explanation. So I'm going to ask you plainly, and you're going to answer plainly, because we have an arrangement, you and I, and arrangements survive on plainness."

Voss stood. She came around the desk slowly, and stopped close enough that Isandro could smell the lamp oil on her sleeves.

"Did you find anything out there? Anything at all—a paper, a chart, a story about treasure?"

* * *

Later, Isandro would take the moment apart the way she took apart a jammed lock, piece by piece, examining each one. And every piece would tell her the same thing: Voss already knew. Not about the map itself—about the *kind* of thing it was. Treasure stories. She'd used the phrase herself, weeks ago, in this same room, when the rumor first crossed her desk that someone was buying up charts of the flooded country east of the Verge. *If anyone brings you a treasure story, Sterling, you bring it to me before you breathe a word to anyone else. That's not a request. That's the arrangement.*

So Isandro lied.

It was not even difficult. That was the part that frightened her afterward, lying awake in a rented room above the fish market—not the danger, but the ease, the smoothness with which the falsehood came off her tongue, as if six years in the Guild's ledger had taught her their language better than she'd admitted.

"Treasure," she said, and let a little dryness into it, the weariness of a woman too tired and too broke for fairy tales. "There was a prospector. Half-dead when I found him, shot through the belly. He babbled. They do that." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her nothing, and that frightened her most of all. "He talked about a city under the water. Vess. Said he'd been there, said he'd found doors in the drowned streets. I gave him water. He died anyway."

"And?"

"And nothing. Dead men's stories don't spend." She met Voss's eyes and held them, steady as her compass needle. "I searched the camp after. Bedroll, cook pot, a pick head. No papers. If he had a chart, the thief took it, and the thief's floating face-down in a culvert with his pockets turned out—which tells me whatever he had, someone wanted it badly enough to kill for it and didn't get it."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes then. Not belief—Isandro doubted the woman believed her own mother on principle—but calculation. The arithmetic of a ledger-keeper deciding whether a number balanced.

"A prospector," Voss said, "who claimed he'd walked the streets of Vess."

"Prospectors claim a lot of things. It's why they're prospectors."

"Mm." Voss returned to her side of the desk, and Isandro felt the interview shift the way weather shifts—pressure dropping, something gathering out past the horizon. "Here is what you should know, since you've been honest with me." The irony passed over her like wind over stone; she either didn't notice it or had priced it in years ago. "You're not the only one asking after Vess. There are men in this port—Corvane men, if the shape of their coin means anything—who've been chartering boats and buying flood-charts for a month. Quietly. Badly. They've scared off two pilots already and put a third in the canal." She picked up her pen. "Whatever that prospector found or claimed to find, the Corvane syndicate believes in it enough to bleed for it. And the Guild does not involve itself in syndicate wars. We collect debts. We hold collateral. We do not dig in drowned cities."

"Understood."

"But you—" Voss looked up, and for just a moment the ledger-page eyes were not flat at all; they were very bright, and very cold, and Isandro understood that she had never once in six years been talking to Maren Voss. She had been talking to the Guild, wearing Maren Voss. "You are collateral, Sterling. Do you remember what you owe? Shall I say the figure aloud?"

"No need."

"Four hundred and eleven marks, six years of interest compounding on the principal, and every mark of it incurred in a single night of generosity that the Guild was kind enough to extend when no one else would lend to a grieving woman." Voss said it gently, almost kindly, which was the cruelest register she owned. "Four hundred marks, Sterling. A ranger's wage is forty a year. You have given us six years and you are further from free than the day you signed. Do you know why?"

Isandro said nothing.

"Because the Guild does not want you free." Voss set the pen down with a small, precise click. "We want you exactly where you are: able to walk places our enforcers can't, ask questions our clerks can't, and come back and report. You are the best tracker on the payroll and the cheapest, because you cost us nothing but patience. A debt like yours isn't a burden, Sterling. It's a leash. And leashes only work on dogs that hope."

The room was very quiet. Down on the quay, a crane groaned as it swung a load of salt barrels, and the gulls rose screaming off the pilings, and the river went on moving toward the lowlands with everything it carried.

"So," Voss said, "one more time, and then you may go. If anyone offers you a chart of Vess—if you find one, if you hear of one, if a dying man presses one into your hands—you bring it here. To me. First. Because I promise you, Sterling, that the Corvane men hunting it will not be gentle with whoever stands between them and it, and the Guild will not spend one mark to pull you out of that water. Your debt survives your death. Did you know that? It passes to your estate. To your next of kin." A thin smile. "You haven't any. So it would pass to the crown, and the crown sells such debts to whoever bids. Perhaps Corvane. Wouldn't that be tidy."

"I'll remember," Isandro said.

"See that you do." Voss dismissed her with a flick of two fingers, already reaching for the next paper, already becoming a woman to whom Isandro Sterling was a closed file. At the door, Isandro paused.

"The thief," she said. "Odrik. Who killed him, do you think?"

Voss didn't look up. "A man who steals for hire dies by hire. It's arithmetic, Sterling. Everything is."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and the first spit of the monsoon was ticking against the Guild house steps, fat warm drops that struck the stone and died. Isandro stood under the eave a long while, watching the water darken the quay, feeling the map's corner press against her ribs with every breath like a second heartbeat.

*Bring it to me first.*

Which meant Voss knew there was something to bring. Which meant the Guild wanted it—or wanted it kept from Corvane, which amounted to the same weight on the same side of the scale. Either way, the map in Isandro's coat had just become the most dangerous piece of paper between the mountains and the sea, and she was carrying it into a port full of syndicate charters and Guild spies with a lie sitting fresh on her tongue and a dead man's promise rotting in her chest.

*Deliver it to my daughter. Bram. Before the dry season ends.*

Bram Halloway. Isandro turned the name over. The prospector had said the daughter worked the water—said it with the helpless pride of a man who knew he'd earned none of it. Nets, maybe. Piloting. Something on the river.

She asked the chandler's boy, casually, paying him a copper for the trouble, and got a direction and a sneer in the same breath: *Halloway? The navigator? She keeps her boat down past the breakwater, the patched-up wreck. Fair warning, miss—she's got a tongue on her.*

Isandro walked down through the rain toward the breakwater, rope over her shoulder, badge on her chest, debt on her back, and a dead man's map against her heart, and thought: *One more lie won't sink me. But the truth might.*

The girl on the skiff looked up as her shadow fell across the boards—and Isandro saw at once that everything the boy had warned was true, and worse.
