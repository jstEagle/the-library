## 4. Bram

Callow's Rest smelled of tar and rotting fish and the green promise of rain that hadn't yet come. Isandro walked its length twice before she let herself stop at the third net-rack from the waterline, because the first two passes had been for looking, and looking was a thing you did before you committed to being seen.

The woman on the net-rack was mending a trawl with the economy of someone who had done it ten thousand times. Her hands moved without her eyes—dark eyes, quick, set in a face browned by weather and sharpened by something less forgiving than sun. She was maybe twenty-five, wiry as whipcord, sleeves rolled past elbows scarred white with old rope burns. A coil of twine sat at her hip like a holstered weapon.

Bram Halloway. The prospector had given Isandro little enough to go on: a name, a riverport, *she works the nets or the boats, she'll be near the water, she was always near the water*. But there was no mistaking her. The dead man's face had been gaunt and long-jawed, and this woman wore the same architecture, rebuilt in harder lines.

Isandro had rehearsed this on the road down from the flats. She'd rehearsed it through the sleepless night in Callow's Rest's only inn, listening to the river slap the pilings, turning phrases over like stones. *Your father is dead* — no, too blunt to open with. *I have something of his* — a lie by implication, since what she had was not so much his as it was him, the last weight of him pressed into oilcloth against her ribs.

She'd settled on nothing. Some conversations you couldn't rehearse. You just walked into them the way you walked into weather.

"Net's fine," Bram said, without looking up. "If you're buying, the fishmonger's two racks down. If you're selling, I don't buy."

"I'm not here about nets."

"Everyone in this town is here about nets. It's a fishing town." She bit a knot tight, pulled it home. "You've got dust in your boot seams and a ranger's walk and you've been circling my rack for ten minutes like a gull deciding whether to steal. So either you're Guild, which means I'm busy, or you're Corvane, which means I'm busier."

The names landed like dropped anchors. Isandro kept her face still, but inside, something cold turned over. *Corvane.* The thief Odrik had died with that name carved into the mud of his camp—not by his own hand—and now it came out of a net-mender's mouth like a word she'd learned young.

"You know that name," Isandro said.

"I know a lot of names." Bram finally looked up, and her gaze was a fisherman's gauge: measuring, weighing, already deciding if Isandro was worth the hook or better thrown back. "Men came through last month asking after my father. Well-dressed. Bad manners. Wanted to know if he'd been home." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I told them he hadn't been home in eleven years, which was true, and that if they found him they could keep him, which was also true. They didn't like either answer much."

"They found him."

The twine went slack in Bram's hands. Not still—slack, the way rope goes when the tension leaves it all at once. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Isandro watched the sentence arrive in her the way a wave arrives under a hull: lifting everything, changing everything, before the sound of it even finishes.

"How," Bram said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"Gut wound. Out past the Sere Flats, alone in a camp he'd stripped down to nothing. He'd been walking a long time on it. He knew." Isandro made herself hold the woman's eyes. She owed him that much, and she owed this stranger the truth delivered straight, undiluted by mercy, because mercy would only taste like pity. "He didn't suffer long at the end. He was past suffering mostly. He was—" *What? What had he been?* "He was thinking about you."

Bram stood. It happened slowly, like a boat taking on water settling onto new bearings. She laid the net down across the rack with exaggerated care, smoothing it flat, and Isandro understood it was the only thing keeping her hands occupied, the only ritual between her and whatever was breaking loose underneath.

"He's been dying for eleven years," Bram said. "Every time he left. Every time the money went where the money always went. Every time my mother sat up waiting with the lamp until the lamp oil ran out and then sat up in the dark instead." She picked up her knife—a netting knife, short and hooked—and put it down again deliberately, as if she'd caught herself reaching for it. "So don't stand there telling me how he died like it's news. Men like him die a hundred times. It's just that the last one is the only one anyone bothers to count."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be specific." And there it was—the sharpness Isandro had heard in the prospector's voice when he spoke of her, pride and grief braided into one cord. *She's sharp as broken glass, my Bram. Always was. Got it from her mother, thank every god there is.* "Who are you? Why were you with him? Nobody finds Aldous Halloway out on the Sere unless they're following somebody else, and nobody follows somebody else out there unless there's something worth following."

Straight to it. No tears, no collapse. Isandro felt something shift in her chest, an unfamiliar loosening, the sensation of meeting a mind that worked like her own did—cutting past the surface to the load-bearing wall beneath.

"My name is Isandro Sterling. I'm a ranger—or I was; the badge spends more time in my pocket than on my chest these days. I was tracking a thief across the flats. Stolen goods, Guild contract, the usual ugly arithmetic. I lost him in a dust storm. I found your father instead."

"And the thief?"

"Dead too. Later. Different hands." Isandro let that sit. She watched Bram absorb it, watched the quick eyes do their arithmetic—thief, pursuers, syndicate, a dying man alone in the empty quarter—and arrive at the same sum Isandro had reached three days ago standing over the body.

"So whoever killed the thief killed my father."

"Or sent the people who did. Either way, they wanted what he was carrying."

"What was he carrying?"

Here it was. Isandro reached inside her coat, slow, deliberate, showing her empty palm first the way you'd show a skittish animal, and drew out the oilcloth packet. She unwrapped it on the net-rack, weighting the corners with two floats and a wooden bobbin, and let the map open under the grey pre-monsoon light.

It was beautiful. She'd thought so the first night, studying it by candle stub, and she thought so now. The drowned city rendered in a surveyor's hand so precise it looked printed: channels and depths, gate-houses and sluice-works, annotations in a cramped, patient script. Water-stains at the edges. A corner folded soft with handling, worn the way paper gets worn when one person folds and unfolds it over years, alone, in the dark, thinking of someone.

Bram looked at it for a long moment. Then she laughed—one hard note, no humor in it at all.

"A map." She said it like a curse. "Of course. Of course it's a map. Eleven years of nothing—no letters, no coin, no word whether he was alive or dead—and the one thing he sends home is a *map*." She turned away, braced both hands on the rack, and stared out at the river as though it might offer a second opinion. "Do you know what he took with him the last time? Do you want to know? He took the boat money. Not his share—the boat money, the pooled fund, six families' worth of season. My mother had to tell the Vashtis we wouldn't be sailing. Old Man Vashti cried. In front of everyone. And my father—" her voice cracked and she forced it level, "my father wrote us one letter from somewhere upriver saying he'd found *a lead*, singular, like that was a word that fed anybody. That was the last we ever got. A lead."

"Bram—"

"No." She spun back, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet, and she was fighting the wet like it was an enemy boarding her deck. "Whatever this is, whatever he promised you or paid you or charmed you into doing, take it back. Take it to the Guild, sell it to Corvane, drop it in the river, I don't care. I'm not his heir. I'm not his redemption. He spent everything he had—including me, including my mother, including every person who ever trusted him—chasing drowned cities and easy fortunes, and look where it got him. Alone. Gut-shot. On the flats." Her breath shuddered once, and she mastered it. "I repair nets. I navigate boats for honest wages. I am not going to be the last chapter of Aldous Halloway's stupid story."

Isandro let the silence run. She'd learned that from interrogating smugglers and grieving widows alike: the truth needed room, and people filled silence with the things they actually meant.

Then she said, quietly: "He told me you'd say that."

Bram went very still.

"He was clear-eyed about it. He knew what he was. He said—" and here Isandro closed her own eyes briefly, calling the words up from the place she'd been keeping them, hoarding them like the last water in a skin, "—he said, *My daughter will hate me, and she should. Don't argue with her about it. Just tell her I never stopped reading the water. Tell her every channel on that chart, I mapped it thinking about the day she'd sail it better than me. Tell her the corner fold is hers. She'll know.*"

"The corner fold," Bram repeated. Barely a whisper.

Isandro touched the soft-worn edge of the map, the one corner handled to tissue-thinness. "This one. He said you'd know."

For a moment Bram didn't move at all. Then she reached out—not for the whole map, not yet, but for that one corner, and her fingers found the crease and followed it down, and Isandro watched eleven years of fury meet eleven years of something else, something older, and lose. The anger didn't vanish. Anger like that doesn't vanish. It just broke open, the way a levee breaks, and behind it came everything it had been holding back.

Bram Halloway sat down on the net-rack bench like her legs had been cut from under her, and she cried the way she did everything else—fiercely, silently, furiously, one hand pressed flat over the worn corner of the map as if holding a wound closed.

Isandro stood beside her and said nothing. There was nothing to say. She knew about wounds you held closed yourself. She'd been holding one for seven years, and it had never once healed, and she suspected it never would, and some part of her recognized the shape of Bram's grief the way you recognize a coastline you've sailed past a hundred times.

The river slapped the pilings. Somewhere down the dock a man cursed a fouled winch. The monsoon clouds stacked themselves higher over the western hills, patient as creditors.

Finally Bram wiped her face with the heel of her hand, one rough pass, done. When she spoke, her voice was scraped raw but level.

"He really said all that. About the channels."

"Word for word. He talked about you for most of an hour. It took him most of that hour to die." Isandro hesitated, then gave her the rest, because the rest was the truest part. "He didn't ask me to bring the map to you. Not first. First he asked me to promise he hadn't wasted his life. I told him I couldn't promise that—I didn't know him. So he asked for the second thing instead. Deliver it to his daughter before dry season ends. He said you'd know what to do with it, even if he never figured it out himself."

"What do you think it is?" Bram asked. "Really. You've had it longer than I have. You're a ranger—you read terrain for a living. What do you see?"

And Isandro, who had been avoiding exactly this question for four days, finally answered it honestly. "I see depth soundings where there shouldn't be any. I see gate-houses marked with flow rates. I see a survey, not a treasure chart. Somebody spent years measuring how the water moves through that city—which means somebody spent years figuring out how the water *could* move. Where it could be sent. What it could be made to do." She met Bram's red-rimmed eyes. "I don't know if that's worth anything. But I know men died for it, which usually means it's worth something to somebody dangerous."

Bram looked down at the map—at her father's cramped, patient handwriting, at the corner worn soft by a lonely man's thumb—and Isandro saw the war finish behind her eyes. Not won. Wars like that don't get won. But a truce, drawn along some interior line.

"I'll look at it," Bram said at last. "That's all I'm promising. I'll look at it, and I'll decide what it is, and then—" she jabbed a finger at Isandro, and even wrecked and tear-streaked, the gesture had real authority, "—then you're going to tell me why a Guild tracker with dust in her boots is delivering dead prospectors' maps for free. Because nobody does anything for free. Especially not rangers."

"That's fair," Isandro said.

"It's more than fair. It's suspicious." Bram began rolling the map with careful hands, and her voice dropped, almost to herself. "Dry season ends in five weeks. If this is what it looks like—if it's the floodgates—then it isn't just his story anymore. It's everybody downstream's."

She tucked the roll inside her shirt, against her skin, the way Isandro had carried it. Their eyes met over the net-rack, two women holding the same weight from opposite ends.

"There's a skiff at the east moorings," Bram said. "The Verity. She leaks, she's ugly, and she's mine. If I decide this needs doing, we do it my way, on my water, by my rules." A pause. "And you start talking. All of it. Whatever you're carrying besides the map."

Isandro felt the old walls close around her, the ones she'd built brick by brick since her sister died, since the debt, since the day she'd learned that everything she loved could be priced and seized. *Nobody does anything for free,* Bram had said. She was right. And sooner or later Isandro would have to explain what she was charging herself.

"First light tomorrow," Isandro said. "I'll be at the east moorings."

Bram nodded once and turned back to her nets—but her hands, Isandro noticed, didn't pick up the work. They stayed resting on the rack, over the spot where the map had lain, as if she could still feel the warmth of it.

Overhead, the first fat drops of the season struck the dock planks, one, then two, then a scattered handful, like a drummer testing his skins before the storm.
