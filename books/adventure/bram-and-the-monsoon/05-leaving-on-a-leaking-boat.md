## 5. Leaving on a Leaking Boat

Bram Halloway had three rules about strangers, and the woman sitting on her dock had already broken two of them.

Rule one: never let anyone see where you keep your boat. The Verity lay under a tarp of woven reeds behind the net-shed, half-hidden in the reed-beds where the river slackened into marsh, and Bram had told no one—not the harbor master, not her neighbor Odji with his gossiping gulls, not a living soul. But this Isandro Sterling had watched her walk to it yesterday evening with the patient eyes of a heron watching fish, and had said nothing, and that silence was worse than any question.

Rule two: never take work from someone who won't say what they're running from. And this ranger—she'd shown a badge once, briefly, like a coin flipped and palmed again—had a way of looking over her shoulder at the river road that told Bram everything she needed to know. People were following her. People she didn't want named.

The third rule was simpler: never go anywhere with a Halloway's map. And that one, of course, was already drowned beyond saving, because there it sat between them on the dock planks, wrapped in oilcloth, drawn by a dead man's hand.

"I said I'd think about it," Bram said now, mending the same stretch of net she'd been mending for an hour without finishing a single knot. "Thinking takes time."

"You've been thinking since dawn." Isandro sat cross-legged at the far end of the dock, hands loose on her knees, doing that thing she did—being perfectly still, perfectly quiet, as if stillness were a courtesy rather than a tactic. The morning fog was burning off the water in long white ribbons. Somewhere upstream a barge horn moaned. "I don't need an answer today. But the dry season doesn't wait, and neither do the people behind me."

"Then maybe you should stop telling me about them."

"It's honest."

"It's reckless." Bram jabbed the needle through the mesh harder than the work required. "You come to my dock, you show me my father's dying words written in another woman's hand, you expect me to pilot you into the lowlands during the first swell of monsoon—and when I ask why anyone would chase you, you tell me *because they would*. That's not honesty, ranger. That's just trouble with good manners."

Isandro considered this. She had a face built for considering things—the kind of lean, weather-cured face that gave away nothing until it decided to. "Fair," she said finally. "Here's what I know. A man named Odrik took a copy of that map south along the river road. He was working for money, and the money works for Corvane. If Corvane wants the original badly enough to kill a prospector in the flats, they'll want it badly enough to follow me here. That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

Bram's needle stopped.

She hadn't known the name Corvane. But she knew the shape of it—the syndicate barges that had been passing Callow's Rest all week, heavy in the water, flying no colors at all, which was its own kind of color. She'd seen their clerks buying up every jar of pitch and roll of canvas in the chandlers' shops, as if outfitting something large. As if outfitting a search.

"They were here," she said quietly. "Three days ago. Buying supplies."

Something moved behind Isandro's stillness—a tightening, like current under ice. "Then we've less time than I thought."

"We?" Bram laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "There's no *we*, ranger. There's you, and there's a favor I haven't agreed to."

* * *

Her father had taught her to read water before he'd taught her letters. That was the joke of it, the bitter little joke she turned over at night like a stone with a sharp edge: Tammas Halloway could vanish for two years at a stretch, could forget birthdays and debts and his own daughter's age, but he had made sure she could read current off the surface of moving water the way priests read scripture. *Watch where the foam hesitates,* he'd say, crouched beside her on some dock in some town whose name she'd forgotten because he always forgot it first. *That's where the bottom changes. Water remembers what men bury.*

He'd left for the last time when she was sixteen. Fourteen years ago. He'd kissed the top of her head, smelled of tar and river mud, and promised—promised, the word itself a kind of blasphemy in her mouth now—that this run would be different. This run would make them rich. This run would let him buy back her mother's house in Callow's Rest, the one with the blue shutters, the one sold out from under them the winter the fever took her mother.

Bram had stopped waiting by the time she was twenty. She'd kept the skill and lost the hope, and she'd told herself for fourteen years that this was growth.

And then a desert ranger with tired eyes had walked onto her dock holding a strip of oilcloth covered in her father's handwriting, and every one of those years had come due at once.

She found him at dusk, in the net-shed, checking the Verity's seams by lamplight. Not snooping—or not only snooping. When she stepped through the door he held up the lamp without being asked, angling the light so it fell where she'd want it, the way a crewman does, the way her father used to before he became a man who only existed in postscripts.

"She's taking water," Isandro said. "Starboard side, aft of the second strake. Old patch, gone brittle."

"Everything about her is old and brittle." Bram took the lamp from her, more roughly than intended. "She holds. That's what matters."

"What's her name mean? Verity?"

Bram set the lamp down on the workbench with a click. "It means truth. My mother chose it. She said a boat that tells you honestly when it's failing is worth more than a man who lies about anything." She heard her own voice and wished she could call it back. "My father named her, actually. My mother just made the name mean something."

Isandro didn't smile, exactly. But something at the corner of her mouth shifted, some small concession to the shape of the wound. "I'm sorry. About him. I only saw him for a few minutes, but—" She paused, choosing words the way she seemed to choose everything, deliberately, one at a time. "He talked about you the whole time. Even while he was dying. Mostly about the blue shutters."

Bram turned away and busied her hands with the bilge pump, which needed nothing. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make him gentle. You met him for five minutes. Five minutes of a dying man is the best version of him there's ever been. I got thirty-four years of the rest." Her voice cracked on the last word and she despised it, despised the heat behind her eyes, despised this stranger standing witness to it. "He'll be whatever I decide he was. Not whatever makes *you* comfortable."

For a long moment the shed was silent except for the lap of water against the hull and the hiss of the lamp.

"All right," Isandro said at last. Quietly. No argument in it, no flattery. Just acceptance, laid down flat between them like a plank across a gap. "Then decide however you need to. But the map's real, and the promise is real, and I can't deliver it alone. I don't know these waters. I don't know the lowlands. I'm a desert ranger, Bram—I read sand, not current. Without you, the map dies with me somewhere in the flooded cane, and his last act on this earth was handing treasure to a corpse."

"That's melodrama."

"That's arithmetic again."

Bram stared at the dark hull of the Verity, at the pale scar of the old patch below the waterline, and felt the trap closing around her—not Isandro's trap, nothing so deliberate, but the older one, the family one, the one her father had sprung on her from beyond his own grave. Because she could refuse. Of course she could refuse. She could hand the map back and watch the ranger walk into the flooded lowlands alone and drown or get knifed by Corvane's men, and the map would wash downstream, and the lowland villages would never know what had been drawn for them, and Bram Halloway would spend the rest of her life knowing exactly what her refusal had cost and pretending she didn't.

Her father had done this to her his whole life. Left her holding the consequences of his choices.

"One trip," she said, before she could stop herself. "Callow's Rest to the edge of the flooded country. I put you ashore where the channels start braiding, and after that you're on your own. I don't fight your fights. I don't meet your enemies. And if Corvane's barges show up, I beach this boat and disappear, and you can explain yourself to them."

Isandro nodded slowly. "One trip."

"And I want payment. Not coin. Coin I have." She jerked her chin at the map, still rolled in its oilcloth on the workbench. "When this is done, whatever that survey shows—if it shows treasure, if it shows floodgates, if it shows nothing but his handwriting—you tell me everything. All of it. Why he died carrying it. What he thought it was worth. I get the whole story, start to finish, no gentleness."

This time Isandro did smile, faintly, and it changed her whole face the way light changes a room. "No gentleness. Agreed."

They shook on it. Her hand was rough with rope-callus; Isandro's was rougher, sun-cracked across the knuckles, a desert hand adrift in a boatshed. Bram noticed she held on a half-second longer than a bargain required, and made herself not wonder why.

* * *

They left three nights later, on the dark of the moon, because Bram refused to leave any earlier and Isandro, to her credit, didn't push.

Those three days were a strange apprenticeship neither of them named. Bram stripped the Verity's old patch and laid new canvas and pitch, and Isandro handed her tools before she asked for them, learned the names of things—gudgeon, pintle, thole-pin—with a student's unembarrassed attention. Bram caught herself explaining the difference between a sweep and a scull and realized she was talking more than she had in a month, and stopped abruptly, and Isandro let the silence close over it without comment, which somehow made it easier to start again later.

She also watched the roads. Twice a day, morning and dusk, Isandro walked into Callow's Rest and came back with bread and news and that particular set to her shoulders that meant the news was bad. On the second evening she stood in the shed doorway a long time before speaking.

"Barges again," she said. "Two of them, anchored midstream. Flying grain colors, but they're riding too deep for grain. And there's a man asking questions at the harbormaster's office. Big fellow, gray coat, pays in Corvane scrip."

"Questions about what?"

"A woman ranger traveling alone. A boat leaving after dark." Isandro's jaw worked. "It's my name he's carrying, Bram. Whatever comes down on us comes down looking for me. I want you clear on that before we cast off. There's still time to say no."

Bram looked at her—at this woman who kept offering exits, who kept naming the danger out loud as though honesty were ballast, who slept on the shed floor with her boots on and her knife unsheathed beside her hand. She thought about the gray-coated man asking questions. She thought about the blue shutters. She thought, unwelcome as rain, about how her father had spent his whole life running toward things and how she had spent hers standing still, and how both of them had ended up empty-handed.

"No," she said. "No more time. We go tomorrow night, before that gray coat gets bored of asking."

* * *

They cast off at the hour the harbor lamps guttered low, when even the drunks were asleep and the tide stood slack between turns.

Bram poled the Verity out of the reed-beds by feel, the pole whispering through the black water, the skiff answering with the soft obedience of a thing that loved her. Behind them Callow's Rest was a scatter of embers against the hills, one dog barking at nothing. Ahead the river opened wide and starless, running high with the first melt-swells of the monsoon, carrying sticks and blossoms and the smell of wet earth from countries they'd never see.

Isandro sat amidships with the map in her lap and the spare oar across her knees, and did not talk. Bram decided she liked that about her.

An hour out, past the last of the channel markers, the Verity began to speak in the language Bram knew too well—a soft, rhythmic slosh beneath the floorboards, a little behind the rhythm of the wake.

"There it is," she murmured. "Starboard aft. Hand me the bailer."

Isandro passed it without question and took up the pump instead, working it in steady strokes, and together they kept the boat's secret conversation going—slosh and answer, slosh and answer—while the current carried them down into the drowned country. The patch would hold. Patches always held, right up until they didn't; that was the first law of boats and the second law of fathers.

Somewhere behind them, far back upriver, a lantern swung at the end of a dock, and a shape in a gray coat stood watching the black water as if it might confess.

Bram didn't see it. She was facing forward, reading the current, watching where the foam hesitated.

But Isandro, turning to check their wake, saw it—and said nothing, and rowed on.
