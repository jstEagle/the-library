## 6. First Rain

Bram smelled the storm before she saw it—a green, mineral tang riding the wind off the lowlands, the scent of a thousand drowned fields exhaling all at once. She had grown up with that smell. It meant the monsoon had stopped toying with them and meant business.

"Reef the sail," she called over her shoulder. "Now."

Isandro didn't argue. That was the first surprise of the night. Bram had expected argument—expected the desert ranger to stand rigid in the stern with her hand on that battered compass and tell her how things were done in the flats, where there was no water worth rigging for. Instead Isandro moved along the gunwale like she'd been born on it, hauling the line, taking in canvas while the *Verity* bucked under them like a mule refusing a load.

"You've sailed before," Bram said, not quite a question.

"I've been rained on before." Isandro cinched the reef knot with two hard pulls. "The rest is just rope."

*Just rope.* Bram almost laughed. She'd spent eleven years learning that rope was never just rope—that a fouled sheet in a squall could take a finger, an arm, a whole boat, a whole family. Her father used to say something similar, though. *It's only water, Bramble. Only wind.* He'd say it right up until he sailed off on some half-baked expedition and left her mother to explain why supper had gone cold again.

She pushed the thought down where she kept all thoughts of Tam Halloway, next to the map rolled in oilcloth beneath the thwart, and turned her face into the coming wind.

The sky over the flooded lowlands had gone the color of a bruise going bad. Behind them, Callow's Rest was a smudge of smoke and lamplight already swallowed by cloud. Ahead, the drowned country spread out—orchard tops breaking the surface like drowning men's hands, fence lines running under brown water, the roofs of farmhouses standing waist-deep in their own fields. In another month the whole expanse would be a shallow sea forty miles wide. Right now it was still deciding what it wanted to be.

And the rain arrived to help it decide.

It came not as drops but as a wall—a gray curtain sweeping across the water toward them so fast Bram could watch it come, hissing where it struck the surface. She had time to shout "Hold on!" and then the world went white with noise.

Rain hammered the deck like thrown gravel. The wind hit a heartbeat later and laid the *Verity* over until the water lipped at the leeward rail. Bram threw her weight against the tiller, boots skidding on wet planking, and felt the little skiff answer—reluctantly, groaningly—coming back upright with its patched seams weeping in three places she could feel through her soles.

"Bail!" she screamed.

Isandro was already bailing.

That was the second surprise. The ranger had found the wooden scoop stowed under the bow seat without being told where it lived, and she worked it in a rhythm—scoop, heave, scoop, heave—with the economy of someone who understood that panic wasted motion and motion wasted strength. Water arced out over the side in silver ropes. Her hood had blown back; her dark hair plastered to her skull, and still her face stayed level, eyes narrowed against the rain, watching the water in the bilge the way she'd probably watched horizons on the flats.

Bram hauled the tiller across and brought the bow around to take the swell at an angle. The *Verity* climbed a wave, hung at the top with her sail slack and shuddering, then slid down the far side with a sound like a long exhale.

They ran like that for an hour. Maybe two. Time went strange in weather like this—measured not in minutes but in waves survived, in scoops of water thrown, in the slow ache spreading up from the small of Bram's back into her shoulders.

* * *

The lightning started after full dark, which made it worse, because now each flash wrote the whole drowning world onto the inside of Bram's eyelids: the orchards, the rooftops, the pale snarl of a current where the river channel wandered loose through the flood. Thunder followed close enough behind to feel in the hull.

"We can't run this all night," Isandro shouted, crouched low near the mast. Water streamed off her chin. "Where do we make land?"

"There is no land." Bram jerked her chin toward the east, where the drowned country stretched unbroken to the horizon. "Not for twenty miles. There's a church spire past the old toll road—used to be a village called Merrow's Bend. If the roof holds, we can tie off under the eave and wait it out."

"If it doesn't hold?"

"Then we learn to pray very quickly."

Something flickered across Isandro's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, there and gone like the lightning. "I've prayed before. Didn't take."

"No," Bram said, steering through a trough, "I don't imagine it did."

She hadn't meant it as cruelty. It came out flat and automatic, the way she'd learned to talk about everything that mattered—her father's leaving, her mother's silence, the way the whole town of Callow's Rest had watched her grow up as *the prospector's girl*, waiting for her to inherit his restlessness. Flat words were armor. But she saw the words land anyway, saw something shut behind Isandro's eyes, and felt an unexpected, irritating twinge of having kicked a dog that hadn't bitten her yet.

The *Verity* took another wave broadside and shipped a gallon over the rail. Bram stopped apologizing to herself and steered.

* * *

They found Merrow's Bend by the third lightning stroke that showed it: a stone spire leaning out of the flood like a broken finger, the roof of the nave just below it, black and slick and half-submerged. The bell tower's arch still stood above the waterline, and beneath it, if you got close enough, there was a ledge of masonry wide enough for a boat to nestle against, sheltered from the worst of the wind.

Getting there was the work of a lifetime. Bram worked the tiller in short, brutal corrections, reading the current by the debris streaming past—branches, a drowned door, a dead sheep turning slowly—and Isandro read the water too, calling depths by the color of it, calling obstructions a half-second before they surfaced. A fence post loomed out of the dark and Isandro's arm shot out, fending the hull off it with the boat hook before Bram even saw it.

"Port side," Isandro said calmly. "There's a roof ridge under you. Two oars' length."

"How in the salted hells can you see that?"

"I can't. I can hear it. Water breaks different over stone."

Bram had no reply to that. Eleven years on this water and she'd never thought to listen to it. She filed the fact away in the place where she kept things that unsettled her about the desert ranger, a place growing crowded despite her best efforts.

They came under the tower arch and the wind dropped away all at once, cut off like a door closing. The sudden quiet rang in Bram's ears. Rain still fell in the open beyond, a silver-gray roaring curtain, but here in the lee of God's abandoned house there was only the drip of the eaves and the slap of water against stone and the *Verity*'s tired timbers ticking as they cooled.

"Tie off," Bram said. "Two lines. The water's still rising—it'll climb another foot by morning."

Isandro looped the lines around the iron ring where the church bell had once hung, testing each with her full weight. Then, without being asked, she began to rig a shelter—stretching the spare canvas from the bow cleat to the tower's stonework, pegging it low, angled so the runoff would sheet away from the sleeping space instead of pooling in it. She did it badly at first, twice, muttering to herself, and then found the logic of it and did it right.

"You've done this," Bram said. "Camped in weather."

"Desert storms are worse." Isandro ducked under the canvas and emerged a moment later with the small spirit stove from the stern locker, setting it on the flat of the thwart. "Sand gets into everything. Your teeth. Your grief." She struck a spark, and the burner caught with a soft blue cough. "Water at least has the decency to stay outside."

Bram stared at her. "Did you just make a joke?"

"I'm told it happens occasionally. Usually in bad weather."

"It's extremely bad weather."

"There you are, then."

* * *

They ate cold rice and smoked fish, passing the tin plate back and forth without ceremony, and drank rainwater caught in the upturned bail-scoop because neither of them trusted the flood. The canvas shelter breathed overhead with every gust that found its way around the tower. Below, the *Verity* took on her slow inches of water, and every hour or so one of them would climb down and bail by feel, working in the dark with the scoop, listening to the hull.

On Isandro's third descent, Bram heard her stop. Heard the scoop go still.

"What?" Bram leaned over the edge of the shelter. Lightning lit the world below: Isandro kneeling in six inches of water, her palm pressed flat against the starboard seam just forward of the mast step.

"This patch," Isandro said. "It's opening. Your father's work?"

"My work." Bram felt the defensiveness rise in her throat like heat. "I patched her in Callow's Rest with what I had. Tar and canvas and prayer. You want to criticize the sewing, wait until we're somewhere the needle won't freeze to my fingers."

"I'm not criticizing." Isandro's voice came up out of the dark, level and unhurried. "I'm saying it'll hold if we don't push her hard tomorrow. Slow water, no wake. And I'm saying whoever sewed it sewed it tight enough to last this long in a monsoon, which is more than most boats would give you." A pause. "You did good work."

Bram opened her mouth to say something sharp and found nothing there. The compliment sat in her chest, warm and strange, like a coal passed hand to hand. When had anyone last told her the work was good? People told her she was stubborn, that she was her father's daughter, that she should smile more. Nobody watched her stitching.

"Go to sleep, ranger," she said finally, gruff, and heard Isandro's low huff of breath that might have been laughter.

* * *

Bram took first watch because it was her boat and her water, and because she didn't yet trust sleep in the company of a woman who lied about small things—the way Isandro had dodged every question about herself since Callow's Rest, deflecting with competence instead of answers. Competence was its own kind of concealment. Bram knew that. She used it herself.

But sometime past midnight, shivering under the shared blanket they'd finally admitted the cold demanded, she woke to find Isandro awake, sitting propped against the tower stone with the compass open in her cupped hands, shielding its face from the wet.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," Bram murmured.

"So are you."

"That's my line. You're stealing my lines now."

Isandro tilted the compass so the lightning-flash caught the needle. It swung, settled, swung. "It doesn't point north anymore," she said quietly. "Hasn't for years. Out on the flats it points toward the Guild house in Saltmere. Every compass I've ever owned ends up pointing there. Like it knows who owns me."

Bram propped herself on an elbow. In the dark, with the storm erasing the world beyond the tower arch, it was easier to look at Isandro Sterling—at the hollows under her eyes, the scar splitting one eyebrow, the way her thumb kept moving over the compass's cracked casing like a rosary bead.

"Why do you keep it, then? Pawn it. Buy yourself a hundred miles of distance."

A long silence. Rain filled it.

"Because it was my sister's," Isandro said at last, so quietly Bram had to lean close to catch it. And then, as if alarmed at having said even that much, she closed the compass with a snap and lay back. "Sleep, navigator. Dawn comes early, and your leaky boat and I have an understanding to keep."

Bram lay staring up at the breathing canvas, listening to the storm spend itself against the stones of Merrow's Bend, and understood that she had just been given something—not trust exactly, not the whole story, but a single true coin from a purse kept tightly shut. A sister. Dead, from the way the word had sounded. A debt that pointed home like a needle.

She thought of the map beneath the thwart, her father's careful handwriting on the oilcloth, and of the man himself, dying alone on the flats with his last breath spent on a stranger's promise. Everyone she'd ever loved had been owned by something—the water, the road, the Guild, the past. Maybe that was why she'd hated her father so completely and so cheaply. It was easier than recognizing the shape of the cage.

The rain softened toward dawn, settling from roar to whisper. Bram slept again, briefly, and dreamed of a boat that didn't leak, sailing on water that had no bottom and no shore, with someone at the tiller whose face she couldn't see but whose hands she somehow knew.

She woke to gray light and Isandro already up, bailing, rigging, moving through the small routines of survival with that same unhurried economy—and to something else besides. To the eastern horizon, where the clouds had torn open just enough to show a line of white water moving against the current.

Not floodwater. Boats.

Three of them, maybe four, low and dark, running up the drowned channel toward Merrow's Bend with the wind behind them, and flying from the lead boat's mast a pennant that even at this distance Bram recognized, because everyone in the lowlands knew it: the black-and-copper of House Corvane.

"Isandro," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We have company."
