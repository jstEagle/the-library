## 7. The Thief's Trail

The dead man had been dead for two days, and the river knew it before they did.

Bram smelled it first—she was the one with the navigator's nose, tuned to every wrongness in the water—and she eased off the tiller without a word, letting the Verity drift toward the bank where the flooded willows stood waist-deep and dripping. Isandro crouched low among the cargo crates, one hand on the hilt of her knife, watching the green curtain of the shoreline slide past. Three days out of Callow's Rest, three days of rain and leaking seams and a silence between them that was slowly learning how to be comfortable, and now the river had gone quiet in a way that made the hair on her arms stand up.

"There." Bram pointed with her chin. "Past the willows. That's a boat."

It was a boat, or what remained of one—a flat-bottomed skiff, larger than the Verity, wedged into the drowned brush with its stern half-submerged. Rain pattered on its hull. Something pale hung over its gunwale like laundry left out too long.

Isandro didn't want to look closer. She looked closer.

"Odrik," she said.

* * *

They tied off beneath the willows and waded the last stretch, water cold as confession around their thighs. The thief she had chased across the salt flats—the man whose trail had led her instead to a dying prospector and a folded map that now rode against Isandro's ribs like a second heartbeat—lay slumped across his own gunwale, one arm trailing in the water, fish-nibbled and white. His throat had been opened with something narrow and professional. His eyes were open too, fixed on the grey sky, wearing an expression Isandro had seen before on men who died surprised: not the surprise of violence, but of betrayal.

She checked him anyway, because checking was what rangers did, even former ones, even ones whose badge lived in a Guild strongbox three hundred miles east. Pockets turned out. Boot soles sliced open and empty. Belt pouches gutted. Whoever had killed him had searched him with the same thoroughness Isandro would have used herself, and that told her more than the wound did.

"They took everything," Bram said from the bow of the dead man's skiff. She'd climbed aboard without hesitation, which said something about her that Isandro was still cataloguing. "Or they were looking for something specific and didn't find it."

"He was carrying a copy." Isandro straightened, wiped her hands on her trousers though there was nothing on them. "Of the map. He must have copied it after he stole it—that's why he ran south instead of east toward Corvane territory. He meant to sell it twice."

"Twice?"

"Once to whoever hired him, once to anyone else with coin. Thieves do it all the time. Insurance." She looked down at Odrik's ruined face. "Except his employers found out. And they don't strike me as people who forgive insurance."

Bram had gone still in the way she went still when she was working a problem—chin tucked, eyes narrowed at the middle distance, lips slightly parted as if tasting the air for lies. It was, Isandro had noticed, the same expression she wore when reading currents.

"So someone killed him," Bram said slowly, "and searched him, and didn't find what they wanted. Which means they know there's an original. Which means—"

"Which means they know my prospector had it." The words came out of Isandro flat and cold, and she heard in them the thing she'd been refusing to hear since Callow's Rest. "And they'll know he was dying, and dying men talk. Taverns. Waystations. Anyone he begged a drink from between here and the flats might have seen him hand something to a desert ranger with a Guild debt and a reputation for never breaking a promise."

The rain thickened. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled along the valley floor like a barrel being dragged over stone.

"You think they followed you," Bram said. Not a question.

"I think I've been so busy watching the water rise that I forgot to watch our wake." Isandro scanned the bank—the willows, the drowned fence line of some farm abandoned a generation ago, the long brown ribbon of the river bending away north. Nothing moved. That proved nothing. People like the ones who'd opened Odrik's throat were good at being nothing. "We need to be gone from here."

"Wait." Bram crouched by the dead man again, and this time she did what Isandro hadn't: she looked at his *boat*. "His supplies are still stowed. Food, mostly spoiled, but—" she pulled a tarp aside "—a signal lamp. Rope. And this."

She held up a leather folio, swollen with damp, stamped with a sigil Isandro knew from bounty notices and bad dreams: a black corvid wings-spread over crossed keys. Corvane. The syndicate's freight manifests, or a fragment of one—shipping schedules, toll payments, a route marked in fading ink from the northern passes down through the flooded lowlands.

"He was carrying their paperwork," Isandro said. "Proof of employment. He wasn't just their thief—he was their *contractor*. Documented."

"Which means they're documented too." Bram's mouth curved, sharp and humorless. "My father always said paper is the only weapon the poor are allowed to carry. I thought he was being poetic. He was being practical."

Isandro stared at her. There it was again—the thing under Bram's anger, the thing that looked nothing like anger at all. Her father had been gone less than a season and already his voice lived in her mouth.

"We take it," Isandro said. "And we go. Now."

* * *

They burned downstream with the flood current helping, Bram reading the water while Isandro bailed, the rhythm of it almost meditative—dip, lift, throw; dip, lift, throw—while the drowned orchards slid past and the rain came down in sheets that turned the whole world the color of pewter. But Isandro's mind would not settle into the rhythm. It kept circling back to the flats, to that dusk, to the prospector's blood-wet grip on her wrist.

*I'm not asking you to save me. I'm asking you to outrun me.*

She'd thought he meant the Guild. She'd thought the urgency in him was just death talking, the way death talks, making every small errand enormous. But he'd been shot by professionals—gut-shot, the slow cruel way, the way you shoot a man when you want him to lead you somewhere first. They'd let him run because a wounded man runs to his treasure. And he had run—to his daughter? No. To the daughter's town, yes, but to Isandro specifically, a stranger, because a stranger could be trusted precisely because she owed him nothing.

He'd known he was being hunted. He'd handed the map to the first honest fool he found and told her a story about a promise because the truth—*they will kill you for this*—would have made her refuse.

Or maybe not. Maybe she was building ghosts out of guilt. Dying men weren't strategists. They were just dying.

But Odrik's throat argued otherwise. Odrik had been found, followed, opened up, and searched—all within days of the prospector's death, all within the same widening circle of the hunt. The Corvane syndicate didn't spend that kind of effort on a stolen chart of fishing holes. Whatever the map showed, whatever lay under the floodwaters of Vess, it was worth killing for twice over: once to have it, once to make sure no one else did.

"Isandro." Bram's voice cut through the rain. She'd stopped bailing—no, Isandro realized, she'd stopped *talking*, and had been standing at the tiller with her head cocked for some time. "Smoke. Ahead. Right bank."

Isandro rose carefully. Through the rain-haze, a thin grey thread rose from the tree line—not hearth smoke, which rises fat and domestic, but a lean, deliberate column. A signal fire. Or a cook fire for men who didn't mind being seen, because being seen was the point.

"How far to the toll bridge?" Isandro asked.

"Half a day, if the water holds high enough to run it. Less if we push." Bram's jaw tightened. "There's a side channel past the burnt ferry landing. Shallow, twisty, but it skirts the bridge entirely. My father used it when he didn't feel like paying tolls."

"Your father sounds like he was good at not being found."

"He was good at leaving," Bram said, and there was the old flint in her voice again, but underneath it something had shifted—something that sounded, for the first time, almost like grief doing the work of understanding. "Take the bail bucket. We're going to earn our passage."

* * *

They took the side channel as the light failed, poling where the current slackened, dragging the Verity over two sandbars that hadn't been sandbars last month. The channel was barely wider than the boat in places, walled in by cane and drowned timber, and the darkness came down like a lid. Bram navigated by touch and memory and the occasional flash of lightning, murmuring to herself—*cane bend, deadfall, the split rock, keep left*—a litany she'd clearly learned young, learned from a man she claimed to hate, and the intimacy of that was almost more than Isandro could bear to witness.

They camped on a mudbank barely above the waterline, no fire, cold rations shared in silence. Isandro took first watch, sitting with her back against a cypress knee, knife drawn, listening to the rain and the river and the small wet sounds of a world drowning quietly.

Her compass sat in her pocket, and her thumb found it without her permission, worrying the worn brass rim. North. It always knew north. She envied it that.

Behind her, Bram's breathing evened into sleep, and Isandro let herself look at her in the lightning-flash—curled against the cargo crates, one hand still loosely holding the coil of rope she'd refused to stop coiling even as her eyes closed. Stubborn to the marrow. Brave in the specific, unglamorous way of people who fix things: nets, boats, other people's mistakes.

*They know there's an original,* Isandro thought. *They know a ranger has it.*

Tomorrow they'd reach the toll bridge, and beyond it the lowland villages, and beyond those the drowned rooftops of Vess, rising out of the flood like a question the monsoon asked every year. Somewhere behind them—or ahead, or both—Corvane's hunters were working the same water, following the same logic she'd followed: find the prospector's path, follow it forward.

She had spent her whole adult life chasing people. It had never once occurred to her, until this week, what it felt like from the other end.

The rain fell. The river rose. And in the dark, far off through the cane, Isandro heard a sound that froze the breath in her chest: the low, rhythmic knock of a pole against a hull. Once. Twice. Then silence—deliberate, patient silence, the kind that waited.

She reached over and put her hand on Bram's shoulder, and Bram's eyes opened instantly, fully awake, as if she'd only ever been pretending.

"They found us?" Bram whispered.

"No," Isandro whispered back. "Not yet. But they're on the water."

Above them the storm grumbled northward, and the river carried its secrets toward the sea, and somewhere in the drowning dark a second boat slipped through the cane, patient as the flood itself.
