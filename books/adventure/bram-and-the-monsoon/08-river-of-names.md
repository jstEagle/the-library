## 8. River of Names

The river had no name on any map Bram had ever seen, which was why she'd named it herself, years ago, in the privacy of her own head: the Remembering. Because that was what it did. Every monsoon it rose up out of its banks and went looking for the things people had buried—fences, foundations, old arguments—and carried them all downstream like a confession nobody had asked for.

Now it carried them.

Bram sat at the tiller of the Verity with one bare foot hooked under a cleat and watched the drowned country slide past. The lowlands were three feet deep in brown water where there should have been pasture. Fence posts stood in rows like drowned men waiting for a ferry that wasn't coming. A whole farmhouse roof floated by at midmorning, intact and absurd, a crow riding its ridgepole like a figurehead. Somewhere beneath them lay roads Bram had walked as a girl, orchards she'd stolen from, the graves of two grandparents and one dog—all of it underwater, all of it going about its business as if drowning were just another season.

"You're drifting us toward that snag," Isandro said from the bow.

"I'm not drifting. I'm *choosing* our line." Bram eased the tiller over and took them wide of the half-submerged tree, its branches fanned out of the water like something reaching. "There's a difference. Drifting is what happens to you. Choosing is what you do."

Isandro said nothing to that. She'd been saying nothing to most things since they'd left Callow's Rest two days ago, and Bram had begun keeping a tally of it—not out of spite, exactly, but the way you keep a tally of anything that doesn't add up. Isandro Sterling talked like a woman paying for every word out of a purse she couldn't afford to empty. She bailed when bailing needed doing. She rigged the storm canvas without being asked twice. She could read water the way Bram read current, spotting the shallow lies of a sandbar under six inches of flood. But ask her where she was from, and she looked at you like you'd asked to see her teeth.

The rain came and went in moods. Right now it was only a fine gray sifting, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air and let you walk through it. Bram pulled her oilskin hood back anyway. She liked the feel of it. Her mother used to say the first rains were the sky clearing its throat, and Bram had spent twenty-two years listening for the cough.

"Tell me about this channel," Isandro said, which was not a story but was at least words.

"The locals call it the Cutthroat, on account of—" Bram gestured at the water ahead, deceptively smooth between two drowned levees, "—it looks like the easy way and isn't. There's a weir under there somewhere, washed out three floods back. Old man Sarno hit it with a grain barge and lost his whole cargo and most of his temper. We go around, along the east levee, even though it costs us an hour."

"It doesn't look deep."

"That's the trick of it. That's always the trick of it." Bram found herself smiling despite the gray day. "My father taught me that. First thing he ever taught me, maybe the only thing that stuck. *The easy-looking way is the one that kills you.* He said it about rivers and he meant it about everything."

Something moved behind Isandro's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "He sounds like he knew water."

"He knew water the way some men know drink." Bram heard the edge come into her own voice and didn't bother filing it down. "He loved it and it ruined him and he kept going back. Every dry season he'd be home long enough to mend a net and tell me I'd grown, and every wet season he'd be gone chasing whatever it was he chased. Gold, mostly. Or the idea of gold. He had a head full of maps and a heart full of holes and he tried to fill both at once."

"And your mother?"

"Dead eleven years. Fever, the year the rains came late and the wells went bad." Bram adjusted the tiller more than the line required, just to give her hands something honest to do. "After that it was him and me, and him-and-me mostly meant me alone with a boat and a grudge. He'd send money when he remembered. He'd send letters full of weather. Once he sent me a compass with my name scratched on the back, which I sold inside a month, because sentiment doesn't patch a sail."

That fish turned behind Isandro's eyes again. Bram caught it this time, or thought she did—something like recognition, quickly drowned.

"What?" Bram said.

"Nothing. Keep her pointed at the levee."

"No. Not nothing." Bram let the skiff drift a lazy degree off her line, enough that Isandro would have to notice. "You did a thing with your face just now. I've been watching you do it for two days. Every time I mention him—the prospector, Halloway, my father—you do this small flinch, like a rope going taut. So either you knew him better than you've said, or you're carrying something heavier than a map, and either way I'd like to know which."

"We made a trade," Isandro said evenly. "A map for a promise. That's the whole of it."

"The whole of it." Bram laughed, short and unkind, at the river more than at her. "You know what I've learned about men who say 'that's the whole of it'? The whole of it is never the whole of it. My father's dying words to a stranger were 'deliver this to my daughter,' and you want me to believe there's nothing else in his pockets? No letter? No explanation? Twenty years of leaving and his last act is a parcel?"

"He was dying fast," Isandro said. "Gut-shot men don't dictate memoirs."

"Gut-shot men choose their last words carefully. That's the one speech everybody gets to rehearse." Bram swung the bow around properly now, done with the game, and let the Verity run straight down the flooded lane between the levees. "So here's mine, since we're trading. You want to know how I got so good at reading water? Because reading water was the only conversation my father and I ever reliably had. He'd come home, and we couldn't talk—didn't know how, neither of us—but we could take the boat out and he'd point at a stretch of flat brown nothing and say *what do you see*, and if I got it right, if I saw the bar under the surface, the dead water hiding the drop-off—that was as close to love as he ever came. And I got good at it. I got so good at seeing what's hidden under a smooth surface that it's ruined me for people."

She looked at Isandro straight on, rain beading on her lashes.

"So. What's under your surface, ranger?"

* * *

Isandro didn't answer right away. She sat in the bow with her forearms on her knees, watching the water, and Bram gave her the length of a full minute before she decided silence was itself an answer.

"There it is again," Bram said. "The flinch."

"My name is Isandro Sterling," Isandro said at last, as if reciting it off a document. "I'm a ranger, third class, out of the Saltwater Guild charter. I have a debt. That's who I am."

"That's what a ledger says. I asked who you are."

"They're the same thing, where I'm from."

"Well, where I'm from, they're not, and I'm rowing the boat." Bram shipped the spare oar and came forward along the gunwale, careful, deliberate, until she was close enough to see the salt-cracked corners of Isandro's eyes. "You tracked a thief across the flats alone. You held a dying stranger's hand while he bled out. You've slept sitting up for two nights with one hand on that map roll like it might grow legs. And you haven't told me one true thing about yourself except your name and your debt, and I'd wager the debt's a lie too, or at least dressed up in one."

Isandro's jaw worked. For a moment Bram thought she'd actually pushed through—thought the surface would break and whatever swam underneath would come up gasping.

"I had a sister," Isandro said.

And then stopped. Just—stopped, like a man walking into a door he'd forgotten was there.

Bram waited. The rain thickened, softened, thinned. Somewhere off to the north thunder cleared its throat the way her mother used to say the sky did, and the water dimpled with a thousand small fists.

"She liked the same trick," Isandro said finally, quieter. "Reading what's underneath. She could look at a person and tell you what they were hiding. It was insufferable." A ghost of something crossed her mouth—not quite a smile, but related to one, the way ash is related to fire. "She'd have liked you. She'd have taken one look at you and told me exactly what you were doing just now, pushing, and why, and she'd have been right about all of it."

"And what am I doing?"

"Making me pay for his sins by making me pay for mine." Isandro looked up then, and her eyes were dry and hard and terribly tired. "I don't have stories, Bram. That's not evasion, it's arithmetic. Everything I had went into a debt, and everything the debt didn't take, the desert did. There's no river of names behind me. There's a flat white emptiness with my footprints in it, and the wind's been eating them for years."

The words landed harder than Bram expected. She'd wanted to wound, or at least to crack; she hadn't expected to feel the crack open under her own feet too.

"That's not nothing," Bram said slowly. "That's the opposite of nothing. People who have nothing left don't guard it like that."

"Don't romanticize my poverty."

"Don't mistake my curiosity for cruelty." Bram held her ground, knee against the thwart, rain running off her chin. "I spent my whole childhood loving a man who wouldn't tell me where he went. He'd come back smelling of salt and secrets and hand me a trinket and call it an explanation. And I swore—I swore on his boat, on our boat, on this boat—that I would never do that to anyone. Never be the one with the closed face across the table. And now I'm stuck on a leaking skiff with a woman who does it better than he ever did, and I can't—"

Her voice went. Just went, mid-sentence, the way a candle goes, and she hated it, hated the weakness of it, and pressed on anyway because the alternative was worse.

"I can't watch someone else do it. Not again. Not when the last person who kept his secrets took twenty years to explain himself and had to die to do it."

The rain filled the silence. The Verity rocked on the flood, patient as a cradle.

"I'm not him," Isandro said.

"No. He at least brought presents."

That startled a sound out of Isandro—half laugh, half breath, gone as fast as it came, but real. Bram clung to it like a handhold.

"Ask me something," Isandro said abruptly. "One question. Anything. I'll answer it true."

Bram considered the whole catalog of questions she'd been hoarding—the debt, the Guild, the scar on Isandro's forearm, the way she checked the horizon every quarter hour like something owed her money there. All of it. And what came out instead, because grief is stupid and honest:

"Why did you take the promise? You didn't know him. Dying strangers make promises at rangers every day, I'd imagine. Why carry his map two hundred miles?"

Isandro was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Bram thought the offer had expired.

"Because he called me by name," she said at last. "Not ranger. Not tracker. He looked at me like I was a person standing there, and he said, *Sterling, I need someone who keeps their word.* Nobody's asked me for my word in six years. They ask for my badge, my boat, my labor. He asked for my word." Her thumb moved over the compass at her belt, an unconscious gesture, the way Bram touched the tiller. "It was the only thing anyone had offered to leave in my hands instead of take out of them."

Bram exhaled. Something in her chest unclenched by a single degree—not forgiveness, not yet, but the loosening of a knot.

"All right," she said. "That's a start. One truth per day. I'll hold you to it."

"One's all I can afford."

"Then we'll go slow. Like the east levee." Bram went aft and took up the tiller again, and the Verity leaned into the current, obedient, alive under her hands. "We'll get there. Both of us. Even if it costs an hour."

They ran on through the soft rain, past the tops of drowned trees and the ghosts of fences, and Bram named the country for her as they went—Sarno's field, the Widow Pell's orchard, the church at Marrow Bend whose bell still rang sometimes when the floodwater rocked it—and Isandro listened, and said little, but she listened with her whole body, and Bram counted that as payment on a debt of her own.

It was near dusk when Bram saw it first: a dark shape snagged in the willows ahead, hung up on a drowned fence line, rocking gently in the current. Too big for driftwood. Wrong shape for a log.

She put the tiller over and brought them closer, and closer, and then stopped wanting to bring them closer at all.

"Isandro," she said quietly. "Is that a body?"
