## 9. The Toll Bridge

The river they'd been following since dawn wasn't on any map Bram owned—or if it was, it had a different name there. She called it the Serein, and said it had been a creek before the monsoons turned generous, and that her father used to joke it changed its name every season out of shame for what it did to the banks.

By midmorning the Serein had widened into something with opinions. It carried whole trees past them, root-balls first, like drowned men swimming. The current leaned hard against the Verity's patched hull, and Bram stood at the steering oar with her weight low and her jaw set, reading the water the way Isandro read tracks—noticing everything, explaining nothing.

Then Bram went still in a way that made Isandro's hand drift toward her knife.

"Smoke," Bram said.

Isandro smelled it a moment later: woodsmoke and tar and under both of them something sharper, like hot metal. Around the next bend, where the river narrowed between two bluffs, a bridge crossed from bank to bank—timber pilings driven deep into the flood-swollen mud, a plank deck maybe eight feet above the waterline, and strung across its length, garlands of signal flags that snapped and cracked in the wind like a line of small angry birds.

A toll bridge. And not a legitimate one.

"Corvane," Isandro said.

"You can tell from here?"

"I can tell from the flags." She counted them without meaning to—ranger habit, cataloguing terrain. "Guild crossings fly blue at the ends, white in the middle. Corvane flies whatever they've stolen most recently. That green-and-gold is a merchant house banner out of Callow's Rest. I watched them auction it off after the house defaulted."

Bram's mouth twisted. "Charming."

Below the bridge, moored to the pilings, sat two flat-bottomed barges with canvas awnings and mounted crossbows at their bows—the kind of armament no toll collector needed against fishermen. A chain hung slack in the water between the barges, ready to be raised. On the near bank, a checkpoint of raw lumber and corrugated tin squatted at the foot of the bridge ramp, and Isandro could see figures moving behind it. Four, maybe five. One wore a red sash, which meant an officer, which meant someone who could think rather than merely obey.

"We go around," Bram said, already easing the oar.

"There's no around." Isandro nodded upstream, where the bluffs drew together and the current funneled through a gap barely wider than the Verity. "That's whitewater through there this time of year. We'd be swamped inside a hundred yards, and we'd come out the far side in pieces, if we came out at all. Downstream's worse—they'll have another crossing by nightfall, and we'll have spent the day proving we're afraid of bridges."

"So we're not afraid of bridges."

"I'm afraid of drowning," Isandro said. "It's different."

Bram looked at her for a long moment, rain starting to stipple the river around them, fine as scattered salt. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and brought the Verity back around toward the checkpoint.

"All right, ranger," she said. "Show me how you're going to get us past five armed men who are looking for exactly two people carrying exactly one map."

* * *

The answer, Isandro knew, was that she couldn't. Not with lies. Liars got searched, and searched meant the oilskin packet against her ribs, and the oilskin packet meant everything ended right there under a tin roof while the rain came down.

So it would have to be the truth, or enough of it. Which meant the thing she had sworn never to use again unless she was dragging a body out of a ravine.

Her ranger credentials lived in a waxed-leather wallet sewn into the lining of her coat, and she hadn't opened that seam in four years. Her thumbnail found the stitches by feel as Bram brought the skiff alongside the landing planks. The leather had stiffened around the papers inside; when she finally worked the wallet free, it came out smelling of mildew and old decisions.

The badge itself was a disc of hammered bronze, the Saltwater Guild's wave-and-anchor struck on one face, her name and commission number on the other. *I. STERLING. RANGER, SECOND CLASS.* The metal had gone dull in the dark. She rubbed it once against her sleeve and it caught the gray light and threw it back, and for a moment she was twenty-three years old again and the badge had felt like a future instead of a leash.

"You're a Guild ranger?" Bram said quietly. Not surprised, exactly. Recalibrating.

"I was. Am. The paperwork doesn't care whether I feel like one." Isandro slid the badge into her breast pocket, face out. "Rangers have transit authority on all navigable waterways. Guild charter, article nine. Even syndicate checkpoints honor it, because the alternative is the Guild sending auditors, and everyone is more afraid of auditors than of each other."

"And they're looking for us."

"They're looking for a thief and a prospector's map. They are not looking for a Guild ranger conducting an escort." Isandro met her eyes. "That's the bluff. I'm not hiding you. I'm *assigned* to you. You're a civilian consultant, a hydrographic surveyor, and I'm transporting you and your father's survey work downstream for Guild review. Every word true except the ones that matter."

Bram stared at her. "You've done this before."

"No," Isandro said. "But I arrested people who had."

She stepped onto the landing planks first, boots wet, coat buttoned to the throat, badge showing. Behind her she heard Bram gather up the netting and the sounding lines and the rolled charts that were mostly Bram's own honest work, and arrange herself into something that looked professional and slightly bored. Good. Boredom was armor. Guilty people couldn't afford to be bored.

The checkpoint smelled of wet timber and kerosene. Under the tin roof, a clerk sat at a plank desk beneath an oilcloth awning, a ledger open before him, a stove ticking beside him, and a boy of perhaps fifteen feeding papers into a drying rack. Two guards flanked the desk with crossbows slung but hands free, and the officer in the red sash stood apart, studying the river through a brass spyglass with the unhurried attention of a man being paid to be thorough.

The clerk looked up. He was young, soft-handed, with ink stains to the second knuckle and the particular flat courtesy of someone whose job was to write things down and never to decide anything.

"Purpose of passage?"

"Official Guild business." Isandro set her badge on the desk, face up, and let the silence do its work. She'd learned that from Maren Voss, ironically—the pause after the credential, long enough for the other person to fill it with their own anxiety.

The clerk picked up the badge. Turned it over. His eyes moved across the stamped name, and Isandro watched him check it against some internal ledger of things he'd been told to watch for, and saw nothing in his face either way, which was worse than either answer.

"Ranger Sterling," he read aloud. "Second class." He glanced past her shoulder at the Verity, at the patched hull, at Bram standing among her nets with rain beading in her dark hair. "And your passenger?"

"Bram Halloway. Hydrographic surveyor." Isandro kept her voice level, unhurried, faintly impatient—the voice of a woman who had better places to stand in the rain. "Daughter of Aldous Halloway. The Guild contracted his flood surveys before his death. I'm escorting his remaining work downstream for review by the water commission."

Something flickered at the edge of her vision. The officer in the red sash had lowered his spyglass.

"Halloway," the officer repeated. He crossed to the desk with the easy stride of a man who owned the ground he walked on, or believed he did. Up close he was younger than his sash, with a neat beard and a dueling scar through one eyebrow, and eyes that did arithmetic. "Aldous Halloway. There was a bulletin about a Halloway. Prospector. Deceased, I believe. Something about property of his coming to light."

"His survey work," Isandro said. "Which is why I'm here. The Guild doesn't leave dead men's contracts floating around the lowlands during monsoon season. Half the drownings downstream happen because somebody's flood data is out of date." She tapped the badge where it lay on the desk. "Article nine gives me transit authority. If you want to dispute it, your syndicate can take it up with the Guild's audit office, and I promise you—" she let the ghost of a smile touch her mouth, "—you don't want that. Nobody wants that."

The scarred eyebrow rose. For three full seconds the officer looked at her, and Isandro stood inside the look the way she'd once stood inside dust storms: breathing shallow, wasting nothing, letting the emptiness do the work.

Then he smiled back, thin as a knife laid flat. "Of course, Ranger. We honor the Guild's charter. We're law-abiding people." He gestured, and the clerk dipped his pen. "Standard procedure requires all transits to be logged. Name, vessel, cargo, destination."

"Cargo is survey documents and navigational instruments," Isandro said. "Destination, Serel's Bend, then downriver as the commission directs."

"And your business at Serel's Bend?"

"Flood assessment." She held his eyes. "The rains this year are running ahead of the ten-year mean. Someone has to tell the villages what's coming. That someone is me."

It was, at least, true. That was the strange grace of the whole rotten arrangement—that the lie she was telling had a floor of truth underneath it, and she could stand on it.

The clerk wrote. She heard the nib scratch and thought, absurdly, of the prospector's blood soaking into salt crust, of promises made to dying men, of how every road she'd walked in four years had led her to stand in the rain while a boy with ink-stained fingers wrote her name into a syndicate's book.

*Name, vessel, cargo, destination.*

There it was. The trap she was walking into with her eyes open, because the only alternative was the chain rising out of the water between those barges, and crossbow bolts, and the map going to the bottom of the Serein or into Corvane's strongbox, and a dead man's daughter watching her fail him.

The clerk finished the entry and blew on the ink. "Toll's waived for Guild transit," he said, almost apologetically. "Officer Kestrel?"

The officer—Kestrel—waved a hand, magnanimous. "Waived. Of course." He stepped closer as Isandro retrieved her badge, close enough that she could smell the bay rum on him, and spoke at a volume calibrated for exactly two people. "Strange season, Ranger Sterling. Lots of people on the river claiming to be somebody. We had a fellow through last week with a Guild warrant—he was very convincing. Turned out he'd bought it off a corpse." His smile didn't reach his eyes. "We hanged him from the bridge. You probably passed it. The one with the missing plank."

"I noticed the missing plank," Isandro said.

"Mm. So you understand why we're careful." He straightened. "Safe passage, Ranger. Give my regards to the water commission. And if you should happen to see anything on the river worth seeing—treasure stories, sunken cities, that sort of nonsense—you'll report it, won't you? Law-abiding people, all of us."

"Everyone's law-abiding," Isandro said, "until the audit office arrives."

He laughed at that, genuinely, and waved them through, and Isandro collected Bram with a jerk of her chin and walked up the ramp onto the bridge as though bridges were things she crossed every day without her heart counting the planks.

* * *

Halfway across, the wind gusted and the flag garlands snapped taut overhead, and below them Isandro saw the hanging place Kestrel had mentioned—a beam jutting from the piling, rope still knotted, board still nailed up with charcoal letters she chose not to read. The river ran fast and brown beneath it, carrying its trees, carrying everything away.

"Don't look down," Bram murmured.

"I'm not looking down."

"You're looking at the rope."

"I'm assessing the rope."

They came off the far end of the bridge onto a muddy track that wound up through scrub willows, and only when the checkpoint dropped out of sight behind the first bend did Bram speak again, and then it burst out of her all at once:

"*Hydrographic surveyor.* You made me a—my father spent thirty years being laughed at for calling himself that, and you handed it to me like a—like a *pass*, like it was nothing—"

"It isn't nothing. It's the truth wearing a uniform." Isandro kept walking. "Your father surveyed floodgates. That's hydrography. I just put a Guild seal on it."

"You put your *name* on it." Bram caught her sleeve, pulled her around, and her eyes were bright and furious and frightened all at once. "They logged you, Isandro. I watched the clerk write it. *Sterling, I., escorting Halloway survey material.* In a Corvane ledger. When they figure out what we're actually carrying—"

"When they figure it out, they'll know where we went and what we took, which means they'll know where we're going, which means we'd better be faster than their messengers." Isandro freed her sleeve gently. "I know what I did, Bram. I've known since I saw the flags."

"Then why—"

"Because the alternative was the chain." She looked back downriver, at the bridge shrinking into the rain-gray distance, at the smoke of its stove, at the small figures already too far away to read. "Listen to me. Somewhere between here and Vess, that ledger gets copied and sent ahead. Kestrel strikes me as a man who covers himself. By tomorrow night, someone in Corvane's chain of command will know a Guild ranger named Sterling passed his bridge escorting Halloway survey documents. And Maren Voss will hear my name attached to yours, and she'll stop sending warnings and start sending boats."

Bram was quiet. Rain dripped off the willows. Somewhere upstream, thunder cleared its throat.

"So we don't stop," Bram said at last.

"So we don't stop." Isandro turned up the track, toward the next bend of the swollen river, toward everything waiting downstream. "We run the water while it runs high. Your father's gates are at Vess, and Vess is six days' sail if the weather holds—"

The weather, as if offended by the optimism, split open above them, and the monsoon came down like a verdict.
