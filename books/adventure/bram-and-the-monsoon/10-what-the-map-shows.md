## 10. What the Map Shows

The rain had found a rhythm by the second night out of Callow's Rest — not the violent arrival of that first storm, but something steadier, a drumming on the Verity's patched canvas awning that Bram had begun to think of as almost companionable. Almost. The skiff still took water. She still bailed it every hour, and Isandro still woke without being asked to spell her, and neither of them had said anything about how easily they'd fallen into that pattern, because saying it would make it a thing, and things could be taken away.

They had tied up for the night in the lee of a drowned ferry landing, its pilings rising black and crooked from six feet of brown water, the ferry house itself half-sunk, roofline tilted like a hat knocked askew. Somewhere in the flooded timber below them, fish were nesting in what had once been someone's parlor. Bram tried not to think about that too hard.

She had the map spread across the little table under the lamp, weighted at the corners with a tin cup, a coil of line, a river stone she'd picked up years ago and never explained to anyone, and Isandro's knife, borrowed without asking because Isandro was asleep and Bram wanted it near her hand while she worked. That was another thing she wasn't going to examine.

The map itself was a disappointment at first glance. She'd expected treasure — everyone expected treasure from Vess, from any of the drowned cities. Gold leaf, jeweled reliquaries, the sunken wealth of a kingdom that had made the mistake of building too close to a river with ambitions. Her father had been chasing exactly that kind of fortune his whole life, or so she'd believed, and she'd built a whole architecture of resentment on top of that belief: the man who left to get rich, who came back empty-handed and left again, who died gut-shot in the flats holding nothing but a chart to somebody else's gold.

So when she unrolled it and saw no X marks, no dotted lines to vaults, no annotations reading *here lies the treasury*, she'd felt something sour and vindicated rise in her chest. *Of course,* she'd thought. *Even dead, he brings me nothing.*

But she was a navigator before she was anything else, and navigators don't read maps the way other people do. Other people look for destinations. Navigators look for intent.

* * *

She started with the ink.

It was good ink — iron-gall, the expensive kind that goes brown as it ages, not the cheap soot-wash prospectors usually carried. He'd bought this recently. Within the last few years. Which meant he hadn't drawn this in some fevered youth and carried it around like a relic; he'd sat down somewhere, deliberately, with money he probably didn't have, and rendered this.

And it was beautifully rendered. That was the thing that kept catching her. She'd forgotten — or chosen to forget — that her father had been trained. Not guild-trained, but apprenticed once, briefly, to a hydrographer in Callow's Rest before the wanderlust took him. He used to draw channels in the mud for her when she was small, explaining how water decided where to go, how it always chose the path of least stubbornness. *Water is honest, Brammy. It never pretends to be somewhere it isn't. People could learn from it.* She'd thought it was just talk, the kind of pretty nonsense leaving-men told their daughters to excuse themselves.

The map disagreed.

It showed Vess — the drowned city, three days' sail south into the deep floodlands — but not as a treasure plan. It showed the city the way a surgeon shows a body: cut open, layered, annotated. The rooftops were there, yes, marked with depths beneath the present waterline. But around them, in a careful web of lines and symbols she recognized only slowly, was something else entirely. Channels. Culverts. And then, unmistakable once she saw them, drawn in a heavier hand and circled twice: seven great structures arranged along the city's northern rim, each labeled in her father's cramped, slanting script.

*Floodgates.*

Bram sat back. The lamp flame leaned in a draft off the water and righted itself.

Seven gates. She knew what that meant, because every child in the lowlands knew what that meant, the way children in fire country know the smell of smoke. The old kingdoms had built flood-control works — massive ones, gateworks and diversion channels, engineering from the age when people still remembered how to move rivers instead of merely arguing with them. Most of those works had failed or silted shut centuries ago. That was why the monsoon floods now ran wild, why whole villages downstream went under every few years, why the lowland councils spent more on coffins than on schools.

But if even two or three of Vess's gates could be cleared and made to work —

She pulled the map closer and began, properly, to read it.

Her father had done more than find the gates. He'd surveyed them. Each one had depth soundings noted beside it — *silt depth, four feet; mechanism status unknown; approach channel clear at high water* — and condition assessments in a shorthand she had to decode letter by letter. Three of the seven he'd marked with a symbol she didn't know until she found the key tucked in the margin, small as an apology: a circle with a slash. *Unrecoverable.* Two more got a triangle. *Possible, costly.* And two — two — got a star.

*Functional. Clearing required only.*

Two working floodgates. Her father had found two working floodgates in the corpse of a drowned city, and he'd mapped the approach channels, the silt loads, the seasonal water levels, everything a crew of engineers would need to open them and turn the monsoon's fury aside before it reached the villages strung along the lower delta. Villages like hers. Villages like the ones they'd passed all week, stilted and sandbagged and praying.

This wasn't a treasure chart. This was a rescue plan, drawn by a man with expensive ink and no money, over years, in secret.

Bram discovered she was gripping the table edge hard enough to hurt.

* * *

Isandro woke sometime past midnight — Bram heard the small sounds of her, the rustle, the hand going instinctively to where the knife should have been and finding the space empty. A ranger's habits. Bram didn't return the knife. She didn't look up either.

"You're still awake." Isandro's voice was rough with sleep. She came and stood at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, hair flattened on one side, looking — Bram noted with irritation — entirely too composed for a woman who'd been unconscious thirty seconds ago.

"Come here," Bram said. "I need your eyes. You've seen more charts than I have."

Isandro hesitated, then crossed the small space and leaned over the table, shoulder nearly touching Bram's shoulder. Bram was aware of it. She decided not to be.

"Tell me what you see," Bram said.

Isandro studied it in silence for a long moment. "A city," she said finally. "Old survey work. Very fine." Her finger traced the rim of gates. "These are fortifications?"

"Floodgates."

That landed. Bram watched it land — watched Isandro go very still in the way she did, the way she went still before anything mattered.

"Say that again."

"Vess has floodgates. Old kingdom works. My father surveyed them." Bram's voice came out flatter than she intended, which was how she knew how much it was costing her. "Two of them are functional. Or were, when he sounded them. Open them during peak flood and you divert the water east, through the old spillway channels, away from the delta villages. Thousands of people, Isandro. Every family between here and the sea that loses a roof or a child every time the monsoon runs high." She tapped the margin key. "He labeled them himself. This isn't speculation. This is engineering."

Isandro straightened slowly. In the lamplight her face had gone unreadable, which Bram was learning was its own kind of information. "Then why does everyone believe it's treasure?"

"Because treasure is simpler." Bram laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because a map to gold makes sense coming from my father. A map that could save ten thousand people—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know what I've called him? For fifteen years? A deadbeat. A dreamer. A man who loved rivers more than his own daughter. And the whole time—"

"The whole time he was doing this." Isandro touched the map, lightly, the way you touch something sacred or fragile. "Alone. Unfunded. While syndicates chased him for it."

"And killed him for it." Bram looked up. "Odrik stole a copy. Odrik is dead. Whoever hired him thinks this leads to gold, and when they figure out it doesn't — when they realize what it actually is — do you know what happens?"

Isandro didn't answer, which was an answer.

"They don't destroy it," Bram said. "That's the thing. They're not going to burn it. They'll use it. Corvane, or whoever holds it — they'll clear the gates themselves, or they'll sell the knowledge, or worse, they'll keep it locked away and charge the delta for every dry year. A thing like this isn't a rescue in the wrong hands, Isandro. It's a ransom note. Control the water, control everyone who needs it. That's older than any kingdom on this map."

The lamp hissed. Out on the black water, something large moved — a drifting log, maybe, or something with opinions about moored boats — and settled.

"So it's not a prize," Isandro said quietly. "It's a lever. And we're carrying it through country full of people who want levers."

"Yes."

"And your father knew that. Knew what he was carrying. Knew why men were following him." Isandro's jaw tightened. "He gave it to a stranger dying in the salt flats rather than let it be taken."

"He gave it to me." Bram heard her own voice crack on the word and hated it. "Through you. He didn't know you. He was bleeding out, and he bet his life's work on a promise from a woman he met in the last hour of his life, because—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Because there was nobody else. Because I wouldn't have come even if he'd sent for me. I told him — the last time he left, I told him not to come back. I told him the next time I saw his face I'd—"

She didn't finish it. Isandro didn't fill the silence with comfort, which Bram was grateful for. She just stood there, steady as a piling, until Bram lowered her hand and breathed.

"I delivered bodies for the Guild for six years," Isandro said at last. "Not corpses — documents. Ledgers. Things that decided whether families kept their boats or lost them, and I never once asked what was in them, because asking would have made me responsible, and I couldn't afford to be responsible for anything except my own debt." She looked down at the map. "This is different."

"It's different," Bram agreed. "Because now we know."

"That's exactly what I mean." Isandro met her eyes. "You can't unknow this. Whatever we do with it — deliver it, sell it, sink it in the floodlands — we choose, and the delta lives with the choice. That's not a debt, Bram. Debts end. This doesn't."

Bram looked at the map again — at the two starred gates, at her father's careful, hopeful, secret handwriting, at the margin key small as an apology. *He knew,* she thought. *He knew what it was worth and what it would cost, and he drew it anyway, year after year, alone, with ink he couldn't afford.* All those years she'd spent hating him for chasing gold, and he'd been chasing something that couldn't be pocketed at all.

Grief arrived in her chest like floodwater finding a channel — not dramatic, just inevitable, filling every low place.

"We take it to the lowland council," she said. "In Vess itself, if we can — the gatehouse records need verifying against his soundings, and the council engineers winter at the Vess overlook. But the council gets it. Not the Guild. Not Corvane. The council."

"The Guild won't like that."

"The Guild can stand in line behind the syndicate and drown together."

Something flickered across Isandro's face — surprise, and under it, unmistakably, delight. "You've changed your tune about this delivery."

"No." Bram rolled the map carefully, along its original creases, her hands steadier than she deserved. "I haven't changed my tune. I still wish he'd stayed. I still wish I'd never seen this. I'd trade every gate in Vess for one more bad homecoming, one more argument on the dock." She tied the cord. "But he trusted me with the truest thing he ever made, and he was right to, and I am going to finish it if it kills us both. So."

She looked up and found Isandro watching her with an expression she couldn't name — something open and startled, like a woman seeing land after believing herself permanently at sea.

"So," Isandro echoed softly.

Bram snuffed the lamp, and in the sudden dark the rain filled the world, steady and honest, going exactly where it was told by nothing but gravity and ground.

She was almost asleep when Isandro spoke again from her corner, quiet enough that Bram could pretend not to hear.

"Bram. The clerk at the toll bridge. He logged my name."

Bram opened her eyes. Across the dark skiff, she could just make out Isandro's silhouette, upright, listening to the rain.

"Guild enforcers read those logs," Isandro said. "If Voss knows where we crossed, she knows where we're headed." A pause. "We'll have company tomorrow."

Bram lay in the dark, the map's weight seeming to press through the deck boards into her spine, and listened to the monsoon gathering strength somewhere to the south — over the drowned city, over the gates, over whatever was coming upriver to meet them.

"Then we sail tonight," she said, and reached for the lamp.
