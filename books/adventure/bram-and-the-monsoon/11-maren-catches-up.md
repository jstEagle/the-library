## 11. Maren Catches Up

They sailed through the night, and the night did not want them.

The rain came in sheets that turned the flooded lowlands into a single gray country without edges — no horizon, no shoreline, only water and the sound of water and the small island of lamplight where Bram stood at the tiller, reading currents by instinct and memory. Isandro bailed. She bailed until her shoulders burned and the bail of the Verity's bilge became a rhythm as natural as breathing: dip, lift, throw, dip. The skiff took water the way an old man takes insults, steadily, without complaint, and gave it back to the river one bucket at a time.

Somewhere past midnight the rain slackened to a drizzle, and the world reassembled itself in pieces. First the sound of frogs, thousands of them, singing from drowned orchards. Then the black shapes of treetops breaking the surface like the backs of swimming animals. Then, far off, a smear of orange light against the clouds — some village burning its lamps against the wet.

"Sleep," Bram said. "I'll take us through the narrows."

Isandro didn't argue. She wrapped herself in the oilcloth and lay against the mast step, and sleep took her the way deep water takes a stone — fast, total, and cold.

She dreamed of the salt flats. In the dream she was tracking someone whose footprints kept erasing themselves behind her, and every time she looked down, the trail was gone, and every time she looked up, the figure was closer, and when it finally reached her it was her own face looking back, wearing the Guild's brass badge, saying *you signed, you signed, you signed.*

She woke to sunlight on her eyelids and the smell of frying fish.

Bram had anchored in the lee of a drowned granary, its upper story standing clear of the water like a monument to some forgotten harvest. A line trailed off the stern with three silver bodies on it. The morning was impossibly gentle after the night — pale gold light, steam rising off the floodwater, birds arguing in the treetops.

"Eat," Bram said, handing her a fish crisped on a tin plate over a spirit stove. "You look like something the river rejected."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome." Bram sat across from her on the thwart, knees drawn up, eating with quick efficient bites. Her eyes were shadowed but bright. "We made good distance. By my reckoning we're past the toll bridge's reach. If Voss is coming, she's coming from Callow's Rest, which means she's a full day behind us."

"If she's coming."

"She's coming." Bram said it without cruelty, just certainty, the way you'd say *the sun will rise.* "You told me yourself. The clerk logged your name."

Isandro ate. The fish was good — fresh-caught, salted with the coarse grey salt Bram kept in a stoppered jar, tasting faintly of the sea the floods had carried inland. She let herself have ten minutes of the morning. Ten minutes of warmth and quiet and the sight of Bram Halloway with the sun catching the wet ends of her hair.

Then the ten minutes ended, because that was what ten minutes did.

It was Isandro who heard it first — a sound under the birdsong, under the lap of water against the hull. An engine. Not the coughing two-stroke of a fishing boat but the deep, even thrum of something heavier, something built for cargo or for war.

Bram heard it a heartbeat later. Her head came up like a hound's.

"That's not local," she said.

"No."

"It's coming up the channel."

"I know."

They moved without speaking about moving. Bram pulled anchor; Isandro struck the stove and stowed the plate. But the Verity was a patched skiff with a jury-rigged sail, and whatever was coming around the bend of the drowned orchard was faster, and they both knew it, and neither said that either.

The boat appeared through the trees at mid-morning: a Guild cutter, thirty feet of black-hulled steel, its smokestack trailing a thin grey ribbon, its deck crowded with armed men in oilskins. And standing at the bow, one hand resting on the rail, her coat dry despite everything, her face carrying the particular patience of a woman who has never once been surprised by the behavior of other people —

Maren Voss.

* * *

"There is no point running," Voss called across the water, and her voice carried the way voices carry over still floodwater, flat and clear and impossible to pretend you hadn't heard. "Your hull is slower than mine and your pilot knows it. Come alongside and we'll talk like professionals."

Bram's hand was white on the tiller. "Talk," she muttered. "That'll be refreshing."

"Bram." Isandro kept her voice low. "Let me handle this."

"Oh, I intend to let you handle it. I'm going to sit here and watch how you handle it, and then I'm going to know exactly who you are." But she brought the Verity around, sullenly, and let the cutter close the gap.

Voss stepped aboard alone. That was its own kind of message — she didn't need her men close, because there was nowhere the Verity could go. Two of the enforcers remained visible at the cutter's rail, crossbows held loosely, pointed at nothing in particular, which is to say at everything.

Up close, Voss smelled of cedar oil and gunmetal. She was perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had decided long ago that handsomeness was a poor investment. Her eyes went around the little skiff with the unhurried attention of an appraiser: the patched hull, the coiled lines, the spirit stove, Bram, Isandro, and finally — lingering — the oilcloth-wrapped bundle lashed beneath the forward thwart, which was not the map, which was a spare coil of rope, but which Voss looked at anyway, because she wanted Isandro to know she'd seen it.

"Sterling." She said the name like a balance being checked against a ledger. "Three days out of Callow's Rest, heading south into the flooded lowlands during monsoon season, in company with—" a glance at Bram, brief and dismissive "—local talent. You'll forgive me if I don't find the fishing explanation persuasive."

"We weren't going to offer one."

"No. You weren't." Voss smiled. It was not a warm expression; it was the smile of a door opening onto a room you'd rather not enter. "Good. I appreciate efficiency. Let's be efficient, then."

She reached into her coat and produced a slim ledger bound in green leather — the Guild's color, the color of money and mildew. She opened it on her palm and turned it so Isandro could see the page. Columns of figures marched down it in a clerk's careful hand, and at the top, underlined twice, a number Isandro knew better than her own birthday: four hundred and twelve marks, eight coppers. Eleven years of interest recalculated quarterly. Eleven years of a number that never got smaller no matter how many bounties she ran down, how many thieves she dragged back across the flats, how much of herself she fed into the machine of it.

"The Guild," Voss said, "is prepared to make you an extraordinary offer. We do this rarely. I want you to understand that. This is a courtesy extended to our most productive assets." She tapped the page. "Deliver the map to me — today, now, here — and this debt is struck. All of it. Four hundred marks, gone. Your badge stays yours. Your boat stays yours. Your name comes off our books, and you walk away a free woman with a clean record and my personal recommendation for a senior posting in Callow's Rest. Tracker-General, eventually, if you keep your nose clean. There are men who've served thirty years who haven't been offered half that."

The floodwater lapped against the hull. Somewhere in the drowned orchard a bird screamed and was answered.

"And if I don't," Isandro said.

Voss closed the ledger. "Then the debt stands, and grows, and I take this vessel as collateral — I'm within my rights, Sterling, a debtor's conveyance is seizable — and I take you back to Callow's Rest in irons if necessary, and I find the map anyway, because I will search every inch of this boat and every inch of you, and maps are small things, easily found. The only question the Guild cares about is whether this ends pleasantly or unpleasantly. I'd prefer pleasantly. I suspect you would too."

There it was. Laid out on the deck between them like a fish gutted open: the whole shape of her life, offered back to her in exchange for a dead man's promise.

Isandro looked at the ledger. She couldn't help it. Four hundred and twelve marks, eight coppers. She had first read that number at nineteen years old, ink still wet, her sister's medicine already bought and already failing, the Guild clerk sliding the contract across the desk with the bland kindness of a man who did this forty times a day. *Standard terms,* he'd said. *Very reasonable.* And she had signed, because Sera was dying and the medicine cost more than a ranger earned in six years, and what else does a person do?

Eleven years. Eleven years of flats and dust and dragging people back in chains, all of it poured into a hole that never filled. And here was Voss, holding out her hand, offering to fill it. One map. One promise. One dead prospector's last words, traded for a life.

It would be so easy.

She felt Bram watching her. She didn't look at Bram. Looking at Bram would have made it harder, and she needed, for one moment more, to look at this clearly — not through Bram's eyes, not through a dead man's eyes, but through her own.

And what she saw, looking clearly, was this: the Guild did not forgive debts. The Guild had never once forgiven a debt in the history of its ledgers. Which meant either Voss was lying, or the debt was worth more than four hundred marks to her — worth enough that the Guild would burn eleven years of investment to get its hands on a drowned city's paper. Either way, Isandro understood suddenly, completely, with the cold clarity of a diver surfacing into air, that she was not being offered freedom.

She was being appraised.

"You said treasure stories," Isandro said quietly. "In Callow's Rest. You warned me away from them. Why warn me, Voss? If you wanted the map, why not just ask?"

Something flickered behind Voss's eyes — fast, quickly smoothed. "Because I assumed you were smarter than to chase one."

"No. You warned me because you wanted to know if I'd heard of it. If I flinched, you'd know I had it. And I flinched." Isandro laughed, short and bitter, at herself, at eleven years of flinching. "Gods. I've been carrying it straight to you since Callow's Rest, haven't I. Every mile. Every day. I've been your courier."

"Sterling—"

"The answer's no."

The word came out of her before she had fully decided on it, and having come out, it settled into her chest like a keel dropping — steadying her, giving the whole boat something to swing against. She heard herself say it again, louder, so there could be no mistake, so that the enforcers on the cutter's rail could hear it too:

"No. I don't accept. The debt stands. Take your ledger and get off this boat."

For a moment nobody moved. The bird in the orchard screamed again. Water dripped from the rigging.

Voss regarded her for a long, level moment, and Isandro watched the recalculation happen behind her eyes — the asset reclassified, the pleasant ending set aside, the unpleasant one taken down off the shelf and dusted. When Voss spoke again, her voice was softer, which was worse.

"Eleven years, Sterling. Do you know what you are to the Guild? You're a return on investment. You are a line item that performs. And a line item that refuses instruction—" she shook her head, almost gently, "—is a line item that gets written off. Think about what written-off means. Ask yourself why no one ever escapes the Guild's books alive."

"I'm asking myself why anyone signs them."

"Desperation," Voss said. "Same as everyone. Same as you." She snapped the ledger shut and slid it back into her coat. "Very well. You've made your choice. I'll make mine."

She nodded toward the cutter. Two enforcers stepped aboard, and Isandro's hand went to her knife, and Voss raised one finger — *no* — and the enforcers stopped, and instead of weapons they took hold of the spare sail, furled and lashed along the starboard gunwale, Bram's second-best canvas, patched at three corners, the difference between one sail and none if the monsoon split the first.

"Collateral," Voss said. "Against your cooperation. You'll want it back. People always want things back." She stepped to the gunwale, paused, and looked back at Isandro with something that in a kinder woman might have been regret. "You were the best tracker I ever leased, Sterling. That's all you ever were to me — leased. Remember that, out here, when the water gets high. Everything you own is on my books. Everything."

They took the sail and stepped back to the cutter. The engine thrummed. The black hull swung away through the drowned orchard, trailing its ribbon of smoke, and the wake rocked the Verity side to side, side to side, like a thing being shaken awake.

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke.

Bram sat at the tiller with her jaw tight, watching the cutter shrink. Isandro stood in the middle of her own boat, in the wet gap where the spare sail had been, feeling the shape of the absence the way you feel a tooth pulled.

"Well," Bram said finally. "That was either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever watched a person do, and I once saw a man wrestle an eel for a bet."

"It might be both."

"Four hundred marks." Bram turned to look at her, and there was no mockery in her face now, only a hard, searching attention. "You could have walked free. Free, Isandro. Clean books, clean name, a posting. All you had to do was hand over my father's map to the woman who—" she stopped. Started again. "Why?"

Isandro looked down at the empty spot on the gunwale. "Because she lied," she said slowly, working it through as she said it, hearing the truth assemble itself out loud for the first time. "Guilds don't forgive debts, Bram. They refinance them. They move them. If Voss was willing to strike four hundred marks for a piece of paper, then that paper is worth more than four hundred marks, and the debt was never really the point. The debt was the leash." She flexed her hands, remembering the weight of the compass she'd pawned, the badge she wore like a brand. "She wasn't buying the map. She was testing whether the leash still held."

"And it doesn't."

"No." Isandro met her eyes. "It doesn't."

Bram held her gaze a moment longer, then nodded once, sharply, and turned back to the channel. "Then we'd better move. She'll report back that you refused. Whatever Corvane's people are paying the Guild for that map, refusal means they stop being polite about it." She unshipped the oars. "And we just lost our spare sail, so if the sky opens up again, we bail. We bail and we row and we pray to whatever drowned god runs these floodwaters."

Isandro took the second pair of oars. The blades bit the brown water, and the Verity leaned into the current, southward, toward Vess.

Her shoulders ached. Her debt sat on her back same as always, four hundred and twelve marks, eight coppers, heavier now than it had ever been — because now she knew it for what it was, and knowing hadn't lifted it, and refusing hadn't freed her, and somewhere behind them a woman with a green ledger was writing her name into a different kind of column.

But her hands, pulling the oars, were her own.

That was new. That was terrifying. That, she thought, pulling, pulling, the drowned orchards sliding past, was worth a stolen sail.

Behind them, far to the north, thunder rolled — though the sky overhead was clear.
