## 12. Stormbound

The temple found them the way drowning men find driftwood—by accident, and with no gratitude to spare.

Bram saw it first: a pale shape rising out of the rain like a whale's back breaking water. The flooded orchards had ended an hour ago, and beyond them the lowlands opened into a brown sea broken only by treetops and the occasional roof ridge, and then this—a stone shoulder, then a stair, then the carved head of some forgotten god staring down at the flood with an expression of ancient patience.

"Temple," she shouted over the wind. "There—port side!"

Isandro didn't argue. That was new. Three days on the Verity had taught Bram that Isandro Sterling argued about everything—course, rations, whether it was wise to sleep in shifts or together, whether the rain would slacken by morning (it wouldn't). But when Bram hauled the tiller over and pointed the skiff's nose at the drowned stair, Isandro simply shipped her oar and began bailing faster, as if she understood that some arguments the weather had already settled for them.

They ran the boat up the steps until the hull scraped stone. The temple's entrance stood half-submerged, its lintel high enough to duck under if they crouched, and inside—

Inside was dry.

Not dry the way land is dry. Dry the way a held breath is dry. The interior rose in tiers above the floodline, a great pillared hall climbing toward a broken oculus in the roof, and the water stopped at the second step as though it had been paid off and told to wait. Rain came through the oculus in a silver column and struck a basin of black stone, where it had been falling, Bram judged, for a very long time.

"Somebody built this well," Isandro said, hauling their soaked packs up onto the third tier. Her voice was hoarse from shouting into wind. "Built it to be flooded."

"They built it to last," Bram corrected. She ran her hand along a pillar. The carvings were worn smooth but still legible under her fingers: rivers, mostly. Rivers and the things that lived in them. "My father used to say the old kingdoms never fought the water. They made room for it. Gave it a throne room so it wouldn't take the whole house."

Isandro looked at her across the darkening hall, rain streaming off her jaw, and something passed over her face too quick to name. "He sounds like he'd have liked this place."

"He'd have *loved* this place." Bram heard the bitterness come out of her own mouth before she could stop it, sharp as a fishhook. "That's the whole problem with him."

She said nothing else. Neither did Isandro. Outside, the storm gathered itself for the night's work.

* * *

By morning they understood what they had run into.

It wasn't a storm. It was the storm—the one the old boatmen called the Mother of Rains, the monsoon's heart, which came once a season and sat down on the lowlands like a brooding hen and refused to move for days. The column of rain through the oculus thickened until it was less water than white noise, a roaring curtain that swallowed sound and light alike. The flood outside climbed the temple steps. By noon it had reached the threshold. By dusk it lapped at the second tier, and the Verity, tied to a pillar, knocked against the stone like a knuckle on a door.

"We're not going anywhere," Bram said, stating it plainly because Isandro seemed the sort who needed things stated plainly before she'd accept them.

Isandro stood at the flooded entrance, watching the brown water slide past in sheets. "Three days," she said. "Maybe four."

"You've done this before."

"Once. In the flats. Different kind of trapped." She turned back. In the grey light her face looked carved, all planes and shadow. "We have food for two days if we're careful. Less if we're honest."

"Honest, then."

"Half rations. Water we can boil from the basin—it's clean, coming straight off the sky. Fire's the problem." She glanced up at the oculus, at the impossible wet dark beyond it. "Everything out there is soaked through. Everything in here is stone."

Bram had already thought of this, because thinking about practical problems was how she kept herself from thinking about other things—about Odrik's body floating in the reeds with his pockets turned out, about the syndicate barges sweeping the waterways behind them, about her father's map rolled tight in its oilcloth sleeve, sleeping between them like a third passenger. She dug into the stern locker and produced the boat's emergency kit: a tin of oil, a lamp, three candles gone soft with age, and a bundle of waxed shingles she used for patching.

"Wax burns," she said. "Shingle splinters catch. We'll make do."

Isandro watched her lay it all out with the economy of long habit, and Bram caught the look again—that flicker, that recalculation. People always did that with her. Looked at her hands, her tools, her patched skiff, and revised whatever they'd first assumed about a woman who mended nets in a riverport.

"What?" Bram said.

"Nothing." Isandro crouched and helped her stack the shingle splinters. "You're good at this."

"I'm good at everything on a boat. It's the rest of life I'm terrible at."

To her surprise, Isandro laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, like a hinge unused for years—but it was real, and it changed her whole face, and Bram had to look away from it, back to the fire she was building, because something in her chest had shifted without permission.

* * *

The first day, they talked about nothing.

Or rather, Bram talked about nothing and Isandro listened, which Bram slowly realized was a skill of hers, maybe the only soft skill she owned: the ability to sit silent in a way that invited speech. Not waiting—not the cold patience of someone biding time—but a kind of open attention, like a door left unlatched.

So Bram filled the silence because filling silences was also a skill of hers, and because the alternative was listening to the rain think.

"—and the worst of it was the eel. Not the capsizing. The eel. It had gotten into my boot somehow, don't ask me how, and I'm standing in knee-deep water trying to right the skiff and there is *something moving in my boot*, and I screamed loud enough that old Sefa heard me from half a mile downstream and came poling out thinking I was being murdered." She fed a shingle splinter to the small flame they'd coaxed in the basin's lee. "In a way I was. Murdered by an eel. In my boot."

"How old were you?"

"Nine. My father taught me to sail that summer. Or—" She paused. The correction arrived on schedule, the way it always did. "He was *there* that summer. Teaching implies staying. He was around long enough to teach me to capsize, mostly."

Isandro said nothing. The rain roared. The flame leaned.

"He was good at beginnings," Bram went on, quieter now. "That's the thing nobody understands about people like him. They're not liars, exactly. Every promise he ever made me, he meant it *in the moment*. He meant he'd come to my birthday. He meant he'd take me upriver to see the falls. He meant it completely, with his whole heart—and then the moment would pass, and some other beginning would call to him, some map or rumor or half-buried city, and he'd be gone before the promise cooled." She poked the fire harder than it needed. "A man made entirely of first chapters."

"That's a writer's way to put it."

"I read. Sue me." She glanced up. "What about you? Any sisters, brothers, a family made of endings?"

And there it was—the door swinging shut. Bram watched it happen. Watched Isandro's face do the thing it did, the smooth settling, the pleasant blankness of a woman closing shutters before a storm.

"No," Isandro said. "No family."

The lie was so smooth it was almost beautiful. Bram let it lie there between them, unchallenged, and added another splinter to the fire, and thought: *three days.*

Three days was a long time to keep a door shut with the rain hammering on it.

* * *

The second day, the food ran low enough that pretense became a luxury neither of them could afford.

They ate half-rations of dried fish and hard bread softened in boiled rainwater, sitting cross-legged on the fourth tier with the map between them—not open, just present, a presence like a sleeping animal. Bram had spread her charts beside it, working the route forward, and the arithmetic was grim: even if the storm broke tomorrow, the detour around the syndicate's patrol lines would cost them two days they no longer had. The dry season's end was a wall rushing toward them, and somewhere behind it, the lowland villages waited for a survey that could save them—or bury them, depending on whose hands it landed in.

"The gatehouse at Vess," Bram said, tracing the map's edge. "If my father's readings are right, the eastern gates can be winched open manually. Divert the surge before it hits the lower channels. But someone has to *be* there, at peak flood, with the mechanism clear." She looked up. "Someone has to hold the gatehouse while the water does its work."

"Then we'd better arrive before the water does."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what you meant." Isandro's eyes were steady. "You've been circling it since the toll bridge. You want to know if I'll stand there with you when Corvane's men come, or if I'll cut and run with the map and buy my debt back."

The directness of it stung, mostly because Bram had been circling exactly that. "And?"

"And I don't know." Isandro said it flatly, without performance, and somehow that was worse and better than any reassurance. "I've spent six years being owned, Bram. I don't know what I do when I'm free. Nobody does, the first time."

"Being owned doesn't excuse—"

"I know it doesn't." For the first time since Bram had met her, Isandro's voice cracked along a seam. Just slightly. A hairline fracture in the carved face. "I know better than anyone what it doesn't excuse."

The rain filled the silence. Down on the second tier, the flood had risen another hand's width overnight, touching the base of the river-god carvings, so that the old stones seemed to be standing waist-deep in their own element, patient as ever.

Bram should have pushed. Part of her wanted to push—to grab that hairline crack and widen it, the way you'd worry a loose plank. But she'd spent her whole childhood pushing at her father, and all it had ever won her was the sight of a man deciding, in real time, to leave.

So instead she said, "Tell me about the compass."

Isandro went still. "What about it."

"You check it every hour. Even here. Even trapped. There's nowhere to go and you check it anyway, like it might wander off." Bram kept her voice gentle, which took effort. "You pawned everything else, Voss said. Everything but that. So tell me about the compass, or tell me to mind my own business, but stop pretending the question isn't sitting in this room with us."

For a long moment Isandro looked at her, and Bram watched the war happen behind her eyes—the war between the shuttered woman and whoever was underneath, the one who laughed at eel stories like a hinge remembering how to swing.

Then Isandro reached into her coat and took out the compass.

She didn't hand it over. She held it in her palm, brass worn bright at the edges, and stared at it the way people stare at graves.

"My sister gave it to me," she said.

Bram didn't breathe.

"Her name was Lira. She was four years younger and twice as reckless, and when I got my ranger badge she bought me this compass with her first wages—she worked the ferry landings, taking coins from travelers, getting cheated half the time because she smiled too easy." Isandro's thumb moved over the brass, slow, automatic, a gesture worn smooth as the metal itself. "She got sick when she was nineteen. Lungs. The flats dust, or the river damp, or just bad luck—the healers disagreed, which is what they say when they mean they don't know and treatment costs money either way."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, thunder rolled through the oculus like a cart crossing a bridge.

"There was a medicine. New. From the coast. It cost more than I made in two years." Isandro's voice had gone very level, the levelness of someone walking a narrow ledge. "I owed the Guild a little before that—advances, gear, a fine I took for someone else. Nothing. But I went to Maren Voss and I borrowed against my badge, against my future wages, against everything, and I bought the medicine." She finally looked up. "She died anyway. Eleven weeks later. The medicine slowed it, the healers said. Slowed it. Eleven weeks of slowing, at the price of the rest of my life."

Bram's throat ached. "Isandro—"

"So yes. I check the compass every hour. Because it's the only thing she ever gave me that I couldn't sell, and because—" The ledge crumbled. Isandro's voice went rough, and she didn't look away, and Bram realized with a jolt that she was watching this woman cry for perhaps the first time in years, quietly, angrily, without any permission asked. "Because when she was dying she told me not to pay it back. She said, *let them keep the debt, it's not worth your whole life.* And I have spent six years paying it back anyway, because if I stop paying, then it was for nothing. If I stop, then she died for a debt instead of for me."

The words fell into the roar of the rain and vanished, and the silence they left behind was enormous.

Bram moved before she decided to move. She crossed the tier and sat down beside Isandro—not touching, not yet, just near, shoulder almost brushing shoulder, close enough that the heat of her was a fact.

"She died for love," Bram said. "The debt is just the receipt. You don't have to keep the receipt forever."

Isandro made a sound that was half laugh, half something else. "You always talk like that? Like sentences are boats you're steering?"

"Only when someone's sinking."

And then Isandro leaned—just slightly, barely, the way a leaning wall leans—and her shoulder came to rest against Bram's, and she stayed there, looking at the compass in her open palm, and Bram looked at it too, and neither of them said anything for a long time, because some cargoes you carry in silence.

* * *

The third day, the storm tried to kill them, and failed, and in failing finished what the confession had started.

It came near midnight—a gust front off the open water that hit the temple like a fist. The column of rain through the oculus bent sideways, whipping across the hall; the flood surged up the tiers in a single hungry wave, snatching at their packs, their charts, the precious dry corner where they slept. Bram woke to water around her knees and Isandro already moving, already shouting, hauling the packs to the highest tier while Bram fought the door of wind to get the lamp lit.

They worked. That was the whole of it—they worked, wordless, in the strobe of lightning, Isandro bracing the chart case with her body while Bram anchored the food tin under a fallen pillar drum, and when a gust tore the lamp from its niche and the flame died, they kept working in the dark, guided by each other's voices, by hands finding hands in the black.

Afterward—soaked, shaking, everything saved—they sat pressed together on the highest tier under Isandro's coat, listening to the storm spend its fury, and Bram found she was laughing. Small, breathless, unhinged laughter.

"What?" Isandro said.

"You put yourself between the charts and the wave. Like a wall. Like—" Bram shook her head. "Nobody does that. Nobody throws themselves at a flood for *paper*."

"It wasn't paper. It was your father's work. It was the villages." A pause, and then, drier: "Also I wasn't going to explain to a dead prospector's ghost why I let his life's work wash away on my watch."

"You believe in ghosts now?"

"I believe in debts," Isandro said. "Ghosts are just debts with better stories."

Bram turned her head. In the dark she couldn't see Isandro's face, only the outline of it against the lesser darkness, and the warmth of her along Bram's whole side, and the fact—which had become a fact sometime in the last three days, undeniable as the flood—that Bram did not want this to end. Did not want the storm to break, or the waters to fall, or the gatehouse to arrive with its hard choices. Wanted, with a selfishness that startled her, three more days in a drowned temple with a woman who checked a dead girl's compass every hour.

"Isandro."

"Mm."

"When this is over. When the survey's delivered and the Guild has taken whatever it takes." Bram made herself say it, made herself steer straight at the thing instead of circling. "What happens to us?"

The silence stretched. Lightning lit the hall—one white instant in which Bram saw Isandro's face, wide-eyed, stripped of every defense—and then the dark came back, and with it Isandro's voice, quiet and unarmored:

"I don't know. But I know I've spent six years owing everyone, and for the first time I want to owe somebody something I *choose* to owe." Her hand found Bram's in the dark. Rough palm, careful grip. "Ask me again when the water goes down. Ask me when I'm free. And if I still answer like that—"

"When the water goes down," Bram agreed, and squeezed her hand, and outside the rain went on falling on the roof of the world.

Neither of them slept much after that. And near dawn, through the thinning roar, they both heard it at the same moment: a sound beneath the rain, rhythmic and wrong. Oar-strokes. Many of them, keeping cadence.

Bram crawled to the flooded entrance and peered out at the grey water. Far off across the drowned flats, torches moved in a line—five, six, seven of them—sweeping the floodwater in a patient grid.

Corvane's barges had found the temple country.
