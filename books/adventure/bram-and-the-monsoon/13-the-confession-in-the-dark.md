## 13. The Confession in the Dark

The rain did not stop. It had not stopped in three days, and somewhere in the second night Isandro stopped expecting it to.

The flooded temple held them like a cupped hand. Its walls were older than any kingdom Isandro could name, sandstone gone soft and black with water, carved with gods whose faces the damp had worn to suggestions. The roof still held over the inner sanctuary—a dome of fitted stone that some forgotten mason had built to outlast everything—and so they sat beneath it on a ledge above the floodline, wrapped in the Verity's one remaining sail, listening to the monsoon hammer the world outside like a fist on a door that would never be answered.

Their food was gone. Not low—gone. The last of the dried fish had gone down Bram's throat at noon without ceremony, and now her stomach growled with an honesty Isandro found almost funny. Almost. Hunger made a strange companion after three days; it hollowed you out until there wasn't much left but what you actually were.

Isandro turned the compass over in her hands. She didn't need it. There was nowhere to go. The needle swung anyway, patient and stupid, pointing north through solid stone toward nothing.

"You're going to wear a groove in it," Bram said from the other end of the sail.

"It's already got grooves."

"Then you're deepening them." Bram shifted closer—not all the way, but closer than she'd sat yesterday, which was closer than the day before. The geometry of three days trapped together. "Give it here."

"I don't need it fixed."

"I'm not fixing it. I'm holding it." She held out her palm, and in the lamplight—their single lamp, burning its last inch of oil—Isandro saw the calluses on it, the net-scar across the thumb, the ink stain on the middle finger where Bram chewed her pen when she worked charts. A navigator's hand. A working hand. Isandro put the compass into it and felt something loosen in her chest that she couldn't name.

Bram turned the instrument over once, twice. "Your father's?"

"No." The word came out faster than Isandro intended. "I bought it. Eight years ago. In Callow's Rest, actually—from a junk stall by the fish market. The man swore it belonged to a sea captain. It was probably stolen off a corpse."

"And you've kept it ever since."

"It keeps time better than I do."

"That's not why you keep it."

Isandro looked at her. Bram Halloway, twenty-six years old and sharp as a chart-knife, sitting cross-legged in a dead god's house with another woman's compass in her hands, looking back with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Three days ago Isandro would have deflected. Two days ago she would have lied. Yesterday, in the worst of the storm, when the water had come up the steps and Bram had screamed her father's name at the rain without meaning to—yesterday something had cracked open in both of them, and neither of them had bothered to seal it again.

"No," Isandro said. "That's not why."

* * *

The lamp guttered. Bram fed it the last of the oil without asking permission, because there was no more to ration toward, and the flame steadied, throwing their shadows huge against the carved walls. The drowned gods watched with their faceless patience.

"My sister's name was Lira," Isandro said.

Bram went very still. In three days of shared hunger and shared danger, Isandro had given her almost nothing—ranger training, Guild debt, a first name offered like a coin tossed to a beggar. Bram had called her out on it on the river, days ago now, in that voice that cut: *You take everyone's story and give back weather reports.* And Isandro had said nothing then. But the temple dark had a way of making silence heavier than speech.

"She was two years younger than me. We grew up in the dry camps east of the flats—my mother ran a water-still, my father died before I could remember his face. Lira had the fever when she was nineteen. Lung-rot, the kind that comes up out of the wet season and settles in the young ones." Isandro heard her own voice as if from a distance, level, ranger-flat, telling it like a report. She hated that voice. She kept using it anyway. "The medicine existed. Guild-controlled, like everything. I had a badge and a reputation and nothing else, so I signed."

"The debt."

"The debt." Isandro laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what they tell you when you sign? They tell you the number. They don't tell you the number is a door that only opens inward. I paid for the medicine. Lira took it. It bought her four months. Four months of being able to breathe, to sit up, to laugh at my terrible jokes about the Guild clerks. Then she died anyway, and the debt didn't die with her, because debts don't care who they were for."

Bram said nothing. Her hands had stopped moving around the compass. She just held it, the way you hold something someone gives you when they can't carry it anymore.

"I tracked people for six years to pay it," Isandro went on. "Thieves, mostly. Runners. Men like Odrik. Women too. I told myself it was honest work—finding what was lost, returning what was taken. But you know what a tracker is, Bram? A tracker is a person the powerful point at other people. That's all. I was a finger. And every year the debt got smaller and somehow heavier, because the smaller it got, the more I understood it was never going to end. The Guild doesn't want it paid. Paid trackers walk away."

"And Voss."

"Voss is the leash-holder. She's good at her work. You have to give her that." Isandro pulled her knees up under the sail, rested her chin on them. "That's my whole story. That's everything. No treasure in it, no drowned city, no redemption waiting at the bottom. Just a woman who signed a bad contract to buy her sister four months of air."

The rain filled the silence. Outside, the monsoon moved through the flooded forest like a herd of animals, tearing at the canopy, and somewhere far off a tree went down with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"Lira," Bram said finally. "That's a good name."

"She'd have liked you. She liked people who talked back."

"I talk back at everyone. It's not a compliment system."

"It is with me."

* * *

Bram set the compass down between them, carefully, on the stone, and Isandro understood it was being returned and also that it was not—that some things, once handed over, stay handed over.

"My father used to say the monsoon was honest," Bram said. "Everyone else lies to your face, but the rain tells you exactly what it's going to do. It's going to drown you or it isn't. He said it like it was a comfort." She shook her head. "He left every dry season. Did I tell you that? No—I told you he left. I didn't tell you how. He'd stand at the edge of the dock with his pack on and kiss the top of my head and say, *Keep the corner, Bramble.* Because he always tore a corner off whatever map he was carrying and gave it to me. A piece of wherever he was going. Like a promise. Like a *payment plan.*" Her voice caught on the words and she pushed through it, jaw tight. "And every year I believed the next corner meant he was coming back for good. Every year. Until the year the corners stopped coming and the letter came instead, and the letter was from a stranger telling me my father had died in the flats with no boat and no money and a map nobody wanted."

"He wanted it delivered."

"He wanted—" Bram stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "He wanted me to finish his work. That's different from wanting. Wanting is coming home. Finishing is—" She gestured at the flooded dark beyond the sanctuary mouth, at the whole drowning world. "This. This is finishing. Sitting in a temple eating nothing, hunted by a syndicate, chased by your Guild, for a stack of drawings of gates under forty feet of water."

"They're more than drawings. You said so yourself. The floodgates—if they're opened right, the surge diverts around the lowland villages instead of through them. Your father mapped a way to save thousands of people, and Corvane wants it buried because saved villages don't need to sell their land cheap."

"I know what it's worth." Bram's eyes glittered in the lamplight, and Isandro realized with a small shock that she was crying—not sobbing, not even properly weeping, just leaking, the way the temple leaked, steadily and without drama. "I know exactly what it's worth. That's what frightens me. Because I keep doing the arithmetic, Isandro. I keep sitting here doing it. What it costs us to deliver it. What it costs if we don't. And every way I run the numbers, somebody pays, and I can't make it be nobody."

"No," Isandro said. "You can't. I've tried. Six years of trying."

Bram wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at herself, and Isandro did the thing she had never done with anyone, not Lira, not anyone—she reached across the space between them and took Bram's hand, the scarred navigator's hand, and held it. Not gently. Gently would have been a lie. She held it the way you hold on in a current.

"Bram."

"Don't." But she didn't pull away. "Don't say something kind. I'll fall apart and I don't have the food for it."

"I'm not going to say something kind. I'm going to say something true, and you can decide afterward whether it was kind." Isandro's heart was going hard enough that she was sure Bram could feel it through her palm. Good. Let her. Let her know this cost something too. "Three days ago I would have traded your father's map to Voss for my freedom and slept fine. I'd have told myself the villages weren't mine to save, that promises to dead men are just guilt wearing a coat. And then—" She stopped. Started again. "And then somewhere on this river, watching you read water like other people read letters, watching you grieve him and hate him in the same breath and keep rowing anyway—I stopped being able to imagine handing anything over that would hurt you. That's the truth. The debt, the badge, the freedom—all of it got lighter the moment it weighed against you. Do you understand what I'm saying? Because I've been very careful not to say it, and I'm tired of being careful, and if the roof comes in tonight I refuse to die having said nothing."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

Bram stared at her. Rain. Rain. Rain.

"You absolute idiot," Bram whispered, and her grip closed on Isandro's hand hard enough to grind bone. "You think I haven't been doing the same arithmetic? You bail all night and rig the sail half-drowned and hand me the dry blanket without being asked, and you think I don't notice? I notice everything. It's my curse. I noticed you the first hour on the Verity and I've been furious about it ever since, because I came on this boat to deliver a dead man's map and get clear, not to—" Her breath shuddered. "Not to find the one person I can't leave behind."

"So don't leave me behind."

"That's not how duty works."

"No," Isandro agreed. "It isn't. So let's say it plainly, since we're confessing. Tomorrow—or the day after, when the water drops—we go on. To Vess. To the gatehouse. And Voss will be there, or Corvane will, or both, and one of us might pay for this with more than a boat." She held Bram's gaze. "I'm not promising we live. I'm promising we finish. Whatever it costs. Both of us, all the way to the end of it. Can you live in that?"

Bram laughed—wet, cracked, real. "Can I *live* in it. You're asking the woman whose whole life is reading currents if she can commit to a heading."

"Yes. Exactly that."

"Then yes." Bram leaned her forehead against Isandro's, and the lamplight made one shadow of them on the ancient wall, two figures joined at the brow like the faceless gods above. "All the way. Whatever it costs. And Isandro—when this is done, whatever's left of us, we sail out on the receding water and we don't look back at any of it. Not the Guild. Not the syndicate. Not the ledgers."

"Not the ledgers," Isandro echoed, and kissed her, slow and salt-stung and certain, while the monsoon roared its honest roar against the stones.

They stayed that way a long time. The lamp burned to nothing, and neither of them lit the last candle, because the dark, for once, was kinder than the light.

It was Bram, hours later, half-asleep against Isandro's shoulder, who heard it first: the rain thinning. The long steady hammering breaking into gaps. Silence where silence hadn't been in three days.

She lifted her head. "Water's dropping."

Isandro listened. Under the fading rain, faint but unmistakable, came another sound—oars. Many oars, muffled, moving downstream through the drowned forest. Toward Vess.

Toward the gatehouse.

Bram met her eyes in the dark, and Isandro saw her own resolve reflected there, sharpened to a point.

"They're early," Bram breathed.

"Then so are we."
