## 14. Corvane's Barges

Bram heard them before she saw them—the deep, patient throb of engines that had no business on the flooded lowlands, where the water was too shallow for anything heavier than a skiff and a lie.

She was at the tiller, knees braced against the gunwale, watching the drowned orchards slide past in the grey-green light of midmorning. The monsoon had been generous this year. Whole groves stood submerged to their shoulders, their branches furred with algae, their fruit gone soft and strange beneath the waterline. The trees made a labyrinth of the flood—channels threading between trunks that had once marked the boundaries of farms nobody farmed anymore—and Bram knew the labyrinth the way she knew the lines of her own palm.

The engines did not know it. That was the only advantage they had left.

"Isandro." She said it quietly, because there was no need to shout and every need not to be heard. "Wake up."

Isandro came awake the way she always did—all at once, no groggy middle ground, her hand already finding the bail bucket before her eyes found Bram's face. Three days since the temple. Two days since they'd run the gauntlet of Voss's patrol boat with a borrowed sail and a story about salt merchants. One day since Bram had started sleeping with the map strapped against her ribs instead of stowed below, because some instinct older than reason kept whispering that the water itself was listening.

"How many?" Isandro asked.

"Two. Maybe three. Upstream, coming down the main channel."

Isandro rose slowly, one hand on the mast step, and turned her face into the wind like a woman tasting for rain. Her hair had grown shaggy since Callow's Rest; the wind pulled it across the scar on her cheekbone, the one she'd never explained and Bram had stopped asking about. "Corvane's barges don't come down from the north," she said. "They hold the toll bridges. They let the river do their chasing for them."

"Then someone told them we were coming down."

"Voss's clerk logged my name at the crossing." Isandro said it flatly, without blame, which somehow made it worse. "I told you it would cost us."

"You said it might."

"I said it would." Isandro crouched at the rail, studying the water ahead. The Verity ran light now—they'd eaten through most of their stores in the temple, and what remained sat high in the bilge—but light didn't mean fast, not with her patched hull dragging a memory of every reef she'd ever kissed. "How long before the channels converge?"

Bram did the arithmetic without wanting to. It was the kind of arithmetic she'd learned at nine years old, standing beside her father at a tiller half this size, while he named the channels after people who'd wronged him so she'd never forget them. *This one's the Liar's Throat. This one's the Widow's Elbow.* She had hated those lessons. She had hated how much she remembered them.

"Fifteen minutes if we take the open channel. Forty if we go through the orchards."

"And the barges?"

"The open channel is theirs. They'll have it covered by the time we reach the confluence." Bram looked at the drowned trees, their black branches combing the sky, their roots lost somewhere in ten feet of tea-colored water. "Through the orchards, they can't follow with anything bigger than us. But—" 

"But?"

"But I haven't run the orchards since I was sixteen, and the flood's two feet higher than it was then. Some of those channels are dead ends now. Some of them are worse than dead ends."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. There was something in her face that Bram had learned to read in the temple dark, when pretense ran out before the food did: not fear, exactly. Calculation wearing fear's clothes. A ranger's habit of weighing one death against another and finding both acceptable as long as they were hers.

"You know these waters better than anyone alive," Isandro said.

"That's flattery."

"That's inventory." The ghost of a smile, there and gone. "Take us into the trees, Bram."

* * *

The orchards swallowed them whole.

Bram killed the little sail and shipped the oars to muffle-drops, rowing with the blades half-lifted, skimming rather than striking. The Verity slid between the first of the drowned fig trees, and the world changed—sound collapsed to the drip of leaves and the knock of branches in current, light came down green and broken, and the air went cool and thick with the smell of rot-sweet fruit and standing water. Somewhere overhead a heron lifted off with a sound like a sail filling.

Behind them, faint but steady, the engines throbbed on.

"They'll lose us at the tree line," Bram murmured, more to herself than to Isandro. "Barges draw too much. They'll put men in longboats, but longboats are slow, and they don't know the channels."

"You do."

"I knew them." Bram feathered an oar around a submerged fence post, its wire still strung with last summer's rot. "My father used to bring me through here. He said the orchard was the best school on the flood—you couldn't cheat it, couldn't argue with it. You either knew where the water went or you fed the fish." She heard herself and stopped, jaw tightening. She hadn't meant to say his name, even sideways. Not today.

Isandro, to her credit, said nothing. She'd gotten good at that silence—not the cold kind she'd carried out of the desert, but a chosen quiet, a held door. Bram hated how much she'd come to rely on it.

The channel narrowed. Branches closed overhead until the sky was a ragged ribbon, and Bram shipped the oars entirely, poling now off the tops of submerged walls—old orchard terraces, stone under silt, still holding their shape after forty years of drowning. Her father's voice lived in her wrists: *feel for the wall, feel for the wall, the water remembers what the land forgot.*

"There," she breathed. "Left fork. The Widow's Elbow."

"You're naming channels after your father's grudges again."

"I'm navigating by them. It's different." But something loosened in her chest as she said it, some knot she'd been carrying since a dying man pressed a rolled chart into a stranger's hands and made it her inheritance. The Elbow opened ahead—a hard bend around a farmhouse roof just breaking the surface, its chimney leaning like a drunk—and Bram took it close, so close the Verity's rail brushed the eaves and startled a colony of bats up into the green light.

And that was when she saw the longboat.

It came around the bend ahead of them, silent, oars wrapped in cloth, six men sitting like stones. Corvane colors at the bow—a black chevron on grey. They'd entered the orchard from downstream. Of course they had. They'd known the confluence was the trap, so they'd set the second trap inside it, patient as spiders, and Bram's stomach dropped through the hull into the drowned dark below.

"Backwater," she hissed. "Now."

Isandro didn't ask questions. She had the spare oar out and backing water before Bram finished the word, and the Verity swung, heavy and graceless, into the mouth of a side channel Bram could only pray wasn't a dead end. The longboat crew saw them—she saw the shout pass down the boat like a spark along a fuse—but the men were slow to turn, and the Elbow's current was on Bram's side, shoving the skiff into the narrow water before the longboat could answer.

Engines, behind them now. Closer. The barges had reached the tree line after all—or rather, they'd stopped at it, and were disgorging everything they carried.

"They boxed the orchard," Isandro said. No panic in it. Just the inventory voice, cataloguing the walls of the room they'd locked themselves into. "Downstream longboats, upstream barges. How many ways out?"

Bram's mind flew over the flood like a heron, reading it from above. Channels. Dead ends. The old terraces. And then—one thing. One impossible thing her father had shown her once, laughing, calling it the orchard's private joke.

"One," she said. "Maybe."

"Maybe is my favorite number."

"It won't be." Bram pointed the Verity toward a stand of trees so dense the branches wove into a single woven wall of green. "There's a drainage cut under there. Old irrigation works, pre-flood. It runs under the orchard and comes out in the millpond above the weir. My father took a boat through it exactly once, and he swore afterward he'd never do it again, and then he did it twice more because he was who he was."

"Clearance?"

"Six inches of air over the gunwales. On a good day. Today the flood's higher." Bram was already stepping the mast down, lashing the boom flat, stripping everything that stood proud of the hull. "We'll have to lie flat. All the way through. And if the water's risen since spring, we won't fit at all, and we'll find that out about halfway, when there's no going back."

Isandro looked at the woven wall of branches, then at Bram, and something passed across her face that wasn't calculation at all. Trust, maybe. Or the thing trust grows from, when two people have bailed the same water long enough.

"Do it," she said.

* * *

The cut took them the way the sea takes a swimmer—completely.

Branches raked the hull with a sound like fingernails. Bram lay along the floorboards with her cheek against the wet wood, one hand on the painter-line she'd rigged to the bow, steering blind by the pressure of current against her palm, by the memory of a man's laugh in the dark. Isandro lay opposite her, folded nearly double, the map case strapped to her chest now too, both of them guarding it like a shared organ. Water sheeted over the gunwales in cold ropes. Once a branch caught the rudder fitting and held, and for three endless seconds the Verity hung there, groaning, while Bram clawed the line and Isandro pushed off the stone ceiling of the world with her bare hands, and then they tore free and the dark took them again.

And then—light. Sudden, shocking, silver. The cut spat them out into the millpond like a seed, and Bram surfaced from the floorboards gasping, laughing, actually laughing, a sound she didn't recognize as her own.

"We're through—we're—" 

The engine note changed.

It didn't get louder. It got *happier*. Deeper-throated, full-throated, the sound of a big hull given open water, and Bram's laughter died in her throat as she understood: the millpond wasn't a refuge. It was a bowl. The weir blocked the downstream end, the orchard wall the upstream end, and the Corvane barge—only one, but one was enough, one was a fortress—was already swinging its blunt iron bow across the pond's mouth, cutting off the millrace channel on the far side. Men lined its rail. She counted eight, then twelve, and gave up counting. At the bow, mounted on a swivel, sat something that caught the grey light and threw it back in a long dull gleam.

A harpoon gun. Salvage-rigged, the kind used for pulling sunken timber. Also the kind used for pulling sunken boats apart.

"Bram." Isandro's voice had gone very quiet. "Tell me you have another private joke."

Bram's mind raced the pond's perimeter and found nothing. Weir behind. Barge ahead. Orchard walls on both sides, and the cut behind them already filling with the sound of longboats entering it, oars knocking stone in the dark.

"No," she said. "I'm out of jokes."

"Then we run the weir."

"The weir's a twelve-foot drop onto rocks."

"The weir's a twelve-foot drop onto rocks," Isandro agreed, "and the harpoon gun is a harpoon gun. I've seen what those do to hulls. I've done it to hulls." She was moving as she talked, hauling the mast upright, bending on the patched sail with hands that didn't shake. "The millrace funnels right at the crest. If we hit it straight, at speed, we might clear the rocks and land in the deep pool below."

"*Might.*"

"Might is my favorite number," Isandro said, and for the first time since Bram had known her, the woman smiled without any ghost in it at all. "Sail up. Steer true. Whatever happens—keep the map dry."

The barge's harpoon gun spoke first.

The sound was a flat crack, like the sky breaking a knuckle, and the iron head came across the pond trailing its rope in a rising arc, and Bram hauled the tiller over and the world tilted and the Verity answered—old girl, faithful girl, answering one last time—and the harpoon missed by the width of a prayer, slamming into the orchard wall behind them and tearing a tree loose at the roots.

Then they were running. Sail full, wind fair, the millpond's grey water peeling away from the bow, the barge turning ponderously to cut them off and finding itself too slow, too heavy, built for tolls and terror and not for this—for a patched skiff with two women aboard running at a waterfall with nothing to lose that wasn't already spent.

"Brace!" Isandro shouted.

The weir rose up white and roaring. Bram felt the bow lift, felt the pond drop away beneath them, felt one clean weightless instant in which the whole flooded world hung silent—

—and then the impact.

Not the rocks. Worse, in a way: a submerged log, driven against the weir's base by the flood, lying just under the foam where no eye could find it. The Verity struck it amidships with a crack Bram felt in her teeth, a sound like the boat's spine breaking, and the skiff slewed sideways down the fall, shipping half the pool in one green avalanche, and slammed into the shallows below.

Silence, then. Just the roar of the weir and the drip of the rigging and Bram coughing floodwater, on her knees in six inches of standing water that should not have been inside the hull.

The hole was below the waterline, portside, aft of the mast step. Splinters stood around it like teeth. Even as she watched, the flood beyond the hull pulsed through it, patient and unstoppable, and the Verity settled a hand's width deeper, and deeper.

"Isandro." Bram's voice came out small. "She's holed."

Isandro was already moving—bailing with the bucket, then with the cooking pot, then with her cupped hands, water flying in silver arcs. Behind them, above the roar, the barge's engines idled at the top of the weir, and men's voices carried down through the spray, unhurried. They had all the time in the world. The water was doing their work for them.

Bram looked at the boat her father had built, that she had patched with her own hands through storm and siege, taking the flood into her lungs. Then she looked at the map case, dry against her ribs. Then at Isandro, bailing like a woman arguing with the sea itself, and losing.

"How long do we have?" Bram asked.

Isandro paused, one hand dripping, and judged the waterline with a ranger's eye.

"An hour," she said. "Less, if the rain comes back."

Above the weir, the rain was already coming back—a grey curtain walking down the valley toward them, swallowing the orchards one drowned tree at a time.
