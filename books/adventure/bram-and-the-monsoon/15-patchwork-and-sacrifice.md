## 15. Patchwork and Sacrifice

The Verity sat in the shallows like a wounded animal that had crawled off to die alone.

Isandro stood knee-deep in the drowned orchard's green water, one hand on the gunwale, feeling the boat shudder every time the wind pushed through the broken trees. Below the waterline, on the starboard side just aft of the mast step, a gash the length of her forearm gaped in the planking—torn open when they'd run aground fleeing Corvane's barges through the submerged fence-lines of what had once been somebody's farm. Bram had stuffed the hole with a shirt and a hank of oakum and prayed, and the prayer was failing. The bilge water rose an inch an hour no matter how diligently they bailed, and the monsoon had three hundred miles yet to cross before it reached Vess.

They had perhaps two days of floating left in the Verity. Less if the wind rose.

"Stop looking at it," Bram said from the stern. "It doesn't get better because you stare at it."

"Everything gets better if you stare at it long enough," Isandro said. "That's half of tracking. You look at a thing until it tells you its story."

"And what's this one telling you?"

"That we're sunk."

Bram made a sound that was almost a laugh and mostly not. She was wringing out her hair, which had not been dry in four days, and there was a smear of pitch along her jaw where she'd been re-caulking seams by feel in the dark. In the flat grey light she looked younger than she had at Callow's Rest, and harder too—the softness burned off her like mist off morning water. Isandro caught herself looking and looked away, out across the flooded orchard, where the tops of apple trees stood in rows like drowned men reaching up.

"There's a salvage camp," Bram said. "Half a mile east, maybe less. I saw smoke yesterday when we came through, before—" She gestured at the hole in the boat, at all of it. "Salvagers work these floods every season. Pulling wire and timber and whatever else the water gives up. They'll have pitch, canvas, maybe a plank or two cut to fit."

"They'll want paying."

"They always want paying."

Isandro turned that over. Her pockets held a fishing hook, a stub of tallow candle, and eleven coppers wrapped in oilcloth. Eleven coppers would buy them a look of contempt from any salvage crew on the lowlands.

But it wasn't the pockets she was thinking about. It was the cord around her neck, under her shirt, where the ranger badge hung against her sternum—brass worn smooth by nine years of sweat and weather, stamped with the saltbush sigil of the flats command. And it was the compass, sitting in the bottom of her satchel where it had ridden since Callow's Rest: her father's compass, brass-cased, glass cracked but true, the needle still finding north with the stubbornness of a thing that had never once lied to anyone.

She had carried both through everything. Through the debt, through the dust storms, through the gut-shot prospector's last breath and the Guild's ledger and Maren Voss's smile. They were the last two pieces of the woman she had been before the Saltwater Guild bought her name and leased her back to herself at interest.

"You've gone quiet," Bram said. "That's never good."

"I'm doing arithmetic."

* * *

The salvage camp occupied the roofline of a barn whose walls were ten feet under. A dozen structures of driftwood and salvaged tin perched along the ridge like seabirds, connected by rope walkways, and the smell of woodsmoke and hot pitch carried across the water long before they could make out faces. Two skiffs were beached on the roof itself, hauled up on rollers, and a crane of lashed poles swung lazily over the edge, its cable disappearing into the green flood.

The man who met them at the rope ladder was built like something the river had coughed up—wide, scarred, bald except for a fringe of grey he'd clearly stopped fighting years ago. He looked at the Verity, then at Isandro, then at Bram, in that order, and his face said he'd already done his own arithmetic.

"Heard you come through the orchard last night," he said. "Fast. Somebody chasing you?"

"Somebody ahead of us," Isandro said. It wasn't even a lie; Odrik's corpse was behind them now, days back upriver, and Corvane's barges were everywhere at once. "We need hull work. Planking, pitch, a day of your time."

"A day." He spat over the edge, into the water, with the ease of a man who did it a hundred times a day. "Hull work on a boat taking water that fast is two days minimum, and I've got a crew of six and contracts with people who pay in silver. What are you paying in?"

Isandro set the oilcloth packet on the planking between them. He didn't touch it. He looked at it the way a man looks at a joke told badly.

"That's a fishhook and pocket lint," he said.

"It's eleven coppers."

"It's nothing." He started to turn away. "There's a government station forty miles downriver. Rangers do relief work in flood season. You could row there, if you row fast."

Something went cold and quiet in Isandro's chest. She heard herself speak before she had finished deciding to.

"I have things worth more than coppers."

He paused. Turned back. Salvage men knew worth when they heard it in a voice.

"Show me."

* * *

She took off the badge first.

It should have been simple—a cord over the head, a small weight leaving her hand—but her fingers shook, and she hated them for it. Nine years. Nine years of dawn patrols across the salt flats, of pulling strangers out of sandstorms and burying the ones she'd found too late, of sleeping under stars with no roof and no master except the horizon. The badge had been the whole argument for her life: *I am this. I am useful. I belong to the flats and the flats belong to me.* The Guild had taken her wages, her freedom, her future—but the badge had felt like hers, somehow, the one thing the debt hadn't touched.

Now she held it out on her open palm, and it was just brass. Small. Light enough that the wind nearly took it.

"Ranger issue," the salvage man said. His voice had changed—flatter, more careful. "Flats command. That's a real commission."

"It is."

"Where's the rest of you? Sidearm, warrant papers?"

"Gone. This is what's left." She lifted the cord clear of her neck and let it hang from her fist. "It's real, and it's mine to sell, and I'm selling it."

Behind her, she heard Bram go very still. She didn't turn around. If she turned around and saw Bram's face, she might stop, and she could not afford to stop.

"And the other thing?" the salvage man said.

Isandro knelt and opened her satchel. The compass lay in the bottom, nested in a strip of blanket she'd wound around it years ago so it wouldn't rattle. She unwound the blanket slowly, fold by fold, the way you unwrap a thing you're burying. The brass case caught the grey light. The crack in the glass ran from north-northwest to south-southeast, a hairline scar from the night her father dropped it running for the door when she was eleven years old—and even that memory came with the needle's steadiness attached, because the compass had survived the fall and kept working, and her father had laughed and said, *See? True north forgives you anything.*

"This too," she said.

"That's a surveyor's instrument. Old. Good maker." The salvage man's eyes had gone sharp and hungry in the way of men who dealt in other people's histories. "Together, badge and compass—I can move both downriver to a collector in Tarn's Landing. I'll give you full hull repair, new plank, re-caulked seams, pitch, canvas for a jury sail patch, and two days' food." He hesitated, and then, because salvage men were honest in exactly one direction: "And I won't ask why a ranger sells her badge."

"The why isn't for sale."

"No," he agreed. "It never is."

Isandro closed her fist around the badge. For one breath—one long, slow breath, the kind you take before diving into cold water—she let herself feel the whole weight of it. Every mile those two objects had carried her. Every version of herself she'd been building toward, someday, when the debt was paid: the ranger with her own patrol, her own stretch of flats, the compass passed down another generation, the story finally making sense.

Then she opened her hand and held them both out.

"Done," she said.

* * *

Bram said nothing while the salvage crew worked.

That was the thing Isandro noticed most, in the hours that followed—that Bram said nothing. She sat on the barn roof with her knees drawn up, watching the crew haul the Verity onto the rollers and flip her, watching them pry out the ruined plank and fit the new one, watching Isandro stand in the shallows passing tools and holding lines, and she watched with an expression Isandro couldn't read and didn't dare ask about. Not pity. Pity she could have handled; pity was cheap and she'd have thrown it back. This was something else. Something that looked, uncomfortably, like recognition.

The work took the afternoon. Rain came and went in ragged curtains, and the crew worked through it with the boneless efficiency of people who had spent their whole lives being wet. By dusk the new plank sat white and raw against the Verity's weathered grey hull, slathered in pitch, and the old boat floated level for the first time since the orchard. Isandro bailed her dry and watched the water line hold, and hold, and hold, and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched.

She paid the salvage man at sundown. Cord first, then compass. He weighed each in his palm with a professional's indifference, nodded once, and stowed them in a lockbox without ceremony—which was, Isandro thought, the only mercy available. No speeches. No eulogy. Just a box lid closing on nine years.

She walked back along the rope walkway to where Bram sat, and lowered herself down beside her, and the two of them watched the last light fail over the drowned orchard together.

"You didn't hesitate," Bram said at last.

"It was arithmetic."

"Don't." Bram's voice was quiet, but it had edges. "Don't do that. Don't make it small. I watched you put your whole life in a stranger's lockbox and you didn't flinch, and I've been sitting here for four hours trying to decide what that means, and I've decided it means you don't get to call it arithmetic."

Isandro looked at her hands. They were pitch-stained and rope-burned and they had shaken when she'd taken off the badge, and Bram had seen, of course. Bram saw everything.

"What does it mean, then?" Isandro asked. "You tell me."

"It means the woman I met at Callow's Rest—the one who lied to my face about why she was there, the one who talked about her debt like it was weather, something that just happened to her—" Bram turned, and in the dark her eyes caught the last ember-light from the cookfire. "That woman believed the badge and the compass were who she was. I could see it. You touched that compass like it was a relic. And today you gave both away in under a minute, for a boat, for a promise you made to a dying man you never met." She shook her head slowly. "I don't know whether that's the saddest thing I've ever seen or the bravest. Maybe it's both. But it isn't arithmetic. Arithmetic doesn't cost anything."

The rain started again, soft and steady, drumming on the tin roofs. Neither of them moved to find shelter.

"My father gave me that compass," Isandro said. The words came out low, half-drowned by the rain, and she let them. "When I got my commission. He said, *This will keep you honest. A person who knows where north is can't get too lost.*" She almost smiled. "He was wrong about a lot of things, but he was right about that. It kept me honest right up until the Guild bought my debts and made honesty a luxury item."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know where north is." Isandro spread her hands. "Except—" She stopped. Started again, because Bram was waiting and Bram deserved the truth even when the truth was unfinished. "Except I know which way Vess is. I know we have to be there before the flood peaks. I know a man died in the dirt holding a map he wouldn't sell to save his own life, because it wasn't for sale—it was for his daughter. So maybe north isn't a direction anymore. Maybe it's just the next thing that has to be done."

Bram was silent for a long moment. Then she reached over—not dramatically, not tenderly either, just practically, the way she did everything—and pulled the blanket off the gear pile and dropped it over both their shoulders. The rain hissed on the tin above them. Somewhere out in the flooded orchard, an apple tree groaned and settled lower into the water.

"For what it's worth," Bram said, "the badge was never you. I've watched you work. You'd be exactly as stubborn and exactly as reckless with no badge at all. The compass, though." A pause. "The compass I'm sorry about."

"So am I." Isandro found, to her surprise, that she meant it and that saying it didn't break anything. "But the man in the dirt didn't hesitate either. He was dying and he handed me the only thing he had and trusted a total stranger with his daughter's name. I figure the least I can do is travel light the same way."

"Travel light." Bram snorted. "You're sailing into the worst monsoon in twenty years, hunted by a syndicate, with a hole in your boat that a stranger nailed shut, toward a city that's been underwater for a century. That's not traveling light. That's traveling with nothing *but* weight."

"Yes," Isandro said. "But none of it's mine anymore. That's the difference."

Bram looked at her then—really looked, the way she'd studied the map by lamplight, hunting for the truth underneath the ink—and whatever she found there made her lean, just slightly, until her shoulder rested against Isandro's. Not a declaration. An anchorage.

They stayed like that until the fire burned down, and neither of them mentioned it after, and neither of them forgot it either.

* * *

They launched before dawn, in that hour when the rain pauses as if considering its options.

The Verity rode clean and high on the flood, the new plank holding, the patched sail catching a thin southeast wind. Bram took the tiller; Isandro worked the sheet; and the drowned orchard slid away astern, its apple trees sinking another inch as the waters rose, patient and unstoppable, carrying everything toward Vess.

It was Bram who spotted it—standing on the foredeck, shading her eyes against the pearly light. She went rigid, and Isandro read the rigidity before she read the words.

"Sail," Bram said. "Two points off the bow. Big. Square-rigged."

Isandro turned. Out of the grey curtain of rain, where the river widened toward the old shipping channel, a shape was resolving: a barge, heavy-laden, flying no colors at all—and flying no colors, on these waters, was its own answer.

At its prow, hands clasped behind her back like a woman admiring the scenery, stood a figure in a Guild-grey coat.

Maren Voss had found them again.
