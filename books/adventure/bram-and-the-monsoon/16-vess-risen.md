## 16. Vess Risen

The water changed first.

Bram felt it in the Verity's hull before she saw it with her eyes—a new resistance in the current, a slow muscular pull that dragged the skiff sideways as though the river itself had turned to look at them. She shipped the oars and let the boat drift, one hand trailing in the water. It was warmer here. Murkier. Full of silt and old stone and things dissolved so long ago they had become flavor.

"We're close," she said.

Isandro sat at the stern with the survey rolled across her knees, one boot braced against the gunwale, watching the treeline the way she watched everything—like it might owe her money. "Close to what? All I've seen for two days is water and more water."

"That's the point." Bram stood, steadying herself against the mast step as the Verity yawed. "Vess doesn't have an approach. It has a drowning. You don't sail *to* it. You sail over it until the water gets shallow enough to remember what's underneath."

She had grown up on these stories the way other children grew up on lullabies. Her father's voice, low and half-drunk on cheap arrack, drawing shapes in spilled salt on the tavern table: *the drowned city sits at the elbow of the Serein where the river forgets which way it's going.* He'd been gone most of those years, but he'd always come back with stories, and Vess was the best of them—the city that traded its soul to the river, the floodgates built by masons who understood water better than they understood God, the spires that rose out of the monsoon like drowned men reaching for a rope.

She'd stopped believing the stories around the same time she'd stopped believing his promises. Apparently the two had been made of the same material all along: beautiful, and unreliable, and hers anyway.

The fog came off the water in slow ribbons. Through it, the light went strange—green-gold, submarine, as if they were already underwater and hadn't noticed. Isandro moved up beside her without being asked, and Bram was grateful and hated being grateful and said nothing about either.

"There." Isandro's voice dropped, ranger-quiet. "Left of the sun."

Bram looked. At first there was nothing—just fog and the flat pewter sheet of the flooded plain. Then the fog tore, briefly, like cloth catching on a nail, and something dark stood up out of the water a mile distant. A tower. A bell tower, by the shape of it, its top third bare above the surface, its windows blind with silt. Around it, lower shapes: rooftops, ridgelines, the spine of some great hall submerged to its eaves.

Vess. Not risen, exactly. But showing its shoulders above the waterline for the first time in a season, because the monsoon had pushed the floods higher than high, and the drowned city had come up for air.

Bram found she couldn't speak. Something was happening in her chest that had no name she wanted to give it. Her father had drawn this tower for her once, in charcoal, on the back of a grain ledger, when she was seven. She had kept that scrap of paper until she was nineteen and angry enough to burn it. She could still draw it from memory now, every crooked line of it, and here it was, real, wet, indifferent to both of them.

"Your father stood here," Isandro said quietly. "Or somewhere near here."

"I know." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "He drew it wrong, actually. The tower's narrower than he made it. He always exaggerated everything." She swallowed. "Everything except how much he loved this place. That part he undersold."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of the bell tower, where the current broke and swirled into a lazy eddy full of drifting reeds. The Verity's patched hull groaned but held; Isandro's salvage repairs were ugly and effective, which Bram had learned was the definition of the woman herself.

Getting onto the roof was a matter of nerve and a grapnel line. Isandro threw it twice, hooked it on a chimney the second time, and went up first, testing each hold with the methodical suspicion of someone who had been lied to by terrain before. Bram followed, the survey case strapped across her back, her heart going hard against the leather.

The rooftop was a garden of ruin. Moss had colonized the tiles in great soft continents. Rainwater stood in the valleys between them, green as bottle glass, and small silver fish—actual fish, stranded or migrated, Bram couldn't decide which was stranger—flickered through the shallows. From up here the whole drowned city spread out around them: streets become canals, plazas become lakes, a whole civilization lying just beneath the surface like a face under clear ice.

And everywhere, doors. Doors in the sides of buildings facing water that should never have reached them. Doors with iron frames, iron hinges, iron wheels beside them—wheels meant to be turned.

Bram unrolled the survey on the flattest stretch of roof, weighting the corners with four pieces of broken tile. Her hands were shaking. She told herself it was the cold.

Her father's hand covered every inch of the vellum. She knew that hand—she'd inherited it, the same slanted letters, the same habit of pressing too hard so the lines showed through on the reverse. But she had assumed, all these years, that his maps were the maps of a man chasing fortune. Prospector's maps. Treasure maps. *Here be gold, here be the thing that will finally make me stay.*

This was not that.

"This is a hydrographic survey," she said aloud, mostly to hear it confirmed.

Isandro crouched beside her. "Meaning?"

"Meaning it's not about what's *in* Vess. It's about what goes *through* it." Bram traced the inked channels with one finger. Every street, every plaza, every canal was annotated—not with depths, not with markers for vaults or caches, but with flow rates. Cubic measures per season. Contour lines showing where the floodwater would go if it went one way, and where it would go if it went another. And threaded through all of it, drawn in a heavier hand, almost reverent: the gates. Dozens of them. Each one numbered, each one keyed to a note in the margin in her father's cramped script.

She read the notes. She read them twice, because the first time her eyes kept blurring.

*"Gate 4 opens the old millrace — diverts east channel toward the Serein backwash. If opened at peak flood, takes pressure off the Callow levees."*

*"Gate 11 is jammed — rusted shut since '61. Needs two men and a lever. Worth it: saves the Fenwick cut."*

*"All of it depends on Gate 1, the master. In the gatehouse under the bell tower. If Gate 1 holds closed while the others open, the water goes where we want it instead of where it wants to go."*

"He wasn't looting," Bram said. The words came out flat, stunned. "Everyone said—he told everyone he was going to find the treasury of Vess, the sunken vaults, the—God, I believed it too, I thought he was chasing the same stupid dream that kept him away from us every dry season—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "He was mapping the floodgates. He spent twenty years mapping the floodgates. So that when the monsoon came bad—and it comes bad, Isandro, you've seen it, you've seen what it does to the lowland villages—someone would know how to make the water go around them instead of through them."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. When Bram lowered her hand, the ranger was looking at the map with an expression Bram had never seen on her—not suspicion, not calculation. Something older and sadder. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman reading a debt she understood.

"The villages downstream," Isandro said. "Callow's Rest. The Fenwick settlements. All those places the Guild taxes for 'flood protection' and delivers nothing."

"Because there's nothing to deliver. Or there wasn't. The engineering died with Vess." Bram laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "The Guild sells protection from a flood they could prevent, and my father—the deadbeat, the liar, the man who missed my mother's funeral because he was *out here*—he was the only person in forty years who tried to actually do it."

The wind moved across the rooftop, pushing ripples through the standing water, and the drowned city seemed to shift beneath them, restless, waiting.

"He knew," Bram said softly. "At the end. Gut-shot in the flats, dying, and he didn't give you the map to sell. He could have. You'd have paid—I know what you're carrying, I know what the Guild has on you, one treasure map would have bought your freedom twice over." She looked at Isandro. "He gave it to you to *deliver*. Because he cared more about the villages than about himself, and he trusted a stranger with a ranger's badge more than he trusted his own reputation. Which—" her voice caught, "—which was fair, honestly. My whole life he taught me exactly why nobody should trust him. And then he did this."

Isandro said nothing. She was good at saying nothing; Bram had learned that about her, that silence was the woman's native language and speech a foreign country she visited carefully. But she put her hand over Bram's on the edge of the map, briefly, and left it there, and that was its own kind of sentence.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the light went from green-gold to copper.

Bram copied the gate sequence onto a waterproofed slate from the Verity's stores, cross-referencing her father's marginalia with what they could see of the actual city. The survey was astonishingly accurate—of course it was; he'd been coming here for twenty years, slipping away every dry season while she'd built a whole identity out of resenting him. Gates 3 through 9 were visible from the rooftop, their iron mechanisms furred with rust but intact. Gate 11, true to his note, was jammed—they could see the bent lever arm from two hundred yards. Gate 1, the master, lay beneath their feet somewhere in the bell tower's flooded base.

"It's still possible," Bram said, as the sun sank behind the western hills and the water turned to hammered bronze. "That's what I keep—look at this, Isandro. The tolerances. The timing. Open the secondary gates at peak flood, hold the master for six hours, and the Serein takes the overflow through the backwash instead of through the levee country. The villages stand. It's not a legend. It's *engineering*. It's sitting right here in his handwriting, waiting for someone to turn the wheels."

"And Corvane wants it," Isandro said. "Which means Corvane understands what it is, too."

Bram looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"A map this valuable has two uses." Isandro's voice was level, but Bram had learned to hear the tension underneath it, the wire pulled taut. "You can use it to save the lowlands. Or you can use it to threaten them. Control the gates, control the water. Control the water, control every village from here to the delta." She tapped the master gate's notation. "Whoever holds Gate 1 doesn't need to loot Vess. They can tax the living the way the river used to. Corvane isn't chasing treasure, Bram. They're chasing leverage."

The cold that went through Bram then had nothing to do with the evening air. She thought of Odrik, the thief, dead in the mud with a copy of this map. She thought of the barges sweeping the waterways, the checkpoint clerk logging Isandro's name, the net closing with the patience of something that had done this many times before.

"Then we can't lose it," she said. "And we can't use it halfway. Either the gates open for the villages or they open for Corvane."

"Either the water serves people or it serves owners." Isandro nodded slowly. "Your father understood that. It's why he stayed dead broke his whole life, I'd wager. This kind of knowledge is only worth a fortune to people you'd never want to sell it to."

Bram stared out across the drowned city. The first stars were coming out, reflected in the flooded streets so that Vess seemed to contain a second Vess beneath it, deeper and darker, a city of reflections under a city of stone. Somewhere below them, under forty feet of monsoon water, the master gate waited in its gatehouse, rusting, patient, holding back nothing and everything.

Her father had carried this alone for twenty years. Had let her hate him rather than tell her, because telling her would have meant telling the world, and the world was full of Corvanes. He had died on the flats with the weight of it and passed it to a stranger because he had no one else left who wouldn't sell him.

Well. He had someone else now.

"Tomorrow," Bram said. "Peak flood is in three days—the moon tides will push it higher. We find the gatehouse, we find Gate 1, and we learn every wheel and lever before the water reaches its height. And when it does—" she pressed her palm flat against the survey, against her father's heavy, careful, loving lines, "—we open them. All of them. Exactly the way he wrote it."

Isandro was looking at her with that expression again, the one that undid Bram more than any smile could have. "You sound like him," the ranger said. "In the good way."

Bram laughed—wet, surprised, real. "Don't tell anyone."

Below them, the bell tower's flooded arches breathed with the current, a slow rhythmic sigh, like the city itself inhaling after a long drowning. And far off to the south, past the black line of the hills, Bram thought she heard—or imagined, or feared—a sound that did not belong to water or wind: the deep-throated pulse of barge engines, working their way upriver through the dark.

Isandro heard it too. Her head came up, sharp as a hound's.

"They found us," she said.

"No," Bram said, rolling the survey with steady hands, folding her grief into purpose the way her father had folded his into ink. "They found the city. We're just the ones who know what it's for."

She strapped the case to her back and started down toward the boat, toward the water, toward the gates.
