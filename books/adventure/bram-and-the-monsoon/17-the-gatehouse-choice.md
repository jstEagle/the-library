## 17. The Gatehouse Choice

The gatehouse of Vess rose out of the water like the ribcage of something that had once been enormous and was now only remembered.

Isandro saw it first as a smudge against the western sky, then as a shape—black stone streaked white with a century of birdlime, its towers leaning where the flood had undermined them, its great gate half-drowned and hanging open on rusted hinges like a jaw that had forgotten how to close. The monsoon had pulled back overnight into a sullen drizzle, and in that gray hush the drowned city made no sound at all. No gulls. No wind. Just water lapping at stone, patient as a creditor.

Bram stood at the tiller of the Verity, one hand on the patched hull below her, feeling for the tremor she said meant the repair was holding. It was holding. Barely. Everything about them, Isandro thought, was holding barely—the boat, the promise, the two of them.

"There," Bram said quietly, and pointed.

The gatehouse's lower landing emerged from the mist: a shelf of wet stone where the flood had stranded a barge, hull-up, its Corvane sigil—a black wheel on gray—still legible beneath the slime. And beside it, tied to a mooring ring older than either of their grandmothers, a Guild skiff with a green lantern burning at its prow.

Maren Voss had not followed them. She had *arrived*. There was a difference, and Isandro felt it settle into her bones like cold water into a boot.

"She knew," Isandro said. "She always knew where we were going."

"The clerk at the toll bridge logged your name." Bram's voice was flat, but her knuckles were white on the tiller. "You told me it would work."

"It did work." Isandro watched the green lantern sway. "That was the problem."

* * *

They came in slow, oars shipped, letting the current carry them the last hundred yards because anything faster felt like flight and flight would be answered. Isandro sat in the bow with her hands loose on her knees and her ranger's training running through her like a second pulse: count the exits, count the weapons, count the men. Two figures on the landing. Voss in her gray coat, arms folded, rain beading on her shoulders as though even the weather paid her deference. Beside her, a Corvane man in oilskin, broad, unhurried, a long knife worn openly at his belt because men who traveled with syndicates didn't need to hide them here.

"Isandro Sterling," Voss called across the water, and her voice carried the way voices carry over still water—perfectly, mercilessly. "You've brought me a long way from Callow's Rest."

"You could have sent a letter," Isandro called back.

"I did send letters. Eleven years of them." Voss smiled without warmth. "You never read the fine print."

The Verity's hull kissed stone. Bram made the line fast, and Isandro stepped up onto the landing, boots finding the wet granite, and felt the whole weight of the drowned city at her back—every flooded street, every drowned bell tower, every gate her promise had dragged her toward. She heard Bram come up behind her, light on her feet, and took a small, stubborn comfort in it.

"Voss." She kept her voice level. "Corvane."

The big man inclined his head. "Tallow Meric. I handle acquisitions." He said it the way other men said *I handle funerals*.

"You're standing on my father's work," Bram said, before Isandro could speak. Her voice cracked only slightly on *father*, and Isandro heard her force it level again. "Halden Halloway surveyed these gates. Whatever you think is down there, it isn't treasure."

"Everything is treasure," Meric said pleasantly, "priced correctly."

Voss lifted a hand, and Meric subsided with the ease of long partnership, and that ease told Isandro more than any confession could have. The Guild and the syndicate were not rivals converging on the same prize. They were colleagues dividing one.

"You've been useful, Sterling," Voss said. "Better than useful. Eleven years, and you never once failed to bring back what I sent you after. Runaways. Debtors. One map thief, last month, though he got ahead of you in the end." She glanced at Bram, brief and clinical. "You found the prospector instead. You found the daughter. You crossed the lowlands in a leaking skiff through the worst monsoon in nine years. Do you know why?"

"Because I keep my word," Isandro said.

"Because I let you." Voss stepped closer, her boots ringing on the stone. "Every trail you ever followed, I'd already walked. Every debtor you ran down, I'd already priced. You think you tracked Odrik across the flats? We put him there. We let him run so you'd chase him, and when he sold a copy of the map to men who weren't us, we closed that account ourselves—you saw how neatly. All you ever had to do was follow, Sterling. You were very good at following."

The words went into Isandro like a blade going into old wood—slow, splitting along grain that had been there all along. She thought of the compass she couldn't pawn. Of the ledger Maren kept in green ink. Of eleven years of trails that had always, somehow, been warm.

"The debt," Isandro said. Her voice came out strange, distant, as if someone else were asking. "My sister's medicine. Four thousand crowns."

"There was no debt." Voss said it simply, the way one states the weather. "There was a story, and you were grieving, and grief makes excellent paper to write on. Your sister died in the spring. You signed in the summer. The Guild advanced you nothing but a badge and a boat, and charged you four thousand crowns for the privilege of belonging to us." She tilted her head. "You were the best tracker in three provinces, Sterling. Did you truly believe we'd let a thing like you walk free over a sum of money? Money forgives. Leashes don't."

The rain thickened, fine as sifted flour. Isandro became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, and of Bram beside her, utterly still, watching her with an expression Isandro couldn't look at yet—not if she wanted to keep standing.

"So the badge," Isandro said. "The salvage crew. The compass I traded—"

"Was yours. Always was. That's the joke of it." Voss almost sounded fond. "You've spent eleven years buying back things that were never taken."

* * *

For a moment Isandro let herself feel all of it at once—the fury, the humiliation, the strange vertigo of a life restructured around a lie—and then she did what she had trained herself to do in dust storms and floods and eleven years of Maren Voss's green-ink arithmetic. She set it aside. Not gone. Filed. There would be time to bleed later; right now there were exits to count and a choice to make, and Voss was still talking, which meant Voss still believed she was winning.

"Here is the choice," Voss said, and produced from inside her coat a single sheet of paper, dry under oilcloth, and held it up between two fingers. "Your ledger. Every crown of a debt that never existed, cancelled by my signature—which I am authorized to give, today, now, in exchange for the survey. Corvane wants the gates. The Guild wants Corvane's gratitude and a share of whatever the lowlands will pay to keep their feet dry. You want—" she smiled again, "—well. You want to be free. Everyone wants that. Most people just can't afford it."

"And if I refuse?" Isandro asked.

"Then Meric takes the survey off the girl, takes the boat, and takes you back to Callow's Rest in irons—for absconding, for obstruction, for whatever the paperwork requires. The paperwork is very flexible." Voss turned the ledger page so Isandro could see the signature line, blank, waiting. "Or you sign nothing, hand over nothing, and I burn this page in front of you, and you spend the rest of your life owing me a debt I invented. Which, I'll note, you already do."

Behind her, Isandro heard Bram draw breath—the sharp inhale of someone about to say something costly—and Isandro spoke first, because some prices should be paid alone.

"You're asking me to choose between myself and ten thousand people downstream," Isandro said. "Villages that drown when those gates fail. Children in Lowmarsh. Farmers in the Sedge country. You've seen the same rains I have. You know what happens if Corvane sells those gates to the highest bidder and lets the lowlands flood for leverage."

"I know what happens," Voss agreed. "I helped price it."

Isandro looked at the ledger. At the blank line. Eleven years. Her sister's face, which she had not let herself picture in six of them. The compass, gone. The badge, gone. The boat holed and patched and holed again. Everything she had traded away to a lie, piece by piece, believing each piece was the last one, believing that someday the ledger would balance and she would step out from under it into a life that was hers.

And here was the truth of it, arriving late and clean as cold water: the ledger had never weighed anything. It had never been real. She had been carrying air for eleven years and calling it stone.

She started to laugh. It came out of her low and helpless, and she saw Voss's composure flicker for the first time, because Maren Voss understood debts and ledgers and leashes, but she had never once understood laughter.

"What?" Voss demanded.

"You can't sell me my freedom," Isandro said. "It isn't yours. It never was. You wrote a number on a page and I believed the page, and that was the whole trick—that was *all* of it. And now I don't." She looked at the blank signature line a long moment, memorizing it, the way you memorize a grave. Then she stepped forward and took the ledger out of Voss's fingers, and before anyone could move she tore it—once, twice, methodically, the way she'd torn strips for bandages in the temple dark—and dropped the pieces into the black water between the stones.

The paper floated a moment, ink blooming green, and then the flood took it down.

"That," said Tallow Meric softly, "was a mistake."

"No," Isandro said. "It was the first thing in eleven years I've done for free."

* * *

Meric moved fast for a big man. The knife came off his belt in a single smooth draw, and Voss stepped back with the unhurried calm of a woman watching an investment mature, and Isandro had one heartbeat to register all of it—blade, distance, the wet stone, Bram behind her—when the gatehouse spoke.

Not the gatehouse. The city. Somewhere out in the drowned streets, deep under the flood, iron moved against iron with a groan that came up through the stone and into their boots, vast and grinding, the sound of a mechanism the size of a cathedral remembering its purpose. The water around the landing shivered. Ripples ran outward in rings.

Everyone stopped. Even Meric's knife hung mid-air.

Bram stood at the edge of the landing with her father's survey open in both hands, rain streaming down her face, and she was reading it—not studying it, *reading* it, the way you read a letter from someone you loved—and her eyes had gone wide and bright and terrible.

"He built a bypass," she whispered. "Under the gatehouse. A maintenance channel—he mapped it, he marked it—it's still intact, it's *here*, under our feet—" She looked up at Isandro, and there was no hesitation left in her anywhere, none of the girl from the net-sheds who wanted nothing to do with her father's legacy. "If we open the inner sluice from the gatehouse side, the water diverts before it ever reaches the lowland channels. Corvane can't sell what won't flood. But it has to be done from inside, and the tide window is—" she checked the sky, the waterline on the ancient stones, some clock only she could read— "hours. Maybe less."

"Then we go inside," Isandro said.

"You go nowhere," Voss said, and this time there was steel under the silk, and behind her, out on the gray water, Isandro saw the rest of it arrive: two more Corvane barges rounding the drowned rooftops, polesmen bending, coming on fast and silent. "Sterling, you are under Guild detention. Girl, that document is syndicate property. Neither of you is walking into that gatehouse."

Isandro rolled her shoulders once, loosening them, and felt eleven years fall off her like a coat she'd finally been permitted to remove. She had no badge. No debt. No name in any ledger worth tearing. What she had was a knife, a promise, a woman with a map who had opened a floodgate in her own heart, and several hours.

"Maren," she said, backing toward the great dark mouth of the gate, drawing her blade as she went, keeping herself between Voss's men and Bram, "you should have let me read the fine print."

The first barge pole struck stone. Meric came on. And Isandro Sterling, free for the first time in her adult life, turned and fought for the door.
