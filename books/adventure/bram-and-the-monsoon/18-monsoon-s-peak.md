## 18. Monsoon's Peak

The rain came down like the sky had finally given up pretending to be merciful.

Bram stood at the base of the gatehouse stairs with her father's survey pressed flat against her chest beneath her oilskin, and she could not stop shaking. Not from cold—though the water running down her collar was cold enough to ache—but from the sheer weight of what the map had shown her in the lamplight three nights ago, and what it demanded of her now.

Twelve gates. Twelve iron-and-stone floodgates set into the spine of Vess's old seawall, built by engineers four hundred years dead, drowned and forgotten and waiting. Her father had found them not to loot them but to *open* them—to divert the monsoon swell away from the lowland villages downstream before the river crested and took everything: the net-sheds, the stilt-houses, the children who played on the mudflats when the water was low enough to forgive them.

He had drawn it all in his cramped, slanting hand. He had annotated the winch mechanisms. He had written, in the margin beside Gate Seven, a note that had undone her completely: *If Bram ever sees this — the third cog sticks. Oil it first. She always was impatient.*

She always was.

"Gatehouse is clear," Isandro called down from the top of the stairs, her voice half-swallowed by the rain. She stood silhouetted against the grey violence of the storm, one hand on the doorframe, the other resting near the ranger's knife at her belt—the only weapon they had left after the salvage crew took everything else worth taking. "Voss's boat went around the east point ten minutes ago. We have maybe a quarter hour before she lands."

"Then I'd better start with Gate One."

"Bram." Isandro came down two steps, close enough that Bram could see her face under the hood: pale, set, the scar across her brow standing white against wet skin. "Once you open the sluices, there's no closing them. The whole western channel floods. Vess goes under again—all of it, everything above the second tier."

"I know."

"And if your father's calculations are wrong—"

"They're not wrong." Bram heard her own voice crack and hated it. "He was wrong about everything else. About leaving. About us. But he wasn't wrong about this. He spent eleven years on this survey, Isandro. Eleven years coming home broke and lying about where he'd been, and it was *this*, the whole time. He was mapping a way to save them." She wiped rain from her eyes with the back of her wrist. "I'm not letting Corvane sell it to the highest bidder and I'm not letting the Guild bury it in a vault. I'm opening the gates."

Isandro looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the stone between them. Then she nodded, once, the way she did everything—economical, final.

"Then go. I'll hold the stair."

"You can't hold a stair against six men with a knife."

"I can hold it long enough." A ghost of the dry smile. "I've been told I'm stubborn."

"You've been told worse."

"That too." Isandro touched her shoulder—brief, warm through the soaked oilskin—and turned back up the stairs. "Oil the third cog first. I remember."

* * *

The first gate was a hundred paces along the seawall, reached by a flooded causeway that came up to Bram's knees. She waded with the survey wrapped in waxed canvas held high, cursing her father with every step—*you couldn't have built these things on dry land, you couldn't have left me a legacy in a nice shallow field*—and loving him so fiercely it felt like drowning.

The mechanism housing rose out of the water like a broken tooth: a stone drum studded with corroded iron fittings, the great wooden gate visible below the surface as a darker shadow in darker water. She climbed onto the housing, knelt, and threw back the canvas.

Her father's instructions were precise. Lift the inspection plate. Clear the debris from the ratchet. Engage the handspike—he had even drawn the shape of it, and there, wedged in a niche where he must have left it years ago, an iron bar waited, rusted but sound.

*She always was impatient.* She made herself go slow anyway. Debris first: mud, a bird's nest, the small white bones of something. Ratchet clear. Plate reseated. Then the handspike into the primary socket, and her whole body's weight thrown against it.

Nothing.

She braced her boots and pulled again, teeth bared, rain hammering her shoulders, and this time something moved—a groan of metal deep in the stone, a grinding shudder—and then the ratchet caught and the drum began to turn, inch by screaming inch, and below her feet she felt the gate begin to rise.

Water thundered into the gap. The old channel, dry for centuries, drank it greedily.

One.

* * *

Gates Two and Three went easier—or rather, she got better at them. By the fourth she had learned the rhythm: clear, seat, drive, catch, turn. Her palms were raw. The rain had stopped being weather and become a fact of existence, a grey wall she worked inside. Each opened gate changed the sound of the flood around her—a new roar joining the chorus as the diverted water found its path westward, away from the lowlands, away from Callow's Rest and the net-sheds and the stilt-villages whose names she knew because her father had named them in the margins, one by one, like a prayer.

*So the children at Ferrow's Landing keep their sandbar. So the ferry at Ostermere runs another year.*

He had thought about all of them. He had thought about everyone except the daughter waiting at home counting the days between his returns, and Bram understood now, wading to Gate Six with her arms burning, that this was not the same thing as not loving her. It was maybe the most terrible kind of loving her there was.

She was driving the handspike on Gate Six when she heard the shot.

It cracked flat across the water, swallowed almost instantly by the storm, but she knew it for what it was. Her hands went still on the bar. Somewhere behind her, back toward the gatehouse, Isandro was holding a stair against six men with a knife, and the arithmetic of that did not work no matter how stubborn she was.

*Keep turning,* said the part of her that was her father's daughter. *Every minute you spend worrying is a minute the water keeps going the wrong way.*

She turned the drum until the ratchet locked. Then she ran.

* * *

The gatehouse stair was exactly as bad as she'd feared.

Two men lay crumpled at the bottom of it—one groaning, one very still—and a third sat against the wall clutching his arm and swearing in a loop. At the top, in the doorway, Isandro stood with her knife low and her breathing ragged, and there was blood on her. Some of it was not hers. Some of it was, quite a lot of it, welling dark from a gash along her ribs where her shirt was torn open.

"Bram." Her voice was steady, which was how Bram knew it was bad. Steady was what Isandro did instead of admitting pain. "How many gates?"

"Six. Six of twelve."

"Go finish."

"There are more of them coming—" She could see them across the water, figures in a coracle and a skiff, fighting the current toward the seawall. And behind them, larger, cutting a white bow-wave through the flooded streets: Maren Voss's longboat, Guild-grey, moving fast.

"I know." Isandro leaned against the doorframe. "Go."

"No." Bram climbed over the groaning man, up the slick steps, and put her shoulder under Isandro's arm before she could argue. "We do both. You can't stand, and I can't leave you, and we're both too stubborn to die today, so we're going to be practical about it."

For a moment Isandro let herself lean—let her weight settle, real and heavy, into Bram's side. "Practical," she repeated, and there was something in her voice that was almost laughter.

"The seventh gate has a housing right below this tower. I saw it from the stair." Bram got them moving, one lurching step at a time, down and around. "You sit where you can see the approach. I'll turn the drum. If anyone comes up the causeway, you tell me, and we'll improvise."

"We're very good at improvising."

"We're terrible at it. We just survive it."

* * *

They opened Gate Seven together, Isandro sitting propped against the housing with her knife across her knees and her free hand pressing her shirt against the wound, calling corrections while Bram hauled on the handspike. *Harder. Your angle's wrong—use your legs. There. There.* When the ratchet caught and the water roared through, Isandro actually smiled, white-lipped, and Bram thought: *if we live through this I am never letting this woman out of my sight again.*

Gates Eight and Nine were a blur of rain and iron and screaming shoulders. On Ten, the first of the syndicate men reached the causeway—a big man with a boarding axe, fighting the knee-deep water—and Isandro rose off the stone like something out of a story Bram would never have believed, met him at the edge of the housing, and put him into the flood with three movements and a look on her face Bram had never seen: not anger. Arithmetic. The same cold accounting she gave the Guild's ledger, applied to flesh.

He went under. The current took him. Two others behind him thought better of it and fell back.

But the fourth one didn't come up the causeway. He came over the roof of the housing, dropping out of the rain with a knife already moving, and Bram had just enough time to see it—not enough time to move—and then Isandro was there, stepping between, and the blade that had been meant for Bram's throat went into Isandro's side instead, below the ribs, sliding deep before the man seemed to understand what he'd done.

What happened next happened fast. Isandro's knife came up and across; the man folded; the knife clattered away into the water. And Isandro stayed standing, which frightened Bram more than anything—stood swaying with the hilt of the syndicate man's blade still jutting from under her arm, and said, in that maddeningly level voice:

"Ten done. Two left. Go."

"I'm pulling it out."

"If you pull it out I bleed faster. Leave it. Bind it. *Turn the drum.*"

Bram bound it—strips of her own shirt, wrapped hard around the blade where it entered, her hands shaking so badly Isandro had to guide them—and then she went back to the handspike, and she drove Gates Eleven and Twelve with her father's voice in her head and Isandro's blood on her fingers, and when the last ratchet locked and the last gate groaned up out of its ancient bed, the sound the water made was not a roar anymore.

It was a chord. Twelve voices, all singing west.

* * *

The change was visible within minutes. From the top of the gatehouse tower, where Bram half-carried Isandro to watch, they could see the main channel's furious rise beginning to slacken—the brown swell that should have crested over the lowlands in three hours' time losing its hunger, spilling instead into the old western channel Vess's builders had cut for exactly this purpose, four centuries before anyone alive needed it. The water found its second path and took it, and somewhere downstream, in villages Bram had never seen, people she would never meet were going to wake tomorrow to a flooded season that stayed inside its banks.

Her father's margins, come true. *So the children at Ferrow's Landing keep their sandbar.*

"He knew," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "All those years, all those lies about prospecting—he knew exactly what he'd found and he couldn't tell anyone because Corvane would have buried it or sold it, so he just... kept working. Alone. For us. For strangers."

Isandro sat slumped against the parapet, grey-faced, one hand pressed over the binding, the other finding Bram's and holding on. "Sounds familiar," she managed. "Carrying something alone because telling someone would cost too much."

"Don't you dare make this about your arc right now."

A breath that might have been a laugh. "Wouldn't dream of it."

Below them, the Guild longboat grounded against the seawall, and Maren Voss stepped out into the rain with six enforcers fanning out behind her, and every one of them stopped—stopped dead—when they saw the twelve open gates and the water going west.

Voss looked up at the tower. Even at that distance, Bram saw the exact moment the arithmetic failed her: the moment she understood that the map was worthless now, delivered, spent, its purpose accomplished in front of witnesses, and that whatever she'd promised Corvane and whatever she'd hidden from the Guild had just become evidence instead of profit.

"She's going to run," Isandro said quietly.

"Let her." Bram watched the enforcers exchange looks among themselves—men who had been told a story about treasure and were watching a story about salvation instead. "Look at them. They know which story they were sold."

Voss shouted an order. Two of the six obeyed. Four did not. And far off across the flooded city, from the direction of the eastern channel, came a sound neither of them expected: horns. Deep, formal, unmistakably official. Boats rounding the point flying the Guild's auditor colors—three of them, four, sweeping toward the seawall through the rain.

Isandro's grip on Bram's hand tightened. "Auditors," she breathed. "Someone filed a complaint about Corvane. Someone with access to Guild records." Her eyes found Bram's, and despite the blood loss, despite everything, there was light in them. "Voss didn't just lie to me, Bram. She lied to the *Guild*. And the Guild does not forgive that."

On the seawall below, Maren Voss stood alone in the rain, her enforcers drifting away from her one by one like a tide going out, watching the auditor boats come for her with the expression of a woman reading her own ledger's final line.

Bram put her arm around Isandro and held her upright, and together they watched the water take everything that was owed.
