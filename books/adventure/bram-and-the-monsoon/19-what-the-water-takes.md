## 19. What the Water Takes

The knife wound in Isandro's side had stopped arguing with her. That was the worst part. For the first hour it had screamed, and she had known exactly where she was and what it was costing her. Now it had gone quiet and warm, a coal tucked beneath her ribs, and she could feel herself starting to negotiate with it — *just a little longer, just hold the door a little longer* — the way a drowning woman negotiates with the current.

She stood in the gatehouse doorway with a salvaged boat-hook in both hands and blood soaking through the sash she'd knotted around her waist, and she watched Maren Voss come across the flooded plaza in a longboat with six of Corvane's hired blades and the particular unhurried confidence of a woman who had already won.

The rain came down in ropes. Behind Isandro, in the gatehouse's drowned interior, Bram was somewhere in the dark with a lamp and her father's survey, hauling on mechanisms that hadn't turned in two hundred years, opening the floodgates of Vess. Isandro could hear it — a deep, grinding groan beneath the water, the sound of an old machine remembering its purpose. Every minute it continued, the water that would have drowned the lowland villages went somewhere else. Somewhere harmless.

That was the whole of it, then. That was what the promise had turned into: a door held open in the rain, with her body as the hinge.

"You look terrible, Sterling." Voss's longboat bumped against the gatehouse steps. She did not rise. She sat with her hands folded on her knee, her Guild-grey coat dark with rain, and looked up at Isandro the way she had looked at her across ledgers for nine years — patient, proprietary, faintly amused. "You always did take other people's fights personally."

"They're not other people's." Isandro's voice came out rougher than she intended. She shifted her grip on the boat-hook and felt the wound pull, a bright wire of pain from hip to rib. "The villages downstream. Twelve thousand people. You knew what the map was."

"I knew what it was *worth*." Voss tilted her head. Water ran off the brim of her hat in a steady thread. "Which is more than the prospector knew, and more than his daughter knows, and — if we're honest — considerably more than you know. Corvane wanted the survey to sell to the river lords, who'd have used it to hold the floodgates shut and sell dry land to the highest bidders. The Guild wanted it for the same reason, with better bookkeeping. And you—" she smiled, and it was almost fond, "—you wanted to be the kind of woman who keeps promises to dead men. I did try to warn you about treasure stories."

"You didn't warn me. You threatened me."

"I *invested* in you." For the first time, something moved behind Voss's eyes, something that was not patience at all. "Nine years, Sterling. Nine years of interest and fees and a debt I could have called in any season, and I let you walk around the flats with your badge and your compass and your little reputation, because a tracker who believes she's working her way free is worth ten who know they're owned. And now you're standing in a ruin, bleeding out for a stranger's daughter, and the debt is still there. It will always be there. That's the lesson. That's the whole of the Guild's arithmetic." She unfolded her hands. "So. Here is the last offer I will ever make you. Give me the survey. Give me the girl's cooperation. And I will write *paid* in the ledger with my own hand, and you can walk out of this rain a free woman."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, the floodgates groaned again, deeper now, and Isandro felt it through the soles of her boots — the whole drowned city shifting its weight, the water beginning to move the way a dead man had drawn it moving, forty years ago, in ink that was mostly hope.

Isandro thought about the ledger. She thought about the number that had hung over her since she was nineteen years old — the price of a medicine that hadn't worked, the price of a sister's last winter, converted into fees and compounded into a figure no honest labor could ever touch. She thought about how she had measured every year of her life against it, every trail followed, every thief run down across the flats, and how the number had never once grown smaller.

And she thought — with a clarity that the knife wound seemed to have burned clean into her — that she had never actually seen the ledger.

"Show it to me," Isandro said.

Voss's smile thinned. "What?"

"The debt. The original entry. Nine years, and I've never once seen the paper. You've told me the figure, you've told me the interest, you've told me what happens if I run. Show me the entry."

"You're in no position to—"

"Show me," Isandro said, "or I put this boat-hook through the bottom of your longboat and we all find out how deep this plaza is."

One of Corvane's men laughed — a short, ugly sound — and Voss's hand came up just slightly, and the laugh died. She studied Isandro for a long moment, rain running down her face, and then she did something Isandro had never seen her do in nine years.

She hesitated.

"You're dying," Voss said. "You're standing there bleeding into your boots, the girl is going to open gates that will cost Corvane a fortune, and your play is *bookkeeping*."

"My play," Isandro said, "is that you flinched."

And that was when the horns sounded.

* * *

They came up the flooded river road in a line of grey boats with grey lanterns — not Corvane's gilded barges, not the Guild's black-hulled enforcer skiffs, but something between, something official in a way that made Isandro's spine straighten before her mind caught up. She had seen that grey twice in her life. Once, as a girl, when the auditors came to her home village to hang a water magistrate. Once, from a long way off, on the flats, and she had ridden hard in the other direction.

Guild auditors. The Guild's own conscience — the thing the enforcers pretended did not exist, the ledger that checked the ledgers.

"Voss." The lead boat's speaker was a thin, grey woman with a voice that carried flat across the water like a blade laid on a table. "Maren Voss, Senior Enforcer, Callow's Rest district. By authority of the Third Audit, you are relieved of your commission. Stand your boat down."

Voss rose, finally, to her feet in the longboat, and Isandro watched nine years of certainty drain out of her face in the space of a breath. "Auditor Sef. This is an active Guild matter—"

"This is an active *Corvane* matter," Sef said. "Which you have been an active part of for — we believe — six years. The syndicate's river ledgers were seized at the Toll Bridge three days ago. Your name is in them, Maren. Your name is in them a great many times, and in a great many interesting columns." The grey boat slid closer, oars lifting, dripping. "Including a column marked *S. Sterling*. Would you like to tell me about that column, or shall I?"

The rain came down. The floodgates groaned. Isandro stood in the doorway with her boat-hook and her wound and her heart going like a drum, and she watched Maren Voss — who had owned her for nine years, who had been the fixed star of every fear she had, the number at the bottom of every night — look around at Corvane's hired blades and find, one by one, that they were looking away from her. Hired blades know which way the water is moving. It is the first thing they learn.

"The entry," Isandro said quietly. "The debt. Was it real?"

Voss didn't answer. Auditor Sef did, from the grey boat, without even looking at her — the way a person answers a question that has already been answered in a room Isandro had never been invited into.

"The Sterling debt was entered nine years ago with no originating instrument," Sef said. "No loan record. No purchase of claim. It exists in one ledger and one ledger only — the district enforcement ledger, under Voss's seal." She turned her flat grey eyes on Isandro then, and something in them was almost gentle, in the way a scalpel is gentle. "There was never a debt, Ranger. There was a leash, and a collar shaped like a debt. It's a technique we've found in four districts now. You are the fifth person it was used on that we know of."

Isandro heard the words. She understood each of them individually. But they did not seem to want to assemble into meaning — because if they assembled, then nine years came apart. The winters she had spent in a ranger's hut eating salt fish to make payments. The compass she had carried like a stone in her pocket because she could never quite bring herself to pawn it, because it was the last thing she owned that the ledger couldn't touch. The trails she had run down for the Guild for free, for *credit*, for the slow imaginary light at the end of a tunnel that had been painted on the tunnel wall.

The medicine hadn't worked. Her sister had died anyway. And the price of that failed mercy had never been owed to anyone at all.

She didn't know she had sat down until the stone step was under her and the boat-hook was clattering on the flagstones and Voss was shouting — actually shouting, the composure gone clean out of her — about jurisdiction and the Third Audit and *do you know what I've built*, and none of it was addressed to Isandro anymore. Isandro was no longer the thing in the room that mattered. After nine years of being the thing that mattered — the asset, the tracker, the debt — she had become, in the space of a single grey boat's arrival, nobody's business at all.

It should have felt like falling. It felt like being set down.

* * *

They took Voss away in the grey boat, sitting very straight with her hands bound in her lap, and to the end she held her spine like a woman who had simply been moved to a different office. But as the boat pushed off she looked up at Isandro one last time, rain streaming down her face, and what was in her eyes was not hatred.

It was arithmetic. The look of a woman recalculating, one final time, an equation that had stopped balancing — and finding that the error had been hers, all along, in the very first entry.

"Your sister," Voss said, and her voice was almost ordinary, almost the voice from a hundred ledger conversations. "The medicine. It was never going to work. I knew that too. I read the physician's report before I ever came to your door." The oars dipped. The grey boat slid out into the rain. "I chose you *because* you were grieving. Grieving people make the best leashes. Remember that about the Guild, Sterling, when they come to offer you your badge back."

Then the rain took her, and the grey lanterns shrank, and the plaza was quiet except for the water and the deep slow groan of the floodgates doing what a dead man had drawn them to do.

Isandro stayed on the step for a while. She found she could not stand yet, and she found, examining the fact without much alarm, that she did not especially want to. The wound was singing again. The rain was cold on her neck. Somewhere below her, in the dark of the gatehouse, a lamp was coming up the stairs.

Bram appeared in the doorway with the lamp held high and her father's survey rolled and oilclothed under one arm, soaked to the skin, her hands bleeding from the gate mechanisms, her face streaked with rust and rain — and she took one look at Isandro sitting on the step in a spreading pool of her own blood and went white.

"You're hurt."

"I'm *free*," Isandro said, and heard how it sounded, and started, helplessly, to laugh — a wet, cracked sound that hurt the wound and kept going anyway. "Bram. Bram, listen to me. There was never any debt. Nine years. It was — she made it up. She wrote it in a book and I paid it for nine years and it was never—"

She couldn't finish. Bram got down on her knees on the wet stone, lamp set aside, and took Isandro's face in her cold, cut, rust-streaked hands, and looked at her the way she had looked at her father's survey the night she finally understood it — grief and fury and something underneath both of them, something with a heading.

"Isandro." Her voice was steady. Her thumbs were wiping rain off Isandro's cheeks, and it took Isandro a moment to realize some of it wasn't rain. "Listen to me. Whatever she took — she can't keep it. Whatever the book said — it's a book. It's paper. You know what else is paper?" She lifted the oilcloth bundle, her father's survey, the thing two men and a syndicate and an entire Guild district had bled for. "This. And this one's *true*, and it's going to save twelve thousand people, because you carried it and I opened the gates and she can't have either of those things back. Do you hear me? The water's already gone where it's going to go. Nobody gets to un-deliver it."

The floodgates, below them, fell silent — a deep, settled, finished silence, the silence of a machine that had done its work. And through the gatehouse arch, Isandro could see the water of the plaza beginning, very slowly, to move — sliding east, away from the lowlands, away from the villages, toward the empty salt country where it could spread and thin and do no harm. Where it could become, in a few seasons, just weather. Just the monsoon, doing what the monsoon does.

"Your father," Isandro said. Her voice was failing her now; the wound and the relief were pulling her down toward the stone, toward the dark, and she let them, because Bram was there. "He drew it true."

"Yes," Bram said, and her voice broke on it, just once, and she pressed her forehead to Isandro's, lamp-flame and rain and blood between them. "He drew it true."

Isandro went down into the dark with the taste of rain and the sound of water going where it was meant to go, and the last thing she thought, before the dark took her, was that she had spent nine years being owned by a number — and that the number was dead now, drowned, taken by the water along with everything else it had ever touched.

* * *

She woke to lamplight and the smell of boiled herbs, on a pallet in the gatehouse's high dry room, with Bram asleep sitting up against the wall beside her, one hand still wrapped around Isandro's wrist as if she'd been counting a pulse in her sleep. Through the arrow-slit window, dawn was coming up grey-gold over a transformed world: the floodwaters of Vess sliding away eastward, rooftops emerging black and dripping from the retreat, and on the river road below, a line of grey boats with grey lanterns, and clerks with oilskin satchels, and — Isandro squinted, disbelieving — a Guild banner run up over the gatehouse, and a clerk at a folding table, and a queue.

A queue of people waiting to give testimony. Waiting, she realized, to be un-owned.

The door opened and Auditor Sef came in, grey and thin and quiet, carrying a ledger under one arm and a small paper packet in her hand. She looked at Isandro for a moment, and at Bram's sleeping hand on Isandro's wrist, and her flat grey eyes did the almost-gentle thing again.

"You're awake. Good. Two things." She set the paper packet on the pallet. Inside, when Isandro touched it with a finger, was the cold familiar weight of a ranger's badge — hers, scratched and salt-pitted, the one she'd traded to a salvage crew for hull-pitch and planks. The auditors had recovered it. Of course they had. The Guild recovered everything. "The badge is yours. The commission is yours, if you want it — the Guild would very much like you to want it, after the spectacle you made of us, and I'd think hard before you say yes, because they'll want it *very* much, and wanting things very much is how districts like Callow's Rest got the way they are." Sef opened the ledger, and turned to a page near the front, and held it out so Isandro could see.

A single line, struck through with a heavy grey rule. *S. Sterling — debt — nil. Entered in error. Expunged.*

Nine years, reduced to a line of ink and a rule drawn through it.

"The second thing," Sef said, closing the book, "is that the lowland council has sent word ahead. They're expecting the survey — and the surveyor's daughter — at the dry-season council in eleven days. There will be a boat provided. There will be an escort, if she wants one." The auditor paused at the door, and for the first time she looked almost, faintly, like a person. "I'm told the surveyor's daughter has opinions about escorts."

Isandro looked down at Bram, still asleep against the wall, her cut hands open in her lap, her face for once unguarded — and felt, beneath the herb-smell and the wound and the enormous lightness of a life with nothing owed in it, something that was not duty and not debt and not promise, but simply want, plain as a heading on a chart.

"Tell them," Isandro said, "she'll bring her own."
