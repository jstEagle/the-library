## 20. Delivered

The lowland council met in a granary that had been a granary for three hundred years and would be one for three hundred more, if the waters behaved. Bram Halloway stood outside its doors with her father's survey rolled tight against her chest, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the same cord he had used to bundle his tools when she was small enough to ride on his shoulders through the reed-beds. The cord was frayed. She had not replaced it. Some things you kept broken because the breaking was part of what they meant.

The village of Sallowmere smelled the way all lowland villages smelled after the peak of the monsoon: mud and woodsmoke and drying nets, the green rot of orchards standing to their waists in floodwater, and underneath it all the clean mineral breath of water that had finally begun to let go. The receding had left its marks everywhere. A brown line on every wall like a tide chart written by a careless god. Boats moored to second-story windows. Children racing paper hulls down what had been, two weeks ago, a street.

Bram watched them and thought: *this is what it would have been.* Not this street drowned. Not these houses gone dark under six feet of moving water with their doors open and their tables set. Her father's map — no, his *survey*, she was learning to call it, the way he would have insisted, the way he had insisted in every marginal note in that cramped, slanting hand — had bent the monsoon aside. Had taken the worst of the flood and turned it into the empty country west of the gates, where nothing lived but salt grass and the ghosts of old claims.

Where Isandro had bled for it. Where Isandro still lay healing, thirty miles north, in a bed she'd refused at first and then surrendered to with the particular gracelessness of a woman who did not know how to be cared for.

Bram touched the oilcloth roll through her shirt, felt its edges against her sternum, and went up the steps into the granary.

* * *

The council chamber was the granary's upper floor, where the grain bins had been knocked out to make room for a long table scarred by generations of elbows and ledger-corners. Nine councillors sat behind it, which seemed too many until Bram remembered that nine was how you made sure no single family owned the decision. At the center sat an old woman with river-clay eyes and hands folded like something waiting to be opened: Councillor Ashe, whom Bram had never met and knew anyway, because everyone in the lowlands knew Ashe the way you knew weather.

They had heard she was coming. Of course they had. Word traveled faster than boats in the lowlands, and the story had arrived ahead of her wearing better clothes than the truth — tales of a ranger who held a gatehouse alone against armed men, of a prospector's daughter who opened floodgates older than anyone's grandmother, of the Guild's own auditors arriving out of nowhere like crows to a carcass. Bram had spent the whole journey downstream wincing at what the story must be becoming.

"Name yourself," said Ashe. Not unkindly. The way you name yourself before a crossing.

"Bram Halloway. Daughter of Edric Halloway." It came out steadier than she expected. Saying it plainly, without the flinch, was new. "Prospector. Surveyor." She laid the roll on the table and untied the frayed cord. "He mapped the Vess floodgates. I've come to deliver it."

She unrolled the survey across the scarred wood, weighting the corners with the inkpot, a candlestick, a chipped cup a clerk pushed toward her without being asked, and her own two hands.

For a moment nobody spoke. The lamplight caught the vellum the way it had caught it in a leaking temple, in a holed boat, in a gatehouse shaking with thunder — except now it lay flat and still, and there was time to look at it properly. The drowned city drawn in section and elevation, its gates numbered in her father's hand, each one annotated with flow rates calculated across thirty years of monsoons he had apparently studied from a distance he'd never explained to anyone. The diversion channel marked in red. The villages downstream named in a column along the margin — Sallowmere first, in letters pressed so hard into the vellum they stood up like braille.

Bram had looked at that column a hundred times since the gatehouse. She looked at it now and felt the grief shift in her chest, the way a load shifts when you finally find the right place to carry it.

"He wasn't looking for treasure," she said. "I need you to understand that, because everyone will tell you otherwise, and because I believed otherwise for most of my life. He surveyed those gates to save these villages. All of them." Her finger rested on Sallowmere. "He died before he could bring it here himself. He gave the map to a stranger and asked her to finish the delivery. She did." Bram's throat tightened around the next words and she let them come anyway. "Her name is Isandro Sterling. Whatever the Guild says about her now, whatever the auditors dig up — she held the gatehouse so I could open the gates. She took a knife doing it. If your gratitude has anywhere to go, it goes to her."

Ashe's clay-colored eyes moved from the survey to Bram's face and stayed there a long moment. "We have heard the name Sterling," she said. "We have heard other things too. That the Saltwater Guild claims her debt is settled. That the Guild's own men came for her and left in chains instead." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "The Guild does not often leave anything in chains unless the chains were meant for themselves."

"The auditors found ledgers," Bram said. "Isandro told me some of it. Enough. The debt they held over her was written in a book that doesn't balance. They invented it to own her compass, basically. To own her tracking." She heard the edge come into her voice and didn't file it down. "They leashed her for years with a lie about money, and the first thing she did with her freedom was bleed for strangers in a flooded gatehouse. So."

Silence again. One of the younger councillors, a man with a net-mender's forearms and an accountant's squint, leaned over the margin column and read the village names aloud under his breath, like a prayer or an inventory.

"Master Halloway's figures," he said at last. "Are they sound?"

"They're sound," Bram said. "I checked them. Every gate, every rate. I'm a navigator; numbers are my trade. He taught me the trade badly and late and mostly by leaving, but he taught it true." She swallowed. "There are corrections in the margins. Things he learned after he drew the main sheets. He never stopped working on it. That's the thing I keep—" Her voice snagged. She breathed through it. "That's the thing. He never stopped. However far away he was, however long he was gone, he was always working on coming back with something worth the leaving. This is what he came back with."

Ashe reached out and turned the vellum slightly, studying the red diversion channel, the gate sequence, the neat annotation beside Gate Four: *open last, and only after Three holds — E.H.*

"Then the council accepts the delivery," Ashe said, "in the name of Edric Halloway, surveyor, and we will say so in the record, and we will say it loudly enough that the record reaches every village in that column." Her gaze lifted. "And what does the daughter ask in return?"

Bram had prepared for this question on the whole journey down. She had rehearsed answers in the bottom of the patched skiff — timber for the Verity's new planking, a stipend, a letter of standing that would keep the Guild's remnant enforcers from ever sniffing at Isandro's door again. She had rehearsed them and discarded most of them, and now, standing in the grain-smelling light with her father's work spread out like a life finally read correctly, she found only one answer left in her mouth.

"Nothing," she said. "It isn't mine to sell. It was his to give, and giving it was the point." Then, because honesty had become a habit somewhere between a temple and a gatehouse and she couldn't seem to shake it: "Though if the council ever needs a navigator who knows these channels now better than any chart afloat — my boat is called the Verity, and she's for hire."

Something moved through the room that was almost a smile, passed from face to face like a lamp.

* * *

They fed her afterward — fish stew and flatbread and a sweetness made from drowned-orchard pears that had ripened underwater and somehow survived — and the younger councillor, whose name was Pell, walked her out along the levee as the light went long and coppery over the draining fields. The water was going down fast now, revealing the world in stages: first rooftops, then fence lines, then the pale backs of drowned fields steaming in the evening sun. People were already walking out into the shallows, feeling for their doorsteps.

"Your father," Pell said carefully. "Did he see it? The water turning?"

"No," Bram said. "He saw the flats west of the gates. Empty country. That's what he bought with the last of his blood — empty country, thirty miles from anywhere, where nobody had to watch it happen." She looked at the levee grass, bent flat by weeks of current and already springing back. "I used to think that was a small thing to die for. An absence. Now I think an absence is the biggest thing there is. Everything that's still here —" she gestured at the village, the smoke, the children, the stubborn lights coming on along the shore — "is here inside his absence. Like a boat floating in the hole where a stone used to be."

Pell considered that with the squinting seriousness of a man who balanced accounts. "The council will want him remembered," he said. "A marker, maybe. At the gates."

"He'd hate that," Bram said, and surprised herself with a laugh that came out wet. "He hated markers. He said a man's work should stand where the work stands." She stopped at the top of the levee. Below, moored among a dozen borrowed and battered hulls, rode the Verity — patched twice over, scarred along the port side where Corvane's barges had nearly ended everything, riding low and patient and utterly herself. Bram's chest did the thing it always did when she saw her boat. "But thank you. Say his name in the record. That's enough. Names travel in this country. He taught me that too, without meaning to."

She went down to the boat alone.

* * *

Night came up soft off the water. Bram sat in the Verity's stern with a lantern turned low and the survey unrolled across her knees — the copy, that is; the original was locked in the council's strongbox upstairs, and Pell had pressed a fair copy on her at the door, saying the daughter should keep her father's hand. She hadn't argued. She was done arguing with gifts.

She had already decided what to do with it.

Her fingers found the corner. Lower right, where the vellum was softest from handling, where the main sheet faded into blank space — and into a drawing. Small, childish in its confidence, unmistakably old: a little boat with a sail like a shark's fin, and two stick figures in it, one tall and one short, and above them, in the slanted hand she had spent her childhood resenting and her adulthood missing, the words *for Bee, who steers true.*

He had drawn it the year she was seven, the year of the long stay, the good year, when he'd actually been home through an entire monsoon and taught her knots on the porch and called her Bee because she'd once steered them straight through a reed-bed fog without panicking. She had forgotten the drawing existed. She had spent twenty years certain her father had left nothing behind but debts and departures, and here it was, in the corner of the thing that saved ten thousand lives: proof that once, for one season, he had simply been her father, and had thought of her even while he worked.

Grief, she was learning, didn't leave. It just changed harbors. Tonight it came to rest somewhere quiet — not the raw anchorage of the temple, not the storm-moor of the gatehouse, but something sheltered and deep, a place a boat could sit out weather. *You steered true, Bee,* the drawing said. And she had. All the way here. With a stranger's promise and a dead man's map and a woman who had given up her badge and her compass and her name in a Guild ledger to get it delivered.

Bram took out her knife and cut the corner free. Clean, along the fold-lines, taking nothing that carried data — Pell had checked, half-laughing, that she wasn't mutilating a document of civic record. Just the corner. Just the boat with the shark-fin sail and the two figures and five words in her father's hand.

She folded it small and put it inside her shirt, over her sternum, where the oilcloth roll had ridden for three hundred miles. It fit there like it had been measured for the spot.

Then she sat a while longer in the dark with the lantern out, listening to the water drop. Somewhere north, thirty miles and a day's sail, Isandro was lying in a borrowed bed with a stitched side, pretending she didn't count the hours. Bram smiled at the dark. She'd read the letter Isandro had sent down with the last courier — four lines, grudging, formal, and then the fifth line, unprompted, unguarded, in handwriting that hurried: *The dry season's starting. Someone should teach me what people do with a day that isn't owed to anyone.*

Bram looked south, where the floodwaters were letting go of the land mile by mile, opening channels and cutting new ones, the whole delta rearranging itself around her father's red line. The Verity rocked under her, patched and scarred and ready.

"Well," she said aloud, to the boat, to the corner in her shirt, to the man who had drawn it, "he asked me to stay through the dry season. And I find I'm considering it."

She lit the lantern, cast off, and turned the Verity's bow north, into the dark and the falling water, steering true.
