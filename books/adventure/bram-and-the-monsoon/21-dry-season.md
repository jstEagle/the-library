## 21. Dry Season

The wound in Isandro's side had opinions about the weather.

It ached when the wind came off the flats, throbbed when she laughed, and kept its own counsel the rest of the time—like an old partner who'd decided to stay on out of spite. She'd learned to read it the way she'd once read the salt horizon: a pressure change in the flesh, a barometer stitched into her body by Voss's knife. Some mornings it told her rain was coming. Most mornings it simply reminded her that she was still alive, which was, she was discovering, a stranger thing than being alive.

The village of Sallowmere sat at the edge of the lowlands like something the water had considered taking and then, on reflection, left alone. Houses on stilts. Nets strung between poles like laundry. A bell tower whose lower third had been underwater for so long that barnacles crusted the brick and the bell itself rang with a drowned, muffled voice. The people there had looked at her the way villagers always looked at strangers—with polite arithmetic behind their eyes—and then Bram had said, "She's the one who held the gatehouse," and the arithmetic changed to something warmer, though no less weighing.

Isandro didn't know what to do with warmth. She'd spent eleven years learning what to do with debt.

* * *

The first week, she counted things.

It was habit, not compulsion—or so she told herself. She counted the steps from Bram's stilt-house door to the water: nineteen, twenty when the river ran high. She counted the boats moored along the pilings: thirty-one, of which nine were seaworthy past the sandbar. She counted the coins in her pocket, because counting them was easier than admitting how few they were: four coppers and a bent silver piece with a face worn smooth as an egg.

And she counted the days since the Guild's auditors had come upriver in their grey boat, since Maren Voss had been taken off the gatehouse dock in irons, since a clerk with ink-stained cuffs had unrolled a ledger on the veranda of the tollhouse and said, in the toneless voice of a man reading someone else's eulogy:

"There is no record of any debt against Isandro Sterling. There never was."

Eleven years. Eleven years of rations half-eaten, of boots resoled until the leather gave out, of a compass she could never pawn because pawning it would have meant admitting she needed the money more than she needed north. Eleven years of a number that grew no matter how much she paid into it, a hole with no bottom because there had never been anything in it to begin with. The Guild hadn't owned her labor. They'd owned her belief in the debt, and belief, it turned out, was the only currency that never devalued.

The clerk had offered restitution. A sum, he said, adjusted for hardship. He'd said *hardship* the way you'd say *weather*, and Isandro had looked at the figure he named—a figure that wouldn't have covered one year of the medicine for Lira, let alone the eleven she'd paid—and understood that the Guild's apology was just another ledger entry, balanced and closed.

"Keep it," she'd said. "Put it toward the villages. The floodgates cost somebody something."

Bram had squeezed her hand under the table hard enough to leave marks, and later, walking back along the levee, had said nothing at all, which from Bram was the loudest kind of agreement.

So now Isandro had four coppers, a bent silver piece, no badge, no boat—the Verity was holed and hauled out on the mud below Bram's house, ribs showing like a beached whale—and no debt. No debt. She turned the phrase over some mornings like a stone found on a path, checking it for tricks.

* * *

"You're mending my nets wrong."

Bram stood on the walkway with two buckets and the particular expression she wore when she'd been watching someone for a while and had decided to say so. Her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth Isandro recognized as having once been part of a signal flag. Monsoon season was ending; the light came slantwise now, gold instead of silver, and it caught the salt-and-sun map of freckles across her nose.

"They look mended," Isandro said.

"They look like a ranger tried to mend them." Bram set down the buckets, knelt, and pulled three knots loose with brisk, unoffended fingers. "You tie everything like it might have to hold your weight off a cliff. It's a net, not a rope bridge. It's supposed to give." She glanced up. "Everything with you holds too tight. You know that?"

Isandro looked out at the water. "I'm aware."

"I know you're aware. That's not the same as doing something about it." Bram took the netting needle from her hand, their fingers brushing, and did not make a production of it, which Isandro appreciated. Bram never made productions. She just said things, the way water said things, steadily, until you were worn into a new shape. "Come on. There's fish to gut and I refuse to do it alone while you sit there performing convalescence."

"I was shot."

"You were *knifed*, months ago, and you've been milking it ever since because being wounded is the last useful thing you know how to be." Bram said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse, and got up with her buckets. "Well? Are you coming?"

Isandro came.

That was the thing she kept noticing, in this season between seasons: how easy the yeses were here. Yes to gutting fish. Yes to hauling the Verity's sails up onto Bram's mother's old drying racks. Yes to sitting on the walkway at dusk while the village children dared each other closer and closer to the stranger with the scar, until finally a girl of about seven planted herself in front of Isandro and demanded to see it, and Isandro lifted her shirt hem without thinking, and the girl examined the puckered line with the gravity of a magistrate and pronounced it "not even that impressive," and ran off, and Isandro had laughed until her stitches—long dissolved—ached, and Bram had watched her laugh with an expression Isandro couldn't name and didn't try to.

In Callow's Rest, every yes had been a transaction. Here, they were just yeses. She kept waiting for the invoice.

* * *

The dreams were the last territory the Guild occupied.

They came most nights: the salt flats, the dust storm, the shape of Odrik's camp dissolving in the haze. Or worse, the ledger itself, vast as a temple wall, columns of figures marching to the horizon, and somewhere in it her name repeated ten thousand times, each entry a year, and a voice—not Voss's, something older and more bureaucratic, the voice of the institution itself—saying *you haven't finished paying*. And she would wake with her hand pressed to her side, over the knife scar, counting her own heartbeat like coins.

One such night, deep in the dry season's beginning, she woke and found Bram already awake beside her, propped on one elbow, watching her with the flat patience she brought to storms.

"The ledger again," Bram said. Not a question.

"It doesn't exist," Isandro said. "I know it doesn't exist. I watched a man say so out loud."

"Dreams don't read audit reports." Bram settled back down, pulled the blanket over them both. "My father haunted my sleep for two years after he died. Kept leaving in the dream. Every night, the same door, the same back, the same hat going down the same road. And then one night I dreamed he stayed, and I woke up furious, because staying wasn't true either. What finally worked wasn't waiting for him to stop haunting me. It was deciding what I wanted him to have left me." She was quiet a moment. "He left me the water, in the end. So now when he shows up, I put him in a boat and we go somewhere good."

Isandro stared at the ceiling beams. "You're saying I should dream about ledgers differently."

"I'm saying the Guild taught you who you were for eleven years, and dead or arrested, they're still teaching you, every night, unless you take the chalk out of their hand." Bram's voice was drowsy now, but level. "Nobody's owed anything here, Isandro. Not by you. That's the whole point of the place. You'll get used to it or you won't, but either way, stop counting the steps to the water. I've seen you doing it. Nineteen, twenty. Nobody's measuring you."

Isandro lay awake long after Bram slept, listening to the river slap the stilts, and thought: *then who is?*

And heard, for the first time in eleven years, no answer at all.

* * *

The Verity came out of the mud in the second month.

It was Bram's idea, announced the way all her ideas were announced—as accomplished fact. "We're re-bottoming her before the dry season ends. Salvage crew left us two planks and good will; I traded them the jib for the rest. You're on caulking duty. Your knots are ugly but tight, and tight is what a seam wants."

So Isandro learned a boat from the inside out, which was a thing she'd never done. She'd used boats the way she'd used roads: as means, as escape routes, as lines on other people's maps. But kneeling inside the Verity's hull with a mallet and a reel of oakum, driving cotton into seams that had drunk half the lowlands during their flight from Corvane's barges, she began to understand the vessel as Bram understood her—not as transport but as argument, as biography. Every patch was a story. The plank Bram's father had replaced the summer Isandro was still paying phantom interest. The scar where the syndicate's grapnel had bitten. The new wood, pale as bone, that they laid themselves, side by side, in the smell of pitch and hot timber.

"You're smiling," Bram observed, around a mouthful of tacks.

"I'm working."

"You're smiling at a boat."

"It's a good boat."

"It's a leaky boat we nearly died in twice."

"That's probably why it's a good boat." Isandro drove a tack home. "Things you survive get to be good. That's the rule."

Bram looked at her for a long moment, mallet resting on her knee, and Isandro realized she'd said something true without calculating it first, and that Bram had noticed, and that neither of them was going to make a production of it.

* * *

The letter came in the third month, carried upriver by a trader who also brought salt, lamp oil, and gossip. It bore the seal of the Saltwater Guild—new seal, reformed seal, the auditors' seal—and inside was a single sheet offering Isandro Sterling reinstatement: badge, commission, back pay, and a posting of her choosing, in recognition of services rendered in the matter of the Vess floodgates.

*Services rendered.* As if the Guild could only understand a person through the grammar of accounts.

She carried the letter down to the water and sat on the walkway with her feet above the current and read it four times. It was, she had to admit, well made. The clerk who drafted it knew his trade. Badge, commission, back pay. A purpose, pre-fabricated. A self, ready to wear. All she had to do was sign the enclosed acknowledgment and the machine that had lied to her for eleven years would take her back and call it honor.

Lira would have wanted her to take it. That was the first thought, and she let it come, because refusing to think a thought had never once stopped it. Lira, feverish and fierce in the narrow bed, saying *you're too good for the flats, Sandro, you're meant for bigger ledgers than ours.* Lira, who had died anyway, with the medicine money spent and the debt just beginning. Lira, who had never known that the debt was a lie, and who had therefore died believing her sister's ruin was her fault.

Isandro sat with that a long time. The river went by, brown and unhurried, carrying somebody else's season downstream.

Then she folded the letter along its original creases, walked to the cookfire where Bram was frying the morning's catch, and held it over the flames until the corner caught. Bram watched it burn without asking, and when the fire had taken it all, she said only:

"So. Who are you, then? With nothing owed?"

And Isandro, who had spent eleven years being what the ledger said she was, and one monsoon being what the promise required, opened her mouth to answer and found—nothing. An honest nothing. A cleared field.

"I don't know yet," she said.

"Good," said Bram, and handed her a fish. "Start with breakfast."

* * *

The dry season deepened. The flooded lowlands shrank back toward their channels, exposing mudflats ribbed like the palms of enormous hands, and the village turned to the work of the receding water: repairing weirs, cutting reeds, dragging nets across ground that had been lakebed since spring. Isandro worked alongside them, and discovered that her body—ranger's body, tracker's body, built for distance and endurance—was good for more than distance. She could haul a weir into place. She could carry a roof beam alone. Old woman Sefa, who ruled the net-sheds like a small tyrant, declared after a week that Isandro's hands were wasted on wandering and assigned her permanently to the heavy frames, and Isandro, who had taken orders from the Guild under protest for a decade, found herself obeying Sefa gladly, because Sefa's authority rested entirely on being right about rope.

She learned the village the way she'd once learned trails: by repetition, by failure, by the slow accretion of small competences. Which stilt-houses leaned in the northeast wind. Where the eels ran in October. How to read the bell tower's waterline bricks like a calendar of floods. Children stopped daring each other near her and started simply arriving, wanting to see the knife scar again, wanting stories of the gatehouse, and Isandro found she could tell those stories now without the middle of her chest closing. She told them plainly: the storm, the knives, Bram at the wheelworks with her father's survey in her teeth like a bit. She left out nothing important and invented nothing, and the children were satisfied, because children can smell a lie faster than any auditor.

Some evenings she climbed the levee and looked south, toward the flats, toward the routes her feet still knew by heart. The old restlessness would rise in her like weather—the horizon pulling, the compass-rose in her mind swinging. For eleven years movement had been the only freedom she could afford, and even now, free in fact, the old equation whispered: *go, go, before they find out, before the bill comes due.*

But there was no bill. That was the whole of it, the strange and terrible whole of it. She could go anywhere, and therefore nowhere called to her with the urgency of escape. The flats were just flats. The horizon was just the edge of the water, and on the other side of the water was more water, and somewhere in all of it was a life that belonged to no one's ledger but her own, blank as fresh paper, terrifying as fresh paper.

On one of those evenings Bram found her on the levee, climbed up, and sat close enough that their shoulders touched.

"Thinking about the road," Bram said.

"Thinking about whether I'm still the person who needs it."

"And are you?"

Isandro considered. Below them the village lamps were coming on one by one, reflected doubled in the calm water, a constellation someone had spilled. Somewhere a child was being called in to supper and ignoring it. The Verity rode at her mooring, re-bottomed, tight-seamed, riding high and honest on the falling river.

"When I was tracking," Isandro said slowly, "I always knew who I was. Ranger Sterling, debt of the Saltwater Guild, asset number whatever-it-was. It was a cage, but it was *my* cage, and I'd memorized every bar. Now the bars are gone and I keep reaching for them in the dark." She turned her hand palm-up on the planking between them, an offering, an admission. "But here's what I've noticed. When I reach, I don't find bars. I find this. The levee. The lamps. You. And it turns out a person can stand on open ground if somebody's standing on it with her."

Bram looked at her hand, then at her, with that directness that had undone Isandro from the first day at the net-racks. "That sounded almost like a decision."

"It's the closest I've come to one."

"Then let me help." Bram laced her fingers through Isandro's, rough palm to rough palm, navigator's grip, the grip of someone accustomed to holding a course through weather. "Stay through the dry season. Not because anyone asked, not because anything's owed. Because the fishing's good in the dry months and the Verity needs a second pair of hands and I—" for the first time since Isandro had known her, Bram Halloway hesitated, and Isandro understood she was being given something rare, "—I want to see what you're like when you're not running. I've only met her twice. Once in a storm. Once in a burning doorway. I'd like a longer acquaintance."

The restlessness rose in Isandro one more time, the old tide, the old pull of the empty flats—and broke, harmlessly, like water against a seawall that had finally been built.

"The dry season," she said. "Through the end of it."

"That's not a very romantic way to say yes."

"I'm not a very romantic person."

"You held a gatehouse against eight men with a knife in you."

"For practical reasons."

"Nineteen steps to the water," Bram said, grinning now, "and every one of them yours. Come on, Sterling. Supper's getting cold and the children will want the gatehouse story again, and this time you're telling them the part where I opened the floodgates, and you are going to make it sound *magnificent*, because I earned magnificent and you owe me—"

She stopped. Caught herself. And Isandro felt the word pass between them like a coin tossed in the air and deliberately not caught.

"*Nothing,*" Bram finished softly. "You owe me nothing. First time for everything."

They walked down the levee together, into the lamplight, and Isandro did not count the steps.

* * *

Far to the south, past the sandbar and the drowned orchards and the flats where a thief had once vanished into dust, the first thunderheads of the next season were already building over the sea—weeks early, the traders said, bigger than anyone remembered. In Sallowmere the old men squinted at the horizon and sucked their teeth, and the bell tower's waterline bricks waited to be read again, and the river lay low and quiet in its channel, gathering itself.

Bram stood at the window a long time that night, looking south, and Isandro, waking briefly in the dark, saw her silhouette against the stars and heard her say, half to herself, in the tone of a navigator sighting weather on the edge of the chart:

"That's early. That's *very* early."

And the thunder rolled up the valley like something answering.
