## 3. The Navy Won't Discuss It

The harbormaster's office at Greyhaven smelled of tar, old paper, and the particular staleness of a kettle that had been boiled a thousand times without ever being cleaned. Sebastian Fairweather stood in the doorway for a long moment before entering, the ledger pressed flat against their ribs beneath an oilskin that was two sizes too big—a deliberate choice, because clothes that fit invited looking, and looking was the one thing Sebastian had spent thirty-one years training the world out of.

Morning light came through salt-hazed windows in gray bars. Behind the counter, Dunc Calloway—the harbormaster's clerk, a man shaped like a barrel and about as forthcoming—was stamping mooring renewals with the mechanical rhythm of someone who had stopped thinking about his work sometime in the previous decade. He glanced up, registered Sebastian, and returned to his stamps. That was the arrangement. Sebastian Fairweather was a fixture of Greyhaven Point the way the breakwater was: useful, unremarkable, not worth a second glance.

Invisibility had always been the armor. It didn't come free—Sebastian had paid for it in small currencies for three decades. Never win big at cards. Never argue loudly. Never let your boat look better than it should. Salvage divers who stayed poor stayed safe; salvage divers who got noticed got audited, taxed, undercut, and occasionally disappeared into the kind of accidents the northern coast manufactured so efficiently. Their father had understood this too, or so Sebastian had believed until last night, when they'd sat up until four in the morning with the waterproof ledger open under a lamp, reading numbers that made no sense in any world Sebastian knew.

Wreck designation MRD-1147. Cargo classification: sealed. Loss report: total. Salvage rights: **closed by order**.

Not lapsed. Not expired. *Closed*. As if the sea itself had been told to keep its mouth shut.

"Mooring renewal?" Dunc asked without looking up.

"Actually—" Sebastian kept their voice at the register of small talk, the tone you'd use to ask about weather. "I was out past the point yesterday. Low tide like I've never seen. Got me thinking about old wrecks. There's a designation I ran across in some flotsam paperwork—MRD-1147. Ring any bells?"

The stamp came down mid-word and did not come back up.

For three full seconds Dunc Calloway's hand hung in the air above the document, and in those three seconds the office changed temperature. Sebastian felt it the way you feel a pressure drop before a storm—not anything you could point to, just the animal certainty that something had shifted. When Dunc finally lifted the stamp, he set it down beside the ink pad with exaggerated care, the way a man puts down a live thing he doesn't want to startle.

"That's not a Greyhaven number," he said.

"It came ashore near here."

"Then it came ashore wrong." Dunc's eyes came up now, and they weren't unfriendly. That was somehow worse. They were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy. "Look, Fairweather. Flotsam paperwork's not worth the salt water on it. Half of what washes up off this coast is navy surplus that fell off a truck forty years ago. You want wreck designations, there's a registry in the county archive. Costs eleven pounds to search. Go spend your eleven pounds."

"I might do that."

"Don't." The word came out flat and quick, and then Dunc seemed to hear himself, seemed to realize how much the word had cost him, and covered it with a shrug that fooled no one. "Don't bother, is what I mean. Eleven pounds for a number that won't tell you anything. The registry's mostly burned anyway. Fire back in—" He waved a hand. "Years ago. Records gone. Whole sections of it."

"What sections?"

"The ones people ask about." 

* * *

Sebastian bought the conversation a second round the only way conversations were ever bought in Greyhaven: they waited while Dunc made tea, made a show of studying the notice board—regatta dates, a lost dog, a fisheries advisory—and let the silence do its work. Silence was a diver's tool. You dropped it down and watched what rose toward it.

Dunc lasted four minutes, which was longer than most.

"You found something out there." It wasn't a question. He poured tea into two chipped mugs and pushed one across the counter, which he had never done before, and which Sebastian understood immediately as either kindness or a bribe. "At the low tide. Something in the rocks."

"I find things out there every week. Mostly rust and kelp."

"Mm." Dunc drank. His hands were steady but his jaw worked around the mug's rim like he was chewing the words before letting them out. "You know who came through here in March? Navy survey team. Two officers and a civilian fella in a coat too nice for this weather. Asked me to flag any unusual finds along the point. Any artifacts, any documents, anything that looked—" he searched for the word, found it, and delivered it with distaste, "—*provenanced*."

"And did you flag anything?"

"Nothing's been flagged in nine years of them asking." Dunc set down the mug. "Which tells you something, doesn't it. Nine years. They've been sending men up here every spring for nine years to ask about nothing. Nobody sends men to ask about nothing, Fairweather. Not twice, let alone nine times."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and gave up.

"So what do I do," Sebastian said quietly, "if I have found something?"

Dunc looked at them for a long time. And here was the thing Sebastian would remember afterward, would turn over at night in the weeks to come: Dunc wasn't frightened for himself. He was frightened *for Sebastian*, and he was deciding, visibly, painfully, how much truth a man could hand over without getting both of them drowned.

"You bring it to me," Dunc said finally. "Whatever it is. You bring it here, no copies, and you tell me you found it in general debris, and I log it, and it goes in the navy post Thursday, and that's the end of it. That's the clean way. That's the way where nobody gets hurt."

"And if I don't?"

Dunc exhaled through his nose. He came around the counter, which he also never did, and stood close enough that Sebastian could smell the tea and the tobacco and the fear underneath both, faint as iodine.

"Then I'll tell you what the officer told me in March, since it seems relevant now. He was polite. Bought my lunch. Shook my hand at the end of it. And then he said, 'Mr. Calloway, harbors are complicated ecosystems. So many moving parts. Ropes that fray. Boats whose papers go stale. Inspections that take longer than expected.' And then he smiled and said, 'But I'm sure none of that will be necessary.'" Dunc's voice had gone very quiet. "He never raised his voice once. Never threatened anything specific. And I didn't sleep right for a week. That's the thing you need to understand about these people—they don't have to say the threat. They just describe the harbor to you, and suddenly you see all the ways a man can sink in it."

* * *

Sebastian walked out of the office into a morning that looked exactly the same as every other morning and felt entirely different.

The harbor was waking in its usual order: gulls first, then the fish vans, then the slow cough of engines as the day boats went out. Old Petros was hosing down his deck. Two kids were crabbing off the inner quay with the patience of the unemployed. Everything ordinary. Everything observed, now, with the new knowledge that observation ran in both directions—that somewhere behind the ordinariness there might be eyes cataloguing it, noting which boats left when, which faces asked which questions.

Sebastian had spent a lifetime being no one. It had felt like freedom. Now, walking the length of the quay with the ledger's weight seeming to show through the oilskin like a brand, they understood for the first time that invisibility hadn't been freedom at all. It had been camouflage, and camouflage only works until something makes you move.

They'd moved.

At the end of the quay, Sebastian stopped and looked back at the point—the black shoulder of rock where yesterday the sea had pulled back farther than any chart predicted, exposing the cave mouth the old-timers called the Devil's Ledger and everyone else pretended not to name. From here it was just geology. Just stone and spray. But Sebastian knew what slept inside it now: a strongroom stamped with naval markings and a date forty years old, and a ship called—whatever MRD-1147 was called—that the registry had supposedly burned and the navy sent men every spring to make sure no one remembered.

Nine years of asking about nothing.

Forty years of a closed file.

And one diver, broke and invisible an hour ago, standing on a public quay holding the thing itself against their ribs.

Sebastian turned for home, and it was only halfway up the cliff path, heart still hammering with the particular cold that comes from being seen for the first time, that they remembered the other thing—the detail they'd noted last night and filed without understanding. In the ledger's margin, in faded pencil, in handwriting that had made their chest seize before their mind caught up:

*Coordinates matching Dad's logbook.*

The father who had died at sea. The father whose logbook sat in a drawer upstairs, waiting.

Sebastian climbed faster.
