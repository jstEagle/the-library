## 4. Father's Logbook

The cottage smelled of salt and old paper, the way it always had, the way it still did three years after my father stopped breathing into it. I sat at his desk—my desk now, though I'd never quite claimed it—and spread the ledger out under the lamp beside a cup of tea going cold.

Outside, Greyhaven Point was a black hump against a bruised sky. The tide had come back in the night like nothing had happened, erasing the cave mouth, smoothing the sea to its usual gray indifference. But I could feel it under my skin still: that room of rusted steel and naval stamps, sitting patient as a held breath beneath forty years of water.

The ledger was worse in good light. The leather cover had gone soft as skin, and the pages inside were waxed canvas, military issue, the kind meant to survive a dunking. Whoever had sealed it in that strongroom had expected it to be read someday—or never. The ink had bled but not surrendered. I turned pages slowly, wearing cotton gloves from my dive kit because I'd learned the hard way what salt water and handling did to old documents.

Most of it was cargo manifests. Crates, weights, serial numbers. Routine. The kind of paperwork that made a ship real—the way a body is made real by its inventory of bones.

Then I reached the page stamped in red, faded now to the color of an old wound:

**H.M.T. MERIDIAN — FINAL MANIFEST — CLASSIFIED — DO NOT DUPLICATE**

My tea went cold all the way through while I stared at that word. *Classified.* On a transport lost with all hands in '84. A ship whose name appeared exactly twice in the public record: once in the loss register, once on a memorial wall in Greyhaven with nineteen names cut into slate.

I knew those names. I'd grown up walking past them every Sunday on the way to the chippy. Old men touched them when they thought no one was looking.

I copied the wreck designation onto the back of my hand like a boy cheating at school—*MRD-114*—and then sat there, looking at it, feeling the first cold thread of something I couldn't yet name.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that had been itching at me since the cave, since before the cave, maybe since I was nine years old and found my father's logbook hidden behind a false panel in this very desk and he'd taken it from me gently and never explained why it was hidden at all.

The Meridian went down off Greyhaven Point.

And my father spent thirty years diving off Greyhaven Point.

* * *

I didn't go to the harbormaster that morning. Some instinct older than sense told me to sit on it, to read everything twice before I let anyone see me asking questions. So instead I pulled the loose floorboard in the corner of the front room—the one Da had always said was rot, though rot doesn't get sanded smooth on its underside—and lifted out the tin box.

His logbook lay on top, wrapped in oilcloth. Same waxed canvas as the strongroom ledger. Same navy-issue stock. That detail landed in my stomach like a dropped anchor.

I hadn't opened it since the funeral. Grief is a country you can live in without visiting; you just stop going to certain rooms. This was the deepest room in the house.

The cover read, in his square, patient handwriting: **T. FAIRWEATHER — DIVE LOG — PROPERTY OF THE OWNER AND NO ONE ELSE.** Even his possessiveness sounded kind.

I started at the beginning, because that's where he would have wanted me to start, and because I needed the long walk toward whatever waited at the end.

* * *

> *14 May, year of the big storm. Water temp 9 degrees. Vis two meters if you're optimistic, which I am, because the alternative is quitting.*
>
> *Worked the *Kestrel* wreck again. Brought up four ingots of lead and a chamber pot some admiral probably paid a fortune for. Sebastian asked me today why the sea keeps things. Told her: because we keep throwing them away. She's seven and already smarter than the harbormaster.*

Sebastian. Me. Seven years old and already written into the record of a man who wrote everything down, who trusted paper more than people, who taught me to log every dive because "memory lies, love, but ink just fades honest."

I kept turning pages. Years of them. Wrecks named and unnamed, coordinates logged to three decimals, sketches of hulls and rudders and one memorable drawing of a conger eel labeled *BASTARD (DO NOT TRUST)*. His whole working life in fading ink. And I was halfway through, reading for love, not looking for anything—

—when the Meridian surfaced.

Not the ship. The name. In his handwriting, dated eleven years after the official sinking:

> *3 Sept. Dove the point at slack water. Found more than I was looking for.*
>
> *There's a cave below the low-tide line that isn't on any chart. Locals call it the Devil's Ledger. I can see why. Inside, past the fall-in, there's worked stone. Steel. A door with numbers on it.*
>
> *I know the numbers. Everyone in Greyhaven over fifty knows the numbers. MRD-114.*
>
> *The Meridian was listed lost with all hands, no cargo. I've read the register a hundred times. But I'm looking at a naval strongroom bolted into rock that shouldn't hold it, and strongrooms don't travel empty.*
>
> *I'm going back down tomorrow with better light.*

I read it three times. Then I set the book down flat on the desk, very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and I looked out the window at the point for a long time.

He'd found it. Thirty-odd years ago, my father had found the same cave I'd stumbled into yesterday, exposed by the same impossible tide, and he'd stood where I stood, in this room, holding the same knowledge—and he'd put it behind a false panel and said nothing.

Or so I believed, right up until I turned the next page.

* * *

> *4 Sept. Went back. The door won't open—not for crowbar, not for patience. There's a lock mechanism I don't recognize, brass, naval pattern. Someone built this to keep people like me out, which means someone knew people like me would come.*
>
> *Here's what troubles me. The tide tables say the cave floods at half-meter below chart datum. Today it stayed dry at full low water. Either the charts are wrong or the seabed has shifted. I've logged both possibilities. What I haven't logged—I'm writing this part separate, and I'll keep it separate—is the third possibility.*
>
> *Someone moved the rocks.*

I sat very still. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up.

*Someone moved the rocks.* My father, who logged everything, who trusted ink because ink fades honest—he'd started keeping secrets *inside* his own logbook, splitting the truth across pages, hiding the dangerous parts even from himself. That wasn't caution. That was fear. I'd seen fear write like that once before, in a fisherman's notebook recovered after the *Maiden's Loss*, entries getting shorter and stranger until they stopped altogether.

Da's entries didn't stop. They changed.

> *11 Sept. Asked old Colm about the Meridian tonight, casual-like, over pints. He went quiet in a way I've only seen quiet go once before—at a funeral. Said: "That ship never sank where they say she sank, Tam." Then he bought me a whiskey I didn't ask for and told me to leave the point alone.*
>
> *Colm's been dead six years. Before he died he told his daughter the same thing he told me, and she told me last week that her father took payments for years. Envelope money. From men who never gave names.*
>
> *Envelope money for saying nothing about a shipwreck. In Greyhaven. Where half the town lost somebody on that ship and the other half buried them.*
>
> *I'm a salvage man. I don't believe in coincidence. I believe in current, and current runs somewhere.*

* * *

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the lane a dog was complaining about the weather. Ordinary sounds, ordinary night—and I was reading my dead father describe being paid off by ghosts.

I turned the pages faster now, hunting. The Meridian came up again and again through the following year, always in fragments, always circling:

> *19 Oct. Dove the outer shelf. Found plating. Naval gauge, riveted, painted gray over black. Gray over black. You paint a ship gray to hide her. You paint her black underneath when you don't care who sees the lie.*

> *2 Nov. Found a boot. Leather, service issue, size ten. It was wedged under the plating in a way boots don't wedge naturally. Arranged. I keep using that word and I hate it.*

> *23 Dec. Christmas Eve and I'm writing this by lamplight like a madman. Met a woman on the cliff path today. Older, well-dressed, wrong coat for the weather. She asked if I was the diver who'd been working the point. I said lots of us work the point. She said: "Not like you." Then she gave me a card with no name on it, just a number, and walked on.*
>
> *Sebastian turns eight tomorrow. I burned the card.*

Burned the card. Kept the knowledge. And then—this was the part that made my hands unsteady on the pages—then he did the most my-father thing imaginable.

He went back down. For years. Quietly, between paying jobs, logging the dives in plain language and the findings in a shorthand I had to work to crack: *the ledger room,* he called it. Not the Devil's Ledger. *The ledger room.* As if it were a place you kept accounts. As if the sea were owed something and the room was where the debt was recorded.

> *June, third year. Got the inner door open. Won't say how in these pages. What's inside changes things. Not gold—there's gold, more than I'll ever touch—but that's not what matters. There are papers. Lists. Names.*
>
> *Names of people who weren't supposed to be aboard a cargo run. And dates that don't match the official story anywhere.*
>
> *I took one document. One. Left the rest sealed, because taking more means becoming the kind of man they send men in wrong coats to find.*
>
> *If anything happens to me, this log is for Sebastian. She'll understand. She's the only one who ever asked the right question: why does the sea keep things?*
>
> *Answer: because we keep throwing them away. And sometimes because someone threw them there on purpose.*

* * *

I closed the logbook.

The fire had burned down to embers. My tea was cold sludge. I realized I'd been sitting in the dark for some time, reading by the last of the lamp, and that the house around me felt different—not emptier, but *fuller*, haunted in the opposite direction. Not by a ghost. By a man who had been more awake than I'd ever known.

One document. He'd taken one document and left the rest. Which meant—

I got up so fast the chair went over, and I dug through the tin box with hands that wouldn't behave, past the logbook, past the spare regulator parts and the photograph of my mother laughing on the quay, down to the bottom, where a manila envelope sat fat and yellowed, sealed with tape gone brittle as sugar glass.

Inside: a single sheet of waxed canvas, folded in thirds. Navy stamp. Red classification mark faded to a wound-color.

A copy of a passenger manifest. **H.M.T. MERIDIAN.**

Nineteen names cut into the memorial slate in town. But this list ran to thirty-one.

Twelve names that had never been carved anywhere. Twelve people the sea officially never carried. And along the margin, in my father's handwriting—small, precise, dated *after* the date the navy said the ship went down, dated *after*, impossibly, a note I had to read four times before the words would hold still:

*Verified against survivor account. One lived. Find her before they do.*

Below that, a single line of coordinates, and beside the coordinates, in letters pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the canvas, a name:

*M. ODUYA — LIGHTHOUSE ROAD.*

I stood in my father's house holding a list of the erased, and outside the window the tide was turning, and somewhere on Lighthouse Road, if she was still alive, if fifty years hadn't taken her the way the sea takes everything—a witness was waiting for someone to come asking.

The spring tides were coming. Six windows, maybe less. And I finally understood what my father had left me: not treasure. Not a map.

A debt.
