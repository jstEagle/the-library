## 5. Six Tides

The fleet came in on the morning tide, three hulls deep, riding low with the arrogance of money.

Sebastian heard them before seeing them—the deep-throated churn of diesel engines built for tonnage, not the sputtering outboards Greyhaven Point was used to. From the deck of the *Maggie's Grace*, still tied up at the quay with a fuel line that needed replacing and a bilge pump that had opinions, Sebastian watched the first vessel round the breakwater: a converted naval survey ship, gunmetal grey, its superstructure bristling with antennae like the spines of an angry fish. The name on her bow was painted in clean white letters that hadn't yet learned to flake.

*Vantage.*

Behind her came two work barges, cranes folded against their decks like sleeping herons, and a fast tender cutting white water in their wake. Men moved along the rails with the unhurried efficiency of people who were paid whether they found anything or not.

Sebastian's hands went still on the fuel fitting.

Everyone knew the name Corven Ashe. Everyone who worked water for a living, anyway. His salvage company had pulled wrecks out of every hostile sea from the Hebrides to the Coral Triangle—cargo ships, war graves, things governments preferred stayed down. There were stories. There were always stories about men like Ashe, and half of them were even true. The one Sebastian liked least was the oldest: that Ashe never salvaged by accident. That he found what he went looking for, always, and that what he did with the people who got in his way was less a question than a rumor you didn't repeat twice.

"Meridian Fleet," old Hollis said at Sebastian's elbow, spitting tobacco into the grey water. He'd come up the quay without being noticed, which was how Hollis did everything. "That's the big one. Vantage. Her two tenders. Sonar suite on the main mast you wouldn't believe."

"I see them."

"You don't." Hollis chewed a while. "I seen her work off Skerrin two years back. She don't sweep like other boats sweep. She *reads*. Like a hand moving over skin, feeling for something under it." He spat again. "Question is what she's reading for here."

Sebastian said nothing. The ledger was in a waterproof bag inside the cabin of the *Maggie's Grace*, tucked beneath the floor panel where the spare anchor rode lived, and it felt suddenly very heavy and very far away.

"They'll want berthing," Hollis went on. "Harbormaster will fall over himself giving it to them. Money like that talks, and our harbormaster's ears are for sale cheap." He glanced sideways, watery eyes narrowing. "You've been quiet lately, Fairweather. Quiet and diving alone at odd tides. I'd think about that, if I were you. Men like Ashe notice quiet."

He shuffled off before Sebastian could answer, which was fine, because there was no answer that wasn't a lie.

* * *

By noon the harbor had transformed. The *Vantage* took the deep-water berth nearest the point, and her tenders rafted alongside, and suddenly Greyhaven's little quay—all weathered creosote and lobster pots and rusted cleats—looked like a child's drawing beside a photograph. Crews in matching oilskins unloaded cases of equipment. A crane lifted something long and shrouded onto the survey ship's aft deck. And everywhere, threaded through the ordinary noise of the harbor, was a new sound: the high electronic whine of sonar transducers warming up.

Sebastian sat in the cabin of the *Maggie's Grace* with the ledger open on the chart table and the father's logbook beside it, and did arithmetic.

The ledger gave coordinates—faint, salt-blurred, but legible where it mattered. The wreck designation. The grid reference off Greyhaven Point. And now, cross-referenced against the father's logbook entries from thirty-odd years ago, the same numbers appearing in a different hand, in different ink, circled twice.

Two documents. Two generations. One patch of black rock under cold green water.

And above that rock, somewhere in the harbor behind Sebastian's back, a man named Corven Ashe was switching on machines designed to find exactly that.

Sebastian spread the tide tables flat and weighted the corners with a spanner and a coffee mug.

Spring tides. That was the whole game, and everyone who'd ever worked these waters knew it. The Devil's Ledger cave only opened when the sea pulled back past all reason—those freak lows that came with the spring series, when sun and moon lined up and dragged the ocean out like a sheet being drawn off a bed. Between springs, the cave mouth stayed drowned, sealed behind ten feet of water and current that could fold a diver in half. In another month, the equinoctial pattern would shift entirely, and the cave would close for the season. Not for weeks. For *months*.

Sebastian ran a pencil down the columns.

Today was the second day of the spring series. The great low that had exposed the cave—that once-in-a-decade drawdown—had been yesterday. Today's low would be lower than normal but not as low as yesterday's. Then the sequence would decay, each successive tide a little weaker, each window into the cave a little narrower, until the seventh day when the range collapsed and the sea closed its account until autumn.

Six windows. Six chances.

Sebastian marked them in red pencil, one through six, times and heights, and stared at the numbers until they stopped looking like numbers and started looking like a countdown.

The problem wasn't just the tides. It was what each window demanded. The antechamber was reachable from the second window onward—but the strongroom door, the inner passage, whatever lay deeper, those would need dives in worsening light, worsening current, worsening margin. The father's logbook had been explicit about that much, in the terse shorthand of a man who wrote for himself: *Inner passage only on windows 3–5. After that the surge comes through the blowhole wrong. Do not be inside when it does.*

*Do not be inside when it does.* Thirty years dead and the old man was still giving orders.

And now there was a third variable, one the father had never had to factor: a fleet with sonar reading the seabed like a palm, and a man running it who didn't leave loose ends.

Sebastian looked up through the cabin's salt-hazed window. Out past the breakwater, one of the *Vantage*'s tenders was already working—a slow, patient circuit between the point and the shoal, trailing its sensor fish below the surface like a lure. Circling. The way a shark circles isn't hunting, exactly. It's *learning*.

They weren't here for the Meridian. They couldn't be—no one outside a navy vault was supposed to know that designation existed. But Ashe's kind didn't need to know what they were looking for. They only needed to know that someone small and poor had been asking questions in the wrong office, and that the wrong office had made phone calls afterward. Sebastian had watched the harbormaster's face during that conversation. Whatever he'd passed along, he'd passed it up the chain, and chains have links, and links have prices.

Dread settled into Sebastian's chest, cold and specific, the way it does when you realize the race has already started and you were stretching at the starting line.

But dread, Sebastian had learned a long time ago on a bad night in a sinking boat, is just momentum that hasn't picked a direction yet.

So Sebastian picked one.

* * *

The plan assembled itself over the afternoon, piece by piece, the way salvage plans do—not designed but accumulated, each element scavenged from what was available.

Window three was tomorrow's dawn tide, and it was the critical one. Windows one and two were spent—one lost to discovery, one to today, wasted watching the harbor fill with money. That left six minus two, and the deepest objectives would eat four of them if everything went right, which nothing ever did. No room for a failed dive. No room for equipment trouble. No room, above all, for Ashe.

Which meant the *Vantage* had to be looking somewhere else tomorrow at dawn.

Sebastian spent an hour with the charts, and the answer was almost embarrassingly simple. Three miles north of Greyhaven Point lay the *Corriedale*, a coaster that went down in '71 with a load of scrap copper—worthless, well-known, dived-out decades ago. But her hull made a beautiful sonar return, and more importantly, she sat directly in the acoustic shadow of the point. Any survey work around the *Corriedale* would take hours, and hours was what Sebastian needed.

A decoy. But decoys require the hunter to believe the prey is stupid, and Corven Ashe, by every account, did not hunt stupid prey.

Unless the prey gave him something to believe.

Sebastian turned that over for a long time, watching the light fail through the cabin window, listening to the generators of the *Vantage* humming across the water like a hive. Then, deliberately, Sebastian climbed out, walked the length of the quay in the blue dusk, and made sure to be seen—visibly, clumsily, drinking one beer too many at the Anchor's Reach, complaining loudly to anyone within earshot about the *Corriedale*'s copper. Scrap value was up. Old wrecks were worth a second look. A man could retire on a hull full of metal if he got there before the fleet did.

It was bad acting. Sebastian knew it was bad acting. But bad acting aimed at a clever man works differently than good acting aimed at a fool—a clever man sees the performance and reads it as a small player trying to hide a small score, and dismisses it, because dismissing it flatters him. That was the bet, anyway. It was the only bet on the table.

Halfway through the third beer, Sebastian felt the weight of being watched and looked up.

A man stood at the end of the bar. Big through the shoulders, silver at the temples, wearing a coat too good for Greyhaven and wearing it like he didn't know it was too good—or like he wanted everyone to know. He was nursing nothing. His glass was dry and had been for some time. He was simply standing there, watching Sebastian with the mild professional interest of a man appraising a horse.

When their eyes met, he smiled. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse.

Then he touched two fingers to his brow—a little salute, courteous as a knife laid flat on a table—and left.

Sebastian sat very still and finished the beer, which had gone warm and tasted like tin.

That night, back aboard the *Maggie's Grace*, Sebastian checked the ledger bag twice, checked the floor panel, checked the lines on the boat, and finally lay down in the bunk without undressing, the father's logbook open on his chest, rising and falling with his breath.

Outside, the sonar pings came through the hull in slow, patient pulses—so faint they were felt more than heard, a heartbeat in the rock beneath the harbor, counting something down.

Sebastian pressed a fingertip to the red-pencil marks on the tide table one last time in the dark.

Six windows. Five and a half, now.

And somewhere out in that black water, the sea was already deciding who it would give back.
