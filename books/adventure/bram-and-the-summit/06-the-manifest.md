## 6. The Manifest

The rain came in off the channel around dusk, the kind of thin, needling rain that made Greyhaven's windows weep. Sebastian Fairweather sat at the kitchen table with every lamp in the house burning—three of them, a extravagance they'd have flinched at a month ago—and spread the salvage of the Devil's Ledger out on oilcloth like a surgeon laying out instruments.

The strongroom had given up more than the ledger. There had been the brass plate stamped with the admiralty fouled anchor, pried loose and now sitting in a bowl of vinegar to eat away forty years of corrosion. A canvas pouch gone to leather, its contents fused into a single brick of rot that Sebastian hadn't yet dared open. And the ledger itself—waterproofed in waxed canvas, thank God for navy paranoia—its pages swollen but legible, smelling of salt and something older, something like a church.

They had spent two days not opening it.

That was the honest truth of it, and Sebastian knew it. Two days of legitimate work—patching the *Kestrel*'s hull paint, selling the season's last copper ingots to a dealer in Portmellon who paid low and counted slow—while the ledger sat wrapped in a towel beneath the floorboard under the bed, where their father used to hide the money for the harbor dues. Two days of telling themselves there was no hurry. The tide would bring six more windows. The cave wasn't going anywhere.

But the fear was going somewhere. It had started as a cold drop behind the sternum at the harbormaster's office and had been spreading ever since, slow as bilge water rising, until Sebastian could feel it sloshing at the edges of everything. Mr. Calloway's face when he'd seen the wreck designation. That pause before he said *forget the number*—the pause of a man choosing his words the way you'd choose footing on scree.

So tonight, with the rain sealing the house against the world, Sebastian unwrapped the ledger and began.

* * *

The first third was inventory, dry as ballast. Cargo manifests for a transport designated **MERIDIAN — HULL 7734**, loading at a naval yard whose name had been inked over so thoroughly the paper had thinned beneath it. Crates of machinery parts. Medical stores. Rations by the ton. And then, listed without comment between the tinned beef and the typewriters:

**Bullion, non-standard issue: 214 cases, sealed. Weight certified at loading. Destination: per separate order.**

Sebastian read the line four times. Two hundred and fourteen cases. Even allowing for the navy's talent for inflating paperwork, that was a fortune beyond anything Greyhaven Point had ever dreamed of, sunk in forty fathoms off a coast where men broke their backs for crab pots and engine scrap. The Meridian wasn't a tragedy. It was a bank vault someone had dropped into the sea.

And no one had come back for it in forty years.

That was the part that kept snagging, like a hook in a net. Salvage fleets had crawled over this coast since before Sebastian was born. Sonar, magnetometers, divers with more courage than sense. If the Meridian's position were known, it would have been stripped to the rivets decades ago. Which meant the position was *not* known—which meant the loss of it was either incompetence on a scale the navy would never admit, or intention.

Sebastian turned the page and stopped breathing.

It wasn't cargo. The heading read **SUPPLEMENTARY MANIFEST — PERSONS TRANSPORTED**, and below it, in the same clerk's careful hand, a list of names. Not crew—the crew lists came later, thirty-one men, ranks and ratings. These names had no ranks. Just names, ages, and a single column headed **STATUS**, in which every entry read the same word:

*Witness.*

There were eleven of them. Sebastian read them aloud to the empty kitchen, because saying them made them real in a way silence didn't.

*Aldous Wren, 44. Ilse Marchetti, 31. Tomas Oduya, 38.* Sebastian's finger stopped on that one—a flicker, an itch of recognition, gone before it could be caught. *Priya Nandakumar, 27. Edvard Lund, 52...*

Eleven witnesses, being transported on a bullion ship, in wartime, under a manifest filed separately from every other document aboard. Sebastian had grown up on salvage law and maritime custom the way other children grew up on nursery rhymes, and everything about this entry was wrong. Witnesses weren't cargo. Witnesses traveled on passenger ferries and troop transports with proper berths and lifeboat assignments. Witnesses did not get folded into a sealed manifest on a ship whose destination was *per separate order*.

Unless the witnesses themselves were the secret.

Sebastian sat back. The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere down the hill, the harbor bell buoy clanked its idiot rhythm, patient as ever.

Think it through, their father used to say. The sea lies, but paper mostly doesn't. Paper is written by people who expect to be believed.

If you had people who had seen something the government needed buried—something bad enough that the word *witness* followed each of them like a brand—you wouldn't put them on a public ferry. You'd move them quietly. And if the thing they'd seen was bad enough, you might want them somewhere they could never testify from.

The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way icebergs arrive: most of it already underwater.

Sebastian looked at the column again. Eleven names. And then, at the bottom of the page, a twelfth entry—not typed. Handwritten, in ink that had feathered with damp but remained perfectly legible, added after the fact by someone with access to this sealed ledger and the authority to amend it:

**12. Passenger transferred aboard at last port. No papers. Manifest amended by order.**

No name. Just that. A ghost slipped aboard a ghost ship.

* * *

Sebastian worked until midnight, and it was near the end—when the eyes burned and the tea had gone cold twice—that the handwriting changed.

Page forty-one. A maintenance log, routine entries about condenser scaling and a jammed cargo winch, all in the clerk's neat hand. But in the lower margin, squeezed small, someone had penciled a note. Pencil survived water better than ink; whoever wrote it had known the sea, or feared it.

The note read: *Tide tables wrong by 11 min. Someone wants us late.*

And Sebastian's heart did something complicated and painful, because they knew that handwriting.

Not remembered it—knew it, the way you know your own reflection. The cramped, forward-leaning script with the crossed sevens and the way the letter *t* ended in a little upward flick, like a wave breaking. Their father had written supply lists in that hand, dive plans in that hand, the margins of his own logbook crowded with notes in exactly that pencil, exactly that pressure. Sebastian had spent twenty years reading those margins. They had learned letters from them.

Their father's handwriting was in the Meridian's ledger.

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a deck tilts. Sebastian gripped the table's edge and made themselves look again, checking the way you check a bearing twice before committing the boat. Crossed sevens. The flick on the *t*. The habit of writing numbers above words—*11 min* with the numeral repeated over *min*, a habit their father had drilled into them for dive logs: *never trust a number written once.*

He had been aboard. Or he had held this ledger in his hands, after. Either answer rearranged everything Sebastian knew about their own history.

Because here was the thing—the thing that had been true since Sebastian was nine years old and a harbormaster's runner had come up the path with his hat in his hands: their father had died at sea eleven years ago, lost off a wreck job south of the point, body never recovered, logbook recovered instead, dried and boxed and handed over like a consolation prize. Sebastian had built a life on top of that grief the way you build a house on a filled-in well. You know what's underneath. You don't dig.

And now the well was talking.

Sebastian went to the bedroom, knelt, and pulled up the floorboard—not for the ledger's hiding place but for what else lived there: their father's logbook, the original, the one Sebastian had read a hundred times and understood perhaps a tenth of. They carried it back to the kitchen and laid it beside the naval ledger, open, side by side under the strongest lamp.

For a long moment they just looked. Then they began to match.

The pencil in the Meridian's margin matched the pencil in the logbook's margins. More than matched—it *continued*. In the logbook, dated three weeks after the Meridian went down, their father had written: *Checked the tide tables against the yard records. The error was deliberate. Eleven minutes is enough to miss the window. Ask why the Meridian sailed into a window she couldn't make.*

Ask why. He'd been asking for years, apparently. Sebastian turned pages with increasing speed, seeing for the first time what had always been there, hidden in plain sight among the dive depths and air calculations: fragments. *Hull 7734 — cargo list doesn't add up.* *Met a man in Portmellon who signed her manifest. He wouldn't talk. He was afraid of the wrong thing—not the sea.* *Dived the outer shelf again. Nothing. She's not where the record says she sank. Records lie. Tide tables lie. Find the woman who keeps the light.*

Find the woman who keeps the light.

Sebastian read the sentence five times. There was only one light on this stretch of coast old enough—Greyhaven Light, automated now, but manned for sixty years before that. Their father had been here, in these waters, hunting this same ship, years before Sebastian found a cave the sea had hidden. He had gotten close. Closer than close—he'd held the ledger, or a copy of it, and he'd written in it.

And then he'd stopped writing about it. The last mention was dated eight months before his death. After that, the logbook returned to ordinary work, ordinary dives—but with a new habit, one Sebastian had never questioned: a series of dives along the base of Greyhaven Point, logged simply as *bottom survey, no findings,* over and over, season after season, right up until the final month.

He hadn't stopped looking. He'd stopped *writing it down*. He'd spent his last years searching the Devil's Ledger's waters in total silence, knowing somehow that paper could be read by the wrong eyes.

And he'd found it—or the sea had finally shown him—and whatever happened in his last week, it happened within swimming distance of the cave Sebastian had walked into four days ago.

Sebastian became aware, gradually, that their face was wet. They let it be. The kitchen was dark except for the lamp pool, and grief in the dark is private even when you're the only one watching.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when they went back to the manifest with new eyes, and found what exhaustion had hidden.

The twelfth entry. *Passenger transferred aboard at last port. No papers.*

Handwritten. Added later. And Sebastian, who had spent the evening learning their father's hand inside the navy's, now looked at *that* hand—the one that had amended the manifest—and saw it was different. Older style. A clerk's training underneath, but hurried, and pressed hard enough to emboss the next page.

Below the entry, almost invisible unless you tilted the page against the lamplight, was a pencil ghost: something written and erased, the graphite grooves still scored into the paper. Sebastian fetched the lamp close, angled the light low across the page the way their father had taught them to find tooling marks on recovered brass, and read the shadow of the erased words one groove at a time.

Three words survived clearly enough:

*...alive when loaded.*

Sebastian set the ledger down very carefully, as though it might go off.

Eleven witnesses. A ship sailing into a tide window she couldn't make, sabotaged by eleven minutes. A hold full of gold to make certain no one would ever stop looking for the wreck—or perhaps to make certain no one would ever ask what else was aboard. And a manifest amended by a hand that had needed to write, then couldn't bear to leave standing, the truth: that the passengers were alive when loaded.

This wasn't treasure. Sebastian had spent four days thinking in terms of salvage—cases, weight, finder's percentages, the debt-shaped hole of their bank account. That arithmetic was dead now, sunk somewhere off the point. What lay in the Devil's Ledger was testimony. Eleven names that someone had paid in gold and seawater to erase, and a twelfth name erased harder than all the rest.

And their father had died holding the edge of it.

Sebastian stood at the window a long time, watching the rain march down the hill toward the black harbor. The buoy clanked. The tide, unseen in the dark, was already turning, beginning its patient count toward the next window. Five openings left after tomorrow. Five chances to finish what a dead man had started in pencil.

Then they saw it—the thing that snapped the night taut as a wire. Down at the harbor mouth, where the water opened to the channel, lights were moving. Not fishing lights, strung and wandering. Running lights, white over white, riding high and fast, three vessels standing in past the point in weather no working skipper would choose, in formation, like something arriving on purpose.

A salvage fleet didn't run at night in rain unless it wanted to be in harbor before anyone could ask questions.

Sebastian watched the lights swing toward the quay and felt the race change shape. It was no longer about beating the tide. Someone else had done the same arithmetic they had—someone with sonar and money and, very possibly, the same official silence that had made Mr. Calloway sweat.

The lead vessel's searchlight swept once across the harbor water, casual as a lighthouse beam, and caught the *Kestrel* at her mooring, held there a half-second too long, and moved on.

Sebastian closed both books, slid them under the floorboard, and began, quietly, to pack a dive bag by feel.

Tomorrow the tide would open the cave. Tomorrow, apparently, so would everything else.
