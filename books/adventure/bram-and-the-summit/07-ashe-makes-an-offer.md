## 7. Ashe Makes an Offer

Corven Ashe had a theory about harbors: every one of them was the same town wearing different clothes. There was always a bar with a warped floor and a landlord who watered the whiskey, always a quay boss who could be bought by the crate, and always—always—one lean, hungry diver who'd found something they shouldn't have and hadn't yet figured out what to do with it.

Greyhaven Point's version of that last creature was named Fairweather, and Ashe had been watching them for two days without once letting his eyes rest long enough to be caught.

He sat in the back booth of the Salt & Anchor with a view of the door, the harbor, and the corner table where Sebastian Fairweather sat nursing a single beer like it was a wage. That was the tell, if you knew how to read it. A diver who'd struck nothing drank to forget. A diver who'd struck something drank to think—and thinking divers ordered cheap, because every coin might soon matter more than comfort. Fairweather's boat had been at its mooring for three days running during working tides. Nobody in this harbor idled through spring tides unless they were waiting on something, or hiding something, or both.

Ashe lifted two fingers toward the barman. "Another round for the house," he said, loud enough to carry, "on me."

The Salt & Anchor turned as one animal. He let them look. He was good-looking in the way that money makes men good-looking—silver at the temples kept short, a jaw that photographs well, hands soft except for the old white scar across the left knuckles that he never explained and never needed to. His fleet rode at anchor out past the point: three survey vessels, a dive tender, and the *Merrow's Debt*, his command, black-hulled and hungry. The whole harbor had spent the morning speculating about what brought Corven Ashe to a dying fishing port on the wrong side of nowhere.

Let them speculate. Speculation was free advertising.

"Mr. Ashe." The barman set down the tray, sweating gratitude. "That's generous of you."

"Generous is a strong word." Ashe smiled. "I'm buying goodwill. It's cheaper than gold and spends better in small towns."

The room laughed because he wanted it to. He watched Fairweather over the rim of his glass—not laughing, not drinking the free round either. Just watching him back, level and unblinking across forty feet of sawdust floor, the way one dog watches another across a yard.

*There you are,* Ashe thought, with something close to pleasure.

* * *

He'd made his living, thirty years of it, off other people's secrets. Wrecks were only ever half the business; the other half was paper. Cargo manifests, insurance claims, admiralty filings, salvage rights—the sea kept its dead, but governments kept their paperwork, and paperwork was where fortunes went to be buried alive. Ashe had learned early that the man who owned the documents owned the dive site, no matter whose flag flew over it. He'd taken sites out from under national navies. He'd taken them out from under families. Once, memorably, out from under a museum, though that story ended with lawyers and he preferred not to remember the lawyers.

His sonar sweeps had spent two days circling Greyhaven Point and come back with garbage. That was the interesting part. The seabed there read clean—too clean, a smoothness that didn't occur in nature, as though something between the transducers and the rock was eating the returns. Scattering layers did that. So did certain kinds of construction. So did naval countermeasures, and naval countermeasures meant naval interest, and naval interest meant value.

And then there was Fairweather's ledger.

Ashe's people were thorough. The harbormaster's clerk had a gambling problem and a memory that could be refreshed for fifty pounds, and what she remembered was this: three days ago, Sebastian Fairweather had come in asking after a wreck designation. Had written it down. Had gone pale when the harbormaster closed the office door behind them.

A wreck designation. At Greyhaven Point. During the lowest tide in forty years.

Ashe turned his glass slowly on the table, watching the lamplight swim in it. He did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was a word poor people used to explain rich people's timing.

"Mind if I sit?"

He was already sliding into the corner seat opposite Fairweather before the question finished, which was deliberate. Ask permission and you gave them time to build walls. Take the seat and you started the conversation inside them.

Fairweather didn't flinch. That was the first mark in their favor. Most people, finding Corven Ashe suddenly across the table, performed some small theater of surprise or flattery. Fairweather just looked at him with those grey eyes, sea-colored and patient, and said, "You bought the whole bar a drink. I assume this is the part where I find out why."

"I'm told you're the best diver on this coast."

"You were misinformed."

"I'm told you're the most stubborn, then." Ashe spread his hands, easy, self-deprecating. "Which around here amounts to the same thing."

"The drinks are a bribe, then. For what?"

"For information." He said it plainly, because Fairweather plainly wasn't a person you charmed—you circled them. "My fleet is working the shelf north of here. Deep salvage. And my surveys keep coming back strange near your point. Strange costs me money. Money makes me curious. Curiosity, Mr.—Ms.—"

"Fairweather works fine."

"—curiosity, Fairweather, makes me generous." He slid a card across the table. Heavy stock, embossed, nothing on it but a satellite number. "If you hear anything about the seabed out there—old wrecks, obstructions, anything the charts missed—I pay finder's fees. Substantial ones. And not just for steel." He held the grey eyes. "Paperwork too. Old documents wash up along this coast all the time. Logs, manifests, letters in bottles. Collectors pay for them. I pay better."

Something moved behind Fairweather's expression—small, controlled, gone in half a second. But Ashe had spent thirty years reading faces in bad light, and he caught it: not guilt exactly. Recognition. The flicker of a card player who has just heard another player name the game.

"No paperwork washing up around here," Fairweather said. "Unless you count the fish market receipts."

"None at all?"

"None at all."

"And the tide?" Ashe leaned back, gesturing lazily toward the dark window and the harbor beyond, where the water lay pulled back off the rocks like a sheet drawn off a body. "Lowest water anyone's seen in a generation. You didn't go have a look?"

"I work for a living. I don't beachcomb."

"Mm." Ashe smiled and let the silence stretch, because silences were tools and he kept his well-oiled. "You know what I admire about you, Fairweather? You lie like someone who's practiced. Most people, when they lie to me, they talk too much. Fill the space. You give me four words and let me sit in them." He tapped the table once. "It's almost professional."

"Almost?"

"I'd have given you five."

For the first time, something that was nearly a smile touched Fairweather's mouth. "What is it you think I found, Mr. Ashe?"

"Nothing. I think you found nothing, and it's keeping you up at night, and you're drinking one beer very slowly while you decide what to do about it." Ashe stood, buttoning his jacket, unhurried. "Here's the arithmetic, since you strike me as someone who respects arithmetic. Whatever's under that point, it took the sea forty years to offer it up, and it'll take the sea six tides to take it back. My fleet has the equipment to take it sooner. If we find it first, we find it with drills and charges, and whatever history is down there comes up in pieces—if it comes up at all. If you come to me first—" he tapped the card, still lying on the table between them, "—it comes up whole, and you get paid like a partner instead of treated like an obstacle. I'd think carefully about which of those futures you prefer."

"And if I just want to be left alone?"

Ashe paused at the edge of the booth. Around them the bar had gone quiet in that particular way bars go quiet when everyone is listening while pretending not to.

"Nobody wants to be left alone," he said gently. "They want to be safe. It's different." He smiled his photograph smile. "Enjoy the drinks."

* * *

Outside, the night smelled of salt and diesel and coming weather. Ashe walked the quay slowly, hands clasped behind his back, letting the harbor see him seeing it. His operations manager, a slab-faced ex-navy man called Brannoc, fell into step beside him.

"Well?"

"They've got it." Ashe watched a gull argue with a crab pot on the pilings. "Or they know where it is. Either way, they lied to me twice, cleanly, and they didn't touch the card."

"That's a no, then."

"That's a *not yet*. There's a difference, and the difference is six tides." He stopped at the rail. Below, the tide was turning already, the black rocks of Greyhaven Point going under inch by inch, patient as a debt collector. "Get me everything on Fairweather. Father, mother, debts, mooring fees, who holds the note on that rust-bucket they call a boat. And quietly—no heavy hands. Not yet."

"Heavy hands are faster."

"Heavy hands are louder." Ashe turned his collar up against the wind. Somewhere out past the point, lightning walked along the horizon without sound, counting off the storm that was three days out. "This one's clever, Brannoc. Clever ones don't run when you shout. They dig in. You don't dig clever people out—you make the ground they're standing on worth leaving."

"And if the ground doesn't move?"

Ashe looked out at the darkening water, at the place where the charts went smooth and lying, and felt the old cold pleasure settle in his chest, the one that had carried him through thirty years of drowned ships and drowned men.

"Then we move the ground," he said. "There's always blasting powder. There's always a next tide. And there is always—" he smiled at the black water, "—always a price somebody else can pay."

Behind him, in the Salt & Anchor's lit window, he saw Fairweather finally pick up the card from the table, turn it over once, and slip it away into a coat pocket rather than leave it on the wood.

Ashe's smile widened in the dark.

Good. They'd kept it. Which meant they were already deciding how much their silence was worth—and nobody, in all his years, had ever guessed that number high enough.
