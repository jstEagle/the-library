## 8. Dive One: The Antechamber

The tide window opened at 4:17 in the morning, and Sebastian was in the water by 4:20.

They had chosen the dark hours deliberately. Greyhaven Point was a working coast—kelp boats out at first light, the fishery tender making its rounds, and now, worse, the distant geometry of the Corven Ashe fleet anchored two miles east, its survey lights burning like a string of dropped stars. Daylight meant witnesses. Witnesses meant questions. And questions were the one thing Sebastian could no longer afford.

So they went down in the black.

The entry was the easy part. The cave mouth that the low tide had exposed still lay half-drowned even at slack water, a jagged slot in the basalt that swallowed the swell and gave nothing back. Sebastian had spent two days studying it from the cliff path with binoculars, timing the surge, watching the way the sea breathed into the rock—four seconds of pull, seven of push, a rhythm like something sleeping. The old fishermen called the place the Devil's Ledger because, they said, the sea kept accounts there. Sebastian had laughed at that once, years ago, from the deck of their father's boat.

They weren't laughing now.

*Twenty meters to the antechamber,* read the note in their own handwriting, waterproof ink on a slate strapped to their wrist. *Then the passage. Then whatever's behind it.*

Their father's logbook lay open on the boat above, weighted under the anchor winch, its pages protected by three layers of plastic. The coordinates in the margin were precise in a way that had unsettled Sebastian more than anything else—the handwriting of a man who had been here before, who had measured this place, who had written *inner passage floods at +1.4m, do not linger past slack* in the same steady script he'd used for grocery lists and birthday cards. A man planning dives. A man planning *this* dive.

Sebastian spat into their mask, rinsed it, and pulled it down over their face. The cold hit the skin around their eyes like a slap. Northern water in early spring was a punishment you accepted, not a thing you braved.

"Okay," they said aloud, and the word came out small and fogged inside the mask. "Okay."

They rolled backward off the gunwale.

* * *

The surface layer was green-gray and restless, kelp swaying in long slow whips thirty feet below like the hair of something drowned. Sebastian descended hand over hand along the shot line they'd rigged at dusk, watching their depth gauge tick past ten meters, fifteen, twenty. The light died by degrees—not all at once but the way evening dies, reluctantly, each fathom taking a color with it until there was nothing left but the pale glow of their own dive torch and the sound of their breathing, loud and mechanical in their ears.

At twenty-two meters the wall of the point rose up out of the dark like the side of a ship.

Sebastian had dived wrecks for eleven years. They knew how stone felt underwater—indifferent, ancient, blind. But this rock had a shape to it, a worked quality, as if the cave mouth hadn't been carved by the sea alone. Blast scars, maybe. Or something older. They ran a gloved hand along the basalt and felt grooves, regular as drill marks, running down into the black.

The entrance to the inner passage was exactly where the logbook said it would be: a horizontal fissure at the base of the wall, wide enough for a diver, narrow enough that the surge through it made a sound—a low, hollow moan, like wind across a bottle the size of a church.

Sebastian checked their gauges. Air at 210 bar. Bottom time planned to the minute, because the passage flooded on the rising tide and the charts said slack would hold for perhaps forty minutes. Their father's margin note was blunter than any chart: *If you feel the water move against you, you are already late.*

They looked into the fissure. The torch beam went in maybe eight feet before the silt and the dark ate it whole.

Every instinct Sebastian owned—the ones that had kept them alive through a collapsed wreck off Skerren Deep, through a fouled propeller in freezing fog, through eleven years of diving alone because alone was cheaper—every instinct said *no*. Said turn back, said come back with a team, with lights, with a plan B and a plan C.

But teams cost money, and money was the thing the sea owed them. And besides—Sebastian thought, with the grim humor that was the last luxury poverty allowed—Dad went in here with 1970s gear and a torch held together with tape. Whatever courage this took, it ran in the family.

They tucked their chin, angled the torch forward, and kicked into the throat of the rock.

* * *

The passage was a hundred and forty feet long. Sebastian knew this because their father had written it down, and because every foot of it passed in a silence so complete that counting became a kind of prayer.

It was not a straight swim. The fissure bent twice, dog-legged like a corridor in a fortress, and between the bends the ceiling came down close enough that Sebastian's tank scraped stone with a sound like a struck bell. Silt hung in the water everywhere, stirred by their own fins, so that within a minute they were swimming inside a cloud of their own making. The torch beam shrank to a brown halo two feet across. Beyond that: nothing.

This was what divers called a blackout environment, and it was where divers died. No visible surface. No visible bottom. No way to tell up from down except the weight of the regulator hose and the pull of gravity in the inner ear—and in a surge channel, even those lied. Sebastian slowed their breathing, made each kick short and deliberate, and followed the guideline they'd clipped at the entrance, paying out line through their left hand the way their father had taught them before they were tall enough to see over the wheelhouse rail.

*Line is your spine,* he'd said, crouched on the dock, wrapping the rope around their small fist with his big weathered one. *In the dark, the line remembers the way out even when you don't.*

His hands. God, she remembered his hands. Forty years gone from the water and still she could feel them.

Sebastian blinked salt and memory away and kept moving.

At the second bend the passage widened without warning, and the silt cloud fell away behind them, and the torch found the antechamber.

It was a cavern, roughly spherical, maybe sixty feet across, its ceiling lost somewhere above the reach of the beam. The floor sloped down toward a far wall where a second opening gaped—an inner passage, deeper in, deeper than today's plan allowed. Fallen rock lay tumbled across the floor like the bones of the mountain itself. And against the far wall, half-hidden behind a shelf of basalt, stood a shape too regular to be geology.

Steel. Rusted to the color of dried blood, furred with marine growth, but unmistakably a door. Riveted plate, a frame bolted into the living rock, and across its face, when Sebastian scraped away a curtain of plumose anemones with a bare glove, stamped letters deep enough to survive four decades of salt:

**H.M.S. MERIDIAN — STRONGROOM — AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY**

Below the stamp, a date. Sebastian's torch hovered on it for a long moment. Forty-one years ago. Two years before the Meridian was officially reported lost with all hands, no cargo, position unknown.

*No cargo.* Sebastian heard the harbormaster's careful voice again, the polite terrible weight of it: *I'd advise you to forget the number, Mr.—Ms. Fairweather. Some designations are retired for reasons of state.*

The navy didn't lose strongrooms. Navies *abandoned* them, sealed them, wrote them out of the record. Which meant everything behind that door was not wreckage.

It was evidence.

Sebastian's heart was going hard now, and they made themselves stop, made themselves run the numbers: air at 160 bar, bottom time twelve minutes spent, forty minutes of slack water remaining outside. Enough to look. Not enough to force anything—and the door wasn't going to be forced anyway, not with rusted hinges fused by four decades of sea. It would need tools, time, a key or a cutting rig. Another day. Another window.

They turned to go.

And the torch swept across the floor of the antechamber, and Sebastian stopped breathing.

* * *

There were six of them.

Six shapes seated against the base of the far wall, arranged in an arc beneath the strongroom door, evenly spaced, facing outward toward the passage like a congregation. Like sentries. Like witnesses called to testify and never dismissed.

Bones.

Human bones, articulated, still clothed in what the sea had left of uniform fabric—rotted to lace, but the buttons survived, brass and uncorroded, and Sebastian's torch picked them out one by one. Naval buttons. Six skeletons in naval dress, seated upright, their spines propped against the rock, their skulls tilted slightly downward as if in contemplation or exhaustion or grief.

Not scattered. Not tangled in wreckage, not drifted into a corner by current the way the drowned always end up, folded into the geometry of the flood. These had been *placed*. Someone had carried them here—or led them here, alive, and sat them down—and arranged them with care, in order, under the door they had presumably died trying to open or been posted to guard.

Sebastian floated in the middle of the antechamber and felt the primal thing rise in them, the thing no training ever fully drowned: the animal recognition of death in the dark, of being watched by the dead. Their breath rasped. The regulator sounded suddenly obscene, a machine's heartbeat in a cathedral.

One of the skeletons differed from the rest. The fifth from the left wore no buttons at all—civilian cloth, what remained of it—and its posture was different, slumped sideways against the sailor beside it, one arm extended, fingers resting on the other's knee. Comforting. Or being comforted.

And at its feet, half-buried in silt, something glinted.

Sebastian should have turned back then. Every rule of salvage, every rule of sanity, said turn back. But their body moved without consulting them, drawn down the slope the way mourners are drawn to graves, and they knelt in the silt beside the civilian skeleton and brushed the sediment away with two fingers.

A pocket watch. Silver case, chain snapped. And clutched—actually *clutched*, the finger bones curled around it—in the skeleton's other hand, a scrap of oilcloth wrapped tight and tied with wire, the kind of wrapping sailors used to keep papers dry.

Sebastian's hands were shaking. They told themselves it was the cold.

They took the watch first—it came free of the bone with a soft, horrible tenderness—and turned it over. An engraving on the case, worn but legible:

*M.O. — Keep the light burning.*

Maren Oduya. The name surfaced from the harbor's collective memory like a body from deep water: the old lighthouse keeper up the coast, half legend, half recluse, who had tended the Greyhaven light for decades and whose family had, people said, come from the sea somehow, though nobody liked to say how.

But the oilcloth bundle—Sebastian hesitated over it, hovering in the dark among the dead, aware suddenly of their air gauge reading 110 bar and of the faintest change in the water's taste, a thread of movement in the stillness that might have been imagination and might have been the tide beginning to think about turning.

They took the bundle. Slid it into their thigh pocket, cinched the strap down over it, and did not let themselves look again at the face of the skeleton that had held it for forty years.

"I'm sorry," Sebastian said into their mask, and the words fogged and vanished. "I'll come back for you. All of you."

The dead, of course, said nothing. But as Sebastian turned toward the guideline, the torch beam caught the arc of bones one final time—and this time they saw what they'd missed, what the angle had hidden. Scratched into the basalt wall above the seated figures, deep, deliberate, by someone with time and a tool and a desperate need to be understood:

**WE DID NOT DROWN**

Three words. Sebastian stared at them while their pulse hammered in their ears louder than the sea.

Then the water moved.

Not imagination. A real current, cold and insistent, sliding past their fins from the direction of the inner passage—the flood beginning, early, the way the sea always cheated. The silt on the floor lifted in ghostly sheets. Somewhere overhead, rock boomed as the surge found the blowholes in the cliff top.

*Late,* their father's handwriting said in their memory. *If you feel the water move against you, you are already late.*

Sebastian swam.

* * *

The return through the passage was the longest hundred and forty feet of their life.

The current pushed them from behind, which should have helped and instead was terrifying, because it meant the water filling the tunnel behind them was rising, and the ceiling that had scraped their tank on the way in was now a countdown. Silt exploded around them until the torch was useless, a coin of brown light, and Sebastian abandoned sight entirely and trusted the line—left hand on the rope, pulling hand over hand, kicking, counting strokes because counting was the only prayer available.

At the first bend, the line snagged.

It happened in half a second: the guideline, paid out carelessly in their panic, looped over a spur of rock, and when Sebastian surged forward the rope went taut across their chest and the reel jammed and they were suddenly tethered to the bottom of a flooding tunnel in total darkness, and the animal in their brain screamed *cut it, cut it, cut it*—

Their knife was out. They knew it was out because they felt the handle in their fist, though they had no memory of drawing it. One pass severed the guideline, and the loose end whipped away into the black like something fleeing, and Sebastian was free and lost in the same instant.

No line. No visibility. Rising water.

They did the only thing left. They went neutral, killed their torch to save the batteries and to kill the glare off the silt, and listened. Under the roar of their own breath, under the hammer of their heart, the tunnel spoke: the current hissing through the fissure ahead, a thin thread of lighter water, of *moving* water, flowing toward the entrance. Fish follow flow. Divers follow fish.

Sebastian followed the sound.

Thirty feet of pure blind faith, bumping once against the ceiling hard enough to see stars, scraping along the floor, lungs burning even through the regulator as if the body couldn't tell the difference between drowning and fear. Then the fissure opened, the pressure changed, and grey-green light bloomed around them like mercy, and Sebastian shot out of the cave mouth into the open sea with the surge pushing them up the wall like a hand.

They broke the surface inside the cavernous dark of the entry chamber, gasping, treading water below the slot of night sky, and for a full minute they simply hung there and shook.

Above them, through the cave mouth, the sky was going pale at the edges. Dawn. And out on the water, two miles east, the lights of the Ashe fleet were switching off one by one as the day crew woke—which meant the sonar crews were waking, which meant the sweeps would resume within the hour, circling closer, patient as sharks.

Sebastian hauled themselves aboard, stripped the mask down around their neck, and sat dripping on the deck with the watch in one hand and the oilcloth bundle heavy in their thigh pocket.

They unwrapped it with numb fingers.

Inside, dry after forty years, lay a single page torn from a notebook, water-stained at the edges but intact at its heart. A list of names—eleven names, handwritten, some crossed out, one circled twice. And beneath the list, in a different hand, smaller, pressed hard into the paper by someone writing in the dark or in a hurry or both:

*If anyone finds this — we were put off the ship at Greyhaven. They scuttled her with the rest still aboard. Tell Maren I got us out. Tell her I'm sorry about Thomas. — E.O.*

E.O. Oduya. Maren's kin. And the handwriting—

Sebastian went very still.

Because they knew that hand. They had grown up under it, on shopping lists and chart margins and birthday cards signed *Love, Dad*—and their father had been nine years old when the Meridian went down, which was impossible, which was wrong, unless the hand on the page belonged to someone else in the family, someone whose writing theirs resembled, someone whose logbook lay open under the anchor winch not three feet away with coordinates to this cave copied out in margins dated *after* his official death at sea.

Sebastian looked at the logbook. Looked at the page. Looked at the dawn coming up over the Ashe fleet.

Whatever their father had been, he had not been a simple salvage man. And the only person alive who might explain it—the woman whose name was engraved on a dead man's watch, sitting in a lighthouse at the edge of the map—was waiting up the coast.

Sebastian started the engine and turned the bow north.
