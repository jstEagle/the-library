## 10. Sabotage

The smell woke me before the light did.

Diesel. Not the faint ghost of it that lived in every seam of my boat after eleven years of hard service—this was fresh, sharp, wrong. It came through the cabin's open porthole in a greasy ribbon, and beneath it something else: the sweet chemical bite of fuel that had gone where fuel should never go.

I was on my feet before I was fully awake, boots half-laced, and out onto the quay with the grey pre-dawn pressing down like a wet thumb.

*Gannet* sat low at her mooring. Too low.

I knew her lines the way I knew my own hands—the way she rode high when her tanks were full, the particular list she took to port when they weren't. This morning she wallowed, sluggish, sitting maybe four inches deeper than she had any right to sit with only a quarter tank aboard. And from the transom, spreading across the black water of the harbor in an oily rainbow bloom that caught the first weak light like a bruise catching sun, ran a slick of diesel wide enough to gild the hulls of three neighboring boats.

"No," I said aloud, to no one, to the boat, to whoever had done this. "No, no, *no*."

I dropped to my knees on the quay boards and leaned out over the water. The fuel line ran from the tank through the transom fitting—a rubber hose, eleven years old, cracked at the collar but sound enough. Except now it wasn't. Someone had taken a blade to it, not once but twice, two clean slashes an inch apart just above the fitting, angled so the cut wouldn't look deliberate to a casual eye. To anyone who didn't know better, it would read as chafe failure. Old hose, old boat, old diver who couldn't be bothered with maintenance. An insurance assessor would nod sagely and write *wear*.

But I'd replaced that hose in March. I remembered the ache in my shoulders from wrestling the clamps, remembered swearing at the seized fitting, remembered Tessa Vane laughing at me from the next slip over and telling me I babied the old girl more than I'd ever baby myself.

Two cuts, angled clean, made by someone who wanted it to look like rot.

I sat back on my heels and let the cold come up through the boards into my bones while the harbor slowly woke around me. Gulls were already working the fuel slick, screaming their idiot grievances at nothing. Somewhere behind me, the bakery ovens were breathing warmth into the lane. A fishing crew clattered down the steps toward the north pontoon, voices rough with sleep and tea.

And there, at the fuel dock on the far side of the basin, stood the *Vigilant*—Ashe's survey vessel, all white superstructure and black hull, looking less like a salvage ship than a knife laid on a table. Her deck lights were on. Her crew moved with the brisk economy of men already halfway through their day.

Refueling. At dawn. After a night when nobody else in Greyhaven had been anywhere near the water.

I told myself it meant nothing. Fleets refueled early; Ashe ran his operation like a military unit because he *was*, effectively, running a military unit—one that happened to carry sonar arrays instead of torpedoes. There were forty reasons his crew might have been up before first light, and ninety-nine of them had nothing to do with me.

Then one of them looked up and saw me kneeling on the quay beside my crippled boat, and smiled.

He was a big man, red-faced, forearms like mooring lines, and he raised a hand—not a wave exactly, more a salute of recognition—and called something across the water I couldn't make out over the gulls. His crewmates laughed. One of them said something back, and the laughter got louder.

I stood up. My hands were shaking, and I made fists until they stopped.

* * *

By eight o'clock I'd mopped what diesel I could from *Gannet*'s bilge, bagged the slashed hose as evidence I wasn't sure anyone would ever want, and sat in the harbormaster's outer office waiting for a man whose patience for me had visibly expired last week.

Harbormaster Cullis was sixty, built like a bollard, and had spent forty years perfecting the art of hearing nothing. He listened to my account of the sabotage with his eyes fixed on a point somewhere over my left shoulder, and when I finished he was quiet long enough that I could hear the tide clock ticking on his wall.

"You've got enemies, Fairweather?" he asked at last.

"I've got a rival fleet in the harbor cutting my water."

"Corven Ashe is a licensed operator with a spotless record in three jurisdictions." Cullis said it the way a man recites scripture—by rote, without belief. "You're suggesting his people came ashore in the night and vandalized your vessel."

"I'm suggesting someone did. And his crew was topping off tanks at dawn."

"Boats burn fuel. That's generally why they have tanks." He finally looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not contempt, not boredom. Something closer to pity, or its meaner cousin, embarrassment. "Look. You file a report, I'll log it. Marine unit might send someone down from Kirkwall if there's a slot. But between you and me—" he lowered his voice, though the office was empty, "—you've been asking questions you shouldn't ask, diving places you shouldn't dive, and now your boat's been cut. Maybe take the hint. Some hints are cheaper than others."

"The hint being?"

"The hint being that whoever did this could have scuttled her. Could have burned her at the dock with you sleeping aboard." He let that settle. "They didn't. They nicked a fuel line. That's not an attack, lad. That's a *conversation*. Somebody telling you they know where you sleep."

I walked out of his office into a morning that had turned bright and cold, and the sunlight felt like exposure. Every face on the quay became a question. The fishmonger hosing down his slabs—had he been up in the night? The two deckhands coiling rope outside the chandlery, watching me pass with expressions just a shade too neutral? The woman at the pie cart who'd always given me the end piece free?

Invisibility had been my armor my whole life. Poor, unimportant Sebastian Fairweather, the diver nobody bothered to remember—that had kept me safe through every bad season and every desperate gamble. I had spent thirty years being too small to crush, and I'd mistaken it for freedom.

Now I was visible. Now I was a problem someone had decided to solve, and they'd started with the cheapest possible solution: not killing me, just stranding me. Grounding me. Taking away the one thing that mattered more than money, which was mobility, because the tide windows were burning down like a fuse and we both knew it.

Five tides left. Maybe four, if the weather turned.

And no boat.

* * *

I spent the afternoon in the chandlery arguing with a supplier about hose stock and paying triple for overnight delivery I couldn't afford, because the alternative was admitting I couldn't afford anything at all. While the man wrote up the order, I watched the harbor through the salt-fogged window.

The *Vigilant* sat fat and satisfied at her berth. Two of her tenders were out past the point—I could see their wakes stitching the swell, running grid patterns over the water where the Devil's Ledger slept under forty feet of sea. Ashe's sonar sweeps had been circling Greyhaven Point for three days now, patient as sharks, and every pass brought them tighter to the truth. He didn't need my cave mouth yet. He needed my *tide tables*. He needed the arithmetic of when the sea pulled back far enough to expose it, and that arithmetic lived in exactly one place: the head of a broke diver whose boat now leaked diesel instead of holding it.

Coincidence, I told myself again. Fleets refuel at dawn.

But coincidence doesn't smile at you across the water.

Tessa Vane found me at dusk on the quay, where I sat with my back against *Gannet*'s hull drinking tea gone cold in the wind. She dropped down beside me without asking, all sharp elbows and sharper eyes, her hair stuffed under a wool cap against the cold.

"Heard about your boat," she said.

"News travels fast."

"News travels *paid*." She glanced sideways at me. "Cullis logged your report at nine-fifteen. By ten, three different people had offered me fifty quid apiece to find out what you'd written in it. I took two of the fifties."

"Tessa—"

"Relax, I gave you a glowing review. Said you were a sad little salvager with delusions of treasure and a boat held together with prayer." She blew on her own tea. "Which, no offense, is now literally true. Fuel line's shot, yeah? Heard that too."

"Everyone's heard everything tonight."

"That's what happens when you become interesting." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. She stared out at the darkening water, at the lights of the *Vigilant* burning steady across the basin, and her jaw worked like she was chewing on words she didn't want to swallow. "Sebastian. I've known you six years. You're careful. Careful diver, careful businessman, careful liar when you need to be—which is a compliment, in case you're wondering. So tell me why a careful man suddenly has enemies with knives."

I weighed it. Six years of trading favors and keeping each other's secrets, and here was the moment where either trust existed or it never had.

"Somebody sank a ship full of witnesses forty years ago," I said quietly. "And somebody else is trying to make sure it stays sunk."

Tessa didn't laugh. Didn't scoff. She went very still, the way she did at cards when she drew the hand she'd been waiting for, and after a long moment she said, "The Meridian."

My heart stopped and restarted. "How do you know that name?"

"Because my grandfather crewed the boats that helped cover it up, and he drank himself to death whispering about it." She drained her tea and crushed the cup in her fist. "And because Corven Ashe bought my silence yesterday morning, same as he's buying everyone's, and I took his money, and I've been sick about it since." She turned to face me fully, and in the lamplight her eyes were bright and frightened and resolved all at once. "Your boat's dead, your tide windows are dying, and the only other fast hull in this harbor belongs to me. So before you ask—yes. I'll take you out. But not for money."

"What, then?"

"First claim. Anything you bring up that isn't navy paper, isn't evidence, isn't going to hang anybody—cargo, coin, whatever the sea kept—I get first pick. One item. My choice." She spat into her palm and held it out. "And Sebastian—if you're lying to me about what's down there, I will drown you myself and sleep fine after."

I looked at her hand, then at the *Vigilant*'s lights across the black water, then at my gutted boat rocking gently at the dock like a patient who'd been bled.

Somewhere out past the point, the sea was already beginning to think about turning.

I spat in my palm and shook.
