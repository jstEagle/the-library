## 11. Borrowed Time, Borrowed Boat

Tessa Vane's grip was dry as rope and twice as strong. She held the shake a beat longer than a handshake needed to last, the way she did everything—long enough that you understood it was a choice.

"Done," she said, and wiped her palm on her coat like she was wiping away a signature she might want to deny later. "Now walk with me, Fairweather, because we've got maybe four hours of dark left and I don't waste either."

She moved down the quay without waiting for me to agree. That was Tessa. People followed her or they didn't, and she'd already spent whatever she cared about on the outcome.

I fell into step beside her, past the gutted hulk of my own boat. The *Kestrel* sat low in the water where they'd cut her fuel line, diesel still ghosting off her bilge in a rainbow slick that caught the harbor lights and broke them apart. Forty feet of honest work, my father's hull, my father's name still legible under the salt scab—*Kestrel*, after the bird that hunts by hovering, by watching, by striking only when the wind holds still. I'd been named for patience I never had.

"Whoever did it knew boats," Tessa said, not looking at me. "Cut from below the waterline access, clean stroke, one pass. Not some dockside drunk with a knife. Somebody who wanted you stranded, not dead."

"You sound admiring."

"I sound observant." She stopped at the end of the pier where a tarp-covered shape crouched against the pilings, low and black and hungry-looking even under canvas. "There's a difference between admiration and respect, and only one of them gets you killed."

She pulled the tarp back in one motion.

The *Sable* was thirty-two feet of smuggler's arithmetic: a hull built for running the night channels between here and wherever customs cutters weren't, twin diesels tuned past any sense of decency, and a deck plan designed around one question—*how fast can this thing be gone?* She wasn't beautiful. She was efficient, which is a different kind of beauty, the kind that doesn't care if you notice.

"She'll do forty knots in flat water," Tessa said. "Greyhaven Point in twenty minutes, weather permitting. Weather rarely permits." She looked at me sideways, and there was something underneath the flint in her face, something almost like curiosity. "You're going out on tide three. Tomorrow morning, before first light. Which means tonight you tell me everything I need to keep us both alive, and nothing I don't."

"And how do you decide which is which?"

"I decide." She dropped the tarp. "That's the arrangement. You're buying silence and speed, not partnership. Don't confuse them."

* * *

We sat in the wheelhouse of the *Sable* while the harbor slept its dishonest sleep around us. Tessa poured tea from a dented thermos into two tin cups, and I noticed hers had a chip in the rim shaped like a crescent moon, and I thought about how every object in this woman's possession told a story she'd never volunteer.

I gave her the shape of it without the heart of it. A cave at Greyhaven Point, exposed by a tide that shouldn't have turned. A strongroom inside, naval-stamped, forty years sealed. A ledger I'd already lifted. Coordinates that matched entries in my father's logbook—entries he'd made, according to every official record, after he was dead.

I didn't mention Maren Oduya. I didn't mention the brass key warm in my pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool. And I didn't mention Corven Ashe, though I watched her face when I said the word *salvage fleet* and saw the small tightening around her eyes that meant she'd already done the math herself.

"Ashe," she said anyway, because of course she had. "His survey tender's been running grids off the point for two days. You know what that costs per hour? Men like Ashe don't spend money circling nothing." She sipped her tea. "He knows something's there. He just doesn't know what, or exactly where. Yet."

"Then we're racing him."

"We're not racing anyone. *You're* racing him. I'm ferrying cargo." She set the cup down with a click that sounded final. "One more thing, and then you go home and sleep, because you look like a man who hasn't slept since the tide turned, and tired divers die. If it goes wrong—if Ashe's people are on the water when we come out, or navy, or God, or anything—I put you over the side and I'm gone. No heroics, no going back for you. You swim or you sink. Are we clear?"

"Crystal."

"Good." For just a moment, the flint cracked and something else showed through—young, tired, guarded in a way that had nothing to do with business. "My brother ran this channel for six years. Customs took his boat and the sea took him the same winter. So understand me, Fairweather: I'm not sentimental about the water, but I'm not stupid about it either. It keeps what it takes."

She looked out at the black harbor, at the lights of the *Vigilant* riding at anchor like a patient predator.

"Tide turns at 4:17. Be at the north slip at 3:30. Bring your own kit, your own air, and nothing that floats if you drop it." She stood, ending the audience the way she'd begun it—without asking. "And Sebastian. Whatever's down there that made the navy seal a room for forty years—" she paused at the wheelhouse door, half her face in shadow, "—make sure it's worth my brother's channel."

Then she was gone, and I sat alone in the smell of diesel and cold tea, holding a tin cup with no chip in its rim, wondering which of us had just made the worse bargain.

* * *

I didn't go home. Home was a rented room above the chandlery with a window that faced the harbor, and windows that face harbors are two-way mirrors. Instead I walked the long way around the point, along the shingle where the sea had pulled back two nights ago and shown me the Devil's Ledger gaping like a wound in the world's skin, and I let myself think about what I was actually doing.

Because here was the truth I hadn't said aloud, not to Tessa, not to the harbormaster, not to the dark: I wasn't doing this for gold. Gold would have been simpler. Gold you could count, spend, bury, lose. What I was doing this for fit in a coat pocket—a brass key and a handful of names, and the growing, unbearable certainty that my father hadn't drowned the way the record said, hadn't died the way the record said, and had spent whatever years he actually had carrying something so heavy he'd hidden it in plain sight, in a logbook his own child inherited like a box of old receipts.

*Coordinates confirmed. Room intact. Do not approach alone. Do not approach armed. They will say the ship carried no passengers. They will be lying.*

His handwriting. His careful, patient hand, the hand that taught me to tie a bowline before I could write my own name. And beneath it, in the margin, smaller, almost erased: *M.O. knows.*

Maren Oduya knew. Maren Oduya had pressed a key into my hand yesterday and said, *Your father waited too long to send someone. Don't make his mistake.* As if she'd been expecting me for fifty years. As if my surname were a knock at her door she'd been listening for.

I stopped at the edge of the shingle and looked out at Greyhaven Point, a black shoulder of rock against the lesser black of the sky. Somewhere under that water was a strongroom with an inner door, and somewhere behind that door was a dispatch case, and inside the case was either my inheritance or my death warrant, depending entirely on who reached it first.

The tide was thinking about turning. I could feel it in the shingle under my boots, that faint sucking shift, the whole coast inhaling.

* * *

Sleep found me eventually, in the chandlery loft, in fits, with the key under my pillow like a child and my dive knife under the other one like an adult. I dreamed of my father's hands—just his hands, tying a knot, over and over, a knot I couldn't identify—and woke at two in the morning with the dream still clinging to me like weed.

The harbor at two a.m. was a different country. Fog had come in off the channel, thin but determined, softening the masts into charcoal sketches. And moving through it, running dark, came a tender from the *Vigilant*, its engine throttled down to a murmur, sweeping slow along the moorings.

I flattened against the chandlery wall and watched it pass. Two silhouettes in the cockpit. One of them raised something—glasses, probably, though at two in the morning in fog, glasses were theater—and swept them across the north slips.

Across the north slips. Where the *Sable* lay under her tarp.

They weren't looking for me. Or rather—they were looking for everyone, the way Ashe did everything, comprehensively and without hurry, a man who believed the sea would eventually give up everything and all he had to do was outlast it. But the sweep lingered on the north slips a moment longer than the rest, and I felt the cold slide down my spine that wasn't fog.

Somebody had talked. Or somebody had guessed. With Ashe it hardly mattered; both roads led to the same place.

I went to the north slip early. Tessa was already there, of course, loading air bottles with the economy of long habit, and when I told her about the tender she didn't stop working, just grunted and said, "Told you. Cargo, not partner. Get your kit aboard and stay low till we clear the mole."

"But—"

"Fairweather." She looked up, and in the light of her shielded lantern her face was all angles and decision. "Ashe's tender ran the moorings at two a.m., which means he thinks the answer's in this harbor, which means he's close, which means we leave now instead of 3:30, and we leave quiet. Unless you'd rather stand here explaining yourself to the fog."

We cleared the mole at ten past three, engines cold-started outside the breakwater where the *Sable*'s exhaust baffles could swallow the noise, and ran south through the fog at a speed that would have gotten us arrested anywhere with laws. Tessa drove the way she did everything—no wasted motion, total attention, a woman negotiating with the sea at forty knots and winning on points. Spray came over the bow in sheets. The compass light glowed green on her face. She never once looked at me, and I understood that this, too, was discipline: you didn't take your eyes off the water to look at passengers.

Greyhaven Point rose out of the fog at 3:40, black and absolute, and Tessa brought the *Sable* around into the lee with a turn so smooth I felt it in my teeth before I heard it.

"Tide bottoms at 5:02," she said. "That gives you—" she checked her watch, "—eighty-one minutes from the moment you roll off my transom until I start getting nervous. Ninety until I start weighing whether you're worth waiting for. You remember our terms."

"I remember."

"Then go find whatever it is that's got the whole coast holding its breath." And then, as I swung my fins over the side, quieter, almost against her will: "Come back up. My brother didn't. It's a bad trade, Fairweather. Don't make it."

I rolled off the transom into water so cold it rang like a bell through my whole body, and the fog closed over my head, and the *Sable*'s engines faded to nothing, and there was only the sea and the key in my pocket and eighty-one minutes.

Below me, as I descended, the cave mouth opened in the rock like a held breath finally released.

And below that—deeper than the tide chart said anything should be, deep enough that my light barely reached it—the inner door of the strongroom stood waiting, and set into its iron face, a lock shaped exactly like the brass in my hand.

I kicked toward it, and behind me, far above, faint as a heartbeat through forty feet of black water, I heard an engine that wasn't Tessa's.
