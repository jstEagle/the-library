## 12. Dive Two: The Strongroom Door

The second tide window opened at 4:17 in the morning, and Sebastian was in the water by 4:09.

They had spent the hours between dives the way a gambler spends the night before a final hand—counting, checking, recounting. The regulator stripped and rebuilt twice. The backup light taped to the forearm where it couldn't wander. Maren Oduya's brass key on a lanyard of braided fishing line, tucked inside the right glove, its teeth pressing a cold signature into the palm. The key was heavier than it looked. Most inheritances were.

Tessa Vane's boat sat anchored off Greyhaven Point with her running lights doused, and Tessa herself stood at the rail with a thermos and an expression that managed to be bored and watchful at once.

"Two hours," she said as Sebastian rolled over the gunwale into the black water. "Two hours and I'm gone, Fairweather, with or without you."

"You said ninety minutes last time."

"I'm feeling generous." She took a sip from the thermos. "It won't last."

Sebastian gave her a thumbs-up, bit down on the regulator, and dropped beneath the surface before she could see their face. There was no point letting anyone read what was written there. Not fear exactly. Something older than fear—the animal certainty of a creature walking back into a trap it had already sprung once and survived.

* * *

The flooded passage to the antechamber was exactly as they'd left it three days ago, which was somehow worse than if it had changed. The sea did not forget. It simply waited, patient as arithmetic, for you to come back and make your next mistake.

Sebastian swam the line they'd laid on dive one—a thin polypropylene guide rope clipped to bolts they'd drilled into the rock, because the first lesson of cave diving was that silt killed more divers than drowning ever had. One wrong fin kick and the water turned to milk, and then the dark wasn't just around you, it was *in* you, filling your eyes and your mouth and your confidence all at once.

The passage narrowed. The walls closed in like a throat deciding whether to swallow. Sebastian moved through it in short, disciplined kicks, elbows tight, breathing slow the way their father's logbook had taught them without ever meaning to—in the margins, in the cramped annotations beside depth readings: *slow is smooth, smooth is alive.* Their father had written that above a sketch of this very coastline, forty years before Sebastian was born, and Sebastian had read it a thousand times as a child without knowing why the man had bothered to write it at all.

Now they knew. Now every margin note in that ruined journal had become a set of instructions from the dead.

The antechamber opened around them like a held breath released. Sebastian's light swept across the collapsed rubble, the rusted framework of the outer door hanging open on its hinges, the strongroom beyond—and stopped.

The inner door was shut.

On dive one, it had been ajar, wedged by decades of sediment and corrosion, showing only a black seam of darkness. Now it stood closed. Firmly. Deliberately.

Sebastian hung in the water, heart tapping against the ribs, and made themselves look at it properly. Current could move things. Sediment shifts, tides push, doors swing. That was the rational explanation, and they offered it to themselves the way you offer a blanket to a shivering child—knowing it won't fit.

But the door hadn't swung. The hinge side showed fresh scoring in the rust, bright metal exposed in two parallel lines, as if something had been dragged across it. As if someone—or something—had pulled it shut from the outside.

*Something got in,* Sebastian thought. *Or something got out.*

Their father's logbook had a phrase for moments like this too, scrawled beside a sounding taken off the point in his last season: *the ledger keeps its own accounts.* Sebastian had thought it poetry once. Standing in the drowned dark before a door that had closed itself, they understood it was a warning.

They checked their gauges. Fifty-one minutes of bottom time at this depth, and they'd already spent six getting here. The math was simple and merciless. The door was between them and the dispatch case, and the dispatch case was the whole reason any of this was worth dying for.

Sebastian swam forward and pressed the brass key into the lock.

* * *

It resisted, then didn't. The mechanism turned with a grinding reluctance, like an old man being woken, and somewhere inside the door a bolt drew back with a sound Sebastian felt through their chest rather than heard—a deep, tectonic *clunk* that rolled through the rock and out into the water beyond.

The door drifted inward under its own weight.

Beyond it, the strongroom proper: a concrete box perhaps four meters square, shelving bolted to the walls, most of it empty, some of it collapsed into drifts of rust and silt. Naval stenciling still clung to one wall in flaking white letters—*H.M.S. PROPERTY / AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY*—and beneath it, half-legible, a date. Forty years old. Older than Sebastian. Older than their father's death, though not by much.

And there, on the lowest shelf, sitting in its own shallow grave of sediment: a dispatch case. Green canvas over steel, brass corners gone green-black with age, the naval crown stamped into the lid. A canvas strap sealed it, and over the strap someone had poured a bead of lead and pressed an official seal into it while it cooled. The seal had cracked with time but held its shape—a ship's wheel, and letters around the rim that Sebastian couldn't read through the murk.

They lifted it. The case weighed almost nothing, which meant whatever was inside was paper. Paper was better than gold. Paper, Sebastian was beginning to understand, was the only cargo aboard the Meridian that anyone had ever been willing to kill for.

They clipped the case to their harness, cinched it flat against their body, and turned to go.

That was when the main light died.

No flicker. No warning dimming. One moment the beam carved the room out of the ocean, and the next it simply quit, and the dark came down on Sebastian like a lid.

* * *

Panic is a diver's first passenger and worst crewmate. It arrives uninvited, weighs nothing, and sinks everything.

Sebastian felt it climb their spine and did what their father's margins had trained them to do: nothing. They hung motionless in the absolute black, one hand on the shelf, one on the case, and breathed. In for four. Out for six. The bubbles rose past their face with a sound like whispering, and the whispering was the only thing in the universe.

*Backup light. Left forearm. You taped it there for exactly this.*

They found it by touch, thumbed the switch, and a weak yellow cone bloomed—half the power of the primary, maybe less, the batteries aging in the cold. Enough to see a meter. Enough to see the door. Barely enough to see the way home.

Enough, and no more.

Sebastian turned toward the doorway and froze.

The guide rope was gone.

Not slack, not shifted—*gone*. The line they'd clipped to the bolt outside the door hung in a loose, drifting arc, its far end trailing away into darkness, and the bolt itself—the bolt they'd drilled and torqued with their own hands three days ago—was missing. Not unscrewed. Missing. The hole remained, a clean dark socket in the rock, and around its lip the stone was scarred with fresh gouges, pale against the ancient black, as if something with tremendous grip and no patience at all had torn the fitting free.

Sebastian's mind offered explanations in descending order of sanity. Corrosion. A shifting boulder. The survey charges Ashe's fleet had been testing up-coast, sending shockwaves through the rock. All plausible. All comforting.

None of them explained the gouges. Rock doesn't gouge itself in pairs.

Then the water moved.

Not current—current has texture, rhythm, a long breathing pull and release. This was displacement. Something large passing through the passage behind them, pushing a wall of water ahead of it, and Sebastian felt the pressure change against their ears the way you feel a door close in another room. The backup light caught movement at the edge of its beam—a shape, vast and low, sliding past the antechamber's mouth with a grace that had nothing human in it.

A seal, they told themselves. A grey seal, a bull, big ones run two hundred kilos up here, they come into caves all the—

The shape passed again. Closer. And this time the light touched it for half a second, half a heartbeat: a flank, pale and wrong, too smooth, too long, folding around the corner of the collapsed doorway like smoke pouring uphill.

Then it was gone, and the water settled, and the silence that followed was worse than the noise had been, because the silence was listening.

* * *

Sebastian did not remember making the decision to move. They were simply moving—through the doorway, along the wall, one hand tracing the rock while the other pinned the dispatch case flat, the backup light trembling in a grip they couldn't stop shaking. The air in their lungs went thin and fast and they hauled it back down to a crawl, *slow is smooth, smooth is alive*, the words beating in time with their pulse like a second heart.

The passage to the entrance was a hundred meters of narrowing stone. They'd swum it outbound in eight minutes. They swam it inbound in four, and every meter of it the pressure of the water told them the same story: something was pacing them. Not attacking. Not fleeing. *Escorting.* The displacements came in waves now, regular as breath, always behind, always offset to the left where Sebastian couldn't turn to look without losing the wall.

At the junction where the passage forked toward the blowhole—the emergency route their father had marked, the one Sebastian had memorized and prayed never to need—the displacements stopped.

Sebastian stopped too, hovering in the throat of the tunnel, and looked back the way they'd come.

The backup light reached perhaps five meters into the returning dark. Nothing filled it. The water hung empty and green-grey and utterly still, and somehow that was the most terrifying image yet, because the thing had been there, it had *been there*, and now the passage was vacant as a held-open mouth, and Sebastian understood—with the wordless certainty of prey—that it had let them go. That whatever owned these waters had escorted them to the edge of its territory and released them the way you release something too small to be worth the trouble.

Or the way you release bait, so it can swim back out and show you where the rest of the shoal is hiding.

Sebastian turned and kicked for the surface and did not look back again.

* * *

They broke into grey pre-dawn light gasping, one arm hooked over the anchor line, the other locked around the dispatch case like a drowning sailor around a spar. The sky over Greyhaven Point was the color of old pewter, streaked with the first bruised pink of morning, and the wind had come around to the northeast while they were below—it carried the smell of a storm still two days out at sea, but coming, unmistakably coming.

Tessa hauled them aboard with a grip like a winch and stood over them while Sebastian coughed half the North Atlantic onto her deck.

"Well," she said. "You're late."

"There's—" Sebastian's voice came out shredded. They swallowed and tried again. "There's something living in the caves. Big. It tore my guide line out of solid rock."

Tessa looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked at the dispatch case, strapped to Sebastian's harness, its lead seal glinting dull green in the early light. Then back at their face.

"Did you get what you went down for?"

Sebastian worked the case free and held it up between them. Water sheeted off the green canvas. The cracked seal caught the dawn.

Tessa's expression did something complicated—greed and fear fighting for the same ground—and lost to both. "Ninety minutes," she said finally, turning for the helm. "I said I'd give you ninety. Next window, Fairweather, I want first claim on anything non-naval, and I want it in writing, and I want to be somewhere else when you open that."

She threw the throttle forward and the boat leapt for the harbor, and Sebastian sat in the stern with their back against the transom, watching Greyhaven Point shrink behind them. The cave mouth was already underwater, the tide swallowing it whole, keeping its accounts.

In their lap, the dispatch case sat cold and patient. Inside it, sealed for forty years behind lead and canvas and concrete and the weight of everything the navy had denied: an order. A list of names. And—if their father's margin notes were true—a second copy of that list, annotated in handwriting Sebastian had known since childhood, dated after the day the sea had officially taken him.

Whatever had paced them through the dark had been guarding more than territory.

Sebastian put a hand flat on the case and felt, absurdly, that it was warm—as warm as anything could be, rising out of that cold—and understood that the next tide window wouldn't just be a dive.

It would be an exhumation.
