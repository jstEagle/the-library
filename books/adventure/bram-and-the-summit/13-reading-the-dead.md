## 13. Reading the Dead

The dispatch case sat on Sebastian's kitchen table like a patient animal, still dripping, its brass clasps furred green with forty years of salt. It had ridden home wrapped in Tessa Vane's spare tarp, wedged between a fuel can and a coil of rope, and the whole way across the harbor Sebastian had kept one hand on it—not to steady it, but to be sure it was real. That something this heavy with consequence could come up out of the dark on a length of line and a prayer of air.

Now, under the single bulb above the table, they dried their hands on their trousers and looked at it.

The case was naval issue, gunmetal grey, stenciled in faded white: **H.M.S. MERIDIAN — DISPATCHES — DO NOT REMOVE**. The irony of that last instruction, after everything, almost made them laugh. Almost. Their hands weren't steady enough for laughing.

They'd waited until full dark to open it. Not because anyone was watching—though after the slashed fuel line, after Ashe's crew refueling at dawn with the smug patience of men who knew exactly what they'd done, Sebastian no longer trusted the empty spaces between things—but because some doors you didn't walk through casually. Some doors you stood in front of first and let yourself feel the weight of what was on the other side.

Their father had taught them that. *A wreck tells you when it's ready,* he used to say, oiling a regulator at this same table, in this same light. *You don't rush a door underwater. You wait for it to tell you.*

Sebastian pulled the lamp closer, took a breath, and worked the clasps.

The hinges shrieked—a long, corroded complaint—and the lid came up on a smell that hit like a fist: brine and rust and something older underneath, something like a closed room, like paper that had been holding its breath since before Sebastian was born.

Inside, three things.

A canvas-wrapped bundle, tarred and tied with waxed cord. A leather folio, swollen with water but intact, stamped with the admiralty crest. And beneath both, flat as a coffin lid, a second copy of the manifest—the same manifest whose waterlogged pages Sebastian had already half-deciphered from the strongroom ledger—except this one was dry. Sealed in an oilskin sleeve. Preserved deliberately, carefully, by someone who had wanted it to survive.

Someone who had written on it in a hand Sebastian knew better than their own heartbeat.

* * *

The admiralty order came first. It always comes first; that's how these things are built. Guilt is procedural.

Sebastian untied the canvas bundle with fingers gone clumsy and unrolled it on the table, weighting the corners with a mug, a tin of matches, the salt dish. The paper inside was thin, official, typed on a machine that had been obsolete for decades, and headed with a classification stamp so heavy it had embossed through four pages:

**MOST SECRET — ADMIRALTY — OPERATION LEDGERLINE**

*By direction of the Lords Commissioners, and pursuant to authority delegated under wartime provision 14-C, the vessel MERIDIAN (transport, requisitioned) is to be disposed of at position GREYHAVEN DEEP on the night tide of [date redacted], with all documentation pertaining to her final voyage secured aboard.*

*It is further directed that no rescue operation be mounted, that no inquiry be convened, and that surviving documentation be accounted destroyed.*

*Disposal to be conducted by embarked party. Crew of escort vessel to be briefed per standard protocol. No record of passenger complement to appear in subsequent filings.*

*Signed, by order—*

The signature was a scrawl, but the name beneath it was printed clearly enough, and Sebastian did not recognize it, and somehow that was worse. Not a monster with a famous face. A clerk. A man with a desk and a stamp and a signature like everyone else's, authorizing the drowning of a ship full of people who had seen something they were never supposed to see, and then authorizing the drowning of the paperwork about the drowning.

*No record of passenger complement.*

But there was a record. It was sitting right there, dry as bone, in an oilskin sleeve. Someone had disobeyed.

Sebastian read the order twice more, lips moving, and each time the words rearranged themselves into new shapes of horror. *With all documentation pertaining to her final voyage secured aboard.* They had sunk the ship with the evidence chained to its corpse, and called that accounting. They had sent witnesses down with their testimony strapped to their chests.

And the Meridian hadn't been lost. She hadn't foundered, hadn't struck anything, hadn't been unlucky. She had been murdered, methodically, on a night tide, at coordinates that matched—to the tenth of a degree—the ones in the ledger, the ones in the father's logbook, the ones the navy spent forty years pretending pointed at nothing.

Sebastian pushed back from the table. Stood. Walked to the window and looked out at the black water of the harbor, at the riding lights of boats they knew by name, at Greyhaven Point a dark shoulder to the north where the cave waited under six feet of returning tide.

The sea owed them something. They'd thought that two weeks ago, standing on the point watching the impossible low tide peel back the rock. They'd thought it was about money—about the season's empty account, the rent, the engine rebuild they couldn't afford. The sea owed them a living.

It turned out the debt ran deeper than coin. The sea had been keeping a receipt for forty years, and it had Sebastian's family name written on it.

* * *

They made tea they didn't drink and came back to the table and opened the oilskin sleeve.

The manifest inside was a fair copy—typed, formal, thirty-one names in three columns. Passengers. Not crew. Thirty-one civilians boarded at two ports, listed with ages and occupations, and every occupation was the same in substance if not in letter: clerk, surveyor, telegraphist, accountant, port official, customs officer. Thirty-one people whose jobs had put them in rooms where numbers were kept, where orders were filed, where the machinery of the war ground its grain into flour.

Thirty-one witnesses, dressed up as a passenger list.

Sebastian had known this already, in outline, from the waterlogged ledger. What they hadn't known—what stopped their breath in their throat and made the kitchen suddenly very cold—was the handwriting in the margins.

Small, precise, slanted slightly left. A draftsman's hand, a diver's hand, a hand that had annotated tide tables and air calculations and the margins of Sebastian's childhood schoolbooks with the same careful pressure. Notes in pencil, dated in a column down the right edge.

Dated across a span of eleven years.

Dated beginning **four years after the date their father had died at sea.**

Sebastian sat down. The chair scraped. Somewhere outside a gull complained once and went quiet, as if even the birds understood that something had just broken open.

Their father, Tomas Fairweather, had been lost off the northern coast when Sebastian was nine. Lost with his boat, the *Kestrel*, in a November squall; lost with two crew, bodies never recovered; lost officially, formally, with a board-of-trade finding and a plaque in the harbormaster's lobby and a grave that contained only a stone. Sebastian had grown up in the shape of that absence. Had learned diving partly because the water had taken him and partly because going down into it felt like visiting. Had inherited his logbook—water-stained, half-burned in some old fire, its pages a jumble of coordinates and depth readings and fragments of journal—and had spent twenty years reading it like scripture, looking for the man in the margins.

The man in the margins was here. On this table. Writing dates that could not exist.

"No," Sebastian said aloud, to the kitchen, to the dead, to nobody. "No, that's not—you're dead. You're dead."

But the handwriting didn't argue. Handwriting never argues. It just sits there being itself, patient as stone, and the more you look at it the more unmistakably it becomes what it is. The looped descenders. The way he crossed his sevens. The little hook he put on the letter *d*, which Sebastian had inherited without knowing it, which appeared in their own dive logs to this day.

Tomas Fairweather had held this document. Had annotated it over eleven years. Had known the names, had traced the story, had written in the margin beside the entry for a customs officer named E. Halloran: *knew too much re: Ledgerline cargo manifests — why aboard?*

Beside a telegraphist named R. Osei: *sent three signals night of — none logged ashore.*

And beside the final name in the third column, a woman listed as M. Oduya, age 11, occupation left blank—because she was a child, because children don't have occupations, because whoever typed this list couldn't quite bring themselves to type *witness* next to a girl—Tomas had written only two words:

*She lived.*

* * *

Sebastian didn't remember getting up. They were at the shelf by the window, pulling down the father's logbook, the original, the relic, the thing they'd handled maybe a hundred times and never once like this. They laid it beside the manifest and began, page by page, with hands that shook and then, strangely, steadied, comparing.

The coordinates matched. Of course they matched; they'd known that since chapter one of this whole catastrophe, since the night they'd cross-referenced the strongroom ledger against the logbook and felt the first cold finger of coincidence touch their spine. But now the matching meant something different. Now the logbook wasn't a dead man's diving notes. It was a surveillance record. Tomas Fairweather hadn't stumbled onto the Meridian. He had been *watching* her. For years. Before his official death and—God, the vertigo of it—after.

Because here, in the logbook's ruined middle section, half-eaten by fire and water, was an entry Sebastian had always read as grief-madness, as a drowning man's nonsense. It was undated, or its date was burned away, and it read:

*Third pass tonight. They've moved the buoy again. Whoever pays the harbor office pays well. She's down there and they want her forgotten, but paper floats, boy. Paper always floats. Find the room that doesn't flood.*

*Find the room that doesn't flood.* The strongroom. The cave. The Devil's Ledger, exposed once a generation by a tide low enough to shame the charts. Their father had been looking for it. Had maybe found it. Had maybe—

Sebastian turned pages with increasing desperation, hunting dates, hunting anything with a number that would anchor this madness to a calendar. And there, near the end, on a page so damaged it was nearly lace, a fragment in that same unmistakable hand:

*If I go quiet, it's because I went loud. Don't look for a body. Look for M.O. She knows where the rest sleeps. Tell her the tide keeps its promises.*

*Tell her the tide keeps its promises.*

Maren Oduya. M.O. The child on the manifest, the blank occupation, the two words in the margin—*she lived*. The lighthouse keeper at Cragholt Point whom Sebastian had been planning to visit anyway, following the margin notes like breadcrumbs, before any of this had detonated under their feet.

Their father had known her. Had trusted her. Had, apparently, arranged his own disappearance—or someone had arranged it for him—with instructions folded inside it like a note in a bottle.

Sebastian stood in the middle of the kitchen, the logbook in one hand and the manifest in the other, and felt the floor of their life tilt. Every story they had told themselves about their father reorganized itself around this new center of gravity. The man who drowned. The man whose death had taught Sebastian that the sea takes and takes and gives back nothing but salvage. He hadn't drowned. Or he had, but not the way the plaque said. He had gone *quiet* because he went *loud*—he had found the truth, had copied the documents, had hidden them in a room that doesn't flood, and had vanished into the same silence the navy had imposed on thirty-one names and one small girl.

Was he alive? Forty years? No. No, he'd be past seventy, and the sea doesn't grant sabbaticals. But the question wouldn't die, and it didn't matter anyway, because the answer that mattered was worse: their father had known. All those years Sebastian spent mourning a man taken by weather, the man had been taken by *choice*—or by conspiracy—and had left his child nothing but a riddle and a reputation for recklessness.

He had left them an inheritance. Just not the kind you cash.

* * *

Dawn was greying the window when Sebastian finally sat back down and forced themselves to think like a diver instead of a grieving child. Air, bottom time, ascent rate. One problem at a time, deepest problem first.

Deepest problem first: Corven Ashe. The dispatch case had come up out of a cave Ashe's sonar was circling like a shark testing a swimmer. When the fleet moved—and they would move; men like Ashe didn't buy drinks for a whole harbor out of generosity—they would find the strongroom opened, the inner door unlocked, the case gone. There was no version of this where Sebastian stayed invisible. Invisibility had cracked the day they asked the harbormaster about a wreck designation; it had shattered completely somewhere between the slashed fuel line and tonight's kitchen table.

Second problem: the documents. The order, the manifest, the annotations. Paper proof of murder, annotated across eleven years by a ghost. This wasn't treasure. Treasure you could sell, spend, lose, regret. This was testimony, and testimony has only two possible fates: it gets buried with the witness, or it gets spoken.

Third problem: Maren Oduya. *Look for M.O. She knows where the rest sleeps.* The rest of what? The rest of the documents? The rest of the survivors? The rest of the truth? And *tell her the tide keeps its promises*—what promise, made by whom, kept how?

Sebastian looked at the manifest one last time, at the child's name in the third column, at their father's pencil hovering beside it across four decades: *She lived.*

"You lived," Sebastian said quietly. "And he told me to find you. So I guess we're both keeping his appointments."

They packed the documents into a waterproof bag, sealed it, and hid it—not in the boat, not in the house, but in the one place Ashe's people would never think to search because no sane person would leave evidence there: the cold locker at the fish market, behind Tessa Vane's crates, under a layer of ice and herring guts. Then they stood at the window and watched the sun come up over Greyhaven Point, and calculated tides, and understood with total clarity that the next person they needed to see was an old lighthouse keeper at Cragholt—who might tell them where the rest slept, or might tell them something worse.

Something like who their father really was.

Something like whether he was ever really theirs at all.

The tide would turn at noon. Sebastian shrugged into their coat, pocketed the brass key Maren hadn't given them yet but was already waiting for, and went out into the cold morning to keep a dead man's appointment with the living.
