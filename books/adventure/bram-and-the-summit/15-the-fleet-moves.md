## 15. The Fleet Moves

The sonar didn't lie, and that was the problem.

Corven Ashe stood in the survey cabin of the *Merrow's Debt* with his arms folded and his jaw set, watching the third sweep of the morning paint the same beautiful, useless picture across the display. Greyhaven Point rose off the seabed like a broken tooth—sheer on the seaward face, scalloped with ledges, riddled with the kind of acoustic shadow that could hide a wreck the size of a chapel or nothing at all. Three days of grids. Fourteen passes. Two hundred thousand pounds of fuel, charter fees, and crew wages burning through his account like kerosene through a paper funnel.

And nothing. No hull signature. No mass of displaced metal consistent with a naval transport. No gold-shaped absence where a gold-shaped presence should be.

"Run it again," he said.

His sonar tech, a pale young man named Ilbury who had learned in three days to be afraid of him in the specific way Ashe preferred—quietly, without performance—reset the towfish without complaint. The winch groaned. The cable paid out over the stern into water the color of old pewter.

Ashe turned to the chart table. He had pinned the Admiralty charts together with dividers and red thread, the way he'd once seen an intelligence officer do it in another life, another sea, before he'd learned that governments kept their secrets in filing cabinets and men like him kept theirs in ledgers and graves. The Meridian's last recorded position was wrong—he'd known it was wrong for twenty years, ever since a dying deckhand in a Freetown bar had sold him a bearing and a name and died before he could sell it twice. But wrong in which direction? North put the wreck under two hundred feet of open water where his magnetometers would have found her by now. South put her inside the point itself, which was geologically absurd, hydrographically impossible, and exactly the sort of lie the Navy loved best: hide the truth somewhere no one would think to look because looking there was stupid.

He'd spent three days being stupid on schedule. He was done with it.

"Mr. Ilbury." He didn't raise his voice. He never needed to. "Overlay pass nine against pass eleven."

Ilbury did. The two sweeps flickered on the display, ghosting over each other—and there, at the base of the point where the sand met black rock, something shifted between them. Not a hull. A smear. A soft-edged anomaly where the return came back muddled, diffused, as though the sound were passing through something that wasn't water and wasn't stone.

"Sand drift," Ilbury offered. "Storm's been moving it all week."

"Sand doesn't eat returns." Ashe leaned closer. "Rock does. Or air." He straightened. "Air pockets, Mr. Ilbury. In a cave."

The word landed in the cabin and sat there. His first mate, Dreegan—a slab of a man with hands like mooring cleats and the imagination of a bollard—shifted his weight by the door.

"You're saying there's a cave in the point."

"I'm saying the tide went out further this week than it has in forty years, according to every fisherman I've bought drinks from since we anchored." Ashe tapped the anomaly with one finger. "I'm saying a low tide like that exposes things that stay drowned for a generation. And I'm saying that four days ago, a salvage diver with a rusted boat and no future was working this exact stretch of coast when it happened, and has not worked since."

Dreegan frowned. "You think the Fairweather kid found it."

"I don't think anything. Thinking is for men with time." Ashe rolled the chart closed. "I know the harbor master got a visit. I know the harbor master made a phone call he shouldn't have, to a number that isn't in any directory, and I know that call lasted ninety seconds and ended with the harbor master going home early and drinking more than usual. I know our friend Sebastian Fairweather asked questions about a ship designation that officially doesn't exist." He smiled, thin as a blade laid flat. "And I know that when I bought that boy a drink and offered good money for paperwork, they lied to my face with a smile so smooth you could have varnished a hull with it."

"They're nobody," Dreegan said. "A gypsy diver. One boat, half a tank of fuel, no crew."

"Nobody is exactly who finds things, Dreegan. Nobody is small enough to fit through the gaps the rest of us are too important to squeeze into." Ashe crossed to the porthole and looked out at Greyhaven Point, black against the grey afternoon, gulls wheeling around its crown like ash caught in a draft. "Somebody dove that cave. Somebody took something out of it. And somebody is going back, because people like that always go back—they can't afford not to."

* * *

He convened the fleet's skippers in the *Merrow's Debt*'s saloon at dusk, and he did it properly, because Corven Ashe had learned long ago that cruelty was cheap and discipline was expensive, and he paid for the expensive kind.

Six skippers. Six faces ranging from hungry to hostile. He fed them first—roast pork, real bread, whisky that hadn't seen the inside of a plastic bottle—and let them eat before he spoke, because men decide with their stomachs before their heads, and a man eating your food is already halfway to agreeing with you.

"We've burned a week," he began, standing at the head of the table with a glass he never drank from. "A week of grid work, a week of wages, and nothing to show but a smudge on a screen. Some of you are wondering if you backed the wrong horse. That's fair. So I'll tell you what I know, and then I'll tell you what we're going to do, and after that anyone who wants to weigh anchor and go home can do it tonight with their pay up to date and no hard feelings."

He let that sit. Nobody moved. Hunger is a leash.

"The Meridian is inside the point." He unrolled the chart and weighted its corners. "Not off it. In it. There's a cave system in the base of the rock, flooded at most tides, exposed at extreme lows—the kind of low we had four days ago, the kind that comes with the spring series. The Navy scuttled that ship with her cargo aboard and her witnesses aboard, and they chose this rock because it's the one place on this coast no salvage man would ever look, because looking here is a waste of time." He looked around the table slowly. "They counted on us being smart. Being smart is how they win."

One of the skippers, a leathery Cornishman named Polgrain, cleared his throat. "Even granting all that—even if she's in there—she's behind thirty feet of granite. You can't dive through rock."

"No," Ashe agreed pleasantly. "But you can remove rock."

The silence that followed had texture. Polgrain's face went through several stages of understanding, like a man watching a sunrise he didn't want to see.

"Blasting," someone said. "That's not salvage, that's—"

"That's engineering." Ashe set both palms flat on the table. "Controlled charges, placed by professionals, at slack water, timed to the tide series. We crack the outer lip of the cave mouth, widen it enough to run a diver or a small ROV through, and we take what's inside. The Navy buried a fortune in that rock and wrote it off. As far as any court will ever know, we're clearing a navigation hazard."

"And the diver?" It was Dreegan who asked it, blunt as a hammer. "If Fairweather's already found a way in—if they're down there when the charges go—"

Ashe held his gaze and let the pause do the work that words would only make uglier.

"Then Fairweather should have stayed in harbor," he said finally. "I offered that person a finder's fee. A generous one. I offered partnership, in writing, in front of witnesses. They chose to lie to me instead. Whatever happens to them now is arithmetic, not malice." He picked up his untouched glass and turned it slowly on the blotter. "I want that understood by everyone at this table. We are not murderers. We are salvors. If a man drowns because he swam into a blast zone he was warned away from—" he shrugged, a small economical motion, "—the sea takes people. It's what the sea does. Everyone at this table has lost someone to it. No one will be surprised."

No one spoke. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere above them, the wind had begun to find the rigging, worrying it with small cold fingers.

Polgrain broke first, the way old men always do when the choice is between their scruples and their debts. "What's the split?"

Ashe smiled, warm as lamplight, and named numbers that made six pairs of eyes shine, and just like that it was decided, and everyone in the room could pretend afterward that it had been inevitable, that they'd never had a choice, that the devil's arithmetic had always balanced itself.

* * *

He walked the quay alone that night, collar up against a wind that smelled of coming rain, and thought about fathers.

Not his own—that account had been closed decades ago, settled in full, the ledger torn out and burned. He thought about Sebastian Fairweather's father. The old man had logged these waters forty years back; Ashe had read the fragments of it secondhand, through a harbormaster who talked too much after two brandies, and the shape of it bothered him. A diver who knew the point. Who'd marked coordinates. Whose child now haunted the same rocks with the same hunger. Blood was the oldest chart there was, and it never wore out.

Which meant Fairweather wasn't guessing. They were following. And a person following a dead father's map through a drowned cave was a person who would keep going back until the sea or Ashe stopped them.

He stopped at the rail overlooking the harbor and watched the lights of Fairweather's boat riding at its mooring, small and stubborn against the dark water. Beside it, newer, faster, sat Tessa Vane's craft—because of course the smuggler had been drawn into it; everything on this coast eventually flowed toward everyone else, a harbor was just a whirlpool with bars along its edge. Ashe noted the pairing without surprise. Desperate divers and patient smugglers found each other the way iron finds lodestone.

Two days, he calculated. The next spring low was in two days, near dawn, and the tide series after it would close the cave for the season. Whatever Fairweather meant to do, they'd have to do it in those windows—which meant Ashe knew exactly where the diver would be, and exactly when, and the knowledge sat in him with a terrible clean comfort, like a loaded gun in a drawer.

He didn't hate Sebastian Fairweather. Hatred required regard, and regard was a luxury he'd stopped affording himself years ago. What he felt was closer to respect, the cold professional kind a chess player feels for an opponent who has found an unexpected line—and beneath that, fainter, almost unwelcome, something like recognition. He knew what it was to be poor and brilliant and owed by the world. He'd simply chosen, long ago, to collect.

The difference between them was that Fairweather still believed the truth was worth more than gold. Ashe had watched men die of that belief. It was, in his experience, the most expensive thing a person could own.

"Mr. Ashe." Dreegan's voice behind him, hushed against the wind. "Demolition contractor's aboard. Ex-navy engineer, comes recommended, asks no questions. Says he can place the charges in one window and fire them on the next. Wants paying half up front."

"Pay him." Ashe didn't turn from the rail. "And Dreegan—when the charges are placed, I want a watch on the cave mouth day and night. If Fairweather goes in during a window, no one stops them. No one warns them. And no one follows." He finally turned, and whatever was in his face made even Dreegan look away. "We let the tide do what tides do. If they come out, we talk again. If they don't—"

He left it there. Some sentences were better unfinished; they weighed less on the tongue and more on the listener.

"Aye," Dreegan said, and went.

Ashe stayed at the rail a while longer. Out past the breakwater, Greyhaven Point was a blackness deeper than the night around it, and the swell was beginning to lift and fall against its base with a slow, patient rhythm, like breathing, like waiting. The forecast said the storm would arrive with the spring tide. Wind against low water; the worst sea on this coast in a decade, the fishermen said, spitting over their shoulders.

Perfect conditions, Ashe thought, for no one to see anything.

He took out the folded page he'd carried for three days—the transcript of the harbor master's phone call, purchased from a telephone operator with a fondness for gin—and read the single line that mattered one more time. *Designation Meridian. File sealed. Advise the gentleman to forget the number.*

Advise. Not arrest. Not investigate. Advise.

Someone in the Admiralty was nervous, and nervous institutions did two things: they paid, and they buried. Ashe intended to present them with a finished fact—a hole in the rock, a hold full of bullion, a salvage claim filed before dawn—and let them choose which. Either way, Corven Ashe would walk away rich, and the dead would stay quiet, and the world would keep turning on the axis of money as it always had.

Below him, at the far end of the quay, a light moved. Small, hooded, quick. Someone boarding Fairweather's boat in the dark—or leaving it. He watched until the light vanished, unhurried, cataloguing it, and felt the last pretense fall away from himself like paint off a hull. There had been a week, once, when he might have called this salvage. When he might have told himself the story about navigation hazards and controlled engineering and the sea taking its own.

That week was over. Tomorrow the demolition gear came ashore. Tomorrow the charges went into the rock, and the day after, at slack water before the storm, the fleet would fire them, and Greyhaven Point would give up its dead whether it wished to or not—and anyone inside it, diver or ghost, would become part of the site.

He turned up his collar and walked back toward his ship, and behind him the tide began to turn, right on schedule, indifferent as God, faithful as arithmetic.
