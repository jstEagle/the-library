## 16. Tide Four: The Deep Hold

The tide turned at 3:47 in the morning, and Sebastian was in the water by 4:02.

They had wanted to wait for light. Every instinct honed across eleven years of salvage diving said *never go down on a black water*, never trust a sea you can't read from the surface. But the window was shrinking—tide four of six, and Ashe's survey boat had been anchored off Greyhaven Point since dusk, its running lights burning like patient eyes. Whatever the man intended, he intended it soon. Sebastian could feel it the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

So they went down in the dark, with Tessa Vane's borrowed boat idling above them and Tessa herself leaning over the rail, watching the water with an expression that said she'd seen divers not come back before and expected to see it again.

"Bottom time's your god now," she'd said, handing over the line. "Not me, not the gold, not whatever ghost is dragging you down there. You chase that clock or it kills you."

Sebastian had nodded and lied with their eyes, because they already knew the numbers were wrong. The father's logbook gave the hold at forty-one meters. Forty-one meters on mixed gas, cold North Sea water, through a flooded passage with two turns and a choke point wide enough for one diver and their pride—nothing about it. The tables allowed maybe fourteen minutes of workable bottom time if everything went perfectly.

Nothing ever went perfectly. That was practically the definition of the sea.

* * *

The descent through the antechamber was familiar now, almost insultingly so—the way the rock narrowed at three meters, the shelf where the air line had snagged on dive one, the deliberate arrangement of bones in the second chamber that Sebastian still made themselves look away from. The dead of the Meridian had been placed here, not drowned here, and no amount of diving hardened you against that particular knowledge. It sat in the back of the skull like a stone.

At the choke point, Sebastian paused, checked gauges, and did the arithmetic again as if repetition might improve it. Fourteen minutes. Maybe twelve, accounting for the current pushing against them in the inner passage—a current that shouldn't have been there at all, this far inside the rock, unless something below was breathing.

The father's logbook had a page for this. Sebastian had read it a dozen times by lantern light until the pencil strokes were more memory than ink:

*The hold lies past the third bulkhead. The vault ruptured when she went down—not torn open by the sea but by men, which tells you what you need to know about what she carried. Gold everywhere, if that's what you want. I took nothing. I couldn't carry what I actually needed and the gold both, and a man only has so many hands.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, pressed hard enough to score the paper:

*The captain kept his chest locked to his bunk frame. Starboard side, aft of the ladder. If anyone ever reads this: the chest matters. The gold doesn't. The gold is just the bait they used to make sure nobody looked too closely at the rest.*

Sebastian had spent three days telling themselves they believed that. Three days of looking at the ledger's estimate—two tons of bullion, enough to buy every debt they owed and every debt they'd ever incur—and repeating *the chest matters, the gold doesn't* like a prayer in a language they didn't speak.

Now, thirty meters down, with the cold pressing in like a hand, they would find out whether the words survived contact with the actual metal.

* * *

The passage into the hull opened at thirty-eight meters, and the Meridian swallowed them whole.

Even wrecked, even forty years drowned, the ship had presence. Sebastian's light swept across bulkheads furred white with anemones, across a corridor collapsed into itself, across the ghostly geometry of a vessel built to carry people—and then built, secretly, to end them. The sonar images Ashe's fleet had been chasing all week would show a shape here, a mass there. Only a diver got the truth of her: that the Meridian felt less like a ruin than a held breath.

The bullion vault was exactly where the logbook promised, and it stopped Sebastian's heart for one full, unforgivable second.

The door had been blown inward from the outside—cutting charges, clean and professional, decades before Ashe was born. Beyond it, the light found metal. Not scattered coin, not loose bars, but stacked ingots in rotted crates, whole pallets of them slumped against each other like sleeping animals, gleaming dully under the silt of four decades. A rupture in the deck plating above let a column of grey light fall through the water, and where it landed, the gold seemed almost warm.

Sebastian hung in the water and understood, finally, why men killed for this. Not greed, exactly. It was the *certainty* of it. Everything else in a salvage diver's life was contingency—weather, tides, luck, the harbor master's mood—but this was solid. This was weight you could hold. This was the sea finally paying what it owed.

Their hand was on a bar before they knew they'd moved it. Cold, heavy, real. One ingot. They could rig a lift bag, take one, be out in—

The gauge needle caught their eye. Nine minutes of bottom time left. The swim out alone would eat five.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the other voice—the one that sounded like Maren Oduya saying *they paid us to forget, and some of us took the money, and God forgive me, I was a child and I took it*. The gold had bought fifty years of silence. Every ingot on these pallets had a name attached to it, someone who'd been aboard when the scuttling order came down, someone whose family had gotten an envelope instead of an answer.

If Sebastian took the gold, they became the envelope.

They let the bar slide from their fingers. It settled back onto the pile with a sound like a coffin lid.

"The chest," they said aloud, and the regulator turned it into a meaningless burble, but saying it helped. "The chest matters."

* * *

Starboard side, aft of the ladder. The father's directions were good—better than good, precise in the way of a man who had been here before and had time to measure. Sebastian followed the corridor aft, light sweeping, fins stirring up clouds that turned the beam into milk. Twice they passed human shapes in the sediment and refused to look twice. There would be time for grief ashore. Down here, grief was just another thing that could drown you.

The captain's cabin was a collapsed jaw of twisted steel, but the bunk frame had been bolted to the hull rather than the deck, and it had held. And there, fused to the frame by corrosion and time, sat a chest the size of a sea chest's meaner cousin—iron-banded, brass-cornered, sealed with a naval wax stamp that had somehow survived underwater like a small stubborn miracle.

Sebastian worked the chest free with a pry bar and ten minutes they did not have. The corrosion fought them. Their hands, numb inside the gloves, fought them. At one point the pry bar slipped and gashed the chest's lid and Sebastian's own knuckle, and they watched their blood bloom pink into the water and thought, with strange calm: *that's the toll. The sea always takes a little blood.*

Four minutes. The chest came free with a groan of parting rust.

It weighed perhaps twenty kilos—manageable, brutal, real. Sebastian clipped it to their harness, checked the attachment twice, and turned for the exit.

That was when the first charge went off.

* * *

It wasn't close. That was the horror of it—it wasn't close at all, and it still came through the water like a fist through paper. The pressure wave hit Sebastian's chest, their mask, their teeth, a physical blow that had nothing to do with sound. Silt exploded off every surface at once, and the grey light from the ruptured deck plating flickered and died as something shifted far above.

Ashe's survey charges. Firing early—or firing on purpose, testing the rock, mapping the cave's bones with small murders. Either way, the message was identical: the fleet had stopped pretending.

Sebastian swam.

The corridor back toward the vault junction was filling with silt, visibility dropping from bad to nothing, and Sebastian navigated by touch and memory and the counted rhythm of kicks—*left turn at the bulkhead, twelve kicks to the choke point, don't think about the ceiling, don't think about the ceiling.* The gauge said two minutes of bottom time, which meant nothing anymore, because the bottom time assumed a world that still obeyed tables.

A second charge, deeper, closer. The hull rang like a struck bell, and somewhere behind Sebastian a section of corridor let go—not collapse, not yet, but a slow exhalation of steel and stone, the ship settling into a new shape, closing doors that had stood open for forty years.

The choke point. Sebastian reached it and the chest jammed.

Of course it did. Of course. Twenty kilos of iron destiny catching on rock at the exact width of a diver's shoulders, while the water behind them darkened and the pressure waves came faster now, irregular, panicky—Ashe's crews firing without pattern, or the rock itself beginning to answer.

Sebastian did not panic. Later, they would not be able to explain this. There was simply a moment where fear arrived, was acknowledged, and was set aside like a tool no longer needed, and what remained was the flat calm of a person who has decided how they want to die and discovered it isn't here.

They turned the chest on its edge. Fed it through like a midwife. Scraped skin off their shoulder doing it, felt the suit tear, felt the cold find the wound and write its signature there. Then they pulled themselves through after it, regulator rasping, and the passage behind them sighed shut with a sound like sand through an hourglass.

Five kicks. Ten. The antechamber. The shelf where the line had snagged on dive one snagged again, because the sea keeps its habits, and Sebastian tore free of it with a violence that would have shamed them ashore.

Then—light. Not the torch. Daylight, thin and green and impossibly beautiful, filtering down from the cave mouth as the tide bottomed out, and Sebastian rose through it toward the surface with the chest dragging at their harness like a sin they'd chosen to keep.

* * *

Tessa hauled them over the gunwale by the harness straps, swearing with a fluency that suggested genuine feeling, and for a long moment Sebastian just lay on the deck of the borrowed boat, breathing air that tasted like diesel and salvation, watching the sky do its ordinary business of being sky.

"You're bleeding," Tessa said.

"I'm rich," Sebastian said, and then, hearing how it sounded, corrected: "No. I'm not. That's the point."

They sat up. Below them, through the green water, a dull concussion rolled up from the deep—another charge, farther off now, or the rock finishing its own sentence. A plume of silt bloomed at the base of Greyhaven Point like smoke from a chimney, and Tessa watched it with narrowed eyes.

"He's not salvaging," she said quietly. "You know that, right? Nobody blasts a site they intend to dive. He's not trying to get in." She looked at Sebastian, and for the first time since the bargain at the quay, there was something in her face that wasn't calculation. "He's trying to bury it. And everyone inside."

Sebastian's hand found the chest. Through the torn glove, they could feel the brass corners, the wax seal, the weight of names.

"Then we'd better open it," they said, "before he finishes the job."

Behind them, out past the headland, the first grey edge of dawn touched the water—and with it, running low and fast, came the silhouette of a second boat flying no lights at all.
