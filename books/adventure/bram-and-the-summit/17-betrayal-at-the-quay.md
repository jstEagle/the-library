## 17. Betrayal at the Quay

Tessa Vane had spent thirty years learning the exact weight of a lie, and she could feel this one coming before it arrived.

It came in the shape of two men who didn't belong at Greyhaven's quay. She clocked them from the wheelhouse of the *Kestrel* while she was coiling line—coiling line being the universal occupation of people who wanted to look busy and see everything. They came down the ramp in matching grey coats that had never once been wet, shoes too clean for the fish guts and diesel sheen of the boards, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who had never been told *no* by anything smaller than a government.

They stopped at her gangway. Of course they did.

"Miss Vane." The taller one had a voice like a filing cabinet closing. He held up an identification wallet so briefly it was almost a courtesy—a flash of navy blue, an embossed crest, a name she was meant to forget. "Lieutenant Commander Hale. This is my colleague, Mr. Pryce. Might we come aboard?"

"You might not," Tessa said pleasantly. "This is a working vessel."

"So is a submarine," said the shorter one—Pryce—and smiled like he'd rehearsed it in front of a mirror until it stopped meaning anything. "We'd rather do this the easy way. But we're flexible."

She set down the coil. Slowly. Her hands were steady because she made them be; thirty years of running cargo that wasn't on any manifest had taught her that the moment your hands shook, you'd already lost. Below her feet, the *Kestrel*'s hull ticked against its fenders, and somewhere under the harbor floor, in a cave the tide was slowly swallowing again, Sebastian Fairweather was either alive or dead, and Tessa Vane was about to find out which side of that question she was standing on.

* * *

They sat in her cramped cabin because refusing twice would have been a statement, and statements were what got smugglers audited, arrested, or occasionally drowned. Hale took the bench seat across from her chart table as though he'd measured it first. Pryce stayed standing by the door, which told her which one of them did the talking when talking stopped working.

"We understand you've recently entered into an arrangement with Sebastian Fairweather," Hale began.

"I enter arrangements with everyone. It's called commerce."

"The arrangement involving your vessel. The *Kestrel*. Berthed here since Tuesday, fueled Wednesday night, provisioned Thursday morning for a dive profile nobody in this harbor has filed paperwork for." He laid a photograph on the chart table: the *Kestrel* at her mooring, taken from the cliff road, timestamped yesterday. On the quay beside her, blurred but unmistakable, Sebastian hauling tanks. "We also understand Mr.—or Miss; the records are charmingly inconsistent—Fairweather has been diving Greyhaven Point."

Tessa looked at the photograph for a long moment, then up at Hale, and let her face do what it did best, which was nothing at all. "Greyhaven Point is a rock with a lighthouse on it. People dive there all the time."

"People dive the shallows. Fairweather has been diving deep. Past the safe limit, if our information is correct." Hale folded his hands. "Here is my difficulty, Miss Vane. There is a wreck off that point that does not officially exist. Anything recovered from it is crown property. Anyone assisting in its recovery is committing an offense I am authorized to resolve either through cooperation—" he gestured mildly at the cabin around them, "—or through the less pleasant mechanisms available to me. Your boat. Your fuel. Your silence. All of it purchasable, one way or another."

"And if I say I don't know where they are?"

Pryce laughed softly from the door. It was not a kind sound.

Hale didn't laugh at all. He reached into his coat and produced a second document, unfolded it, and turned it to face her. It was a customs ledger page. Her customs ledger page—or rather, a copy of three years of them, annotated in red ink with discrepancies she'd have sworn no one could find. Run numbers. Cargo weights that didn't match manifests. A route in March two years ago she'd thought was buried deeper than the *Meridian* itself.

"Miss Vane," Hale said gently, "you are a woman who understands leverage. So let us not insult each other by pretending there isn't any. Tell us where Fairweather dives next, and this file goes into an incinerator. Refuse, and it goes to a prosecutor who owes me a favor, along with a charge sheet I can write before lunch. You'll lose the boat. You'll lose the harbor. And Fairweather will still be found—we have six boats and sonar and time—but you'll have gained nothing except company in the ruin."

The cabin was very quiet. Water slapped the hull. Somewhere above, a gull screamed once and gave up.

Tessa looked at the customs pages. Three years of work. The *Kestrel* was the only thing she'd ever owned outright, the only thing her father had left her besides a talent for lying and a distrust of uniforms, and both of those were in this room right now, wearing grey coats.

And underneath the fear, cold and clear as the water over the cave, was the other thing. The thing she'd been carrying since the night she'd struck the bargain with Sebastian on this same quay—their handshake rough with rope burn, their eyes holding hers with that particular stubborn honesty that poor divers get when they've decided to bet their life on something. *First claim on anything non-naval,* they'd said. *And your silence.* And Tessa had given her word, and Tessa Vane's word was the only currency she'd never counterfeited, because the day it became counterfeit she'd be just another broker in a coat, and there were enough of those.

But her word hadn't counted on this. Hadn't counted on the navy knowing about the cave, or caring, or having photographs and files and the patience of an institution that outlived everyone it squeezed.

"They're diving the inner passage," she heard herself say. "Through the blowhole side. Tide five—tomorrow evening, the storm window. That's when they go."

Hale wrote nothing down. He simply nodded, the way a man nods when he already knew and only needed to know whether she'd tell him. That nod frightened her more than the file had.

"Pryce will remain in the harbor," Hale said, rising. "Purely as an observer. If your conscience troubles you, Miss Vane, consider that you've just saved a diver's life. We intend to bring them up safely. What happens afterward is paperwork."

He paused at the door, and for one unguarded second something moved behind his eyes—not quite regret, but its distant cousin. "For what it's worth, I read the file on the Meridian once. The real one. There are things in it I'd rather my daughter never learned her country did." He straightened his coat. "Which is why it stays closed. Good day."

Then they were gone, down the ramp, past the fish sheds, two clean coats dissolving into the grey of the town, and Tessa Vane sat alone in her cabin with her father's boat rocking beneath her and the taste of the words still in her mouth like bilge water.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She tried the usual remedies—rum, engine maintenance, a long argument with herself conducted aloud in the empty wheelhouse—but every remedy circled back to the same fixed point: tomorrow evening, storm window, tide five, and somewhere beneath the boiling grey water of Greyhaven Point, Sebastian Fairweather would descend into a flooded cave while two navy intelligence officers and whatever force they'd quietly requisitioned waited at the entrance like spiders at a drain.

*We intend to bring them up safely.* Hale had said it like a kindness. But Tessa had watched men say *safely* before, and she'd noticed that the ones who said it most smoothly were always the ones whose definition of the word had fine print. Sebastian had the dispatch case. Sebastian had the admiralty order, the manifest, the proof of a scuttling the navy had spent forty years denying. Men like Hale didn't bring evidence up safely. They brought it up, and then the evidence stopped existing, and sometimes the diver stopped existing alongside it, regrettable but tidy, an accident in bad weather during an unauthorized dive.

She stood at the wheelhouse windows most of the night, watching the harbor breathe. Toward dawn the wind came around northeast and started dragging cloud off the sea in long torn banners, and the barometer on her bulkhead began its slow sick fall, and she thought: *storm window.* Sebastian would dive in that. Sebastian would dive in anything, because Sebastian had a father's handwriting leading them down and a dead ship's testimony waiting below, and some people couldn't be talked out of their inheritance even at gunpoint.

Tessa had been many things in her life—thief, courier, liar, occasional hero by accident—but she had never yet been the person who fed someone to the machinery and slept well after. She poured the last of the rum into her tea, drank it standing up, and made the only decision left that didn't rot.

She couldn't warn Sebastian directly. Pryce would be watching the harbor; her radio might already be monitored; a note under a door was how amateurs got people killed. No. It had to be something Sebastian would understand and no one else would—something written in the private language of the bargain they'd struck on this quay.

She went below and dug through the locker under her bunk until she found it: the signal flags, rolled in oilcloth, inherited with the boat. Among them, the code flags proper sailors used and smugglers mostly ignored. She pulled out two.

November. *You are running into danger.*

Oscar. *Man overboard.*

No—that was too plain, too legible to a naval eye. She sat back on her heels and thought harder, and what came to her was not flags at all but the night of the bargain, the way Sebastian had checked her compass twice before shaking on it, the way they'd talked about the old wreck buoys their father used to mark dive sites—*red can means stay out, white stick with black band means the site's hot, someone's watching.* Harbor children's rules. Divers' rules. Rules older than the navy's interest in this cove.

There was a white stick in her deck locker, cut for a jury-rigged antenna mount last spring. She took it out, wrapped a band of tarred canvas around its middle, and stood looking at it. Crude. Obvious, to anyone who knew the code. Invisible to anyone who didn't—which, thank God, described every officer the navy had sent to Greyhaven in forty years, because men in clean coats did not learn fishermen's signals.

At first light, when the harbor was still blue-grey and empty and Pryce's rented skiff bobbed innocently at the far moorings, Tessa ran the *Kestrel* out past the breakwater on the pretext of testing her engine in the building swell. Off Greyhaven Point, where the rocks showed their teeth between swell-sets, she slowed, dropped the marked stick onto the ledge above the high-tide line where any diver surfacing from the cave mouth would see it first thing, and left it standing in the stones like a small pale sentinel.

*Site's hot. Someone's watching. Stay down, stay away, don't surface here.*

Then she opened the throttle and brought the boat home, heart hammering, face composed, and found Pryce waiting on the quay with coffee from the harbor kiosk and a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Nice morning for a trial run," he said.

"Every morning's nice when you own the boat," Tessa said, and tossed him a line to make himself useful, and hated him, and hated herself more, and did not let a single muscle of it show.

* * *

By noon the storm had teeth. The harbor master ran up the small-craft warning, the fleet came in to double their lines, and the whole town battened itself down with the practiced rhythm of people who had done this a hundred times. Through it all Tessa worked—checking lashings, pumping bilges, being loudly, visibly ordinary—while inside her chest a war raged with no honorable outcome on either side.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, no matter how she arranged it: she had sold Sebastian's location, and the warning stick might not be enough. If the navy sealed the cave mouth from outside—if they posted boats over the entrance during the dive window—Sebastian would come up into a net, or worse, wouldn't come up at all, and the stick on the ledge would be a gravemarker instead of a lifeline. A warning wasn't rescue. A warning was just betrayal with better manners.

Unless.

She stood in the wheelhouse as the rain came slanting in off the north, watching the press of grey water over the point, and let herself think the thought she'd been circling all night. There was one person in this harbor who owed her, whom she trusted exactly as far as she could throw and precisely because of it. Someone with a fast boat, no love for the navy, and a sentimental streak she'd deny under torture. Someone who could stand off the point during tide five, engines warm, riding the storm line where no government vessel would dare loiter—and be there when Sebastian surfaced, if Sebastian surfaced, a door out of the trap that Hale's clean-coated arithmetic hadn't accounted for.

Tessa Vane had traded away Sebastian's location to save her boat. Fine. Then she'd spend the boat buying it back.

She went below, took down the battered handheld radio she kept for conversations no base station should hear, and keyed it twice—the old harbor signal, the one that meant *meet me, alone, now.* Out past the breakwater, through the rain-smeared glass, she watched the storm build over Greyhaven Point, and beneath her feet the *Kestrel* trembled at her lines like something alive that knew what was coming.

"Hold on, Fairweather," she murmured to the water, to the cave, to whoever was listening down in the dark. "I sold you cheap. Let me see what I can get on resale."

The barometer fell another notch. Out at the point, the sea was already turning the color of iron, and the tide was beginning to think about turning with it.
