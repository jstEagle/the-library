## 18. Storm Surge

The barometer had been falling all afternoon, and by the time Sebastian tied off at the cave mouth, the sky over Greyhaven Point had gone the color of wet slate. Tide five. The second-to-last window before the sea closed the Devil's Ledger for the season—and the last one Sebastian intended to use.

The radio had been screaming since noon. *Storm force winds from the southwest, seas building to five meters offshore, all small craft advised to seek shelter.* Every sensible boat in Greyhaven harbor was already hauled out or double-moored. Sebastian's borrowed boat—Tessa Vane's *Kestrel*, fast and light and worth more than everything Sebastian owned—rode at anchor in the lee of the point with a scope of chain that would either hold or it wouldn't.

There was no other choice. Ashe's survey charges had torn through the outer passage on tide four, and whatever the blasts hadn't collapsed, they'd cracked open like a struck bell. The strongroom was exposed now, its inner door hanging off Maren Oduya's brass lock, and the captain's chest already sat wrapped and dry in the *Kestrel*'s forward locker. But the father's logbook—the restored one, the one with the annotations dated after his official death—had marked one more thing inside the cave. A sealed compartment behind the bullion vault. A final cache. And a line in faded pencil that Sebastian hadn't understood until tonight:

*If the water comes early, don't go back the way you came. The blowhole breathes on the quarter hour.*

Sebastian had thought it was about diving. Standing at the cave mouth with the first spit of rain stinging their face and the swell already running long and green beneath them, they understood it was about exactly this.

The entry was still doable. Barely. The low tide window would be short—forty minutes, maybe less, before the surge turned—and the storm would compress it further, shoving the sea inland hours ahead of schedule. Sebastian checked the dive kit twice, checked the knife, checked the backup torch clipped inside the suit where a snagged line couldn't take it, and went down the rock ladder into the throat of the cave as the first real gusts began to moan across the point above.

* * *

Inside, the cave had changed.

The navy's old antechamber, the one Sebastian had crawled through on the first day, was gone—collapsed into rubble by Ashe's charges, a fan of shattered basalt blocking the original route entirely. The air pocket beyond it remained, domed and dripping, lit gray-green by Sebastian's torch beam, but the geometry was wrong now, unfamiliar, like walking into your own house after someone has rearranged the walls in the dark.

Sebastian swam the flooded inner passage toward the vault level, following the father's margin notes by rote: *past the third rib of rock, down two fathoms, port side where the hull split.* The water was colder than it should have been. Storm pressure pushing deep, stirring up silt from the wreck field outside until the beam of the torch barely cut three meters. Diving alone, in surge, at the edge of no-decompression limits, in a collapsing cave—every rule Sebastian had ever learned said turn back.

But the water was already rising behind them. Sebastian could feel it in the way the passage breathed—a slow, rhythmic push from the seaward end, each pulse stronger than the last, the ocean clearing its throat.

The captain's chest had been the prize. This last compartment was something else. The logbook called it *the paymaster's safe*, and the single line beside it read: *He kept more than money. Ask Maren what he carried for her mother.*

Sebastian found it wedged behind a shelf of fallen plating, a rusted iron box smaller than a bread loaf, its dial fused with corrosion. No time to work the mechanism underwater. Sebastian jammed the pry bar into the seam, felt the hinge scream and give, and stuffed the whole thing—box, contents, whatever fifty years of salt had left of them—into the mesh bag strapped to their harness.

That was when the first charge of the surge hit.

It came through the flooded passage like a train through a tunnel—not water anymore but water *with intent*, a brown-black fist that picked Sebastian up off the seabed and threw them against the rib of rock. The torch spun away on its lanyard, strobing wild circles. The regulator hose cracked against stone. For ten seconds there was nothing in the world but noise and pressure and the animal certainty of drowning, and then it passed, sucking back out toward the sea, dragging silt and loose rock with it, leaving Sebastian clinging to a stanchion with blood thinning pinkly from one knuckle and the taste of copper and fear at the back of the throat.

The passage behind—the way back to the air pocket, the way back to everything—was filling. Fast. The storm surge wasn't waiting for the tide table. It was coming now, hours early, and every pulse shoved the waterline higher up the walls of the inner cave.

*Don't go back the way you came.*

Sebastian pulled themselves up onto the last dry ledge below the vault level, ripped off the mask, and shouted into the dark just to hear a human voice: "All right. All right. Think."

The logbook. Waterproof case, inside the suit. Fingers shaking too badly to work the zipper—Sebastian bit it open, dragged the book out, and held the torch beam on the page with the cave chart. Their father's hand, forty years old, precise even in pencil: the cave drawn in cross-section, the flooded passages inked blue, and then—there, in the upper corner, almost lost in the mildew bloom—a narrow chimney of rock rising from the vault level to the surface, marked with three small crosses and a single word:

*Breathe.*

And beneath it, in handwriting that had gone hurried, scratched hard enough to tear the paper:

*Quarter hour. Count the swells. If you miss it, you die waiting. — E.F.*

E.F. Elias Fairweather. The father who had officially drowned decades ago. Who had stood on this same ledge, or one like it, with this same storm in the rock overhead, and left a map out of hell for whoever came looking.

"Thanks, Dad," Sebastian whispered, and it came out half laugh, half sob.

* * *

The climb started well and got worse.

The chimney was exactly what the chart promised—a vertical fissure in the cave roof, wide enough for shoulders, not much more, rising through twenty feet of wet black stone. Sebastian clipped the mesh bag tight, killed the main torch to save it, and climbed by the small backup light clenched in their teeth, testing every hold twice because the rock was weeping with spray and grew slicker by the minute.

Halfway up, the cave spoke.

Not the surge this time—something else. A deep, grinding groan from the seaward end, the sound of tons of basalt shifting on tons of basalt, and then a concussion that Sebastian felt in the sternum rather than heard. Dust and grit rained down the chimney. Somewhere below, the water rose another foot with a sound like a door slamming.

Ashe's charges. Or the storm. Or the two of them working together, the demolition crew above and the ocean below, squeezing the cave shut like a fist closing around a fly. Sebastian pressed their forehead against the cold rock and made themselves breathe—in four, hold, out four, the way the dive instructors taught, the way grief counselors taught, the way anyone who had ever been buried alive in anything learned—and climbed.

The top of the chimney opened into a low chamber, and the chamber had a ceiling of water.

That was the trick of the blowhole. From below it looked like drowning waiting to happen—a smooth lens of green-gray sea pressing down on the chamber's highest point, flexing with every swell, brightening and dimming like something alive. From above, somewhere beyond it, was open water off the seaward face of Greyhaven Point. The father's crosses marked the timing: when the big swells drew back, the pressure eased, the lens thinned, and for a few seconds the hole *exhaled*—a burst of trapped air punching upward through the water, a window a diver could ride if they hit it exactly right.

Miss it, and the returning surge would slam you back down the chimney or pin you against the lip until your air ran out.

Sebastian floated just beneath the lens and listened.

Count the swells. The father had written it like a man who'd done it himself. Sebastian pressed an ear to the water and felt them come through the rock—long, heavy pulses, the storm's heartbeat transmitted through fifty fathoms of sea. One. Two. Three. Learning the rhythm the way you learn a stranger's breathing in a dark room. The big ones came every seventh or eighth, and between the big ones, smaller pulses that meant nothing, false windows, traps.

Air check. Enough for one attempt, maybe two if panic didn't eat it. The mesh bag with the paymaster's safe knocked against their hip with each swell, absurdly heavy, and some cold practical part of Sebastian's mind said *drop it, drop it, you can't climb out of a blowhole dragging iron*—but Maren's mother's name was in that box, or had been once, and Sebastian had spent this entire season learning the difference between treasure and testimony.

They kept it.

On the ninth swell—the big one, the one that made the whole chamber groan and the lens above thin to a trembling sheet—Sebastian exhaled, kicked, and rose.

The world became violence. Green light, white churn, the roar of the blowhole exhaling around them like a whale spouting, and then—air. Actual air, rain-lashed and howling, as the surge hurled them up and out through the vent and clear of the rock in a column of spray. Sebastian hit open water beyond the point like a dropped doll, tumbled, surfaced gasping, and was immediately slammed under again by the next wave.

But they were outside. Outside, in a sea gone insane, in a storm with no mercy in it—but outside, with sky above instead of stone, and the black bulk of Greyhaven Point rearing to starboard through the rain.

* * *

Getting to shore took everything the sea had left unspent.

Sebastian rode the swells in toward the rocks on the leeward side, taking the hits, swallowing half the Irish Sea, hands shredded on barnacles when the waves finally drove them onto the shingle below the cliff path. They crawled out of the surf on all fours, vomited seawater, lay on their back in the rain laughing at the sky with a sound that didn't belong to any sane person, while the storm leaned on them and the lightning walked along the horizon like something pacing a cage.

Then they saw the lights.

Up on the cliff road, maybe four hundred meters north: vehicle headlights, three sets, parked in a staggered line with their beams pointed seaward. And below them, at the mouth of the cave—Sebastian's stomach dropped through the shingle—at the mouth of the Devil's Ledger, figures moving in the strobe of work lamps. Big figures. Men in oilskins hauling equipment, and among the equipment, unmistakable even through the rain, the tripod legs and coiled cable of seismic charges.

Ashe hadn't sent divers in behind Sebastian. He'd done simpler arithmetic. Whatever was in the cave, the storm would seal it—and if the storm left anything findable, his men would crack the entrance open at dawn and take it. Sebastian had been allowed to go in because going in was never the danger. Coming out was. And Corven Ashe had watched the blowhole side of the point too—he'd have men posted there, or he'd have the whole cliff road locked, because a man like Ashe did not leave exits unwatched.

Except.

Sebastian lay flat on the shingle, rain hammering their back, and looked at the layout with the cold clarity that adrenaline leaves behind like residue. The cliff path ran south from the cave mouth toward Greyhaven village—toward the harbor, the *Kestrel*, the captain's chest, the dispatch case, the manifest with its names. Toward the truth, sitting in a boat locker a mile away while the people who wanted it buried stood four hundred meters from it with explosives.

The figures at the cave mouth were sealing the north approach. The blowhole vent above Sebastian still smoked faintly with each exhale, a ghost-plume of spray visible even in the dark. If anyone was watching the seaward face—

A flashlight beam swept the shingle thirty meters to the south. Sebastian flattened, heart slamming, rain masking everything, and waited through three full passes of the light before it swung away toward the cliff stairs.

One watcher. Maybe two. Between Sebastian and the path.

The father's logbook, clutched against their chest inside the sodden suit, had gotten them out of the mountain. It had one more thing to give. Because Sebastian remembered now—remembered the sketch on the last annotated page, the one they'd taken for a diver's route marker: a dotted line running from the blowhole terrace along the cliff face, under the overhang, to a gully the locals called the Cut, and beside it, in that same hurried posthumous hand:

*Old smugglers' path. Tessa Vane's grandfather used it. She'll know.*

Of course she would. Of course the whole coast was stitched together with these hidden threads—smugglers and keepers and salvagers, everyone the sea had ever employed, passing routes and keys and silences down like heirlooms. Sebastian had thought they were racing Ashe through the water. They'd been inside a network the whole time, a web older than the Meridian, and every strand led back to the same question their father must have asked on some storm-black night fifty years ago:

Who do you trust with the truth?

Sebastian got to their feet, bent double against the wind, and started along the base of the cliff toward the shadow of the overhang, counting the watcher's flashlight sweeps, waiting for the dark between them.

Behind them, muffled by rock and rain, came a low rumble—the first dawn charge being test-fired, or the cave giving up its dead. Ahead, a mile south through the storm, the *Kestrel* rode at anchor with the evidence in her locker, and somewhere between here and there, Corven Ashe's men were learning that the fly had escaped the web.

The last tide turned at dawn. Sebastian intended to be standing on the cliff above Greyhaven Point when it did—with everything.
