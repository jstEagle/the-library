## 19. Confrontation at Greyhaven

The storm gave Sebastian one gift on the climb: the wind at their back, shoving them up the goat track like a hand between the shoulder blades, as if the sea itself had decided to finish what it started.

They climbed with the flare pistol in their waistband and the captain's chest strapped to their back in a salvage harness, and by the time they hauled themselves over the lip of the cliff their arms were shaking and their lungs burned with salt. They rolled behind a wind-scoured hawthorn, the only thing that grew up here, and lay in the wet grass getting their breath back and counting.

Two men at the cave mouth below. A third on the ridge to the north with a flashlight he kept sweeping in slow, confident arcs, the way men sweep when they believe no one is coming. Beyond them, on the flat ground where the old coastguard station used to stand, the operation: a generator tent flapping in the gale, crates of survey charges stacked under tarps, and Corven Ashe's flagship trawler, the *Iron Meridian*—Sebastian had to admire the arrogance of the name—riding at anchor a quarter mile offshore, her deck cranes silhouetted against the graying east.

Dawn was coming. Not the dawn the charts promised—clean and gold—but a bruised, boiling light pushing up through the storm cloud like a bruise through skin. The last tide. Sebastian could feel it turning in their bones the way their father had claimed he could, the way Sebastian had once thought was superstition. The sea was drawing breath for the final surge, and in an hour the cave at Greyhaven Point would flood for the season and keep its secrets until spring.

If there was still a cave. The rumble they'd heard at the cliff base had not stopped entirely—faint now, rhythmic, like a giant's slow heartbeat. Ashe's crews were setting charges inside the antechamber. Not to open the site. To close it. To bring the roof down on whatever the sonar had missed and whatever the fly had taken.

Sebastian checked the flare pistol. One cartridge. They'd brought two; the second had drowned in the blowhole passage, along with most of their hope, somewhere in the black hour before dawn. One cartridge. The navy's patrol boat was forty minutes out at best, if Tessa's warning had reached them, if Tessa's warning had been real, if the woman who'd sold them out had actually sold them back. The press boat was a rumor. The wind was a certainty. And Corven Ashe was fifty feet below, watching the tide come in with his hands in his coat pockets like a man admiring his own garden.

Sebastian got up out of the grass and walked down the slope with the flare pistol held low and steady, and let him hear their boots on the rock.

* * *

Ashe turned slowly. He didn't reach for anything, didn't startle, didn't do any of the things a surprised man does. He simply rotated on his heels the way a lighthouse turns, and when his eyes found Sebastian they were calm, interested, almost warm.

"Fairweather." He had to half-shout against the wind, but he made it sound conversational. "You look terrible. The blowhole, I assume. My grandfather used to swear by that route. Said it was the only honest door on this coast—it only opens for people who've got nothing left to lose."

The two men at the cave mouth had straightened. One had a hand inside his jacket. Sebastian kept the flare pistol level and their voice flat.

"Call them off."

"They're not on anyone, as far as I can see." Ashe smiled. Rain ran off the brim of his cap. Up close, in the gray light, he looked older than he had in the harbor bar—there were lines around his eyes that charm had papered over, and Sebastian understood suddenly that the charm was the papering. That underneath it was something tired and cold that had been at this a very long time. "You've had a hard night. Why don't you put that thing down before you hurt yourself, and we'll talk like professionals."

"Call them off, or I put a flare through the charge crates."

Ashe's eyes flicked—just once, just briefly—toward the tarped stack by the generator tent, and Sebastian saw the calculation land. Flare pistol, incendiary round, forty feet of drift and a gale to carry the spark. The math was simple and Ashe was very good at math.

"Ollie. Danny. Walk up to the trawler and make tea." His voice didn't rise. The men hesitated for half a heartbeat, looking at Sebastian, and Ashe said, "Now," in a tone that closed the discussion like a door, and they went, picking their way up the path with their hands visible, not looking back.

Then it was just the two of them on the cliff, and the wind, and the sea below beginning its long gray inhale.

"You went through the strongroom," Ashe said. Not a question. "The inner door. We found it blown open this morning. Old brass lock—wartime make. I've been wondering for two days how you were going to get through that, and then I remembered the old keeper. Oduya." He shook his head, almost admiring. "You went and saw the keeper. I had her cottage watched for a week and wrote her off as nothing. That's the lesson, isn't it. The paperwork is always the weapon. Everyone else goes looking for gold."

"You've read the dispatch case," Sebastian said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "You know what's in it. You know what the order says. And you're still going to drop the roof on the cave."

"I'm going to *stabilize* the site," Ashe said mildly. "Unstable rock, storm damage, tragic loss of a heritage wreck that no one can prove was ever anything else. The sea does the rest. The sea's been covering for the Admiralty for forty years, Fairweather. I'm just giving it a hand." He stepped closer, and Sebastian didn't step back, and the two of them stood close enough that Sebastian could see the rain beading on his eyelashes. "Here's what I actually came to say. Because I did come to say it—I had my men leave the south path open all night. You noticed. You're not a fool. I wanted you to have the option."

"The open path was an invitation."

"The open path was an *option*." Ashe spread his hands. "Here's my arithmetic. That chest on your back is worth—what do you think? The manifest, the order, the annotations in your father's hand. As evidence, it's worth a fortune in leverage. As paper, it's worth nothing. Paper needs institutions. Courts, inquiries, journalists with budgets, ministers with consciences. You know what you have? A borrowed boat, a dead father, and a lighthouse keeper who'll be dead before the first hearing. And me." He smiled again, and it was, Sebastian had to admit, a magnificent smile. It reached all the way to his eyes. That was the horror of it. "Versus me. I have three vessels, a legal team, contracts with two governments, and forty years of practice making inconvenient things disappear. Partner with me, and the Meridian comes up legal, clean, and *rich*. Your father's name cleared in the process—I'll see to it, I'm good at that too. You'll get a finder's percentage that buys Greyhaven Point and every point around it. The families get their compensation fund, generous, public, tax-deductible. Everyone eats. The dead get their memorial. The truth gets—" he shrugged, rain flying off his shoulders— "a footnote. Which is more truth than the dead ever got before."

The tide was turning. Sebastian could hear it now below them, a change in the voice of the water in the cave mouth, a deepening, a gathering. The last window was closing.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you're a trespasser with a firearm standing on my operational site in a storm," Ashe said pleasantly. "And the rock comes down at slack water with or without your blessing, and the chest goes into the sea with the rest of it, and I grieve at your funeral, because I do that very well also." He paused. "You're not hearing me, so let me say the quiet part. I've read your father's file. I've had it longer than you've known it existed. He was offered this same conversation, forty years ago, by men better at it than me. He chose the paper too. You know how that ended."

The wind took the words and shredded them, and still they landed.

Sebastian had spent the whole climb rehearsing this. Not the words—those didn't matter—but the feeling. They'd imagined hating him, and found instead that what they felt was colder and more useful. Ashe wasn't a monster. That was the thing no one warned you about. He was an accountant. He counted everything—gold, men, tides, grief—and everything went into the same column. And the offer wasn't even a lie. That was the trap of it. The families *would* eat. The father's name *would* be cleared. The devil's arithmetic always balanced, because the devil was very careful with his books.

It balanced. It just didn't add up to anything.

"My father logged the coordinates in 1983," Sebastian said. "Three years before they told us he died. He came back here dive after dive, and he never took the gold, and I used to think it was because he couldn't crack the strongroom." They shifted the flare pistol, very slightly, so it pointed not at Ashe but past him, at the tarped charges, at the cave, at the sea. "He could have. Maren's key was cut in '84. He took the chest out once—there's a dive log entry, forty-one meters, eleven minutes of bottom time, and then nothing. He put it back. Do you know why?"

"Enlighten me."

"Because he read it." The wind tried to steal the words and Sebastian shouted them. "He read what was in the chest and he understood that the moment it surfaced, it would be bought or buried or drowned, and he wasn't strong enough to fight that alone. So he waited. He spent the rest of his life waiting for someone the arithmetic didn't work on." Sebastian looked Ashe in the eye. "You counted everything, Mr. Ashe. You just never counted on the fact that some of us already lost everything. There's nothing left in my column to buy. That's not courage. That's just the books."

Something moved behind Ashe's eyes—not anger, not yet. Recalculation. A man discovering a line item he'd failed to enter.

"Your father waited," Ashe said quietly. "And died waiting, and the sea kept it all. Sentiment is expensive, Fairweather."

"So bill me."

Sebastian raised the flare pistol and fired—not at Ashe, not at the charges. Up. Straight up into the boiling gray belly of the storm, and the cartridge went with a sound like the world's smallest cannon, and the flare burst three hundred feet over Greyhaven Point and burned there, scarlet, obscene, a wound in the sky that the whole coast could see.

Ashe moved then—fast, much faster than a man his age should move—and his hand was inside his coat, and Sebastian was already running, because they had never intended to win this, only to spend their one cartridge buying the next four minutes.

* * *

They ran north along the cliff top, into the wind, which was like running into a wall that kept rebuilding itself, and behind them Ashe's voice cracked across the rocks—not charming now, not warm, the accountant's voice with the ledger torn open: *"Find them. FIND THEM."*

Sebastian ran for the old coastguard station because it was the only cover and the only high ground and because the press boat, if Tessa's tip had been worth anything, would come around the point from the south, and the flare had marked this cliff like a pin on a chart. They heard a shot and felt it more than heard it—rock spitting off a boulder to their left—and they went down and rolled and came up running again, lungs full of broken glass, the chest hammering against their spine like a second heart.

The generator tent first. Sebastian put three rounds of nothing through it—not the pistol, the pistol was spent, but the old signal whistle their father had taught them to carry, three blasts, the distress pattern, though no whistle on earth could carry through this gale. It didn't matter. The flare had done the work. The whistle was for Sebastian, a ritual, a hand on the shoulder from the dead: *this is how it's done. You hold the high ground and you make them come to you and you do not go down to the water.*

They reached the station's ruined wall and put their back against it, chest-first, and looked south.

The *Iron Meridian* was turning. Not toward the cave—toward the cliff. Toward them. And behind it, farther out, cutting through the storm swell with the unmistakable white slash of a hull that didn't care about weather: a gray shape, angular, fast, running lights blazing.

Navy.

And behind the navy, absurdly, miraculously, a smaller vessel, a charter boat with a camera crane bolted to her deck and a press flag whipping itself to rags in the wind, and Sebastian heard themselves laugh—a short, cracked, disbelieving sound that the wind took and scattered.

Tessa. Tessa, who had sold them, had un-sold them, had called in every marker she had in this harbor and probably two harbors over. Sebastian would find out later what it had cost her. Right now there was no later. Right now there were only boots on the path below—two men, then three, fanning out through the rocks with the storm-light rising behind them—and Corven Ashe standing alone at the cliff edge with his hands empty at last, looking at the gray hull coming around the point, doing the only arithmetic left to him.

Sebastian stepped out from behind the wall, into the open, into the wind, with the captain's chest strapped to their back and nothing in their hands at all.

Ashe turned. For a long moment the two of them looked at each other across forty feet of ruined ground, and the rain fell between them like a curtain half-drawn, and the tide below turned for the last time that season with a sound like a door closing.

"You could still swim for it," Sebastian called. "Your boats are faster than theirs."

Ashe almost smiled. Almost. "And leave the site unclaimed? Everything on that wreck belongs to whoever's holding it when the lawyers arrive, Fairweather. That's not cynicism. That's maritime law." He straightened his coat, and even now, even with the navy closing and the cameras coming, he managed to look like a man arriving rather than a man caught. "You've cost me a fortune this week. I hope your paper was worth it."

"It wasn't paper," Sebastian said.

Below them, the sea surged into the cave mouth one final time, and from deep inside the rock came a long, grinding, tectonic groan—the storm and the tide and the charges all working together now, the point itself shifting, the sands that had hidden the *Meridian* for forty years beginning to move.

And then, through the rain, across the water, Sebastian saw it: a dark shape in the surf line where no shape should be, a long black ridge of hull breaking the surface of the boiling sea like the spine of something rising from a very long sleep.

Ashe saw it too. Whatever he said next was lost to the wind.

The wreck was coming up.
