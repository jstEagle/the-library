## 3. Sable Remembers

The trouble with remembering too much is that you can never forget how to be afraid.

I was cataloguing a crate of pre-Consolidation hymnals when the boy came through my back door — the one that doesn't exist, behind the shelf of water-damaged almanacs that nobody has wanted in thirty years. He moved like a man who'd been running since morning and expected to run all night, which told me two things before he said a word: he had something valuable, and someone with money wanted it.

Both instincts proved correct. They usually do. That's what memory is for, when anyone bothers to use it.

"Door's locked," I said, not looking up from the hymnals. "And if it isn't, it should be."

"It was," he said. "I unlocked it."

"Well. At least you're honest about your crimes." I set down the hymnal — *Psalms for the River Folk*, printed in a press that hadn't existed since before the Consolidation, its ink still sharp enough to cut — and gave him a proper look. Tall, hollow-cheeked, with the particular gray pallor of a man who has recently learned his life is not his own. Railroad engineer's hands, calloused in the pattern of brake levers and coupling pins. And tucked under one arm, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that bordered on reverence: something book-shaped.

"I'm looking for an archivist," he said.

"You're looking at a bookseller. Secondhand. Mostly damp."

"The woman at the chandlery said—"

"The woman at the chandlery says a great many things she shouldn't." I stood, brushed hymnal dust from my skirts, and let him see me decide about him. It took perhaps three seconds. His eyes were frightened but level, and frightened-but-level is rarer than you'd think; most men are one or the other, and both kinds get people killed. "Come through. And touch nothing, or I'll have your fingers as bookmarks."

* * *

My archive occupies the cellar beneath the shop, three rooms deep into the riverbank, and every inch of it is illegal. Not the books themselves — old paper is only contraband when it contradicts the Consortium's licensed histories, and nearly everything contradicts the licensed histories, so nearly everything is contraband. The Consortium calls places like mine "sediment." As in: what settles to the bottom when the current of official truth runs fast enough. As in: dredge it out.

I call it the only honest room in Vell's Landing.

Juniper Brightwater — though I didn't know his name yet — unwrapped his bundle on my worktable with the tenderness of a man undressing a wound. A ledger, bound in green leather gone black with river rot. And beside it, a compass whose needle did not point north.

It pointed upstream. Steadily. Unwaveringly. Toward the source.

I had seen one once before, twenty years ago, in the hands of a dying man who'd carried it out of the mountains and died in my predecessor's back room whispering a word over and over. *Vhalen.* The word meant nothing to me then. It means everything to me now, but we'll come to that.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"Dredging the closed lines below Marrow Cut. It came up in a salvage net." He hesitated. "There was a chest. Consortium collectors were at my lodgings within six hours."

"Six hours." I turned the ledger toward the lamp. "They didn't follow the chest. They followed the chest's reputation. Someone has been waiting for this thing to surface, and they've got watchers on every dredge from the delta to the falls." I opened the cover. The first page stopped my breath.

Not because of what it said. Because of how it said it.

The script was Vhaleni. Running cursive, left-tilted, with those characteristic doubled ascenders — a language that does not exist. Officially, it never existed. There is no entry for Vhalen in any licensed atlas, no mention in any schoolbook, no ruin on any survey map bearing the name. When I was a girl, I asked my grandmother why the mountains upstream had so many drowned towers visible at low water, and she looked at me with pleasant blankness and said, *What towers?*

She wasn't lying. That's the part people can't stomach. She wasn't lying. She genuinely remembered nothing, because there was nothing left to remember. The recall houses had seen to it before I was born.

"What does it say?" Juniper asked.

I read aloud, because some things deserve air:

*"Entry the four-hundredth. By order of the Nine Tables, the archive of the nation shall pass into keeping beyond the water. Let every hand that signed remember what was traded, and let the compass find the way home when the trading is undone."* I traced a signature line with one finger. "It's a transfer ledger. An inventory of surrender. Names, dates, items surrendered — and these aren't goods. Look. *The Lamentation Bells, bronze, eleven. The Census of Households, forty-one volumes. The Grammar of Rivers, complete.* You don't surrender a grammar unless you're surrendering the language that needs it."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "You read it. Fluently. How?"

"Because I'm unlicensed," I said, "and because I stole my education one banned page at a time, and because—" I stopped. Decided. He'd shown me his wound; fairness demanded I show him mine. "Because I'm supposed to be incapable of it. Like everyone else."

"I don't understand."

"No," I said. "Almost nobody does anymore. Sit down, Mr.—?"

"Brightwater. Juniper Brightwater."

"Sit down, Mr. Brightwater, and I'll tell you what the Consortium sells besides loans."

* * *

Here is what I told him, and I'll tell it now the way I told it then, because it deserves telling properly.

In every town of any size along the river, there stands a building with brass letters over the door: RECALL HOUSE. The name sounds gentle, almost medical, and that's deliberate. Citizens go in troubled — anxious, grieving, haunted by debts or regrets or inconvenient loyalties — and they come out serene. For a fee scaled to income, the recall houses will remove what troubles you. A dead child's face. A betrayal. A war. A language.

The procedure is called *clarification*. The clerks wear gray and speak softly and offer tea beforehand. What they actually do involves a machine — I've seen fragments of its specifications in confiscated engineering notes, enough to know it works on memory the way a smelter works on ore, drawing certain recollections up and out and refining them into something the Consortium keeps. Your grief becomes their asset. Your past becomes their property.

And here is the refinement upon the cruelty: most clients pay gladly. Why wouldn't they? The Consortium spent fifty years making sure that remembering hurts. They taxed the past, restricted the archives, licensed the historians, and let the unlicensed ones hang. Grief became expensive. Memory became a liability. So people lined up to sell theirs, and thanked the gray-clerked clerks for the purchase, and went home lighter, and taught their children to want lightness too.

"They don't just erase Vhalen from maps," I said. "They erased it from minds. Every mind that held it, except the ones they couldn't reach — and mine, apparently, because I was born wrong somehow, born with a seam they missed. My mother worked in a recall house. Laundry, not clarification. But she came home with residue on her sleeves, and residue does things to a child in utero, or so I've decided, since the alternative explanation is that I'm simply a freak of nature, and I prefer the version where I'm a freak of industry."

Juniper stared at me. "You're saying history itself has been edited."

"I'm saying history has been *typeset*, Mr. Brightwater, and the Consortium holds the plates. Everything you know about the last hundred years is a licensed edition. And the corrections—" I tapped the ledger, "—the corrections are sitting on my table in your hands."

He looked down at the green-black leather for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "There's a name in here I need translated. Page nine. I saw it and my hands started shaking and I haven't stopped."

I turned to page nine. Found the signature block. Read the name.

And understood, immediately, why his hands were shaking, because the name was *Brightwater*, and the title beneath it, in formal Vhaleni, was *Chief Engineer of the Surrender* — and because I had spent twenty years collecting fragments of Vhalen, and I knew exactly what that office had done.

I didn't tell him. Not all of it. Not then.

Understand me: I am not a liar. I am an archivist, and archivists know that some documents must be read in order, or they corrupt the reader. He needed the language lesson first. He needed to understand what a recall house was before he could understand what had been done to the man whose name he carried. Truth delivered out of sequence isn't truth; it's just damage with citations.

So I closed the ledger gently and said, "This will take time. Days, maybe. The cursive is formal register — court hand — and I'll need my reference grammars." Which was true, as far as it went.

What I didn't say was: *I already know what it says, and I don't yet know if you're strong enough to hear it.*

That hesitation cost us both dearly, later. Remember that I admitted it.

* * *

He stayed three days in the cellar, sleeping on a pallet between crates of banned cartography, helping me collate while I worked through the ledger's opening sections. On the second night, he asked the question I'd been dreading.

"How do you live? Doing this. If they catch you—"

"They hang unlicensed archivists from the toll bridge," I said pleasantly. "It's very efficient. The body swings over the water where the bargemen can see it, and bargemen talk, and fear travels faster than any machine they've built. There have been four of us in Vell's Landing in my lifetime. I'm the fourth. I intend to die of old age out of sheer spite, but I make no guarantees."

"And you keep doing it anyway."

"Mr. Brightwater, I speak a language that officially never existed. I remember a country that officially never was. Do you understand what that makes me?" I let him sit with it. "It makes me the last living copy of a burned book. If I stop doing the work, Vhalen isn't just erased. It's finished. Extinction requires silence, and I decline to be silent."

He nodded slowly. Then: "The compass. Where's it pointing?"

"Upstream. Toward the source of the river." I watched his face. "And toward whatever the ledger means by *home*."

"That's Consortium territory. Closed lines, checkpoints, the whole—"

"Yes. Which is why you're going to need more than a dredger's nerve to get there." I pulled a battered rail schedule from under a crate — a real one, pre-Consolidation, showing lines that officially had never been built — and spread it across the worktable between us. "But fortunately for you, there's a coal barge leaving tomorrow that works the last permitted stretch, and a captain who owes me for certain services rendered, and a route through the mountains that appears on precisely zero licensed maps."

He looked at the schedule. At the compass needle, steady as a vow, pointing upriver. At me.

"Why help me?" he asked. "You could take the ledger, disappear deeper. Safer without me."

"Because," I said, and here I told him a partial truth, which is the most dangerous kind, "you found it, and finding creates obligation. The chest was meant to surface. The compass was meant to point. And I have waited twenty years for someone to walk through that door carrying a piece of my country." I met his eyes. "Besides, you're honest about your crimes. That's rarer than courage."

He almost smiled. First time. It didn't suit him yet — too much grief underneath, unexamined — but it would learn.

"One condition," I said, gathering the ledger, the compass, my reference grammars, and the forged travel papers I'd been keeping for an emergency I'd always hoped would arrive. "We do this in order. Page by page. Whatever the ledger says about your father, you hear it from me, in sequence, with context. No skipping ahead. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said.

Reader, he kept that promise for exactly as long as it took us to board the barge. But that is the next chapter's business.

At dawn we climbed the river stairs out of my cellar — out of the only honest room in Vell's Landing — and I locked the door behind us, and hung the CLOSED sign that I would never take down again.

Behind us, downstream, the recall houses were opening their doors for morning clients. Gray clerks, gentle tea, memories drawn up like ore.

Ahead of us, upstream, the water ran dark and cold toward everything the Consortium had drowned.

And somewhere behind us, though I didn't know it yet, a man named Corvane had already bought the chandlery woman's silence for less than the price of the tea she served him — and was asking her, very politely, which direction the archivist had gone.
