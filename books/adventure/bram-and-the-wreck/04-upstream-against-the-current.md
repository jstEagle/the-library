## 4. Upstream Against the Current

*Juniper*

The barge was called the *Patient Widow*, which struck me as the most honest name on the river. She hauled coal from the last permitted stretch of the Vhalen Reach — a phrase that meant nothing to me then and means everything now — and her captain was a woman named Osk with forearms like dock pilings and a policy of not asking questions.

"Two hundred coppers," she said, "and you shovel."

"I'm an engineer," I said.

"You're a man in a coat that doesn't fit who's looking over his shoulder every four seconds. You're a shoveler." She spat over the rail, which seemed to be both punctuation and philosophy. "Two hundred coppers and you shovel."

I paid. Sable had insisted we pay, actually — she'd gone pale at my suggestion that a man of my talents might negotiate passage down to nothing. "You don't haggle with the last boat on a dying river," she said. "You thank it."

So I shoveled coal. Me, Juniper Brightwater, who had once graded a mountain pass so smooth that the surveyors wept — or would have, if surveyors were the weeping sort — feeding a furnace like a man feeding a mouth that never said thank you. The indignity of it had a certain rhythm, though. Shovel, turn, shovel, turn. And between turns I could watch the compass in my breast pocket without anyone seeing, its needle straining upstream like a dog against a leash.

Sable spent the days belowdecks with the ledger, copying it out in a hand so small it looked like insect tracks. She wouldn't tell me what she'd found. She'd look up when I came down, close the book with one finger marking her place, and say, "Not yet," in a voice that made *not yet* sound like a country I wasn't cleared to enter.

I told myself I didn't mind. I told myself a lot of things that week.

It was on the third night, standing watch while Osk's crew slept off their wages, that I saw the lantern on the south bank keeping pace with us. Not fishing. Fishermen don't travel at exactly the speed of a coal barge for six hours.

"Sable," I whispered down the hatch.

She was awake before I finished her name. That should have told me something about how well she slept these days — or rather, how little.

* * *

*Sable*

Here is what nobody tells you about remembering: it is exhausting. Everyone thinks forgetting is the burden, but forgetting is easy. Forgetting is a warm bath. Remembering is carrying water uphill in your hands, all day, forever, watching it leak through your fingers.

I remembered Vhalen. All of it, or enough of it. My mother singing the counting song that goes up by sevens because sevens were lucky in Vhaleni arithmetic. The smell of the archive at Merrow Street — cedar and lamp oil and old glue. The day the maps changed. I was nine. One morning the capital had a name and by evening it was a smudge labeled REACH WATERS, HISTORIC, and my mother burned our books in the yard and cried without noise, which is a skill they teach you fast when you're on the wrong side of an erasure.

So yes, I kept watch. Yes, I woke before he finished saying my name. A person who remembers things the Consortium has spent thirty years drowning learns to sleep like a knife: edge up, ready.

The lantern on the bank was trouble. I knew the make — Consortium issue, brass-shuttered, the kind that can be dimmed to a coin's width of light. And I knew the pace. Collectors walk. Agents pace themselves. But Corvane *glides*. He moves like a debt: always behind you, always gaining.

"He'll come aboard at the lock," I told Juniper. "There's only one left on this stretch. He'll want witnesses. He wants everything witnessed — that's how men like him work. They make obedience look like consensus."

Juniper frowned at the dark. "Then we get off before the lock."

"And swim the Reach in autumn? The water comes off the mountains. It doesn't kill you quickly; it kills you thoroughly."

He looked at me then, and I watched him do the arithmetic I'd already done — the odds, the cold, the compass in his pocket worth more than either of our lives. I watched him arrive at the answer I wanted and then, infuriatingly, keep going past it.

"We could give him the compass," he said quietly.

"No."

"It's just brass and glass—"

"It's the needle that points at the Engine, Juniper. It's the only instrument in the world that does. Without it, the source is a rumor and I'm a madwoman with a stolen ledger." I hadn't meant to say *the Engine*. It slipped out, the way truths do when you're tired. He stared at me. I stared back. "Not yet," I said again, weaker this time.

"Not yet," he agreed, and something passed between us there in the coal dust — not trust, not yet, but the scaffolding of it. An agreement to be lied to briefly, for good reasons.

* * *

*Juniper*

Corvane boarded at Harrow's Bend lock wearing gray, which I have since learned is the Consortium's favorite color precisely because it is no color at all. He was pleasant the way ice is smooth.

"Engineer Brightwater." He pronounced my name correctly, which unsettled me more than if he'd butchered it. "You've been difficult to reach. Your creditors worry."

"My creditors sold my debts to you. That makes you not a creditor but a buyer of men, and I don't transact with the merchandise."

Something flickered behind his eyes — appreciation, maybe. The worst people always appreciate a good line; it's why they collect them.

"Debt forgiveness," he said, spreading his hands. "Full discharge. Clean papers. A letter of recommendation to any line office in the provinces. All of it, for one item of salvage. A broken compass."

Osk's crew had found urgent business belowdecks. Even the river seemed to quiet down to listen.

"How did you find me?" I asked.

"The river finds things. We simply stand downstream." He smiled. "You're a reasonable man, Brightwater. Your father was a reasonable man too."

That landed harder than he could have known — or exactly as hard as he intended. My father, dead twenty years, invoked like a password. I felt Sable's presence behind me at the hatch, silent as ballast.

"I'll consider it," I heard myself say, because a cornered animal that growls gets shot, and a cornered animal that says *maybe* gets a night.

Corvane nodded, satisfied, and stepped back onto the lock gate. "Tomorrow at dawn. Bring the compass. Bring your future." He paused. "Bring nothing else."

We cast off after midnight, ahead of schedule, into the dark.

* * *

*Sable*

They came at the second bend, where the cliffs pinch the river narrow and the current runs mean. No lanterns this time. Two skiffs, muffled oars, and men who moved like they'd done this to other barges, other fugitives, other nights.

I heard them before Juniper did — I'd been sleeping sitting up, knife in my belt, ledger strapped to my ribs like a second heart. Grappling hooks went over the rail with a sound like teeth.

What happened next happened fast and badly, which is how most true things happen.

Juniper got a hook in the shoulder of a boarder and swung him into the rail. I put my knife through a rope instead of a man, which bought us seconds and cost us the deck. Osk burst from her cabin roaring words I won't repeat and some I will treasure forever. And then a lantern flared on the cliff road — Corvane, watching, directing, patient as interest accruing — and someone fired at the barge's coal store, and the world went orange.

Fire on a coal barge. Do the mathematics yourself.

"Overboard," I said. "Now."

"The water—"

"Now, Juniper!"

He hesitated half a heartbeat — I saw him weigh the compass, the ledger, the fire, the river — and then he did the thing that I will remember about him until I forget everything else, which given my profession is saying something. He grabbed my wrist and jumped, taking me with him, choosing the both of us over the cargo, the barge, the plan, the odds.

The Reach took us like it takes everything: completely. Cold isn't a temperature at that speed; it's a decision the water makes about you. I lost the surface twice. The second time, a hand closed on my collar — his — and hauled, and we came up together gasping among the reeds of the north bank, coughing black water, watching the *Patient Widow* burn downstream like a festival we weren't invited to.

* * *

*Juniper*

We lay in the mud and the reeds, shaking, soaked to the soul, and I started laughing. I couldn't help it. Sable turned on me with murder in her eyes.

"You're laughing."

"The compass," I managed. "I checked. Underwater. In the dark. While drowning." I held it up, dripping, its needle still straining upstream, faithful as a bad habit. "Still pointing."

She stared at me, then at the compass, then she laughed too — a ragged, furious, alive sound — and then she stopped laughing and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and said, "You grabbed me. Before you jumped. You had one arm for the rail and one for the bag, and you used the arm on me."

"You'd have drowned."

"So would the compass."

"The compass floats," I said, which was a lie, and we both knew it, and neither of us cared.

Somewhere downstream, the fire settled into embers. Somewhere upstream, the needle pulled toward whatever waited at the source of all lines. And somewhere behind us, on a dark road, a man in gray was recalculating.

Sable sat up, wrung out her sleeve, and said, "There's a town ahead. Harrow's Bend. There's something you need to see there — about the ledger. About your father." Her voice caught on the last word, just slightly, like a wheel finding a flaw in the rail.

"What about my father?"

She stood, silhouetted against the burning river, and offered me her hand.

"Not yet," she said. "But soon. Sooner than either of us wants."

I took her hand anyway. Upstream, the dark waited, and for the first time since the chest came up out of the water, I wasn't facing it alone.
