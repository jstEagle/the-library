## 5. The Town That Forgot Its Name

Harrow's Bend announced itself the way a man announces a lie — too smoothly, with nothing left over.

We came down off the hills at dusk, Sable and I, soaked to the marrow from three days of walking after the river took our barge, and there it was: a lock town, neat as a ledger line, sitting on the last navigable stretch before the water went wild. Smoke from chimneys. Lamplight in windows. A church bell that rang six o'clock with the confidence of a bell that had been ringing six o'clock since the beginning of time.

Except it hadn't. I'd learn that by morning.

The lockkeeper's cottage sat where all such cottages sit — close enough to the water to hear it working, far enough to pretend otherwise. The man who answered my knock was old in the way furniture is old, worn smooth in the places people touch and rough everywhere else. He looked at us the way you look at weather. Unpleasant, probably temporary.

"Rooms," I said. "Two nights' work for two nights' board. I'm an engineer. Or I was."

"Was," he repeated, tasting the word. "What happened?"

"The river ate my employment."

That got something like a smile. "Rivers do that here." He stepped back to let us in. "Name's Oskar Vell. Lockkeeper, thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one years," Sable said quietly, behind me. I heard what she heard. Thirty-one years was a suspiciously round number. It was also, give or take, twenty years longer than anyone in this town would be able to remember anything.

* * *

I noticed it first at supper, in the lockhouse common room, over a stew Oskar's wife — or the woman he introduced as his wife, though they had the easy unfamiliarity of recent acquaintances — set in front of us.

"Good harvest this year?" I asked, making conversation.

"Best in memory," Oskar said.

"And before that?"

He frowned at his spoon. "Before that there was... a bad one? A flood, maybe. Or a blight." He shook his head like a man clearing water from his ears. "It doesn't come. Twenty years back is a wall. You can see it standing there if you push on it, but you can't climb it."

"You've tried pushing," Sable said. Not a question.

"Everyone tries, first few years." He said it the way you'd say *everyone cries at funerals*. "Then you stop. What's the use? The Consortium built its recall house up past the mill — big white building, pretty as a chapel. They hold a fair every spring. Free assessments, they call them. Free pie, too." His mouth did something that wasn't a smile. "My wife remembers our wedding day perfectly. She does not remember me proposing. Says she woke up married one morning, twenty years ago, and that's just how it was."

The woman — Marta, her name was Marta — laughed from the stove. "Oskar tells that story like it's sad. I like my version better. No nerves, no speeches, no mother-in-law objecting."

She laughed again. Nobody else did.

I looked at Sable. She was staring into her bowl with the particular stillness she got when the world confirmed something terrible she'd already suspected. Later, in the room Oskar lent us — two cots, a candle, a window rattling with river wind — she said it out loud.

"It's a test site. Harrow's Bend. They've been running recall cycles on the whole town for twenty years. Every citizen, every twenty years, wiped back to a clean baseline. They're not selling memory here, Juniper. They're farming it. Testing yield."

"Farming what, though? What did this town ever have worth taking?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She turned the ledger's pages by candlelight, careful as a surgeon. "Towns don't get erased for being nothing. Towns get erased for being something."

* * *

In the morning I went down to the locks to earn our keep, because a man who can't pay shouldn't sleep free, and because work keeps the hands busy while the mind drowns. The gates were old — older than twenty years, which made them the oldest things in Harrow's Bend by default — and the gearing had gone soft in ways I could fix.

Oskar watched me work with his arms folded. "You handle iron like you were raised on it."

"My father was an engineer. Chief engineer, they said. Of something." I'd said those words a hundred times in my life and never once thought about how strange they were. *Chief engineer of something.* My father had died — or stopped existing, or whatever the paperwork called it — when I was nine, and everything I knew about him fit in a tin box: a few tools, a photograph gone silver with age, and a signature I used to practice copying because it looked like a signature from a bigger world than ours.

"What was his name?" Oskar asked.

"Juniper Brightwater. Same as mine. He signed things *J. Brightwater, Eng.*, with a little flourish under the J, like the letter was trying to swim upstream."

The wrench in my hand stopped moving.

Because Oskar had gone still too. Not confused-still. Reaching-still. A man leaning against a wall that wasn't supposed to be there.

"Swimming upstream," he said slowly. "Under the J. There was a man — I can't — there was a man who signed like that. On the gate plans. These very gates. He stood where you're standing and complained the pivot bearings were undersized and he was right, they've never held true—" He pressed both hands flat against the lock wall as if steadying himself against a current. "Why do I know that? Why do I know *that*?"

"How long has this lock been here, Oskar?"

"Twenty years." Automatic. Then his face worked. "No. The stone's older than that. The stone is—" He looked down at the blocks under his feet, mossy, mortared in a style I recognized now, a style I'd seen sketched in the margins of a certain ledger. "The stone is older than twenty years. Everything older than twenty years is older than twenty years. Why did I never—"

"Because someone charged you a fee to forget," Sable said from the top of the stairs. She came down with the ledger open across her arms like a tray. "Oskar Vell. Do you mind if I read you something?"

He didn't answer, which I took for permission.

She read. Gate specifications. Maintenance schedules. A column of names — engineers, inspectors, lockkeepers — and dates running back decades, back past the wall, back past the beginning of the country that officially had never existed. And there, third page, dated thirty-four years back, in ink gone brown:

*Approved for construction. J. Brightwater, Eng.* With the flourish under the J. Swimming upstream.

Oskar made a sound I have no honest word for. Grief, maybe, but grief usually has an object, and his had none — just a shape, a hole in the exact silhouette of a thing he'd once known. He sat down on the lock gate like his legs had been repossessed.

"I met him," he whispered. "I was young. I carried his theodolite. He paid me in — in coins with a — with a tree on them—" He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and lost all at once. "Who was he? Who *was* he?"

"He was my father," I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to.

"Then why can't I remember him?" And there it was, the question underneath every question in Harrow's Bend, the one the white building past the mill existed to make unaskable. "I *carried his tools*. A man doesn't forget carrying another man's tools. Unless someone makes him."

* * *

We spent the day pulling threads, and every thread ended the same way: in a hole.

The tavern keeper remembered a song but not its words — hummed four bars of something in a minor key that Sable identified, white-faced, as a Vhaleni river hymn. The schoolteacher kept a drawer full of children's drawings of a city none of them had ever visited: towers, bridges, a palace with a green roof, drawn over and over in the way children draw things they've been told to forget, the images leaking through the seal like water through bad mortar. The drawings went back generations of students, each batch twenty years apart, each artist unable to say where the pictures came from.

"They drew it from memory," Sable said, "and then the memory was taken, and the drawing stayed. Paper holds what heads can't." She gathered a fistful of them like evidence, like relics. "This is what erasure actually looks like, Juniper. Not empty maps. Empty *people*. A nation dies twice — once when it falls, and once when the last person who grieves it is edited."

By evening Oskar had recovered perhaps a dozen fragments, each one surfacing and sinking like a body refusing to stay down. Coins with a tree. A green-roofed palace visible from the upper locks on a clear day. A festival where they floated lamps on the current and the whole river glowed for a mile. Each fragment matched an entry in the ledger. Each one cost him something to surface — I could see it, the effort, a man hauling on a rope tied to a drowned weight.

At supper he pushed his bowl away and looked at us across the table with an expression I'll carry to my grave.

"You want to know the worst part? It isn't losing it. Losing is clean. It's that they left me *reaching*. Twenty years of reaching for something at the edge of every thought, and never knowing what. They didn't take my past. They took my hand off the rope and left the rope."

Marta touched his arm, gentle, puzzled — she loved him, I believed that, but she couldn't remember loving him longer than her last wipe, and love without history is a fire without fuel. She smiled at him anyway. Maybe that was the bravest thing in Harrow's Bend.

* * *

Past midnight, Sable shook me awake. She had the ledger open and the candle low, and her face was doing something I hadn't seen before. Fear, yes, we'd shared plenty of that. This was worse. This was pity.

"There's more in the Brightwater entries," she said. "Payments. Dates. I cross-checked them against the recall house records Oskar described — the spring fairs, the twenty-year cycles." She turned the book toward me. "Juniper. Your father didn't just build their locks. Look at the disbursement column. Someone was paid, regularly, for twenty years, to maintain a *baseline*. A controlled population. Harrow's Bend wasn't a test site chosen at random."

I read the entry three times. The handwriting was my father's — I knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, that swimming flourish under the J — and the words beneath it were simple, administrative, monstrous:

*Site maintained per agreement. Population stable. Memory yield consistent.*

"Yield," I said. "They're harvesting what they wipe. All those songs, those drawings, those fragments Oskar keeps drowning for — they go somewhere. They go *upstream*."

Sable nodded slowly. "And there's one more thing. The last entry in your father's hand. Dated the year he disappeared." She hesitated, and I understood then that she'd been deciding all night whether to show me, and had decided that a man deserves his own grief even when it arrives with teeth. "It says: *Final delivery accepted. Payment rendered in full. Subject released per terms.*"

"Subject," I repeated.

"*Subject,*" she said softly. "Not engineer. Not signatory. Subject. Juniper — I think your father wasn't just the Consortium's builder. I think he was their first customer. And I think whatever he sold them, he sold it here, in this town, and then he paid his own memories as the fee."

Outside, the river ran dark and constant, carrying everything downstream the way rivers do — except in Harrow's Bend, where for twenty years it had been carrying things *up*, into the white building past the mill, into whatever waited at the source of all lines.

And somewhere upstream, at the end of that current, was a man with my father's name who might be walking around with my father's emptied head.

I closed the ledger. "We leave at first light."

Sable was already packing.
