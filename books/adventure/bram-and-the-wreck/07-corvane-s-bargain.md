## 7. Corvane's Bargain

I have been called many things in my career with the Consortium — fixer, hound, "that man," and once, memorably, by a woman in Vell's Crossing whose husband I was repossessing, a word I won't repeat in polite company. But the one that stings most is *thief*, because it is imprecise. A thief takes what isn't his. I take what has already been signed away. The paperwork is always immaculate. That is the difference between me and the river, which steals without so much as a receipt.

The river had stolen from us for three weeks now. Brightwater and the archivist woman had slipped past my agents at Graylock Weir by hiding in a coffin barge — clever, I'll grant them; the manifest said typhoid, and no Consortium man will search a typhoid coffin twice. They'd surfaced at Harrow's Bend like drowned men who refused to stay drowned, and every report since had read like an apology.

So when the wire came through that they'd been spotted boarding the *Merry Adela* — a coal barge licensed for the last permitted stretch above Harrow's Bend — I did not send agents. Agents were how you lost things. I went myself, with six men and a portable checkpoint, because there is one law older than the Consortium: all rivers narrow eventually, and everything floating on them must pass under somebody's bridge.

Mine, as it happened. An old rail trestle, half-collapsed, spanning the gorge where the water ran green and mean. We strung chains across it, hung lanterns, set up a table with a ledger book and a decanter of brandy I'd brought for atmosphere. Checkpoints are theater. The chain is the prop; the signature is the play.

* * *

They came at dusk, as I knew they would, because the *Merry Adela*'s captain was paid either way and coal doesn't steer itself around obstacles. I watched the barge come downriver low in the water, smoke dragging behind it like something reluctant, and I saw Brightwater at the rail — tall, hollow-cheeked, wearing the expression of a man who has done arithmetic all day and hates the total. Beside him, the archivist: Sable, dark-haired, watchful, hands folded like a woman holding her own secrets down.

"Mr. Brightwater," I called up, pleasantly. "You're looking better than your debts."

He came down the gangplank because the alternative was explaining himself to the chain, and chains are poor conversationalists. Up close he looked worse than the reports suggested. Sleepless. Guilty about something. Good. Guilt is leverage that never needs sharpening.

"You've been difficult to find," I said.

"You've been easy to avoid," he said. "One of us should work on that."

I smiled the smile I keep for debtors with wit; it saves time later, when the wit runs out. "Here's the shape of your evening," I told him. "Your loans — all of them, the dredging equipment, the salvage license, the little matter of the engine parts you bought on credit in Vell's Crossing — total nine hundred forty marks. Your employer sold those papers to us four months ago. Which means every hour you float free, you're not just a debtor. You're an asset in flight." I opened the ledger book to a fresh page. "Or you can sign here, hand over the compass, and walk away owing nothing. Safe passage downstream. Clean slate. I'm told clean slates are popular these days."

His eyes went to the compass pocket of his coat — a tell so obvious I nearly wrote it down — then to Sable, standing at the top of the gangplank with the wind pulling at her hair.

"And if I don't?"

"Then we impound the barge, search it top to bottom, and you learn what our search procedures look like. I'll be honest with you, Mr. Brightwater, because honesty is cheaper than lying to a man who's about to find out anyway: the compass matters to people well above my pay grade. People who don't do bargains. I am the bargain. I'm the last reasonable voice you're going to hear on this river."

I meant it, too. That's the thing nobody understands about my work. I am the mercy in the machine. Every consortium has a Corvane, or it has a massacre.

Brightwater stood there a long moment, and I watched him do the arithmetic again — the same arithmetic, the hated total — and then he reached into his coat and set the compass on my table.

It was a handsome thing. Brass case gone green at the seams, glass clouded, needle swinging lazy circles before settling toward the mountains upstream instead of north. Wrong, and beautiful in its wrongness, like everything else that came out of Vhalen.

"All of it," he said. "The debt. The passage. You leave Harrow's Bend alone."

"Harrow's Bend," I repeated. "What an interesting town to bring up unprompted."

"I didn't bring it up," he said, too fast.

He absolutely had. I noted it, signed the release, and pushed the pen across to him. He signed. His hand shook only a little, and I gave him credit for that; most men shake more.

"Pleasure doing business," I said, and meant that too, which is the strangest part of this profession. I liked him. I liked his stubbornness and his terrible poker face and the way the archivist watched me like she was cataloguing me for some future exhibit. In another life we'd have shared that brandy.

In this one, I put the compass in my breast pocket, felt its small weight settle against my heart like a second conscience, and waved the *Merry Adela* through.

* * *

We made camp on the trestle's broken span. My men drank. I sat with the compass under a lantern and did what I always do with acquisitions: verified them.

The case was right. The patina was right. The needle swung wrong in exactly the documented way.

But the hinge — the hinge was too smooth. Twenty years underwater, per the salvage reports, and the hinge moved like it had been oiled yesterday. And when I tilted it to the lantern light, I saw what only a man who has spent his life reading documents sees: the engraving inside the lid was crisp where it should have been worn soft. Fresh-cut letters. Beautifully aged, but aged by a hand, not by water.

Someone had forged it. Recently. Skillfully.

I thought of the archive woman's hands, folded on the gangplank. Hands that worked for a living. Hands that had clearly spent weeks — months? — learning to make brass lie.

"Well," I said aloud, to nobody, "isn't that something."

The real compass was somewhere behind me, going downstream on a coal barge, wrapped in the smug silence of two people who thought they'd outfoxed the Consortium. And I sat there with a counterfeit warming in my palm and felt something I rarely feel in this line of work, which is respect, immediately followed by something I feel even more rarely, which is fury.

Because here is what they didn't understand, those two clever children on their barge: the compass was never the point. The point was the exchange. A bargain is a confession. When Brightwater handed it over, he told me three things: that he valued it above his freedom, that he believed I could be trusted to honor terms, and — the third thing, the important one — that whatever else he carried, the ledger, the knowledge, the archive woman herself, he considered survivable losses.

He'd given me the decoy because he thought the decoy was what I wanted.

Which meant the real prize was still aboard the *Merry Adela*, drifting downriver toward Harrow's Bend, and I had just personally signed it safe passage.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It was, genuinely, an excellent forgery. Somewhere in a burned nation's ashes there had been a teacher who taught that woman to age brass, and I hoped the Consortium had shot him, because if they hadn't, he deserved a pension.

Then I stopped laughing, got up, and started giving orders.

* * *

The *Merry Adela* made perhaps six miles before we caught her, running dark, engines banked, hugging the far bank like a drunk avoiding his own house. We came alongside in a launch with the lanterns doused, and I'll say this for Brightwater: he heard us before anyone and had the archivist moving for the cargo hold before my grapnels bit. They went over the side into the shallows — I saw the splash, heard the woman swear in a language that has officially never existed — and my men swarmed the deck.

The barge itself was empty of anything worth taking. No compass. No ledger. A false bottom in the coal bunkers, recently pried and re-nailed, containing nothing but a scrap of paper with a Vhaleni word chalked on it, which my linguist consultant later informed me meant roughly *"you're late."*

Charming.

But the crew — ah, the crew. Six men and a boy, the captain's nephew, maybe fifteen, feeding the boiler. And lashed at the stern, three passengers who'd boarded at Harrow's Bend that morning: a lockkeeper's widow, a fisherman, and a girl of nineteen carrying a market basket, all of them now flat on the deck with my men's boots on their necks, all of them from the town Brightwater had been too frightened to mention and too honest to abandon.

I stood on the coal-dusted deck and looked at them and did the arithmetic myself, for once. Two fugitives, loose in the hills above the source, carrying the last uncontrolled record of a nation the Consortium had paid a great deal to erase. Against: three civilians and a boy, and a barge, and my own signature on a release promising safe passage.

A bargain, I have found, is only as strong as the paper it's written on, and paper burns beautifully.

"Mr. Corvane," the captain said, hands raised, "we carried them honest, we didn't know—"

"No," I agreed. "Nobody ever knows. That's the whole design of the thing."

I gave the order for the barge. Oil from the launch stores, a lantern, and the *Merry Adela* went up against the night sky like a second sunset, coal smoke and orange flame, the river underneath turning briefly to hammered copper. It was, I admit, wasteful. But messages must be legible, and ash reads clearly at a distance.

Then I had the hostages bound and loaded, and left word — chalked on the trestle, in English, plain as a bill of sale — that the three from Harrow's Bend and the boy would be held at the checkpoint station at Kettle Gap, and that they would remain alive, fed, and unharmed for exactly as long as it took Juniper Brightwater and his archivist to come down out of the mountains and surrender what they'd kept back.

Guilt, I'm told, is a debt like any other. It accrues. It compounds. And I have never once met a man who could outrun the collector.

Somewhere upstream, in the dark above the tree line, I imagined Brightwater watching the fire on the water and understanding, finally, the interest rate on cleverness.

I almost pitied him. Then I remembered the forged hinge, and stopped.
