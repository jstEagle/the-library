## 8. The Source of All Lines

The rail line ended at a place called Kiln Nine, which was not a kiln and had never been nine of anything. It was a rusted spur jutting out of the mountainside like a broken tooth, and beyond it the maps went blank — not politely blank, the way mountains are blank, but *edited* blank, the kind of blankness that costs money.

"Charming," Sable said, stepping off the handcar. "They even erased the survey markers."

"They didn't erase them." I crouched by a granite post where the letters had been chiseled out and filled with lead. "They overwrote them. That's different. Erasure leaves scars. This is just — silence with good posture."

She gave me one of her looks, the ones that made me feel like a ledger entry she hadn't finished auditing. "You've been making jokes since Harrow's Bend. You only make jokes when you're terrified."

"I'm an engineer. I convert terror into torque."

"And yet here we are, walking."

We walked. There wasn't much choice in it anymore. The compass — my father's compass, though I didn't know that yet in the way you know a thing, only in the way you carry it — pulled us up through pine and scree along a grade so steep no sane railroad man would have attempted it. But someone *had* attempted it. That was the thing I kept noticing. Cut stone under the moss. Iron pins in the rock face, rusted to lace. A roadbed climbing at four percent where nothing should climb at all.

"Somebody built this," I said, for maybe the tenth time.

"Somebody Vhalen," Sable said.

"You can't know that."

"I can read the cut marks. Vhaleni masons dressed stone on the uphill face first. It's a signature. Like your father's handwriting on the ledger." She said it lightly. Too lightly. I'd learned by then to hear when Sable was being light on purpose, the way you learn to hear a bearing running dry before it seizes.

The mountain closed around us as we climbed. The air went thin and cold and smelled of snowmelt and something else — something faintly electrical, like the smell after lightning, or before it. By the second day my fillings ached. By the third, the compass needle stopped spinning entirely and simply strained against its glass, quivering like a dog at a door.

That night we found the tunnel.

It was bored straight into the mountain's shoulder, mouth framed in dressed stone, and above the arch — chiseled away, lead-filled, same as everything else — there had once been words. Sable put her palm flat against the blank space and stood there a long moment.

"It said 'VHALEN ENDURES,'" she said quietly. "In the old script. The formal one. They used it on gatehouses and graves."

"You can read the blank?"

"I can read what they were afraid of." She looked back down the slope we'd climbed, toward the river we couldn't see, toward every town along it sleeping under twenty years of borrowed memory. "Juniper. Whatever's up there — whatever the compass has been dragging us toward — I need you to understand it isn't treasure. Nations don't get erased by accident. Somebody built a machine to do it, and machines have keepers."

I told her I understood. I was lying, but I said it with conviction, which is most of engineering anyway.

* * *

The tunnel ran two miles, dead straight, and it was the finest work I have ever seen or expect to see. Perfect bore. Ventilation shafts every quarter mile, still drawing breath after fifty years. Rails of an alloy I couldn't name, bright as the day they were laid, because — I realized somewhere in the dark — because the mountain itself hummed along them. Something at the end of that tunnel was *live*, and the rails were carrying it, and every hair on my arms stood up like iron filings finding their field.

Sable walked close enough that our shoulders touched. Neither of us mentioned it.

Then the tunnel opened, and I stopped being an engineer, because what I saw broke the part of me that names things.

There was no valley. That was the first wrongness. The map showed a headwater basin, a cirque, a lake at worst. Instead the mountain opened onto a bowl of water — vast, black, unnaturally still — held back by a dam that was not a dam. It was a *machine*. Miles of it. Brass and iron and glass rising out of the floodwater in terraces, tiered like an opera house, every surface crawling with conduits that converged upward into a single spire at the center. And from that spire, threads. Thousands of them. Hair-fine filaments of light arcing out across the sky and down, downstream, following the river's course into the dark — drinking from every town, every recall house, every licensed little parlor where people paid good money to have themselves edited.

"That's not a source," I heard myself say. "That's a *pump*."

"The capital," Sable whispered. Her voice had gone strange. "Juniper, this is the capital. They flooded it. They drowned the whole city and built this on top of it, and it draws the memories up like ore. Out of the water. Out of the people. Out of—" She pressed the ledger against her chest. "Out of everyone who ever paid to forget."

The water below us moved then. Not waves. *Light.* Shapes rising beneath the surface, vast and slow — rooftops, a bridge, a bell tower leaning like a drunk — the drowned city turning over in its sleep. And every shape that rose shed a little brightness that streamed up into the spire, and the spire drank it, and somewhere downstream a town forgot a little harder.

I am not a religious man. Railroaders can't afford religion; the schedule won't allow it. But standing on that ledge I understood that I was looking at a church, and the god was debt, and the whole river valley was its congregation.

"There'll be a maintenance way down," I said, because my hands needed something to do besides shake. "Machines like this have galleries, catwalks, access. Nobody builds a pump without stairs to reach the valves."

"You're going down there."

"I'm going down there."

"Juniper—"

"Sable." I made myself look at her. "My father's name is in that ledger. Whatever he did, whatever he was — it ends up there, in that machine, or it ends with me. There's no third option where I walk back down the mountain and take up dredging."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she did the last thing I expected: she handed me the ledger.

"Then carry it properly," she said. "Both hands."

* * *

The access gallery clung to the machine's flank like scaffolding on a cathedral, and we descended it into a wind that smelled of ozone and old rain. Up close the Recall Engine — Sable named it that; I'd have called it something less polite — resolved into terrible detail. The brass tiers were lined with glass cylinders, thousands of them, each one holding a slow-turning coil of light. Memories. Bottled, sorted, *owned*. Some of the cylinders were dark and empty. Some blazed. And stamped on every rack, every valve, every plate, small and neat and everywhere:

**CONSORTIUM PROPERTY. DO NOT TAMPER. PATENT PENDING.**

Patent pending. Fifty years, and pending. Even the erasure of a nation was still, legally, an application.

We were three galleries down, working along a catwalk toward the spire's base, when I saw him.

He was an old man in a Consortium coverall, gray and gone soft at the edges, pushing a cart of replacement fuses along the tier below us. He moved with the unhurried competence of a man who has done a job ten thousand times. He stopped at a rack of cylinders, checked a gauge, adjusted a valve, patted the machine's flank the way you'd pat a horse.

And I knew his shoulders.

You can erase a name off a map. You can chisel a nation off a gatehouse and pour lead in the letters. But a body keeps its own records, and I had spent my childhood watching those shoulders bend over drafting tables, watching them shrug into a coat, watching them carry me on them through a rail yard while my mother laughed about something I no longer remember. My father had been dead eleven years. Dead men do not check gauges.

"Sable," I said. My voice came out in pieces. "Who is that."

She followed my eyes. I felt her go still beside me, felt the exact moment she recognized what I was recognizing — and felt, too, some hesitation pass over her like a shadow, there and gone, a door opening and shutting. Later I would learn what she'd already deciphered in the ledger and chosen not to say. Later. In that moment there was only the old man, and the cart, and my own heart trying to climb out through my teeth.

"Dad?" I said. Stupidly. Loudly. The word fell down the galleries and came back to me twice, mocking.

The old man turned. He had my jaw. He had my mother's gray eyes, the ones I see in mirrors now and hate. He looked up at me with the mild, vacant courtesy of a stranger interrupted at his work, and he smiled.

"Afternoon," he said. His voice was roughened but familiar, a song played in the wrong key. "You two lost? Public aren't permitted past Gallery Four. Insurance reasons." He chuckled. "Not that there's much insurance left in the world, eh?"

I gripped the railing. The catwalk swayed. Somewhere below us the drowned city turned over again, shedding light.

"Do you—" I started, and stopped, and started. "Do you know your name?"

He considered this with genuine, untroubled interest, the way you'd consider a riddle in a newspaper. "Name," he repeated. "Now that's funny. They ask me that every year. Annual review." He tapped his temple. "Nothing up here but the work. Best thing that ever happened to me, they tell me. Clean slate. No debts." He smiled again, warmer, and it was unbearable. "No debts. Can you imagine? A man my age, owing nobody anything."

The ledger burned against my chest. *Traded the archive for his son's protected future, then paid with himself.* I didn't know that yet — wouldn't know it until Sable finally found the courage or the cruelty to tell me — but I knew the shape of it, the way you know a bridge is overloaded before the math confirms it. He had sold everything so that I could owe nobody anything. And here he was, owing nobody anything, tending the machine that ate his country, happy as a lamp.

"What's your name?" I asked him again, quieter.

He frowned, reached into his collar, and drew out a tin tag on a chain. He read it the way a man reads instructions.

"Employee 4471-C," he said. "But you can call me Keeper. Everybody does."

Below us, deep in the black water, a bell tower rang — once, faintly, a sound with no right to exist — and every light in the Engine flickered. The old man glanced down, mildly concerned, and made a note on his clipboard.

"Happens sometimes," he said. "Old wiring. Now then — you two best come with me. There's been a signal from downstream. Someone's coming up the river with hostages, and the Engine's expecting visitors."
