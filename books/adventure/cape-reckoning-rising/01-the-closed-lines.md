## 1. The Closed Lines

The map room smelled of lamp oil and old paper and, faintly, of the river — a smell Cyrus Nystrom had long ago stopped noticing the way other people noticed weather. It was simply there, like her own pulse.

She sat at the big drafting table with her boots up on an empty crate of survey stakes, a cup of chicory coffee going cold at her elbow, and drew a line that did not exist anymore.

That was most of her work, when it came down to it. Drawing lines that did not exist anymore. The Cape Reckoning had once been stitched together with rail — forty-one spur lines running timber out of the high country, ore down from the Kestrel range, passengers and mail and hope to towns that had since gone quiet or gone under. The main lines still ran; the coastal packet still made its rounds. But the spurs, one by one, had been closed, pulled up, flooded, or simply forgotten, and the men who'd drawn the original surveys were dead or scattered or lying about where they'd put things.

Cyrus kept the truth of them. Every grade, every trestle, every tunnel mouth. It was in her head and in her hand and in the great linen-backed map that covered half the wall of the old signal house she rented above the tideline in Port Merrow.

She was finishing the last curve of Spur Nine — the one that climbed through the Narrows and ended, according to every official record, at a rockslide that had never actually happened — when the knock came.

Three knocks. Then a pause. Then two more, soft, almost apologetic.

"Door's open," Cyrus said, not looking up. "It's always open. That's the entire point of a door in a public office."

"It's also," said Bram Tullow, easing himself inside with the careful dignity of a man whose knees had opinions, "the entire point of a door in a building full of maps people would steal."

Bram was seventy-one, formerly the stationmaster at Merrow Junction, currently a self-appointed nuisance, and he carried a folded newspaper under one arm the way other men carried grievances. He set it on the corner of her table without being asked, which was how Bram delivered all news: by leaving it where you'd have to deal with it.

"I'm working," Cyrus said.

"You're drawing a line through the Narrows that the company swears is buried under forty feet of scree."

"The company is wrong."

"The company," Bram said, settling onto the stool by the stove with a sigh that suggested the sitting itself was a performance he expected to be applauded for, "is paying you to be wrong."

"The company is paying me to draw what's *there*." She tapped the pencil against the paper. "What's there is a tunnel. What's *recorded* is a rockslide. I don't get paid enough to lie about geology."

"You don't get paid at all for this map. You do it because—" Bram waved a hand at the walls, at the decades of her work pinned and rolled and stacked around them, "—because you're your father's daughter and your mother's temper, and neither of those is a compliment."

Cyrus smiled despite herself. It was true, and they both knew it, and it was the kind of truth that only got said between people who'd survived each other for eleven years.

Her father had been a road engineer before her. Her mother had been a ferry pilot who'd once, famously, told a customs cutter exactly where it could moor. Cyrus had inherited the straight lines from one and the refusal to follow them from the other, and the result was a woman who kept the most accurate map of the Cape Reckoning in existence and let almost no one see it.

"Is there a point to the newspaper," she said, "or are you just decorating my table?"

"There's a point." Bram unfolded it with the reverence of a man handling something explosive. Page three. He turned it toward her and tapped the headline twice, hard, as though the ink might be hiding something.

**SALVAGE CONSORTIUM ANNOUNCES UPRIVER RECOVERY OPERATION**, it read. **MERIDIAN STAR WRECK TO BE RAISED BEFORE SPRING FLOOD.**

Cyrus put down her pencil.

The Meridian Star. Everyone on the Cape knew the name the way they knew the names of storms. A treasury packet, pre-war, gone down in the flood of '61 with a sealed cargo that rumor valued at more than the whole territory's annual take — gold bonds, specie, papers nobody had ever confirmed because nobody had ever reached the wreck. The river had swallowed it whole, along with the town it had been steaming toward. Kestrel Bend. Drowned so completely that at low water you could sometimes see the church weathervane breaking the surface like a fish rising, and then not even that.

For thirty years the wreck had been a story. Now it was going to be an operation.

"Corvan Salvage," Bram said, tapping the article again. "That's Aldous Corvan's fleet. Six boats. Steam cranes. They're staging at Grayling Landing already — my nephew saw the barges come through Tuesday, riding low with machinery."

"Mm," said Cyrus.

"'Mm,'" Bram repeated. "That's all I get? I bring you the single biggest piece of news on the Cape since the war and I get 'mm'?"

"Bram." She looked at him over the rim of her cold coffee. "Why are you bringing me this? You don't care about salvage fleets. You care about the price of coal and whether the packet runs on time and whether Mrs. Ellery's cat has gotten into your garden again."

"The cat has, incidentally. But that's beside the point." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used for gossip he considered genuinely serious. "The expedition went silent."

"What expedition?"

"Marsh's."

The coffee cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Odile Marsh. The name landed in Cyrus's chest the way a dropped wrench lands on a plank floor — heavy, sudden, impossible to pretend you didn't hear.

"Survey expedition," Bram went on, watching her face with the satisfaction of a man who has found the right lever. "Out of the university, upriver, charting the drowned valley ahead of the salvage operation. Odile Marsh leading. Left six weeks ago. Last word from them was a supply request from the Kestrel Bend station — three weeks ago now. Nothing since. The consortium says they've 'lost contact due to river conditions.' The university says they're 'not alarmed.'" He snorted. "You know what it means when a committee isn't alarmed. It means they're terrified and have agreed to say nothing until someone else has to say it first."

Cyrus set the cup down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, something older than steadiness was moving around.

Odile Marsh. Twenty-two years since Cyrus had stood in Marsh's survey office learning how to run a transit and how to hold a grudge properly — both lessons delivered in the same dry voice, usually in the same sentence. *Nystrom, if you're going to be stubborn, be accurate about it. Inaccurate stubbornness is just being wrong loudly.* The best teacher Cyrus ever had. The last person she'd ever wanted to disappoint, and the one person she had, thoroughly, at twenty-six, in a way involving a falsified report and a bridge and a night neither of them had ever spoken about since.

They hadn't exchanged letters in nineteen years. Cyrus had counted. Not obsessively. Just — accurately. Stubbornness, after all, should be precise.

"She wouldn't go silent," Cyrus said. "Not accidentally. If Marsh lost a radio she'd build another one out of spite and spare parts."

"That was rather my thinking." Bram folded the paper with a snap. "And here's the other thing, since you're going to sit there pretending to be calm. The consortium's announcement came out *yesterday*. Marsh's last transmission was *three weeks ago*. Which means Corvan knew the survey had gone quiet before anyone public did — and instead of waiting, or sending help, he moved his schedule *up*."

Cyrus looked at the map on her wall. Forty-one spur lines. And threading upriver through the high country, past the Narrows, down into the drowned valley — Spur Nine. Closed. Abandoned. Officially dead for thirty years.

Unofficially passable, if you knew what she knew, in maybe four days' hard going, versus nine days by river around the long bend — and the river route ran directly past Grayling Landing, where six salvage boats sat waiting to stop anyone they didn't like the look of.

"How much would you charge," she heard herself say, "to watch the office for a month?"

Bram blinked. Then his face did the thing it did when he was delighted and determined to hide it, which was to become extremely interested in the stove.

"A month," he said. "A month of sitting in your chair, drinking your coffee, telling visitors you're out. And what am I supposed to tell Mrs. Ellery?"

"The truth. You're minding the map room while I'm away checking a line."

"Which line?"

Cyrus stood, took the pencil from behind her ear, and drew one clean stroke up the wall map — through the Narrows, past the false rockslide, down into the valley of the drowned town.

"This one," she said. "The closed one."

* * *

Packing took her an hour, because packing light was a discipline her father had beaten into her before he'd taught her anything about rail. One canvas pack. Instruments wrapped in oilcloth. The small brass sextant that had been Marsh's gift to her, back when gifts between them had still been possible. A waterproof roll of blank survey paper. Her father's river coat, patched at both elbows, still smelling faintly of tar no matter how many times she washed it.

The revolver she took down from the shelf last, weighed in her hand, and packed without ceremony. The upper river wasn't lawless exactly. But it wasn't lawfuller than it needed to be, either.

At the door she stopped, as she always did, and looked back at the wall map. Forty-one lines. Thirty years of her life inked into linen. It occurred to her, standing there with the morning fog pressing gray against the windows, that she had spent her whole career keeping the memory of dead lines — and that somewhere upriver, in the silence around Kestrel Bend, there might be a living person who needed one of them remembered badly enough to matter.

"Well," she said to the empty room, because saying things to empty rooms was another inheritance, this one from her mother. "Let's go be accurate about it."

* * *

Merrow Junction at dawn was steam and gulls and the particular chaos of a coastal packet taking on freight. Cyrus moved through it with the ease of thirty years' habit, ducking under the loading crane's arc, nodding to the yard crew, and letting herself into the cab of the little inspection engine — a squat, bad-tempered machine called the Puffin, which the railway officially used for track inspection and unofficially used for whatever Cyrus asked of it, because Cyrus had once diagnosed a cracked axle on the superintendent's own car and the superintendent had never forgotten it.

"You'll want the high line," said Josie Ferro, the yardmaster, falling into step beside her. Josie was sixty pounds of muscle and opinions wrapped in a railway uniform two sizes too small. "Tide's low till ten. You can make Grayling cutoff before the packet traffic if you leave now."

"I'm not taking the high line."

Josie stopped walking. "You're taking— " Her eyes went up, involuntarily, to where the old spur grades climbed into the fog above the yards. "Nystrom. Nobody's run Spur Nine since before I was born."

"Somebody maintained it longer than that."

"Somebody *what*?"

Cyrus swung up into the cab and started the pre-fire checks, hands moving on their own. "There was a section keeper up there. Old man Vasseur. Kept the Narrows stretch clear off the books for years — said a railroad shouldn't die just because its paperwork did. He died himself, oh, eight years back. But stone and timber don't rot as fast as men do, and I've walked it twice since. It'll hold."

"It'll *hold*." Josie said the words the way other people said *it'll sink*. "On a line with no signals, no water stops, and a tunnel through the Narrows that the company pretends is a hillside."

"That's why I'm taking the Puffin and not the big engine. She's light. And she's too ugly to die."

"Cyrus." Josie caught the handrail, and her voice lost its banter. "The salvage fleet's at Grayling. Six boats and Corvan's own flag steamer. Whatever you're doing up that line — and I know better than to ask — you're doing it ahead of men who've bought half the territory's magistrates and most of the rest of its patience. You sure?"

Cyrus looked out over the yards, at the fog burning off the harbor, at the far gray shape of the headland beyond which the river ran north toward Kestrel Bend and whatever silence waited there.

Somewhere up in that fog was a woman who had taught her that accuracy was a form of respect — that to truly measure a thing, to record it honestly, was to honor it. And that a lie on a map was a small cruelty done to everyone who came after.

Odile Marsh had gone silent on purpose. Cyrus was certain of it the way she was certain of grades and gauges. And certainty, in her family, was not a feeling. It was an instruction.

"No," Cyrus said honestly. "But I'm going anyway. Watch the junction for me, Josie. And if a tall woman with a limp comes asking after me — tell her I finally learned to be stubborn accurately."

She opened the throttle. The Puffin complained, coughed, and began to climb.

Behind her the yards fell away into fog. Ahead, the old grade rose through wet fern and rusted fence-wire, following a line that existed nowhere but in her head and on her wall and, if the fates were kind, in the memory of one stubborn old section keeper whose rails might yet carry her to the drowned town before the tide of salvage ships arrived.

Four days to the Narrows. Three weeks of silence behind her. And somewhere ahead, six tides turning over a secret worth killing for.

Cyrus Nystrom drove her ugly little engine into the clouds, and did not look back.
