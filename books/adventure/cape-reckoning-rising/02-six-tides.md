## 2. Six Tides

The Puffin did not so much climb the mountain as argue with it.

She was a yard engine by birth — forty years old, eleven tons, built to shuffle ore cars on grades so steep that sensible locomotives refused the work — and she had been rebuilt so many times that Cyrus privately suspected only her boiler and her grudges were original. Her name came from her first owner, who had watched her wallow through a flooded cutting with her wheels half-swimming and declared she moved like a bird that had given up on flying but not on trying. The name had stuck, as names do when they are accurate enough to be cruel.

Cyrus drove her standing up, one hand on the reverser, the other holding a tin mug of coffee that sloshed in time with the rails. The old grade wound up through the ferns in long switchbacks, and at each curve the fog opened for a moment like a curtain, showing her the world below: the gray sheet of the Sound, the yards of Port Ansel shrinking to a smudge of rust, and — far out, where the water went silver — the faint suggestion of masts.

Salvage fleet. Already gathering.

"Keep your eyes on your own work," she told the masts, and shut the curtain again with a curve.

The line she was climbing was called the High Grade, though nobody had called it anything but *the dead line* in twenty years. It had been built to haul copper down from the Kestrel mines before the war, back when the territory believed in itself, and abandoned after the flood took Kestrel Bend and the company decided the river was a business partner they could no longer afford. The rails were still there. That was the miracle of steel — it outlasted intention. Weeds grew up between the ties, alder saplings leaned against the rails like drunkards against a fence, and in places a slide had carried whole sections of track downslope, leaving the roadbed bare as a scar. But the grade itself held. Cyrus's predecessors had built well, because they had expected their work to be judged by God and gravity both.

She knew every mile of it from the map on her wall, which was to say she knew it the way you know a face in a photograph. Knowing it in fog, in rain, with an engine that hated switchbacks, was another education entirely.

* * *

The rain found her an hour past the second summit, which was not a summit at all but a shoulder of rock the surveyors had named Observation Point on the theory that optimism was free. She put the Puffin under a rock overhang that some forgotten section crew had blasted out for exactly this purpose, banked her fire, and ate bread and cheese and a pickle she had been saving since Tuesday, sitting on the running board with her boots dangling over the ballast while the water came down in ropes.

It was there, in the dripping green quiet, that she heard the bell.

One stroke. Then nothing. Then, after a long minute, one stroke more — the flat, patient toll of a hand-bell rung by someone who wanted attention but not urgency.

Cyrus stood, cupped her hands, and called into the rain: "If you're a ghost, I'm not impressed. If you're a bear, I've got a pickle."

The answer came from downslope, through the trees, in a voice like gravel poured slowly: "*Section keeper's hut's got a roof on it, girl. Bears don't build roofs.*"

The hut sat fifty feet below the grade, half-swallowed by salal, its shingles furred with moss, its chimney smoking — actually smoking, which meant somebody had cut wood recently, which meant somebody intended to stay. The bell hung on a post by the door, and beside the post, nailed up like a warning or a welcome, hung a rail joint bar polished bright by years of hands.

The man who opened the door was small, round, and approximately the color and texture of his own stove. He wore oilskins gone amber with age and a beard that seemed to have been combed with the specific goal of hiding his mouth entirely. His eyes, above all that hair, were quick and blue and missed nothing.

"You're Nystrom," he said.

"I'm Nystrom," Cyrus agreed. "You're supposed to be dead, Mr. Fennick. Or retired. The company file says retired. My father always said dead."

"The company file says what keeps the company happy." He stepped aside to let her in. "Your father said dead because I asked him to. Dead men don't get letters asking them to sign things. Come in before the rain comes in after you."

* * *

The inside of the hut was a museum of one man's stubbornness. Tools hung in ranks, each handle worn to the shape of Fennick's grip. A pot of stew considered itself over the fire. And covering one entire wall, floor to ceiling, pinned and layered and annotated in pencil until the paper beneath had nearly vanished, were maps.

Not printed maps. Drawn ones. Hand-inked, tide-tabled, annotated with thirty years of observation: the river's moods, the Narrows' currents hour by hour, the depth of the channel at Kestrel Bend in every season since the flood.

Cyrus stood in front of that wall the way other people stood in front of altars.

"You've been surveying," she said.

"I've been *watching*," Fennick said. "Surveying is what young people do when they think the land will hold still. Watching is what old people do once they know better." He ladled stew into two bowls without asking. "Sit. Eat. Then tell me why a Nystrom is driving a tea-kettle up my dead line in the rain, three weeks after Odile Marsh stopped writing to anybody."

The name landed in Cyrus's chest like a dropped coupling.

"You know about Odile."

"I know Odile Marsh sent me a letter every month for nineteen years, asking about the water level at the Bend, and then sent me nothing." He handed her a bowl. "I also know that four days ago a steam launch went upriver flying no colors at all, which on this coast means one color: money. And I know that yesterday morning, a man walked past my hut on the old tote road heading down toward Spur Nine, wearing boots too clean for the woods and carrying a rifle too good for deer." He blew on his stew. "So. Tell me your half, and I'll tell you mine, and between us we might make a whole."

So Cyrus told him. The silence from the expedition. The telegram from Odile's daughter in the city — three words, *find her first*, which was either a plea or a warning depending on how you read it. The salvage fleet massing at Port Ansel. The Meridian Star, sealed since the night she went down, and the treasury shipment the company men whispered about in bars when they thought the barmaids weren't taking notes. Barmaids always took notes; it was practically the job description.

Fennick listened the way he watched the river: completely, without interrupting, missing nothing.

When she finished, he set down his bowl and said, "Six tides."

"I'm sorry?"

"Six tides." He got up, joints cracking like green kindling, and went to the map wall. His finger found the estuary below Kestrel Bend, where the river widened and met the sea's pull. "Everyone thinks the drowned town sits in still water. It doesn't. It sits in the throat of the tide. Twice a day the sea pushes up the river and lifts the wreck — floats her hull just clear of the mud, turns her a few degrees, settles her down again. Like a sleeper turning over." He tapped the channel. "At high slack water, a diver can walk her decks. At low, she's buried to her rail in silt and the current runs hard enough to peel a boat's planking off. So whoever wants into that hold has to work the tides. And the spring tides—" his finger moved to a table penciled in the corner, "—begin tomorrow evening. Six working tides before the water drops away and doesn't come back high enough for a month."

"Six chances," Cyrus said.

"Six chances for them. Six chances for you." He looked at her over the beard. "And here's the arithmetic that matters, girl: a salvage outfit works tides one and two getting set up. Anchors, derricks, diver's gear. They don't touch the wreck till tide three. Which means if you're going to reach Odile Marsh before the money does, you have three tides to cover ground that took the mail boat two days in her prime."

"That's not possible."

"No," Fennick agreed comfortably. "But it's *nearly* possible, and nearly possible is the entire history of this railroad." He began pulling maps off the wall, selecting with the decisiveness of a man who had rehearsed this conversation with himself for years. "The High Grade dies at Mile Forty-One — slide took it out in '09 and nobody paid to fix it because the copper was done. From there you go on foot to the Kestrel tramway, which still has a hand-car on it if the tramps haven't burned it, which they haven't, because I pay them not to. The tramway drops you to the river at the old log dump. From the log dump—"

He paused. Something crossed his face that was almost embarrassment.

"From the log dump," Cyrus prompted.

"From the log dump you take the ferry."

"There is no ferry. There hasn't been a ferry since the flood."

"There's no ferry *on any map*," Fennick said, with dignity, "because the man who runs it does not wish to be mapped. He is, I will warn you now, the single most irritating human being on this coast, and I say that having attended forty years of company meetings. But he knows the river between here and the Bend better than the river knows itself, and he owes me a favor from a winter neither of us will discuss." He pressed the maps into her hands. "Go at dawn. You'll make the tramway by noon, the log dump by dark, and if the ferryman's mood permits, you'll be on the water for the second tide."

Cyrus looked down at the maps — his life's watching, surrendered in ten minutes to a stranger with soup on her chin.

"Why?" she asked quietly. "Why help me?"

Fennick poked the fire. For a moment the beard hid everything, and then the blue eyes came up over it, and they were not old at all.

"Because in 1898 I ran the section gang that laid track through Kestrel Bend," he said. "Nice town. Church, schoolhouse, a hotel with a piano nobody could play. Four hundred souls. And I remember the morning the company's own surveyor stood on the riverbank and told the owners the levee would fail, and I remember the owners telling him to keep it out of the report because a failure would scare the bondholders." He stirred the coals. "That surveyor's name was Corvan. He kept it out of the report. The town drowned anyway, and everyone said it was the weather." The poker clinked against iron. "Weather gets blamed for a lot on this coast, Miss Nystrom. I've spent thirty years learning what else it's blamed for. If Odile Marsh went silent on purpose, it's because she found something worth silencing. So yes. I'll help."

* * *

Dawn came gray and rinsed-clean, the fog burned off to show the mountains scrubbed and glistening, and Cyrus rolled the Puffin out of the rock shelter onto wet rails that steamed faintly in the early light like something alive and breathing.

Fennick stood on the ballast beside the cab, having insisted on seeing her off, holding a parcel wrapped in oilcloth.

"Bread, cheese, matches, and a flare," he said. "The flare's for the ferryman. Show it to him and tell him the winter of '11 is paid in full. He'll grumble. Grumbling is how he says yes."

"And if I need to signal you?"

"Ring the bell at the Kestrel tramway summit. Three strokes. I'll hear it if the wind's right, and if the wind's wrong I'll hear it anyway, because I've spent thirty years listening for bells on this line." He stepped back and patted the Puffin's running board, twice, the way you'd pat a horse. "Take care of her. She's older than she looks and so am I."

Cyrus climbed aboard, cracked the throttle, and felt the little engine gather herself under her like a dog deciding to run.

"Mile Forty-One," she said aloud, to the Puffin, to the mountain, to the six tides already turning somewhere far ahead in the river's throat. "Let's see what's left of us when we get there."

The Puffin answered in her usual language — a cough, a clang, a surge of stubborn power — and took the grade.

Behind her, growing smaller with every curve, the section keeper raised one hand, then turned back up the path to his hut, his bell, and his thirty years of watching.

Ahead, around the shoulder of the mountain, the rails ran on into a country no train had crossed in twenty years — and on the wind, faint but unmistakable, drifting up from the river far below, came the sound Cyrus had been dreading since Port Ansel.

A steam whistle. Not hers. Not the mail boat's.

Deep-throated, confident, expensive — the whistle of a salvage tug, and it was blowing for the bend at the mouth of the Kestrel, hours ahead of any schedule Fennick had drawn.

They weren't waiting for the third tide after all.
