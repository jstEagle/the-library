## 3. What the River Keeps

The tide office at Cape Reckoning smelled of tar, old paper, and the particular melancholy of men who had spent their lives predicting water that never did what they asked.

Cyrus pushed through the door at half past seven in the morning, before the clerks had finished their coffee, and found exactly who she'd hoped to find: old Bramwell Hodge, seventy-some years of age, asleep in a chair by the window with his boots crossed on a second chair and a tide almanac open on his chest like a fallen bird.

"Bramwell," she said.

He woke the way he always did — instantly, completely, as though sleep had merely been a formality he observed out of politeness. "Nystrom." He squinted at her. "You've got river mud on your boots and a look on your face like a woman about to do something foolish."

"I need to know about Kestrel Bend."

Bramwell took his boots off the chair slowly, which was how he took everything, and studied her with eyes gone pale as sea glass but still sharp underneath. "Kestrel Bend drowned in '61. Whole town under nine feet of water when they raised the weir for the reservoir scheme. You know this. Everybody knows this. It's the one thing about this coast everybody agrees on."

"I know why it drowned. I want to know what it was before."

"And why would a railroad engineer care about a drowned farming town?"

"Because Odile Marsh is camped above it and won't answer her wire."

Bramwell was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, the harbor was doing its morning business — gulls arguing over fish heads, a trawler backing out with its engine coughing like an old man climbing stairs. When he spoke again, his voice had changed register, dropped into the low careful tone he used for things that mattered.

"Sit down, girl."

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit. This is a sitting-down story." He waited until she pulled a stool from beneath the chart table, then nodded once, satisfied, and settled back into his own chair. "Kestrel Bend was a river town. Grain, timber, one good dock, two bad churches. And in the summer of 1919 — before your time, before mine even, I was a boy hearing my father tell it — there came upriver a steam packet called the *Meridian Star*, riding so low in the water her scuppers were kissing the current."

"The treasury shipment," Cyrus said.

"So you've heard that much." He gave her a look. "Everyone's heard that much. That's the trouble with treasure stories — the treasure is the only part anybody remembers. Nobody remembers the men."

"Tell me the men."

Bramwell rose, creaking, and went to a cabinet against the far wall. From its bottom drawer he drew a ledger bound in cracked green leather, swollen with damp, its spine mended twice with different-colored thread. He set it on the table between them with the gentleness of a man laying down a sleeping cat.

"Tide office ledger, 1919 through 1921. Every vessel registered moving cargo on this stretch of river. My father kept it. I inherited it along with the chair and the gout." He opened it to a ribbon-marked page. "Now. The *Meridian Star* was owned by a partnership. Four names."

He turned the book toward her. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea, but the handwriting was firm, Victorian-firm, each letter standing at attention:

*Halloway & Voss Salvage and Riverworks. Partners: E. Halloway. M. Voss. J. Corvan. R. Aldery.*

Cyrus read the names twice. "Corvan."

"There's a Corvan still living up past the bend," Bramwell said. "Tamsin. Daughter of J. Corvan, if the gossip holds true, and the gossip usually does hold true on this river because nobody has anything better to do than talk." He tapped the page. "But here's the part that matters, Nystrom, the part the salvage fleet boys don't put in their prospectuses. Look at the dates."

She looked. Entries in the margin, small notations beside each partner's name:

*E. Halloway — sole signatory after March 1920.*
*M. Voss — deceased, February 1920. Drowning.*
*J. Corvan — departed district, April 1920.*
*R. Aldery — deceased, January 1921. Drowning.*

"Drowning," Cyrus said. "Both of them."

"River towns lose people to the water. It happens." Bramwell closed the ledger and rested his hand flat upon it, as if holding something down. "But Voss drowned in February, in a dry winter, in three feet of slack water off his own dock, with no drink in him according to the coroner — and the coroner was Halloway's brother-in-law, which is a detail worth savoring. And Aldery drowned fourteen months later, the very week the survey maps of the wreck site went missing from the county office."

Cyrus sat very still. Through the window she could see the salvage fleet's masts at the far quay, three vessels now where there had been two yesterday, cranes being swung aboard with the busy optimism of men who expected to get rich.

"Halloway," she said. "Is he—"

"Dead these thirty years. His company passed to a nephew, and the nephew to a son, and the son is—" Bramwell waved a hand toward the harbor, toward the cranes, toward all of it. "Edmund Halloway the third. Owns the fleet. Owns half the docks. Owns, if rumor serves, at least one judge and portions of two magistrates. A respectable man. Very generous to the church. Both of them."

"And Odile found the ledger?"

"Odile Marsh doesn't need me to tell her where ledgers are buried. She taught half this coast how to read water, and the other half how to read men." Bramwell leaned forward. "Now here's my question, and you'll answer it or you won't come back into this office: why do you care? Marsh cut you loose six years ago. Everyone knows that story too."

Cyrus looked at the ledger. At the faded ink. At the word *drowning* written twice in a dead man's neat hand.

"She didn't cut me loose," she said finally. "I walked. And I've spent six years telling myself it was because she was wrong about something, and I've never once been able to say what." She stood. "Maybe this is what I couldn't say."

Bramwell watched her for a long moment. Then he pushed the ledger across the table.

"Take it. My father would have wanted it used, and I'm too old to row upriver myself, which is the only reason I haven't." He held up one finger. "One condition. If you find Tamsin Corvan — and you will, because she finds people before they find her — you listen to her. All the way through. She's been waiting fifty years for somebody to listen all the way through, and most folk can't manage ten minutes of an old woman talking about the dead."

"I can manage it."

"I know you can. You were always good at listening. It was the other thing you were bad at." He smiled, and it made his face younger and sadder at once. "Apologizing. Go on, then. Tide turns at noon. If you're going upriver, go with it."

* * *

She went home first, to the narrow house on Coil Street with the railroad lamps in the windows and the map room at the back where the afternoon light came in amber through salt-hazed glass.

The map room was the one clean argument in her life. Every spur line, every abandoned siding, every trestle that had rotted or burned or simply given up between Cape Reckoning and the high country — drawn, dated, annotated in her own cramped hand. Twenty years of walking dead rails. Odile had taught her the habit: *a line is never dead, Cyrus, it's only resting. Water remembers routes better than men do, and rails follow water.*

She cleared the big table and unrolled the river charts, weighting the corners with lamp oil cans, and began to work.

The upper river above Kestrel Bend was a puzzle she knew in fragments. The main channel ran deep and honest as far as the old weir; above that it braided into channels that rearranged themselves every spring flood like a card sharp shuffling for his own amusement. There were three ways up to the survey country. The steamer route, long and exposed — any salvage boat with a spyglass would see her coming a day out. The logging road on the east bank, washed out in two places since her last survey. And Spur Nine.

Spur Nine. She traced it with one finger, feeling the old ache the way you feel an old scar. The branch line Odile had sent her to walk alone, the summer everything broke between them. Six miles of rail along the cliff shelf above the narrows, abandoned since the slide of '09, and every year since, the river had eaten a little more of the shelf beneath it.

*It's a death trap,* she had told Odile, nineteen years old and certain of everything.

*It's a shortcut,* Odile had said, *and courage is mostly arithmetic.*

Cyrus stood over the map for a long time. Then she took a pencil and marked the tide tables in the margins — six tides, Bramwell had said, though he hadn't said how he knew, and she suspected he knew because Odile had wired him too, in that sideways way she communicated with everyone she refused to speak to directly.

Six tides. Twelve days, roughly, before the spring flood peaked and the narrows became impassable even for fools. The salvage fleet would take the steamer route and be slow and visible and certain of itself. She could be at Kestrel Bend in four days if she went by rail to the end of the living line, then walked the dead ones north.

She was rolling the charts when she saw it — a slip of paper that had ridden up inside the roll, thin as a leaf, folded twice. Not her handwriting. Small, precise, unhurried:

*Cyrus — If you're reading this, you finally opened the river charts, which means you're coming, which means I owe you either an apology or an explanation, and I haven't decided which yet. Ask Tamsin Corvan about the night of the 14th. Don't trust anyone whose boat has a crane on it. — O.M.*

Cyrus read it three times. Then she laughed — one short, involuntary bark of a laugh, at the sheer nerve of the woman, planting letters in other people's map rooms years in advance like a general salting a countryside with orders.

*Years in advance.* She stopped. Turned the note over. The paper was old. The fold-lines were soft with handling. Odile had written this — when? Before she went silent? Or before she ever left Cape Reckoning at all?

Before, Cyrus decided. Before. Which meant Odile had known she would go silent. Had planned it. Had planned, somehow, that Cyrus specifically would come looking.

"That manipulative old river witch," Cyrus said fondly, to the empty room, and put the note in her breast pocket, over her heart, where important documents and grudges both belonged.

* * *

She provisioned through the morning with the efficiency of someone who had done this many times and enjoyed it moderately: hard bread, smoked fish, coffee in a tin that had dented opinions about its life, rope, the good lantern, the spare lantern she would inevitably need when the good one failed. At the chandler's she bought lamp oil and, after a visible internal struggle, resisted buying a beautiful but unnecessary brass compass shaped like a fish.

"You're going upriver," said the chandler, Mrs. Petraglia, wrapping the oil in brown paper. It wasn't a question. Nothing in Cape Reckoning ever was.

"I'm going upriver."

"The Halloway boats went up Tuesday. Big fellow came in the day before, bought chain and grapnels, paid in new notes, tipped like a man apologizing for something." Mrs. Petraglia handed over the parcel. "He asked after you, you know."

Cyrus paused. "After me."

"Wanted to know if you still kept the old spur maps. I told him I don't discuss customers." She sniffed. "Which is technically true, since you're not a customer until you buy the compass, and we both know you're not going to buy the compass."

"I'm not going to buy the compass."

"Pity. It's a fine compass." Mrs. Petraglia's eyes, small and bright as currants, held hers. "Whatever's up there, Nystrom — the river keeps what it keeps, and it gives it back on its own schedule, and men with cranes have never once hurried that schedule along without making everything worse. Mind the narrows. Mind the flood. And mind that if Odile Marsh went quiet on purpose, she had a reason with teeth in it."

"Everyone keeps telling me to mind things."

"We're a minding kind of town." The chandler smiled briefly. "Go on. Noon tide waits for no one, except when it does, and then it's insufferable about it."

* * *

The living line ended at Marrow Junction, forty miles upcoast, where the last working siding curled into a depot the color of rust and regret. Cyrus rode the mail car north with a driver named Fenn who talked the entire way about his hens, each of whom had a name, a personality, and a criminal record.

"And that's the one that killed the other one," Fenn said, pointing at nothing, somewhere around mile thirty. "Henrietta. Lovely bird. Absolute murderer."

"What happened to the victim?"

"Ate her, mostly. That's the thing about Henrietta." He shook his head with genuine philosophical sorrow. "You love a thing, and it eats its sister, and you still feed it every morning. That's chickens for you. That's people too, if I'm honest."

At Marrow Junction the world changed key. South of it: wires, whistles, schedules. North of it: the dead lines, silver with weather, running straight into country where the forest had begun quietly reclaiming the right-of-way, alder and birch shouldering up between the ties like children interrupting a conversation.

Cyrus shouldered her pack and started walking the rails.

There was a rhythm to it that her body remembered before her mind did — the tie, tie, tie of it, the slight cant of roadbed gone soft, the way you learned to read a line's health by sound, stepping and listening, the hum of good iron versus the dull knock of rail that had worked loose from its spikes. By late afternoon she had covered eleven miles and the river appeared on her left, wide and brown-green and moving with the patient confidence of something that had never once been lost.

She camped on a bridge abutment, the old trestle overhead black against the first stars, and drank coffee from the opinionated tin, and thought about the ledger.

*Two drownings. One convenient departure. One man left holding everything.*

And Odile, upriver in the dark, sitting on the proof, silent as a held breath — while below her, coming on slow and certain, a fleet full of men who wanted the cargo and had no interest at all in what else the *Meridian Star* might be keeping.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Cyrus saw it now, turning her cup in her hands. A sealed treasury shipment was money. But a wreck was also a room. A preserved, flooded, untouched room, holding whatever had been aboard her the day she went down — papers, correspondence, a partner's private log, anything Corvan or Voss or Aldery had put aboard for safekeeping because rivers were trusted more than partners, and rightly, as it turned out.

The river kept what it kept. And Odile Marsh had gone up to stand guard over it.

Cyrus banked her fire early and lay watching the stars wheel over the black trestlework, and somewhere in the gray hour before dawn she heard it — faint, downstream, carried on the water the way sound travels at night: engines. More than one. Steady, purposeful, pushing upriver against the current with all the patience of money.

The salvage fleet wasn't waiting for the tide tables.

They were already coming.

Cyrus sat up in the dark, pulled her boots on, and reached for the good lantern. Four days to Kestrel Bend, she'd figured. Looking at the black water, listening to those engines, she revised the arithmetic.

She'd have to make it three.
