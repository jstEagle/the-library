## 4. The Salvage Fleet

The harbor at Graywater Landing smelled of tar, dead fish, and money — the last one newest and least honest. Cyrus came down the hill road at first light with her satchel over one shoulder and Odile's letter folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and stopped at the crest where the whole bay opened below her, and understood at once that she was already too late.

Not too late to reach the wreck. Too late to reach it *first*.

There were nine boats in the anchorage where three had been a week ago. She counted them twice, the way you count a bad number hoping arithmetic will take pity on you. Two steam tugs, squat and black-chimneyed. A barge riding low with cranes lashed to its deck. A long, mean-looking salvage lighter with a derrick arm folded back like a mantis at prayer. And behind them, tied up at the company wharf with its paint still bright enough to insult the rest of the harbor, a white-hulled steam yacht flying the pennant of the Reckoning Salvage & Dredge Company.

Cyrus had grown up around boats the way other children grew up around weather. She knew what she was looking at. This wasn't an expedition. It was an argument, and every boat in it was a sentence.

"Well," she said to nobody, "that's not encouraging."

Nobody agreed with her, which she chose to find polite.

She went down into the town itself, because a town is always the cheapest map there is. Graywater Landing had been built by the railroad and abandoned by it in the same decade, which gave its streets the particular melancholy of a dog left tied outside a store that has closed forever. The boardwalks were gray as driftwood. The hotel's porch sagged in the middle like a tired horse's back. But the saloon was open, and the saloon, as any engineer worth her transit knew, was the true terminus of every line.

Inside, it was early enough that the only customers were men whose relationship with morning was professional rather than recreational: two deckhands splitting a plate of eggs, a harbor pilot drinking coffee like it owed him an apology, and a fat, cheerful man behind the bar polishing glasses that did not need it.

Cyrus took a stool, ordered coffee, and paid for information with the oldest currency there is, which is listening.

"—third trip down from the yards this month," the pilot was saying. "Brought up that derrick barge on the *Marta Lee*. You know what a derrick barge costs a day to feed? Nobody floats one for fun."

"Fun's got nothing to do with it," said one of the deckhands. "They've got papers. Wreck rights on the *Meridian Star*, signed, sealed, stamped. Judge Corvan's own court issued them."

At the name *Meridian Star* the bar went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when everyone in it has been waiting for someone else to say the thing.

Cyrus kept her eyes on her cup. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"Treasury ship," the second deckhand went on, encouraged. "Went down in the Narrows the last year of the war, running gold north. Half the territory's been dreaming about it for thirty years. Now here comes Mr. Aldous Vane—" he pronounced the name with the careful neutrality of a man who wanted very much to be overheard saying it "—with his lawyers and his wreck rights and his nine boats, going to pull a fortune out of the river like pulling a splinter."

"That's the rumor," said the pilot.

"That's what the paymaster says," said the deckhand. "I signed on. We all signed on. Hazard wages, double rations, and a bonus if we bring up so much as a spoon."

Cyrus sipped her coffee. It was terrible, which meant it was honest, and she liked it immediately.

Aldous Vane. She turned the name over. She'd heard it before, of course — everyone on the Cape had; the man owned half the mills and all of the patience of anyone who'd ever tried to collect a debt from him. But it snagged now on something older, something from the years when she'd been Odile Marsh's apprentice and Odile had talked in her sleep the way other people talked in their cups. Names. Dates. A ledger. *Vane,* Odile had said once, sitting bolt upright in a rented bed in Kestrel Bend with dawn coming through the shutters, *kept the books that don't balance.*

Cyrus had been nineteen and had assumed it was a dream about arithmetic.

She was no longer nineteen.

* * *

The trouble with being a woman alone in a harbor full of hired muscle is that you are either invisible or interesting, and Cyrus could never predict which until it was too late. She made it off the stool, out the door, and halfway along the boardwalk before she became interesting.

"You there. Miss. Hold up."

He was big the way furniture is big, in a checked coat that had been cut for a bigger man, with a face like a fist that had learned manners late in life. He fell into step beside her with the amiable menace of a dog that has been told to fetch rather than bite.

"Mr. Quill," he said, offering a hand like a ham. "Company man. Mr. Vane's chief of operations, more or less. I couldn't help noticing you were noticing us."

"I notice everything," Cyrus said. "It's cheaper than a newspaper."

Quill laughed, genuinely, which was somehow worse. "That's good. That's a good line. You want to write copy? We're hiring a chronicler for the expedition. Mr. Vane likes history written while it happens." He walked with her, matching her pace without seeming to, which took talent. "Fact is, Miss —?"

"Nystrom."

"Miss Nystrom. Fact is, we've had a survey crew go quiet on us. Up past Kestrel Bend. Led by a Dr. Marsh — Odile Marsh, you'd know her maybe, famous woman, maps and such. They were doing preliminary soundings for us. Contract work." He said it lightly, but Cyrus felt the sentence settle over her shoulders like a wet coat. "Now they've gone dark, and Mr. Vane is a man who dislikes surprises. So I'll ask plain: you wouldn't know anything about that, would you?"

"Why would I?"

"Because you're carrying a surveyor's satchel, wearing a surveyor's boots, and you came down the hill road from the direction of the old rail depot where Dr. Marsh kept her field office, and you've been in that saloon twenty minutes asking nothing and listening to everything, which is exactly what Dr. Marsh does, God help us both." He smiled. It was a fine smile, well maintained. "Also your name is Nystrom, and there's a Cyrus Nystrom who used to run the spur lines, and Dr. Marsh's file cabinet had your name in it underlined twice."

Cyrus considered several replies and discarded them for lack of legal standing. "Underlined twice," she said instead. "Flattering."

"She thought highly of you. Past tense is doing some work in that sentence, mind. Because right now, Miss Nystrom, Dr. Marsh is either lost, drowned, or hiding, and each of those is a problem, and problems cost money, and Mr. Vane does not like spending money on problems he can solve with people." Quill stopped walking. The boardwalk creaked under them both. "So here's my offer, generous as sunrise. Come aboard. Ride upriver with the fleet. Help us find her. You get hazard wages, your mentor gets found, everybody wins, and nobody has to be dramatic about anything."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no," Quill said, spreading his hands, "and I write your name in my book anyway, because I write everyone's name in my book, and the book goes to Mr. Vane, and Mr. Vane reads it on the yacht with his little glasses on, and then things happen downstream of that which neither of us controls." He tipped an imaginary hat. "No hurry. Fleet sails on the afternoon tide."

He ambled off toward the wharf, a large man making small sounds, and Cyrus stood on the broken boards of Graywater Landing and let herself feel, briefly and thoroughly, afraid.

Then she did what she always did with fear, which was to make a list.

* * *

The list looked like this:

One. The fleet sailed on the afternoon tide, which meant the tide was her schedule now, whether she liked it or not.

Two. They had wreck rights, money, cranes, and nine boats. She had a satchel, a letter, and a head full of dead rail lines. On paper this was not a contest. But paper, Cyrus had spent her whole life learning, was exactly where contests were decided — and she had something on paper they didn't have. She had the spur lines. Every abandoned grade, every washed-out culvert, every stretch of track the Cape had forgotten and the river had half-swallowed. The fleet would go up the main channel, slow and loud, dragging their draft behind them. She could go up the spine of the country the way the rails used to, quiet as a rumor.

Three. Odile had gone silent *deliberately*. Cyrus didn't know how she knew it yet, only that the letter in her satchel read less like a cry for help than like a held breath. *Don't trust the company. Don't come by water. Trust the river.* You didn't write that to a rescue party. You wrote that to a witness.

Four. There was a survivor in Kestrel Bend. Odile's letter had named her — Tamsin Corvan — and the name had landed in Cyrus's stomach like a dropped wrench, because Corvan was the judge who'd signed the wreck rights. Either that was a coincidence, or it was the most dangerous kind of family reunion, and Cyrus had never once in her life benefited from a coincidence.

Five. Aldous Vane had murdered his partners. Not suspected — the letter hadn't quite said it, but it had leaned on it the way a bridge leans on a pier, and Cyrus had spent enough years reading Odile's handwriting to read her hesitations too. Thirty years ago, four men had gone into the treasury business and one ship had gone down in the Narrows with the gold and three of the partners conveniently aboard. The river had been called an accident. The river, Cyrus was beginning to understand, had been called a great many things it did not deserve.

She bought supplies with the last of her honest money: rope, lamp oil, hardtack, a coil of copper wire, and, after brief negotiation, the harbor pilot's spare skiff, which he sold her with the expression of a man divesting himself of a gambling debt. Its name, painted hopefully on the stern, was *Second Chance*. Cyrus found this either ominous or encouraging and declined to decide which.

By noon she had her plan, such as it was. Take the old coastal tramway north out of Graywater to the junction at Mile Forty. From there, the Kestrel Branch — dead twelve years, but grades don't die, they just wait. Follow it upriver on foot and handcar if any handcar survived, which was doubtful, and on foot and stubbornness if it didn't. Reach Kestrel Bend ahead of the fleet, find Tamsin Corvan, find Odile, and do it all before nine boats full of hired muscle dragged the truth out of the Narrows along with the gold.

It was a bad plan. She acknowledged this fairly. It had the structural integrity of a footbridge in flood season. But bad plans had gotten her across worse country than this, and besides, the alternative was Quill's offer, and she had seen his book.

She was loading the skiff — not to sail it yet, but because a boat is a fine place to think — when the yacht's whistle blew, one long note rolling across the harbor, and the whole anchorage began to move at once. Deckhands cast off lines. The derrick barge's crane swung up like a finger pointing north. Steam climbed from the tugs in white columns, and the *Second Chance* rocked gently at her mooring as the wakes reached her, patient as a dog hearing its name.

And there, at the yacht's rail, stood a small neat man in a gray suit, looking at the water through little glasses. He was too far away for Cyrus to see his face. She didn't need to. Some men you recognize by their posture alone — the posture of a person reading a ledger, deciding which column you belong in.

Aldous Vane raised one hand, briefly, toward the shore. A wave, perhaps. A greeting to the town.

Or a salute to the single figure standing on the broken boards with a surveyor's satchel, watching him go.

Cyrus did not wave back. She wrote his name in the margin of her map instead, next to the Narrows, in pencil, small — the way you mark a rock that has sunk ships.

Then she shouldered the satchel, cast off the *Second Chance*'s line, and rowed for the mouth of the old tramway slough, where the dead rails ran north into the fog like stitches holding a wound closed.

Behind her, the salvage fleet fell in line and followed the honest channel, loud and certain and wrong.

Ahead, somewhere past Mile Forty, the river waited with everything it kept.
