## 5. Spur Nine

The handcar was called Bessie, which Cyrus had not named her and resented slightly, because the man who had — a section foreman named Dill Hargrove, dead eleven years now of a heart that gave out the same week the rails did — had also named his mule Bessie and his boat Bessie, and there was something in that repetition that told you everything about how Dill's mind worked. He found one good thing and he stuck to it.

Cyrus pumped the handle and tried to think kindly of him anyway. It was hard. The morning fog off the estuary had soaked through her coat, her boots were squelching in a way that suggested she'd be peeling them off like skin by nightfall, and Bessie the handcar had a wheel bearing that sang a note somewhere between a kettle and a dying gull every fourth rotation.

*Whine-whine-whine-KEEEEER.*

"You're doing it again," said Tamsin Corvan from the rear platform, where she sat with her back against the tool chest and her boots up on a crate of survey stakes she absolutely should not have let Odile Marsh's expedition leave behind in the Kestrel Bend depot. "The face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a whole weather system of faces. Right now it's low pressure moving in from the northwest." Tamsin tilted her head back against the chest and closed her eyes, gray eyes gone milky with the fog, though Cyrus knew she wasn't looking at anything anyway. Hadn't looked at anything since the fever took her sight at nine years old. That hadn't stopped her from being the best reader of people on the whole Cape, which was its own kind of seeing, and which was currently being aimed at Cyrus like a lamp. "You're thinking about Dill."

"I'm thinking about the bearing."

"You're thinking about Dill *through* the bearing. You do that. You get mad at dead men through machinery." She said it without judgment, the way she said most things, and then added, "He'd have liked this. Us out here. He always said the line would outlive the town."

"The line outlived three towns," Cyrus said. "It's not sentimental, it's just well-built."

"That's what sentiment is, mostly. Things built better than they needed to be, for people who never came back." Tamsin smiled at the fog. "Dill taught me that too. Or maybe I made it up and gave him credit. Hard to say anymore."

Cyrus pumped harder, partly for speed and partly so she wouldn't have to answer, because Tamsin had a way of saying true things while lying flat on her back on a handcar that made them impossible to argue with.

They were eleven miles up Spur Nine, which was not supposed to be passable, which was precisely why Cyrus had chosen it. The salvage fleet would come upriver — the *Meridian Star*'s wreck lay in the drowned channel above Kestrel Bend, and water was the only road wide enough for their cranes and barges. The old rail spurs, abandoned when the flood took the valley twenty-six years ago, ran along the ridge shoulders where the water couldn't follow. Nobody used them. Nobody remembered them. Except, apparently, whoever had been through here recently enough to leave tracks.

She'd seen them an hour ago: fresh grease on the fishplates at the junction, a cigarette end tucked under a tie with the paper still white. Somebody had walked this line within days. Maybe hours.

She hadn't told Tamsin yet.

* * *

The fog began to lift around midmorning the way fog does on the Cape — not lifting so much as losing interest, thinning into rags that hung in the alders and then gave up entirely, leaving a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Below them, through breaks in the trees, the drowned valley showed itself in pieces: rooftops just breaking the surface of the flooded channel, a church steeple leaning like a drunk against nothing, the black fingers of a pier reaching out of green water.

Kestrel Bend. Or the top half of it. The river had taken the bottom half without apology when the dam failed, and the territory had taken the rest by forgetting.

"Tell me what we're passing," Tamsin said. She always asked, on the water or the rails, wherever the world went by too fast for her hands to read it. It was the only favor she ever asked, and Cyrus had learned early that refusing it — or worse, describing things *kindly*, editing out the rust and rot — offended her more than cruelty would have.

"Depot's coming up on the left. Half the roof's gone. There's a rowboat tied to the loading dock, upside down, painted blue once. Somebody's garden is growing out of the second-story window box of the hotel — dill, I think, and something yellow."

"Dill," Tamsin repeated, delighted. "The universe has a sense of humor after all."

"There's a heron standing on the church steeple. It looks like it's giving a sermon."

"To a congregation of rooftops." Tamsin nodded slowly. "Small turnout. Times are hard."

Cyrus laughed despite herself, and the laugh bounced off the cut walls and came back sounding younger than she felt.

They rolled into the depot siding and Cyrus set the brake, and the silence afterward was enormous — the particular silence of places where the water had swallowed all the noise and kept it. She could hear the river below, a low continuous mutter, like someone reading aloud in another room.

Tamsin swung down first, cane tapping, reading the platform by touch. "Fresh wood here," she said, crouching. "New-sawn. And rope burns on the bollard. Someone's been tying off." Her fingers walked along the dock edge and stopped. "And this — wax? Candle stub, still soft."

Odile's expedition had come through here. Of course they had; the depot was the natural staging point for any approach to the wreck from the land side. But the candle stub bothered Cyrus. Odile ran a tight camp — she'd drilled that into every student, *a surveyor who wastes supplies wastes lives* — and Odile did not leave candles burning down to stubs on open docks.

Unless she hadn't left them. Unless someone else had been here after.

"Stay close to the cars," Cyrus said.

"I was going to say the same to you." Tamsin's voice had lost its humor, which meant it had gotten serious, which meant pay attention. "There's someone else on this platform, Cyrus. Has been. Recently. I can smell tobacco — pipe, not cigarette — and underneath it, lamp oil. Someone stood right about where you are and burned something."

Cyrus looked down. At her feet, half-hidden in the gravel between two ties, lay a scrap of charred paper no bigger than a playing card. She picked it up carefully. Most of it was ash and ghost, but one corner survived, and on it, in a small precise hand she would have known anywhere — a hand that had corrected her homework for six years and signed her journeyman certificate —

*— not the cargo. Never the cargo. The names.*

Odile's handwriting. Odile's words. Burned, but not thoroughly — as if the person burning it had been interrupted, or as if Odile herself had burned it in front of witnesses and wanted the witness to find the piece that survived.

Or as if Odile had wanted *her* to find it. Odile knew Cyrus kept the maps. Odile knew Cyrus would come up the spurs, because the spurs were the only honest roads left, and Odile had taught her that too, back when the lessons were about surveying and not about murder.

"Cyrus," Tamsin said quietly. "Your breathing changed."

"Someone burned a page of Odile's notes on this platform."

"And left a corner?"

"And left a corner."

Tamsin was quiet a moment, her head cocked, listening to the river muttering below. Then: "Is that a message or bait?"

"That," said Cyrus, folding the fragment into the oilskin wallet next to her father's watch, "is the question that keeps surveyors alive."

* * *

They ate cold lunch in the lee of the depot wall — salt pork, hard bread, and the last of the crabapples Tamsin had shaken down from a tree in somebody's drowned front yard, which were sour enough to make conversation difficult and therefore excellent company. Cyrus spread the Spur Nine chart across her knees and weighted the corners with stones.

Beyond the depot, the line climbed. That was the trouble with Spur Nine, and the reason nobody used it: two miles ahead the grade steepened through a rock cut, and at the top of the cut the old trestle over Gannet Creek had burned sometime in the years nobody was counting. Twenty yards of gap. A handcar didn't jump gaps. Neither did people, generally.

"There's a bypass," Cyrus said, tracing it. "Old haul road on the north shoulder. Steep, washed out in three places, but walkable if we lighten load and take the car apart. Two trips each."

"Or," Tamsin said, "we ask the ferryman."

Cyrus looked up. "What ferryman?"

"The one who's been watching us for ten minutes from the water side. Don't turn your head, he'll think we're rude. He poling a flatboat, and he's got a dog, and the dog is very interested in our crabapples." She paused. "Also he's humming. Badly. Something that might be a hymn or might be a work song, depending on his mood."

Cyrus turned her head.

The flatboat hung in the green water thirty feet off the drowned loading dock, and the man poling it was broad and gray-bearded and wore a sou'wester that had been patched so many times it was less a hat than a biography. The dog at the bow was a brindled thing of no particular ancestry, ears up, tail going like a metronome set to allegro. The man stopped humming when he saw he'd been noticed, considered his options, and then raised one hand in a wave of profound unhurried friendliness.

"Morning!" he called. His voice carried the rounded vowels of the upper river. "You'll be the map-woman, then!"

Cyrus went very still. "I might be. Who's asking?"

"Ferryman," he said, as if that settled everything, which in a sense it did. "Name's Ambrose Pike. Everyone calls me Ferryman, including my mother, rest her. Word came down the water yesterday that a woman with a handcar and a blind girl would come up Nine looking for the Marsh woman." He poled closer, unhurried. "Word also came that I'm to tell you: *the ledger sleeps under the third bell.*"

The words dropped into Cyrus's chest like a stone into a well. She heard them fall a long way before they landed.

"That's a strange message," she managed.

"It's a strange river," said Ferryman agreeably. "Dog's name is Bishop. He'd like a crabapple. So would I, frankly, if you're sharing."

* * *

They talked for an hour, there on the drowned dock with the water lapping at pilings that had held a roof over travelers once. Ambrose Pike had lived in Kestrel Bend before the flood — had been fourteen when the dam went, had watched his street become a canal — and he'd stayed after, one of the handful who never left, running passengers and freight along the drowned channel in a boat his father had built. He knew every submerged fence line and chimney. He spoke of the town the way other men spoke of a wife who'd died young: plainly, with love, without self-pity.

And he knew Odile Marsh. Had ferried her expedition up-channel six weeks ago, seven of them, quiet people with quiet instruments. And he'd ferried one of them back down again, alone, ten days later — a big man with a city coat and city boots, who paid in new-minted silver and asked questions about the wreck's cargo manifest that a surveyor had no business asking.

"He weren't one of hers," Ambrose said. "She knew it too. I saw her watching him from the bank, and her face —" he shook his head — "her face was like ice deciding whether to crack."

"The salvage fleet," Cyrus said. "Corvan Metals. They've bought the harbor office, the magistrate, probably the weather. They're coming upriver for the treasury shipment."

"They can have the shipment." Ambrose said it flatly, and then, softer: "It's not the money this river's holding. You know that, or you wouldn't be up here burning your boots on a dead line." He nodded at the fragment in her wallet, because of course he'd seen her take it; a man who reads water reads everything. "Marsh left that for you. Deliberate. She asked me would the map-woman come, and I said the map-woman keeps the lines, and she said, *then she'll come, because the lines are the only promises this territory ever kept.*"

Tamsin, sitting cross-legged with Bishop's enormous head in her lap, scratched his ears and said, "Third bell. What's the third bell?"

Ambrose's face changed. Not darkened exactly — deepened, the way water deepens over a hole.

"Kestrel Bend had three churches," he said. "Only one steeple still stands — the one with the heron. The other two bells went down with the roofs. First bell's in the old Methodist, forty feet down off the channel mark. Second bell's the schoolhouse, smaller, brass. Third bell—" he looked out at the green water, at the leaning steeple, at the drowned roofs holding their breath below — "third bell is St. Brigid's, and it never fell with the church. The priest took it down and sank it himself, the last week, on purpose. Said some things ought to be buried where the whole town could sit on top of them." He turned back to Cyrus. "Under the third bell is where Odile told me to tell you the ledger sleeps. And under the third bell is also where I'm telling you not to go until the tide turns, because the current through that channel will take a strong swimmer and hand him back to his mother in pieces."

Bishop sneezed into Tamsin's lap, profoundly.

"So we wait for slack water," Cyrus said.

"We wait for slack water," Ambrose agreed. "Which gives us—" he squinted at the sky, reading it like a timetable — "about four hours. Long enough to get the handcar over the trestle gap, if you're set on the rail. Or long enough for me to pole you up-channel proper, quiet, under the fog line, past the fleet's picket boat, which is anchored at the Narrows and whose pilot drinks." He grinned, sudden and boyish under the gray beard. "I know his brand."

Cyrus looked at Tamsin. Tamsin, who had heard all of it, was already smiling the smile she saved for decisions already made.

"She wants the boat," Tamsin translated. "And honestly, Cyrus, I want the boat. My boots are ruined, the handcar sings in a key that hurts my teeth, and this gentleman has a dog."

"Bishop decides nothing," Ambrose said. "But yes, the boat."

Cyrus looked down the drowned channel, toward the Narrows, toward the wreck she could not see and could feel anyway, the way you feel a storm behind a hill. Somewhere up there, Odile Marsh was keeping silence like a held breath. Somewhere up there, a fleet was coming with cranes and lawyers and men with city coats. And somewhere under the green water, under a bell a priest had sunk with his own hands, a ledger slept with names in it — names that a murdered man's partners had trusted to the river, because the river was the only witness left who couldn't be bought.

"All right," Cyrus said. "The boat. But we go quiet, and we go at slack, and if I say dive, everyone dives."

"Aye," said Ambrose Pike, and spat companionably over the side. "That's the spirit. Now — about those crabapples."

They were loading the boat, the tools and the oilskin charts and the crate of survey stakes, when Bishop stiffened at the bow, ears swiveling, and growled — low, once, aimed up-channel.

Out of the ragged mist to the north, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Cyrus had hoped not to hear for another day: the deep-throated churn of a steam launch, running fast, running heavy, and closing.

Ambrose's grin vanished. "That'll be the picket boat," he said quietly, "and she's not at the Narrows anymore."
