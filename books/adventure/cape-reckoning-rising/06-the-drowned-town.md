## 6. The Drowned Town

The fog came off the water like a held breath, and Cyrus Nystrom walked into it with her boots in one hand and her socks stuffed into the other.

Barefoot was the only way to do it. The mudflats of Kestrel Bend, exposed now by the third low tide of the season, were paved with the town itself — roof slates, bottle glass, door hinges, the occasional tooth of a church pew — and she had no intention of explaining to Odile Marsh, if she ever found her, why her expedition had ended with a tetanus shot and a limp.

"Mind the well shafts," Tamsin Corvan had told her that morning, back at the ferry landing, in the tone of a woman giving directions to a neighbor's house rather than warnings about a graveyard. "There's eleven of them still open. The river took the covers but not the holes. And don't step on anything that rings."

"Rings?"

"Bell bronze. The church bell went down whole. It's under the silt by the old green, and when the tide drags a chain across it, you'll hear it. Folk used to say it meant someone was coming home." Tamsin had smiled then, and it had been the saddest smile Cyrus had ever seen on a living face. "It never once meant that."

So Cyrus walked barefoot through the drowned town of Kestrel Bend, forty-one years after the river took it, and tried not to think about bells.

* * *

She had left the *Plover* anchored in the channel off Spur Nine with the tide tables weighted under an iron spike, because the last thing she needed was to come back up from the flats and find her boat doing lazy circles toward the sea. The salvage fleet was two days behind her at most — she'd seen their mast lights working upriver the night before, patient as lighthouse beams — and every hour she spent here was an hour they gained.

But Odile's trail led here. It had to. The last entry in the survey journal Cyrus had recovered from the dead drop at the Narrows wasn't coordinates or depth soundings. It was a single line, in Odile's cramped, furious hand:

*K.B. — ask the one who stayed.*

Cyrus had turned that sentence over for a full day of poling upriver before understanding it. Ask the one who stayed. Everyone knew the story of Kestrel Bend, the way everyone knew any story that made a territory feel older than it was: the flood of '09, the dam company's broken promises, the town given four days' notice and eleven hours of actual warning, the people who loaded their pianos onto wagons and lost them anyway when the water came up faster than the engineers said it would. The town had emptied. The town had drowned. End of story.

Except stories never ended where the tellers got tired. Someone had stayed. And Odile Marsh, who did nothing without three reasons and a contingency, had gone silent on purpose to come ask them something.

Cyrus intended to find out what, and whether it was worth the salvage fleet burning diesel to get here first.

* * *

The fog thinned as she worked her way down what had been Harbor Street, though "street" was generous; it was a lane of cobbles the river had chewed into gravel, running between the shoulders of drowned houses whose upper floors broke the surface at high tide like the backs of sleeping animals. At low water, you could see the whole skeleton of the place. Doorsteps. Garden walls. A child's tricycle fused to a fence post by forty years of rust, its handlebars pointing inland, as if it had been trying to escape.

"Gently does it," she murmured to herself, testing each step. The mud had a crust that held for exactly one step longer than you trusted it, which was how it caught people. She'd learned that much from Tamsin, who had talked for two hours at the ferry landing while mending a net, and Cyrus had written none of it down because every word of it had lodged somewhere permanent.

Tamsin Corvan. The one who stayed.

Not stayed-stayed — she lived upriver now, in a shack on stilts near the old ferry crossing, close enough to walk down at every low tide. But she was the last living soul who had stood in these streets when they were streets, and she came down twice a day like a woman visiting a sick relative, picking her way along the same route every time: past the bakery foundation, past the schoolhouse steps, down to the green where the bell lay buried.

"She'll be down there," Tamsin had said. "Your surveyor. She comes at the ebb, always the ebb. Says the light's honest then." A snort. "Light's light. But I didn't argue. Woman pays for her tea in advance and doesn't touch my biscuits, which is more manners than the salvage men ever showed."

"You've met the salvage men?"

"I've met their money." Tamsin's needle had paused. "Man named Verrick came up from the fleet last month. Offered me a number with so many digits I thought he was reading me a serial code. Wanted to know where the Meridian Star's hold hatch sat, wanted to know if anyone had papers from before the flood." Her eyes had come up then, sharp as gulls. "I told him the river keeps what it keeps. He didn't like that answer. Said everything keeps for a price."

"And what did you say?"

"I said he should meet my late husband." She'd gone back to her net. "He's under the green there. Let him negotiate."

* * *

Cyrus found the green by feel before she found it by sight — the ground changed under her feet, firmer, older, packed by a century of market days before the water came. The fog lifted in rags around her, and there it was: the sunken square of Kestrel Bend, laid bare by the ebb, beautiful in the way that wounds are sometimes beautiful.

The church stood to the north end, half-collapsed, its steeple leaning like a drunk against the air. The bakery was a hip of brick with an iron oven door hanging open, black as a mouth. Along the east side ran a row of shopfronts, their false fronts still standing above the waterline at high tide, and painted on one — she could just make it out, ghost-letters under the silt film — **CORVAN & SONS, MARINE SUPPLY**.

And in the middle of the green, kneeling in the mud with her sleeves rolled and her hands black to the wrist, was a woman Cyrus had known for half her life and hadn't seen in six years.

Odile Marsh did not look up.

"I counted your footsteps from the bakery," Odile said, to the mud. "You walk like your mother. Heel first, apologetic."

"Hello to you too."

"You're standing on the old bandstand footing. Move left. There's a cellar trap under the silt and I haven't marked it yet."

Cyrus moved left. Six years, and the woman's first gift to her was a hazard assessment. Some things held true like bedrock.

"What are you doing?" Cyrus asked.

Odile sat back on her heels. She was thinner than Cyrus remembered, gray coming in at the temples like frost on a window, and there was a new scar along her jaw that Cyrus decided, in the interest of the mission, not to ask about. In front of her, half-excavated from the mud, lay a strongbox — bank-green, salt-eaten, its lock hanging open like a broken jaw.

"It's empty," Odile said. "They're all empty. I've opened nine of them since the spring tides started. Every safe, every strongbox, every cash drawer in this town, and every one of them was cleaned before the water came." She finally looked up, and her eyes were the same flat gray as the river, and just as full of things moving underneath. "Do you understand what that means, Cyrus?"

"That the townsfolk took their money when they evacuated."

"That's what it means if you want it to mean that." Odile wiped her hands on her trousers, accomplishing nothing. "But the bank ledger says otherwise. I pulled it from the bank vault two weeks ago. Final entries, dated the morning of the flood. Deposits made *during* the evacuation. People carrying their life savings down Harbor Street while the water was already in the lower lanes, and handing it to a teller who was still at his window. Why would they do that? Because they were told the bank vault was watertight. Because they were told they'd be back in a week to collect."

Cyrus felt the cold come up through the mud into her feet. "Who told them?"

"The bank's president. A man named Aldous Verrick."

The name landed like a dropped anchor. "Verrick. As in—"

"As in the man who owns the salvage fleet that's currently steaming up this river to tear this town apart looking for a treasury shipment." Odile stood, joints cracking, a woman built like a survey stake. "Aldous Verrick was president of the Kestrel Bend Trust. When the flood came, the vault contents vanished — not washed away, *vanished*, inventoried as lost, written off, insured. And eleven months later, a man named Aldous Verrick bought his first tugboat with money nobody could account for, and started a salvage company, and has spent forty years buying up river rights and wreck claims and judges, waiting for the technology and the law to catch up with the one wreck he could never legally touch." She smiled, and it was not a warm thing. "The Meridian Star. Sealed cargo. Treasury shipment. His own personal resurrection, sitting in sixty feet of water off the point."

"But the treasury shipment — that's real? That's separate from the bank money?"

"Oh, the treasury shipment is real. That's the beauty of it, from his perspective. Even if someone dug into the bank records, the Star's cargo is legitimate salvage on paper — pre-war currency shipment, declared lost, claim filed and granted by a judge he owns. He doesn't need the bank money anymore. The bank money is just—" she gestured at the empty strongbox between them, "—the pattern. The proof of who he is. The man cleans out the vaults and lets the river take the blame. He did it to this town, and he's spent forty years making sure no one alive could testify to it."

"Not no one."

Odile went very still. It was a skill of hers, going still — Cyrus had watched her do it in rockslides and boardrooms, the total suspension of a person, as if motion might cost something precious.

"Tamsin Corvan," Cyrus said. "She's the one who stayed. She's the last survivor. And her family name is on that shopfront, and her husband is under the green, and Verrick offered her a serial-code number for information, and she told him to go negotiate with a corpse." She crouched beside the strongbox. "That's why you went silent. Not hiding from the fleet. Hiding from *him*. Because if he knew you'd found the survivor, he'd—"

"He'd do what he's always done," Odile said quietly. "Which is why, Cyrus, I need you to listen very carefully, because I have spent six years being angry at you and I am about to ruin all of it by trusting you again."

Somewhere out in the fog, far down the river, a sound carried over the water. Faint. Mechanical. The rhythmic thump of a pump barge, working against the current.

Two days behind her, Cyrus had estimated.

She'd been optimistic by half.

* * *

They climbed out on the schoolhouse steps as the fog began to lift, sitting side by side like two women waiting for a class that had been dismissed forty years ago. Below them, the tide had turned; the first thin fingers of water were finding the old gutters, filling the streets from the bottom up, the town drowning again in miniature, twice a day, forever.

Odile talked for an hour. About the ledger. About the insurance filings with forged signatures. About a second document, the one she'd risked everything to confirm existed — a deposition, taken the winter after the flood, from the bank's own teller, a young man named Ellery Finch, who had helped load the vault contents onto the Meridian Star herself, three days *before* the flood, on Verrick's orders, and who had signed a statement and then recanted it when Verrick's lawyers arrived, and who had then, two years later, drowned in a boating accident that the coroner — also owned — ruled accidental.

"The deposition copy went missing from the county records. Verrick thinks it's destroyed. It isn't." Odile looked out at the rising water. "Finch sent it to someone he trusted the week before he died. Someone who kept it hidden for forty years, because keeping it hidden was the only way to keep it existing."

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin. Finch was her cousin. She's been sitting on the truth of this town since she was nineteen years old, alone, because everyone else who could corroborate it is either dead or bought." Odile's voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and Cyrus understood suddenly the shape of the silence, the six years, all of it — Odile had been carrying this alone the way Tamsin had, and had pushed away everyone she couldn't afford to lose. "I came down here to convince her to testify. She won't. Not yet. She says the law is bought, and she's right, and testimony to a bought court is just a confession with extra steps. We need something the law can't refuse."

"The river," Cyrus said slowly. "You said it yourself — the empty boxes. Physical evidence. If the vault contents went onto the Star, some of it's still in the wreck. Bank notes don't rot. If the salvage fleet raises that cargo, Verrick controls it forever. But if we can document the hold *before* they reach it — prove the bank seals, the dates, the connection—"

"Then the cargo becomes evidence instead of treasure." Odile nodded. "And evidence gets seized, and seizures get investigated, and investigations are the one thing forty years of bought judges can't fully insulate him from. It's narrow. It's insane. It requires us to beat a salvage fleet to a wreck in a narrowing channel during spring tides." She glanced sideways. "I don't suppose you still keep maps of the dead spur lines."

Cyrus reached into her coat and drew out the oilskin packet, and for a long moment neither of them moved, and the water rose gently over the bandstand footing, and over the tricycle, and up the schoolhouse steps toward their boots.

"I burned nothing yet," Cyrus said. "If that's what you're asking."

"It's not what I'm asking." Odile stood, and offered her hand — black-mudded, salt-cracked, steady as rail iron. "What I'm asking is whether you can get us to Spur Nine before that pump barge finds the channel marker. Because Tamsin has agreed to give her testimony — *conditionally* — and the condition is that we put it somewhere Verrick can't buy, burn, or drown it. And there's a ferryman upriver, an old friend of hers, who owes her a debt older than both of us, and he runs the Narrows at night, and he's the only way through that the fleet won't expect."

Cyrus took the hand. Down below, the water closed over the CORVAN & SONS sign, letter by letter, until only the ampersand showed, holding on.

"And if we're too slow?" she asked.

Odile looked down at the drowning street, and then upriver, where the fog was lifting to reveal, impossibly far off but unmistakable, a smudge of smoke against the pale sky.

"Then we'd best not be," she said.
