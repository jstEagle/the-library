## 7. Tamsin Corvan

The house sat on the last dry knuckle of land before the river took everything, and it had the look of a thing that had argued with the water for forty years and won on points.

Cyrus saw it first from the ridge above the old mill road — a low, salt-bleached cottage with a tin roof gone the color of a heron's back, a garden that was mostly driftwood arranged with suspicious intentionality, and a row of bottles strung on wire between two posts, catching the morning light like a string of small stubborn suns. Smoke rose from the chimney in a thin, unhurried thread. Somebody lived here. Somebody who, judging by the garden, did not intend to be rescued from anything.

The path down was knee-deep in sedge, and Cyrus's boots found every hidden channel of water the river had left behind as a parting joke. By the time she reached the gate — which was not a gate so much as a wagon wheel half-buried upright, with a gap to walk through — she had given up any pretense of arriving dry.

A woman stood in the doorway. She was small and square-built, somewhere past seventy, with hair the white of gull feathers cut short as a hedge, and she held a shotgun in the relaxed way of someone who had carried it so long it had become an arm. She looked Cyrus over once, top to bottom, the way a ferryman looks at cargo.

"You're the third one this week," she said.

"The third what?"

"Third one come asking about the Meridian Star." The woman spat neatly off the porch, a feat of accuracy Cyrus could only admire. "First one was polite. Second one offered me money. Which are you?"

"Neither, I hope." Cyrus raised both hands, palms out, mud dripping from her cuffs. "I'm looking for Odile Marsh."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes — quick, like a fish under ice. "Survey woman. Been upriver near three weeks now." She tilted her head. "You family?"

"Her student. Once." Cyrus hesitated. "More than that, maybe. It's complicated."

"Most true things are." The woman stepped aside, opening the door behind her. "Come in before the midges eat you. And wipe your boots. I don't care if you're drowning, you wipe your boots."

* * *

Inside, the cottage smelled of woodsmoke, dried fish, and lamp oil, and every surface told a story whether Cyrus wanted one or not. Charts hung from nails — hand-drawn, decades old, annotated in a cramped sure hand. A wall of photographs, most curled and silvered. On the table, a half-finished carving of a cormorant sat beside a cup of tea so strong it might legally have been tar.

"Tamsin Corvan," the woman said, offering a hand like a knot in good timber. "And you'll be Nystrom. The map woman."

Cyrus blinked. "How—"

"Odile talks in her sleep." Tamsin said it flatly, but there was warmth buried in it, deep down, like coal under ash. "Has done since she came here. Talks about rail lines and tide tables and a student who argued with her about gradients at three in the morning." She poured a second cup without asking. "Sit. You look like you've been walking since Tuesday."

"I've been walking since Sunday," Cyrus said, sitting, "but it feels like Tuesday."

The tea was terrible and perfect. Cyrus drank half of it in one go and felt her heartbeat change its mind about something.

"You know where she is," Cyrus said.

Tamsin lowered herself into the chair opposite, the shotgun leaning against the wall within reach, though Cyrus suspected that was habit rather than warning. "I know where she *was*. Up past the Narrows, at the bend where the town went under. She came down twice — once to ask my leave, once to bring me coffee she'd stolen from her own expedition cook, which tells you the state of things up there." Tamsin's mouth twitched. "Second time, she asked me questions I hadn't answered in forty years. And then she stopped coming down."

"When?"

"Nine days ago." Tamsin said it precisely, the way people say numbers that have been living inside them. "She said she'd be back by the third tide. That's tomorrow's tide, by my count. Or yesterday's, depending how you keep your ledger."

Cyrus set down the cup. "There's a salvage fleet coming upriver. Three boats, maybe more. They're after the Star's hold. And the man who owns them—" she paused, choosing, "—has reasons to want certain evidence at the bottom of the river to stay there."

Tamsin studied her for a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the sedge with a sound like slow applause.

"You say 'evidence,'" Tamsin said at last. "That's a lawyer's word. Odile used it too, second visit. Sat right where you're sitting and asked me if I remembered the night the Star came in early. Asked me if I remembered who signed the manifest." Her voice didn't change, but her hands did — they closed around the teacup, knuckles whitening, and then deliberately loosened, one finger at a time, like a woman setting down something heavy. "I told her no. I told her I was eleven years old and asleep."

"And were you?"

"No." Tamsin smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a person who has kept a secret the way other people keep a knife — oiled, wrapped, never forgotten. "But I was the only one left to remember it, and remembering is dangerous work when the men involved own the magistrates. So I slept on it. Forty years of sleeping on it." She looked out the window toward the river. "Turns out you can't drown a town and expect the water to forget."

* * *

The story came out slowly, the way water comes out of ground — not all at once, but everywhere at once, if you knew where to dig.

Kestrel Bend had been a working town once: a customs post, a chandlery, a church with a bell they'd rung for fog as much as for God. The Meridian Star had been a treasury packet running gold and bonds upriver ahead of the war's collapse — a shipment meant to buy the territory's debts down and its loyalties up. It had arrived early, in fog, on a night when the manifests said it should have been two days downstream.

"There were four partners in the salvage consortium that claimed the wreck rights," Tamsin said. "Four signatures on the recovery charter. And within six years, three of them were dead. One fell off a bridge. One took fever. One walked into the sea at Cape Reckoning with his coat pockets full of stone, which the coroner called melancholy because the coroner had a new house by then." She tapped the table with one blunt finger. "The fourth partner built a company on their graves and named it after his mother, which I have always thought was the foulest touch of all."

"Harrow Vane," Cyrus said quietly.

"You know him."

"I know his fleet is upstream of me and moving faster than I am."

Tamsin snorted. "Harrow Vane couldn't find his own conscience with a lantern and a search party. But he can find money. He's been sending men past my door for a month — polite ones, rude ones, one very memorable one who tried to buy my testimony sight unseen, which rather gave away the game." She leaned forward. "He knows something's written down somewhere. He doesn't know what, or he'd have burned this cottage around my ears already."

"Is something written down?"

Tamsin sat back. For a moment she just looked at Cyrus, and Cyrus had the strange sensation of being weighed — not her strength or her speed, but something else entirely. Her steadiness, maybe. Or her capacity for trouble.

"My father was the harbormaster," Tamsin said. "The night the Star came in early, he logged it. True arrival time, true draft marks, true count of crates moved ashore by lamplight — crates that never appeared on any manifest. He wrote it all in his private logbook, because my father was a careful man, and careful men die of it." Her jaw tightened. "Two years later he drowned in a river he'd swum since boyhood. The logbook went into a tin box, and the tin box went into the ground, and I have spent forty years being a harmless old woman with a garden full of driftwood."

She rose, joints cracking like green firewood, and went to the cold hearth. She reached up into the chimney throat — past the soot, past the swallows' old nest — and brought down a brick that wasn't a brick. Behind it, a cavity. From the cavity, a tin box, rusted at the corners, light as a bird's bone.

She set it on the table between them.

"I'm too old to testify," she said. "My memory wanders, my hands shake, and Harrow Vane's lawyers would make mincemeat of me before lunch. But paper doesn't age badly if you keep it dry, and my father's handwriting was carved by angels." She pushed the box an inch toward Cyrus. "Odile asked me for this nine days ago. I told her I needed to think. Then she didn't come back down, and I've been thinking ever since, and what I've thought is that I'm a selfish old woman who let a better woman walk into danger carrying half the truth."

Cyrus looked at the box. It seemed very small for what it held. Most important things did.

"Why give it to me? You've known me ten minutes."

"Forty minutes," Tamsin corrected. "And because Odile talked about you in her sleep, and a woman's sleep doesn't lie even when her letters do. She wrote you off years ago — pride, mostly, hers — but she never once dreamed about anyone else's maps." Tamsin's eyes crinkled. "Also, you wiped your boots."

"That's the deciding factor?"

"It's the whole of civilization, girl. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

They opened the box together at the table. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a logbook bound in gray canvas, its spine cracked, its pages foxed with age but legible — every line of it in a hand so precise it looked printed. Cyrus turned the pages with the care owed to scripture.

Harbormaster's log, Kestrel Bend. Dates, tides, drafts, tonnages. And there, on the page dated the night of the fog:

*Meridian Star arrived 0340, two days ahead of manifest. Draft marks read heavy — cargo aboard exceeds declared. Crates moved ashore under lamplight, count 41, unlisted. Witnessed: E. Corvan, harbormaster; J. Aldous, mate; R. Quill, customs clerk.*

Three witnesses. Three dead partners. Cyrus felt the shape of it close around her like cold water.

"He didn't just murder them," she said softly. "They were the only men besides her father who could swear the Star came in heavy. He killed everyone who'd seen the real arrival — except a harbormaster nobody would believe, and a child nobody counted."

"And the child kept the book." Tamsin refilled both cups, though hers was mostly ceremony at this point. "Forty years, I told myself I was waiting for the law to grow honest. Law doesn't grow, girl. It gets bought or it gets shamed. There's no third option." She nodded at the logbook. "That's the shame. All of it, in my father's neat little hand. Take it upriver. Find Odile. Put the two halves together and make the river do what the courts won't."

Cyrus wrapped the logbook back in its oilcloth and stowed it inside her coat, against her chest, where it beat against her ribs like a second heart.

"The Narrows," she said. "How bad are they?"

Tamsin made a sound that in a younger woman would have been a laugh. "The Narrows aren't bad. The Narrows are a rumor the river tells about itself. What's bad is what's moored at the mouth of them." She jerked her chin toward the window, toward the brown gleam of water beyond the sedge. "Saw a boat this morning. Black hull, no name painted, riding low. Salvage tender, or I'm a bishop. They're ahead of you, girl, and they're not hurrying, which means they think they've already won."

Cyrus stood. Every mile of her body protested, and she overruled all of them.

"Is there a way through that they won't know?"

"There's a way through that *I* barely know." Tamsin walked her to the door, and in the doorway she stopped, and for a moment the flint dropped away and Cyrus saw the eleven-year-old who had watched her father's world drown. "Old Man Ferris runs the ferry at the Bend — what's left of it. Half-wrecked, half-mad, entirely the best boatman on this river. He doesn't take passengers. He takes *reasons*." Tamsin pressed something into Cyrus's hand: a river-worn wooden token, carved with a cormorant, matching the unfinished bird on her table. "Show him that. Tell him the Corvan girl finally sent somebody worth the fare."

Cyrus closed her fingers around the token. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Tamsin's chin lifted toward the river, toward the drowned town lying quiet under the current, keeping its dead and its secrets in the same brown dark. "Thank me when the truth's on the front page and Harrow Vane's empire is a footnote. Now get off my porch. The tide won't wait, and neither will whoever's in that black hull."

Cyrus went. At the wagon-wheel gate she glanced back once — Tamsin Corvan standing in her doorway, small and square and immovable, forty years of patience finally aimed at something.

The path to the ferry landing ran along the water's edge, and the water was high and rising, and somewhere upstream, faint on the wind, Cyrus heard it: the deep-throated churn of a steam winch turning over, patient as a promise.

Someone was already raising the dead.
