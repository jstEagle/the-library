## 8. The Ferryman

The man who kept the last working ferry on the Reckoning was named Ambrose Pell, and he was mending a net when Cyrus found him, which struck her as a profession chosen entirely for its honesty about futility.

He sat on an overturned dory at the edge of a slough the maps called Wren's Cut but which everyone within thirty miles called simply the Crossing, and his hands moved through the net's tangles with the patience of a man who had long ago stopped expecting the river to give anything back. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post that had decided to lean, and he wore a coat that had once been a good coat in a decade Cyrus had only read about.

"You're the one walking the dead lines," he said without looking up.

Word traveled faster than she did. That had been true since Spur Nine, and it hadn't gotten any more comfortable.

"I'm the one trying to," Cyrus said. "The line ends at your water."

"The line ends everywhere. That's what makes it a line." He looked up then, and his eyes were pale river-gray, the color of things that had seen everything twice. "You'll be wanting to cross."

"I'll be wanting to cross."

"Everybody wants to cross. Nobody wants to hear what crossing costs." He set down the net — or rather, abandoned it, the way a man abandons an argument he's been having with himself for years — and stood, joints announcing each stage of the operation like a stationmaster calling stops. "Sit. There's coffee. It's terrible. You'll drink it anyway, because you've been walking since dawn and pride is a luxury of the well-rested."

It was unnerving being read so accurately by a stranger. Cyrus sat on the other end of the overturned dory and accepted the tin cup he produced from somewhere near a cookfire that consisted of three stones and a great deal of faith. The coffee was, as promised, terrible. She drank it anyway.

"Ambrose Pell," he said, offering a hand like a bundle of kindling tied together with rope.

"Cyrus Nystrom."

"Nystrom." He turned the name over the way he'd turned the net, looking for holes. "There was a Nystrom ran engines on the upper spur. Before."

"My father."

"Hm." He drank his own coffee and gazed out across the slough, where the morning fog was lifting off the water in slow rags. "Good engineer, they said. Careful man. The careful ones lasted longest out here. Not longest overall, mind. Just longest per unit of sense."

"That sounds like my father."

"They all say that." He smiled, briefly, and it transformed his whole face from driftwood into something almost boyish. "So. Kestrel Bend."

Cyrus kept her own face still. "I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. Fog's been full of salvage boats for two days, every one of them pointed upriver like geese, and now here comes a woman off the dead lines with surveyor's boots and a look like she's carrying something breakable." He shrugged. "Kestrel Bend is the only thing up there worth that expression."

She decided, on balance, that arguing would cost more than admitting. "I'm looking for Odile Marsh."

Something changed in his face. Not much. A tide going out an inch further than expected. "Marsh," he said. "Now there's a name I haven't said aloud in twenty years."

"You knew her?"

"Everyone knew Odile Marsh. Woman surveyed half this delta before the company decided maps were bad for business." He refilled her cup unasked. "And if Odile Marsh has gone quiet upriver, then either she's dead, which I doubt, because that woman could out-stubborn a flood, or she's gone quiet *on purpose*, which means somebody ought to ask themselves why before they go charging after her."

"I've asked myself."

"And?"

"And I keep getting the same answer: she's my mentor, she taught me everything I know about reading water and land, and if she's silent, it's because silence was the safest place she could find."

Pell nodded slowly, as though she'd passed some examination she hadn't known she was sitting. "All right. That's an honest answer. Honest answers get you across. They don't necessarily get you back, but that's a separate fare."

* * *

The ferry itself was not, strictly speaking, a ferry. It was a flat-bottomed barge maybe thirty feet long, patched in six places with materials ranging from proper planking to what appeared to be the door of a church pew, and it was poled rather than rowed, which Cyrus found both archaic and oddly reassuring. Engines attracted attention. Poles made no promises.

"She'll hold," Pell said, catching her look. "She's held through four floods, two fires, and the year the Corvan boys decided she'd make a fine racing craft."

"The Corvan boys raced a barge?"

"The Corvan boys raced *everything*. That's rather the point of being Corvan." Something flickered behind his eyes again, that inch of extra tide, and Cyrus filed the name away next to the survivor she was hunting. Tamsin Corvan. The drowned town's last living daughter. "They're gone now, most of them. River took its share, the war took more, and Kestrel Bend took the rest. But that's a story for open water, and we're not on open water yet."

He pushed off from the mud with a competence that made the whole operation look easy, which Cyrus knew from experience meant it was anything but. The slough opened around them, brown-green and patient, overhung with willows that trailed their fingers in the current like women mourning something specific.

"How deep is it here?" she asked, mostly to fill the silence.

"Under us? Four feet. Under the old channel, forty. And under the town—" he tilted his head toward the north bank, where the trees thinned into a gray suggestion of rooftops and a church spire leaning like a drunk against the sky, "—under the town, nobody's ever measured, because the people who could have told us are the reason we'd be measuring."

Kestrel Bend. Even from this distance, even as a rumor of a skyline, it pulled at her. She'd grown up on her father's stories of the place: the mill, the market street, the treasury office with its vault sunk below the waterline because the founder had been a superstitious man who believed money should sleep close to the earth. Then the dam project upstream, the government's grand plan, the buyouts, the holdouts, and finally the water itself, rising over the streets in a single autumn while the territory looked elsewhere.

"They say the Meridian Star went down in the channel with the treasury still aboard," Cyrus said carefully. "Sealed holds. Never salvaged."

"They say a lot of things." Pell poled them past a deadhead snag, unhurried. "They say the cargo's cursed. They say it's guarded. They say the company that owned the Star sank her on purpose for the insurance, and they say no, no, she broke her back on the Narrows rock like any honest ship, and they say—" he paused, and for a moment the humor drained out of him entirely, leaving something old and tired, "—they say a man named Aldous Vane signed the manifests himself, and that Vane's signature has a way of making things disappear."

Cyrus's breath caught. "Vane. The salvage fleet — it belongs to a Vane."

"Aldous Vane's grandson runs it now. Same name, same handwriting, if the stories hold." Pell resumed poling, and the humor crept back into his voice like fog returning to a hollow. "Which is why I ask, Miss Nystrom, purely as a professional courtesy between people sharing a boat: when you find whatever it is you're finding up there — and you will find it, you have the face of a woman who finds things — whose side of the disappearing do you intend to be on?"

* * *

They crossed the main channel at midday, and it was worse than the slough in every way that mattered. The current ran hard and brown, carrying whole trees with their roots still clutching at the banks they'd been torn from, and the fog came and went in curtains so that sometimes Cyrus could see a mile and sometimes she couldn't see the bow.

Pell poled through it all without hurry, reading the water the way she read rails — by feel, by habit, by a hundred small signs that never announced themselves to strangers. Twice he stopped the barge entirely, pole planted, head cocked, waiting for something Cyrus couldn't see to pass. Both times, a few minutes later, a wall of debris came through exactly where they'd have been.

"You've done this a long time," she said.

"Thirty-one years." He planted the pole again. "Before that I did other things. Worse-paying things. Before *that*, I lived in Kestrel Bend, which pays nothing at all anymore, except in memories, and the exchange rate on those is criminal."

"You were a townsman."

"I was a ferryman then too. Smaller boat. Shorter crossings." His voice stayed light, but his knuckles had gone pale on the pole. "My wife's family were Corvans. My daughter married one. Tamsin's my granddaughter, if you're the sort who collects connections, and I suspect you are, because surveyors collect everything."

Cyrus felt the ground shift under her, metaphorically, the way it always did when the map and the territory touched. "Tamsin Corvan. People say she's still up there. In the Bend."

"People say a lot of things." He said it again, but gentler this time. "Tamsin stayed when the water came. Not because she couldn't leave — the company bought her out twice and she burned both checks, which tells you most of what you need to know about Tamsin — but because someone had to stay and remember where the streets were. Somebody has to know where the houses are under the water, or the drowned don't have addresses, and if the drowned don't have addresses, then nobody can ever testify to what happened to them."

Cyrus stared at him. "That's a strange way to put it."

"It's Tamsin's way of putting it. She says the river took the town but not the testimony, and she intends to be the transcript." Pell finally looked at her directly, and there was nothing humorous left anywhere in him. "Now. Here's what I think, Miss Nystrom, and you can take it or leave it in the water. I think Odile Marsh went upriver because she found something in an old ledger, something about the Meridian Star and the men who owned her, and I think she went silent because the men who own things now are the grandsons of the men who owned things then, and silence is the only lock they haven't figured out how to pick. And I think you're going to find her, and I think when you do, you're going to need Tamsin Corvan, because a surveyor's word is worth paper and a survivor's word is worth blood. So I'm going to take you across, and I'm going to tell you where to find her, and then I'm going to pretend this conversation never happened, because I'm seventy-two years old and I'd like to make seventy-three."

The fog closed around them again. Somewhere ahead, faint as a memory, a bell buoy clanked in the current.

"Why?" Cyrus asked quietly. "Why help me?"

Pell was silent for three strokes of the pole. Four. Five.

"Because in '61, when the water was coming and the company men were burning the records that said whose land they'd stolen to flood, a young surveyor named Odile Marsh rowed out to my house in the rain and told me to move my family to high ground a day early, off the record, against her own employer's orders." His voice was steady, but the pole trembled, just slightly, just enough. "She saved eleven of us. I've owed her a debt for twenty years and the interest alone has become unbearable."

* * *

The far bank, when it came, arrived all at once — a wall of cottonwoods rising out of the fog like the edge of the world. Beyond them, invisible but unmistakably present, lay the drowned town and the wrecked channel and whatever Odile Marsh had decided was worth her silence.

Pell brought the barge alongside a rotted landing with the tenderness of a man docking a living thing, and held it steady while Cyrus gathered her pack, her father's instruments, and the oilskin-wrapped map she'd already begun to think of as a hostage.

"There's a trail," he said. "Old corduroy road, mostly drowned, but the high parts will hold you as far as the Narrows overlook. From there—" he hesitated.

"From there?"

"From there you'll see the channel, and the wreck fleet's lanterns if they've come up this far, and the church spire, and if the light's right and the fog's wrong, you might see smoke. Small smoke. Cooking smoke. From the roof of the old customs house, which is the only building on the Bend tall enough to keep a stove above the waterline." He handed her a coil of rope and a lantern with the air of a man settling accounts. "Tamsin doesn't like visitors. Tell her your grandfather's name was Nystrom and that he once hauled her mother's piano up the spur road for free, and she'll feed you. After that, you're on your own, and may whatever gods watch over drowned towns watch over you too."

Cyrus swung her pack onto her shoulders and stepped onto the landing, and the boards groaned but held, which felt like a fair summary of her situation.

"Pell," she said, turning back. "The salvage fleet. How far behind me are they?"

He shaded his eyes and looked downriver, into the fog, and his answer came slow and flat as a depth-sounding.

"This morning they were at Two Sisters Reach. By tomorrow's tide they'll be at the Narrows." He pushed off from the landing, the barge sliding back into the brown water like a secret returning to its keeper. "And Miss Nystrom — the Narrows is the one place on this river where a barge can't turn around."

Then the fog took him, and Cyrus Nystrom turned north, toward the smoke.
