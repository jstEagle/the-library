## 9. Narrows

The river narrowed the way a fist does — slowly at first, all patience and suggestion, and then all at once.

Cyrus felt it before she saw it. The current under the *Maggie Bright* changed its mind about them, going from a lazy shove to something with opinions. Old Pell, who had been dozing against the tiller with his hat over his eyes like a man attending his own funeral out of politeness, opened one eye and said, "Ah. There she is."

"There what is?" Cyrus asked.

"The Narrows." He said it the way other men said a wife's name after forty years — fondly, warily, both. "You'll want to sit down, miss. And hold onto something that loves you back."

Cyrus looked around the deck of the little stern-wheeler. There was the cargo crate they'd been using as a bench, which loved no one. There was Tamsin Corvan, sitting cross-legged near the bow with her arms wrapped around her knees, watching the banks slide past with the flat attention of someone counting exits. And there was the river itself, which, as far as Cyrus could tell, did not love anybody.

She sat down on the crate and put her hand flat on the rail.

The banks were closing in. On the left, a wall of black basalt rose sheer out of the water, streaked white with old high-water marks like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal. On the right, a tangle of alder and deadfall leaned over the current, dropping its shadows across the surface in long fingers. Between them, the channel ran maybe thirty yards wide where an hour ago it had been a quarter mile of brown silk.

"How wide is the throat?" Cyrus asked. She'd studied the charts, of course — she'd studied every chart of this river since she was nineteen years old and Odile Marsh first put one in her hands and said, *learn it until you can draw it blindfolded, Nystrom, because the day will come when the paper's gone and only your head is left.* But there is a difference between knowing a thing and feeling it come for you.

"Twenty-two feet at low water," Pell said. "Nineteen if the tide's running wrong, which it always is when you're in a hurry."

"Are we in a hurry?"

Pell took off his hat, considered the sky, and put it back on. "We're always in a hurry on this river. That's how she gets you."

* * *

Tamsin hadn't spoken much since they'd picked her up two days ago at the burned-out fish camp, and Cyrus hadn't pushed. Some silences were doors; you didn't kick them open, you waited beside them until whoever owned them chose to turn the handle. But now, as the walls drew closer and the water began to talk to itself in a low, muscular mutter, Tamsin unfolded herself and came aft.

"You've done the Narrows before," she said. Not quite a question.

"I've read about them," Cyrus admitted. "I've never run them."

Tamsin's eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch, which from her was roughly equivalent to another woman screaming. "And you got on the boat anyway."

"I got on the boat because the boat was leaving."

"That," said Tamsin, sitting down on the deck beside the crate with her back against it, "is the most honest reason I've ever heard for anything." She almost smiled. It was a small, rusted thing, that smile, like a hinge that hadn't been used in years but still remembered its job. "Odile would have liked that answer."

The name landed between them the way a dropped tool lands on a wooden floor — loud, and then very quiet.

"You knew her well," Cyrus said carefully.

"I knew her long," Tamsin corrected. "Well is different." She pulled a piece of dried fish from her coat pocket and worried at it with her teeth. "She came through my camp four years ago. Said she was surveying the abandoned spurs. I told her the spurs were dead and the men who'd killed them were rich, and she wrote that down in her little book, word for word, like it was cargo she'd been asked to sign for." Tamsin chewed. "I thought she was a fool. Rich men don't get written down. They do the writing."

"And now?"

"Now I'm on a boat." Tamsin shrugged. "Draw your own conclusions."

* * *

The throat of the Narrows announced itself with sound before sight. The river's mutter deepened into a growl, and then into something that wasn't a growl at all but a voice — a long, grinding syllable repeated over and over, the water arguing with stone. Around the last bend, Cyrus saw it: the channel pinched down to a slot barely wider than the *Maggie Bright*, and through that slot the whole river poured itself like tea through a cracked cup, boiling white at the edges, heaving in the middle.

Pell stood up. The transformation was remarkable. The dozing old man folded himself away somewhere, and what remained was lean and quick, hands moving on the wheel with the tenderness of a man touching a horse's flank.

"Miss Nystrom. Miss Corvan. If you'd take your seats and keep your teeth apart."

"Why our teeth—" Cyrus began, and then the bow hit the standing wave and the question answered itself as her jaw snapped shut hard enough to ring bells.

For the next four minutes — she counted them, because counting was the only prayer she knew — the world became water. The *Maggie Bright* climbed, shuddered, slid sideways, and climbed again. Spray came aboard in cold handfuls. The boiler groaned below decks like an old man being asked to dance. Pell talked the entire time, a low continuous stream of encouragement and insult directed at the river — *"that's it, girl, that's it, no you don't, oh yes you do, fine, have it your way, we'll go sideways then, sideways is legal"* — and somehow the boat obeyed him, or obeyed the river through him, threading the slot with inches to spare on either side. Cyrus watched black rock slide past her elbow close enough to touch, streaming with water, ancient and utterly indifferent.

Then the walls fell away, the boil smoothed to chop, the chop smoothed to ripple, and they were through, drifting in a long green pool where the current gathered itself again and pretended nothing had happened.

Pell took off his hat, bowed to the river with genuine courtesy, and put it back on. "She let us by," he said. "That's not the same as letting us pass, mind. She remembers everyone who comes through. You'll be in her ledger now."

"I thought the river was the honest witness," Cyrus said, a little shakily.

"Oh, she is. That's why you don't want to be in her book." Pell glanced back at her, and for a moment his eyes were not old man's eyes at all. "Everything the river sees, the river keeps. Ask me how I know."

"How do you—"

"Another time." He waved a hand at the water ahead, where the channel widened and split around a gravel bar crowded with herons. "Look there. Company boats."

* * *

There were three of them, moored along the far bank in the lee of the bar: flat-bottomed salvage scows with derricks folded down, flying the gray pennant of the Corvane Salvage & Dredge Concern. Men moved on their decks, small with distance. One of the scows had a steam winch, and even from here Cyrus could hear it testing itself — a short coughing shriek, over and over, like a bird learning a bad word.

"They're ahead of us," Tamsin said. Her voice had gone flat again, the door shut. "How are they ahead of us?"

"Pell knows the Narrows," Cyrus said slowly. "They don't. They must have portaged the scows light around the falls above Kestrel Bend and run the rest." She was doing the arithmetic in her head and disliking every sum. "If they're here, they're less than a tide behind Odile. Maybe less than half."

"Or they know exactly where she is," Tamsin said, "because someone told them."

The words sat on the deck between them like a wet cat nobody wanted to claim.

Cyrus turned it over. Someone had tipped the salvage fleet about the expedition's route — that much she'd known since Grayharbor, since the harbor master's too-smooth smile and the fleet that sailed suspiciously complete. But there was a difference between a leak and a guide. A leak could be a clerk selling a schedule. A guide meant someone who knew the river, knew the lines, knew—

Knew the way Odile thought.

"She wouldn't have taken anyone she didn't trust," Cyrus said aloud.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "But trust isn't a wall, miss. It's a door. And doors swing both ways, especially when a man's been offered enough money to stop remembering which side of them he sleeps on."

Pell, at the wheel, cleared his throat with the delicacy of a man interrupting a funeral. "Begging your pardons, ladies, but there's a decision coming up, and the river won't wait on it. Main channel runs on past the Bend — deep water, easy going, and straight past those three gentlemen and their winch. Or there's the old mill race." He pointed upstream-left, where a seam of darker water slipped away behind a screen of alders toward what might once have been a canal cut. "Shallow. Snaggy. Runs right over the top of Kestrel Bend itself, more or less — you can look down into the town from the levee if the light's kind. Nobody uses it. Nobody's used it in thirty years."

"Can the *Maggie Bright* fit?"

Pell smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "That depends on how attached you are to the sides of the *Maggie Bright*."

Cyrus looked at the salvage scows. She looked at the dark seam of the mill race. Somewhere upriver, past the drowned rooftops of Kestrel Bend, Odile Marsh was keeping silence on purpose, guarding whatever she'd found in the wreck of the *Meridian Star*, and every hour Cyrus spent being sensible was an hour the gray pennant spent catching up.

"The race," she said.

Tamsin made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. "Of course the race."

"You could put me ashore," Cyrus offered. "Take the main channel, stay clear of it all."

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into her coat and brought out not the dried fish this time but a battered tin compass, and set it on the crate between them, needle swinging lazily north.

"My grandfather worked the Meridian Star," she said quietly. "Before she went down. Before everything. I spent twenty years telling myself the river took him fair and square, the way rivers do." She closed her hand over the compass. "Four years ago a fool with a notebook told me maybe it wasn't the river at all. I didn't believe her. Then she stopped writing, and I started believing things I didn't want to." She stood up. "Put you ashore. Honestly, miss. You people."

"I'll take that as a no," Cyrus said.

"Take it however you like. Pell!" Tamsin called forward. "The race. And if we scrape paint, I'm blaming her."

"Everyone blames someone," Pell said serenely, and swung the wheel. "It's how they stay warm."

* * *

The mill race was exactly as bad as promised, which is to say it was worse. The cut had been dug a century ago to feed a gristmill whose bones they passed within the first mile — a roofless hulk of blackened timber rising out of the shallows, its wheel long gone, its stones scattered like pulled teeth. After that the channel threaded itself between drowned fence posts and the tops of orchard trees, apples gone wild generations ago, small and bitter and everywhere, bobbing in the current like green hail.

Twice the *Maggie Bright* touched bottom with a long, agonizing shudder of timber, and twice Pell coaxed her off with language Cyrus made a private note to never repeat in front of a clergyman. Once they passed so close to a submerged rooftop that Cyrus could have stepped off onto the shingles, and she did step — one foot, just briefly, onto the ridge of a house that had been underwater longer than she'd been alive — and looked down through the clear green water into a parlor. A chair. A table, upright. A shelf of books fanned open like sleeping birds, their pages forever mid-turn.

She stepped back aboard and said nothing, and Tamsin, watching her face, nodded once, as if a question had been answered correctly.

Near dusk the race widened, the snags thinned, and the levee of Kestrel Bend rose ahead of them out of the evening mist — a long earthen spine, grass-grown, broken in places, with the tips of a church steeple and a grain elevator showing beyond it like the horns of something sleeping just under the skin of the world.

Pell brought the boat alongside the levee with a gentleness that seemed, after the day they'd had, almost sarcastic. Cyrus jumped ashore onto ground that had not held a boot in decades and found it firm, dry, patient.

And there, stamped into the mud of the towpath not six feet from where she stood — fresh, sharp-edged, hours old at most — was a boot print. Large. Deep-heeled. Beside it, caught on a splinter of driftwood, a thread of gray wool.

Survey-issue wool. The color Odile Marsh had worn every day of the fifteen years Cyrus had known her.

Tamsin crouched beside the print, touched the edge of it, and looked up. "She came this way," she said. "Today."

"Yes," Cyrus said. Her heart was doing something complicated and unprofessional. "And look — the print's headed *toward* the water, not away from it."

Tamsin followed her gaze down the levee's landward slope, to where the evening mist lay thick over the flooded streets of the drowned town. Somewhere out in that gray, faint and rhythmic, carried across the water like a pulse, came a sound none of them wanted to hear and none of them could mistake.

A lantern bell. Ringing, slow and steady, out among the drowned houses.

Somebody was signaling from Kestrel Bend. And by the answering gleam of lights far downstream — three lights, in a neat gray line — somebody else had heard it too.
