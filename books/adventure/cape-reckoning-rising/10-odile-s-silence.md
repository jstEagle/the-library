## 10. Odile's Silence

The camp sat in a fold of the riverbank like a secret someone had tried very hard to keep and then, at the last moment, decided to leave half-open.

Cyrus saw it first from the water — the glint of a tarp stretched between two alders, the careful geometry of it. Nothing about it said *abandoned*. Abandoned camps sagged. This one was pitched with the particular fussiness of a woman who had once made Cyrus re-stake a tent three times because the guy-lines weren't "symmetrical to the eye, Cyrus, not to the surveyor's chain — we live in the landscape, we don't audit it."

"You see that?" Cyrus said.

Tamsin Corvan, up front with the paddle, didn't answer right away. She'd been quiet since the Narrows, in the way of a person recalibrating everything she believed about her own town. When she did speak, her voice had the flat care of someone handling glass. "I see it."

"Nobody's home?"

"Nobody's *stupid*," Tamsin said. "If Odile Marsh wanted to be found, there'd be a flag on the ridge and a fire going. If she wanted to be lost, we wouldn't have seen the tarp." She backed water, letting the skiff drift. "That's a message. That's a door left open exactly one inch."

Cyrus felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to call hope. Hope, in her experience, was just a way of scheduling disappointment. But it was true that Odile Marsh had never done anything by accident in forty years of fieldwork, and it was equally true that she had taught Cyrus every trick of deliberate silence she knew.

*If you go dark,* Odile used to say over camp coffee, *go dark properly. Leave one thing wrong. One thing only a friend would notice.*

Cyrus looked at the camp again. The tarp. The neatness. And there — hanging from the alder branch nearest the water, where any passing boatman would see it and think nothing of it — a tin cup, upside down on its nail.

Right side up meant *all clear*. Upside down meant *stay back*.

But the nail itself had been bent. Bent, and hammered crooked, so the cup hung at an angle no surveyor would ever tolerate.

One thing wrong. One thing only a friend would notice.

"Oh, you impossible old woman," Cyrus breathed.

* * *

They beached below the camp, out of sightline, and came up through the alders with the exaggerated stealth of people who were almost certainly sneaking toward a welcome. Tamsin carried the shotgun broken open over her arm, which managed to look both threatening and apologetic at once. Cyrus carried the map case and forty years of accumulated guilt about a letter she'd never answered.

The camp told its story in the orderly way Odile did everything. Bedroll rolled tight. Cook kit scoured and stacked. A surveyor's transit wrapped in oilcloth. On the folding table, weighted down with a stone, a single sheet of paper under a second, smaller stone.

The top paper was addressed to whoever found it, in Odile's drafting hand — that beautiful, severe lettering that looked like it had been ruled by God:

**TO THE SALVAGE PARTY, IF YOU ARE READING THIS: THERE IS NOTHING HERE WORTH YOUR TIME. THE WRECK IS UNSOUND. THE CARGO MANIFEST IS A FORGERY. TURN BACK.**

Beneath it, the smaller stone held a second note, folded twice, with one word on the outside:

**CYRUS.**

Tamsin read the public note twice and snorted. "She's lying. She's lying so hard the paper's embarrassed."

"She is," Cyrus said. Her hands were not entirely steady on the fold. "It's the oldest trick in her book. Tell the wolves the den's empty in language loud enough for the foxes to hear there's a den at all." She broke the seal on her own name.

The private note was shorter. It was also, Cyrus would think later, the most Odile Marsh document ever produced by human hands, because it began with an insult.

*Cyrus —*

*Eleven years without a letter, and now you come up my river in a stolen skiff with a Corvan. I have notes. We will discuss them when this is finished.*

*You are being watched. Vane's men passed me on the third, running lights dark, which tells you what they think of the law they own. I let them pass. I have been letting things pass for six weeks while I put the truth somewhere they cannot burn it.*

*I am not at the wreck. Do not look for me at the wreck. If you are reading this within a tide of my leaving, take the old portage trail behind the camp — the one your precious map says doesn't exist, because I made sure you recorded it wrong eleven years ago, you stubborn girl, and you never once checked my figures, which I counted as either trust or laziness and have decided, finally, on trust.*

*Bring the Corvan woman. Not the gun. The testimony.*

*— O.M.*

*P.S. Your contour lines are still sentimental. Rivers do not flatter themselves. Neither should maps.*

Cyrus read it three times. By the third reading she was laughing, quietly, helplessly, in the way of a person who has been terrified for days and has just been handed permission to stop.

"What?" Tamsin demanded. "What does it say? Is she alive? Is she —"

"She's alive. She's furious. She's been waiting for us." Cyrus folded the note with the reverence other people reserved for scripture. "She also insulted my mapmaking, my correspondence, and my character, in that order, and signed it with initials like a duchess."

"That sounds like her?"

"Tamsin, that is the most her thing I have ever read, and I once received a postcard from her that said only 'Your bridge design is cowardly. Weather fine.'"

Something in Tamsin's shoulders came down an inch — the first time since Kestrel Bend that she'd looked less like a woman carrying a drowned town and more like a woman who might, eventually, set it down. "So she knew. About Harrow Vane. About what he did."

"She's known longer than we have. Six weeks." Cyrus looked around the camp again, really looked, and the details rearranged themselves: the transit packed but not loaded; the bedroll rolled for travel, not storage; a scatter of paper ash in the fire ring, burned hot and thorough, except for one corner of a page that had escaped the flames — a fragment of dense columns, figures, a name half-legible: *...ovan.* Tamsin's family name. Odile had been burning documents and keeping the ones that mattered on her person.

"She went silent on purpose," Cyrus said slowly, working it through aloud, because saying things out loud had always been how she thought and Odile had mocked her for it for decades. "Not because she was in trouble. Because she *is* the trouble. She's carrying evidence Vane can't afford to exist, and the moment she surfaces, every gun on this river points at her. So she disappeared — properly, professionally — and left a trail only two people alive could read. Me, because of the map. And—" she turned the burned fragment toward Tamsin, "—you. Because whatever's in those papers, it needs a witness from Kestrel Bend, and there's exactly one."

Tamsin stared at the scrap. Her jaw worked. "My grandfather's name," she said at last. "That's his handwriting next to it. He kept accounts for the Meridian Star Company. Everyone always said he just did the books." Her voice dropped. "Everyone always said a lot of things."

* * *

The portage trail behind the camp was exactly where Odile said it wasn't.

Cyrus had walked this country for thirty years with a map that said the ridge above the bend was unbroken forest, and here was a cut line, old and grown-in, switchbacking up through the hemlocks with the unmistakable rhythm of a surveyed grade — because it *was* one. A spur grade. Pre-war. The kind of line that appeared on no official document because it had been built by a company that intended, someday, to deny it existed.

"I recorded this as a rock bluff," Cyrus said, half to herself, climbing. "She altered my field notes. Eleven years ago. While I slept, presumably, or while I was off admiring a waterfall like an idiot."

"Would you have noticed?"

"No. That's the humiliating part. Her corrections were better than my originals. She probably improved the whole sheet and I've been navigating on her forgery my entire career." Cyrus ducked a deadfall. "There's a lesson in there about mentorship and I'm too tired to learn it."

Tamsin laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound, and then seemed startled at herself, as if the laugh had escaped custody. It was the first time Cyrus had heard it. It suited her.

The grade climbed for an hour and then, instead of cresting the ridge, turned along it — following the contour the way a railroad follows a river it isn't supposed to admit exists. And there, in a clearing where the hemlocks opened onto sky, Cyrus stopped dead.

Rails.

Real ones. Rusted, buried to the web in moss, but real, running level along the ridgeline into a cutting choked with sixty years of growth. An abandoned spur that connected to nothing on any map — except that Cyrus, standing on it, felt the geometry click together in her chest like a coupler slamming home. This line ran east. Toward the high country. Toward the old treasury route. Toward the mine adits where, according to a manifest everyone believed forged, a shipment of gold had supposedly gone into the river with the *Meridian Star*.

"It was never on the boat," she whispered.

"What?"

"The cargo. Or — maybe some of it was, maybe the manifest was partly true, that's what makes a good lie. But there was a second shipment. Overland. On this line. And the wreck —" Cyrus pressed her palm flat against the cold rail, as if she could read the past through rust. "The wreck is the story. The wreck is what everyone was supposed to find. Three partners dead in a flood, cargo lost, tragedy, closed book. Except one partner —" she remembered the ledger's shadow, the names, the arithmetic of who profited, "— one partner arranged the flood. And Odile found the line that proves it."

Tamsin had gone very still. Downriver, faint on the wind, came a sound that hadn't been there an hour ago: the deep-throated cough of a steam donkey engine, working salvage. Vane's fleet, closing on the wreck.

"They'll tear the hold apart," Tamsin said. "And find whatever he left for them to find."

"And believe they've won. Which is exactly what Odile wants, right up until the moment she doesn't." Cyrus straightened. "We keep moving. Where does this grade end?"

"Ends at the old tunnel mouth, two miles on," Tamsin said, and then blinked. "I know that. Why do I know that? My grandfather used to say — 'the line over the hill, the line nobody rode.'" She shook her head slowly. "He used to say the river keeps what the mountain hides. I thought it was just something old men said."

"Old men say true things at exactly the rate the rest of us fail to listen," Cyrus said. "Come on. Two miles. And Tamsin —"

"Yes?"

"When we find her, she's going to be rude to you. It's how she says hello. Don't take the bait, and whatever you do, don't compliment her cooking. She'll make you eat it for a week."

* * *

They found the tunnel mouth at dusk, and found Odile Marsh standing in front of it like a monument that had opinions.

She was smaller than Cyrus remembered, or the country had gotten bigger. Seventy-one years old, silver hair cropped short as a convict's, dressed in canvas that had outlived several governments. She held a lantern in one hand and, in the other, a surveyor's stake mallet, which she did not lower.

"Well," said Odile Marsh. "Look what the tide brought in. Eleven years late and traveling with a Corvan." Her eyes, pale and exact as instruments, went over Cyrus head to boots — counting, assessing, finding her wanting in the specific ways she'd catalogued years ago. "You look terrible."

"Hello to you too."

"You answered a letter in eleven years with a *visit*, and a visit with *hello to you too*. The postal service weeps, Cyrus." But her voice had roughened on the last word, just slightly, the way a rail sings before the train you can't yet see comes around the bend. She turned the lantern up. "Corvan. Tamsin. Good. You're thinner than your photograph and angrier than your reputation. Both correct, I imagine. Get inside, both of you. There's weather coming and worse than weather."

Inside the tunnel — dry-floored, timbered, older than the war — Odile had built a life the size of a coffin and twice as organized. Maps on the walls. Papers in oilcloth bundles, labeled in that severe hand. And pinned to the timber at the far end, illuminated by the lantern like an altar piece, a single photograph: four men and one woman in front of a locomotive, 1938, all of them laughing at something outside the frame.

Three of the men were dead partners. The fourth was Aldous Vane, young, handsome, and laughing hardest of anyone.

Odile caught Cyrus looking. For a long moment the old woman's face did something complicated and grief-shaped, and then it closed like a ledger.

"Now," she said, setting down the mallet with a click that sounded terribly final. "Sit down, both of you, and listen carefully, because tomorrow the river rises, Vane's divers reach the hold, and we have exactly one tide to make a murderer's own paper hang him." She looked at Tamsin, and her voice — for the first time in Cyrus's memory — went gentle. "Miss Corvan. What I'm about to show you has your grandfather's signature on every page. I need you ready for that."

From beneath the maps, Odile drew out a ledger bound in river-cured hide, and opened it to the first page.

Outside, far downriver, the donkey engines fell silent — all at once, all together, like a held breath.
