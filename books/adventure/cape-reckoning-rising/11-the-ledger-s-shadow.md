## 11. The Ledger's Shadow

The rain had stopped by the time they reached Tamsin Corvan's house, but the river hadn't noticed. It kept rising anyway, patient as a debt collector, lapping at the pilings beneath her porch with the soft insistence of something that intended to win.

Cyrus stood on the boards and let Tamsin look at her for a long moment. The old woman had a face like a driftwood fence — weathered, crooked in places, still standing. Her eyes were the pale gray of water over slate, and they went from Cyrus's mud-caked boots to the survey case slung across her back to the brass compass hanging at her throat.

"You're Nystrom's girl," she said.

"I'm Cyrus Nystrom."

"That's what I said." Tamsin turned and pushed open the door. "Well, come in before the river does. It hates company but it loves an open door."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of cedar smoke and lamp oil and forty years of tea brewed strong enough to strip paint. Every horizontal surface held something: jars of river glass, a taxidermied pike with one reproachful glass eye, stacks of paper so old their edges had gone the color of weak coffee. A cat the size of a flour sack occupied the best chair by the stove and did not consider moving.

"His name is Admiral," Tamsin said, following Cyrus's glance. "He was here before me. I just pay the rent."

"I thought you owned the house."

"I own the house. He owns everything in it." She waved Cyrus toward a bench. "Sit. You've got the look of someone who's been walking Spur Nine, which means you've been walking Spur Nine, which means you're either very brave or very stupid, and Odile Marsh always said you were both, so let's not waste time deciding."

Cyrus sat. "You know where Odile is."

"I know where Odile *was*. Whether she's still there depends on how the last two days have treated her, and on this river, that's never a safe bet." Tamsin filled a tin kettle and set it on the stove without hurrying. "She came to me eleven days ago. Soaked to the ribs, furious, and carrying a book wrapped in oilcloth like it was a newborn."

"The ledger."

Tamsin's eyebrows rose a fraction. "So you know about the ledger."

"I know Odile thinks it proves something. I know the man who wants it is named Harrow Vane, and he's got six boats and a salvage crew coming upriver behind me, and I know he'd burn this whole valley down to get his hands on it."

"Harrow Vane." Tamsin said the name the way other people said the names of diseases. "I knew him when he was just Harrow, no Vane, because the Vane came later, bought and paid for along with everything else. He was a clerk then. A little man with a little mustache and a big appetite for other people's money." She poured tea into two mismatched cups and handed one over. "In the spring of '41, the treasury shipment came through Kestrel Bend on the Meridian Star. Officially, it sank in the Narrows during the flood. Unofficially—"

"It didn't sink by accident."

"It didn't sink at all, not right away. And the cargo wasn't lost. It was *moved*." Tamsin lowered herself into the chair the Admiral permitted her to share, wedging herself against the armrest while the cat radiated disapproval. "Three men knew the plan. Vane. His partner Aldous Grebe. And the boat's purser, a careful little man named Fenwick who kept the books. Fenwick wrote everything down — every crate moved, every signature forged, every dollar split. Because Fenwick understood something important about partners."

"What's that?"

"Partners drown each other." Tamsin sipped her tea. "Two years later, Grebe fell off the Kestrel Bend trestle. Fell, the coroner said, though the coroner had recently paid off a mortgage he'd been drowning under for a decade. And Fenwick—" she paused, and for the first time her voice went quiet in a way that made the room feel smaller, "Fenwick was my husband."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Somewhere outside, the river slapped against a piling, unhurried.

"He saw it coming," Tamsin went on. "Men who keep ledgers see everything coming except the end of the page. He sent the book to me hidden in a crate of survey instruments — your mentor's instruments, as it happens; Odile's predecessor bought them at auction and never opened half the crates. Fenwick always did have a sense of humor. He hid the truth inside the tools of measurement." She smiled, and it wasn't entirely a sad smile. "Then he walked out onto the Narrows ferry one evening and didn't come back, and the river kept him, and I stayed here and kept the book and waited thirty years for somebody to come asking."

"And Odile found it."

"Odile found the *crate*, three months ago, cataloguing the old survey stores. Clever woman. Stubborn as a bent spike. She came here, read the thing cover to cover, and went white as gull bone. Then she told me she was going to take it upriver to the wreck itself, because testimony needs a body, and the Meridian Star is the only witness left that can't be bought." Tamsin snorted. "Romantic nonsense, of course. But Odile Marsh always did believe the land would testify if you asked it properly. I expect that's why she taught you."

Cyrus's throat felt tight. "Where is she?"

"Tidepool Hollow. Old ferry landing above the Narrows, on the east bank — there's a cabin, or what's left of one. She said she'd wait there until the third tide, and if nobody came, she'd go down to the wreck alone." Tamsin looked at Cyrus over the rim of her cup. "That gives you until tomorrow night. And Cyrus — Vane's people reached the bend yesterday. Two boats moored below the falls. They've been asking after a woman with a survey case."

"They've been asking after *me*."

"They've been asking after anyone with legs and a purpose. This far upriver, that's a short list." Tamsin set down her cup. "Now. Before you go running off into the dark like your mentor, there's a question you need to answer honestly, because I'm too old to help fools twice. What are you going to do when you find her? Take the book and run? Or finish what she started?"

Cyrus looked into her tea. Steam curled off it, and in its surface she caught a warped reflection of the lamplight, the room, herself.

"When I left home," she said slowly, "my mother told me the river doesn't care what you carry. Only what you put down." She met Tamsin's eyes. "I'm going to finish it. The fortune can drown. It probably will anyway."

"Good answer." Tamsin heaved herself up, joints cracking like green kindling, and crossed to a shelf crowded with jars. Behind the row of river glass she pulled out a bundle wrapped in waxed canvas and tossed it to Cyrus, who caught it against her chest. Inside: a waterproof slicker, a coil of oiled line, and a small tin lantern with a shuttered flame.

"Fenwick's," Tamsin said. "He was afraid of the dark, my careful little man. Kept better light than any man on the river." Her voice caught, just once, and then smoothed over like water closing after a stone. "Take it. He'd have wanted it used for exactly this."

Cyrus stood and shouldered her pack. At the door she paused. "Tamsin — Vane's men. If they come here—"

"They'll find a deaf old woman and a fat cat and nothing else. I've been hiding things from bought men since before you were born, child. It's the one trade I mastered." She opened the door onto the smell of wet earth and moving water. "East bank. Tidepool Hollow. Follow the sound of the falls until you want to turn around, and then keep going."

* * *

The dark closed around Cyrus like a hand.

She followed the river path east, lantern shuttered to a coin of light, boots finding the old ferry road by memory and instinct more than sight. The Admliral's territory fell away behind her; ahead, the ground climbed through alder scrub, and the sound of the Narrows grew from a whisper to a mutter to a long, grinding roar, the voice of water forcing itself through stone it had been arguing with for ten thousand years.

She thought about Fenwick Corvan as she walked. A careful man, afraid of the dark, who wrote down the truth and then stepped off a ferry into black water. She thought about how courage wasn't the absence of fear — Odile had told her that once, on a survey platform in the rain, shouting over wind — it was just fear that had been given a job to do.

Halfway up the ridge, she heard the whistle.

One long note, low and mournful, drifting down from upstream. Not a train — nothing had run these rails in decades. It was a boat whistle, deep-throated, the kind the salvage steamers carried. And it was answered, a moment later, by a second whistle closer at hand, somewhere on the water below her, between her and the falls.

They were moving upriver. Tonight.

Cyrus killed the lantern and stood in the absolute dark, heart hammering, listening. Engines now — two of them, out of sync, a lazy mechanical heartbeat on the current. Searchlights would follow. They always did. She counted to sixty, then a hundred, waiting for the white blades of light to sweep the bank.

They didn't come. Instead the engines slowed, and a voice carried faintly across the water, amplified through a speaking trumpet:

*"Marsh! Odile Marsh! We know you're on the east bank! Mr. Vane offers terms!"*

A pause. The river roared.

*"Last chance to come in easy, ma'am! After the third tide, we stop asking!"*

Cyrus crouched in the alder shadow, cold seeping through her knees, and felt something settle in her chest — not fear exactly, or not only fear. Recognition. Odile was up there in the dark, hearing the same voice, and she had gone silent on purpose, and silence was her answer.

Cyrus stood and climbed.

The last mile was the worst — scree slope, then root ladder, then a shelf of rock above a churning pool where the falls began their argument. And there, tucked into the cliff like a bird's nest, stood the cabin: roof caved on one side, walls silvered with age, but a door, and a door meant someone had shut it recently.

No light inside. Cyrus knocked softly, three times, the old survey rhythm — *here, here, here.*

Silence. Then, from within, a voice roughened by days of wind and wariness:

"If you're Vane's, you should know I've got a rifle and poor manners."

"If you're Odile," Cyrus answered, "you should know you taught me the knock."

A long pause. Then the scrape of a bar, and the door swung open, and lamplight spilled out onto the rock, and there stood Odile Marsh — thinner, grayer, a bandage on her forearm, but upright, and her eyes when they found Cyrus's were bright and fierce and, for one unguarded instant, wet.

"Well," Odile said. "You took the dead lines after all."

"You said they weren't dead. You said they were sleeping."

"I say a lot of things." Odile stepped aside. "Get in, girl, and bar the door. The third tide's coming tomorrow night — and tonight, I think, we're going to have company."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, a third whistle sounded in the dark.
