## 12. Third Tide

The tide came in the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once.

Cyrus felt it before she saw it, a shudder through the hull of the ferryman's flat-bottomed skiff, the water beneath them turning from brown to a kind of restless green. The river had been running lazy for two days, half-asleep in the summer heat, and now it woke up all along its length like an old dog hearing a can opener three houses away.

"Third one," said Tamsin Corvan, who had not stopped counting tides since Kestrel Bend. She sat in the bow with her back straight as a surveyor's rod, her hands folded on a leather satchel she hadn't let out of arm's reach since the night she'd handed Cyrus the testimony that could hang a man. "You get six. You've spent two."

"I'm aware of the arithmetic, Miss Corvan."

"Tamsin."

"Tamsin." Cyrus adjusted the tiller. The skiff belonged to a man called Brannock, who had introduced himself as the ferryman of the upper reach and then, when asked what he ferried these days, given the question a long, mournful look, as though she'd asked what a cathedral was for. The answer, it turned out, was: whatever needed carrying, and lately almost nothing did. He charged them in coffee and conversation, both of which Cyrus supplied gladly.

Brannock himself stood amidships with a pole he mostly used as a leaning apparatus, squinting at the water with the expression of a man reading a letter from a relative he didn't trust. "She's coming up fast," he said. "Faster than the second tide. River's been fed somewhere upstream — you can taste it." And he did taste it, actually, dipping two fingers over the side and touching them to his tongue, which Cyrus found either deeply impressive or mildly alarming and had not yet decided between.

"Fed how?" Tamsin asked.

"Rain up in the high country, or somebody opened a sluice, or the river's just in a mood. Rivers get moods. Like wives." He caught himself, glanced at Cyrus, and added hastily, "Or husbands. Or engineers."

"Engineers especially," Cyrus agreed. "We're famous for our moods. There's a whole chapter about it in the handbook."

The joke landed the way her jokes usually did — Tamsin snorted despite herself, and Brannock laughed from his belly, and for a moment the skiff felt less like a vessel fleeing a salvage fleet and more like what it had been pretending to be all along: three people going fishing.

Then the water rose another hand's width against the pilings of the drowned church steeple they were passing, and nobody felt much like fishing anymore.

* * *

Kestrel Bend did not drown all at once. That was the thing nobody who hadn't seen it ever understood. The town drowned in stages, tidally, the way grief does. At low water you could walk the main street if you didn't mind mud to your knees and the stares of the windows. At high water the rooftops became islands and the bell tower became a lighthouse for no ships at all.

They had timed their departure from the Bend for the ebb, and now the flood was chasing them upriver, and behind the flood — slower, heavier, hungrier — came the salvage fleet.

Cyrus had seen it once, two nights ago, from the ridge above Spur Nine: nine vessels strung down the channel like beads on a black wire, lanterns burning, dredges groaning. Nine ships hunting one wreck. The Meridian Star had gone down in '61 with a treasury shipment sealed in her hold, and Halvard Voss — owner of the salvage company, murderer of his own partners, if Tamsin's testimony held — wanted that hold opened before anyone else could read what else was inside it. Because the wreck kept more than gold. It kept a ledger. And the ledger kept names.

And somewhere ahead of all of them, alone on the water or hidden in the reeds or possibly dead, was Odile Marsh.

"You keep looking back," Tamsin said.

"I keep listening back," Cyrus corrected. "There's a difference. Looking back is fear. Listening back is tactics."

"And what do you hear?"

Cyrus listened. Wind in the drowned cedars. Water worrying at the steeple stones. Somewhere far off, a loon making its complaint to no committee in particular. And underneath it, so faint she might have invented it, the deep mechanical cough of a steam dredge working against the current.

"Dredges," she said. "Two of them, maybe three. They've passed the Bend."

Tamsin went very still. "That's impossible. We only left—"

"They travel faster than a skiff poled by a man who stops to taste the river." She said it without heat, and Brannock took it without offense, because that was the arrangement they'd all silently made: Brannock would be Brannock, and everyone would pretend this was fine, because the alternative was rowing themselves and nobody wanted that.

"They'll reach the narrows before us," Tamsin said. Her voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a woman holding something heavy by a fraying rope. "If they take the narrows, they take the wreck."

"Not necessarily." Cyrus pulled the chart from inside her coat — her own map, ink on oilcloth, the dead spur lines of the Cape Reckoning rendered in her own cramped, stubborn hand. She'd drawn every abandoned line, every washed-out culvert, every stretch of track the companies had built and cursed and left to rot. Most people thought the map was a record of failure. Cyrus knew better. It was a record of everything the river couldn't easily follow. "The fleet has to stay in the channel. Dredges draw too much water for anything but the main line. But we don't."

"We don't have a channel?"

"We have something better. We have a railway."

* * *

The old portage line ran along the eastern bank where the river bent hard around a granite shoulder — a quarter mile of track laid in '48 to haul freight around the rapids, abandoned in '53 when the rapids were blasted, forgotten by everyone except, apparently, Cyrus Nystrom and whoever had stolen the rails off the first third of it.

"Theft," Cyrus said, standing in the skiff as Brannock nosed it into the willow shallows, "is the sincerest form of respect for infrastructure. Somebody looked at this line and saw value. I choose to be flattered on behalf of the original builders."

"Somebody stole the rails," Tamsin said.

"Somebody stole most of the rails. Which means we walk the ties, and we carry the boat over the worst of it, and we come out below the narrows ahead of a fleet that thinks the narrows are a door with only one key." She hopped onto the ballast, tested a tie with her boot, and found it solid enough to argue with rather than refuse outright. "It's not elegant."

"It's insane," said Tamsin, climbing out after her.

"It's both," said Brannock, handing down the satchels. "That's usually how the good ones are."

He would go no farther. This had been settled the night before, in the low voice men use when they are declining adventures: Brannock's wife was buried above the Bend, in the churchyard that flooded twice a day, and he did not leave her alone with the water any longer than he had to. He'd said it plainly, without self-pity, and Cyrus had understood that there was nothing in the world she could pay him that would outweigh that.

So they shook hands on the bank, and Brannock gave Cyrus his pole — "for the shallows past the narrows, you'll want it" — and told her, as a parting gift, one last piece of river-lore:

"When the third tide meets the narrows, the water stands still. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen. Old-timers called it the breath. Whatever you mean to do in the narrows, do it in the breath." He tapped his temple. "River holds its breath. So should you."

Then he pushed off, and the skiff went back downriver toward the drowned churchyard and the woman buried under the twice-daily tide, and Cyrus stood watching until the willows took him.

"He loves her," Tamsin said quietly.

"Obviously."

"No — I mean he *loves* her. Fifty years, maybe, and he still won't leave her alone with the water." Tamsin hugged the satchel to her chest. "I keep thinking I know what people are worth, and then I meet a ferryman."

Cyrus didn't answer right away. She was thinking of Odile Marsh, who had taught her that maps were moral documents, and who had then disappeared upriver into silence, and whom Cyrus had spent two days being angry at and two minutes at a time being terrified for. "Come on," she said. "The breath won't wait."

* * *

The portage line was a lesson in humility, which Cyrus endured the way she endured most lessons — loudly.

The ties were black with sixty years of rot and mostly sound; the ballast had slid in places; and at the quarter-mile mark the granite shoulder leaned over the track like a disapproving relative, forcing them to climb single file with the satchels held high. Tamsin turned out to be a better climber than Cyrus, which she mentioned exactly often enough to be unbearable.

"You trained for this," Cyrus panted at one point, hauling herself over a ledge.

"I grew up in Kestrel Bend. Everything was either climbing or drowning. Sometimes both." Tamsin reached down, took Cyrus's wrist with a grip that belied her slight frame, and pulled. "My father ran the pilot boat. He used to say the river doesn't care how strong you are. It cares whether you know where the rocks are."

"That's the whole profession, isn't it. Nobody pays you for strength. They pay you for knowing where the rocks are."

"My father never got paid much."

"No," Cyrus agreed, "the honest ones never do."

They came out of the granite shadow into late-afternoon light, and below them, through a screen of alder, Cyrus saw the narrows — and stopped breathing for a second, the way Brannock had prescribed.

The river funneled between two walls of stone no wider than a wagon road, and the water in the throat of it was doing something she had read about in old surveys but never seen: standing. Not flowing, not pooling — *standing*, a column of green glass trembling between the rocks, smooth as poured tea, while white water raged at both ends of it. The flood tide pushing up met the river's own weight and neither would yield, and for a few minutes each cycle the whole argument simply paused.

"In the breath," she whispered. "We cross in the breath."

"Cross?" Tamsin stared. "On what? There's no bridge."

"There was." Cyrus pointed. On the far wall, rusted brackets jutted from the stone in a line — the sockets of a flume, a timber chute built in '49 to carry ore across the throat. The timber was long gone. The brackets remained, spaced like rungs. "It's not a bridge. It's a ladder lying on its side."

Tamsin looked at the brackets. Then at the standing water, forty feet of absolutely motionless river that would become forty feet of absolutely furious river the moment the breath ended. Then at Cyrus.

"You've done this before."

"No."

"But you've *read* about it."

"Extensively."

Tamsin closed her eyes briefly, the way people pray or count to ten. "Odile Marsh taught you this, didn't she. Confidence first, consequences later."

"Odile taught me that a map is a promise you make to your future self." Cyrus was already checking the knots on the rope coil. "This is me keeping it."

* * *

They crossed in eleven minutes, which Cyrus would later describe as comfortable and Tamsin would describe, accurately, as the longest eleven minutes of her life.

The trick was rhythm. Hand over bracket, foot on bracket, weight tested before trusted, and never — never — look down at the standing water, because the standing water looked back. Halfway across, the river made a sound like a great cat clearing its throat, and the surface of the breath trembled, and Cyrus said, in a bright, conversational tone entirely at odds with her position forty feet above certain death, "Did you know the Meridian Star's captain tried to shoot the narrows at flood tide? Against advice. His own mate wrote a letter about it. Said the captain wanted to save an hour."

"What happened?" Tamsin's voice came tight from behind her.

"He saved twenty minutes and lost the ship. She struck the sill below the narrows and went down in the deep pool with her cargo sealed and her crew mostly ashore." Cyrus reached the far bank and hauled herself up onto stone. "Which is why the wreck is where it is, and why Voss needs dredges instead of divers, and why we're about to beat nine ships through a door they think has one key."

Tamsin came over the last bracket, rolled onto the bank, lay flat, and breathed at the sky for a while. Then she sat up, and her face had changed — the fear burned off, leaving something harder and brighter underneath.

"The crew ashore," she said. "When she sank. My grandfather was one of them."

Cyrus went still. In two days of traveling together, Tamsin had spoken of Kestrel Bend constantly and of her family almost never. "You never said."

"There was never a moment that wasn't about surviving the next ten minutes." Tamsin brushed grit from her palms. "But here's the thing, Cyrus. Here's the thing I've been carrying since the Bend. The crew went ashore because my grandfather refused to make the run. He told the captain the narrows were lying. The captain called him a coward, docked his pay, and ordered the ship through with a skeleton crew — and the rest of them, including my grandfather, took the long road around the portage and watched her strike the sill from the bank." She touched the old flume brackets, gently, like gravestones. "The coward saved his life by refusing an order. The brave man lost his ship. And the cargo in her hold wasn't just treasury gold. My grandfather always said there was a strongbox aboard that never appeared on any manifest. A box the partners fought over. A box Voss—" She stopped.

"A box with the ledger in it," Cyrus finished softly.

"Maybe. Or papers. Or proof. Grandfather never knew. He only knew the partners were afraid of it, and that within five years every partner who'd argued about it was dead except one." Tamsin stood, and the light of the lowering sun caught her face, and she looked, for a moment, remarkably like Odile Marsh — same set jaw, same terrible patience. "Voss isn't dredging for gold. Gold he could buy anywhere. He's dredging for the box, because the box is the only thing in the world that can prove what he did. The treasury shipment is the excuse. The wreck is the excuse. We are the only people on this river who know what's actually at stake."

Below them, faint but unmistakable now, the sound of the dredges carried up the channel — two, maybe three, grinding closer to the narrows, closer to the pool, closer to the Meridian Star and whatever slept in her sealed hold.

And then, threading through that industrial growl, Cyrus heard something else. Something small and human and impossible.

From the deep pool below the narrows — from the water itself — came the thin, wavering note of a tin whistle playing a river shanty, badly, with tremendous conviction.

Cyrus froze. Tamsin grabbed her arm. "What is that?"

Cyrus knew the tune. She'd known it since she was nineteen years old, sitting on a surveyor's camp stool, learning that maps were moral documents. It was the song Odile hummed when she was thinking. When she was hiding. When she wanted someone to find her — but only someone who knew to listen.

"She's alive," Cyrus breathed. "She's in the pool. She's *at the wreck*." And already she was running down the bank toward the water, toward the breath, toward the whistle, shouting over her shoulder words that Tamsin would repeat later, word for word, in a courtroom that had been bought and paid for:

"Get the satchel! We're going in on the fourth tide — and God help anyone standing between us and that hold!"
