## 13. The Wreck of the Meridian Star

The fog came off the river at dawn like a held breath finally let go.

Cyrus felt it before she saw it — a cool wet weight settling on her shoulders as she stood at the bow of Tamsin Corvan's leaky flatboat, watching the gray water part around the prow in long slow folds. Somewhere behind them, downriver, the salvage fleet's lamps still burned through the murk, a string of dirty orange beads strung along the current. They'd gained on the fleet during the night by running the old ferry channel, the one that wasn't on any chart anymore because Cyrus had taken it off her own map eleven years ago, out of spite, after the county sold the Kestrel Bend spur for scrap.

Spite, it turned out, was an excellent navigational aid.

"There," Tamsin said quietly.

She was pointing with her chin, the way river people pointed, hands being otherwise occupied with the sweep oar. Ahead and to starboard, where the fog thinned to gauze, something rose out of the water that should not have been there.

A mast. Or what was left of one — a black spar leaning at a drunkard's angle, its rigging gone to lace, a few shrouds hanging like the strings of a drowned harp. And beneath it, just breaking the surface at low tide, the curved shoulder of a hull, furred with forty years of silt and mussel shell, broad as a barn roof.

Cyrus had drawn this wreck a hundred times. She had drawn it from memory, from Odile's old survey notes, from the drunken testimony of pilots who swore they'd seen it at extreme low water. She had drawn it in the margins of her maps as a warning symbol, a small careful X with the notation *MERIDIAN STAR — DO NOT ANCHOR — HULL HAZARD*.

None of those drawings had prepared her for how *large* it was. How patient. It sat in the shallows like a sleeping animal, and every drawing she'd ever made of it suddenly seemed like the sketches of someone who had only ever seen a bear's footprint.

"Forty years," Tamsin said, "and she's barely settled. Look at her lines. She went down soft."

"Soft?"

"No explosion. No fire. She didn't break up on the bar." Tamsin's eyes were narrow, professional. "Somebody opened her to the river. Deliberate. Slow. Scuttled her right here in the shallows where nobody would bother diving, because everybody already knew where she sank." A pause. "Except nobody ever asked why she sank *here*, did they? Two hundred yards from a town dock. Any captain worth his ticket would have run her aground on the sand spit instead. Salvageable."

Cyrus stared at the hull. The fog moved through the rigging and made a sound like very distant wind chimes.

"Odile knew," she said. "That's why she came back. That's why she went silent. She wasn't hiding from the salvage company." Her throat tightened. "She was hiding *the wreck* from them. Keeping them busy chasing rumors upriver while she worked."

"And we've just led them straight to it," Tamsin said, not unkindly, "by being clever about the ferry channel."

Cyrus turned. Downriver, faint as embers under ash, the salvage fleet's lamps were moving. Not toward them. Toward the narrows — which meant toward Spur Nine, which meant they'd found the rail line and were running their lighters up the old portage route. Six hours, maybe eight, depending on how much of the channel had silted since the company last surveyed it.

Eight hours to open a sealed hold that had kept its secret for forty years.

"Well," Cyrus said, "we'd best be rude guests and arrive early."

* * *

Tamsin Corvan ran her boat the way some people played chess — three moves ahead and pretending it was all instinct. She took them in past the wreck's broken bowsprit through water so shallow Cyrus could see the ghost of the keel below them, a dark seam in the sandy bottom, and then swung the flatboat around so its stern kissed the hull with a sound like a door closing softly.

"Mud ladder," Tamsin said, nodding at a rusted iron rung-set bolted into the hull, half-buried in silt. "Rivermen used these to board grounded barges. You first. I'll hold us steady and mind the gear."

"You could wait in the boat," Cyrus offered. "What's down there isn't going to be pleasant. Forty-year-old drownings rarely are."

Tamsin gave her a look that Cyrus was beginning to recognize as the Corvan family's entire emotional vocabulary. It said: *I survived the flood of '09, I buried two brothers, and you think a bad smell will send me home?*

"I'll come down slowly, then," Cyrus said.

The hull's deck, when she reached it, was a garden. Silt had become soil; soil had become meadow. Pale grasses grew in drifts across the planking, and a stunted willow had rooted itself in the ruin of the main hatch coaming, no thicker than Cyrus's wrist but stubbornly, absurdly alive. Somebody's boot sole — leather gone to wax, stitching gone to memory — lay half-swallowed by grass near the foremast step.

It should have been horrifying. Instead it was strangely gentle. The river hadn't destroyed the Meridian Star; it had *adopted* her. Given her a green coat and let her sleep.

"The hold access is aft," Cyrus said, consulting the survey notes she'd copied from Odile's originals — the ones Marsh had left in the Kestrel Bend station safe, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note in her precise drafting hand: *CYRUS. IF YOU ARE READING THIS, YOU DIDN'T LISTEN TO ME, WHICH IS CONSISTENT. THE HOLD IS UNDER THE ENGINE ROOM PLATES. BRING A CROWBAR AND BETTER SENSE THAN YOU'VE EVER SHOWN.*

"She always liked you," Tamsin said dryly.

The engine room hatch was jammed, then it wasn't, and then they were descending into the dark on a ladder that groaned like an old man negotiating stairs, with Tamsin's storm lantern swinging shadows across walls furred white with mineral bloom.

The air below was cold and thick and tasted of iron and old wood and something else — something sweetish and wrong that Cyrus chose not to name. Water stood knee-deep on the orlop deck, perfectly still, perfectly black, holding their reflections like a second, darker crew.

"Engine room's through here," Cyrus whispered, though there was no reason to whisper except that the ship demanded it. Some spaces insist on quiet. Churches. Libraries. Tombs.

The engine itself rose out of the black water ahead of them — a mountain of brass and iron, its great walking beam tilted against the overhead like a felled giant still trying to remember how to stand. Cyrus put her palm flat against it. Cold. But beneath the cold, faintly, she could feel it: the hum. Not sound exactly. More like standing near a large animal and feeling its breath.

Every engineer knew the Meridian Star's engines. They'd been famous — Harrow & Vane compound works, rated the finest on the coasting trade. Cyrus had grown up on stories of them. Her father had helped pour one of the bearing housings, back when he still talked about his work instead of drinking about it.

And now here she stood inside the story, ankle-deep in the dark, with her hand on the metal her father had touched when it was hot and singing.

"He'd have loved this," she murmured.

"What's that?"

"Nothing. Plates." She knelt, wincing as the cold water soaked through to her skin, and began feeling along the deck plates the way Odile's note described. "Under the engine room floor. She hid the access under the engine room floor, which means she read the same insurance reports I did, which means—" Her fingers found it. A plate edge that sat proud of its neighbors by less than a finger's width. Freshly pried. The rust around it broken in bright clean scars. "—she's been here recently. Very recently."

"How recently?"

Cyrus touched the bright metal. It was cold, but not river-cold. Not yet.

"Days," she said. "Tamsin. Days."

* * *

The plate came up with a shriek that echoed through the whole drowned hull, and both of them froze afterward, listening, hearts hammering, absurdly certain the ship herself would object.

Nothing. Just the drip of water somewhere far aft, keeping time like a patient clerk.

Below the plate: a shaft, lined with lead sheeting — someone had *lined* it, someone had cared enough to keep the river out — descending into a space that the lantern couldn't bottom. A rope ladder hung down it, new rope, tarred, Odile's knots. Cyrus knew those knots the way she knew her own signature. A bight hitch with the working end backed twice. Odile Marsh had been tying that exact knot to torment apprentices for thirty years.

"She went down," Cyrus breathed. "Into the hold. Through the engine room. She built herself a private door."

"Why hide the door if the cargo's already down there?"

"Because the cargo isn't what she came for." Cyrus was already swinging onto the ladder. "The treasury shipment is what everyone thinks is down there. What Odile came for is *proof*. Papers. The partners' correspondence. Whatever was in the captain's strongroom that made somebody scuttle a perfectly good ship in a town's front yard." She looked up at Tamsin, and the lantern light caught her face and made her look, for a moment, younger and more frightened than she'd let herself be in years. "The money was never the point, Tamsin. The money is the *lock*. The crime is what's inside it."

"Then let's pick it," Tamsin said, and followed her down.

* * *

The hold of the Meridian Star was dry.

Not damp, not musty — *dry*, in the way of a place the river had agreed to forget. The lead lining and the hull's deep bury in the mud shelf had conspired to seal it, and inside, the air was close and brown and smelled of paper and pitch and time. Their lantern showed crates stacked in careful ranks, stenciled with faded customs marks, and above them, lashed to the deckhead beams, row upon row of sealed strongboxes with the Treasury's stamp still legible under forty years of dust.

Enough money, Cyrus thought numbly, to buy the whole territory. Enough to buy the law, the newspapers, the governor, and a modest navy besides. It sat there in the dark doing nothing, being nothing, worth everything, and it was somehow the least interesting thing in the room.

Because against the far bulkhead, lit by their single flame, stood a camp desk. A folding canvas cot. A stack of document tubes. A cold stove. And pinned to the bulkhead with four rusted nails, a chart of the Cape Reckoning — *her* chart, her own draftsmanship, copied in Odile's hand with corrections and annotations in red ink — covered in lines.

Lines everywhere. Spur lines, ferry channels, sounding tracks. And connecting them, in red, a web of routes and dates and names, and at the center of the web, circled twice, a name Cyrus had known her whole life without ever knowing what it meant:

**A. RENNICK.**

The salvage fleet's owner. The man whose lighters were even now grinding up the narrows toward them.

"Oh," Tamsin said softly. "Oh, that's why she went silent."

"That's why she went silent." Cyrus stepped closer to the chart. Her voice came out strange, thin. "She didn't disappear, Tamsin. She *buried* herself. Right next to the evidence. Because out there—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the world, the bought law, the fleet, "—he owns everyone who might listen. In here, the only witness is the river. And the river doesn't take payments."

The document tubes were labeled in Odile's hand. *Corvan & Marsh, letters, 1881–83.* *Partnership dissolution — unsigned.* *Deposition, E. Quill, boatswain, sworn at Kestrel Bend, 1883 — UNDELIVERED.*

Undelivered. For forty years. A dead man's testimony sitting thirty feet from the drowned town where he'd sworn it.

Cyrus reached for the last tube — and stopped.

Because the cot was warm.

Not warm-warm. But not cold. Not forty-years cold, not days cold. The blanket was thrown back. The stove held ash that, when she touched it, still had a living gray heart. Someone had been here within hours. Someone had eaten, slept, worked, and left in a hurry — the document tubes were packed but not secured, the chart pins freshly pressed, and there, on the camp desk, a tin cup with a ring of tea leaves drying at the bottom.

Beside the cup, weighted under a brass fitting, a single sheet of paper, folded once.

Cyrus unfolded it with hands that were not entirely steady.

The handwriting was Odile's. Of course it was. Precise, unadorned, furious in its calm:

*Cyrus. If you're reading this, you've beaten Rennick's fleet here, which means either you've become competent or the river likes you, and I refuse to believe either.*

*I've gone ahead to Kestrel Bend. There's someone there I need you to hear before anyone else does — the last survivor of the scuttling, and she has waited forty years to testify, and she will only do it once, and only to you, God help us both.*

*Take the tubes. Guard the chart. And Cyrus — whatever you find in the fourth tube, do not read it on the water.*

*— O.M.*

Cyrus looked at the fourth tube. Plain, unlabeled, heavier than the others.

Downriver, muffled by forty feet of water and a hillside and the fog, came a sound she'd hoped not to hear until tomorrow: the deep, carrying boom of a salvage steam-whistle. Then another. Then a third, closer.

The fleet had cleared the narrows.

"They're coming," Tamsin said from the ladder, lantern high, her face half gold and half shadow. "How long do we have?"

Cyrus slung the document tubes across her back, took up the fourth one last, and felt its weight settle against her spine like a question.

"Less than we need," she said. "Move."

Above them, through the open hatch, the fog was lifting off the river — and in the clearing gray, far downstream, the first black shape of a salvage lighter slid into view around the bend, its derricks raised like arms, coming straight for the wreck.

And standing at its bow, small even at that distance, unmistakable even now, a man in a pale coat raised his hat to the Meridian Star — as though greeting an old friend.

A. Rennick had come in person.
