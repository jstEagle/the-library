## 14. Odile

The cabin sat where the map said it shouldn't.

That was the first thing Cyrus understood about it — that Odile Marsh had built her hiding place on a piece of paper Cyrus herself had drawn, and had then drawn a lie over the truth of it, so that anyone following Cyrus's map would walk straight past the cabin and curse the fog. Cyrus had followed her own map, too. She'd simply also followed thirty years of knowing the woman who'd taught her to draw it, and knowing meant noticing that Odile never lied in ink without leaving a small apology somewhere nearby. Here the apology was a cairn of rail spikes, five of them, stacked in the shape of a star. The Meridian Star. Five points. *I'm sorry I erased myself from your lines,* the cairn said, in the only language Odile Marsh had ever been fluent in.

Cyrus stood at the treeline with rain running off her hat brim and let herself feel the whole weight of it for exactly four seconds. Then she walked up to the door and knocked, because Odile hated surprises more than she hated trespassers, and Cyrus had no intention of being shot by her own mentor before breakfast.

The knock was answered by silence. Then, from inside, a voice like gravel poured slowly through a funnel:

"If you're from Corvan's fleet, you've wasted a bullet's worth of walking."

"It's Cyrus."

Another silence, longer. A chair scraped. Bolts turned — two of them, then a third Cyrus hadn't known existed — and the door opened on Odile Marsh looking older than the river itself and entirely unrepentant about it. She was seventy-one if she was a day, thin as a surveyor's rule, with white hair cut short as a man's and eyes the flat gray of a chart in bad light. She held a shotgun in the crook of her arm the way other women held knitting.

"You look terrible," Odile said.

"You went silent for three weeks and made me row up a flooded river to find you."

"Yes," Odile agreed, stepping back to let her in. "So you look terrible. Come in before the fog comes in after you. It follows people. I'm fairly sure it holds grudges."

* * *

The cabin was one room, and every inch of it was paper.

Charts hung from the rafters on strings, overlapping like the scales of some enormous document fish. Survey notes were pinned to the walls in columns. On the single table, under a lamp with a cracked glass chimney, lay a ledger bound in green leather gone soft with age, weighted down at both ends with — Cyrus noted with a pang — two more rail spikes.

"Sit," Odile said. "There's tea. It's terrible. I've been drinking river water boiled with pine needles since Tuesday and I've stopped pretending it's a choice."

Cyrus sat. "You could have come downriver. You could have sent word."

"I could have," Odile said, pouring tea into two tin cups with the steady hand of a woman who had once sighted a bridge alignment through a hurricane, "and then Wendell Corvan's lawyers would have known where I was within the day, and his salvage fleet would have known within two, and everything I've spent eleven years digging out of the mud would have been bought, buried, or drowned. So instead I let the expedition 'go silent.' Which, incidentally, was easy, because half my expedition quit the moment they understood what we'd found, and the other half I paid to go home and keep their mouths shut." She handed Cyrus a cup. "Drink. You're swaying."

Cyrus drank. It was, as promised, terrible. "What did you find?"

Odile didn't answer right away. She crossed to the wall of pinned notes, and Cyrus watched her mentor — the sharpest mind the Cape Reckoning survey corps had ever produced, the woman who had taught a nineteen-year-old track-walker that a map was a moral document — stand in front of her own life's work and, for one unguarded moment, look afraid.

"In 1891," Odile said finally, "the Meridian Star went down in the Narrows with a sealed cargo manifest. Official story: treasury shipment lost in the flood, tragic, everyone mourned, the insurance paid out to three men who owned the salvage rights jointly. Corvan, Hale, and a man named Ambrose Pettibone." She tapped a yellowed clipping. "Hale died in 1894. Fell off a ferry. Pettibone died in 1897. Shot by a hunter who mistook him for a deer. At dusk. In a meadow. While wearing a bright red coat." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "The hunter was paid forty dollars and left the territory. I found him in Ashford. He's a barber now. He cries when he talks about it."

"And Corvan?"

"Corvan became the richest man on the Cape," Odile said, "and bought himself a salvage fleet, a courthouse, and two judges, which is why nothing I put in front of the law will ever see daylight while he breathes." She turned from the wall. "But the river doesn't take bribes, Cyrus. The river keeps books. And eleven years ago I started learning how to read them."

* * *

She showed him — showed *her*, Cyrus corrected herself silently; old habits of the survey corps died hard — the evidence in the order Odile had clearly rehearsed, because Odile Marsh rehearsed everything, including her griefs.

The manifest fragment, recovered from a Kestrel Bend attic: not a treasury shipment at all, but a transfer ledger, listing sums paid to names that matched, in Odile's neat annotation, the payroll of a private militia hired in the winter of 1890 to clear squatters off land Corvan wanted for his rail spur. Clearing that had involved fire.

A letter, water-stained but legible, in Hale's hand, warning Pettibone that "W. means to be sole heir to this venture and we are the obstacle thereto."

And last — laid on the table with both hands, the way you handle something living — a deposition. Signed. Witnessed. Dated eight days ago.

"Tamsin Corvan," Cyrus read aloud, and looked up. "His daughter?"

"His daughter," Odile said, "who was nine years old in 1891, who saw her father pay the men who burned the bend, and who has spent her whole life being careful around him. She came to find me in June. Walked sixty miles to do it. She'll testify — publicly, under oath, knowing what it costs her — but only if there's someone left to swear her in who isn't already bought." Odile sat down across from Cyrus, and for the first time all evening her voice dropped its surveyor's crispness and went quiet. "That's why I went silent. Not to hide the evidence. To stay alive long enough to deliver it. There's a circuit judge coming up from Grayhaven on the fourth tide's steamer. One honest man left on this coast, and he doesn't know he's honest yet because nobody's asked him anything worth lying about."

Cyrus set down her cup. "The fleet's already on the river, Odile. Six boats. They're not salvaging the Star — they're raising that hold before any judge arrives, and they'll burn whatever's in it."

"I know. I've been counting their smoke."

"Then we have—"

"Two tides," Odile said. "Maybe three, if the weather stays foul, and I'd bet on the weather. I always do. Weather is the one opponent who never takes money."

Cyrus laughed despite herself — a short, exhausted sound — and Odile's face softened into something Cyrus hadn't seen since she was twenty-two and had just botched her first solo alignment: pride, worn openly, like a badge on a coat.

"You followed the dead lines," Odile said. "All of them. In flood season. Alone."

"Not alone. I had a boat. And a very judgmental heron."

"Mm. Those herons." Odile rose and began taking charts down from the rafters, folding them with quick, economical movements. "Then here's what your map wouldn't tell you, because I made sure of it. The Narrows can't be run by a fleet. Too many drowned pilings, and they shift with every flood. Corvan's boats will have to anchor above the wreck and work the hold with divers — slow, careful, daylight work. But there's an old maintenance path along the north bluff, a walker's line from when the spur was live. One person, on foot, in the dark, can reach the wreck in a night. Two people," she added, meeting Cyrus's eyes, "if the second one still remembers how to read my hand."

Cyrus stared at her. "You want to go down to the wreck. You. Seventy-one years old and armed with a shotgun and a grudge."

"I want to *see* it raised," Odile said. "Or sunk properly, with the truth still nailed inside it. And I want to be standing on the bank when the judge steams past, so that when Tamsin Corvan testifies, there's a witness of record who watched that hold open." She slung a canvas satchel over her shoulder. It clinked. "Also, I've been eating pine-needle tea for three weeks, and if I die on this river I would prefer it be for a reason."

* * *

They left at dusk, when the fog came up the valley like a tide of milk and swallowed the cabin behind them. Odile walked the north bluff path without a lantern, from memory, calling out hazards in the low, even voice she'd used on survey crews for fifty years — "root, left side; washout, two steps wide; don't step there, that's not ground, that's optimism" — and Cyrus followed, laughing quietly in the dark, feeling twenty-two again and hating it and loving it.

Near midnight they crested the last rise, and the Narrows opened below them: black water threading between black stone, and there, anchored mid-channel with lanterns strung along her deck like a carnival, the lead boat of the salvage fleet. Divers' lamps glowed beneath her hull, pale green ghosts descending into the dark.

And below the fleet, visible only as a distortion in the current, a shadow longer than a church lay on the riverbed.

"There she is," Odile murmured. "Thirty-four years at the bottom, and she's still keeping everyone's secrets." She settled onto a fallen log, pulled a flask from her satchel, and offered it. Cyrus took a swallow. It was not tea. It was significantly better than tea.

"So," Odile said, watching the lanterns. "Two tides until the steamer. One night until the divers reach the hold. Tell me honestly, Cyrus Nystrom, since you're the only honest engineer left on this river—" she turned, and the gray eyes gleamed in the dark, sharp and amused and utterly unafraid, "—how good are you at sinking things?"

Down in the Narrows, a diver's lamp flickered, steadied, and began to descend toward the Meridian Star.
