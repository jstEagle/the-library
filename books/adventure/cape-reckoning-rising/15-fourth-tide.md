## 15. Fourth Tide

The answer to Odile's question, Cyrus discovered, was: not very good, historically speaking.

She had sunk exactly one thing in her life — a rowboat called the *Patience*, which had gone down off the Kestrel Bend pilings in '09 with her father's tackle box aboard, and even that had been an accident she'd spent three summers denying. Sinking things on purpose, on a schedule, under the nose of a salvage fleet with divers already in the water, was a different discipline entirely.

"It's a matter of ballast," Odile said, as though they were discussing a garden fence. She had spread Cyrus's map across the floor of the lightkeeper's shack at Spur Nine and was weighting the corners with river stones, a tin cup, and one boot. "Everything sinks, given time. The art is in choosing what sinks first."

"The hold is already underwater," Cyrus said. "That's rather the point of it being a wreck."

"The *hold* is underwater. The cargo isn't lost yet. Those divers will have it on deck by morning if we let them." Odile tapped the map where the Narrows pinched tight between shale bluffs. "So we don't let them. We make the river take it back."

Cyrus looked at the map for a long moment. Twenty years of drawing those lines, walking those grades, sounding those channels, and she had never once thought of them as anything but a record of what was. Odile looked at them and saw levers.

"You want to drop the bluff," Cyrus said slowly.

"I want to *ask* the bluff to drop. Politely. It's been wanting to for forty years — look at your own notation. Slump zone. Undercut. You wrote 'unstable' twice and underlined it once, which I believe is the closest you come to shouting."

Cyrus felt her ears go warm. It was true. She had underlined it.

* * *

They worked through the slack of the third tide by lantern light, and it was the strangest night's labor of Cyrus's life, because it didn't feel like sabotage. It felt like maintenance.

The old spur line ran along the top of the eastern bluff above the Narrows, and where the line crossed the weakest seam of shale there stood a loading platform built in the boom years and abandoned ever since — a timber trestle jutting out over the drop like a jaw. Underneath it, the hillside was honeycombed with culverts and drainage adits, cut to keep the grade dry, all of them choked now with silt and root.

"Water does the work," Odile said, up to her elbows in muck, hauling debris out of the lowest adit with the enthusiasm of a woman twenty years younger. "It always has. All we do is decide where it goes. You taught me that, incidentally. First lesson you ever gave me, on the survey crew. 'The river always wins, Miss Marsh; our job is only to lose gracefully.'"

"I never said *gracefully*."

"You said something very close to it and then fell in a creek."

Cyrus let that stand. There was a warmth in her chest she didn't entirely trust — the old feeling of working beside Odile Marsh, when every hard thing had seemed like a joke they were both in on. She'd spent eleven years telling herself that feeling was a lie wrapped around a betrayal. It was proving stubbornly difficult to keep believing that down here in the dark, mud to the wrists, while Odile hummed something tuneless and cheerful.

"Tell me about the ledger again," Cyrus said, mostly to keep herself from humming along. "The one in the hold."

Odile went quiet for a moment. When she answered, the cheerfulness had drained out, leaving something older and harder underneath.

"Tamsin Corvan kept the company books before she kept the ferry. She was a girl then — bookkeeper's apprentice to Halloway and Voss and Merrick, the three original partners. When the *Meridian Star* went down, she was the one who noticed the numbers didn't match. The treasury shipment was logged at full weight. But the bills of lading — the ones signed before loading — showed half of it had already been sold off. Drawn down over two years, quietly, in amounts small enough that no auditor would blink."

"Drawn down by whom?"

"That's the question the ledger answers. Every withdrawal is initialed. Three sets of initials on the early ones. Only one set on the last two years' worth." Odile sat back on her heels in the mud. "Edmund Voss. He didn't just steal from the treasury. He stole from his own partners, and when they started asking questions, he scuttled their ship with them aboard and let the river take the blame. Then he bought the salvage rights, the courts, the newspapers, and most of the territory's better manners, and he's been sitting on that wreck for thirty years — not because he wants the cargo back, Cyrus. Because he can't afford for anyone to read what's written on the crates' accompanying manifest. The ledger went down *with* the ship. His initials are at the bottom of the river."

"And the survivor. Corvan."

"She saw the scuttling charges go in. She was sixteen and nobody believed a word of it, because Voss owned everyone who might have. She's been ferrying that stretch of water for thirty years, waiting for someone to ask the right questions." Odile smiled thinly. "I asked them. That's why I went silent. A loud expedition gets bought or buried. A silent one gets to finish."

* * *

By the turn of the tide they had cleared four adits and rigged what Odile insisted on calling "the arrangement" — a phrase Cyrus found suspiciously innocent. It consisted of the old culvert pipes packed with scrap rail and stone, a sluice channel cut to funnel the rising water into the weakest seam, and a timber release gate held shut by a single pin that could be knocked loose with a boat hook from the platform above.

"When the fourth tide comes up," Odile explained, wiping her hands on her coat, "the water fills the adits faster than they can drain. Pressure builds behind the seam. Knock the pin, and everything we've packed comes down the slope into the Narrows, right on top of the channel where their dive barge sits anchored."

"And if the pin sticks?"

"Then we knock harder."

Cyrus looked down at the Narrows. The salvage barge *Vigilant* rode at anchor below, lamps burning, the slow pulse of an air compressor thudding through the dark like a sleeping animal's heart. A diver's lamp moved somewhere beneath the surface, faint and green and patient.

"They'll hear the charge of the rockfall," she said. "They'll know."

"They'll know the bluff came down. Bluffs come down on this river every spring. And by the time they've finished cursing and re-anchoring, the channel will have shifted, the wreck will be buried under forty tons of new shale, and their divers will need a week to find the hold again." Odile's eyes glinted. "A week is more than we need. Tamsin ferries us down to testify at the territorial commission sitting at Reckoning Harbor on the sixth tide. Voss's own lawyers summoned the hearing, thinking to bury me with paperwork. Instead we bring him the ledger."

"And if the ledger's unreadable? Thirty years underwater—"

"Ink fades. Pencil doesn't. Tamsin kept her books in pencil because ink froze in winter." Odile stood, joints cracking, and looked out at the black water with an expression Cyrus couldn't read. "She planned this for a long time, Cyrus. Longer than I've been alive. All she needed was an engineer foolish enough to help."

"Foolish enough," Cyrus agreed, "or loyal enough."

The silence that followed had eleven years inside it.

"Cyrus." Odile's voice was low. "What I did to you—"

"Don't." Cyrus said it gently, but she said it. "Not tonight. Tonight we sink a hillside. You can ruin my evening some other time."

To her surprise, Odile laughed — a real laugh, short and startled, the kind Cyrus remembered from a decade ago and had told herself she'd forgotten. "Fair enough," Odile said. "But I'm keeping the debt. I've kept worse longer."

* * *

The fourth tide began to rise an hour before dawn, and it rose angry.

There was weather behind it — Cyrus had smelled it since midnight, a green metallic edge on the wind, and now the clouds came downriver in a solid wall with lightning walking inside them. Rain hit the shale in fat, slapping drops. Down in the Narrows the salvage crew had seen it coming too; she watched lanterns multiply on the *Vigilant*'s deck, heard the compressor change pitch as they brought the diver up.

"Storm cover," Odile said beside her, shouting over the wind. "They'll pull the diver and ride it out. Nobody dives in this."

"Then we go now, while they're busy."

They climbed to the platform. The rain made the timber slick as soap. Below them the Narrows had gone from black water to boiling gray, whitecaps standing up in the gusts, and the *Vigilant* swung at its anchors with its running lights doubled against the storm.

Cyrus took the boat hook and set its tip against the release pin. It was a simple thing, a foot of iron rod through a timber bracket, holding the gate shut on forty tons of arranged collapse. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere below, at the bottom of the channel, lay a ship full of dead men and a dead man's confession, and a fortune that could buy the whole territory, and she was about to bury all of it under a hillside on the strength of a pencil-written ledger and a ferrywoman's thirty-year grudge.

It was, she reflected, the most honest engineering she'd ever done.

"For the record," she shouted to Odile, "I still think your plan is insane."

"Noted for the record!" Odile shouted back, delighted. "Pull!"

Cyrus pulled.

The pin came free with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the gate swung, and the river did what rivers do.

For one heartbeat nothing happened. Then the adits exhaled — a long, groaning rush of water bursting from the hillside's throat — and the whole face of the bluff shuddered, shrugged, and let go. It went down in stages, great slabs of shale peeling away and folding into the current, taking the old trestle's legs with it, taking tons of Cyrus's carefully packed scrap rail, taking forty years of gravity's patience and spending it all at once. The impact threw up a wave that lifted the *Vigilant* and slammed it sideways against its anchors. On deck, tiny figures scattered like spilled grain. Somewhere a whistle shrieked, thin and furious, swallowed by thunder.

Then the dust and spray settled into the rain, and the Narrows was a different river. The channel Cyrus had charted was gone. Where the deep pool had lain over the wreck there was now a roiling shallows furred with new rubble, and the *Meridian Star* slept under a hillside's worth of fresh grave.

Odile gripped Cyrus's shoulder and shouted something, but the storm ate it. Cyrus turned —

And stopped.

Downstream, around the bend below the Narrows, lights were coming up against the current. Not the *Vigilant*'s work lights. Running lights, white and red, in a line — three vessels, maybe four, big ones, pushing hard through weather no sane pilot would choose.

Odile followed her gaze. Even through the rain, Cyrus saw her go still.

"That's the *Corvan's Wake*," Odile shouted. "Tamsin's ferry. And she's supposed to be *waiting* for us downstream — she's bringing company."

The lead vessel swung into the Narrows, its searchlight sweeping the collapsed bluff, the ruined platform, the two small figures caught on what remained of it like flies in amber. The beam pinned them, blinding, and through the drum of the rain came a voice amplified by a brass megaphone, smooth and unhurried and horribly familiar from a dozen newspaper portraits:

"Well," said Edmund Voss. "Isn't this a coincidence."
