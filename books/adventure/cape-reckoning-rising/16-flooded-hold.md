## 16. Flooded Hold

The Meridian Star had gone down with her bow pointed at the sky, which meant her stern — and the hold beneath it — was the deepest part of the wreck, and the deepest part of the wreck was now a cathedral of black water.

Cyrus stood on the tilted deck in the gray light before dawn and tried to do the arithmetic without flinching. Forty feet of river over the hatch coaming. The cargo manifest Odile had copied said the treasury shipment sat in the aftermost compartment, behind two bulkheads, one of which had been damaged when the ship struck the old bridge piling. The water inside would be at pressure equalization by now, which was good news. The bad news was everything else.

"You're making that face," Tamsin Corvan said beside her. She had a rope coiled over one shoulder and a diver's helmet under her arm, brass and glass, older than either of them. "The face where you're counting all the ways this kills us."

"I'm an engineer. Counting is what I have instead of faith."

"And how's that working out?"

"Ask me again in an hour."

Tamsin laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like a door opening in a house that hadn't been lived in for years. She was seventy-one years old and the last survivor of Kestrel Bend, and she had insisted on coming down to the wreck herself, over Odile's objection and Cyrus's better judgment, because — as she put it — she'd been watching that ship sit in the river since she was nine years old, and she wasn't going to let it give up its secrets to strangers now.

Odile Marsh emerged from the wheelhouse, where she'd spent the night cross-checking the manifest against the builder's plans Cyrus had recovered from the survey camp. She moved stiffly; there was a bandage on her forearm from the encounter at Spur Nine, and she favored her left leg in a way she pretended not to. In the flat early light she looked older than Cyrus remembered, and more tired, and exactly as unbending.

"The after bulkhead is intact," Odile said, without preamble. "The forward one has a breach about eighteen inches wide, low on the port side. That means we can flood the compartments deliberately, equalize, and work inside rather than fighting suction."

"That means," Cyrus translated, "we sink the ship a little more on purpose."

"That means," Odile said, "it's the only way anyone breathes down there and comes back up. If you have a better idea, Nystrom, I've been waiting thirty years to hear you produce one."

It was an old rhythm between them, worn smooth like a stone in a current. Once it would have stung. Now it felt almost like being held.

* * *

They worked through the morning in the way Cyrus loved best in the world, which was to say: precisely, quietly, with three people who each knew their part. They rigged the pumps off the survey launch. They opened the sea cocks they'd fabricated from spare valve bodies and let the river in on its own terms, slow and controlled, listening to the groan of the old hull as it settled another few degrees into the mud. Tamsin sat on the rail with her boots dangling and narrated the sinking like a woman describing weather.

"She rolled a little to port when she went," Tamsin said. "I remember because my father said the whole town felt it in their floors, like the river had cleared its throat. He was on the dock committee. He signed the complaint against the shipping line and six months later he couldn't get credit anywhere in the county." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "That's how Harrow Vane buys things, girl. Not with money. With silence."

Cyrus's hands paused on the wrench. "You've said his name twice now like you know him."

"I knew all three of them. Vane, Aldous, and Merrick. Before they were rich, when they were just three men with a scheme and a boat." Tamsin looked out at the brown water. "Merrick used to buy penny candy for the children at the bend. Then he drowned in this river, and everyone said accident, and Vane bought the inquest verdict the way he bought everything else. And Aldous—" she stopped, and shook her head. "Aldous is why your mentor came looking for me. But that's her story to tell, or his ghost's."

Below them, the last of the trapped air sighed out of the hold, and the Meridian Star lay still at last, flooded and quiet, ready to be entered.

* * *

Cyrus went down first.

She wore the second helmet — Tamsin's old rig, patched and re-gasketed with wax and desperation — and carried a diver's lamp and a slate pencil, because speaking through the air hose was a fool's errand. The tender above was Odile, who had refused every argument about who would stand topside and who would go below, on the grounds that she had spent thirty years sending other people into dark places and it was past time she took her turn holding the rope.

The water closed over the glass faceplate and the world went green-brown and soft-edged. Cyrus descended the guide line hand over hand, past the rail, past the deckhouse windows with their curtains of silt, into the open hatch.

Inside, the hold was a drowned forest. Cargo cradles rose around her like tree trunks, furred with weed, hung with ropes of algae that swayed in the faint current of her own movement. Her lamp carved a small bright room out of the vast dark, and beyond the edge of the light the water simply continued, patient and total.

She found the breach in the forward bulkhead easily enough — Odile's measurements had been exact, because Odile's measurements were always exact — and squeezed through the torn plate into the second compartment, where the visibility was worse and the silence was somehow heavier, as though the river here were holding its breath.

And then her lamp caught it.

The strongbox room was small, built into the very stern, and the treasury crates were stacked in their original cradles exactly as they'd been loaded forty years ago, because nobody had ever come back for them. Nobody except the men who'd arranged for the ship to be sunk, and they had never intended to recover anything. They had only ever needed the wreck to exist, sealed and unexamined, so that the question of what happened to the shipment would stay buried under forty feet of water forever.

Except the crates were wrong.

Cyrus ran her gloved hands along the nearest one, feeling through the sludge. Lead-lined, yes. Heavy, yes. But the seal across the lid — the treasury wax seal that should have been intact — had been broken and re-set, clumsily, long ago. And when she pried at the corner plank, it came away too easily, rotted along a cut someone had made and hidden again.

Empty. Or nearly. She got the lid up a hand's width and swept her lamp inside and saw only river silt, and something else — a shape wrapped in oilcloth, wedged into the false bottom.

Not gold. Paper.

Her heart was going hard enough that she could hear it inside the helmet. She worked the bundle free, tucked it into the canvas bag at her hip, and signaled the line. Twice up, once down. *Coming up.*

* * *

On deck, she stripped the helmet and stood dripping in the wind while Odile watched her with the expression of a woman who had already read the answer on her face.

"It's not money," Cyrus said. "It was never money. The crates are hollow. There's paper inside — documents, bundles of them, wrapped in oilcloth. Someone swapped the treasury shipment before the ship ever sailed."

Odile closed her eyes for a moment, and when she opened them there was something in them Cyrus had never seen there before: relief. Raw and unguarded, like a wound finally allowed to bleed.

"Aldous," she said. "He figured it out. He figured out what Vane had done — that the shipment was a fiction, that the three of them had sunk an empty ship to cover the theft of funds that never existed, that the whole fortune was a ledger entry and a lie. So he put the evidence aboard. He hid the proof inside the thing itself." She laughed, short and bitter. "He sank the truth with the lie. And then he died in this river, and Vane has spent forty years paying salvage crews to look for treasure that isn't there — not because he wants it, but because he needs to be the one who finds whatever *is* there first, and burns it."

"So the fleet—"

"Isn't chasing gold." Odile looked south, toward the narrows, where the water ran fast and the salvage boats would be coming through on this tide. "They're chasing paper. And we're holding it."

Tamsin had come up beside them unnoticed, the way old people who grew up on a riverbank can. She held out her hand, palm up, and Cyrus hesitated only a moment before putting the oilcloth bundle into it.

Tamsin unwrapped it with fingers that shook slightly, and did not pretend otherwise. Inside were dozens of pages — letters, bank drafts, a deposition begun and abandoned, and at the bottom, a single sheet in a careful clerk's hand, headed with the words *Inquest into the death of Elias Merrick*, followed by testimony that named names, dates, and sums, and ended with a signature that had been witnessed by a justice of the peace who had himself been dead for thirty years.

Tamsin read it standing in the wind, and when she finished, she folded it along its old creases with great care, the way one folds a letter from the dead.

"This is my father's handwriting," she said. "He wrote this the week before he drowned. They told us it was drink. He never touched a drop." She looked up, and her eyes were perfectly clear. "Well. The river kept it safe all this time. It seems only right the river's daughter gets to carry it ashore."

For a moment none of them spoke. The Meridian Star lay flooded and gutted beneath them, her secret finally out, and the current pulled steadily southward, toward the narrows, toward the law that had been bought, toward the man who had spent four decades and any number of lives keeping this day from arriving.

Then Odile straightened, and the relief in her face hardened into something more useful.

"We need to move," she said. "The tide turns in two hours, and Vane's lead boat draws less water than ours. He'll run the narrows on the turn, same as we did." She was already gathering the lines. "We take the bundle to the mainland, to the federal assessor at Cape Reckoning — not the county court, the *federal* man, the one appointment Vane couldn't reach—"

Which was when Tamsin, who had been facing south, said quietly:

"Too late. Look."

Out past the point, where the river widened and glittered, three masts had come round the headland in tight formation, running easy, unhurried, absolutely certain of themselves. At the foremast of the lead boat flew a flag Cyrus recognized from the salvage company's stationery: a black star on a field of white.

The Meridian Star's own house flag. Flown as a joke, or a claim, or both.

"They're not running the narrows," Tamsin said. "They already have. And they're coming straight for us."

Odile put the wheel over. The old launch answered sluggishly, heavy with pump gear and diving equipment and forty years of consequence.

"Cyrus," she said, in the tone she'd used thirty years ago in a lecture hall, the tone that meant *pay attention, this will be on the examination*. "Everything we just pulled out of that hold is worth more than every boat in that fleet combined. Which means in about ten minutes, those men are going to try to board us."

Cyrus looked at the approaching masts, then at the flooded wreck settling behind them, then at the bundle in Tamsin's arms, and began, grimly, to count.
