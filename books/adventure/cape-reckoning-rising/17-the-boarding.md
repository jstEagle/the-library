## 17. The Boarding

The fog came off the river at dusk like a curtain being drawn by someone with no interest in what happened next.

Cyrus crouched in the reeds at the edge of Corvan's old landing, watching the *Iron Meridian* swing at anchor a hundred yards out, her running lights doubled and tripled in the black water. Behind her, dimmer, rode two barges and a tug — the rest of Halloran's fleet, gathered now like wolves that had finally cornered something worth eating.

"She's got a watch posted on the stern rail," Tamsin murmured beside her. The old woman's eyes were better than Cyrus's, which was saying something, since Tamsin claimed to be seventy-one and had once shot a heron at eighty paces out of pure spite. "And another one forward. But nobody's watching the water side of the cargo deck. They think the river's theirs."

"The river's nobody's," Cyrus said. "That's rather the point."

Tamsin snorted. "You've been spending too much time around Odile Marsh. You're starting to talk like a sermon."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Don't. She'd be insufferable about it." Tamsin adjusted the oilskin wrapped around her shoulders and nodded toward a shape pulled up under the willows: Odile's skiff, the one they'd found hidden at Spur Nine, patched with tar and prayer. "That boat leaks like a gossip. You know that."

"It leaked *slowly*," Cyrus said. "There's a difference. It's a matter of degree."

"It's a matter of whether you drown."

"Which is why you're going to bail while I row."

Tamsin gave her a long look, the kind that had probably made grown men confess to crimes they hadn't committed. Then she grinned, gap-toothed and unrepentant, and hauled herself up. "Fine. But if we die, I want it noted that I told you so, and I want it noted *loudly*."

* * *

The crossing took eleven minutes and cost Cyrus approximately four years of her life.

The skiff sat low in the water from the first stroke, the tar patch weeping at its edges, and Tamsin bailed with a dented kettle in a rhythm that could only be described as musical if the music were a warning. Fog closed around them until the *Iron Meridian* was nothing but three smears of lantern light floating in gray wool.

"You know," Tamsin whispered between scoops, "Halloran's men shoot first and ask questions never. I've seen them do it. Down at Kestrel Bend, before the flood took the town proper, there was a fellow named Dree who owed them money—"

"Tamsin."

"—and they didn't even ask him to pay. They just—"

"*Tamsin.*"

"What?"

"You're telling me this *now*, in a leaking boat, in the fog, while we approach the ship of the man you're describing."

Tamsin considered this. "Seemed like relevant context."

The hull touched steel with a sound like a coin dropped in a church. Cyrus froze, one hand on the gunwale, breath held. Above them, the watchman on the stern rail shifted his weight, coughed, and resumed the particular stillness of a man being paid to stand somewhere he'd rather not be.

They'd come up on the port side, beneath the shadow of the paddle wheel housing, where a ladder of iron rungs ran from the waterline to the cargo deck. Cyrus went first, testing each rung, listening to the river lap against the plates below. Halfway up, her boot slipped on wet iron and for one lurching second she hung by her fingers over forty feet of black water, and she thought — with a clarity that would have been funny in any other circumstance — *Odile is going to kill me for dying.*

Then her boot found the rung again, and she climbed.

* * *

The cargo deck smelled of coal smoke, bilge, and money. Not literally — though Cyrus supposed money had a smell, ink and metal and other people's sweat — but the crates stacked along the starboard rail had an air about them, stenciled with the Halloran Salvage & Recovery crest, packed tight as teeth. Somewhere below decks, behind a sealed door and a paid crew, sat the strongroom where the Meridian Star's cargo had been carried off the wreck these past two days. Halloran hadn't even pretended otherwise anymore. He had the treasury shipment, or most of it, and come morning he meant to run it downriver past every magistrate he owned.

Which was all of them.

"Left," Tamsin breathed at her shoulder. "Odile said the chart room. Third door past the wheelhouse."

"How do you know she said that? You were asleep when we planned this."

"I sleep lightly, girl. And I listen. It's how I survived thirty years married to a man who talked in his dreams."

The deck was slick with mist. They moved crate to crate, shadow to shadow, Cyrus counting doors — one, two — and there was the third, a green-painted door with a brass handle worn bright. Locked, of course. Everything on this boat was locked; locks were how Halloran expressed affection.

But Cyrus Nystrom had spent twenty years opening things that were supposed to stay shut. She drew her picks from the lining of her coat — the same set she'd used on abandoned signal boxes and forgotten depots all across the dead spur lines — and worked the lock with fingers that only shook a little.

It gave with a click that sounded, in the fog-hush, like a gunshot.

Both of them stood frozen. On the far rail, the watchman turned, squinted into the murk, saw nothing but fog seeing fog, and turned back.

"Charming place," Tamsin whispered as they slipped inside.

* * *

The chart room was small and close, lit by a single shaded lamp someone had left burning. Charts lay rolled in a rack; a desk held a decanter, a ledger, and a photograph of a young man in a fine coat standing on the deck of a much finer boat.

Cyrus knew the face. She'd seen it older, harder, and twice as confident, standing on the dock at Reckoning Harbor buying the law by the pound.

"That's him," Tamsin said quietly. "Emmett Halloran. Before." She tapped the photo's edge without touching it. "That's the *Meridian Star* behind him. His own partners' boat. He stood on that deck and smiled like that the week before she burned."

Cyrus opened the ledger.

It wasn't hidden. That was the thing that made her stomach turn — it wasn't hidden at all. Halloran kept his accounts in plain sight because he genuinely believed no one would ever be in a position to read them. Payments to magistrates, itemized by name. Payments to the harbor commission. A monthly figure marked simply *R.M.* — Reverend Mott, who preached Sundays about the cleansing power of honest work. And going back years, decades, entries in a different hand, older ink: sums paid to three names Cyrus recognized from Odile's research. Verrane. Osk. Delacroix-Muir.

The partners. Paid steadily, year after year — right up until the month each of their entries simply stopped.

"He bought them off one at a time," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat. "And when they stopped taking the money—"

"They stopped taking anything at all," Tamsin finished. She was looking at the photograph with an expression Cyrus couldn't read. "My father worked the Star's route, you know. Before. He always said the three owners fought like cats. Said it was a wonder the boat stayed afloat with all that shouting below decks." She paused. "He never wondered why the shouting stopped."

Footsteps in the passage. Heavy, unhurried, coming closer.

Cyrus snapped the ledger shut. There was nowhere to go — the room had one door and one salt-crusted porthole too narrow for either of them. She looked at Tamsin. Tamsin looked back with the calm of a woman who had already decided how she intended to die and found the prospect merely inconvenient.

The footsteps passed. Continued on. Faded.

Cyrus let out a breath. "We need to move. Now. Before—"

The door opened.

* * *

The man in the doorway was big in the way furniture is big, with a lantern in one hand and a shotgun broken open over his arm like a gentleman's cane. He looked at Cyrus. He looked at Tamsin. He looked at the ledger in Cyrus's hands.

"Well," he said. "Mr. Halloran said if anyone came poking, I should bring 'em down personal. He sounded real hopeful about it."

"Hopeful," Tamsin repeated.

"Eager," the man amended. "Move."

They moved. Down the passage, past the galley where a cook slept sitting up with a spoon in his hand, down a stair that groaned underfoot, to a cabin door at the stern from beneath which spilled lamplight and cigar smoke and the sound of a man humming to himself, tunelessly, contentedly.

The guard knocked once. "Company, Mr. Halloran."

The humming stopped. "Bring them in, Brack. And Brack — mind the rug. It's Persian."

* * *

The cabin was absurdly fine for a salvage tug — mahogany paneling, a real carpet, a decanter of something amber, and Emmett Halloran seated behind a desk with his boots up, reading a novel. He set the book down — Cyrus caught the title, some romance — and regarded his visitors with the mild interest of a man observing weather.

"Cyrus Nystrom," he said. "The map woman. And—" his eyes flicked to Tamsin, and for just a moment the mildness thinned, showing something colder underneath, "—the Corvan woman. Well. The river does deliver."

"You have my mentor's expedition journals," Cyrus said. "Taken from the camp at Spur Nine. And you have cargo in your hold that belongs to the territory, not to you."

"Belongs to whoever can hold it," Halloran said pleasantly. "That's been the law of this river since before either of us was born. You of all people should appreciate that, Miss Nystrom. You make your living off abandoned things. What's the difference between you and me, really? I just have a bigger boat."

"The difference," Cyrus said, "is that I keep maps of what's lost. You make things lost."

Something flickered across his face — not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own eulogy delivered early.

"Brack," he said, without raising his voice, "the lady's tired. Put her and her grandmother somewhere comfortable until morning. After the fifth tide, we won't need to worry about guests."

"And the ledger?" Brack asked, nodding at Cyrus's hands.

Halloran smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practiced on magistrates for thirty years. "Let her keep it. Paper burns, Miss Nystrom. People forget. Rivers especially." He picked his book back up. "Fifth tide's at dawn. Do try to rest."

* * *

The "somewhere comfortable" was a storage locker aft, dry at least, smelling of rope and tar. Brack locked the door with a ceremony that suggested he'd done this before and expected to do it again.

Through the bulkhead, muffled but distinct, came the sounds of the ship settling for night: the engine room ticking as it cooled, men laughing in the mess, and beneath it all, faint and rhythmic, the slap of water against the hull.

Cyrus sat with her back against the wall and the ledger open on her knees, memorizing pages by the light seeping under the door. Names. Dates. Amounts. If the paper burned, the paper burned; she'd carry it in her head, the way she carried every line of every dead spur line, every grade and gauge and forgotten junction.

Tamsin lowered herself onto a coil of hemp with a sigh that took a full minute. "Well. This is cozy."

"We're in a locked room on the ship of a murderer, twelve hours before he sails his stolen fortune past every court he owns."

"Cozy," Tamsin confirmed. She was quiet a moment. Then: "You memorizing that book?"

"Every page."

"Good girl." She leaned her head back against the bulkhead. "Because I've been thinking. There's a grate in the floor of this locker. Saw it when Brack shoved us in. Runs down to the bilge walk. And the bilge walk runs the length of the ship." She opened one eye. "Your mentor taught you anything about boats, Miss Nystrom?"

Cyrus pressed her palm flat against the deck planking and felt, beneath it, the cold breathing of the river.

"She taught me," Cyrus said, "that everything floats, given the right hole."

Above them, through the deck, through the fog, the river rose toward the fifth tide — and somewhere out in the dark, on the drowned flats below Kestrel Bend, a light answered the *Iron Meridian*'s lanterns. One flash. Two. Three.

Odile was still out there.

And she was signaling *them*.
