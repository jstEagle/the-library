## 18. Fifth Tide

The tide came in like a debt collector.

Cyrus felt it before she saw it — a change in the way the water moved around the *Meridian Star*'s hull, a slow muscular shift, as if the river had rolled over in its sleep and decided, on reflection, to drown something. She stood on the wreck's tilted deck with Tamsin Corvan's testimony folded against her heart in an oilskin packet, and she watched the water climb the pilings with the patience of a thing that had been counting for forty years.

"Five," said Odile Marsh beside her.

"I can count, Odile."

"You were counting wrong. You had your fingers bunched." Her mentor held up her own hand, fingers spread, five of them, splayed against the grey morning light like a fan of bad cards. "Five tides. One left after this. I told you we'd make it."

"You told me we'd *probably* make it. There's a difference, and I'd like it noted for posterity."

"Noted," Odile said gravely. "Posterity has been informed."

They were, both of them, soaked to the sternum, salt-crusted, half-starved, and grinning like idiots. Cyrus supposed that was what four days on a dead spur line and two nights in a flooded town did to a person. It either broke you or it made you find things funny that weren't, and she had long ago decided which survival strategy suited her better.

Below them, in the drowned hold they'd spent the fourth tide emptying, the last of the evidence sat sealed in a salvaged strongbox: ledgers, letters, and a confession written in a dead man's hand — Marcus Vail's own partner, Elias Thorn, who had not drowned in the flood of Kestrel Bend so much as he had been *helped* along by it, by a man who now owned three salvage barges and most of the territorial court.

And above them, coming downriver with the tide, came those same three barges.

* * *

"They're early," Cyrus said, glass raised to the bend at Narrows. Three shapes, black against the pewter water, riding low and heavy with men and machinery. The lead one flew no flag, which was its own kind of flag. "Vail doesn't do anything early. He does everything exactly when it profits him."

"He's not early." Odile took the glass, studied the barges with the unhurried attention she gave to track gauges and to people she intended to ruin. "He's desperate. There's a difference, and I'd like it noted."

"Noted," said Cyrus, and found herself smiling despite everything.

The plan was simple, which meant it would probably kill them. They could not outrun the fleet — the *Star* hadn't moved under her own power in forty years and wasn't about to start out of sentiment. They could not outfight it — Odile was sixty-one years old and armed chiefly with conviction, and Cyrus's revolver had been underwater twice and fired once since, unconvincingly. What they could do was be somewhere else when the barges arrived, and take the truth with them, and let the river keep the money.

The river, as it turned out, had opinions about this.

"The skiff's gone," Tamsin announced from the rail, in the tone of a woman reporting weather. She was seventy-eight years old and had lived through the drowning of her town, the burning of her family's ferry house, and forty years of being underestimated by every man on the Cape, and she delivered bad news the way other people delivered bread: fresh, daily, without apology.

"Gone?" Cyrus crossed to her. "Gone how? It was tied to the davit."

"It was tied to the davit," Tamsin agreed. "Now it is tied to nothing, and it is approximately there." She pointed downstream, where a small dark shape bobbed with great dignity through the current, making excellent time toward the sea and none whatsoever back toward them.

Cyrus stared at it. "I tied that knot."

"You did."

"I've tied that knot ten thousand times."

"And yet." Tamsin patted her arm kindly. "The river takes what the river wants, dear. It always has. That's rather the whole theme of this town."

Odile made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Cyrus shot her a look that promised a full accounting later.

"So we have no boat," Cyrus said. "One tide left. Three barges of hired muscle inbound. And the only honest witness in the territory standing on a rotting deck eating the last of our biscuits."

"I'm an honest witness *and* I'm eating," Tamsin corrected. "Multitasking. It's how I survived."

* * *

They retreated below, into the hold that smelled of old iron and older secrets, where the strongbox sat amid the dripping ribs of the *Meridian Star*. Cyrus set a lamp against the bulkhead and did what she did best, which was look at lines — not rails this time, but water, current, the drowned streets of Kestrel Bend visible through the shattered hull as green-gold shadows.

"There," she said finally. "The old ferry channel."

Tamsin looked up sharply. "That channel's been silted since before you were born."

"Silted, not gone. Water remembers its roads. So do I." Cyrus traced it with her finger against the bulkhead, the ghost of a route. "Your family ran the ferry through there for sixty years, Mrs. Corvan. Before the flood. Before Vail bought the right-of-way and closed it. If the channel holds even knee-deep, we can walk it — walk out behind the Narrows, come up on the far bank past the fleet entirely."

"Wade through a drowned town," Odile said, "at fifth tide, with the water rising."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"I say everything like that. It's why my survey reports are so widely admired."

Tamsin had gone quiet, which for Tamsin was remarkable enough that both women turned to look at her. She was staring at the bulkhead where Cyrus had drawn the line, and her eyes were wet, and she was smiling.

"That's the road my father took the morning of the flood," she said. "Last run of the Corvan Ferry. He carried forty-one people out through that channel while the water came over the levee. He went back for the forty-second." She paused. "There wasn't a forty-second. But he went back anyway, because that's what you do with a road like that. You don't let it close."

She stood, joints cracking like green firewood, and gathered her stick.

"Well then," she said. "Let's go open it."

* * *

The channel was worse than knee-deep and better than Cyrus feared, which averaged out to chest-deep and miserable. They went single file, Tamsin first because she refused to be anywhere but first, her stick probing the bottom like a blind man reading braille. The drowned town rose around them — doorframes, a church spire leaning like a drunk against the current, fence posts with their pickets combed flat by four decades of flood. Fish scattered from their legs in silver flashes. Somewhere above, muffled by water and distance, the barges ground against the *Meridian Star*, and voices shouted, and someone found the emptied hold, and someone began to swear with real commitment.

"They've found the box is gone," Odile murmured, treading water beside Cyrus.

"Mm."

"They'll tear the town apart looking for us."

"They'll tear the town apart looking for a box. We're carrying something else." Cyrus touched the oilskin packet. "A piece of paper. Best cargo on the Cape, and it weighs nothing."

"That," said Odile, "is the first genuinely intelligent thing anyone's said all day, and I'm including myself, which costs me."

The channel bent north, following the old ferry road, and here the water shallowed at last — waist, then hip, then knee — until they waded up a street that had been called Harbor Row when it had a harbor, and came out dripping onto a shingle bank on the far side of the Narrows just as the sun broke through, briefly, gilding the whole ruined valley like a coin at the bottom of a well.

Tamsin stood on the bank and looked back at the water covering her town, and for a moment the humor went out of her face entirely, and Cyrus saw what forty years alone in a drowned place actually looked like.

"My father used to say the river keeps what matters," Tamsin said quietly. "I thought he meant the houses. The things." She shook her head. "He meant the truth. Rivers don't lie, dear. People float; the truth sinks. And sunk things stay put."

"Until someone comes to raise them," Odile said.

"Until someone stubborn enough comes along with a map." Tamsin looked at Cyrus, and her eyes were sharp again, and dry. "You burned the rest of it yet?"

"Not yet."

"Burn it tonight. After. Keep only the line home." She started up the bank, stick striking stone. "A map that shows everything shows nothing. A map that shows one true thing — that's worth carrying."

* * *

They made the high ground above the Narrows by late afternoon, and from there Cyrus could see the whole board laid out: the three barges swarming the wreck like flies on fruit, the salvage crews' lanterns already lit against the failing light, and beyond them, far downriver, the smoke of Cape Reckoning itself — the courts, the bought law, the whole soft rotten apparatus that Vail owned.

Except.

Except that at the Narrows landing, small and official-looking, a government steam launch lay at anchor, flying the territorial survey pennant. And on its deck, arguing with the pilot with the expansive gestures of a man whose authority had been purchased and who was discovering the purchase didn't cover this, stood a figure Cyrus recognized even at this distance.

Marcus Vail.

He had come himself. He had left his offices, his lawyers, his comfortable distance — because the box was empty and the paper was missing and for the first time in forty years the man was afraid.

"Oh," said Odile softly, beside her, with a satisfaction so profound it was nearly religious. "Oh, he came. He *came*. Do you know what that means?"

"It means he's stupid."

"It means he's guilty. Guilty men come. Innocent men send telegrams." Odile's hand found Cyrus's shoulder, gripped it. "Forty years I have wanted him within the reach of my voice, Cyrus Nystrom. Forty years. And tomorrow, at sixth tide, we walk into Cape Reckoning with a living witness, a signed confession, and a ledger, and he will be standing on a public dock in front of a government launch, and I am going to use my voice."

"Tactfully, I hope."

"Cyrus. Be serious."

"I'm always serious. That's my problem." But Cyrus was watching Vail too, and watching the launch, and doing arithmetic. One launch. One pilot. Possibly a clerk or two. And the fleet's boats all committed to the wreck, a mile upstream, tearing apart a dead ship for a fortune that was currently feeding the sturgeon.

"If we could take that launch," she said slowly, "we wouldn't have to walk to Cape Reckoning. We'd arrive at sixth tide with the tide itself — fast, dry, and flying a government pennant Vail can't shoot at without confessing everything."

Odile turned to look at her. Tamsin turned to look at her. For a moment nobody spoke, and the river spoke for all of them, sliding past below, patient and honest and rising.

"You want to steal a government boat," Tamsin said at last, "from a murderer, using a route your father's ferry opened, on the last tide." She smiled, wide and bright and terrible. "Dear child, you are your father's daughter after all, whoever he was."

"That's not — how would you even know that—"

"Later," said Odile, already moving down the slope, coat streaming, sixty-one years old and moving like a woman of thirty with a grievance. "Questions later. Stealing now. Try to keep up, both of you — and Cyrus?"

"Yes?"

"Do try to tie the launch off properly."

* * *

Below them, at the Narrows landing, the last light caught the launch's brass rail, and Marcus Vail turned his back on the river to argue with his pilot, and never saw the three figures coming down through the alders — an engineer, a surveyor, and the last survivor of Kestrel Bend, arriving with the fifth tide to collect a debt forty years overdue.
