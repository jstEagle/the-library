## 19. The Testimony

The rain had stopped by the time they reached the chapel, which was a small mercy Cyrus intended to be grateful for, since the universe had been notably stingy with mercies all week.

Tamsin Corvan walked between them — Odile on her left, Cyrus on her right — with the unhurried gait of a woman who had spent sixty years being the only person in Kestrel Bend who knew where everything was buried. Literally, in some cases. She had refused Cyrus's arm twice and Odile's once, and had accepted a third offer from neither of them, which told Cyrus everything about the hierarchy of trust in this particular expedition.

"You'll want to sit," Tamsin said, pushing open the chapel door with her shoulder. "Testimony's like stew. It wants time, and it wants you comfortable enough not to rush it."

The chapel had flooded to the height of three stairs and then receded, decades ago, and never quite recovered. Salt bloom climbed the walls in pale continents. The pews nearest the door were gone entirely — taken for firewood, probably, in the hungry years — but the ones at the back remained, warped into gentle curves like they'd been praying sideways ever since. Light came through a window of colored glass that had lost most of its colors, so that what fell across the floor was less a rose window than a bruise.

Tamsin settled onto the end pew with a sigh of genuine pleasure, as though it were a rocking chair she'd been meaning to get back to.

"Right," she said. "Ask me your questions."

Cyrus had prepared questions. She'd prepared them on the walk down from Spur Nine, in the boat, through the flooded streets — a whole architecture of them, load-bearing questions and decorative ones, arranged in the order a surveyor arranges anything: what holds up the rest. But standing here now, watching the old woman fold her hands in her lap like a schoolgirl called before the magistrate, every question felt suddenly too small, or too large, or both.

So instead she said: "Why did you stay?"

Tamsin smiled. It rearranged her whole face, which was mostly weather and stubbornness, into something young.

"Everyone asks that eventually," she said. "The salvage men asked it. The county men asked it. My sister asked it for forty years by letter until she died, and I expect she's asking it still, wherever letters go after." She looked around the chapel, at the salt continents, the bruised light. "You don't leave a place you're the witness to. Somebody has to remember it properly, or the river tells it wrong."

"The river tells it wrong," Odile repeated quietly. She had taken up a position near the window, arms crossed, in the exact posture she'd used over Cyrus's drafting table twenty years ago when a bridge calculation didn't please her. "That's why we came, Tamsin. That's why I went silent. Because the river's been telling this story wrong for thirty years, and the only person who can correct it is sitting in a pew."

"Flatterer," said Tamsin, pleased.

* * *

It took an hour to get to the night of the Meridian Star, because Tamsin Corvan believed — correctly, Cyrus would later admit — that a story without its ground was just gossip. So they started with the town: Kestrel Bend in its prime, four hundred souls, a cannery, a chandlery, a church with all its windows intact. They started with the treasury shipment itself, which had come upriver on the Meridian Star under guard of six men and one very nervous paymaster, bound for the territorial bank before the war closed the lines.

"They put it in the sealed hold," Tamsin said. "Bottom of the ship, behind a bulkhead they welded shut at the foundry. Not locked — welded. You could hear the coins if you pressed your ear to the hull. Like rain on a tin roof, but slower. Richer." Her eyes had gone somewhere fifty years away. "Half the town came down to listen to it. We called it the money-rain. Children used to dare each other to swim out and put their ear against the starboard side."

"And the partners," Cyrus said gently. "Harrow Vane and the others. Tell me about them."

"Ah." Tamsin's hands stilled. "Now we're at it."

Aldous Crane — the name sat in the room like ballast. The owner of the salvage fleet now anchored downriver, the man whose dredges had been circling the wreck for a decade, the man whose lawyers had bought the county courthouse so thoroughly that the judge reportedly signed documents with a Crane Company pen.

"There were four of them," Tamsin said. "Crane, Vane, the Ostrander brothers. They owned the shipping concern together — the Meridian Star was theirs, jointly, every plank of her. And they were friends, or near enough to friends that nobody thought to count the spoons." She paused. "I counted the spoons. Habit of mine. I was the harbormaster's clerk, you understand. Ledgers were my whole life. Money passing through my little window, all day, every day. You learn what honest numbers sound like."

"What did dishonest ones sound like?" Odile asked.

"Quiet," said Tamsin. "Dishonest numbers are always quiet. Honest money makes noise — coins, arguments, receipts, complaints. Dishonest money slips through like water through a cracked bell." She looked up. "For two years before the wreck, money was slipping through the company accounts like the whole ledger was a sieve. And every crack pointed toward Crane's private account, and every inquiry he filed himself, and every auditor he hired came back saying *all in order*, and Aldous Crane smiling his churchwarden smile the entire time."

* * *

The night of the wreck, according to Tamsin, had been wrong from the start.

"Storm season was over. Everyone knew it. But Crane chartered the run anyway — insisted the shipment go upriver that week, ahead of schedule, against the paymaster's objection and the pilot's objection and plain common sense. And he booked himself aboard, which owners never did. Owners send managers. Managers send clerks. Clerks send apologies." She ticked these off on her fingers. "But Crane came. And he brought his own pilot. A man named Ferro, off the coastal boats, who'd never run the Bend narrows in his life and said so loudly at the dock, in front of eleven witnesses, of whom I was one."

Cyrus felt the shape of it assembling, the way she felt the shape of a line assembling on a map — the moment the dots stop being dots and become a route.

"The ship went onto the shoal at full steam," Tamsin said. "In calm water. In a channel Ferro had been warned about in writing. Fourteen drowned, including Harrow Vane and both Ostranders — the three partners who'd been asking about the missing money. The cargo hold, welded shut, went down intact. And Aldous Crane swam ashore, dried himself off, and spent the next thirty years filing salvage claims on his own murdered ship."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the river made its endless argument with the shore.

"Why did no one say?" Cyrus asked. "Eleven witnesses—"

"Because he was rich and we were a dying town, and grief makes people stupid with obedience. The inquest ruled pilot error. Ferro testified that the charts were wrong." Tamsin's mouth twisted. "And the charts *were* wrong — Crane had paid to have them redrawn, did you know that? Bought the survey office out from under the county. Your own map, Miss Nystrom — the old spur surveys, the channel marks — half of what's printed comes from that bought survey. The lie got into the paper before the truth got out of the water."

Odile moved from the window for the first time. "Which is why I need it from you, Tamsin. On record. Names, dates, the dock statement, the chart purchase. I have the ledgers — Crane's real ledgers, the ones his bookkeeper smuggled out before he died. But ledgers can be called forged. A living witness cannot."

"A living witness," Tamsin said dryly, "can be drowned."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "That's the risk. It's why I sent no word. Every letter Crane could intercept was a map to you."

"I know why you went quiet, girl. I've known since your first runner came upriver two years ago asking careful questions." Tamsin reached over and patted Odile's hand, once, briskly, the way you'd pat a horse that was trying too hard. "You think I've been sitting in this chapel sixty years doing needlework? I've been waiting for someone to come collect what I kept."

She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and led them to the vestry.

* * *

What she kept was behind the altar, under a floor stone, in an oilcloth packet the size of a hymnal: the original dock manifest in the paymaster's hand; Ferro's written objection to the charter, signed and dated three days before the wreck; a sworn statement from the dockhand who'd seen Crane's man alter the channel markers the week prior; and — Tamsin saved it last, unfolding it with the reverence other people reserved for love letters — a page torn from the company ledger in Harrow Vane's own writing, listing the diversions from the joint account, initialed by Crane.

"He wrote this the morning of the sailing," Tamsin said. "Brought it to me at the harbor window. Said, 'If I don't come back, see this gets to a court that isn't bought.' Then he shook my hand like we were closing a trade, and went aboard, and that was the last time anyone in Kestrel Bend saw Harrow Vane alive." She held the page out to Odile. "Sixty years is long enough to keep a promise. Take it to whatever court is left that isn't his."

Odile took it with both hands, the way you take something breakable, though paper doesn't break. Cyrus watched her mentor — the woman who had taught her that a line on a map was a promise too — and saw something in her face that twenty years of estrangement had never shown her: relief. The specific, exhausted relief of a debt finally payable.

"It won't be a court," Odile said. "Not yet. He owns those. It'll be the territory papers, and the federal assessor, and every shareholder he's lied to for thirty years. It'll be loud, and public, and worse for him than any courtroom."

"Good," said Tamsin. "Loud suits me. I've been quiet sixty years; I've earned some noise."

* * *

They were sealing the packet into oilskin again when Cyrus heard it — faint, through the chapel wall, carried flat across the water.

An engine. Not the putter of a fisherman's launch. The deep, patient throb of a salvage tug working against the current.

She went to the broken window. Downriver, past the drowned rooftops, past the skeletal crane of the old cannery, the lights of the fleet had come alive — and they weren't at anchor anymore. Two tugs were moving, slow and deliberate, converging on the channel above the wreck site. Between them, riding low, sat a barge with a derrick, and on the derrick hung something long and dark that caught the moon.

A grab bucket. Big enough to tear a hull open.

"They're not waiting for the tide," Cyrus said. "Odile. The fifth tide's tomorrow dawn. The wreck will be shallow enough to reach from the surface — and deep enough that anything they pull up gets torn apart on the way. He's going to strip the hold tonight and let the evidence go down with the scrap."

Odile was already moving, the ledger page vanishing into her coat. "Then we testify faster than he digs."

"No," Tamsin said.

They both turned. The old woman had not risen. She sat in her crooked pew with her folded hands, and her voice, when it came, was the voice of the harbormaster's clerk, the keeper of honest numbers, the last witness of Kestrel Bend.

"You'll do nothing of the sort. You'll take my testimony *now*, every word, witnessed and written, because paper drowns and memory doesn't. And *then*—" she nodded toward the window, toward the distant lights, and her eyes were bright as coin, "—you'll take me down to that wreck myself. Sixty years I've listened to the money-rain through the hull. Time somebody finally opened the lid while I'm standing there to say whose it is, and how it got there, and what it cost."

Cyrus looked at Odile. Odile looked at Tamsin. Outside, the derrick lights swung, and the river waited, keeping its one honest account.

"Tide turns in four hours," Cyrus said. "We'd better write quickly."
