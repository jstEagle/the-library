## 20. Sixth Tide

The river gave them one day of grace, and it spent that day being beautiful about it.

Cyrus woke on the deck of the *Kestrel's Own* with gulls arguing overhead and the light coming up gold through a haze of river mist, and for a full minute she forgot that anything was wrong. Then she remembered everything, all at once, the way grief and relief like to arrive together holding hands, and she lay there on her bedroll listening to Tamsin Corvan snore like a man who had finally, after sixty years, earned it.

He'd testified. That was the miracle Cyrus kept turning over in her mind like a stone she couldn't stop picking up. Old Tamsin, who hadn't spoken more than forty words to a living soul since the flood took Kestrel Bend, had stood up in the lantern light of Odile's camp and told the whole of it: Ambrose Vell and his partners, the night meeting aboard the *Meridian Star*, the argument over the treasury shipment, the pistol, the rail, the body weighted and dropped between the pilings while the town slept. And the second partner, too, two years later, when he'd grown a conscience and started writing letters. Tamsin had seen both. Tamsin had kept quiet because Vell owned the law and the law owned the town, and then the town drowned and there was nobody left to tell.

Until now. Until a stubborn surveyor with a stolen ledger and an engineer with a map nobody else could read had come upriver and asked him nicely.

Cyrus sat up. Her ribs ached from the boarding fight at the Narrows. Her hands were still raw from the winch line. She felt, all things considered, wonderful.

"You're smiling," said Odile Marsh, from the stern, where she sat wrapped in a blanket with a tin cup of coffee she had clearly been guarding since before dawn. "Stop it. It's unsettling."

"I'm allowed to smile. We won."

"We haven't won anything yet." Odile blew on her coffee. "We have testimony. Testimony requires a court, and every court within four hundred miles has Vell's money in its drawers. What we have is a story, and stories are only as strong as the people willing to repeat them."

"Then we repeat it loudly."

"Mm." Odile looked out at the water. The mist was burning off the channel, and downriver, faint as a smudge, you could see the masts of the salvage fleet where they'd anchored after the boarding went against them — six hulls, sullen, waiting. "Vell's still got six ships and no conscience. I'd hold the smiling until the tide turns."

The tide turned at noon. That was the whole problem.

* * *

They spent the morning preparing, which mostly meant arguing.

"The cargo comes out," Cyrus said, for the fourth time. "One run. The *Kestrel's Own* can carry it if we load light. We get the treasury shipment up, we hand it to the territorial magistrate at Graywater, publicly, with witnesses—"

"And Vell buys the magistrate by supper," Odile said, "and we've done his salvage work for him. No. The plan stands. The cargo goes back to the river."

"It's a fortune, Odile. It could rebuild Kestrel Bend. It could buy Tamsin a house with a roof that doesn't leak sideways."

Tamsin, who was mending a net with the patience of a man who had been mending nets since before either of them was born, spoke without looking up. "I don't want a house." A pause, the needle working. "House would just be somewhere new to watch the water come in."

Cyrus closed her mouth. There were arguments you lost before you opened it.

The plan, such as it was, had been Odile's from the start, and Cyrus had hated it from the start, and had agreed to it anyway around the third tide because Odile had that way of laying out a mad scheme so calmly that objecting felt like shouting in church. The evidence — the ledger, Tamsin's testimony, the letters the dead partner had written that Tamsin had fished out of a drowned post office years ago — would go downriver to Graywater with the ferryman, Brann, whose boat was slow and ugly and blessedly beneath anyone's notice. The cargo itself, raised last night from the flooded hold of the *Meridian Star* in crates still sealed with treasury wax, would be loaded onto the open deck of the *Kestrel's Own* and taken to the middle of the widest reach of the Cape Reckoning, where the channel ran sixty feet deep and nothing salvaged would ever be salvaged again.

And there they would wait for Vell, and let him watch.

"He'll board us," Cyrus said now. "He'll try. Six ships, Odile. We have one flat-bottomed scow and a crew of three and a half." She nodded at the half, which was a river otter named Admiral who had adopted the expedition at Spur Nine and contributed chiefly by stealing gloves.

"He'll try," Odile agreed. "But he'll do it slowly, and carefully, because the moment he puts men on this deck in front of witnesses, in front of the magistrate's own courier—" she tipped her cup toward Brann, who was pretending not to listen and failing beautifully, "—he confirms everything Tamsin said. Vell has spent fifty years buying silence. He can't shoot his way out of a story. He has to negotiate his way out, and a man negotiating is a man standing still."

"And if he doesn't negotiate?"

Odile smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile. "Then we sink the fortune in front of him, and I will spend the rest of my life enjoying what that does to his face."

* * *

They loaded the crates at midday, and it was stranger work than Cyrus had expected. Treasury gold should have felt heavy with triumph. Instead each crate came up the ramp and across the deck with a kind of funereal slowness, and nobody joked, and even the Admiral kept his thieving paws to himself, sensing — as animals do — that the humans were handling something that wasn't really theirs.

Twelve crates. Enough money to buy the territory, the premise had gone. Enough to buy the whole territory, and everyone in it.

"Funny," Tamsin said, watching the last crate settle. "That much gold sitting right there, and it feels like a coffin."

"That's because it is one," Odile said. "It's been Vell's coffin for fifty years. He just hasn't lain down in it yet."

They cast off at half past one. The sixth tide was due at four, a big one, spring-fed, the kind the old pilots called a *grandfather* — the river swelling up from the sea to take back whatever the land had borrowed. Odile had chosen the hour deliberately. Everything, today, would happen on the river's schedule, not theirs and not Vell's. Let him chase a deadline set by the moon.

The *Kestrel's Own* puttered down the channel with Brann's uglier boat trailing behind, and the salvage fleet came alive ahead of them like a nest of hornets poked with a stick.

* * *

Ambrose Vell came out to meet them in a launch flying his company colors, and he was not, Cyrus noted, an old man. That was the thing that unsettled her most. In her head, the villain of a fifty-year-old crime ought to be a ruin, a ghost, something the decades had already begun to collect on. But Vell was perhaps seventy, straight-backed, silver-haired, with the tanned competence of a man who had spent his life outdoors giving orders, and he looked less like a murderer than like somebody's favorite uncle.

Which, Cyrus supposed, was exactly how he'd gotten away with it.

"Marsh." His voice carried across the water easily, pleasant, unhurried. "You've made a great deal of noise for a woman with one boat."

"And you've brought six for a woman with one boat," Odile called back. "I'm flattered."

"I'd like my cargo."

"It isn't yours."

"It was shipped under my company's charter."

"It was shipped," Odile said, "under a charter signed by three men, two of whom you killed, and the third of whom is sitting on that deck over there, and has been talking." She let that sit. Downriver, on the nearest salvage hulk, figures had gathered along the rail. Crews, Cyrus realized. Dozens of hired men, close enough to hear, close enough to carry the story home to every saloon and dockside table on the Cape. Odile hadn't chosen the wide reach for the deep water alone.

Vell's pleasantness flickered — just once, just at the eyes — and Cyrus understood why Odile had wanted to see it happen live. You could read a ledger about a man like this. You couldn't read the pause.

"Old Corvan," Vell said at last, softer. "Sixty years, and he finds his tongue."

"He found it," Tamsin called out, rising from his net-mending with the unhurried dignity of a man standing up in church, "when somebody finally asked me a question worth answering."

Vell looked at him for a long moment. Whatever he saw, it made him turn back to Odile with his voice smoothed over again, reasonable, almost warm. "Whatever you think you know, you can't prove. Not here. Not with these courts."

"I don't need your courts," Odile said. "I need the story. And as of yesterday afternoon, the story belongs to about forty of your employees, one ferryman, one magistrate's courier, and me. You taught this territory how money works, Ambrose. I'm teaching it how gossip works. Gossip's cheaper, and it floats better."

For the first time, Vell looked past her — at the crates, stacked amidships, twelve boxes of sealed wax and paper and gold — and Cyrus watched him do the arithmetic. The launch drifted closer. On the salvage hulks, a capstan began to turn; someone was readying a boarding party despite everything, or because of everything, and Cyrus's hand found the boathook at her feet.

And then the river arrived.

* * *

You heard a grandfather tide before you saw it — a low change in the sound of the water, a long exhale from the sea miles downriver, and then the current simply reversed, the whole channel swinging like a door, and the surface began to rise. Not waves. Rise. The river climbing its own banks, patient and enormous, lifting the *Kestrel's Own* and the launch and the six hulks together, remaking the world's geometry in minutes.

"Fourth hour," Brann said quietly. "Right on time. River's the only honest witness, girl. Told you."

The launch wallowed as the swell took it. Vell grabbed his gunwale, recovered, and Cyrus saw the decision cross his face — the calculation of a man realizing the water was rising faster than his patience. "Last chance, Marsh. Give up the cargo and I'll forget today happened."

"No," said Odile.

"No," said Tamsin.

"No," said Cyrus, surprising herself with how good it felt.

Vell signaled his launch forward anyway — greed, in the end, outrunning sense — and the two boats came together with a crunch, and men with hooks started across, and for about ninety seconds the sixth tide became a brawl. Cyrus swung the boathook and caught a boarding man across the chest and shoved him back into his own boat; the Admiral, inspired, launched himself directly into the face of a second attacker, who screamed in a register Cyrus hadn't known grown men possessed; Tamsin, sixty years of river in his arms, simply picked up a crate of pre-war treasury gold and held it over the side, over the deep water, and said, in a voice that carried further than any shot:

"Come another step, and the fortune drowns instead of the truth."

They stopped. Every one of them. Because Vell's crews had heard the testimony too, and there is a particular kind of courage required to fight a drowning man's witness for the money that bought the drowning, and none of them had it.

Vell stood in his rocking launch, silver hair damp with spray, and looked at the crate suspended over sixty feet of black rising water, and Cyrus watched fifty years leave his face all at once. He looked, finally, his age. He looked like a man standing at his own graveside, trying to bargain with the gravedigger.

"You'll destroy it," he said hoarsely. "It's a fortune."

"It's evidence," Odile said. "Of what money does when it thinks no one's counting. The river kept your secret for fifty years, Ambrose. It can keep your gold forever. They deserve each other."

She nodded to Tamsin.

The crate went over the side without a splash so much as a swallow — the grandfather tide taking it down whole, the river closing over twelve boxes of sealed wax like a mouth. One by one, Tamsin and Cyrus and Odile tipped the rest, working quickly now as the boarding men watched frozen, as Vell shouted something that the wind took apart before it reached shore, until the deck was empty and the deepest reach of the Cape Reckoning ran sixty feet higher in fortune than it had that morning, and none of it would ever be found, and all of it was finally honest.

The salvage fleet did not attack. The salvage fleet, one captain at a time, weighed anchor and turned downriver, because crews talk, and the story was already moving faster than any boat.

Vell stood alone in his launch in the middle of the widening gold-lit water, and for a moment nobody moved at all.

Then he looked up — past Odile, past the empty deck — and fixed his eyes on Cyrus, and there was something in his face that was worse than rage. It was recognition. The look of a man reading a map and finding, marked plain as ink, the one line he'd never bothered to learn.

"You," he said. "The engineer. Nystrom's girl. You knew the spur lines. You led them here."

Cyrus felt the words land somewhere under her ribs, in the old bruise. *Nystrom's girl.* Her father, ten years drowned on a line Vell's company had abandoned and never explained. She opened her mouth —

And Odile's hand closed gently on her arm.

"Not here," Odile murmured. "Not for free."

Cyrus shut her mouth. But she held Vell's eyes, and she smiled, and it was not entirely a nice smile, and she saw him understand that this was not over — that the tide had turned twice today, once in the water and once in the story, and that the story was still rising.

The launch backed away. The *Kestrel's Own* rode the grandfather tide north toward Graywater, toward the magistrate, toward whatever courts were left that could still be shamed into honesty, with an empty deck, a wet otter, a sleeping witness, and a courier carrying the only treasure on board that mattered.

Behind them, the river settled over the *Meridian Star* and everything she'd carried, and the light came down the channel like forgiveness.

Cyrus stood at the tiller and let the current take them home, and thought about her father, and about maps, and about the one line she intended to keep.

She didn't yet know that Vell's launch had turned north too.
