## 21. What Cyrus Kept

The fire took the map in stages.

Cyrus had expected it to go all at once — a whoosh, a curl of ash, done — but paper was stubborn the way old things were stubborn. The first corner caught and flared, showing her a ghost of Spur Nine in orange before it blackened. Then the drowned town of Kestrel Bend, its little penciled streets, gone in a breath of heat that smelled like cedar smoke and forty years of her own handwriting.

She sat on an upturned crate at the edge of the salvage camp — what was left of it, which was not much — and fed the fire page by page, because she had earned the right to be slow about this.

"You're going to burn your *hands* doing that," said Tamsin Corvan, who had materialized beside her with two tin cups and the particular expression of a woman who had spent ninety years refusing to be impressed by anything and saw no reason to start now.

"They're already burned."

"Then you'll burn them *more*. There's a difference between a scar and a habit." She pressed a cup into Cyrus's grip. It was tea, or something that had once been introduced to tea at a party. "Drink. You look like something the river decided against keeping."

"That's roughly accurate."

The last survivor of Kestrel Bend lowered herself onto the crate across from the fire with a series of sounds like a ship settling at anchor, and for a while they just watched the map die together. The main line went first — the great spine of it, every mile Cyrus had walked, ridden, swum, and once, memorably, crawled. Then the branches. Then the spurs, those shy little fingers reaching off into the hills toward nothing, or toward everything, depending on how you looked at it.

"It's a strange thing to watch," Tamsin said. "A person burning their life's work."

"It's not my life's work. It's my life's *index*." Cyrus turned a page with a stick so the flames could reach the margins, where she'd annotated everything from bridge load limits to the best blackberry patches along the Cut. "The work was finding out what happened here. That part I keep."

"And the fortune?"

Cyrus looked out past the firelight, down toward the water. The Meridian Star sat where she had always sat, half-sunk in the silt of the flooded channel, her sealed hold finally open and finally empty — because Cyrus had opened the floodgates herself, third tide, and let the river take back what the river had been holding in trust. Somewhere under all that brown water lay a pre-war treasury shipment worth more than the whole territory, dissolving by degrees into mud and honest oblivion.

"Gone," Cyrus said. "Deliberately, permanently, and with witnesses."

Tamsin snorted into her tea. "You know there are men in this territory who would have wept."

"There are men in this territory who *did* weep. I heard them. It was very satisfying."

* * *

The salvage fleet had scattered the way a kicked anthill scatters — fast, loud, and without any clear plan beyond *away*. Harrow Voss's flagship had limped downriver at dawn with her pumps clanging and her owner aboard, under arrest by the two territorial marshals who, it turned out, were only bought *up to a point*, and that point had been Odile Marsh standing on the wreck's deck with a ledger seventy years old and a witness ninety years old, both of them daring the law to look away.

It hadn't looked away. That was the miracle of it. Or maybe not a miracle — maybe just what happens when you put the truth in front of enough people at once, plainly, in daylight, with the river glittering behind it like a notary's seal.

Cyrus fed another page to the fire. This one was the survey of the Narrows, with her notes on current speed at each tide. She watched the numbers go brown, then black, then nothing.

"Odile's asking for you," Tamsin said, after a while. "Before she goes."

"Where's she going?"

"Home, eventually. But first she's going to sit on that dock until the marshals are done with their paperwork, because Odile Marsh does not leave a job site until the job site is *finished*, and I have known that woman for sixty years and she has not changed one bit except to get more certain." Tamsin's voice did something complicated around the word *sixty years*, and Cyrus had the grace not to look at her directly.

They'd tell each other everything eventually, those two. It wasn't Cyrus's business yet. Some stories needed to be told sitting down, with time, and probably more tea.

"I'll come now," Cyrus said, and folded the last unburned page into her coat pocket without looking at it.

Tamsin noticed. Tamsin noticed everything; she'd survived ninety years partly on stubbornness and mostly on noticing. But she said nothing, which was its own kind of blessing.

* * *

The dock at Kestrel Bend was new — built three days ago out of salvaged planking by people who had no business being cheerful at that hour, and yet. Word of the testimony had traveled faster than any telegraph line Cyrus had ever mapped. There were fishermen down from the headwaters, a newspaperwoman from the coast who kept writing things down with the fervor of a woman being paid by the truth instead of the word, and a surprising number of children, because children could always smell when history was happening and wanted to be able to lie about having been there later.

Odile Marsh stood at the end of the dock looking out at the water, hands clasped behind her back in the exact posture she'd used in Cyrus's first survey lecture, twenty-two years ago, when she'd said: *A map is a promise you make to strangers. Be careful what you promise.*

"You burned it," Odile said, without turning around. She always knew. It was infuriating. It had been infuriating since 1889 and Cyrus suspected it would be infuriating at the funeral.

"The whole thing."

"All of it?"

Cyrus hesitated one quarter-second too long, and Odile turned, and one white eyebrow climbed with the slow inevitability of a tide gauge.

"*Almost* all of it," Cyrus amended.

"Cyrus."

"One line. One. It's the spur behind my house. The one that runs to the mill road. It's not even a secret — anyone could walk it."

"Anyone could," Odile agreed, "but *you* drew it, and you drew it because it leads home, and a person is allowed to keep exactly one line that leads home. That's not cartography. That's ballast." She said it as though quoting scripture, and possibly she was; Odile Marsh had been known to invent scripture when the occasion demanded.

They stood together watching the water. Downstream, the marshals' boat was tied up at the old ferry landing, and Harrow Voss sat in it between the two officers, and he was not struggling, and he was not shouting, and somehow that was worse than if he had been. He looked like a man doing arithmetic. Cyrus had seen that look before, on the faces of engineers reading a stress report on a bridge they knew was failing. He was calculating what he still had, what he could still buy, and whether the arithmetic came out ahead.

It didn't. It couldn't. Not anymore. The ledger was public, the partners' names were public, the grave behind the Kestrel Bend chapel — the one everyone had always called a foundation stone — was public, and Tamsin Corvan had testified for four hours without repeating herself once, which the newspaperwoman described afterward as "the most terrifying display of memory I have ever witnessed."

"He'll appeal," Cyrus said.

"He'll try. He'll find that money appeals differently when it's at the bottom of a channel." Odile finally looked at her, and her eyes were tired in the deep way, the way they got when a long job ended and the body remembered it had been carrying something. "You did well, Cyrus."

"You did the work. I just followed your silence."

"My silence." Odile huffed — almost a laugh. "I went quiet because I was frightened, if you want the truth of it. Seventy years I carried that ledger and never had the courage to open it where anyone could see. I told myself I was waiting for proof. I was waiting for someone to make me brave." She put a hand on Cyrus's shoulder, briefly, heavily. "You made me brave. Don't tell anyone I said that. I have a reputation."

"Your reputation can swim."

"It has, historically."

* * *

They drank the terrible tea on the dock while the light went long and gold over the channel, and the children were called home by mothers using names Cyrus didn't know in voices every mother in the world shared, and slowly the crowd thinned until it was mostly gulls and the two of them and the wreck, dark against the darkening water.

"What will you do?" Odile asked. "Now. With no map."

Cyrus considered lying, decided against it — Odile could smell a lie the way sharks smelled blood, and anyway she was tired of carrying things alone. "There's a stretch of the coast line, north of Greywater, that's never been surveyed properly. The company always said it wasn't worth it. Which means nobody's ever really *looked* at it."

"Unmapped country."

"Unmapped by me. That's different."

"Yes," Odile said softly. "It is."

A gull screamed something rude overhead. Out on the channel, the tide was turning — Cyrus felt it before she saw it, that faint change in the water's breathing, the whole river shifting its weight like a sleeper rolling over. Sixth tide coming. The last one. After this, the story belonged to other people: the courts, the newspapers, the historians who would argue about it for a hundred years in rooms with better chairs than any of this deserved.

"I should go check on Tamsin," Cyrus said. "She threatened to make dinner."

"She threatens that every night. It's how she shows love."

"I know. Last night it was eel stew."

"How was it?"

"Wet," Cyrus said, "and full of bones, and I had three helpings."

Odile laughed — actually laughed, a real one, rusty from disuse — and Cyrus thought: *there it is. Worth the whole six tides.* Not the cargo. Not the vindication. Just this: an old woman laughing on a dock above a drowned town, because the truth had finally gotten out of the ground and stretched its legs.

* * *

Later, alone in the survey tent with a lamp turned low, Cyrus took out the last page.

She'd meant to burn it with the rest. She'd stood there at the fire with it in her pocket and told herself she would, and then Tamsin had brought tea, and then Odile, and then the evening had happened, and now here it was under lamplight, and here she was, looking at it honestly for the first time.

It was the spur behind her house. Just that. A single line running from the junction at the mill road up through the alder stand to the old loading platform where she used to eat lunch as an apprentice, feet dangling over the rail, listening to the freight trains go by below like the earth clearing its throat. In the margin, in her own hand, younger and more confident than she felt now: *Nystrom, C. — first survey, age 24. Got lost twice. Found it anyway.*

She sat with it a long time.

The temptation wasn't the treasure — that was gone, genuinely gone, sleeping in the silt where it could hurt no one. The temptation was smaller and meaner: the idea that she might need it someday. That the world might turn hard again, the way it had turned hard for Voss, and that a person ought to keep a little leverage hidden in a drawer against that day. Everyone else in this story had kept something. Voss had kept his secret. Odile had kept her ledger. Even the town had kept its grave disguised as a foundation stone, seventy years of everyone politely not knowing what they all knew.

And look what keeping had cost them.

Cyrus struck a match.

She held it to the corner and watched the flame walk up the page — past the alder stand, past the mill road junction, past the note in her young hand — and at the last possible moment, when the fire had taken everything but a strip the width of her thumb, she pinched it out.

The strip showed nothing but blank margin. No line. No name. Just paper, and a scorched edge, and room enough to write something new.

She looked at it, and started laughing, quietly, alone in the tent like a madwoman, because of course. Of course that was the answer. You didn't keep the line that led home. Home wasn't on the map. Home was wherever you were standing when you finally stopped needing one.

She smoothed the scrap flat, found her pencil, and wrote on it in careful letters:

*Cyrus Nystrom — last survey, age 46. Didn't get lost. Found it anyway.*

Outside, the sixth tide began to rise, and somewhere down the channel a bell buoy rang once, clear as a struck coin, as if the river itself were calling the roll — and far downstream, where the water went dark toward the sea, a lantern moved against the current, small and steady and heading upstream, toward Kestrel Bend, toward the dock, toward her.

Cyrus blew out the lamp, put the scrap in her breast pocket over her heart, and went to see who was coming.
