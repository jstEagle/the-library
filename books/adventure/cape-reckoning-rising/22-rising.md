## 22. Rising

The sixth tide came in gray and gentle, like an apology.

Cyrus Nystrom stood on the seawall at Cape Reckoning with her hands in her coat pockets and watched the water climb the pilings of the old ferry slip, and for the first time in six days — six tides, as Odile would have counted them, because Odile counted everything in tides the way other people counted in years — she was not in a hurry.

That, more than anything, felt strange.

Behind her, the town was waking up the way it always did: the bakery chimney breathing its first smoke, the gulls conducting their usual argument over the fish market's leavings, Tamsin Corvan's shutters banging open on the third floor of the boarding house because Tamsin had never once in her seventy-nine years closed a window gently. Cyrus could hear her hollering down at someone about coffee. She smiled without meaning to.

Six days ago she had been racing a salvage fleet up a dead river with a map that could buy a territory and a truth that could hang a man. Now the map was ash in her stove, the truth was in the hands of every newspaper from here to the capital, and the fortune —

Well. The fortune was where it belonged.

* * *

It had happened three tides ago, though it felt like another life.

She had been standing on the deck of the *Meridian Star*'s shattered superstructure, ankle-deep in river water, holding the strongbox key while Ambrose Vell's salvage barge ground against the wreck below her like a boar against a fence. Vell himself had come up the ladder last of all, wet to the waist, his fine coat ruined, his face carrying the particular expression of a man who has spent forty years buying everything he wanted and has just discovered the one thing money can't reach.

"The cargo," he'd said. Not even a greeting. "Where is it."

"Down," Cyrus had said.

And it was. That was the whole of it, really. The flooded hold, the rotten decking, the tide coming in through a hull that had waited thirty years underwater for exactly this moment — the moment when enough men with enough greed stood on enough rotten timber at once. The *Meridian Star* had held its secret through three decades of patience, and then it had simply let go. The treasury shipment, the sealed crates, the gold-backed paper that could have bought the whole territory — it had slid into the black water of the hold and then through the hold into the river itself, settling into the mud of Kestrel Bend like seeds going into spring ground.

Vell had screamed. She remembered that. Not words, mostly — just sound, the sound of a man watching forty years of murder and bribery and patient, careful evil dissolve into silt. He'd actually gone down the ladder after it, into water that came up his chest, scrabbling at mud, and Cyrus had stood at the top and watched him do it and felt — not triumph. Something quieter than that. Something closer to pity, if pity could be cold.

"You can't prove anything," Vell had said afterward, dripping, shaking, on the barge deck with his crew staring at their boots. "You have nothing. A madwoman's word. A ledger with no names."

"I have Tamsin Corvan," Cyrus had said. "And I have the river."

He'd laughed at that. It had been a bad laugh, a cracking laugh, but he'd laughed.

Three days later, so did everyone else. Because Tamsin Corvan — seventy-nine years old, half-blind, sharp-tongued, and possessed of a memory that had been waiting since 1891 for someone to ask it the right question — had given her testimony on the courthouse steps in Cape Reckoning, in front of two hundred people and four newspapers, and she had not given it quietly. Tamsin did not do anything quietly. She had named names. She had named dates. She had described, in detail that made the prosecuting attorney wince and one juror faint, exactly what young Ambrose Vell had done to his partners on the night the *Meridian Star* went down, and why, and how he had paid the coroner, and how he had paid the judge, and how he had paid the widow of one of the drowned men out of guilt for eleven years until the payments stopped and the woman drank herself into the river too.

"And I know all this," Tamsin had finished, gripping the railing with both hands, her voice carrying clear across the square, "because my brother was the fourth partner, and they never found his body, and I have spent fifty years watching that man walk past my window with other people's blood dried into his pockets. Ask me anything. I remember *everything*."

And the law, which Vell had bought so carefully and so completely, discovered in a single afternoon that it could not buy two hundred witnesses, four newspapers, and a federal marshal who happened to be passing through on unrelated business and who had, by pure chance, been looking for Vell on charges from three territories away.

They took him at the dock, trying to board a boat with nothing in his hands. Cyrus didn't go watch. Odile did, and reported back that Vell had looked "smaller than his reputation, which is usually how it goes."

* * *

Now the sixth tide was rising, and Cyrus walked down through the town toward the harbor, because there was one more thing to do, and it required company.

Odile Marsh was at the ferry slip, of course. Odile was always where the survey equipment was, and the survey equipment was stacked on the slip like a small tin city: theodolites, sounding lines, crates of brass instruments polished within an inch of their lives. Her mentor stood among them in her ancient oilskin coat, gray hair escaping its braid in every direction, arguing with a gull.

"You can't have it," Odile was saying. "It's a plumb bob, not a herring. What is wrong with you."

"It's a gull, Odile," Cyrus said. "They don't reason. They only want."

"A quality it shares with most of the people we've met this week." Odile turned, and her face did the thing it always did when she saw Cyrus — a complicated flicker, pride and regret and affection all fighting for the same real estate. "You're late."

"I'm exactly on time. The tide's not even halfway in."

"The tide is a state of mind." Odile waved a hand at the water. "Also, I've been thinking. About the spur lines."

"Have you."

"The northern ones. The ones past Kestrel Bend. The grades are still good, you know. Under the brush. You could run a line up that valley again inside of two years if anyone with sense held the charter."

"Nobody with sense holds charters, Odile. People with money hold charters."

"People with money are currently otherwise engaged." There was no cruelty in it, but there wasn't much softness either; that was Odile. "Vell's assets are frozen. His land holdings will be auctioned. Including, I am told, certain rights-of-way along the Reckoning River." She looked at Cyrus sideways, the way she used to when Cyrus was nineteen and hadn't yet learned to hide what she wanted. "I'm told the auction is poorly advertised. I'm told attendance may be sparse."

Cyrus looked out at the water. The tide was climbing the slip pilings, dark and steady, covering the marks she'd known since childhood — the notch her father had cut, the ring the flood of '88 had left, the little iron ring where the ferry rope had tied off before the ferry stopped running and the town stopped pretending it needed one.

"You're asking me to rebuild the dead lines," she said.

"I'm asking you to consider that a map is only ashes if you let it be." Odile picked up a plumb bob, weighed it, set it down. "You burned your map. Good. It was a beautiful map and it was doing you harm. But Cyrus — the lines aren't dead. They're sleeping. There's a difference. Rivers sleep. Rails sleep. Even towns sleep." She nodded upstream, toward Kestrel Bend, invisible around the bend but present in both their minds like a shared scar. "Kestrel Bend will rise again. Maybe not as it was. Maybe as something new. Towns do that, when somebody finally tells the truth about how they died."

* * *

They took Odile's survey skiff upriver in the afternoon, because Cyrus had said she wanted to see it once more in daylight, and Odile had said nothing, which from Odile was agreement, blessing, and mild disapproval all at once.

The river was kind to them. The wind came from the west and pushed them along, and the water ran high and clean over the drowned streets, and when they reached Kestrel Bend the town lay beneath them like something in a photograph developing — rooflines fading up through the green water, the church spire just breaking the surface, wearing a crown of cormorants.

The salvage barges were gone. Two had been seized by the marshal; the rest had scattered downriver like startled birds, their owners suddenly remembering urgent business elsewhere. Where the *Meridian Star* had lain, there was only a smooth brown eddy and, on the bank above it, a wooden cross that hadn't been there six days ago. Someone had planted it. Someone had carved letters into it, rough ones:

FOR THE FOUR. AND FOR THE FIFTH, WHO WAITED.

Tamsin's brother. Cyrus understood, and didn't ask, and was glad of it.

They drifted a while. Odile took soundings, because Odile could not be on water without taking soundings any more than a dog could pass a tree without comment. Cyrus sat in the stern with her boots over the side and let the sun get into her bones.

"I keep thinking about the gold," she said finally. "Everyone keeps asking me. At the courthouse, in the street. *Do you know where it settled, Miss Nystrom? Could it be recovered?* As if I'd trade one treasure for another."

"Would you?"

"No." And she found, testing the word, that it was true all the way down. "It killed four men and poisoned a town for fifty years. Letting it sit in the mud is the first honest work it's ever done."

Odile grunted, which was her version of applause. Then, quieter, in the voice she almost never used: "You chose well. Up there. On the wreck. I watched you choose, and I want you to know — I've surveyed a lot of country, Cyrus Nystrom, and I have rarely seen firmer ground."

Cyrus laughed, surprising herself. "Was that a compliment?"

"Don't get used to it. The tide will turn."

* * *

Evening found them back at the boarding house, in Tamsin Corvan's kitchen, eating a stew that Tamsin claimed was mutton and everyone else suspected was mutton-adjacent. The window was open, because of course it was. The tide sounds came up off the harbor, soft and rhythmic, the whole coast breathing in its sleep.

"So," Tamsin said, ladling out seconds whether anyone wanted them or not. "The auction. For Vell's rights-of-way. My cousin's boy works in the clerk's office, and he says nobody's filed a bid yet. Says the paperwork's sitting there gathering dust because half the territory thinks Vell's rights are cursed and the other half thinks the river's haunted." She fixed Cyrus with her milky, unerring stare. "Which is nonsense, of course. The river isn't haunted. The river's *patient*. There's a difference, and it matters."

"That's nearly word for word what Odile said."

"Well, then maybe listen to us twice." Tamsin sat down heavily, folded her hands. "Fifty years I watched that town drown and stay drowned, and everybody said it was the water that kept it under. It wasn't the water. It was the lie. Water rises, girl. Lies sink. You take those rail rights and you put steel up that valley, and when the trains come through Kestrel Bend, you stop the train there. Right there. Let folks walk down to the water and look at the spire. Put up a stone with four names on it. Five." Her voice caught, just slightly, and recovered. "Let the town rise as whatever it wants to be. But give it the choice. That's all anybody ever needed. The choice."

The kitchen was quiet except for the stew pot ticking as it cooled, and the gulls, and far off the foghorn at the cape making its slow, patient announcement to no one in particular.

"All right," Cyrus said.

Tamsin blinked. "All right, what?"

"All right, I'll bid. Tomorrow morning. First thing." She looked down at her hands, calloused from rope and rail and oar, hands that had held a burning map six nights ago and felt lighter, not heavier, as it went. "I'll need a surveyor."

"You'll need a miracle," said Odile, reaching for more bread. "Fortunately, I know where to find one. She's sitting right there eating terrible mutton."

"It's excellent mutton," said Tamsin.

"It's a crime against sheep," said Odile, "and I'll have thirds."

And Cyrus sat between them, laughing, while outside the sixth tide rose and rose, filling the channels, lifting the boats, climbing the old marks on the pilings — higher tonight than it had been in years, the fishermen would say later, higher than anyone could remember, as if the whole coast were drawing a long breath after holding it for half a century.

Rising. Everything rising.

In the morning she would file her bid on the dead lines. In the season after that, the first survey stakes would go into the ground above Kestrel Bend, white and bright against the green. But tonight there was only the kitchen, and the stew, and the tide coming in — and somewhere inland, faint under the gulls and the foghorn, so faint she might have imagined it, the long low note of a locomotive whistle on some distant mainline, calling across the dark like a voice that remembered the way home.
