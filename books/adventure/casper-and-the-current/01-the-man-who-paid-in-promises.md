## 1. The Man Who Paid in Promises

The thief had a head start of four hours and no water, which meant I'd catch him by sundown or find his bones by morning. Either way, I'd get my ledger balanced. That's the thing about the salt flats — they don't care who you are, only how much you drink.

I'd been tracking Sef Marrow for three days. He'd lifted a season's wages from the Brackwater assay office along with the clerk's dignity, and the magistrate had put a bounty on him that would cover my fuel for a year. I wasn't chasing him out of righteousness. Righteousness doesn't keep an engine running. But there's a particular satisfaction in following a man's mistakes across a landscape that records everything — every stumble, every desperate detour toward a mirage. The salt kept his story better than he did.

By late afternoon his trail turned strange. He'd stopped running in a straight line and started circling, wide loops like something wounded, and then the tracks converged on a low rise where the crust had buckled into ridges the color of old bone. I killed my engine a half-mile out and walked the rest, because some scenes you don't want to announce yourself to.

I found him at the base of the rise, and I found that he hadn't been alone.

Sef Marrow was dead. Not bounty-dead, not brought-to-justice dead — gutted, opened from hip to ribs with a blade that had known its work, his eyes fixed on a sky he'd never once looked at honestly in his life. His hand was still curled around a canvas satchel, and even dead, he gripped it like it mattered more than breathing.

Kneeling beside him, propped against the salt ridge with his back to the world, was an old man I didn't know. Prospector's gear, patched and resoled, a canteen lying empty beside him with its cap off like he'd meant to refill it and forgotten why. He'd taken a bullet high in the chest — not the thief's work, unless the thief had learned to shoot since leaving Brackwater. The wound had that slow, terrible patience about it. He had hours left, maybe fewer, and he knew it, because dying men always do and everyone else has to guess.

His eyes found me before I could decide whether to draw.

"Ranger," he said. His voice was gravel poured through a sieve. "You're the one they send when nobody else comes."

"Noor Okafor." I kept my voice level. "Who did this?"

"Does it matter?" He coughed, wet and long. "You'll write it down either way. Man shot. Man cut. Salt takes the rest."

"It matters to me."

Something moved behind his eyes — surprise, maybe, or just the effort of being seen as more than a line in someone's report. "Fleet men," he said finally. "Vane's. They followed me from the coast. Followed *him*, I think, and found us both." He looked down at Sef with something that wasn't quite grief. "Your thief tried to take what I carried. They took it from him instead. Then I took it back while they argued over who got to finish me." A ghost of a smile. "Old hands are quicker than young ones give them credit for."

I glanced at the satchel in the dead man's grip. "That what they wanted?"

"That's what they *want*." He held out his hand, palm up, fingers trembling with more than weakness. "Help me sit up. Please. I've spent forty years kneeling to this desert. I won't die doing it."

I helped him upright, and the cost of it showed in his face, but he bore it the way prospectors bear everything — like weather. Up close he smelled of iodine and engine grease and the particular staleness of a man who'd been walking longer than he should have. There was a name stitched inside his collar: E. BRANDT.

"Brandt," I said.

"You read fast for a ranger." He fumbled at his coat, and I let him, though my hand stayed near my sidearm out of habit rather than suspicion. What came out was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire, and worn soft at the edges from years of carrying. "My life's work. And my death's, if we're being precise about it."

He unwrapped it right there, blood on his fingers marking the cloth, and I confess my first thought was: another treasure map. The flats breed them. Every third prospector dies clutching coordinates to nothing, and every fourth widow sells the fiction to some salvage outfit hungry enough to believe.

But this wasn't a sketch on hide. It was a proper chart — survey-grade, annotated in three colors of ink, layered over years. Coastlines, depth soundings, and a grid of tide marks so dense it looked like rain. And across the center, in a hand that had pressed hard enough to emboss the paper: **MERIDIAN**.

I knew the name. Everyone past forty knew the name. The Meridian had gone down off the Broken Shelf a generation ago — a salvage fleet's own flagship, ironically, lost with its manifest and most of its crew in a storm the companies still blamed on each other. The courts had spent a decade deciding whose wreck it was and concluded, in the way of courts, that it belonged to whoever could reach it. Nobody could reach it. The tides around the Shelf ran wrong, and the dry season sealed the approaches with reef and sandbar for half the year.

"The window opens soon," Brandt said, watching my face. "Six tides. After that, the sea closes up shop until next season." He tapped the chart with one ruined finger. "Every approach, every safe channel, every hour the water gives you. Forty years of watching and waiting and writing it down."

"Why give it to me? You don't know me."

"I know your type." He almost laughed, and it cost him. "You're the law that shows up too late and stays anyway. I watched you check his pulse." He nodded at Sef. "Didn't have to. Dead's dead. But you checked."

I didn't answer that. Checking pulses is just habit, and habits aren't virtues.

"There's a condition," he said.

"There's always a condition."

"My daughter." The word changed his whole face — cracked it open like the salt crust, showing something raw underneath. "Juna. Juna Okafor-Brandt." He said the name like a man setting down something heavy after carrying it too far. "She'll be twenty-six now. I haven't—" He stopped. Started again. "I left. When she was young. Told myself the flats needed me more than she did, which is the lie every man out here tells, and I believed it for twenty years because believing it was easier than going home."

The wind moved across the flats, hissing through the salt ridges. Somewhere behind us, my engine ticked as it cooled.

"She went looking for me two years ago," he said. "Came out to the coast. Brackwater, maybe you know it. And then—" His jaw worked. "They told me she was gone. Lost. Presumed. All those words that mean *we stopped looking*." His hand closed over the chart. "Vane's fleet knows what I carry. They've known for months. They're the reason I ran, and they're the reason I'm bleeding, and if this map touches their hands, they'll strip that wreck to the rivets and call it salvage and my daughter will never see a grain of what her father actually left her. Which isn't gold." He looked at me, and his eyes were clear in a way that frightened me more than the wound. "It's proof. Manifests, ledgers, names. Everything Vane buried when the Meridian went down. Enough to hang the fleet in any honest court — if you can find one."

"And if you can't?"

"Then it's enough to trade. Enough to bargain. Enough that Juna gets to choose." He pushed the chart toward me, oilcloth and all, and his arm shook with the weight of the gesture. "Swear it. Swear it goes to her. Not to Vane. Not to the magistrate's evidence locker. To *her*."

"I can't swear to deliver things to missing women," I said. "It's not in my remit."

"Nothing's in your remit. That's why I'm asking and not ordering." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "Come on, Ranger. You chased a thief across hell for a year's fuel. Chase a promise instead. It weighs less."

I looked at the chart. I looked at Sef Marrow, whose theft had led me here, whose debt I'd been collecting in my head like interest. I thought about all the reports I'd filed that ended in *presumed* and how none of them had ever kept me up at night, and how that should probably worry me more than it did.

"If I take this," I said slowly, "Vane's people will come after me. Whoever gutted your thief will want what he died holding."

"Yes."

"And you'll be dead, so you won't care."

"Not strictly true." He laid his hand flat on the salt between us. "I'll care enormously. From wherever this desert puts me. So make it a good oath, Ranger. Make it one worth haunting you for."

I should have walked away. I've walked away from harder things than a dying man's last wish — I tell myself that, anyway, on nights when the ledger of my own choices won't balance. But he was looking at me with his daughter's name in his mouth, and the flats were silent in that way they get at dusk, when even the wind holds its breath to hear what you'll say.

"All right," I said. "To Juna. By her own hand, not mine. Never to Vane. On my name and my badge."

He exhaled — a long, rattling sound, like an engine finally allowed to stop. "There," he whispered. "Paid in full." He pressed the chart into my hands and closed my fingers over it himself, both of his around both of mine, brief and fierce. "Brackwater. Ask for Doss at the ferry. Tell him Brandt sent you and watch his face — he'll know more than he says. He always did."

"Brandt—"

"Elias," he said. "If you find her. If she asks. Elias." He settled back against the ridge, arranging himself with the deliberate care of a man making camp for the last time. "Now go. Tide waits for no ranger, and neither does the fleet."

I stood there a moment longer than was practical. Then I did the only useful thing left — I marked the spot, covered him as best I could with the salt crust he'd spent his life reading, and took Sef Marrow's satchel as evidence, because even a dead thief deserves his paperwork.

The engine caught on the second try. Behind me the flats stretched away, white and indifferent, already erasing our tracks.

Six tides, he'd said. I didn't fully understand yet what that meant — how fast a clock made of water runs, or how many people would be racing me to the same shore. I pointed the skiff toward the distant smudge of Brackwater and opened the throttle, and the chart rode against my chest like a second heartbeat.

Somewhere ahead was a woman the world had written off as dead, and a fleet that killed to keep it that way.

I'd sworn an oath worth haunting me for. I intended to outrun the ghost.

* * *

The lights of Brackwater came up out of the dark an hour later, strung along the shoreline like a net full of drowned stars, and the first thing I saw in the harbor was a ship flying Vane's colors, riding at anchor with her salvage cranes folded like a sleeping mantis.

So much for a head start.
