## 2. Six Tides

Brackwater smelled the way dying towns always do: fish guts, tar, and the sweet rot of ambition. I'd been here twice before, years back, when the harbor still had a magistrate worth bribing and a ferry schedule worth trusting. Now half the pilings stood bare above the waterline like pulled teeth, and the ones that remained held up taverns, a chandler's, and a customs house that looked like it had been abandoned mid-sigh.

I tied my camel in the shade behind the chandler's and paid a boy two coppers to water her and ask no questions. He took the coppers and asked three questions anyway. I answered none of them, which he seemed to respect.

The Vane ship at anchor was called the *Charity of Iron*, which told you everything about how salvage fleets name things. Her cranes were folded, her decks quiet, but quiet ships cost money to keep quiet, and Vane didn't spend a copper he couldn't drown someone for. Which meant they weren't salvaging yet. They were waiting. For a map, or for the tide, or for me — though nobody knew about me yet, and I intended to keep it that way until at least breakfast.

The prospector's map rode against my ribs, wrapped in oilcloth under my shirt, warm from my skin like something alive. Tide-charted, he'd said. Six tides before the dry season pulled the sea back from the Meridian's bones and left her high, dry, and legally anyone's. Six tides. I did the arithmetic on my fingers walking down the harbor road: roughly three days, if the moon cooperated, and the moon had never once cooperated with me in my life.

I found Doss where the guidebook of desperate towns said I'd find him: at the ferry dock, arguing with a man who owed him money.

Doss was built like a bollard — short, wide, immovable — with a beard going gray in patches and hands that looked like they'd personally strangled several storms. The argument ended when his debtor saw me coming and decided elsewhere was suddenly fascinating.

"Ferry to the outer shoals," I said. "Tomorrow's morning tide."

"Morning tide's booked." He didn't look at me. He was watching the *Charity of Iron* with an expression I recognized, because I was wearing one just like it. "Been booked since the fleet came in. They've hired every hull in this harbor that floats and two that don't."

"Hunting something?"

"Hunting *someone*, more like." He spat over the rail, expertly, into the gap between two pilings. "Vane's enforcer walked the whole waterfront yesterday asking after a woman. Showed a sketch." His eyes finally came around to me, slow as a drawbridge. "You wouldn't happen to be a woman."

"I'm a ranger. Different animal entirely."

"Mm." He scratched his beard. "Sketch showed a scar through the left eyebrow. Yours is on the right. Lucky you."

"Lucky me," I agreed, touching it without meaning to. Old habit. Old story. "Who are they hunting?"

"That's the strange part." Doss leaned on the rail, voice dropping to the register men use for gossip they've already rehearsed. "They're hunting a dead girl. Juna Okafor-Brandt. Prospector's daughter. Went missing two years back — boat lost off the Meridian shoals, they say. Body never found." He shrugged. "Fleet's been paying good coin for word of her ever since. Alive. Which is a funny thing to pay for, a dead girl alive."

My hand went flat against my ribs, against the map, before I could stop it. Doss noticed. Doss struck me as a man who noticed everything and charged accordingly.

"You knew her?" he asked.

"No." True, as far as it went. I knew *of* her now. I knew her father had died holding my wrist and saying her name like it was the last coin in his purse. And I knew that if Vane's fleet was still paying for word of her two years after she drowned, then either they felt guilty — fleets don't — or they knew something the sea hadn't told the rest of us.

*Presumed dead.* The words sat wrong in my chest. Two years of a father searching salt flats and wrecks for a daughter everyone else had buried, while the fleet that hounded him to death kept a bounty open on her like she was cargo they'd mislaid.

"When's your next unbooked tide?" I asked.

"Afternoon. Day after tomorrow." He paused. "That'll be tide five of six, if you're counting."

I was counting. That was the problem. "I need to be on the water tomorrow."

"Then you need a different boat." He straightened up, and something shifted in his face — not warmth exactly, but the particular solidarity of one small vessel regarding another. "And I need to not be in this harbor when the fleet finishes its shopping. My skiff's moored past the breakwater. She's ugly, she leaks opinions, but she's fast when she's empty and faster when she's scared."

"What's your price?"

"Passage for one, plus whatever you're running from doesn't follow us home." He stuck out a hand like a knotted rope. "Doss."

"Noor." I shook it. "And I'm not running from anything. I'm running *toward* something. It's worse."

He laughed, short and real. "Ain't it always."

* * *

I should have gone straight to the breakwater. Instead I made the mistake of being thorough, because thoroughness is the occupational disease of rangers, and thoroughness meant the tavern nearest the docks, where travelers talk and bartenders listen, and where I wanted one clean look at who else was asking questions.

I got the look. The look got me.

She was sitting alone at a corner table with her back to the wall — a broad woman in fleet gray with a face like a hatchet that had been used on better hatchets, eating stew with the patience of someone who'd learned that rushing meals was how you missed things. Vane's enforcer. I knew it the way you know weather: the way the room bent slightly around her, the way the bartender refilled her cup without being asked and without meeting her eyes.

I turned for the door with the unhurried gait of a woman with nothing in her shirt but ribs.

"Ranger." Her voice carried across the room without rising. "Okafor. Out of the Saltwatch district. Court records say you resigned." A pause, spoon set down with a click. "Records also say you were never very good at finishing things."

Every head in the tavern became very interested in its drink. I turned around. There was no point pretending; she'd have had my description before I'd cleared the harbor road, and the eyebrow trick only worked once.

"You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "You know my name and I don't know yours."

"Serevan." She wiped her mouth. "I'm going to ask you a simple question, and I want you to consider carefully that lying to me is a thing people stop doing halfway through." She laid something on the table: a scrap of oilcloth, torn, stained dark. Bloodstain, by the look and smell of it. Torn from a wrapping like the one currently warming against my ribs. "This came off a dead man on the flats yesterday. His companion didn't come back either. But a ranger's camel was seen heading south." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd seen all year, and I'd spent last year among smugglers. "What did he give you?"

"His regards," I said. "He was very polite."

Serevan sighed, genuinely, almost wearily, and stood. Up close she was bigger than the chair had suggested, and the room got smaller to compensate. "Here's what happens now. You walk out that door ahead of me, we go see the harbormaster, and we sort this out with paperwork. Or—" she glanced at the door, calculating distances the way I calculated tides, "—we sort it out here, and I bill the difference to Vane."

I did the math. Door: eight paces. Serevan: four and a half, and closing. Behind her, the bartender had quietly vanished through the kitchen, which in Brackwater was the local equivalent of ringing a bell.

So I threw the stew at her.

Not mine — hers, the whole bowl, scalding and full of eel, and while she flinched back with a curse that peeled paint, I went over the table between us, out the door, and into the street at a dead run.

Shouts behind me. The particular heavy footfalls of a large woman who ran like she'd done it professionally. I cut through the fish market, vaulted a cart of something that screamed softly, took the alley behind the chandler's two steps at a time — and heard, over the pounding of my own blood, a whistle from the breakwater side. Doss, standing in his skiff, waving both arms like a man signaling a landing plane.

"NOOR! Water's this way!"

Behind me, Serevan hit the alley mouth and did not slow down.

I went over the breakwater rocks rather than around them, hit the deck of the skiff hard enough to knock the opinions out of it, and Doss cast off before I'd finished rolling. The engine caught on the second try — engines always hesitate at moments like that, it's in their contract — and we swung out past the pilings as Serevan reached the dock's edge, skidding to a stop, hatchet-face unreadable, watching us go.

She wasn't shouting. She wasn't even breathing hard. She just raised one hand, not waving — *counting*. One finger, then two. Marking tides, maybe. Or marking me.

"She'll have a fast boat chartered by nightfall," Doss said grimly, hauling us toward the open water as the sun dropped red behind the *Charity of Iron*. "Whatever you're carrying, friend, it'd best be worth it."

Against my ribs, the map pressed warm as a heartbeat. Somewhere out past the shoals, in a drowned hold the sea had kept secret for six years, a dead girl was presumably still dead — and I had three days, one leaky skiff, and a hatchet-faced enforcer's undivided attention to prove the sea wrong.

"Tide's turning," I said. "Let's find out."
