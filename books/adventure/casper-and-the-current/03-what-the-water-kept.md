## 3. What the Water Kept

The Meridian didn't look like a ship. She looked like a ribcage the sea had picked clean and then, in a fit of sentiment, decided to keep.

I came up on her at slack water, poling Doss's skiff through a graveyard of masts and broken cranes that had once been a salvage yard. Somebody's idea of progress, rusting in rows. The hull rose out of the shallows at a drunken angle, barnacled to the second deck, her name still legible in flaking gilt: *Meridian of Brackwater*. Home port painted like an apology.

Doss killed the engine a hundred meters out and looked at me the way he'd been looking at me since we cleared the breakwater — like a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Vane's picket boat circled twice yesterday," he said. "They know she's here. They just can't get inside yet."

"Then I'll be quick."

"That's what the last three people who went in there said." He handed me a lantern and a coil of line without being asked, which was as close to a blessing as Doss gave anybody. "The tide turns in four hours. After that, the lower hold floods to the beams and stays flooded till morning. Whatever you find down there, it'll keep. You won't."

I tied off the skiff to a stanchion that had been a railing once, and I went in through a gash in the starboard side where the fleet's first attempt at salvage had torn her open years ago.

Inside, the Meridian breathed. That's the only word for it — the whole wreck groaned and sighed with the swell, timbers flexing, water sloshing somewhere below like a stomach digesting. My lantern found corridors furred with weed, a staircase going down into black water, doors swollen shut. And everywhere, signs of life: a rain catch strung from a bulkhead. Smoke stains above a tin stove. Fish racks. A cot made of sailcloth, neatly made.

Somebody had been living here a long time.

I had my hand on my rifle before I heard the voice, because that's what four years of chasing thieves across salt flats does to you.

"Drop it or I put this spear through your lantern and you can find your way out blind."

The voice was young. Female. Steadier than mine would've been.

I set the rifle down. "Noor Okafor. Desert ranger. I'm looking for Juna Brandt."

A long silence. The ship creaked between us like it was listening too.

"Nobody's called me Brandt in two years," the voice said finally, and a girl stepped out of the dark with a fishing spear leveled at my chest. Thin as a reed, sunburned, hair cut short with a knife. But I knew her face, because I'd spent three days staring at a dead man's sketch of it while he bled out against my shoulder. "And nobody's *looking* for me. So either you're lying, or you're the first person in two years who isn't."

"Your father sent me."

Something moved behind her eyes — not softening. Hardening, if anything. "My father," she said, "is dead."

"He died three days ago. Gutted by one of Vane's own men, on the flats, eighty miles from here. He paid a thief to carry his map to Brackwater. The thief stole it instead. I got the map back off his body." I watched her take that in, watched her knuckles whiten on the spear shaft. "He made me swear it goes to you. Not to Vane. To you."

"Convenient," she said. "A dying man's promise and a stranger with a gun. You know how many people have come out here claiming to carry something for Aldous Brandt? Vane's enforcer came last spring. Offered me a share if I'd show them the safe route into the hold. I put an arrow in his boat."

"Did you hit him?"

"His boat. He swims fine." She lowered the spear an inch. "Show me the map."

I unrolled it on the cot, weighting the corners with fishhooks. Her father's hand crawled across it in tide-charts and depth-soundings, six tides' worth of approach routes, the dry-season window marked in red. Juna read it the way other people read letters from home — fast, hungry, and then very slowly the second time.

"He charted all this himself," she said quietly. "After they took the ship. He spent eleven years sounding these flats in a leaky dinghy because he couldn't let it go, and he never once—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Never once came to find me."

"He thought you were dead."

"I know." She said it flatly. "That was the point."

* * *

From the journal of Juna Brandt, recovered later, undated:

*Two years ago I stood in the harbor office in Brackwater and listened to a magistrate explain, very kindly, that there was nothing to be done. The Meridian was lost with all hands, insurance paid to the fleet, case closed. My father wasn't dead. I'd had a letter from him eight days before. But the fleet said lost, so lost she was, and the magistrate's clerk wouldn't even look at my papers.*

*So I did the arithmetic myself. If the sea took the Meridian, the sea could give her back. Dry season drains the shallows every year; every year the wreck sits a little higher, a little closer to claimable. I rowed out and I stayed. Two years of fish and rainwater and listening to Vane's boats circle like gulls over a kill. Let them think the daughter drowned with the ship. Dead girls don't file claims.*

*I hated him for it. For letting them call it a loss. For not fighting. For being a man who charted tides better than he charted his own family. And now he's dead and the hating has nowhere to go, and there's a stranger sleeping in my father's old cabin holding his map like it's scripture, and I don't know what to do with any of it except stay angry, because angry is the only thing that kept me alive out here.*

*She snores, incidentally. The ranger. Very slightly.*

* * *

The third night, the sea left us.

It went out further than I'd ever seen water go — the whole bay draining away like a basin with a pulled plug, exposing mudflats stinking of sulfur, sandbars rising where channels had been. The dry season arriving all at once, exactly as the red ink on the map promised. And with the water gone, the Meridian settled, groaning, onto the reef beneath her, and something below decks gave way with a crack like a rifle shot.

Juna and I were in the hold when it happened, prying at a strongroom door her father had charted. The deck tilted. Water that had been ankle-deep became knee-deep became waist-deep in the time it took me to swear, and through the breach in the outer hull — new, ragged, fresh-split against the reef — I saw gray light and open sea pouring in.

"They've come at low water!" Juna shouted over the rush. "That's a cutting crew! Look — torchlight!"

Through the gash, out on the exposed flats, lanterns bobbed. Men with pry-bars and a steam cutter, walking right up to the wreck across seabed that should have been under six feet of water. Vane hadn't waited for the last tide. He was opening the ship like a can.

"We have to move," I said. "Now. Before they're through the inner bulkhead."

"The manifest is in the strongroom—"

"The manifest is paper. You are not. Move!"

We went up through flooding corridors, the water chasing us, cold around our thighs, our waists. Behind us I heard the shriek of the steam cutter finding the inner hull, and voices, and laughter. Ahead, the main stair was half-collapsed. Juna went up it anyway, and the step gave way under her, and she dropped into the black water below and didn't come up.

I went in after her.

I want to tell you I thought about it. I didn't. Four years of numbness, of chasing other people's debts across the flats, and it turned out all that numbness was just waiting for one moment when it wouldn't matter. The water swallowed me whole — cold enough to stop my heart, dark enough to be nowhere at all. I found her by feel: hair, shoulder, arm locked around a stanchion where she'd wedged herself against the current. Her eyes were open. Looking at me. Not panicking — furious, even drowning, furious at the water, at the ship, at everything.

I got my arm under hers and kicked for the light.

We broke the surface in the corridor above, gasping, and dragged ourselves up the last flight as the hold filled beneath us. Juna coughed seawater and laughed — actually laughed, wet and ragged and half-hysterical.

"You came back down," she said. "You didn't have to. Nobody comes back down."

"Somebody came back for your father," I said. "Close enough."

She looked at me for a long moment, dripping, shaking, and whatever wall she'd built over two years of living alone in a dead ship cracked straight down the middle.

"He wrote to me," she said. "Every month. Eleven years. I never answered. Not once. And now the last thing I ever gave him was silence, and he died on a salt flat carrying my name in his pocket." Her voice broke on it. "I need you to understand that I'm not crying about the ship."

"I know," I said. "I know exactly what you're crying about."

* * *

We came out through the top hatch onto the upper deck as the sun went down, soaked, empty-handed, alive. Below us, Vane's crew had their torches in the flooded hold now, and their voices carried across the flat water, celebrating. Tomorrow they'd pump her out. Next day they'd strip her. By week's end there'd be nothing left but the paperwork, and the paperwork would say Vane.

Juna wrung out her shirt and stared down at the lanterns with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"They think the strongroom is the prize," she said.

"It isn't?"

"The strongroom is where my father told everyone the manifest was. It's a decoy — he charted it that way on purpose, in case the fleet ever got in. The real manifest is in the wheelhouse safe, behind the chart locker. Has been since the day she sailed." She turned to me, and in the last light her face was her father's face entirely. "They're robbing an empty room, ranger. And we have until the tide comes back to make sure that when they figure it out — the whole harbor figures it out with them."

Out on the flats, a steam whistle sounded from the direction of Brackwater. Long, low, repeated three times.

Doss's signal. And Doss only had one reason to blow it.

Vane's flagship was coming, and she was coming tonight.
