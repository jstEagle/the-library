## 4. Claimed by Tide and Name

The last tide of the season went out like a man leaving a party early — no ceremony, just the water sliding away and taking everyone's excuses with it.

I stood on the spine of the reef with the prospector's chart unrolled against my knee, watching the sea peel back from the Meridian's bones. Six tides I'd chased this map, from a salt flat to a smuggler's skiff to the flooded dark of a drowned hold, and now here it was: the end of it, written in ink that was already fading as the sun dried the paper.

"Reef's exposed in forty minutes," Juna said beside me. She'd braided her hair back tight, the way her father must have taught her before he taught her how to hate him. "Vane will see the channel looks open. He always trusted his soundings more than other people."

"Soundings taken two years ago," I said.

"By a man who owed him money." She smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile. It was a good one anyway. "Doss is in position?"

"Anchored behind the far spit, engine cold, pretending to be driftwood." I rolled the chart. "And we're supposed to be where, exactly?"

"Dead," she said cheerfully. "We're supposed to be dead. Vane sent divers into the hold yesterday. They found the scuttled section collapsed. He'll assume we went down with it."

"He'll assume wrong. He seems to do that professionally."

* * *

The *Kestrel's Debt* came over the horizon an hour later, all gunmetal and appetite, Vane's flagship pushing through the last shin-deep water toward the channel that the charts said was twelve fathoms deep and the reef knew was four feet of coral and old spite.

Juna and I lay flat on the high shelf of rock above the wreck, close enough to share breath, watching through the salt-crusted gap between two boulders. She was warm against my shoulder. I kept telling myself I was only noticing because of the wind.

"There," she breathed.

The *Debt* turned into the channel, confident as a debt collector at a funeral. Her bow wave was barely a ripple — the water was already so thin she seemed to be sailing on mud and momentum. Vane stood on the foredeck, and even at this distance I could see him pointing orders, certain, proprietary, a man reaching for what he'd already decided was his.

The sound, when she struck, was not dramatic. That was the worst part. No explosion, no splintering roar. Just a long grinding shudder, like the world clearing its throat, and then the flagship leaned hard to port and stopped being a ship and started being a very expensive problem.

"Four feet," Juna murmured. "He grounded on four feet of water."

"His soundings were very thorough."

Boats dropped from the *Debt*'s flanks like fleas off a dying dog. Men waded, cursed, tried to kedge her off with anchors carried out on lines that promptly snagged the same reef that had caught her. Vane's voice carried across the flats, cracking with each repetition. Somewhere in there, the word *sabotage* appeared, and then *find them*, which was less satisfying than I'd hoped, since finding us would require him to get off his own ship.

"Now?" Juna asked.

"Now we go get your inheritance."

* * *

The Meridian's manifest was exactly where the prospector's chart said it would be — welded into a tube beneath the captain's cabin floor, dry as a sermon, because a man who spent his life being robbed learns to hide things properly. The paper inside was brittle but legible: cargo registry, ownership seals, and a bill of lading bearing the name Brandt in a hand I recognized from the map. The prospector's hand. Juna's father's hand, from back when the *Meridian* was his and the world hadn't chewed him up yet.

Juna held it with both hands, the way you hold something that might be alive.

"This is it," she said. "This is everything. This is the whole reason they killed him."

"It's paper," I said. "It's only worth what the magistrate says it's worth."

"Then let's go make it worth something."

Getting off the reef was its own small adventure — Doss ferried us out through the shallows in the skiff, poling where the engine would have announced us, while behind us Vane's crew burned signal flares that lit the stranded flagship like a festival nobody wanted to attend. By the time we reached Brackwater, the tide was a memory and the harbor magistrate was lighting his evening lamp, which meant we arrived at precisely the moment when a tired official is most willing to believe anything that ends his day faster.

Mostly.

"Salvage claim," the magistrate said, squinting at the manifest under his lamp. "On the *Meridian*. Filed by... Juna Okafor-Brandt." He looked up. "You're reported dead."

"I'm reported a lot of things," Juna said. "Dead is the least insulting."

"And you." His eyes swung to me. "Noor Okafor. There's a warrant."

"There was a warrant," Doss said smoothly, materializing at my elbow with the timing of a man who'd spent thirty years reading harbors. "For theft of fleet property. Property which, per this manifest, belonged to Brandt, not the fleet. Which makes the warrant itself stolen goods, in a manner of speaking."

"That is not how law works," the magistrate said.

"No," I agreed. "But it's how Vane's law works, and I figure yours can't be worse."

What saved us wasn't rhetoric. It was testimony — the thief's testimony, or rather the hole he'd left. I laid it out for the magistrate piece by piece: the gutted man on the salt flats, the dying prospector, the map traded for a promise, the fleet's divers breaching a wreck they had no claim to, the *Kestrel's Debt* grounded on a reef because its master trusted greed over soundings. Two of Vane's own crew, it turned out, had already drifted into Brackwater ahead of us and were drinking in the Saltcellar, saying things about their employer that alcohol loosens and fear confirms. The magistrate sent for them. They talked.

By midnight, the salvage claim was provisionally filed in the name of Juna Okafor-Brandt, heir and documented owner, with the fleet's counter-claim suspended pending investigation into, among other things, the death of a prospector whose map had led everyone to this exact table. The warrant for my arrest was quietly shelved, then quietly torn, then quietly used to light the magistrate's pipe, which felt like justice of a sort.

Word reached the reef by dawn. We watched from Doss's deck as the *Kestrel's Debt* broke apart under its own weight, crew scattering across the flats in longboats like ants abandoning a drowned nest. Vane himself took a skiff south, alone, heading for ports where his name still opened doors. Nobody chased him. The sea had already taken his ship; letting him keep his pride seemed a fair trade for never seeing his face again.

* * *

A week later, Juna and I sat on the Meridian's exposed hull with our boots dangling over the coral, eating smoked fish out of paper and watching the dry season finish its work. Each day the sea pulled back a little further, uncovering more of the wreck, more of the reef, more of the world that six tides ago had been underwater and impossible.

"Dry season seals her by month's end," Juna said. "After that she's just a ruin on a plain until the rains come back."

"Ruins are honest," I said. "They don't pretend to be anything but what survived."

She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Is that ranger philosophy?"

"That's tired-person philosophy. Ranger philosophy is more about hydration."

She laughed — really laughed, the first time I'd heard it without seawater involved — and for a while neither of us said anything, because there didn't need to be anything said. The inheritance was hers. The promise was kept. The thief's debt was paid in testimony, the prospector's in a daughter standing on his ship again, mine in... whatever this was. Company, maybe. Anchorage.

"You could stay," Juna said eventually, eyes on the horizon. "Brackwater needs a ranger. Or something adjacent to a ranger. Someone who chases fewer thieves and eats more fish."

"The pay?"

"Terrible."

"The hours?"

"Worse."

"When do I start?"

She grinned and handed me the last of the fish, and out past the reef the first rain clouds of the far-off wet season were already stacking up on the horizon like a promise somebody intended to keep.

Somewhere south, Vane was running. Somewhere inland, the courts were still slow. But here, on a dead ship with a living name, the tide had finally gone out on everything I'd been chasing — and what it left behind looked, for the first time in years, like home.
