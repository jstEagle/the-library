## 1. Salt and Static

The thief left tracks like a confession.

August Okonkwo read them from the saddle of her bike, engine idling, one boot down on the crusted salt. Two days old, maybe less—the edges still sharp, not yet slumped by wind. A lone walker, heavy on the right heel, dragging slightly on the left. She'd been following that asymmetry for four months across three hundred miles of hardpan, from the fuel depot at Corridor Junction to the edge of the Verity Salt Flats, and every set of prints told her the same thing: whoever this was, they were tired, they were alone, and they were heading somewhere they shouldn't be.

She killed the engine and let the silence come down.

That was the first wrongness. Not the tracks—those she understood. The silence. Seven years on patrol had taught her the flats had a voice if you listened: heat ticking in the crust, salt crystals pinging as they expanded, the far-off complaint of a hawk riding a thermal over the rimrock. Today there was none of it. The desert had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone stops talking mid-sentence because you walked in.

August stood a moment longer than she needed to, hand resting on the warm tank of her bike, and made herself name what she felt. Not fear. Recognition. She knew this kind of quiet. It was the quiet that came before records got rewritten.

She crouched at the track line. The walker's pace had been steady for miles—she could see it in the even spacing—and then, forty yards back, it wasn't. The strides shortened. Scuffed. One print showed the full sole, planted flat, the way a person plants their feet when they've stopped to look at something ahead of them.

Then nothing. The tracks simply ended, as if the walker had stepped off the face of the earth between one stride and the next.

August straightened slowly. Wind moved the heat in slow silver sheets across the flats, distorting the horizon into liquid glass. No cover out here. Nowhere to hide. If someone had been taken, they'd been taken in full view of God and the Bureau of Land Survey, and neither had bothered to leave a note.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud kept the old habits at bay. "Okay."

She swung back onto the bike and followed the ghost of the trail another two miles, dismounting twice where the crust grew thin, walking the last stretch with her boots whispering over salt so fine it climbed into everything—her socks, her rifle's action, the creases of her gloves. The thief's prints reappeared briefly near a low rise of gypsum, then vanished again, and this time August saw why.

The man lay facedown at the base of the rise, arms flung forward, as if he'd been reaching for something just beyond his fingers.

She approached the way she'd been trained to approach everything: wide arc, weapon ready, eyes moving. But there was nothing to be ready for. He was dead—that was plain from thirty feet, the stillness of him, the way the heat already had its hands on him. She holstered the rifle and knelt.

He was younger than she'd expected. Mid-thirties, sunburned to leather, lips cracked black. His pack sat beside him, unopened, which meant he hadn't died of thirst—he'd died of something faster. There were no wounds she could find. No blood. His eyes were open, fixed on the gypsum rise, and his expression was the worst part: not terror, exactly. Attention. He looked like a man who had seen something so complete that everything else had stopped mattering.

In his right hand, clenched past rigor, was a scrap of paper.

August worked it loose gently, the way you'd take something from a sleeping child. It was a page torn from a journal, cheap survey-stock paper, watermarked with the state seal. On it, in a cramped technical hand, ran a column of figures—elevations, bearings, dates—and beneath them, underlined twice, a single sentence:

*It isn't geology. It's a record.*

She turned the page over. Blank. She held it up against the light, looking for more, and found only the faint impression of writing pressed through from a missing sheet—a mirror-image ghost of lines, too faint to read.

The thief hadn't stolen supplies. He'd stolen pages.

August sat back on her heels and looked out at the flats, and the silence looked back.

* * *

She should have ridden on. That was the doctrine, and doctrine had kept her alive for seven years: report what you find, log your coordinates, do not investigate, do not improvise, do not become a story. The ranger service wanted clean reports. Clean reports kept rangers employed and unremarkable, and unremarkable was the only currency August had ever managed to save.

But the dead man's prints ended without ending, and the page in her pocket weighed more than paper should, and the silence hadn't lifted.

So she rode on—not away, but toward the only feature on the survey map within walking distance of the body. Sector 12 marked it as a temporary installation: Government Survey Camp Kestrel, authorized ninety days, hydrological assessment, personnel eleven. She'd ridden past its perimeter marker twice in the spring without stopping. Routine science. Routine men.

The camp appeared out of the heat shimmer all at once, the way things did on the flats—tents first, white and orderly, then the antenna mast, then the vehicles parked in a neat row with their hoods still warm-wrinkled from morning maintenance. Everything in its place. That was the second wrongness. Abandoned camps fell apart fast out here; wind took tarps, sand filled open doors, animals got into stores within hours. This camp looked maintained. Looked lived-in.

Except there was no one in it.

August circled the perimeter once on foot, rifle out, calling the standard hail—*Ranger Okonkwo, Bureau authority, anyone on site?*—and the words dropped dead into the salt. Nothing answered. Not a generator, not a radio squelch, not the nervous movement of someone hiding. Eleven people, gone from a camp that still smelled of coffee.

Because it did smell of coffee. That was the detail that undid her. In the largest tent—the mess, by the folding tables—three mugs sat on a table beside an open ration case. One mug was half full. A fourth place was laid but empty, plate waiting, fork squared neatly beside it. Whatever had happened here had happened mid-meal, mid-morning, in the middle of ordinary time, and it had happened to everyone at once.

She checked the comms tent third, after the mess and the command shelter, because she was stalling and knew it. The long-range unit was powered on, tuned to the state relay frequency, transmitting on schedule. She keyed the handset and raised the station at Corridor Junction, and the reply came back as static—thick, granular, the sound of a signal being eaten. She tried the emergency band. Static. She tried her own handheld, the little unit she'd carried seven years without failure. Static, and beneath the static, faintly, almost rhythmically, something that might have been a tone or might have been her own pulse in her ears.

She set the handset down carefully, as if it might bite.

Outside, the light had changed. The shadows of the tents had shortened without her noticing the morning passing, and the air had acquired a pressure she felt in her molars. On the eastern horizon, where the sky met the salt, a brown haze had begun to assemble itself—dust, miles of it, organized and patient. A storm building. Hours out, maybe less.

August stood in the middle of the camp with the dead man's page in her pocket and made herself think clearly.

Option one: ride for Junction, report a dead civilian and an abandoned site, let the Bureau send people whose job it was to care. Clean report. Unremarkable. By the time anyone arrived, the storm would have scrubbed the camp and whatever had happened here would belong to the same category as everything else the desert swallowed.

Option two.

She hated option two. Option two was the pull she'd spent seven years refusing—the instinct to stop, to look closer, to dig at the thing until it bled truth. She knew where that instinct led. It had led a twenty-four-year-old soldier named August Okonkwo to a filing room at Fort Meridian with a patrol roster in her hands and a choice, and she had chosen herself, and she had been running from the shape of that choice ever since. People who looked closer found things. Things required decisions. Decisions had weight, and weight sank you.

But eleven people were missing from a set table. A man had died reaching for a piece of paper that said *it's a record*. And the radios—all of them, every band—were hissing the same dead static, as though the whole desert had been ordered to keep quiet.

August walked the camp one more time, slower now, cataloguing. Gear intact: packs racked, water stores full, weapons locked in the armory crate with the seals unbroken. No vehicle missing. No tracks leaving—she walked the full circumference twice, reading the crust, and found the team's boot prints everywhere inside the perimeter and nowhere outside it. Eleven sets of prints, walking in circles, in pairs, in lines between tents. And then, at the northern edge, nearest the low black teeth of the old mining hills, the prints converged into a single file and simply stopped, mid-stride, all together, like a sentence cut off by a censor's pen.

In the command shelter, on Dr. Halloran's desk—*Dr. E. Halloran, Site Director*, according to the placard—lay a journal, open, and a page had been torn from it. The tear was fresh. The stub of the missing page was all that remained, and pressed into the next sheet beneath it, the mirror-ghost of writing she couldn't read.

The same ghost as the dead man's scrap. Same journal stock. Same hand.

August stood there a long time, listening to the static hiss from the comms tent like the desert breathing wrong, and felt the two halves of her life grind against each other: the woman who kept moving, and the woman who, once, long ago, had stopped and looked and then done the unforgivable thing to make what she'd seen disappear.

The dust wall on the horizon had grown teeth.

"Damn it," she said quietly, to no one, to herself, to a patrol erased from a ledger seven years ago. She went back to her bike, strapped the dead man's page into her document case, and turned the front wheel north—toward the mining hills, against the storm, against doctrine, against every survival instinct she'd sharpened since Fort Meridian.

For the first time in seven years, August Okonkwo chose to stop running toward the horizon and start chasing what was under it.

Behind her, the camp's radio hissed on, transmitting to no one, and the static almost sounded like breathing.
