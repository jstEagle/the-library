## 2. The Team That Wasn't There

August circled the camp twice before she let herself step into it.

The first pass was habit. The second was permission. Somewhere between them she admitted what her body already knew: nobody here needed help. Nobody here was anything at all. And still she dismounted, staked Kestrel in the thin shade of the survey truck, and walked into the silence like a woman wading into water she couldn't see the bottom of.

The camp sat on a low shelf of salt crust at the edge of the flats, four tents and a comms trailer arranged with the tidy geometry of people who expected to be inspected. Government issue. The kind of operation that filed paperwork before it poured coffee. A state survey team, out here where the maps went soft and the horizon lied about distance.

She cataloged the way she'd been trained to, item by item, because the list kept her hands steady.

Four dome tents, guy lines taut, zippers open. Six bedrolls inside, sleeping bags half-unzipped as if their owners had stepped out for a minute and meant to come back. A mess table under an awning: three enamel plates with food still on them, rice gone to concrete in the heat, a kettle sitting on a cold burner. Someone had been mid-meal. Not mid-evacuation. Mid-*meal*.

A instrument case stood open beside the table — ground-penetrating radar, by the look of it, military surplus with the serial numbers filed smooth. A laptop lay closed on a camp chair. A canteen hung from a tent pole, full. Boots by the door of each tent, laces loosened, waiting for feet.

Eleven sets of boots, when she counted. Eleven.

No tracks leaving.

That was the number that wouldn't sit still. She walked the perimeter three times, reading the crust the way other people read print. Wind-scoured salt held prints beautifully — hers, now, and Kestrel's, and older ones from the survey crew's own arrival days ago, a confusion of boot patterns around the camp like a spilled alphabet. But every one of those old prints pointed *in*. There wasn't a single line of departure. No vehicle tracks beyond the truck's own arrival ruts. Nothing walking out, nothing driving out, nothing dragged.

Eleven people had come to this place and stopped being here.

The radio in the comms trailer hissed when she keyed it. She tried the ranger band, then the civil emergency channel, then the survey frequencies printed on a laminated card taped above the console. Static, all of it, but a strange static — rhythmic, almost tidal, swelling and falling in a pattern too regular to be weather. She set the handset down and the sound kept going, patient as breathing.

Outside again, she found the journal.

It sat in the leader's tent, on top of a footlocker, bound in oilcloth against the grit. Standard field log, government stamp on the cover. She told herself she was looking for names, next of kin, anything that would tell her who she was standing among. That was even mostly true.

The last entry was dated six days back. Survey coordinates, soil readings, a note about equipment calibration. Competent, dry handwriting. Then, below it, in the same hand but pressed harder, the pen digging through the page:

*We should not have opened the lower gallery.*

Nothing after that. But the next page had been torn out — not ripped in haste, she noted, but cut, close and clean along the binding, with a blade. Someone had removed one page deliberately and left the rest. Left everything else. Left the boots and the food and the tents and the eleven sets of everything, and taken only one piece of paper into whatever waited past the edge of this camp.

August stood in the doorway of the tent holding the journal, and the desert went very quiet around her, and seven years fell away like a dropped coat.

* * *

The records room at Fort Meridian had smelled of toner and dust and the particular staleness of air that no one was allowed to open a window in.

Corporal August Okonkwo, twenty-three years old and two weeks from a discharge she'd already started dreaming about, sat at a terminal with a patrol report glowing on the screen and a choice in her chest like a swallowed stone.

The patrol had been Sergeant Voss's idea. Four of them, off-book, running cargo across the border sector for a quartermaster who paid in cash and never asked questions. It should have been simple. It should have been *nothing*. But the truck had hit an old mine on a road marked clear, and the fire had been fast, and August had been thrown clear because she'd been riding tailgate smoking a cigarette she wasn't supposed to have, and by the time she got the flames beaten down there was nothing left to save. Three men. Voss, Adeyemi, Brandt. Men she'd laughed with over cards not twelve hours earlier.

And a manifest in the glove box with her signature on it too.

The official story wrote itself in her head with terrible fluency: unauthorized patrol, unrecorded, unsanctioned. If the report existed, the inquiry would follow, and the inquiry would find the cargo, and the cash, and the quartermaster, and it would find *her*. Seven years of a career ahead of her, gone. Possibly a cell behind it. Her mother's face when the news came home.

Or.

Or the patrol simply never happened. Four names, already entered in the duty roster, quietly struck. A route logged as untraveled. A mine strike reported as ordnance disposal, no casualties, location approximate. She had the access. She had the window — forty-eight hours before anyone missed three men who weren't supposed to be anywhere.

She remembered her fingers on the keys. She remembered how easy it was. That was the part that had followed her ever since, more than the smoke or the burned truck or the dog tags she'd buried in the desert rather than turn in: how *easy*. Nine keystrokes. A deletion confirmation. The system asked her, *Are you sure you want to remove these records?* and she had clicked yes the way you'd click yes to closing a window.

Three men became a rounding error. Became nothing. Became eleven sets of boots with no tracks leaving.

She'd told herself for years that it was different, that Voss had led them into it, that she'd been young, that the machine would have chewed her up too. All true, probably. None of it mattered. The arithmetic stayed the same. She had made people disappear to save herself, and then she had spent seven years doing the only penance she could think of: patrolling a desert where no one disappeared on her watch. Where every track led somewhere. Where she brought everyone home, or found their bodies, or died trying.

The desert had accepted the bargain. Until tonight.

* * *

She came back to herself kneeling in the leader's tent, journal open in her lap, the radio's tidal static pulsing faintly through the trailer wall. Her knee ached where she'd dropped down without noticing. The light had gone long and copper while she was gone; the sun was leaning hard toward the ridgeline.

Think, she told herself. Like a ranger, not a ghost.

Possibilities, in order of likelihood. One: the team had moved out on foot and the wind had erased their tracks. Except the wind hadn't blown hard enough in six days to scour prints from salt crust — she knew this flats' moods better than the dispatch board did, and the crust held a print for weeks. Two: they'd been extracted by air. No skid marks, no rotor dust signatures, and no aircraft on the flight log she'd checked that morning. Three: they'd walked into the earth. Into the old mining country at the flats' northern edge, where the shafts went down from workings abandoned sixty years ago, and the salt gave way to rock that took no prints at all.

But that didn't answer the boots. Didn't answer the food. People didn't walk into a mine shaft barefoot, mid-meal, leaving their canteens hanging.

Unless they hadn't walked. Unless they'd been *taken*, in some manner that left the camp neat and the record cut clean and one page missing like a receipt.

August closed the journal and put it in her pack. That was theft, technically. Evidence tampering, if anyone official ever cared. She found she didn't much care what it was called. The page that remained said *we should not have opened the lower gallery*, and the page that was gone had been taken by someone who wanted the rest of the sentence kept from eyes like hers.

She stepped outside. Kestrel lifted her head, ears forward, facing north — not spooked, exactly. Attentive. The way a dog attends to a sound below human hearing.

August looked north too. The mining country sat against the horizon like broken teeth, twenty kilometers out, blue-gray in the failing light. Between here and there, the flats ran flat and featureless and empty, and she let her eyes do the thing they were famous for at the station, the slow systematic sweep that had found lost hikers and smuggler caches and one stolen water tanker buried to its axles.

Halfway to the teeth of the ridge, at the very limit of resolution, the light did something wrong.

A shimmer. Brief, vertical, gone. Heat mirage, she told herself. The flats made them all day, columns of wobbling air over the hot crust. But mirages danced and swam, and this had been still. A straightness in the world where straightness shouldn't be, there and then not, like something enormous holding its breath.

Behind her, in the comms trailer, the static swelled — rose through its rhythm, crested, and for three seconds resolved into something that was almost a voice, almost a word, almost her own name spoken by someone who had learned it from a file — and then collapsed back into tide.

August stood in the middle of eleven people's abandoned supper with the sun going down and her heart going hard, and understood that she had a decision shaped exactly like the one she'd made at Fort Meridian. Walk away. Log the camp as found, request a search team, be a hundred kilometers gone before anyone arrived. Keep moving. Stay nothing.

Or go north, toward the shimmer, against every survival instinct she owned, and find out what sentence the torn page had finished.

Kestrel blew softly through her nostrils, watching the north.

"Yeah," August said, to the horse, to the static, to the three men under the burned truck. "I know."

She broke camp in nine minutes, and rode out under the first stars, due north, leaving the only tracks on the whole white plain — one set, pointing in.
