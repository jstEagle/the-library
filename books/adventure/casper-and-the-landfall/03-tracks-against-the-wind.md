## 3. Tracks Against the Wind

The storm came out of the west like something with a grudge.

August saw it building an hour before it arrived—a brown smear on the horizon that most people would have called weather and she called a decision. You could run ahead of a dust wall if you had a fast vehicle and no pride. She had neither. Her truck was a government surplus relic with a transmission that ground its teeth, and her pride was the only thing she'd managed to keep whole in seven years of running.

So she did what rangers did. She lashed down the gear, sealed the cab's filters, killed the engine, and waited inside a metal box while the desert erased itself.

The wind hit at 1400 hours. It sounded like the end of the world being read aloud—sand against steel in long, hissing syllables, the sky going the color of old bone. August sat in the dark with her hands around a thermos of coffee gone cold hours ago and let the flats disappear.

She'd been tracking the thief for four months. Small thefts, mostly: water caches tapped, a solar panel pried off a waystation, once an entire crate of signal flares. The trail had led her east across the salt, and yesterday it had led her to a body.

She still hadn't filed that report. The thief—Renn Voss, a drifter she'd half-known, half-pursued—had been dead in the shadow of a rock formation, no wounds she could find, eyes open to a sky he hadn't seen coming. And two kilometers past him, the survey camp: eleven cots, eleven mess kits, food half-eaten, radios hissing static into a silence that had no business existing in a place this size.

No tracks leaving. That was the part that kept snagging in her mind like a hook. Eleven people didn't evaporate. Eleven people left sign—the soft scuff of boots, the drag of a heel, anything. But the salt around that camp had been swept clean as a ledger page, as if the land itself had agreed to keep their secret.

Or someone had made it agree.

The storm raged through the afternoon and into the evening. August dozed in fits, hand near her sidearm out of habit rather than fear. Fear required a direction. This was just a low hum under everything, like the static on those radios.

Near midnight, the wind died all at once, the way storms died out here—not fading but stopping, cut off, as if someone had closed a door.

She slept until first light.

* * *

The world after a dust storm was a world remade. Dunes migrated overnight. Landmarks she'd used for years were buried or relocated, and the flats stretched out pale and featureless, wiped down to bare canvas.

Which meant anything written on that canvas now was new.

August climbed onto the truck's roof with binoculars and scanned the salt. Nothing south. Nothing west. Then she swung north, toward the broken country where the old mining shafts honeycombed the hills—the Vermillion Works, abandoned sixty years, fenced and posted and forgotten—and stopped breathing.

There was a line on the salt.

At this distance it looked like a crack, a seam in the crust. But it ran too straight, and it moved with purpose, arrowing northeast toward the works. She dropped off the roof, grabbed her pack, water, rifle, and walked out onto the flats to read it properly.

Footprints.

Eleven sets. She counted twice, then a third time, walking a grid pattern over a hundred meters of salt to be sure. Eleven. Same as the camp. Same as the mess kits with food still in them.

But it wasn't the number that raised the hair on her arms. It was the spacing.

Eleven people did not walk in step. Not soldiers, not dancers, not anyone. Stride length varied with height, with fatigue, with mood. Yet here every print fell in phase—left feet together, right feet together, stride after stride, kilometer after kilometer, like a single creature wearing eleven pairs of boots. The impressions were identical in depth, which meant identical weight, identical pace, identical *everything*.

August had tracked smugglers, fugitives, lost children, and one memorable escaped ostrich. She had never seen anything walk like this.

Her survival instincts delivered their opinion clearly and unanimously: whatever had come out of that camp was not eleven people anymore, and following it northeast into abandoned mine shafts was how ranger Okonkwo became a cautionary tale told at training academies.

Every rule she lived by said turn around. Report it. Let the state send people whose job description included things like this.

But the state, she thought, looking at the line of prints marching toward the Vermillion Works, was exactly the kind of thing that might have made this happen. And the state would arrive not with questions but with bulldozers and paperwork, and whatever these eleven souls had become would be smoothed over, filed, buried—erased from the record so cleanly that in a year nobody would remember there had ever been a survey team at all.

Erased from the record.

The phrase landed in her chest like a dropped stone.

She knew about erasure. She had performed it herself, once, seven years ago, with a pen instead of a storm. A patrol of six, listed as reassigned. Six names she had folded away into nothing so that she could walk out of a hearing room free, and she had been running from the weight of those names ever since, across three postings and a thousand kilometers of desert, moving before anyone could look at her too long.

And here was the desert handing her eleven more names—or taking eleven names away, she honestly couldn't tell which—and offering her the same choice it always offered. Keep moving. Don't look closer. Let it go into the record however the record wants it.

August stood on the salt with the wind at her back pushing her gently toward home, and for the first time in seven years, she turned her face into it and walked the other way.

* * *

She followed the line all morning.

By midday the prints had left the open salt and entered the broken ground below the Vermillion Works, where the terrain got argumentative—washes, rubble fields, ridges of rust-red shale. The unison held. Even climbing, even picking through scree where footing forced improvisation, the eleven sets of prints stayed in perfect concert, as though the ground itself were negotiating with them and losing.

Around noon she found where they'd rested. Or where they'd stopped, anyway—no fire ring, no waste, no bedding impressions. Just eleven shallow depressions in the sand, evenly spaced, occupied for perhaps two hours by bodies that hadn't shifted once. People fidgeted. People rolled over. Whatever had sat here through the night had sat perfectly still.

August ate standing up, watching the mine works hunch against the sky a kilometer ahead. The Vermillion Works had been a copper operation once, then a sulfur claim, then nothing. The headframe still stood, a black lattice skeleton against the white heat. Shafts went down under it—old maps in the station archive suggested levels, galleries, tunnels running deep into the ridge. Deep enough, she supposed, for eleven people to walk into and not come out.

Except they had come out. They'd walked out of their camp without leaving tracks, crossed the flats in lockstep, and gone down into the earth. Which meant the shafts weren't a grave.

They were a destination.

She checked her rifle, topped off her water, and left a note under the truck's wiper—coordinates, timestamp, a single word: *following*. If she didn't come back, some junior ranger would eventually find it and start the machinery turning. It wasn't much of a safety net. It was more than she'd given anyone else.

The last stretch she walked in the prints themselves, boots fitting into boot-prints, feeling absurdly like a woman stepping into someone else's story. The sun leaned west. The temperature began its long slide toward the cold that came with dark out here, and the shadows of the headframe reached for her across the rubble like fingers.

At the shaft mouth, the prints ended.

Not faded. Not scattered. Ended—in a clean line at the lip of the adit, eleven sets of boots arriving together and descending together into darkness. A rope rail still hung by the entrance, and the dust on the ladder rungs below was disturbed, recent, uniform.

August stood at the edge and listened.

From far below—so far she might have imagined it, except her imagination had never produced anything this patient—came a sound. Not voices. Not machinery. Something between a footstep and a heartbeat, repeated at intervals so regular it could have been a clock, or a drill, or a count.

Eleven sets of lungs, breathing as one.

Her hand found her flashlight. Her thumb found the switch. Every survival instinct she owned stood at her shoulder reciting the manual: *unknown number of hostiles, unknown conditions underground, no backup, no extraction, monsoon season approaching.*

"Seven years," she said quietly, to the dark, to the desert, to the six names she carried like stones in a pack. "I've done what you said long enough."

She clicked the light on and started down.
