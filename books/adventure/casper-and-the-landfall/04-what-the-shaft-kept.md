## 4. What the Shaft Kept

*Field Log — Imani Reyes, Junior Geologist, Survey Team Kestrel. Not filed. Not going to be filed. Written in the dark with a pencil stub because Dr. Halloran collected our tablets at the shaft mouth and I want a record that lives somewhere other than his say-so.*

Day nine underground. Or day ten. The lamps run on a rotation that makes days into something you count by hunger and lamp changes instead of light.

I should start at the beginning, but the beginning is a lie we all agreed to tell, so let me start where the lie cracked.

* * *

The shafts under the salt flats are older than the mining company that supposedly dug them. That was the first thing wrong. Osei noticed it before I did—Osei, who notices everything medical and nothing social, which is why I like him. He put his hand flat against the wall of the second gallery and said, "These tool marks are decorative."

Decorative. Mining shafts don't get decorated. Nobody carves chevron patterns into rock they're trying to haul ore out of at profit margins. But there they were: rows of them, worn shallow by time or by hands, running the length of the gallery like a spine.

Dr. Halloran told us it was probably ritual marking from a later squatter population. He said it in his briefing voice, the one that closes doors. We wrote it down because he's the lead and because the state pays for our conclusions as much as our observations, and everyone on this team has always known the difference between those two things even if we never said it out loud.

Then I found the archive.

That's what I'm calling it. Gallery seven, past the collapse the mining company never bothered to clear because they never got this far—their maps stop at gallery four, and their maps are the ones in the state registry, and I have checked. Gallery seven doesn't exist on paper. It exists in stone.

The walls are covered. Not carved—*printed*, if I can use that word, pressed into clay panels that were fired hard enough to survive whatever ended the people who made them. Thousands of panels. Rows and rows, floor to ceiling, receding into the dark past the reach of our lamps. And here is the thing that took my breath and hasn't given it back:

They are records of water.

I've spent three years learning to read sediment the way other people read letters from home. These panels are hydrological logs. Rainfall cycles. Flood stages. Groundwater depths, marked season by season across—I don't know how many generations. Centuries. The civilization that kept this archive watched the monsoon the way we watch stock tickers, and they wrote down every rise and fall, and then—

And then the last panels change. The hand changes. The entries get shorter, more desperate, and the final panel—the one I photographed with my own eyes before Halloran confiscated my camera too—is a map. A map of this basin, drawn from above, with the flood lines marked in relief. The true flood lines.

They do not match the state landfall maps. They do not match them by forty kilometers. Maybe more.

The government's landfall projections—the ones that decide where the levees go, where the resettlement zones go, where the water gets sent when the monsoon comes—are not bad science. They are not outdated models. They are deliberate. Someone drew a line on a map knowing exactly where the water would actually go, and put the line somewhere else, and everything downstream of that decision is a sacrifice zone wearing the name of a planning error.

I sat down on the gallery floor and laughed until Osei came looking for me. I couldn't explain it to him. I still can't, quite. It's the laugh you do when the world turns out to be exactly as crooked as your worst suspicion and you'd been hoping, right up until the proof, that you were paranoid.

* * *

Halloran called a team meeting after I reported the find. Of course I reported it. That's the protocol; that's my whole career. You find something, you report it up, and the state decides what the public gets to know. I have done this a hundred times with things that didn't matter. Fossil beds. Anomalous strata. A meteorite crater the minerals ministry wanted quiet for concession reasons.

This is not that.

He stood us in a half circle at gallery five, all eleven of us, and the lamps made our shadows lean against each other on the wall, and he said: "What you found is a containment priority. Nothing leaves this site. No photographs, no copies, no personal notes. When we surface, the official finding is that galleries five through eight are geologically unstable and were sealed in the pre-mining era. We logged a routine core sample and we went home."

Vasquez asked him why. Vasquez always asks why; it's her best and worst quality.

Halloran looked at her for a long moment, and then he said something I will write down exactly because I don't trust my memory to soften it: "Because the last survey team that found water records like these surfaced in March, and their vehicle burned on the flats with them in it, and the incident report says electrical fault. I read the file before any of you did. My job on this expedition is to make sure none of you end up in a file."

Nobody spoke. The lamps hummed. Somewhere deep in the galleries, water dripped at its patient interval, the way it had dripped for however many centuries, keeping its own records.

"Are you telling us the state would kill us?" Vasquez said finally.

"I'm telling you the state has a method," Halloran said, "and we are standing inside the reason for it. Go back to your stations. We surface in six days per schedule. Until then, nobody works alone in gallery seven."

That last instruction is how I know he's scared. You don't assign buddy systems to data. You assign them to people who might talk.

* * *

Here is what I keep turning over, alone in my bunk slot with the pencil stub and this paper I salvaged from the packing crate manifest, writing small, hiding pages inside the lining of my field jacket:

Halloran isn't lying to protect himself. I've watched him enough now to be sure. He sleeps worse than any of us. He stands at the gallery seven entrance sometimes for an hour, just reading the panels with a hand lens, and once I heard him say—to no one, to the archive itself—"You knew. All of you knew, and you wrote it down anyway." Like an accusation. Like envy.

He believes silence keeps us alive. And maybe it does. Maybe the smart move—the survival move—is to walk out of here in six days, file the false summary, collect the pay, and spend the rest of my career not thinking about the towns downstream of the real flood line. Serrano Crossing. The cattle stations. The school at Miller's Bend that the new landfall map conveniently places outside the projected wash, forty kilometers outside, precisely the width of the lie.

Forty kilometers. I measured it twice against the panel map and the current state survey overlay that's loaded in my head from three years of fieldwork. Forty.

There are eleven of us down here. There are thousands of them up there.

Osei came to my bunk tonight. Sat on the edge without asking, which he never does—he's scrupulous about space—and said, "You've been writing something. Don't bother denying it, you hold your pencil like it owes you money."

I didn't deny it.

"If it comes to a choice," he said, "I want to know which side of it you're on before I have to stand next to you."

"And which side are you on?"

He was quiet long enough that I could hear the drip, far off, keeping time. Then: "My grandmother lived in Serrano Crossing. Moved out in '09 when the first revised map came down. She always said the water would come back for the town someday, and the town would be surprised." He stood up. "She wasn't surprised about much, my grandmother. Think about that."

He left. I thought about it. I'm still thinking about it.

* * *

Day eleven. Something has changed in the air down here—or in us. Vasquez has stopped speaking to Halloran except in single syllables. Adeyemi caught me copying panel transcriptions into my jacket lining and instead of reporting it—which is what she should have done, what the old me would have done—she checked the corridor both ways and then held my lamp steady so I could see the glyphs better. She's the linguistics specialist. She thinks the script is phonetic, partially decodable, and she has started teaching me the signs for *water*, *rise*, and—a sign that recurs on the last panels more than any other, pressed deeper than the rest, almost gouged—*remember*.

Remember. A civilization staring down its own drowning spent its final effort on a verb addressed to whoever came after.

We are the whoever. That's the whole weight of it. We are the ones the archive was written for, and the state's answer to being remembered-to is to burn the readers.

Five days until scheduled surfacing. Halloran has changed the lamp rotation so he controls who's awake when. He's stopped sharing the satellite uplink schedule. This morning I watched him take the flare gun out of the emergency kit and put it in his personal pack, and when he saw me watching, he smiled at me—the briefing-voice smile, the door-closing smile—and said, "Protocol, Reyes."

I smiled back. I'm getting good at that.

But here's what I know that he doesn't: I've finished the transcription. The essential panels—the flood map, the cycle tables, the last desperate entries—are copied onto six pages of crate paper folded into my jacket, and Osei knows, and Adeyemi knows, and yesterday Vasquez looked at me across the mess tins for three seconds too long, and I think she knows too.

Four of eleven. Maybe more. Fractures spread underground the way water does—you can't see them moving, but you can hear them working.

When we surface, Halloran will march us back across the flats in formation, silent, contained, delivered. Unless the formation breaks.

I don't know yet if I'll be the one who breaks it. But I caught a light on the surface today—through the ventilation bore, a pinprick of reflected sun that moved wrong for weather and right for glass. Somebody is out there. On the flats. Alone, by the spacing of the glint.

A ranger, maybe. Or a scout. Or the state's cleanup crew, come early.

Whoever it is, they're between us and home, and in four days we walk toward them, and I need to decide what I'm carrying out of this ground besides paper.

The drip counts on. *Water. Rise.* Remember.
