## 5. Eleven Walking

The wind died an hour after sunset, and that was how she knew.

Out here the wind was a constant, a low argument between the flats and the mountains that never resolved. When it stopped, the silence that followed wasn't peace. It was attention. The desert holding its breath around something.

August had spent three days circling the mining shafts at a distance, rationing her water, watching the spoil heaps and the collapsed headframe through her scope. Three days of nothing. No smoke. No light. No latrine trenches, no trash, no sign that eleven human beings were alive anywhere in the maze of tunnels below her. She'd begun drafting the sentence in her head—the one she'd have to file when she rode back empty: *No survivors located.* She knew how those reports read from the other side. She'd written them before, in another life, in another hand.

Then the wind stopped, and in the new quiet she heard it: footsteps. Not scattered. Not shuffling. *Synchronized.*

She belly-crawled to the lip of the wash and looked down into the flat moonlight, and her skin went cold in a way the desert night couldn't account for.

They were walking.

Eleven figures in a loose column, moving across the salt crust toward the old survey camp with the even, tireless gait of people who had somewhere to be and no interest in anything else. No packs. No lights. Their shadows swung long and identical behind them like a row of scythes. At their center walked a broad-shouldered man whose stride set the tempo for all the rest—Dr. Halloran, if the journal pages she'd found were honest about who led this team—and the others adjusted to him without looking, the way iron filings adjust to a magnet.

August had tracked smugglers, poachers, a fugitive who'd once gone eleven days on cactus fruit and spite. She had never seen people walk like this. It was the walk of soldiers on a route march, except soldiers sweated and cursed and drifted out of step when the sergeant wasn't looking. These eleven moved like one body wearing eleven skins.

*Don't*, said the part of her brain that had kept her alive for seven years. *You found them. You know where they are. Call it in from the ridge and let the state handle its own ghosts.*

But the state was the reason there were ghosts. And besides—she was already moving, sliding down the wash on her heels, rifle slung across her back because you didn't approach eleven hollow-eyed people with a weapon in your hands unless you wanted to find out what they did about it.

* * *

She cut the angle and let them come to her, standing in the open where the moonlight would show her empty palms. When the column was forty meters out she called out, voice pitched to carry but not to startle.

"Survey team! I'm August Okonkwo, district ranger. I'm here to take you home."

The column didn't stop. Didn't slow. Eleven pairs of eyes came up and fixed on her with a focus that should have been reassuring and instead made her stomach drop, because it was the wrong kind of attention—flat, total, incurious. Like being looked at by a photograph.

"Your families filed missing-persons reports," she tried. "There's a monsoon coming, days early. Whatever's down in those shafts, it isn't worth dying on the surface for."

A woman near the front of the column turned her head—a small motion, mechanical—and said, in a voice sanded smooth of everything: "We're not lost."

"We're *found*," said the man beside her.

And then Halloran raised one hand, and the whole column pivoted around him and resumed walking, flowing past her at a distance of ten meters as though she were a rock in a riverbed. Close up she could see them properly now: lips cracked, cheeks hollowed, clothes stiff with salt sweat. They hadn't eaten well in days. They moved anyway, effortlessly, as if eating had become optional. As if something under the ground had decided hunger was a detail no longer relevant.

One of them, near the rear, stumbled. A young man, maybe twenty-two, glasses fogged with his own breath. For half a second his face broke—terror, confusion, the animal panic of a person waking inside a dream he can't control—and his hand shot out and caught August's sleeve.

"Don't—" he whispered. His eyes were wet and wide and *his*. "Don't leave us out here. Please. We walk at night, we dig in the day, we—there are rooms down there, rooms full of—"

"Ikenna." Halloran's voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It landed on the young man like a weight, and August watched the terror drain out of his face and be replaced by that terrible calm, watched him release her sleeve and turn and rejoin the column mid-stride.

Halloran looked at August then. Really looked, for the first time. He was fifty-something, silver at the temples, with the patient competence of a man who had spent decades being the smartest person in whatever room contained him. He smiled at her the way a customs officer smiles at a traveler with nothing to declare.

"Ranger Okonkwo," he said. "You've been watching us for three days. Very disciplined. Very thorough." He glanced back at the shafts, then at the dark horizon where the first thunderheads were stacking up like furniture moved against a wall. "Whatever you think you're rescuing us from—we're exactly where we chose to be. Go home. File your report. There won't be anything in it worth the ink."

"You're marching your team to death," August said.

"I'm marching my team to *understanding*," Halloran said, and there it was—not madness, which she could have fought, but conviction, which she couldn't. "You have no idea what's under this salt. Neither does the government that pays you, despite what they told us when they sent us. In four days none of it will matter. The water is coming, Ranger. Water always comes. The only question is what you'll be standing on when it does."

He turned. The column turned with him. And August stood alone in the moonlight with her empty hands and understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound opening, that she had seen this before. Not the shafts, not the walking—but the machinery. The calm voice. The order that everyone accepts because accepting it is easier than being the one who doesn't. She had been the calm voice once. She had been the hand raised, the word spoken, the patrol erased from the record while nine people walked into a canyon and out of history.

She had run seven years from that woman. Here she was, standing in front of her again, wearing someone else's face.

* * *

She followed the column at a distance until it reached the survey camp—the same camp she'd found abandoned, tents still zipped, meals still congealing in mess tins—and then she did something tactically stupid and tactically necessary. She sat down in the open, built no fire, and waited.

An hour passed. Two. The column stood among the tents, motionless, facing the shafts, like mourners at a grave that hadn't been dug yet. Then, near midnight, one figure peeled away from the others.

It moved badly. Deliberately badly—staggering, dragging one foot, the universal pantomime of exhaustion. It staggered in her direction and collapsed ten feet away, and August was moving before she'd decided to, rolling the figure over.

Imani Reyes. The geologist. Up close her eyes were red-rimmed and ferociously awake, and her mouth shaped words before any sound came: *quiet, quiet, listen.*

"They sleep in shifts," she breathed. "He calls it rest. It's not rest—it's the humming. There's a sound down in the deep galleries, a frequency, and after enough hours near it you stop wanting to leave. You stop wanting anything. He knows. He's known since week two, and he keeps rotating us down there because the ones who've been deepest don't ask questions anymore." Her fingers dug into August's wrist hard enough to bruise. "I've been faking it. Sleeping outside the resonance, chewing bitterroot to keep my eyes dull. I have maybe two days before he notices I'm not going flat like the others."

"What's down there?"

Imani pressed a folded bundle of paper into her hands—pages torn from a field journal, wrapped in a strip of map cloth. "Everything. A civilization older than the landfall maps admit. They recorded the floods, August—all of them, every monsoon cycle for six hundred years, carved in stone because they knew paper rots. The government's flood maps are wrong. Not mistaken. *Wrong.* The zones they've marked safe are the kill zones. The settlements they've been licensing along the eastern rim—" She swallowed. "They'll drown. This monsoon or the next one. And Halloran's orders are containment. We were never a survey team. We're a cleanup crew for a truth the state already had."

August's hands closed around the pages. Under the moonlight she could see the edge of a rubbing—concentric lines, a spiral of glyphs, and beneath them in careful translation: *THE WATER REMEMBERS THE VALLEY.*

"Why give this to me? Why not just run?"

"Run where?" Imani almost laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Ten of them will follow me if I bolt, and I can't outrun the humming. But you—you're outside it. You're the only variable he hasn't accounted for." Her grip tightened. "There's more. The last page. Read it when you're somewhere safe."

"What's on it?"

Imani looked past her shoulder, toward the column standing silent in the dark, and her voice dropped to almost nothing.

"The list of names. Of the last survey team that found these shafts, nineteen years ago. Eleven people." She met August's eyes. "It matches ours. Same number. Same roles. Same leader's signature at the bottom, acknowledging receipt of containment orders." A beat. "Halloran was on that team too, August. He's done this before. And the eleven who walked into the deep galleries that year—" 

Behind them, across the camp, Halloran's voice carried on the dead-still air, mild and unhurried, calling the shift to rise.

"—never came back up."

The column began to move. Toward the shafts. All of them—including, August saw with a lurch, Imani's empty place being noticed, heads turning one by one in the dark like a field of slow flowers finding the sun.

"How long do we have?" August asked.

Imani was already scrambling up, smoothing her face into that drugged calm. "Until he counts us at dawn," she whispered. "Get to the canyon country. If you're getting us out, get us out *fast*—because the water's coming early, and Halloran isn't planning to survive it. He's planning to be *in* it."

She staggered back toward the column, dragged her foot, slumped into line—and eleven figures walked into the dark toward the hole in the earth, and August stood alone with a stranger's journal in her hands and four days, maybe less, to learn how to un-erase people.
