## 6. The Monsoon Ledger

Imani Reyes had learned to write small in the dark.

It was a skill from childhood, from the years when her family's electricity came and went like a temperamental guest, when she'd done homework by candle stub with the paper angled to catch every flicker. Now she did it in a mining shaft two hundred feet below the salt flats, hunched over Dr. Halloran's own field journal—the one he thought was locked in his footlocker, the one she'd lifted the key to on the third night, when his sleeping bag had zipped shut around him like a cocoon and his breathing had gone slow and certain.

The pages she'd already taken were folded flat against her sternum, taped there under her shirt where no one patted down. What she wrote now was new. What she wrote now was for herself, because if she stopped writing it down she was afraid it would start to feel like something she'd imagined.

*Day nine underground. Halloran told us tonight. He called it a briefing. It was a confession he doesn't know is one.*

Her headlamp was off. She worked by touch and memory, the pencil whispering, and around her the shaft breathed—that low mineral exhalation the deep places made, cool and dry and older than any nation that claimed the land above it. Somewhere up the tunnel, Osei coughed in his sleep. Eleven of them had gone into the shafts. Eleven still walked. That was the part Imani couldn't stop turning over: nothing had *happened* to them. No collapse, no gas, no injury. They were all perfectly, accountably alive, and somehow that was worse.

*He said the state has known about this place for sixty years.*

She'd written it and still didn't believe it, so she wrote it again, pressing harder, the graphite nearly tearing through.

*Sixty years. The landfall maps—the ones that say this basin floods once a century, the ones the whole resettlement program runs on—are not wrong. They are maintained. There are older shafts beneath ours, sealed with state-issue concrete, and Halloran's survey permit wasn't a survey at all. It was an inspection. We are a containment team checking whether the containment is holding.*

And they had found what the containment was holding against: a civilization's entire record, pressed into clay and fired hard, predating the monsoon calendar by eight hundred years. A people who had watched the water come and mapped its coming so precisely that their descendants could have planned around it—could have *lived* with it—if anyone above had cared to look. Instead the state had buried them twice. Once in the shafts. Once in the ledgers.

*Halloran says if we report what we found, the resettlement maps collapse. Every village sited on the "safe" high ground, every compensation claim, every family that moved because the government told them the water would never reach them. He says the truth would cause panic. He said it gently. He always says it gently. That's how I know he's practiced.*

She heard footsteps above—soft, deliberate—and closed the journal, sliding it back into the crack between two support timbers where the wood had warped just enough to make a pocket. She killed even the memory of light and sat still.

Dr. Halloran passed her alcove without pausing. He was doing his rounds again, the way he'd done them every night since they'd surfaced, counting his people like a man counting money he suspected someone else was spending. His headlamp beam swept the wall a foot from her face and moved on.

Imani let out her breath slowly.

* * *

The briefing, as he'd called it, had happened at dusk, on the surface, with the whole team arranged in a half circle around him the way soldiers arrange around an officer—which, Imani realized now, was exactly what they'd become without noticing.

"The state has been aware of these structures since before any of you were born," Halloran said. He stood with his hands clasped behind him, silhouetted against the last violet band of sky, and his voice had that measured warmth he used for bad news. "This expedition's mandate was never discovery. It was verification. The upper strata were believed stable. You've confirmed they're stable. That is a success, and I want you to hear me say that."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved grit across the flats with a sound like sand poured over paper.

Osei broke first—he always broke first; it was why Imani loved him. "Verification." He said the word like he was tasting something spoiled. "We spent three weeks cataloguing a dead civilization's flood records, and we were verifying *concrete*?"

"You spent three weeks doing your jobs," Halloran said, "under a cover story that protected you. Ignorance was a kindness. If anything had gone wrong down there—if there'd been a collapse—you'd have been a tragic academic survey lost to the earth. Clean grief. Manageable. Instead—" he spread his hands, and the gesture was almost tender, "—you're carrying knowledge that has toppled governments."

"Then let it topple one," said Vashti, the team's cartographer, who hadn't spoken more than fifty words since they'd come up from the shafts.

Halloran turned to her with genuine sorrow, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Vashti. The resettlement corridor holds four hundred thousand people. You want to tell a grandmother in the river towns that the hundred-year flood line her house sits above is a fiction? That the water her grandchildren were promised would never come is coming? You want to be the one who reads them that ledger?"

"Yes," Vashti said.

The word landed in the silence like a stone down a well. Imani watched Halloran absorb it—not with anger but with the patient disappointment of a man who had expected this and prepared for it—and she understood, with a cold sliding feeling, that the briefing hadn't been a confession or even a warning.

It had been a census. He was finding out who would need managing.

"There will be no report," Halloran said quietly. "There will be a debriefing at the station, conducted by people whose job this is, and your testimony will be taken in controlled conditions, and then you will go home. All of you. Alive, decorated, and free. That is the offer. It does not expire, but I'd advise against testing its edges." He looked around the half circle, meeting each set of eyes, saving Imani's for last. On her, his gaze rested a moment longer. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we walk east."

*East.* Away from the ranger station. Away from August Okonkwo and whatever the woman had seen on the flats. Toward the secondary extraction point that appeared on none of their maps.

Imani lay awake after that, listening to the camp breathe, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for days. Eleven sets of footprints walking in unison. A leader who counted his people nightly. A route chosen to avoid witnesses. She remembered the torn page from the camp journal—the one she'd taken, the one with Halloran's own handwriting listing *contingencies*—and the arithmetic finished itself, ugly and simple.

*Rescue* was a word other people used. From inside, it was called extraction. And extractions could be scheduled around weather, around logistics, around whether the cargo was worth the freight.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bone.

They struck camp in the practiced silence of people who had stopped talking to each other, and Imani took her place in the column because taking her place was what kept her invisible. Ahead of her, Halloran walked with his compass open, and behind her Osei carried the medical kit and carried, too, the question he'd asked her in the dark two nights ago: *"If we run, where do we run to?"* She hadn't had an answer then. She had one now, and it was a woman on a ridge line somewhere west with dust-colored clothes and eyes that missed nothing.

Because Imani had seen her. Two nights ago, at the edge of the firelight—a figure standing off beyond the gear cache, alone, watching them march their hollow-eyed circuit. Halloran had ordered everyone inside the perimeter, had doubled the watch, had called it a security protocol. But Imani had lingered at the tent flap, and the figure had turned, and moonlight had caught the glint of field glasses, and then—she was almost sure, she had to be sure—the watcher had raised one hand. Not a wave. Something smaller. An acknowledgment. *I see you. Do you see me?*

So while the column snaked along the base of the escarpment, Imani let her hand drift to her pack's side pocket, where the signal mirror lived between a water tin and a roll of tape. She'd rehearsed the angle forty times in her head. Sun was climbing behind them, east-northeast, which meant the flash would carry west, toward the broken country where a ranger would be tracking them. One chance per pass. Any repeat flashes and Halloran's watchers would find the source.

She waited until the column crossed a shallow wash, until the terrain dipped and every head dropped below the ridgeline except hers—she'd volunteered for rear guard, another small treason disguised as diligence—and then she tilted the mirror.

Once. Twice. Three short, one long. It wasn't any code she officially knew. It was the code her father had taught her on fishing boats, the one for *come quick, all is well, come anyway.*

The flash leapt off the mirror and ran up the canyon walls across the flats, a white stitch through the morning, and was gone. Imani lowered her hand and kept walking, heart slamming, face blank, rear guard on a prison column marching east toward a place with no name on the map.

Behind them, far to the west, the horizon had begun to change. She'd noticed it at first light and told no one: a bruise-colored band sitting on the edge of the world, too low and too wide for cloud, too dark for heat shimmer. She'd grown up on the coast. She knew what a front looked like when it was still a rumor.

The monsoon was early. Days early. And Halloran's eastern route crossed every drainage basin between here and the borderlands.

That was when she saw it—faint, at the limit of sight, on the western ridge they'd left behind at dawn: a single flash answering hers. One long, three short. *Coming.*

Imani exhaled for what felt like the first time in nine days, and made a decision that felt less like courage than like finally setting down something heavy: whatever happened next, she was done being managed.

At noon halt, she drifted to Osei's side while he checked blisters, and under the cover of tearing a bandage wrapper she murmured, "Tonight. When the watch changes. Bring Vashti."

Osei's hands didn't stop moving. "Bring her where?"

"Wherever the ranger is," Imani said. "She's coming for us. And Halloran's route goes straight through the flood path."

Osei looked up then, and in his eyes she watched the same arithmetic finish that had finished in hers—the rescue that wasn't, the route that was a trap, the gentle man with the census voice. He tied off the bandage, neat as a knot in a story, and said only:

"Then we'd better learn to walk fast."

Far to the west, the bruise on the horizon deepened, and began, very slowly, to grow.
