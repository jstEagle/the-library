## 7. Water on the Horizon

The sky told me first.

I'd been reading that sky for seven years, and it had never once lied to me. It sat low over the eastern rim like a bruise coming up under skin—green-gray at the base, yellow where the light tried to get through, a color I'd only seen twice before in my life and both times it had meant water with teeth. The monsoon was supposed to be nine days out. The barometer at the station had said so. The state's own weather service had said so.

The desert didn't care what the state said.

I stood on the rise above the mining camp with my binoculars up and watched the front build itself out of nothing, stacking cloud on cloud, and behind me eleven people went about their morning like the world wasn't tilting. Osei was repacking the medical kit with the methodical hands of a man who organized things when he was scared. Imani sat apart with Halloran's journal pages copied into her own notebook, writing fast, hiding fast whenever anyone passed. And Halloran stood at the edge of camp looking at the same sky I was, and I could tell from the set of his shoulders that he saw it too.

Good. At least we'd all be lying to each other with our eyes open.

I came down off the rise. The team watched me the way they always did now—not quite hostile, not quite trusting, the way you watch weather. Eleven sets of hollow eyes. They'd been walking in their sleep for days before I caught them, and even awake there was something borrowed about them, something on loan. All except Imani. Whatever lived down in those shafts had gotten its hooks into the others and missed her, or she'd torn loose, or she'd never let it in. She looked at me like I was a door.

"Dr. Okonkwo," Halloran said, without turning. He still used the rank like a leash. "You're staring at the horizon."

"Somebody should be."

"The forecast puts the front at the ninth."

"The forecast is a piece of paper." I stopped beside him, close enough to see the sweat at his temple that he pretended wasn't there. "That front is three days out. Maybe four if the canyons slow it. It's going to come down the basin like God emptying a bucket, and every wash between here and the station is going to run ten feet deep with stuff moving in it that used to be somebody's truck."

He finally turned. Up close his calm had cracks in it, hairline fractures running through the official composure, and I understood suddenly that he wasn't calm at all—he was rigid. A man holding a shape because letting go of it would kill him.

"You have an alternative," he said. Not a question. He'd been waiting for me to propose one, which meant he'd already decided to refuse it, which meant whatever he offered next was already loaded.

"I have a route." I crouched and drew in the dirt with a stick, because some conversations you have to have with your hands. "South and east, through the canyon country. Slot canyons, slickrock, high benches. It's brutal—forty miles of it, no shade, no water except what we carry, and two places where we rope the whole team up a face. But the floods don't run uphill. We stay on stone above the drainages, we move ahead of the water instead of racing it, and we come out at Red Gap two days before the low country drowns."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we're on high ground when I'm wrong, instead of in a wash when you're right."

Behind us the team had gone quiet. They were listening. Of course they were listening—I was offering them a way home and Halloran was offering them a mission, and missions were what had marched them across the flats in perfect unison with no tracks behind them. I kept my eyes on the map in the dirt and gave them time to look at it.

"It means trusting me," I said. "All of you. Every decision for forty miles goes through me and nobody else. No committee. No chain of command. If I say drop the packs and climb, you climb. If I say we hole up in a slot while a flood roars past six feet below us, you sit in the dark and listen to it and you don't panic." I looked up at Halloran. "Can your people do that?"

"They're surveyors, not sheep."

"They're sleepwalkers who woke up in a nightmare, and they'll follow whoever gives them the next right thing to do. So I'm asking you to let that person be me."

For a long moment the only sound was the wind coming off the east, and it smelled wrong—wet and mineral, like a struck match, like the air before the world breaks open. Then Halloran smiled. It was a small smile, controlled, almost kind, and it frightened me more than the sky did, because I knew that smile. I'd worn it once. It was the smile of a man about to name a price he was certain you couldn't refuse.

"There's another route," he said quietly. "One nobody has to die on."

* * *

He walked me away from camp, out past the last of the equipment, to where the old tailings pile blocked the view of the others. He did it smoothly, conversationally, a man stretching his legs, and I let him, because I wanted to hear the offer. That was the shameful part. Some traitor corner of me wanted to hear it.

"August Okonkwo," he said. "Sergeant, Third Recon Patrol, Kestrel Basin action, seven years ago. Your file is thin. Too thin. Files that thin are files someone paid to make thin." He studied my face. "I know what you did. Or rather, I know what was done for you. The patrol record erased. The after-action report rewritten. A young soldier walked out of a catastrophe that killed five of her comrades, and by the time anyone thought to ask how, the paperwork said there was never a catastrophe at all."

The ground didn't move. The sky didn't fall. I'd spent seven years waiting for this moment to feel like something, and it felt like standing in a room where the furniture had been rearranged—everything familiar, everything wrong.

"You've read my file," I said. "Congratulations. So has everyone who ever cleared me for a posting."

"No one else has read it. That's the point." He turned to face me fully, and I saw the machinery underneath him now, plain as gears—the same machinery I'd served, the same quiet men with quiet pens who decided what the record would hold. "I can finish the job they started. Full scrub. Not just sealed—scrubbed. No Kestrel Basin. No Third Recon. As far as any database on earth is concerned, August Okonkwo enlisted at twenty-two with a clean jacket and served without incident until her honorable discharge. You'd be free. Actually free, for the first time in seven years. No more running. No more flinching when someone says the word *patrol*."

The wind gusted, carrying that wet-mineral smell, and somewhere far off thunder spoke in a voice too deep for the distance.

"And the price," I said.

"You walk south. Alone. Today. You report that you found the survey team dead of exposure—a tragedy, the state will mourn, there'll be a plaque. The team comes with me by the low road, we reach the station, we file our findings, and the landfall maps stay exactly as accurate as the government requires them to be." He paused. "And Imani Reyes keeps her career. Because if you force this through the canyon country and we survive it, she becomes the geologist who helped leak classified findings, and the state does not write plaques for people like that. They write case files."

There it was. Not the threat—the gift wrapped around the threat. He'd done his research. He hadn't just found my sin; he'd found the shape of my soul, the exact weight of the guilt I carried, and he'd built an offer to fit it. Walk away and be forgiven. Walk away and save the girl. Walk away and stop running, forever, by agreeing to run one more time.

Seven years ago I would have taken it. Seven years ago I *had* taken it, in a filing office with a pen shaking in my hand, and every mile since had been the interest accruing on that principal.

"You want to know the funny part?" I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "I spent years thinking the worst thing I ever did was erase those names. It took me a long time to understand that wasn't it. The worst thing was believing the erasure worked. Believing I could file my way out of being a person who did that." I looked at him. "You're offering me a clean record, Dr. Halloran. There's no such thing. There's just a longer ledger."

"So that's a refusal."

"That's a refusal."

He nodded slowly, and something in his face closed like a door, and I understood that the offer had been the last civil exchange we were ever going to have. "Then I'm obligated to inform you that the low road remains the team's best chance of survival, and I'll be recommending it."

"Recommend away. They don't follow you anymore. You know that, right? They stopped following you the day you ordered them back into the shafts." I started past him, then stopped. "One more thing. When the water comes—and it's coming—you'll be standing next to me on the same rock. Whatever you think of me, whatever you think I am, when the flood hits, my craft is the only thing keeping any of us breathing. Including yours. Think about whether you want to spend the crossing making an enemy of the person holding the rope."

I left him among the tailings and walked back toward camp, toward eleven faces turning to me against the green-black sky.

Imani met me halfway, notebook clutched to her chest, eyes flicking between me and the horizon. "What did he offer you?"

"Clean slate."

"And?"

"And I told him the slate doesn't exist." I shouldered my pack and looked at the canyon country lying south and east, red stone stacked against the bruised sky, forty miles of the hardest ground in the territory. "Pack light. We leave in twenty minutes. Anyone who wants to take Halloran's low road, now's the time to say so."

Nobody moved. Osei snapped his medical kit shut. Imani tucked the notebook into her shirt. One by one, eleven people picked up their packs and faced south, and Dr. Halloran stood alone among the tailings for a long moment before he, too, bent and lifted his bag.

The first drops wouldn't fall for three days. But the wind had already changed, and as we started toward the canyons I heard it coming behind us like something waking up.
