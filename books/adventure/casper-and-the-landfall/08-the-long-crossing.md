## 8. The Long Crossing

**Imani**

The canyons began where the map ended, which Imani understood now was not a coincidence.

August had drawn the route in the dirt with a stick at dawn: eleven days of slot canyons and dry washes, climbing northeast ahead of the water. "The monsoon's coming up from the south," she'd said, tapping the stick against a stone like it was an enemy's jaw. "Floods run downhill faster than we do. So we don't race them. We climb out of their way and let them pass beneath us." She'd looked at each of them in turn, all ten of them plus Halloran, waiting for someone to object. Nobody had. Even Halloran had said nothing, which from him was a kind of surrender.

By the third day Imani had learned to read August's silences. A flat hand meant stop. Two fingers pointed down-slope meant water coming, get high. The woman spoke maybe forty words a day and every one of them was load-bearing.

She'd also learned the team again — or met them for the first time, really. Seven years of survey work and she'd never once seen Osei laugh until he tripped over a slick of red mud on day two and came up wearing half the canyon. She'd never heard Chen sing until the nights got cold enough that they huddled in pairs against the rock and his voice, thin and off-key, held them together like wire. The shaft had made them into strangers to themselves; the canyon was unmaking that, one hour at a time.

It was Osei who noticed the sound first, on the fourth morning.

They were walking the floor of a wash two hundred meters wide, gravel underfoot, walls rising sheer on either side. The sky above was a hard, cloudless blue — too blue, Imani thought, the way a room is too quiet. Then Osei stopped and tilted his head.

"Does anyone else hear—"

August's hand came up flat before he finished.

"Up," she said. "Now. Everyone. *Up.*"

There was no arguing with her voice. It wasn't loud. It was the voice of someone who had already done the math and gotten an answer she didn't like. They scrambled for the ledges, Imani hauling Chen by the wrist, August shoving the slowest ones ahead of her, and Imani had just gained the shelf when she heard it come around the bend behind them.

Not a roar. That was the terrible thing. She'd expected a roar. It was more like a freight train made of gravel — a deep grinding churn, and then the wall of it came into view: brown water thick as wet concrete, carrying whole boulders, a dead juniper spinning end over end like a toy. It filled the wash wall to wall in seconds, and where they had been walking there was nothing but churning brown violence.

Imani pressed herself against the rock and felt it vibrate through her chest, through her teeth. Beside her, Chen was shaking. Below, August stood on the ledge with her arms crossed, watching the flood with the expression of someone reading a familiar letter.

"How did you know?" Imani asked. "The sky was clear."

"Rain fell sixty kilometers upstream an hour ago," August said. "Desert doesn't warn you where you're standing. It warns you somewhere else." She glanced back at them, and for one unguarded second Imani saw something almost gentle in her face. "Nobody drinks without me checking the side canyons. Nobody walks point but me. You'll live longer if you treat my paranoia as scripture."

Halloran opened his mouth — Imani saw the order forming, some reflex about schedule or protocol — and then closed it. He looked at the flood instead, and whatever he saw there kept him quiet.

**Osei**

Osei had been a combat medic before he'd been a geologist, and he had carried men out of places no vehicle could reach, so he knew what a body looked like when it started spending its reserves. By the sixth day he was counting them like coins: Adeyemi's blistered heels, Pak's dehydration headache, the tremor in Mrs. Okonkwo's hands — no, August's hands, he corrected himself; the woman had earned the name — and worst of all, Dr. Halloran's eyes, which had gone from commanding to calculating to something Osei recognized from field hospitals: a man triaging himself out of the casualty list.

He said nothing about it. Medics learn early which wounds to dress and which to watch.

On the seventh day the canyon narrowed to a slot barely wider than a doorway, walls of sculpted sandstone going up and up until the sky was a ribbon of white. Beautiful, Osei thought, in the way of things designed to kill you. August went through first, alone, and was gone twenty minutes. When she came back her face was set.

"Water's pooled waist-deep through the next hundred meters," she said. "Cold enough to drop your core temp fast. We go in single file, packs held overhead, and we move. Nobody stops in the water."

They went. The cold hit Osei like a fist — meltwater and shadow, a cold that had never once seen the sun — and he counted heads as they came through the far side, the way he always did. Ten. Eleven. Ten.

He counted again. Ten.

"Chen," he said. "Where's Chen?"

August was already turning. Down the slot, Chen stood hip-deep in the pool, frozen, his pack still dry on his head, his face gray. Cramp, Osei thought. Cold-shock cramp, the kind that locks a leg solid.

"I'll get him," Halloran said — and then didn't move. He looked down the slot, and Osei watched something happen in the man's face, some final arithmetic where Chen's weight came out negative. "We can't risk the group going back in. If another surge comes down while we're in that water—"

"He's twenty meters away," Osei said.

"And we're eleven people who all die if the timing's wrong." Halloran's voice was calm, reasonable, the voice of every officer who had ever written a letter home. "I'm sorry. I'm making the command decision."

And August was already moving.

No word, no look back. She just stepped off the ledge and waded into the black water like it owed her money, and Osei watched her reach Chen, watched her say something to him — he couldn't hear what, but Chen's face changed, unlocked — and then she got her shoulder under his arm and brought him through the cold in a steady, brutal rhythm, hauling him the last meters with hands that did not shake at all.

When she pulled him onto the gravel, Osei was there with the foil blanket, working his legs, getting blood moving. Chen was crying and laughing at once. And August stood dripping in the sun, wringing out her sleeves, looking at Halloran with an expression Osei would remember for the rest of his life.

Not anger. Recognition. Like she'd finally confirmed a diagnosis she'd suspected for days.

"That's twice now," she said quietly, "that this canyon has tried to take someone. First time it failed because I listened to it. Second time it failed because I ignored a man who'd stopped listening to anything but himself." She turned away. "We camp high tonight. Move at first light."

Nobody looked at Halloran. That was the verdict, Osei thought. Not shouted, not voted. Just — nobody looking at him anymore. An authority dies exactly that quietly, out here. One day it's a voice giving orders; the next it's a man standing alone in a canyon, talking to people who have already left.

That night Osei sat beside August at the fire line while the others slept. She was drying her boots, methodical, unhurried.

"You've done this before," he said. "Left someone. Or been left."

She didn't answer for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through stone with a sound like breathing.

"There was a patrol," she said finally. "Seven years ago. Eleven names." Her jaw worked. "I know the number because I'm traveling with it."

Osei looked back at the sleeping shapes — ten of them, plus the woman who'd gone back into the water. He didn't ask anything else. Some confessions are doors; you don't push them open. You just stand ready when they swing.

* * *

**August**

The ninth day, the desert told her the truth she'd been dreading.

She read it in the small things first: the air pressure sitting on her eardrums like a thumb, the ants abandoning their lines, a smell of green and ozone riding a wind that should have smelled of nothing but stone. By noon the horizon to the south had gone the color of a bruise, a wall of cloud stacked black and violet, and August stood on a rise doing arithmetic she hated.

The front had turned. It was cutting the angle, moving faster than any forecast model she'd ever trusted, and the canyon mouth — safety, rangers, radios, the world — was two days' march through country that turned into a killing funnel the moment the rain started.

She gathered them under an overhang and laid it out plain, because these people had earned plain speech.

"Two options. We stay in the deep canyons and hope the floods channel past us. Maybe they do. Or we cut cross-country over the mesa, exposed, in the open, and make the mouth before the front does." She let them look at the southern sky. "Option one is comfortable right now and probably fatal tomorrow. Option two is miserable immediately and might save us. I'm asking, not telling. This is your lives."

It was Imani who answered first. "You've kept us alive this long."

"It's your vote, not mine."

"Then we go over the mesa," Imani said, and one by one the others nodded, and August felt something in her chest she hadn't felt since before the patrol, since before the record, since before she'd become a woman who only moved away from things: she was being *trusted*, and it fit worse and better than guilt ever had.

They climbed through the afternoon, the storm wall growing behind them like a tide, lightning flickering inside it in silent white veins. August pushed them hard and hated herself for it and pushed anyway. By dusk they'd gained the mesa top, a broad plateau of wind-scoured rock with a single notch of shelter — an old overhang, shallow, barely enough — and she called the halt because bodies fail in the dark even when wills don't.

They crowded into the overhang as the first drops came, fat and warm and smelling of dust. Then harder. Then a deluge, the mesa vanishing into gray, thunder rolling across the plateau like the land clearing its throat.

August sat at the lip of the shelter, watching the water sheet off the rock, and did the last of the math. The mesa drained north. The canyon mouth lay beyond the drainage. If they moved at first light, ahead of the crest, they had a chance — a real one, narrow as the slot they'd survived.

Behind her, boots on stone. Halloran, standing apart from the sleepers, rain-shadowed, watching her.

"You could still walk away," he said. "The offer stands. Clean record. Your old patrol erased — properly erased, state-level, the way you wanted it seven years ago. All you have to do is stop delivering us."

August didn't turn around. She watched the rain hammer the mesa and thought about eleven names, and ten sleeping shapes, and a young geologist who'd handed her stolen pages in the dark because she'd decided the truth was worth more than her career.

"You misunderstand what I'm selling," she said. "I stopped running the moment I went back into that water."

Lightning lit the plateau white, and in the flash August saw it — down at the mesa's northern edge, where the drainage began: water. Already moving. A tongue of flood curling around the rocks below them, hours ahead of anything she'd calculated, the desert changing the terms one last time.

She stood up, and her voice carried over the rain without her raising it.

"Everyone up. Now. The flood found us first."
