## 9. Landfall

The canyon mouth opened onto the flats like a wound, and through it the monsoon came.

August had seen storms build for a day before they broke—seen the sky stack itself in gray shelves, heard thunder roll across the salt like furniture dragged across an upper floor. This was not that. This was a wall of water and wind arriving all at once, a brown horizon standing on end, and it was coming down the canyon behind them with the sound of a freight train that had jumped its tracks.

"Move!" she screamed, and didn't wait to see who obeyed.

They spilled out of the canyon mouth into the last hundred meters of open ground, eleven of them now instead of twelve—Osei carrying the medic kit, Imani clutching the wrapped records to her chest like a child, the others stumbling in a ragged line that no longer looked anything like Halloran's perfect unison. Three days of flood-chased crossing had broken whatever held them together, and what remained was something rawer and better: people who had chosen each other.

Halloran was last out of the canyon. He had been last since the straggler—the young surveyor named Beck, whose ankle had gone on the second day, whom Halloran had ordered left behind while August went back into the rising water and hauled him out over her shoulder. Since then Halloran walked apart, silent, his authority bleeding out of him with every kilometer. But he still carried the flare gun from the supply cache, and August had watched his eyes track Imani's bundle all morning.

She should have taken it from him at dawn. She told herself there hadn't been time. The truth was she'd wanted to believe he was finished.

The rain hit them at the edge of the flats, a first slap of fat warm drops, then a deluge so total the world went white with noise. August turned to count heads—eleven, all present—and saw Halloran stop ten meters back, at the mouth of the canyon, where the water was already sheeting past his boots in knee-deep pulses.

He raised the flare gun toward Imani's bundle.

"No—" August was moving before the word finished leaving her.

The shot went wide. Whether his hand shook or hers struck his arm in time, she never learned; the flare screamed off into the rain and died somewhere over the salt. Then they were both down in the floodwater, and the gun was gone from his hand, skittering away on the current, and Halloran's fingers were at her throat—not killing, not yet, but holding, the way a man holds a door shut against a wind.

"You don't understand," he shouted over the roar. "Those records end the landfall program. They end the resettlement contracts. Do you know how many people are paid to believe those maps?"

"I know how many people die believing them!" She drove her elbow into his sternum and felt him fold. "I've buried them!"

* * *

Later, she would try to reconstruct the order of things. The rain. The team closing around them in a wet, shouting ring. Osei pulling Halloran off her. Imani standing over the records with her arms spread, screaming something August couldn't hear. The water rising past their shins, the canyon behind them vomiting brown foam, the whole desert turning to river around their one narrow island of higher ground.

What she remembered clearly was Halloran on his knees in the flood, chest heaving, looking up at her with something worse than hatred. Recognition.

"You were military," he said. "I read your file when the state flagged your patrol route. Seven years ago. Second Recon Battalion." He laughed, a wet, broken sound. "You erased people too."

The words went through her like the flare had gone through the rain—fast, bright, gone somewhere she couldn't follow.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said, and heard her own voice do the thing it always did: flatten, professionalize, retreat behind procedure. Seven years of practice. It had never once worked on anyone who mattered.

Imani stepped closer. Water streamed off her hood. "What file?" she said. "August. What is he talking about?"

"There was a patrol," Halloran said, savoring it now, a drowning man trading secrets for air. "Kandahar Province, if the file's accurate. Nine soldiers walked into a valley the state had already written off. Command needed the incident closed clean. One corporal rewrote the after-action log. Struck nine names from the record. Reported them as reassigned." He smiled with blood on his teeth. "The state rewarded her. Gave her a new posting, a new name, a career. And the families waited for letters that never came, because as far as any record showed, those soldiers were alive and simply not writing home."

"That's a lie," Osei said—but he said it carefully, watching August's face.

August stood in the flood and felt seven years come due.

She could deny it. That was the old reflex, the deep one, the one that had carried her out of the army and into the ranger service and across a thousand kilometers of empty salt: keep moving, keep the story small, outrun the weight. She had built a whole life on the principle that a lie carried light if you never set it down.

But Imani was looking at her. Osei was looking at her. Beck, half-carried, propped against a rock with his ruined ankle, was looking at her. Eleven people who had spent three days learning exactly what it cost to be erased by someone else's decision about what the record should say.

And the water kept rising.

"It's true," August said.

The rain filled the silence for a moment, enormous, indifferent.

"Nine names," she said. "I wrote the report myself. I was twenty-three and command told me it was mercy—that the truth would get more people killed, that the valley was compromised, that closure was a kindness. I believed them because believing them meant I got to live with it." Her voice cracked and she let it. "Their families sent letters to a reassignment office for years. Someone answered the first few. After that, nothing. As far as the world knew, they were alive and had stopped loving anyone enough to write."

"August," Imani said softly.

"I ran," August said. "Seven years. I picked the flattest, emptiest country I could find because empty country doesn't ask questions. And then I found your camp, and I found gear sitting in circles with no tracks leaving, and every instinct I had said *this is what you did, this is what it looks like from the outside*, and I almost rode away. I want you to know that. I almost rode away."

"But you didn't," Imani said.

"No." August looked at Halloran, still kneeling, still watching her with that terrible recognition. "Because you weren't ghosts. You were alive. And I have spent seven years being loyal to a lie, and I am done with it. Whatever this costs—all of us, right now—it costs less than the other thing."

* * *

Halloran made one more play for the records. Of course he did. He lunged for Imani's bundle as the water surged, and it was Beck—Beck, the man Halloran had ordered abandoned—who caught his wrist and held it, and then Osei, and then three others, not striking him, just holding, the way you hold a panicking animal in deep water until it remembers how to float. He stopped fighting. He wept, briefly, without dignity, and August found she couldn't hate him for it. He was only the far end of the road she had been walking herself: a person who had decided the record mattered more than the people in it, and had followed that decision all the way down into the dark.

They bound his hands with a length of climbing webbing—not cruelty, just arithmetic, because the records were going to survive and he had proven he would drown everyone to prevent it.

Then the flood peaked.

It came as a single great pulse down the canyon, a brown wall two meters high, and August had maybe four seconds to see it and make the only call there was. "Rocks! High ground, now!" She shoved Imani toward the shelf of stone above the canyon mouth, grabbed Beck under the arms, and the team moved—moved together, the way they hadn't moved in three days, the way Halloran's unison had never taught them because unison is obedience and this was something else. The wave broke against the rocks below them and exploded into spray, and the flats beyond vanished entirely, and for a long minute the world was nothing but white noise and nine people clinging to stone with their arms around each other.

When it receded, the sun broke through the storm's trailing edge, low and gold, and lit the flooded salt flat so brightly it hurt to look at. Somewhere out there was the ranger station. Two days' walk, on high ground the water wouldn't reach.

Imani sat down beside August on the rock, the records safe between her boots, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"The report you're going to file," Imani said finally. "It'll have everything. The shafts. The maps. Him." A nod toward Halloran, slumped and silent under Osei's watch. "And you."

"And me," August said.

"They might hang you for it."

"They might." August watched the water draining silver off the flats, carrying the desert seaward grain by grain. Seven years ago she had written nine names out of existence and called it survival. Tomorrow she would write them back in—her own name beside theirs, in the same ink, in the same honest record—and call it whatever it was. Not redemption. Just the bill, finally paid, in the only currency that counted.

"Nine names," she said. "I remember all of them. I've always remembered all of them."

Imani put her hand over August's, wet and cold and steady.

"Then let's go give them back," she said, and stood, and the team gathered itself around them, and they started walking toward the station through the steaming aftermath of the storm—while behind them, at the drowned mouth of the canyon, something glinted in the receding floodwater that none of them had ever seen before, half-buried in the mud the monsoon had dredged up from underneath the world.
