## 10. What We Carry Out

The rain came for three days and then stopped, the way monsoons do, like a guest who has eaten everything in the house. Afterward the flats steamed. The air smelled of wet mineral and creosote bush, and August Okonkwo stood on the porch of the ranger station at Landfall Post with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands, watching water find its way back to the salt.

Seven years she had kept this station. Seven years of patrol logs written in her small, careful hand, each one a brick in the wall she'd built between herself and a night in the army she never spoke about. The wall was gone now. She could feel the wind moving through where it used to be.

Inside, the survivors were still asleep. Osei in the bunk room, his medic's bag finally unpacked, its contents drying on a line he'd strung above the stove. Voss and Adeyemi sharing the last of the tea, arguing softly about whether the canyon route had been luck or judgment. Imani Reyes at the table with the recovered records spread around her like a hand of cards she intended to play to the end.

And Halloran, in the cell August had locked him in two nights ago, waiting for the state's people to come collect him.

The report sat on her desk. Forty pages. She'd been writing it since dawn, and every page had cost her something.

* * *

She started with the shafts because that was easy. The pre-monsoon civilization, the archive chambers, the maps that showed the landfall line drawn not by nature but by committee—coastlines moved, floodplains erased, whole settlements written out of the record so the state wouldn't have to pay for the levees. She documented the survey team's findings, the photographs Osei had shielded with his own body when the flash flood took his pack, the core samples Imani had carried out strapped to her back through eleven miles of rising water.

That part was duty. Any ranger would have filed it.

Then she turned to a fresh page and wrote the harder thing.

*On the fourteenth of March, seven years ago, I was the last surviving member of Patrol Nine. The official record states that the patrol was lost to a whiteout storm on the northern escarpment. That is false. I wrote that report myself. I survived because I left them.*

She had rehearsed this confession for years without knowing it—in the silence of the cab on long patrols, in the dark before sleep. But writing it down was different. Writing made it evidence. Writing made it hers.

*I falsified our position in the log before the storm hit. Command believed we were twenty miles east of where we actually were. When the search went east, I did not correct them. Sergeant Amara Diallo was alive when I last saw her. I have spent seven years not knowing if that was mercy or murder, and I am done pretending the question has an answer that lets me sleep.*

Her pen stopped. Outside, a roadrunner crossed the yard with something bright in its beak.

The state would come for this. She understood that now better than ever. Halloran hadn't been an aberration; he'd been a function. The same machinery that had buried the shafts had buried Patrol Nine, and she had fed it willingly, gratefully, because the alternative was standing in the wreckage of what she'd done. Erasure was the currency. She had paid in other people.

She signed the page. Then she walked to the cell block.

* * *

Halloran looked smaller than he had in the canyons. Stripped of his authority, he was just a tired man in a ruined field jacket, and for a moment August felt the old pull—the instinct to look away from broken things, to keep moving.

"You could still burn it," he said. "One page. Your page. File the rest, keep your secret. They'll offer you that much. They offered me a career once for less."

"I know what they'll offer." She slid the report through the bars, let him see the signature. "I spent seven years taking deals like that. It's how you end up here."

"Here is a cell," he said. "You're standing outside it. For now."

"For now." She almost smiled. "You told your team the rescue was never planned. You told them they were containment assets. You were right about the state, Halloran. You were just wrong about the ending. Containment failed the moment Imani copied those pages, and it fails again every time someone reads my report. You can't bury a thing once it's being carried."

He looked at her for a long time. "Diallo," he said quietly. "I read your file when they assigned me. I knew who you were. They sent me because they thought your guilt would make you manageable."

"It nearly did." She turned to go.

"Okonkwo." His voice caught her at the door. "The search for Patrol Nine went east because of you. But there was a second search. Unofficial. Diallo's sister funded it herself. It found nothing, because there was nothing east to find." He paused. "West of your false position, the terrain was survivable. There were caves."

August stood very still.

"That's not absolution," Halloran said. "It's just information. I've had seven years to learn the difference."

She left him with the report and walked out into the steam and light, and her hands were shaking, and she let them shake.

* * *

Imani was waiting on the porch, the journal pages squared and bound with wire. Behind her, one by one, the others came out—Osei, Voss, Adeyemi, the rest, drawn by some gravity none of them named.

"They'll be here by evening," Imani said. "The state's people. Helicopters, probably. Lawyers definitely." She held up the bound pages. "Copies exist. Three sets. One goes to my mother in Meridian City. One goes to a journalist Osei trusts. And one—" she pressed it into August's hands, "—goes wherever you think it will do the most damage."

"The most good," August said.

"I said what I said."

Osei came down the steps and stood beside her, arms folded, a man planting himself. "Whatever they take from you—badge, post, freedom—we were there. All of it. We'll testify."

"You don't owe me that."

"We owe Diallo," Osei said simply. "All of us owe the erased ones. That's the whole job now."

Voss grinned, tired and crooked. "Besides, nobody else knows how to make coffee at this station. The place would fall apart."

August looked at them—at the ragged, flooded, half-starved remains of the team that wasn't supposed to come home—and felt something she hadn't felt since before the escarpment. Not peace. Peace was too clean a word. Weight, maybe. The right weight, carried with the right people.

"I'm filing it as written," she said. "Every word. Including mine."

"Then we stand there when you do," Imani said.

* * *

At noon she transmitted the report from the station's old radio array, reading it aloud into the static because the encryption module had drowned in the canyon and she wanted no ambiguity, no version control, no quiet edits. Her voice went out over the flats raw and unencrypted: the shafts, the lies, the names of the erased settlements, and then, in the same flat tone, the name of Patrol Nine and her own confession appended to it like a wound dressed in public.

When she finished, the desert was silent except for the tick of the cooling transmitter.

Imani touched her shoulder. "There," she said. "Now it's being carried."

They came at dusk, as predicted. Two helicopters, low and black against the bruised eastern sky, kicking up salt dust as they circled the station. A state liaison stepped out with a folder under his arm and a smile that didn't reach his eyes, and behind him, men whose job titles would never appear on any manifest.

August met them on the landing pad with the team flanked behind her, shoulder to shoulder, and the copies already gone—three directions, three couriers, three chances the truth would outrun whatever happened next.

"Ranger Okonkwo." The liaison opened his folder. "We're here regarding your transmission. There are matters in your report that require... contextual review. If you'll come with us voluntarily, I'm authorized to discuss certain adjustments. Your service record, for instance. There are older entries that might be—"

"Scrubbed?" August said. "I know. I've heard the offer before. From a man currently sitting in my cell."

The liaison's smile thinned. "Then you understand the alternative."

"I understand exactly what staying costs." She glanced back at Imani, at Osei, at the others who had not moved an inch. "For the first time in seven years, I'm paying it."

They took her then—not gently, but not roughly either, because cameras existed even here—and as the helicopter lifted off the pad, August looked down at the station shrinking beneath her: the porch, the antenna, the small figures standing in the yard who had chosen to be counted.

West, beyond the flats, the escarpment caught the last light. Somewhere out there were caves, and maybe answers, and a sister who had searched alone.

*Hold on,* she thought, pressing her palm flat against the cold window. *I'm done running. I'm coming to find what's left.*
