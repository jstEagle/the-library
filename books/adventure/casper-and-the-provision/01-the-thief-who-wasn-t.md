## 1. The Thief Who Wasn't

The thief was bad at his trade, which was the only reason I caught him, and worse at walking, which was the only reason I didn't lose him first.

Three days across the Corrow salt flats and he'd left a trail a blind man could follow — broken crust where he cut corners, a tin cup abandoned at the second well like an offering to nobody. Small-time men steal small-time things: a purse, a bolt of cloth, somebody's grandmother's ring. This one had stolen from Harrow Fen's provision house, which meant he'd stolen food from people who counted every grain twice. That made it my business. Not because I cared about Harrow Fen's grains. Because in the flats, theft that goes unanswered becomes habit, and habit becomes a gang, and gangs become the kind of arithmetic that gets rangers killed.

I'm not a ranger anymore, strictly speaking. I still carry the badge because the badge opens doors and closes mouths, and because a man needs to be something, even if what he is, is a lie with a tin star on it.

His name turned out to be Sefa. I learned it later, shouted at me across a river by a woman who wanted her brother's debt settled before she'd talk. The thief himself never offered a name. He just walked ahead of me for three days, unaware, humming through cracked lips, stopping to pry salt crystals off the crust and suck them like sweets. Once he sat down in the open and ate half a dried fig with the unhurried dignity of a man who believed the world owed him lunch.

I let him. A fed thief walks farther than a frightened one, and I wanted to see where he was going. Nobody crosses the Corrow for pleasure. Nobody crosses it for anything but the river on the far side — the Serpent's Rest, they call it now, though it had another name when I knew it, back when the land it drowned was above water and called Veymar.

That thought should have stopped me sooner than it did. But dread is a patient animal. It doesn't bark. It just sits down beside you on the trail and waits until you're too tired to pretend you don't feel it.

* * *

He reached the river at dusk on the third day, and I nearly lost him in the reeds because dusk on the flats comes fast and mean, the sun dropping like something pushed. I closed the last hundred yards at a crouch, hand on the rope coil at my hip, running the speech I'd give him. *You'll come back to Harrow Fen. You'll stand before the provision council. You'll return what's left.* Routine words. Routine arrest. Ten years of routine, each one laid down like flagstones over the thing I used to be, so I could walk across my own history without falling through.

"Evening," I said, stepping into the open. "You've got provisions in that pack that belong to other people."

He spun, and I'll give him this — he didn't run. He looked at me, then at the badge, then past me at the empty flats as if hoping I'd brought witnesses I could embarrass myself in front of.

"You walked three days behind me," he said. His voice was young. Younger than his face. "For a sack of meal?"

"For the principle."

"The principle." He almost laughed. "Out here?" He gestured at the flats behind us, all that white nothing going violet in the dying light. "There's no principle past the last well, ranger. There's just who's hungry and who isn't."

"There's law."

"Whose?" And there it was — the question under the question, the one I'd spent a decade not hearing. He didn't wait for an answer. He looked at the water instead, and something in his face changed, some private arithmetic completing itself, and he said, quietly, mostly to himself: "Low tide. It's low tide. Of course it's low tide."

"Sefa," I said — because I'd done my asking in the towns behind us, and a name makes a man feel known, and a man who feels known talks. "Whatever you came out here for, it isn't worth drowning for."

"I wasn't going to drown." He was already backing toward the water, hands loose, the way a man stands when he's about to do something stupid and has made peace with it. "I was going to dive."

"Dive for what? There's nothing out there but mud and dead country."

"That's what everyone says." He smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, or a scared one. It was the smile of a man holding a map inside his head. "That's why it's still there."

Then he dropped the sack of stolen meal on the bank — dropped it, deliberately, like paying a toll — and dove.

* * *

I stood at the edge with the rope half-coiled and my speech still loaded, feeling like a man who's kicked open a door and found a room he recognizes from a dream.

The Serpent's Rest at low tide gives up its skirts. Mudflats emerge in ribs and ridges, and the water thins to knee-deep over places that were streets once, before the waters rose and the treaties failed and the fleet captains drew their maps with the ink still wet. I knew those maps. I'd carried two of them. That was the year I learned that a nation can be convicted the way a man can, on evidence and testimony, and that the verdict drowns everyone inside it equally.

Ten years I'd told myself the flats were safe. Empty. *Nothing out there but mud and dead country* — my own words, thirty seconds old, already rotting in my mouth. The miles I trusted, the whole geography of my exile, had been sitting on top of something the entire time. That's the thing about dread. It doesn't arrive. It surfaces.

He came up gasping, twenty yards out, hair plastered flat, and in his arms —

I want to say I knew immediately. I didn't. First I saw a chest, iron-banded, silt-caked, small enough for one man to carry badly, which he was doing, listing sideways like a drunk leaving a tavern. Then I saw the shape stamped into the lid where the mud had sloughed away, and the world went quiet in the particular way it does before bad news finishes arriving.

A tower. A wave beneath it. Three stars.

Veymar's seal. On a salvage chest, in a river that officially held nothing but the corpses of a condemned nation, ten years after the last court sealed the last record and sank the last truth.

"Put it down," I heard myself say. My voice came out level. I was proud of that, later. At the time I couldn't have told you whose voice it was.

Sefa stood waist-deep, hugging the chest, breathing hard. "It's mine," he said. "Finder's claim. Even your law honors that."

"Where did you learn it was there?"

"Does it matter?"

"Everything matters." I waded in without deciding to, the cold climbing my legs, the mud sucking at my boots like the river had opinions about visitors. "That seal hasn't been legal in ten years. Anything bearing it belongs to the Crown's salvage registry, and the registry sends fleets, and fleets send magistrates, and magistrates—" I stopped. Because I'd been about to say *magistrates ask questions*, and I knew exactly which magistrate the eastern fleet would send, because I'd served under him, back when I had another name and a different set of crimes.

Sefa watched me stop. He was sharper than his trail suggested. "You know them," he said. "The fleet."

"I know of them."

"No." He shook his head slowly, water dripping from his chin. "You know *them*. You went pale in a way the sun doesn't cause." He hugged the chest tighter. "What did Veymar do to you, ranger?"

"Veymar didn't do anything to me." The lie came out smooth and old, worn soft from handling. "Veymar drowned."

"Things don't drown," Sefa said. "They get sunk. There's a difference, and you know it, and so do I, because my father worked these waters before they were waters, and he died saying the same thing your face is saying right now."

The tide was turning. I could feel it in the current's new insistence against my knees, the river beginning to take back what it had lent. Somewhere out beyond the flats, faint as a headache, I heard a horn — one long note, then two short. Fleet signal. Salvage crews marking a find, or marking territory, or both.

Sefa heard it too. His eyes went wide, and for the first time since the reeds, he looked genuinely afraid — not of me, not of the water, but of the sound.

"They're early," he whispered. "They weren't due for six tides."

"Six tides until what?"

He looked down at the chest in his arms, then up at me, and whatever calculation he'd been running since Harrow Fen finally showed its work.

"Until the rest of it surfaces," he said. "Ranger — how fast can you walk?"

The horn sounded again. Two short notes. One long.

And the river, patient as everything else in my life, began to rise around the truth.
