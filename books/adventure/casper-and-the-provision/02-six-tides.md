## 2. Six Tides

The thief's name was Sefa, and she hit me with an oar.

I want it on the record that I saw it coming. I'd spent ten years reading people across open ground—rangers learn to watch hands, not eyes—and her hand had been drifting toward the boat since the moment I said *don't*. But knowing a thing is coming and stopping it are two different trades, and by the time I'd decided to move, the oar had already introduced itself to my temple, and the salt flat had tilted sideways, and the river was suddenly very interested in me.

I came up coughing, which she seemed to find educational. She stood in my skiff, one boot on the gunwale, chest heaving, holding the oar like a woman who'd recently discovered religion.

"Stay in the water," she said. "Think about your choices."

"I have," I said. "They're wet."

She almost smiled. Then didn't. She poled my own skiff away from me with the competence of someone who'd stolen boats before, and I treaded water in the brown shallows of the Vey and considered the fact that I'd been outmaneuvered by a person who owned one change of clothes and a chest she couldn't even lift.

The chest stayed where it was, half-buried in the silt at the river's edge, its seal staring up at me through six inches of water. The drowned lion of Veymar. Ten years dead, and still giving orders.

I waded out and sat on the bank to wring out my shirt, because a man might as well be dignified about his humiliations. The tide was turning. Out on the flats, far to the south, I could see them—the masts of the salvage fleet, a forest of them strung along the horizon like needles waiting for a thread. Six tides until they reached this stretch of river. Five, now, if you counted honestly, and I have always counted honestly. It's one of my few virtues and it has never once paid for itself.

* * *

Here is what Sefa knew, once I'd caught up to her—which took three miles of wading and cost me what remained of my dignity—and once she'd understood that I wasn't fleet, wasn't magistrate, and wasn't going to turn her in:

She hadn't stolen the chest. She'd found it. There's a difference, and she made me say the difference out loud before she'd tell me anything else.

"It washed down after the spring floods," she said. We were sitting in the skiff, beached behind a spine of black rock, sharing water I'd purified and bread she'd produced from somewhere inside her coat, as though her coat were also stolen. "River gives things back. Everyone knows that. Nobody asks where from."

"And you knew where from."

"I know the wrecks." She said it flatly, daring me to make something cheap of it. "I dive them. Little ones. The fleet doesn't bother with the small sites, so I do. I eat." A shrug. "This one wasn't small."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

Because I'd read the seal, and the seal had read me back. Veymar didn't stamp cargo chests with the drowned lion unless the contents were state papers. And there was exactly one wreck on the lower Vey that would carry state papers, and I had spent ten years hoping it had rotted down to nothing, and here was its calling card sitting in the silt like a letter addressed to a man who'd changed his name.

"The *Provision*," I said.

Her head came up fast. "You know her?"

"I know of her."

"You're lying."

"Frequently," I admitted. "Not now."

She studied me the way she'd study a current before diving into it—looking for the pull underneath. "The fleet's been hunting her for two years. Magistrate Dreville posted the chart himself. Half the divers on the coast would sell their mothers for a fix on that hull."

Dreville.

The name went through me like cold water through a boot. I kept my face still, because ten years of practice will buy you that much, but somewhere under my ribs a door I'd bricked over developed a crack.

"Magistrate Dreville," I said carefully, "of the *Charity*?"

"You know him too?" Her eyes narrowed. "Everyone knows him. He hanged two divers last season for false charts. He doesn't inquire. He just weighs."

Yes. That sounded like Aldous Dreville. It had sounded like Commander Dreville, once, when the weighing was done differently—when the condemned were weighed in absentia, in a room smelling of lamp oil and ink, and I had stood at attention while he read out names, and I had not spoken, because speaking would have cost me everything and saved no one. That was the arithmetic I'd lived by for ten years. *Speaking saves no one.* I had built a whole quiet life on that sum.

The chest in the silt was auditing my books.

"How many tides?" Sefa asked.

"Five."

"And the fleet moves how fast?"

"Faster than we'd like."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free."

She threw her bread crust at me. I ate it. Somewhere in that exchange, without either of us signing anything, an alliance was formed.

* * *

Sefa

He lied like other people breathed—easily, constantly, and with a certain craft pride—but he told the truth about the tides, and about the fleet, and when I mentioned the advance divers working the shallows below the bend, he went pale in a way that had nothing to do with fear of drowning. I've seen scared men. This was older than scared. This was a man hearing his own name called from a long way off and pretending not to recognize the voice.

I didn't trust him. Trust is for people whose pasts fit inside a single lifetime. But I needed his boat, and he needed my wreck, and between those two needs we had something sturdier than most marriages.

We dove the chest out together that evening, between tides, while the light went copper on the flats. He was strong in the water—too strong, trained-strong, the kind of swimming they teach soldiers and rangers and nobody else—and when I asked him about it he said, "Farm ditches," and I said, "In the dark?" and he said, "Especially in the dark," and I let it go, because everyone on the river is lying about something, and at least he did it with manners.

Inside the chest: oilcloth, wax, and papers. He read them by lamplight with his back turned to me, which insulted us both, and then he read them again, and his hands were shaking, and I thought: *there it is.* Whatever this man buried, the Provision dug it up.

"We have to reach the wreck before the fleet does," he said.

"Why? What's aboard?"

"Proof," he said. "Of a kind that gets people un-hanged. Or gets us both hanged faster. Depends on who reads it first."

I looked at the south horizon, where the fleet's lanterns were already multiplying like flies on a wound. Four tides. Maybe five, if the weather turned, and the weather owed nobody favors.

"All right," I said. "But I steer."

"You stole my boat. You've earned it."

* * *

Petra

We ran the river by night and hid by day, which is how honest people travel when the law is coming the other way. On the second night, sheltering in the flooded shell of a customs house, Sefa asked the question I'd been waiting for the way you wait for a tooth to finish aching.

"Who are you?" she said. "Your real name. Not the one you use for buying bread."

I looked at the water lapping through the doorway, black and patient, carrying pieces of a drowned country toward the sea. Somewhere south, the fleet was moving, and at its head was a barge flying the magistrate's pennant, and on that barge was a man who had heard my name spoken exactly once in anger and never since.

"Coldharbour," I said. "Petra Coldharbour."

"Liar," she said comfortably. "That's the bread name too. I checked the ferry ledgers."

And I laughed—actually laughed, first time in months—because she was right, and because there was something restful about being caught by someone who checked ferry ledgers. "Ask me again at the wreck," I said. "If we live that long, I'll tell you the true one. Fair warning: it's worth less."

Three tides left. The lanterns of the fleet had come around the bend at dusk, close enough now that we could count them, and at their center burned a light I recognized—the white lamp of the magistrate's barge, the same lamp that had burned over the sentencing table ten years ago while men became names on a list and I became no one.

Aldous Dreville was coming up the river toward the thing I'd betrayed him to bury.

I found I was no longer sure which of us he'd be more surprised to see.
