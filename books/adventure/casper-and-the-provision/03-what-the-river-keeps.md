## 3. What the River Keeps

The sixth tide came in gray and mean, the way all last chances do.

I'd spent ten years learning how to be nobody. It's a trade like any other—you apprentice yourself to silence, you learn which roads forget your face, which names open doors and which ones close them. I'd gotten good at it. Good enough that when Sefa said *the wreck's under the bell tower, forty feet down, where the river swallowed the chapel district*, my first instinct was still to say nothing at all.

But some things don't stay sunk. Ask Veymar. Ask me.

"Tell me again why we're doing this in the dark," Sefa said. She crouched at the waterline of what used to be a market square, testing the current with a knotted line. The flood had taken the city gently, all things considered—no fire, no collapse, just water rising patient as a debt collector until the spires went under one by one. Now the rooftops made a broken archipelago, and between them the streets ran black and deep.

"Because lamplight underwater looks like a signal flare," I said. "And because the fleet's divers work by day. Magistrate Corven runs a tidy operation."

"You say his name like it tastes bad."

"It does." I checked the seal on the little lamp—oil, glass, a shutter that narrowed the beam to a coin of light. Salvage gear, technically. Theft, legally. I was accumulating quite the docket. "Corven taught me everything I know about following orders. I'd rather he didn't see me coming."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment. She had thief's eyes—they go flat when they're calculating, warm when they've decided something—and just then they were doing both. "You knew him. Before."

"I knew everyone before."

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed, and waded in.

* * *

Diving a drowned city is not like diving anything else. The water holds the shape of the place—the doorways, the window frames, the crooked lane where somebody once sold fried eel to children—and it refuses to let you pretend you're anywhere innocent. My lamp swept across a bakery counter furred green with ten years of silt. A child's shoe in the doorway of a house with its roof intact. The river keeps everything, and it keeps it arranged, like evidence waiting for a court that never convened.

I'd been part of the verdict. That was the thing I never said out loud, even to myself, even on the long salt-flat nights when lying is easier than sleeping. Ten years ago I'd been a ranger under Corven's command, and Veymar had been drowning—not just the nation but the truth of it—and there had been a list of names, and I had read them into the record as traitors because Corven told me they were, because reading them was safer than asking questions, because I was twenty-six and a coward with a commission.

One of those names had been Maren Vale. She'd taught me to read currents. She'd taught me other things too, none of which survived being called a traitor in front of a tribunal while I stood there holding the paper.

The manifest would be in the customs house, Sefa had said. Sealed strongroom, third floor—*third floor*, she'd said, like a building that had been underwater for a decade owed anyone a floor plan. But she'd grown up diving these ruins for coins and candlesticks, back before the fleet came with its licenses and its guns, and she navigated the drowned streets better than I ever navigated dry ones.

The customs house rose out of the dark ahead, its windows like empty eye sockets. I hooked my line to the balcony rail and dropped through what had been a skylight, down through the flooded atrium, past the ghost of a chandelier, to the strongroom door.

It was already open.

That should have been my first warning. Instead I thought: *good, less work.* Ten years of survival instincts, and I swam through a doorway like a man coming home.

The manifest was where manifests are—in an iron case bolted to the wall, wax seals intact, the Veymar crest pressed deep enough to survive the water. I got it free and held it against my chest, and through the oilskin wrapping I could feel the weight of it, the weight of thirty-one names, the weight of a lie with a government stamp on it.

I turned to go, and the lamp caught movement above me—a boot, a silhouette, the slow drift of silt disturbed by someone descending.

Then the light found her.

Sefa hung in the strongroom doorway with her wrists bound to a salvage hook, and behind her, silhouetted against the gray ceiling-water, stood two divers in fleet rig, harpoon guns leveled with the bored professionalism of men who did this weekly.

"Well," said a voice from the dark above, amplified and distorted by the water into something almost gentle. "Look what the river kept."

I knew the voice. Of course I knew the voice. Corven had used that same tone reading the list of names, ten years ago, in a dry room where nobody drowned.

* * *

They brought us up to the rooftop of the customs house, because Corven liked high ground even when the high ground was a chimney pot sticking out of a sea. The tide was turning; in an hour the fleet's barges would run the channels, and whatever happened here would be settled here.

He hadn't changed much. Gray had moved into his beard, and the magistrate's chain sat on him like it had been welded on, but the eyes were the same—patient, weighing, always three moves from done. He looked at me the way you look at furniture you'd written off years ago and found in an unexpected room.

"Coldharbour," he said. "Or whatever you're calling yourself now. The thief wouldn't say. Loyal, for a thief."

"She's not loyal," Sefa said, teeth chattering. "She's smart. There's a difference."

"There usually is," Corven said, without looking at her. "Petra. You're holding the manifest of the *Veymar Grace*. Do you know what's in it?"

"I know exactly what's in it."

"Then you know it's fleet property. Recovered assets of a condemned state. It goes to the inquiry barge, gets catalogued, and in due course—" he smiled, small and administrative "—it gets lost. Things get lost, Coldharbour. You of all people understand that. You lost a whole name once."

The harpoons hadn't wavered. Sefa hung from her hook, shivering, watching me with those flat-warm eyes, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't afraid for herself. She'd seen me come up out of the strongroom holding that case like it was a sleeping animal, and she'd understood what it cost me before I had.

Ten years I'd practiced being nobody. And here was the arithmetic, laid out plain: give up the manifest, stay a ghost, keep breathing. Or say the name. Say all of it. Stand on a drowned rooftop in front of the man who made me and hand over the only proof that Maren Vale and thirty others had died honest.

"The manifest doesn't go to your barge," I heard myself say.

"No?"

"No." I set the case down on the wet brick between us, deliberately, so every gun could see it. "My name is Petra Coldharbour. Formerly Ranger Third Class, Veymar Coastal Service. Ten years ago I read false testimony into the record at the tribunal of the condemned. Thirty-one names. I helped drown them twice—once in the water, once on paper." My voice didn't shake. I'd expected it to. "That manifest clears every one of them. And I'm surrendering myself to your custody—freely, publicly, on the record—to see it delivered."

Corven studied me for a long moment. Somewhere below us, the river shifted with the turning tide, patient as ever.

"You'd hang," he said. "You understand that. Testimony like yours, a confession like that—you're worth more to the inquiry dead than alive."

"Probably."

"And the thief?"

"The thief comes under my surety. She found the wreck. She led me here. Whatever happens to me, she walks." I looked at Sefa. "That's the bargain. Me and the manifest, or neither."

For the first time, Corven's patience flickered. "You're trading your life for hers. You've known her six days."

"Six days is longer than I gave thirty-one people," I said.

Sefa made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else entirely, and Corven—because he was, beneath everything, a man who honored procedure the way other men honor gods—unhooked her wrists himself.

"Bind him," he told the divers. "Both of them, if she insists on standing next to him. They ride the barge to the inquiry." He paused, looking down at me with something that in a kinder man might have been regret. "You know, Coldharbour, I always wondered what happened to you. Turns out you were the only honest thing left in Veymar. Just ten years late."

The divers bound my hands. Sefa pressed her shoulder against mine, wet and shaking and stubborn, and out on the black water the first lanterns of the fleet came around the point like a jury being sworn.

"They'll testify for you," she said quietly. "The crew. The ones you called scavengers. You'll see."

"Sefa, they've known me six days."

"So did I," she said. "Look how that turned out."

The barge horn sounded across the drowned city, and the river carried us toward it.
