## 4. Coldharbour, At Last

*Petra*

They put me in the hold of the magistrate's barge, which was a courtesy. A man awaiting judgment could have been put in the river instead, and the river keeps what it takes.

The hold smelled of bilgewater and lamp oil and someone's abandoned dinner of smoked eel, and I lay on a cot bolted to the hull and listened to the water press against the planks like a patient creditor. Ten years I'd kept my name folded up small enough to fit in a pocket. Now it was written on a charge sheet somewhere above my head—Petra Coldharbour, formerly Warden-Adjutant of Veymar, charged with desertion, with false identity, with the theft of one (1) salvage manifest from a licensed recovery operation. The last charge was the funny one. You can't steal back something that was buried with the drowned.

I'd expected exile. Exile is what you get when nobody wants to look at you long enough to hang you. Magistrate Corven Hale had looked at me across his desk for a long time before he gave the order—long enough that I'd seen the years land on him too, the gray in the beard he hadn't worn at Veymar, the scar through one eyebrow I didn't remember giving him but wouldn't swear against. He'd said my name once, just to hear it, the way you test ice before stepping on it.

"Coldharbour," he'd said. "Of all the rivers in all the world."

"I could say the same about a magistrate," I told him, "poking around a drowned nation's back porch."

He'd almost smiled. Almost. Then the hatch closed and I was alone with the eel smell and the arithmetic of what I'd traded: my freedom for Sefa's life, my name for the truth. It had seemed like a fair exchange at the tide line, with guns pointed at her and lamplight trembling on the flooded colonnade below. Lying in the dark, it still seemed fair. Fairness, I've learned, doesn't always feel like winning.

What I hadn't expected was the sound of boots on deck. A lot of them.

* * *

*Sefa*

I'll tell you what nobody tells you about salvage crews: we keep books. Not the fleet's kind of books, with seals and stamps and little boxes for who owns the dead. Our books are debts. Who pulled who out of the water. Who shared water when the canteen ran low. Who stood watch so another soul could sleep.

By that accounting, Petra Coldharbour owed us nothing and we owed him plenty, and when the magistrate's barge dropped anchor off the wreck site, half the crew of the *Marrow's Luck* decided to settle up.

Getting aboard a magistrate's barge is easier than you'd think, if you're willing to be insulting about it. We came alongside as a supply skiff, groaning under crates of lamp oil we'd actually purchased, because Brannick insisted we do it honest—"We're scavengers, not thieves," he said, which got him laughed at for a full minute—and the deck officer waved us up without looking twice. Fleets never look twice at people bringing them things. That's the whole disease of them.

Then we were on deck, eleven of us smelling of salt and tar, and there was Corven Hale himself at the rail, and I watched Brannick do the bravest thing I saw all year, braver than any dive: he walked straight up to the most powerful man on the water and said, "Magistrate. We've come to speak for our diver."

"Your diver," Hale said, "is a deserter and a liar."

"He's a man who dove into a black ruin by lamplight to fetch proof for strangers he'd known three days," Brannick said. "You can call that whatever fits your paperwork. Out here we call it character."

Hale looked at him for a long moment. Then at the rest of us, lined up behind Brannick like a jury that had gotten lost. And I stepped forward, because it was my turn, and because I'd promised myself on the dive boat that if I lived through being held at gunpoint in a drowned city, I would never again let someone else stand between me and a gun.

"Magistrate Hale," I said. "Sefa Marun. Licensed—well, *formerly* licensed—salvage diver, and the person your advance divers held at weapon-point while a man surrendered himself to save her." I let that sit. "He didn't have to surface. He could've stayed down in the dark with the manifest and been a ghost forever. Instead he came up into the light and gave you his name. When's the last time anyone in your fleet did something that cost them?"

There was murmuring along the rail. Fleet divers don't hear much truth; it comes out of them in bubbles.

Hale's jaw worked. "The manifest is under seal pending inquiry."

"Then hold your inquiry," I said. "But hold it knowing this: every name in that manifest has family still living on these flats. People who've spent ten years wearing shame that wasn't theirs. You want order? Order starts with telling them the truth. And the man who brought you the truth is chained in your hold."

* * *

*Petra*

They brought me up on deck at dusk, which is either when they execute people or when they change their minds, depending on the magistrate's digestion.

The whole crew of the *Marrow's Luck* was there—I recognized them now, the faces I'd dismissed over six tides as scavengers, bottom-feeders, people who made their living picking through other people's endings. Brannick with his cracked knuckles. Old Yeva who navigated by smell, she claimed. The twins who never spoke except to each other. And Sefa, arms crossed, chin up, looking at me like I was a problem she intended to solve personally.

Hale read the charges. Desertion. False identity. Obstruction of licensed salvage. I stood at attention out of ten-year-old habit and waited for the word *exile*, and when it came I'd already planned where I'd go—the deep flats east, where even rangers don't bother—

"The inquiry," Hale said, "convenes at season's end. The manifest will be examined under seal. If it proves what you claim, the condemnations of Veymar's final court are void, and the names attached to them are cleared." He paused. "Including yours, Coldharbour. Provisionally."

Provisionally. There was a word with hooks in it.

"But an inquiry needs a surety," Hale went on. "Someone who'll answer for you. Someone who vouches that you won't run before season's end."

And that was the moment, I'll admit it, that I expected the whole thing to collapse, because who vouches for a ghost? Who puts their name under mine?

Sefa did. She stepped forward before Hale finished the sentence, and behind her, one by one, so did the entire crew of the *Marrow's Luck*. Eleven names spoken aloud, entered into the record, tied to mine. Brannick. Yeva. The twins, who spoke together, which was apparently how they did everything. On and down the line until I was standing inside a wall of names and couldn't see the river anymore.

"You barely know me," I said to Sefa, quietly, while Hale wrote.

"I know you dove back for me," she said. "That's more than most people learn about anybody. Besides—" and here she grinned, quick and crooked, "—you owe us. You're in the book."

* * *

*Petra*

They released me at slack water, into the keeping of the crew, and the *Marrow's Luck* took me aboard like they were taking aboard a crate they'd paid good coin for—which, in a way, they had.

That first night, sitting on deck with salt bread and tea that tasted of the river, Yeva asked me my name. Just asked it, casual, the way you ask a man if he takes sugar.

"Petra Coldharbour," I said.

And it came out easy. That was the miracle of it. Ten years of swallowing that name and it came out smooth as water, no taste of rust, no flinch after. Sefa heard it and nodded like I'd told her the weather. Brannick grunted and passed the bread.

Not a ranger. Not a ghost. Just a name, spoken freely among people who'd earned the hearing of it.

Season's end was coming. The inquiry, the sealed manifest, whatever Hale intended—none of it was settled, and I'd be lying if I said I slept sound. But when I did sleep, I dreamed of the drowned city, and in the dream the water was clearing, and far below, the names on the manifest were standing up out of the silt one by one, walking home.

When I woke, Sefa was at the tiller, and she called down, "Coldharbour! Tide's turning. You diving or dreaming?"

"Diving," I said, and meant it, and went up to take my place.
