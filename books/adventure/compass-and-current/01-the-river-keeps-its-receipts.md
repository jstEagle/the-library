## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The delta woke before Vesper did, the way it always did—a low grumble of water against the hull, gulls arguing over something dead, and Bram already hammering somewhere belowdecks as if the Kestrel were a horse he intended to shame into running.

She lay in her bunk a moment longer, listening to the boat breathe. Twenty-two years on this river and she still counted it a good morning when the bilge pump cycled twice instead of three times. A captain's ambitions should stay small enough to fit in an engine room. Anything bigger got people drowned.

Up top, the mist was peeling off the channels in long gray ribbons. The Kestrel sat moored at Sallow Lock, third in line behind a fuel barge and a dredge tender flying Corvin Ashe's colors—black and gold, a chain link for a crest. Vesper looked at that flag the way she'd look at a snake sunning itself on the rail: with respect, and from a distance.

"Captain." Juno came up the companionway with two tin mugs and the particular walk of someone who'd been awake all night and wanted credit for it. She was nineteen, sharp-boned, hair cropped close against lice and vanity both. "Bram says the starboard injector's held together with prayer and baling wire."

"Bram says that every week."

"Bram's right every week." Juno handed over a mug. The coffee tasted like tar sweetened by regret. "Lockmaster says we're second through after the barge. Ashe's tender's going to sit there all day, though. Pilot's drunk."

"Ashe can afford drunk pilots. That's what money's for."

Juno leaned on the rail beside her, watching the mist burn off the far banks—mangrove, stilt houses, the green smear of the interior channels where the maps went soft. "Permit boards went up at the lock yesterday," she said, too casually.

Vesper had seen them. Fresh-painted notices, official script: *Concession Term Expires — 90 Days — Renewal Pending Review.* Three concessions, three colonial charters, all of them dying on the same date like fish in a drying pond. Every landowner, every dockhand, every smuggler and farmer from here to the headwaters was doing the same arithmetic she was: ninety days, then the maps get redrawn, and nobody knows yet who holds the pen.

"Ninety days," Juno said. "Then what?"

"Then lawyers eat well and everyone else pays for the meal." Vesper drained the mug. "Get the lines ready. We move when the lock does."

* * *

The run up to Ferrow Landing was routine, which on the delta meant only moderately cursed. The Kestrel was forty years old and built like an insult—shallow draft, flat bottom, engine loud enough to be heard in the next concession. She hauled whatever paid: rice, diesel drums, untaxed tobacco, once a coffin with suspicious breathing sounds inside it that Vesper had chosen not to investigate. Invisibility wasn't a virtue. It was a business model.

Tobin Sela came up from the cargo hold around midmorning, wiping dust off his hands. He'd been aboard four months now, hired at a fueling post because he could read manifests in three languages and asked no questions about the fourth. Thin man, careful hands, eyes that catalogued everything and admitted nothing. Vesper trusted him the way she trusted a river gauge—useful, so long as you remembered it was measuring its own interests too.

"We've got a passenger request waiting at Ferrow," Tobin said. "And a freight offer. Big one."

"From?"

"That's the interesting part."

Vesper hated the word *interesting*. On her boat, *interesting* meant fire, flood, or paperwork. "How big?"

"Enough to retire on. Enough to stop being a person anyone looks for." Tobin let that hang. He knew exactly how to bait her; that was the problem with clever crew. "Corvin Ashe wants a private run. Interior channel, one delivery, sealed crates. Pays triple rate plus silence bonus."

Vesper said nothing for a while. The wheel hummed under her palms. Ahead, Ferrow Landing resolved out of the haze—crooked pilings, tin roofs, the permit board nailed up crooked beside the customs shed like even the government couldn't be bothered to pretend anymore.

"Sealed crates," she said finally.

"So he says."

"Nobody pays a silence bonus for rice, Tobin."

"No. They pay it for things that need to not exist." He shrugged. "I told his agent you'd hear him out. Refusing outright would've cost us the fueling contract."

That was true, and she resented it. Everything about Ashe's money worked like that—it made refusing it expensive, so that refusal started to feel like the luxury of fools.

* * *

Corvin Ashe did not come to Ferrow Landing himself. Men like Ashe sent their voices ahead of them. His voice, in this case, was a woman named Pell Hargrove, immaculate in white linen that had no business surviving an hour in the delta, standing on the customs shed's porch with a leather folio under one arm and a smile calibrated to the millimeter.

"Captain Iversen." She extended a hand Vesper didn't take. "You have a reputation."

"For what?"

"Discretion." Hargrove opened the folio on the shed's rail. Inside, figures. Real ones—the kind Vesper usually saw only in other people's nightmares. "One run. Seven days, interior route, my employer's own pilot guides you through the cut. Cargo is legal under current permits."

"*Current* permits," Vesper said.

Hargrove's smile didn't flicker, which was its own kind of answer. "Ninety days is a long time, Captain. And afterward—well. Afterward, whoever helped Mr. Ashe position his assets will find themselves very well positioned indeed. New concessions will need boats. Reliable ones. Discreet ones."

"You're offering me a job that's only legal until the paper expires."

"I'm offering you a future. There's a difference, and wealthy people understand it better than poor ones." Hargrove slid a card across the rail. "He'll double it if you answer by tonight."

Behind Hargrove, down on the landing, Vesper watched a family loading their skiff with everything they owned—bedrolls, a goat, a framed portrait wrapped in oilcloth. Eviction notice stapled to the shed post beside them, fluttering. Ninety days. The whole delta was doing the same arithmetic, and men like Ashe had already bought the answer.

Vesper picked up the card, turned it over, and dropped it into the river.

It floated there a moment, ink bleeding, before the current took it. Hargrove watched it go with an expression Vesper would remember later: not anger. Assessment. Like a ledger noting a debt.

"The river keeps its receipts, Captain," Hargrove said pleasantly. "So does Mr. Ashe."

"Tell him I keep busy."

"You keep *invisible*. There's a difference." Hargrove closed the folio. "And invisible things, Captain, are easy to move. Or to bury. Do think about which you'd prefer."

She left. Vesper stood on the shed porch a while longer, watching the family's skiff fight the current, and felt the old familiar weight settle across her shoulders—not fear exactly, but the specific fatigue of a woman who has spent twenty-two years making herself small enough that powerful men forget she exists, and who knows exactly how long that trick lasts.

Not forever. It never lasts forever.

* * *

They took on coal and water through the afternoon. Juno found Vesper in the wheelhouse checking tide tables and leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed.

"Heard you turned down triple rate."

"Heard fast."

"Are we rich?" Juno asked. "I mean, obviously not. But are we stupid?"

Vesper marked the table with a pencil stub. "Men like Ashe don't hire boats, Juno. They hire alibis. First run's sealed crates. Second run's something worse. By the third, you're not crew anymore—you're evidence."

"And when the permits lapse? Everyone says the interior's going to open up. New trade, new routes—"

"When the permits lapse, the strong take what's loose and call it law. Same as always." Vesper set the pencil down. "We stay small. We stay boring. Boring boats finish their runs."

Juno lingered, chewing on something she didn't say, then left. Through the wheelhouse glass Vesper watched her cross the deck to where Tobin stood coiling line, and saw them put their heads together, and saw Tobin glance back toward the wheelhouse once—quick, unreadable.

She didn't like that. Four months of quiet competence, and she'd almost stopped watching him. Almost.

Dusk came down purple over the channels. The Kestrel cast off on the evening tide, heading downriver toward the fueling post at Marrow Cut where they'd overnight. The delta unspooled around them—fishing lights, mangrove shadows, the distant groan of Ashe's dredge working a bar it had no right to work yet, eating the river a mouthful at a time.

Vesper had the last watch alone, which was how she preferred it. Just her and the wheel and the dark water, the engine's heartbeat underfoot. This was the whole of her ambition, she reminded herself: a boat that floats, a crew that eats, a name that appears on no list worth printing.

The river had tried to drown her twice. Both times it had failed, and both times she'd taken it as a professional courtesy rather than a promise. But standing there in the dark, watching Ashe's dredge lights burn on the horizon like a wound that wouldn't close, she felt the first faint pressure of something changing. Ninety days. The maps were going to be redrawn, and redrawn maps didn't care how small you'd made yourself.

At Marrow Cut, they tied up past midnight. The fueling post was quiet except for one lamp burning in the office and a figure on the dock who didn't belong—a lean man in a traveling coat, dry despite the drizzle, watching the Kestrel come in with the fixed attention of someone who'd been waiting hours and didn't mind admitting it.

Juno appeared at Vesper's shoulder on the rail. "Dock walker. Been there since we passed the point. Watching us."

"Not us," Vesper said slowly. Because the man had stepped forward now, and the lamplight caught his face—young, tired, carrying a satchel like it contained something heavier than paper—and he was looking past her, past the boat, at the water itself. At the debris field drifting down from the flooded upper channels after the night's storm. Branches, reed mats, a drowned canoe.

And something else. Something square and heavy, turning slowly in the current, catching the lamp light with a dull gleam of brass fittings and—Vesper squinted—what looked like a stamped seal on its lid.

The stranger raised a hand and called across the water, voice carrying clear over the engine noise:

"That chest! Where did your boat find it?"

Vesper's hand tightened on the wheel.

They hadn't found any chest.

Yet.
