## 2. What the Flood Surrendered

The storm came off the hills an hour past midnight, the kind that didn't announce itself so much as arrive, all at once, like a debt collector. Vesper Iversen stood at the Kestrel's wheel with rain hammering the canopy and the river gone brown and muscular beneath her, hauling whole trees downstream like a man dragging furniture out of a house he'd lost.

"Snag line ahead," Juno shouted from the bow, one hand on the rail, hair plastered flat to her skull. "Big one. Right of center."

Vesper saw it—a black hump riding the current, too low to be a boat, too long to be a log. She could have gone around. Any sensible captain would have gone around. But the flood had pushed them wide of the channel marker, and the sensible route was now a sandbar, and so she did what she always did: she took the river's bad hand and played it.

"Hold her steady," she called back to Bram, whose face was framed in the engine hatch below, gray in the lightning. "We'll kiss it and see."

They kissed it. The Kestrel's hull scraped something soft-then-hard, a sound Vesper felt in her teeth more than heard, and then they were past, wallowing in the swell, and the snag was behind them.

Except it wasn't. It had followed them.

"Captain." Juno again, quieter now, which from Juno was worse than shouting. "It's hooked us. Something's on the line."

* * *

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. They fished it aboard with the cargo winch, groaning cable and all four of them leaning into it—Bram, Juno, old Sef at the tiller lines, and Vesper herself, boots slipping on wet deck planking. What came over the rail was a chest, iron-banded, waterlogged to maybe two hundred pounds, trailing river weed like a drowned man's beard.

And stamped into its lid, under a crust of silt that Bram scrubbed away with his thumb: a seal. A sun rising over three rivers.

Nobody said anything for a long moment. The rain had stopped. The delta lay flat and silver around them, pretending innocence.

"That's Meridian," Sef said finally. He was the oldest of them, sixty if he was a day, and he crossed himself the old way, thumb to brow, the way people did before the Republic went under. "That's the Republic of Meridia."

"There is no Republic of Meridia," Juno said. "There hasn't been for forty years."

"Well," Sef said, "somebody didn't tell the chest."

Vesper crouched beside it. The lock was brass and swollen shut. She sent Bram for the cold chisel while she ran her fingers along the bands, feeling for the seam, the way she'd feel a hull for rot. Her hands knew things before her head agreed to them. Her hands were telling her this was trouble.

Everything worth finding in the delta was trouble. That was practically the definition of worth.

The chisel bit. The lock gave with a crack that sounded obscenely loud in the morning quiet. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its job across four decades of river: paper. Dozens of sheets, heavy stock, ink barely faded. And beneath them, a ledger bound in green leather, its spine cracked, its pages warped but legible.

Juno picked up the top sheet and read aloud, slow, sounding out words like a schoolgirl: "'By these presents, title in fee simple to the lands of the lower Tarn basin, being four hundred hectares more or less, is conveyed to—'" She stopped. Looked up. "These are deeds. Land deeds."

"Give it here." Vesper took the ledger instead, because ledgers were her language. She'd kept books since she was nine years old, running numbers for a smuggler who never suspected the small girl in the corner was checking his arithmetic. This ledger was older arithmetic. Entries in a clerk's neat hand: payments, disbursements, and then a page near the front that made her go still.

*Ashe. A. Ashe, surveyor. Disbursement: consideration to magistrate's clerk, re: boundary revision, Tarn basin. Consideration to militia captain, re: removal of occupants.*

She read it twice. Three times. The name sat there in forty-year-old ink like a hook set deep in her.

"Ashe," Bram said, reading over her shoulder. He smelled of engine oil and river mud, and his voice had dropped into the register he used when he was frightened and wanted nobody to know. "Corvin Ashe's family. His grandfather, I'd wager. This is the fraud. This is the actual fraud, written down by the men who committed it."

"The Republic drowned," Sef said. He'd lowered himself onto the deck planks, knees cracking, and was looking at the chest the way a man looks at a grave. "Flood took the capital, flood took the records, flood took every deed and title and claim. And the Asches walked in after with their own papers and said the land was theirs. Everyone said so. Everyone with money said so."

"And here's the other set of papers," Vesper said quietly. "The ones that got away."

* * *

They argued about it through the morning, in the galley, over tea brewed strong enough to strip paint.

"Sell it," Juno said. She had the deeds spread out on the table, weighted at the corners with tin cups, and she was looking at them the way Vesper had seen her look at nothing else—not even the pay envelope at month's end. "Not to Ashe. To collectors. To the archives in Port Lasseter, or whatever university still pays for Meridian relics. There are men who'd give a year's takings for this chest. We could run the Kestrel clean for five years. Ten. No contraband, no night runs, no looking over our shoulders at every lock."

"You've counted the years already," Vesper said.

"I've counted everything." Juno was twenty-three and had grown up in a river town that no longer existed, though she'd never said which one, and Vesper had never asked, because some cargoes you don't poke at. "Captain, I'm telling you as crew and as someone who'd like to grow old somewhere above the waterline: sell it, split it, and disappear."

"It's not ours to sell," Bram said.

"Whose is it? The Republic? Go find the Republic."

"The villages," Sef said. "The people those deeds name. Some of them are still there. Still fishing the same bends their grandmothers fished, paying rent to the Asche concession for land that was theirs outright." He tapped the ledger with one knuckle. "This isn't treasure. It's a receipt. River keeps its receipts, I told you that the first week you hired me, captain. Well. Here's one come home."

Vesper let them talk. She turned her tea mug slowly on the table, watching the ring it left, and thought about invisibility.

That was the whole art of her life, distilled: stay small, stay dull, stay unremarked. The Kestrel was registered to a dead man. Vesper Iversen appeared on no manifest anyone important read. She paid the right palms at the right locks and paid them just enough to be forgotten rather than remembered. In eleven years on this river she had made herself into a rumor with a boat, and it had kept her alive, and free, and alone, and she had told herself the last of those was a choice.

A chest with a drowned republic's seal did not fit inside a rumor. A chest like that made you a story. Stories traveled faster than boats.

"Bram," she said. "You've been quiet in the wrong way. Out with it."

Bram set down his mug. "Wray," he said.

The name landed on the table like a knife.

"He was at Coalhaven dock yesterday. Before the storm. Asking questions—not about cargo, not about permits. About salvage rights. About what comes downriver in a flood season, and who claims it, and whether the magistrate's office gets first refusal on anything raised from the old Meridian channels." Bram's eyes went around the table. "He described a chest, captain. Iron-banded. He described it before we ever hooked it."

The rain started again against the roof, soft now, indifferent.

"Wray doesn't describe things he hasn't seen," Sef said. "Enforcers describe things they've been told to find."

"Then someone knows it exists," Juno said. "Someone upstream. Someone who lost it in the flood and wants it back—or someone who never wants it found."

Vesper stood. She went to the stern window, where the delta unrolled behind them, brown water braiding into silver, the whole contested country lying flat and patient under cloud. Somewhere downriver, Wray was asking his questions in his soft voice, posting his watchers at his locks. Somewhere upriver, Corvin Ashe sat in a dredge barge pulling gold out of the riverbed, fattening on land his grandfather had bought with bribes recorded in green leather. And somewhere between, forty years of families had been paying rent on their own ground because the paper that proved otherwise had drowned.

Until this morning. Until her winch.

"We can't sell it," she said, without turning around. "Not to collectors, not to Ashe, not to anyone. The moment it leaves this boat, it becomes a thing that can be bought back, burned, buried. While it's aboard—" she turned, and found them all looking at her, waiting, and felt the strange vertigo of a woman who has spent eleven years avoiding exactly this, "—while it's aboard, it's a live round. And I'm the only one holding the gun."

"That's not a plan, captain," Juno said. "That's a eulogy with extra steps."

"It's a start." Vesper gathered the deeds, squared their edges, wrapped them back in the oilcloth with hands that were steadier than she felt. "We deliver them. Village by village, bend by bend, to whoever the names belong to. Before the concessions lapse and Ashe's lawyers file new paper and this window closes forever."

"The enforcer—"

"—is one man with one launch," Vesper said. "And I have spent eleven years learning every cut, chute, and dead channel this river has. If he wants the chest, he can learn the river too. It drowns slower students."

Sef laughed, short and dry, and Bram looked at the floor, and Juno looked at Vesper with an expression that wasn't agreement yet but was no longer argument.

Outside, the current carried on, taking everything and keeping everything, and Vesper Iversen understood that the invisible woman had died sometime between midnight and dawn, hooked to the same line as a drowned republic's secrets.

At noon, when they put in at the fueling post at Halfway Light to take on diesel, there was a stranger waiting at the rail-end of the dock—young, travel-worn, with a satchel clutched to his chest like a second heart—and he watched the Kestrel come alongside with the fixed, hungry attention of a man who had been looking for one particular boat for a very long time.
