## 3. A Stranger at the Rail

Tobin Sela had been waiting at the Corvo Bend fueling post for six days, and he had learned the first rule of waiting: look like you belong to something. So he sat on a coil of frayed hawser with a ledger open on his knees, copying figures nobody would ever check, and he watched the river the way his grandfather had taught him to watch people—patiently, and with the assumption that everything important arrives eventually.

The Kestrel arrived on the fourth morning, riding low, her paint gone the color of old teeth. He knew her from the manifests he'd memorized in his grandfather's archive: forty-two feet, shallow draft, a sternwheel that turned like it was apologizing for itself. Registered out of no port that mattered. Captained by a woman who appeared in exactly one official document in the entire delta—a fuel tax receipt—and whose name was misspelled even there.

*Iversen,* Tobin thought. *Vesper Iversen.* A name chosen by someone who wanted to be forgotten.

He stood when she came up the gangplank to argue with the fuel clerk, because sitting men don't get answers and standing men at least get looked at. She was shorter than he'd imagined, wiry, with sun-cracked hands and eyes that did the arithmetic of every stranger before her mouth opened. He watched her do it now: height, reach, the ledger under his arm, the ink stains on his fingers. Classifying him. Filing him somewhere between nuisance and threat.

"Captain Iversen." He kept his voice level, unhurried. "I'd like passage upriver."

"We're not a ferry."

"I know what you are. That's rather the point."

Her gaze sharpened. Behind him, the fuel clerk suddenly found urgent business with a valve. At Corvo Bend, everyone knew which conversations to be deaf to.

"You have me confused with someone who cares," Iversen said, turning back to the clerk.

"The chest came ashore after the storm," Tobin said quietly. "Two nights ago. Snagged on your line or your wheel—I don't know which, and I don't need to. It's stamped with the Meridian seal. A crowned wave over three stars, pressed in black wax."

She went very still. Not the stillness of guilt—the stillness of a woman recalculating every plan she'd made in the last two days. Tobin had grown up around archivists; he knew the difference between a lie caught and a life interrupted.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Of course not." He opened his ledger to a page he'd prepared and held it out—not offering it, just letting her see. "Then you won't mind knowing that Magistrate's Enforcer Wray filed a warrant four days ago. Before your storm. Before your chest. General seizure order, 'property of the drowned Republic, Meridian.' Which means Wray already knew it existed. Which means someone told him where it would surface." He let that sit. "Wray doesn't ask questions, Captain. He collects. And right now his collection list has one item, and the man carrying it is you."

* * *

She took him aboard anyway, which surprised him more than refusal would have. But she put him at the rail, in plain view, with tea he suspected had been brewed for the crew and watered twice for him. The Kestrel's engineer—Bram, huge and gray-bearded, moving with the careful economy of an old injury—watched from the engine housing without pretending otherwise. A girl with a shaved patch healing over a scar, Juno, perched on the cargo netting and stared at him with open hostility, as if hostility were a chore she'd been assigned.

"So," Iversen said. "Talk fast."

"My grandfather was Halric Sela. Keeper of the Meridian civil registry, the last forty years of it, until the concessions swallowed the republic and burned what they couldn't steal." Tobin kept his hands visible, open. He'd learned that from watching men negotiate with Ashe's factors: the moment your hands disappear, so does their trust. "He spent thirty years reconstructing what was destroyed. Deeds. Titles. The original grant lines. When he died, the work passed to me, and I've spent nine years doing what he did—reading everything, keeping everything, waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For the chest to exist again." He met her eyes. "You think it surfaced by accident? Forty years underwater, and it comes up the same season the concession permits expire? The Republic didn't drown, Captain. It was sunk. And whoever sank it hid the proof where they thought no flood would ever reach. The river disagreed. The river keeps its receipts."

Juno snorted from the netting. "Poetic. You rehearse that?"

"Tobin," he said, ignoring her. "Sela. And yes, I rehearse it. I've given this speech to eleven captains in two months. Ten of them tried to sell me to Wray's watchers for the bounty."

"And the eleventh?"

"Drowned." He let the word land flat, without drama. "Rapids above Serpent's Tooth. His deckhand sold the chest's location before the body was cold. So understand me: I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is for people with time. I'm asking you to listen, because in about a day, Wray's warrant stops being general and becomes specific, and then every lock and landing from here to the headwaters has his men on it, and you'll wish you'd listened at the rail instead of below decks where I can't see your face while you decide whether I'm lying."

Bram shifted. Iversen held up one finger without looking—*stay*—and Bram stayed.

"Say I believe the chest matters," she said. "Say I believe your grandfather's paper trail. What's in it for you? Inheritance? Those deeds make landowners out of river families. They don't make anything out of you."

"They make an archive out of nothing," Tobin said. "They un-burn my grandfather's library. That's not sentiment, Captain—that's the whole job. An archivist who lets the record die is just a man who kept paper warm." He paused, and decided, against every instinct honed in nine years of careful silence, to give her the true thing. "And there's a page missing. From the fraud ledger, if you found one inside. My grandfather wrote that a copy of the original conveyance—the document proving the Ashe family's grant was forged—was sealed *in* the chest. If it's there, the deeds aren't just old paper. They're enforceable. Courts honor them. Without it, they're souvenirs."

Something moved behind her eyes. He'd seen that look once before, on the eleventh captain, the day before he died: the look of a person realizing the thing in their hold is heavier than lead and worth more than gold, and both facts are problems.

"If I say we have a ledger," Iversen said slowly, "and if I say there's a folded conveyance wrapped in oilcloth under the false bottom—what would you know that makes that worth more than throwing you off this boat right now?"

"Wray's warrant names a route," Tobin said. "Not a boat. A route. The interior channels, all of them, gated at Three Sisters Lock. He can't watch every boat, so he watches the water. But warrants run on permits, Captain, and the permits expire in nineteen days. After that, the concessions void, the magistrate's jurisdiction thins to nothing, and Wray becomes a man with a gun and no law behind it. Everything he does between now and then has to be legal-looking. Which means—" he counted it off on ink-stained fingers— "he can board you once. Maybe twice. He can't hold you. He can't search without pretext. And he cannot, under any circumstance, let those deeds reach a village elder before expiry, because a deed delivered to a living claimant before the lapse is a claim the new courts will have to honor. He knows it. That's why the warrant exists."

The wheelhouse was quiet except for the river slapping the hull. Juno had stopped glaring; she was looking at Iversen now, waiting, the way crew waits.

"You said eleven captains," Iversen said. "What happened to the chest on the other ten?"

"Nothing. Because none of them had it yet." He smiled, thin. "You're the twelfth. And the first one who didn't throw me off inside a minute."

"That's not a compliment. That's poor judgment."

"It's both," Tobin said. "Most things worth anything are."

* * *

They argued below decks for an hour. He heard most of it through the bulkhead—Bram's low rumble, Juno's sharper voice, Iversen's flat finality cutting through both. When she finally called him down into the saloon, the chest sat on the table between them, open, the wax seal cracked like old ice. The conveyance lay in its oilcloth exactly where he'd said. He didn't touch it. He'd learned that too: never touch the evidence in front of witnesses.

"Terms," Iversen said. "You ride to the first Meridian-descended settlement—Old Merrow, up the Sable Fork. You advise on the deeds' legal weight, you tell us what Wray can and can't do, and you keep your hands off the wheel, the engine, and my crew. In exchange, passage, food, and not being sold."

"And after Old Merrow?"

"After that, we renegotiate. I don't sign contracts longer than the next bend of the river. It keeps things honest."

"It keeps things deniable," Tobin said. "But fine." He hesitated, then paid out the last coin of trust he had. "There's one more thing. Wray boarded a boat at Corvo Bend yesterday morning. Asked the harbormaster who'd come through asking about Meridian records. The harbormaster is honest, which is worse than corrupt—honest men answer questions. He described me. Wray now knows a Sela is looking, and he knows a Sela would only matter if the family work mattered. You're not hiding a stranger anymore, Captain. You're hiding a lead."

For a long moment Vesper Iversen looked at him, and Tobin watched her decide—not whether to trust him, but how much distrust she could afford. He recognized the arithmetic. He'd been running it for nine years.

"Bram," she said, without turning. "Cast off. And take us past the lock before full dark."

"There's watchers at the lock," Bram said.

"There will be," Iversen agreed. "So we go through before they finish their supper."

Tobin gathered his ledger as the Kestrel's sternwheel began to turn, churning the brown water of Corvo Bend into foam. Ahead, the delta opened into its thousand channels, and somewhere among them a man named Wray was closing a hand that had never once come up empty.

Juno dropped down beside him at the rail, arms crossed, studying him with the frank appraisal of someone deciding whether to hate him efficiently or thoroughly.

"Eleven captains," she said. "What did the tenth one look like?"

"Like a man who trusted his crew," Tobin said.

She considered that. "Yeah," she said. "That'd do it." And she walked away, which he understood, from long practice, was the closest thing to welcome the Kestrel offered.

Behind them, at the fueling post, a lean figure in a magistrate's gray coat stepped out of the clerk's office and watched the Kestrel's wake flatten against the pilings. He didn't signal anyone. He didn't need to. He simply took out a small notebook, wrote something in it, and smiled at the river like a man who had just been handed the second half of a map.
