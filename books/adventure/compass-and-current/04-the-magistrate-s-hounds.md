## 4. The Magistrate's Hounds

The message came upriver on a fuel barge, folded inside a shipping manifest that Vesper had no reason to open and every instinct to. *Inspection authorized under Concession Statute 14. Magistrate's Warrant, Enforcer Wray presiding. Expect boarding at Sallow Lock, morning tide.*

She read it twice by the light of the wheelhouse lamp, then held the paper over the little brass stove until it curled into ash.

"He's coming aboard," she said to the empty room, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things could be planned for.

Below her feet, in the hold beneath the cargo deck, sat a chest stamped with the seal of a republic that hadn't existed in forty years, full of deeds that could unmake three concessions or hang everyone on the Kestrel twice over. She'd slept badly for two nights with that arithmetic running through her skull like bilgewater.

By dawn she'd made her decisions.

* * *

Bram took the news of the inspection without looking up from his engine. He was a long, grey man built like something salvaged off a wreck, and he communicated mostly through tools.

"Statute 14," he said finally, wiping his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth. "That's structural. He can open every compartment, pull every panel."

"I know."

"He can't inspect what doesn't run." Bram set down the wrench and looked at her with eyes the color of old river ice. "Kill the fuel line at the manifold. Engine dies mid-channel, we drift to the lock wall helpless, apologetic. No captain opens the bilge of a boat that's already drowning."

"You'd fake a breakdown for an enforcer?"

"I'd fake a breakdown for anyone who pays me in not being arrested." He turned back to the engine. "Get Juno off the deck when he boards. She looks guilty standing still."

Juno did not take it well. She stood in the galley with her arms crossed, seventeen years old and furious at being managed, which was most of the time.

"I'm not a child."

"You're a witness," Vesper said. "There's a difference. Witnesses stay below."

"And if he searches below?"

"He won't get that far." Vesper hoped it sounded more certain than it felt. "Bram's engine dies, I play the flustered captain, Tobin plays himself—"

"What does that mean?"

"It means he's good at being harmless. Let him be harmless."

Tobin, when she told him, just nodded, and that nod frightened her more than Wray's warrant had. He'd gone quiet in a new way since the chest came aboard—not the quiet of a man hiding something, but the quiet of a man carrying something heavy and learning its weight. He helped her with the false floor without being asked.

They worked through the afternoon while the delta narrowed toward Sallow Lock. The bilge ran the length of the hull like a black throat, knee-deep in cold water and shadow, and above it they laid planking that matched the cargo decking so well you'd need to know it was there—and know where—to find the seam. The chest went down wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with chain so it would sit submerged if anyone lifted the wrong board. Vesper lowered it herself, hand over hand, into the dark, and stood a moment listening to the water lap against it.

Forty years drowned already, she thought. It can swim a little longer.

When she climbed out, Tobin was watching her with that archivist's precision she was starting to distrust and rely on in equal measure.

"You're good at this," he said.

"At hiding things?"

"At making them disappear." He paused. "I mean it as a compliment."

"Don't." She wrung bilge water from her sleeve. "People who are good at disappearing usually had a reason to learn."

* * *

Sallow Lock at morning tide was all wet stone and waiting. The lock-keepers' huts hunched on either bank, and moored against the downstream pier sat a magistrate's launch—white hull, black trim, too clean for this stretch of river, the way a knife is too clean for a kitchen.

Wray came up the gangplank alone, which was somehow worse than a squad. He was a narrow man in a grey coat, unhurried, with the particular stillness of someone who has learned that patience catches more than force. His warrant case was leather worn soft at the corners. He'd been doing this a long time.

"Captain Iversen." He didn't offer a hand. "Enforcer Wray. Routine inspection under Statute 14."

"Of course." Vesper kept her voice bored, her hands busy coiling a line that didn't need coiling. "We're having some engine trouble, fair warning. She may not cooperate any better than the rest of us."

"So I hear." Something flickered behind his eyes—amusement, maybe, or its absence. "Trouble started recently?"

"This morning. River's full of surprises."

"Mm." He stepped aboard without invitation, and the Kestrel seemed to shrink around him. "I'll try not to add to them."

He inspected the way a heron fishes: motionless, then sudden. Cargo manifest first—he read it twice, front and back, while Vesper stood at a comfortable distance radiating impatience. Then the hold, the crew quarters, the galley where Juno sat below decks radiating guilt through the floorboards. Then the wheelhouse, where he lingered over the chart table longer than charts deserved, one finger resting on the interior route marked in Vesper's own pencil.

"Planning a trip?"

"Planning options. Channels close fast this time of year."

"They do." He straightened. "Your engineer reports a fuel line failure."

"Bram reports everything accurately. It's his best quality."

"Then he won't mind if I have a look at the engine room."

Vesper felt the day tilt. "Statute 14 covers structure and cargo—"

"And safety," Wray said mildly. "A dead engine in a lock channel is a hazard to navigation. I'm obliged to verify the failure is genuine." He was already moving. "You understand. If it were sabotage, say—or concealment dressed as failure—that would interest my office considerably."

Bram met them in the engine room, greasy and aggrieved, and delivered a performance so flat it circled back around to convincing. He walked Wray through the manifold, showed him the sheared fitting, let the man crouch and peer with a small electric lamp into the places engines keep their secrets. Vesper stood at the top of the stairs and watched Wray's lamp swing toward the bilge access hatch—

—and stop.

"Water's high," Wray observed.

"Rain," said Bram.

"Rain," Wray agreed. He played the lamp across the hatch a moment longer than necessary. Then he straightened, clicked it off, and Vesper breathed again for the first time in four minutes.

Up top, Wray closed his warrant case and produced instead a folded paper—a list. "Lock schedules," he said, handing it to her. "Every lock between here and the headwaters. My watchers will be posted at each. Any vessel seeking passage will be boarded and logged." He looked at her, and for a moment the mildness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was patient and bottomless. "Routine, Captain. But you'd be surprised how often routine finds exactly what it's looking for."

"I wouldn't know. I avoid finding things."

"Yes." He descended the gangplank, then paused, half-turned. "The Republic of Meridia left a great deal of paper behind when it drowned. Deeds, mostly. Worthless now, of course—the concessions superseded them—but collectors pay strange sums for strange relics." A beat. "If anything like that ever drifted into your nets, Captain, the magistrate's office would take it off your hands. Fair compensation. No questions."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"Do." He stepped onto the pier. "Engines fail so conveniently these days. Do have yours seen to before the next lock. My watchers are instructed to note repairs."

Then he was walking back to his white launch, and Vesper stood at the rail watching the too-clean hull pull away upstream, toward the next lock, and the next, laying his hounds along the whole river like a man laying cards face-up.

* * *

That night the Kestrel lay at anchor in a backwater thick with reeds, engine "under repair," and the crew gathered in the galley around a single lamp.

"He knows," Juno said. "He knows we have it."

"He suspects," Tobin corrected. "There's a distance between those, and right now that distance is the only thing keeping us out of irons. He can't search without cause, and he can't show cause without revealing what he's hunting—which means Ashe doesn't want the chest's existence confirmed. Not yet."

"So we sit here until—" Juno gestured at the walls, the river, everything. "Until what? Every lock from here to the headwaters has his people on it. We can't move without being boarded. We can't stay without rotting."

"We can move," Vesper said slowly. She was staring at the chart, at the pencil line she'd drawn days ago and Wray's finger had rested on. The interior route. The one no one ran because the channels were narrow, unmarked, unpatrolled—and closing fast with the season. "Just not through his locks."

Bram looked up from his tea. "The interior's suicide in low water."

"The interior's suicide in low water," Vesper agreed. "So we hope for high."

Tobin followed her gaze to the chart, then to the porthole, where the sky had gone a bruised, expectant purple over the western hills. He was quiet a moment.

"The monsoon's early this year," he said. "The archives used to track it. Old Meridian records—rain coming two, three weeks ahead of season." He looked at her. "High water."

"High water," Vesper said.

Outside, far off, thunder answered across the hills like something waking up.
