## 5. Monsoon Rising

The rain came in the night, and it came like a debt collector.

Juno woke to the sound of it hammering the deck above her hammock—not the polite, apologetic drizzle of the spring showers, but the flat, relentless roar of water that intended to stay. She lay still in the dark of the fo'c'sle and listened to the Kestrel groan against her mooring lines, and she thought: *early*. Three weeks early, at least. Maybe four.

By the time she climbed topside, the world had dissolved. The delta that yesterday had been a braided glitter of channels and sandbars was now a single brown muscle, flexing. The high channel—the wide, deep, well-marked route every licensed boat took—had become a torrent, its marker buoys dragged under or swept downstream like toys pulled from a child's hand.

Vesper stood at the wheel, hood up, watching the water with the expression Juno privately called her ledger face: nothing given away, everything counted.

"High channel's closed," Vesper said, without turning. "Coast station flagged it an hour ago. Debris field two miles long where the old ferry landing came apart."

"So we wait it out." Bram's voice came up through the engine hatch, half-swallowed by the rain. He emerged wiping his hands on a rag that had been white once, in some previous life. "Monsoon peaks in ten days, maybe twelve. Water drops after. We lose a week, we still make the deliveries."

"We don't have a week," Tobin said.

He was standing under the awning by the chart table, and he'd been there, Juno suspected, since before dawn. There was something about Tobin Sela when he'd been awake all night—he went very still, very precise, like a man defusing something. He spread the chart flat and weighted the corners with brass fittings.

"The permits lapse in nineteen days. That was true yesterday. But Wray posted watchers at every lock on the high channel, and Ashe has fuel contracts with every coaling post between here and the middle villages. If we hole up, we don't just lose time—we lose the ability to move at all. They only need to find us once."

"And if we run the interior," Bram said, "we don't need them to find us. The river will do it for free."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't a joke, exactly. It was arithmetic.

The interior route was the old one—the Meridian route, though nobody said so out loud anymore. Before the concessions, before the dredges and the levees and the tidy surveyed channels, the river had run through the heart of the delta in a hundred tangled veins, and the towns had grown along those veins like moss on a living thing. Then the colonial engineers had come with their straight lines and their permits, and the interior had been left to silt, to flood, to grow over. No markers. No locks. No depth surveys newer than forty years old—which was to say, none at all, because the charts from the Republic's last survey had drowned with everything else.

In monsoon season, the interior filled with water. It also filled with snags, submerged fences, collapsed bridges, whole orchards drifting loose, and sandbars that moved overnight like animals grazing.

"It can be done," Vesper said.

Bram looked at her. "It can be *attempted*."

"It can be done in this boat, by this crew, if we go now, while the water's high enough to float us over the bars but before the current gets worse." Vesper turned from the wheel at last. Her eyes went around the circle of them—Bram dripping at the hatch, Tobin bent over the chart, Juno with her back against the rail. "But I'm not voting for all of you. This isn't cargo anymore. This is a fight, and fights sink boats. So we decide it together."

Juno watched them look at each other. Six weeks ago there would have been no circle to stand in. Six weeks ago she'd have been told what the plan was, same as the weather, and she'd have taken it or jumped ship. Somewhere between the chest in the bilge and Wray's boots on the deck, the Kestrel had stopped being a workplace and started being a thing with a shape she recognized, though she'd never lived aboard anything like it before.

"I'll say my piece," Bram said. "I love this engine more than most people I've known. The interior will try to kill it. Snags in the intake, debris in the screw, and if we ground hard enough to crack a seam, there's no salvage tug coming, no lock keeper, no nothing. We die slow, or we walk out of the bush and get picked up by whoever finds us first, which is probably Wray." He folded his rag into a neat square. "That said. I've been running engines for thirty years and I've never once done it for a cause. I wouldn't mind finding out what it feels like before my hands give out."

"Tobin?"

Tobin didn't look up from the chart right away. When he did, his face had the careful blankness he wore when the thing underneath was too big to show. "My grandfather rowed dispatches on these interior channels. For the Republic. I've got his notes—depths, landmarks, the way the old pilots read the water. They're forty years stale, but stale is not the same as useless." He paused. "And I owe a debt on this route. I sold information about Meridian descendants to Ashe's people once. Names. Villages. I didn't know what it was for, at first, and then I knew, and I kept selling anyway, because it paid. Every village we reach on the interior is a place I helped put on someone's list." He met Vesper's eyes. "So yes. Interior. And if we ground out and drown, I'll consider it fair pricing."

The silence after that was long. Rain filled it.

Then they all looked at Juno, and she understood, with a lurch, that she was the deciding vote—that Vesper had already decided, and Bram had half-decided, and Tobin had never decided anything else, and the whole weight of it had been quietly passed down the line to the youngest pair of hands aboard.

She almost resented it. Then she didn't.

"You all know where I'm from," she said.

They did. She'd told it once, late at night, the way you tell things you've sworn not to tell—flat, fast, no decoration. Saltmere. A river town on the interior route, actually, though the maps called it nothing at all. Evicted when she was nine, when Ashe's father's concession paperwork declared the land "unimproved riparian surplus." The word *surplus* had followed her ever since, like a smell that gets into wool.

"They gave my mother eleven days to clear out," Juno said. "Eleven days, in writing, delivered by a man who wouldn't step off his launch onto our dock because our dock was, quote, structurally unsound. The town had been there ninety years. My grandmother was born in that house. And when the water came up that year—and it always came up—they used the flood as the excuse. Said the river had abandoned us. Said nature itself agreed with the paperwork." She heard her own voice go level and cold, and let it. "I've spent ten years being invisible on boats like this one. Running cargo for people who'd call my town a sandbar with opinions. And I want to tell you what I learned being invisible: it doesn't keep you safe. It just means nobody's looking when they take the next thing."

She pushed off the rail and stepped to the chart table, to the interior route drawn in Tobin's careful pencil—a ghost road through the drowned country.

"My town is three channels off this route. I haven't been back. I don't know if there's anything to go back to. But there are villages out there full of people who got the same letter my mother got, and in nineteen days the paper that stole from them expires, and the deeds in our bilge are the only thing in the world that says it was theirs first." She looked at Vesper. "Interior. Run it now, while the water's high. I'll take the bow watch. I grew up reading water like this—it's the one inheritance they couldn't serve papers on."

Vesper held her gaze for a moment. Something passed across her face—not softness, exactly. Recognition. One orphan of the river acknowledging another kind of orphanage.

"Then it's settled," Vesper said. "Bram, full pressure on the boiler by first light. Tobin, your grandfather's notes—I want them copied, one set in the pilothouse, one sealed in oilcloth in the bilge next to the chest. Juno takes point watch. We cast off at dawn, and we don't stop except to breathe."

* * *

They cast off into the rain at gray light, and the delta swallowed them.

Juno took the bow with a sounding pole and a lantern that was mostly ceremonial, the daylight behind the rain being barely worth the oil. The entrance to the interior route was a gap in the wall of cane maybe forty feet wide, unmarked, invisible until you were inside it. Tobin stood beside her with his grandfather's notebook open under an oilskin, calling landmarks in a low voice—"the split rock should be abeam, the old ferry cable anchored there"—and each time the landmark appeared, roughly where a dead man said it would be, Juno felt something loosen in her chest. The past wasn't gone. It was just waiting underwater, patient as everything else in the delta.

Behind them, the Kestrel moved like she'd been born for this—Vesper reading the current off the surface the way other people read print, backing her down off the first bar with a touch so light Juno felt it in her teeth rather than her feet.

By midday they were deep in it. The channel narrowed, widened, split, rejoined. They passed a drowned church roof breaking the flood like a whale's back, and a fence line running off into brown water toward nowhere, and once—a whole floating island of torn-up cane and flowering tree, drifting with the current, carrying, Juno could swear, a heron standing on it like a passenger who'd paid his fare.

It was beautiful. It was also trying to kill them every ninety seconds.

A log the size of a telegraph pole came out of a side slough at mid-afternoon, riding the crosscurrent straight for the wheelhouse. Juno screamed the bearing; Vesper threw the wheel over; the Kestrel slewed sideways and the log slid past close enough to leave a green smear of weed along the hull. Nobody spoke for a full minute afterward. Then Bram's voice floated up from below, dry as tinder: "Next time, a little warning. I was having a nice day."

Near dusk they made the first anchorage—a cutbank behind a wing dam the Republic had built and the concession government had let rot. Juno took the hook out in the rain and set it well, and when she came back aboard, dripping, she found the others gathered in the pilothouse around the lamp, and found herself handed a mug of tea without having to ask, and found that the space they made for her was exactly her size, as if it had been built that way years ago and she'd simply arrived late.

"To Saltmere," Tobin said quietly, raising his mug. "And every other name the maps lost."

"To the ones the river kept," Vesper said.

Juno drank, and said nothing, because her throat had closed. Through the rain-streaked glass she watched the dark water slide past the anchor line, and she thought about how the flood had been used against them—how the river had been made the villain in the story of her town's stealing. Well. The river was here now, high and furious, and it was carrying them, and tomorrow it would carry them farther.

It was near midnight, Juno at the bow keeping the first watch, when she saw the light.

Not lightning. Lightning flickered and died. This was steady, low on the water behind them, far back down the route they'd come—two lights, moving, the way a launch moves when it doesn't care who sees it.

She watched for five minutes to be sure. The lights didn't turn off. They didn't stop. They were following the interior route, in the dark, in the teeth of the monsoon, and there was exactly one reason anyone would do that.

Juno went aft fast, taking the wet deck in strides, and put her head into the pilothouse.

"Vesper. Wake up. We've got company on the water—and they're not slowing down for the snags."
