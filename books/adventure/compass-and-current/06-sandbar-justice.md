## 6. Sandbar Justice

The river hit them at dawn like a debt collector.

One moment the Kestrel was sliding through the interior channel, water the color of strong tea, banks dripping green on either side. The next, the hull shuddered, groaned, and stopped as if the river had put out a hand.

Vesper was thrown against the wheel and caught herself before her knees did. Below, something heavy toppled—Bram's toolbox, by the sound of it—and Juno's voice came up the companionway with a word that would have gotten her mouth washed out in any decent port.

"Sandbar," Vesper called down. "Everyone breathe."

She climbed to the bow rail and looked over. The water here was shallow enough to read the bottom: a long spine of pale silt running diagonal across the channel, barely a hand's width below the surface, and the Kestrel sat on it like a drunk on a church pew. The interior route was exactly what Tobin had promised—narrow, unpatrolled, unmapped in anything newer than forty years. Unmapped meant unmarked. Unmarked meant this.

The monsoon had shifted everything downstream of them. Bars that had been charted were gone; bars that had never existed were suddenly holding court in mid-channel. The river didn't redraw its maps for anyone's convenience.

Bram came up wiping grease from his hands, squinted at the water, and delivered his verdict with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own autopsy. "We're hard aground. Stern's floating, bow's not. If we try to back off under power we'll dig the screw into that silt and be here until the dry season."

"How long until the dry season?"

"Four months."

"Wonderful." Vesper turned. "Options."

"Lighten ship," Bram said. "Everything that isn't bolted down goes ashore or into the shallows. Cargo, spare chain, the water tanks if we have to. Then we kedge off—run an anchor out to deep water with the dinghy, and winch ourselves over the bar."

Juno appeared at the rail, hair still wet from washing, and looked at the bar with the flat expression she reserved for bad news. "How much weight?"

"All of it," Vesper said. "Including the chest."

That landed among them like a dropped anchor. The chest—the chest—out of the bilge, out from behind the false floor, into daylight where anyone with a spyglass could see it.

"It goes in the dinghy," Vesper said before anyone could argue. "It stays within arm's reach of one of us at all times. We are not leaving it buried on a sandbar in the middle of contested water."

"And if Wray comes while we're lightening ship?" Juno asked. "We'll be spread out on a beach like laundry."

Vesper had been asking herself the same question since they'd entered the interior. She'd run the numbers every way she knew how, and the numbers kept coming back ugly. They needed six hours to lighten and kedge. Wray had watchers at every lock on the high channels, which meant he'd known within hours which way they'd gone. A launch could make this stretch of river in—

She didn't finish the thought, because Tobin Sela was standing at the stern rail with a look on his face she'd learned to read in three days of wary coexistence. It was the look of a man rehearsing something.

"Say it," she said.

He blinked. "Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since midnight. You've been pacing the deck like a caged cat, and I don't pay you enough to wear a hole in my planking."

"I don't get paid at all."

"Exactly. Say it."

* * *

They gathered in the pilothouse because it was small and because Vesper wanted the wheel at her back. Rain had started again, a fine gray sift against the windows, and through it the sandbar stretched away like the backbone of some drowned animal.

Tobin stood with his hands in his pockets and looked, for the first time since boarding, genuinely uncomfortable. Not afraid—she'd seen him afraid, back when Wray's name came up—but ashamed, which was rarer and more interesting.

"You should know who I am," he said. "All of who I am. Before you trust me with what's in that chest."

"We already know who you are," Juno said. "Meridian archivist's grandson. Chasing your grandfather's paper."

"That's the version I sold you at the fueling post." He took a breath. "It's true. It's just not all of it. Three years ago I was broke in Port Meridian, and a man named Calloway—Ashe's factor—found me. He knew my grandfather had been the republic's registrar. He didn't know about any deeds. What he wanted was access to the archives. Shipping manifests, land surveys, who owned what before the drowning. He paid me to copy documents. For two years I copied documents."

Vesper kept her voice level. "You sold information to Ashe's men."

"I sold information to Ashe's men," Tobin agreed. "Surveys of villages that hadn't been surveyed since the republic fell. Boundaries. Water rights. Everything Ashe needed to know exactly which claims were worth buying cheap and which families were worth squeezing first." His jaw tightened. "I told myself it was scholarship. Paper for paper's sake. Then one day I recognized a village name in a purchase order—a place my grandmother used to sing about—and I understood what I'd been doing. I'd been drawing the noose and handing it over with both hands."

The rain ticked against the glass. Down on deck, Bram had started rigging the winch, the sounds of his work drifting up like a metronome nobody had asked for.

"So why come to me?" Vesper said. "Why not just disappear? Men like Calloway don't chase archivists across deltas."

"Because of the ledger." Tobin's eyes came up to hers. "When I found the chest—or rather, when I tracked the flood's debris line and figured out someone else had found it—I wasn't chasing an inheritance, Vesper. My grandfather's estate is a trunk of moldy sermons. I was chasing the chance to undo what I did. Every deed in that chest is a village I helped map for the man who stole it. Delivering them isn't justice falling into my lap. It's the only payment I can make that Calloway can't buy back."

Silence, except for the rain and the winch.

Juno spoke first, and her voice was strange—flat, but with something underneath it, like ice over moving water. "My town was evicted when I was nine. Concession men with survey maps. Somebody drew those maps."

"Somebody drew those maps," Tobin said. "Maybe men like me."

"Do you know their names? The villages you surveyed?"

"Most of them."

"Then you're going to write them down," Juno said. "Every one. And when we deliver these deeds, you're going to stand in front of those elders and say it to their faces. That's your price. That's what forgiveness costs."

Tobin held her gaze. "Agreed."

Vesper watched him take it without flinching and felt something shift in her chest that she didn't entirely welcome. She had built her life on a simple arithmetic: trust no one, owe no one, need no one. It had kept the Kestrel afloat and her skin intact for eleven years. And now here was a man confessing to the exact crime that had orphaned half her crew, standing on her deck in the rain, asking not for absolution but for a job to do.

Forgiveness, she thought, is just another wager. You put down your doubt and hope the other party doesn't fold.

"Bram!" she called through the door. "How's that anchor rode looking?"

"Long enough to reach deep water if the dinghy crew doesn't mind getting wet."

"They mind," Juno said. "But we'll manage."

* * *

By full dark the Kestrel was stripped to her bones.

Cargo stacked on the sandbar under oilcloth. Chain coiled in the shallows. Even the galley stove sat on the mud like a stranded crab. The chest rode in the dinghy, tied to Vesper's wrist by a length of line she'd knotted herself, because she trusted knots more than circumstances.

The moon came up fat and white through breaking cloud, and the work went silver. Juno and Tobin hauled the kedge anchor out in the dinghy, oars dipping quiet, and set it in the dark water past the bar's edge. Bram fed the winch. Vesper took the capstan bar beside him, and together they walked the Kestrel off the sand inch by groaning inch, the hull shuddering, silt boiling up around them like smoke.

"She's moving," Bram grunted. "Half a fathom. Keep walking."

"Keep walking," Vesper agreed, and put her weight into the bar.

That was when Juno, waist-deep at the dinghy line, went rigid and pointed downstream.

A light. Small, far off, riding low and fast. Running without running lights except one lantern, which meant someone who didn't want to be seen, which meant someone who already knew where he was going.

"Wray," Tobin breathed.

"He can't know for certain," Bram said. "Could be a smuggler. Could be—"

The light grew.

"Get her loose," Vesper said. "Now. All hands."

What followed was not seamanship so much as violence. They walked the capstan until the bars sang. The Kestrel ground free of the bar with a sound like a tooth pulled, wallowed, caught the current, and swung her bow downstream. Vesper left the winch spinning and ran for the wheel, hauling the chest aboard with her own hands, line and all.

Behind them, the launch's lantern resolved into a shape: low, black, engine note flat and hungry. Closing.

"Full ahead?" Bram asked.

"Full ahead." Vesper threw the throttle open and felt the old girl answer, engines coughing then catching, the deck trembling under her boots. "But he's faster and he knows it. He's not trying to catch us tonight."

"Then what's he doing?"

Vesper watched the lantern hold its distance astern, patient as a hook. "Herding us. There's a narrows ten miles down—he wants us tired, in the dark, running scared into water he controls." She glanced at Tobin, who stood at the rail with the moon on his face, and found—to her own irritation—that she believed him now. Believed him the way you believe a rope you've tested with your whole weight.

"Well," Tobin said quietly. "At least we won't be bored."

Vesper put the wheel over and ran for the narrows, the Kestrel groaning under full steam, the drowned republic's deeds dry below decks, and Wray's lantern burning steady behind them like an eye that refused to blink.
