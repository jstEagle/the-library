## 7. The Drowned Republic's Heirs

Tobin smelled the village before he saw it—woodsmoke and drying fish and the green rot of the floodplain, a smell that reached back into him like a hand into a drawer. Forty years since the Republic of Meridia went under the water, but the smell hadn't changed. The river kept it, the way it kept everything.

The Kestrel came around the last bend at midmorning, her hull riding low with fuel and intention, and there was Sarn's Landing strung along the levee like beads on a wire: stilt houses, net racks, a temple roof gone silver with age, children already running the bank to see who'd come. Vesper stood at the wheel with her arms folded, watching the channel markers that weren't markers at all anymore—the monsoon had moved every one of them, and she was reading the water by eye, by the color of the boils and the lean of the current against the far bank.

"You've been here before," Tobin said. It wasn't a question. He'd watched her take the fork past the drowned church without touching the chart.

"My father ran mail here." She didn't look at him. "Before the concessions. When it was still legal for these people to own their own ground."

Juno was on the bow with a boat hook, fending off a drifting fence rail, and Bram's engine muttered below in the tone of an old man complaining about weather but going anyway. Tobin stood at the rail with the canvas satchel between his feet and his heart going like a boy's.

He had never seen one of the villages. He had seen the deeds—a thousand times, in the archive, in his grandfather's notes, in the ledger pages they'd pulled from Ashe's family fraud—but paper was paper. This was the thing the paper was *for*. Stilt houses. Net racks. A woman mending a seine in the shade, hands moving without looking, the way his grandmother had once described Meridian women working, in a letter he'd read so many times the creases had gone soft.

*They will tell you the republic failed,* she had written. *It did not fail. It was sold.*

* * *

The elders met them at the top of the levee stairs, seven old men and women in clothes too good for a Tuesday, which meant they had dressed for this, which meant word had come ahead of them somehow, which meant the river's telegraph ran faster than any launch Wray could put on the water.

Their headman was a woman named Ondine Vasq, small and brown and straight-backed as a piling, with a voice that didn't need raising because everyone leaned toward it anyway. She looked at Vesper first, then at Tobin, then longest at the satchel.

"You're the archivist's grandson," she said.

"I'm his grandson," Tobin said. "I don't know what I am besides that."

"Come inside. The deeds don't go on a levee."

They gathered in the temple, on mats, under a ceiling fan that turned with the patience of something that had outlasted three governments. And this was where Tobin learned what he should have known from the books: that the fraud wasn't history here. It was a song.

Ondine's sister began it, low, almost conversational, and the others joined until it filled the room—a counting song, verse after verse, each verse a parcel of land and the name of the family it was taken from and the year the paper was forged. The Iversen verse. The Solano verse. Her own grandmother's verse, sung by a woman who had learned it as a child and never been allowed to stop singing it, because a song is the one deed no magistrate can burn.

"The Ashe company bought the surveyor," Ondine said when the song ended, in the flat tone of someone reciting arithmetic. "The surveyor redrew the maps. The magistrate stamped them. Our families were given ninety days and ninety days is not enough to fight paper." She looked at Tobin. "You have the originals."

"I have the originals," Tobin said. "And the ledger. And the surveyor's confession, signed, witnessed, sealed."

He laid them out on the mat between them, and the room went very quiet, and Tobin understood suddenly why his grandfather had spent his whole life in the archive instead of coming here. Some rooms you can't walk into until you're carrying the right thing.

Ondine touched the seal of the Republic of Meridia with two fingers, the way you touch a sleeping child.

"Forty years," she said. "We sang it into our grandchildren so they wouldn't forget, and half of them thought we were singing about weather." She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "And now you bring us the paper."

"Not me," Tobin said. "Her." He nodded at Vesper, who was standing by the door with the expression of a woman who wanted very much to be elsewhere and would die before leaving. "I found the chest. She hauled it through Wray's blockade and a flooded cut to get it here."

Vesper said nothing. Juno, sitting cross-legged near the door with her wounded arm bound and her chin up, grinned like this was the best entertainment of the season.

Ondine rose and did something none of the histories had prepared Tobin for: she bowed to Vesper. Not deeply. Just enough. Captain to captain, maybe, or elder to the person holding the knife while others do the cutting.

"The permits expire in nineteen days," Ondine said. "When they lapse, the concessions void, and with these deeds filed before a lawful court, the land reverts. We file tomorrow. Word goes downriver tonight to the other villages. You'll carry the rest of the deeds?"

"That's the arrangement," Vesper said.

"Then eat first. Nobody fights paper hungry."

* * *

They ate on the temple steps—river fish and rice and a pickle so hot Juno drank half a jug of water and asked for more—and for about forty minutes Tobin let himself feel something he hadn't felt since he was a boy listening to his grandfather talk about Meridia in the past tense, always the past tense, always *they had, they were, we lost.*

Hope. Stupid, warm, sit-down-and-eat hope.

The village children had adopted the Kestrel on sight. They swarmed the gangplank asking Bram questions about the engine that he answered with increasing alarm, and Juno taught six of them a card game that was definitely cheating them, and Vesper sat apart on the rail with tea she wasn't drinking, watching the water the way she always watched the water, like it owed her money or an apology.

Tobin brought her a refill and sat down at the far end of the rail, not close. He'd learned that much.

"They'll win," he said. "You know that. With the deeds filed and the permits lapsing—"

"I know how permits work." She turned the cup in her hands. "I also know how men like Ashe work. Nineteen days is a long time."

"It's not that long."

"For him it's plenty." She nodded downriver, where the afternoon haze had thickened over the water. "A man who'd forge a survey doesn't wait for a deadline. Deadlines are for people playing by rules."

Tobin wanted to argue. He was, by training and temperament, an arguer—he'd spent three years in the archive learning that every catastrophe has a paper trail and every paper trail has a loophole. But he'd also spent three days on this boat learning Vesper Iversen, and Vesper Iversen did not say things to hear herself say them.

So when the runner came up the levee stairs at dusk, both of them were already standing.

The runner was a boy, twelve maybe, soaked to the waist, having run the towpath from the relay station eight miles down. He went straight to Ondine and gasped out his message twice, and Tobin watched the words move through the crowd like a stain spreading through cloth.

Ondine turned around slowly. Her face had gone still—not calm; still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"Say it again," Tobin said. "All of it."

"The magistrate at Verren's Crossing has issued new instruments," the boy recited, having clearly rehearsed. "The concession expiry is advanced fourteen days. New date is five days from today. All filings must be lodged before the new date or they're void. There's—" he faltered, checked a scrap of paper in his fist, "—there's a reward posted. For the chest. Two thousand crowns. And a description of a woman captain and a man traveling under the name Sela."

The levee went quiet enough that Tobin could hear the temple fan turning.

Five days. They had planned on nineteen. Four villages downriver, the flooded interior route, Wray's watchers at every lock—and now five days, with a price on their heads and the law itself rewritten overnight, bought and stamped like everything else Ashe had ever touched.

"He bought the magistrate," Tobin said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "That's not possible. Magistrates can't amend—"

"He bought the magistrate the way his grandfather bought the surveyor," Ondine said. "Paper obeys whoever pays for the ink. We told you that forty years ago. Apparently the lesson needed repeating."

Vesper set down her untouched tea. When she spoke, it was to Ondine, but Tobin felt it land on him like a hand on the shoulder.

"File tonight. Everything. Before he can buy anyone else."

"We'll need it witnessed by a traveling officer of the court—"

"Bram knows one. Corrupt enough to be reachable, honest enough to be terrified of being caught on the wrong side later. He'll come." She was already moving, already giving orders in that low carrying voice—Juno to the boat, Bram to fire the engine, the satchel counted and resealed. Then she stopped at the rail beside Tobin and looked downriver, where the first stars were coming out over water that would be running high and black and fast all the way to the next village.

"Nineteen days was a gift," she said. "Five days is a race."

Tobin looked at the darkening channel and thought of his grandfather's letter, the last line, the one he'd never understood until now: *Meridia will not be returned. It will be taken back, quickly, by people who are willing to drown for it.*

"How many villages left?" he asked.

"Four." Vesper swung down onto the deck. "And four hundred miles of river that wants us dead."

Behind them, on the levee, the elders had begun to sing again—the counting song, faster now, the verses tumbling over each other like water over a weir—and Tobin took up the satchel and followed her down into the dark.
