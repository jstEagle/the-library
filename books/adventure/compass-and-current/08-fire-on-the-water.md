## 8. Fire on the Water

The attack came at the hour Vesper trusted least—the dead stretch past midnight when even the river seemed to sleep, and a captain's vigilance thinned to a thread.

She was in the wheelhouse with a cup of cold coffee, watching the channel markers blur past under a half moon, when Bram's voice crackled up from the engine room.

"Vesper. Something behind us. No running lights."

She set down the cup. "How long?"

"Long enough that they've been matching our speed. They're waiting for something."

She knew what. The narrows above Sallow Bend, where the channel pinched between two drowned plantations and there was no room to maneuver—no room for anyone. Wray had been herding them all day, his launch hanging back at the edge of visibility like a wolf that had learned patience.

"Juno," she called down the deck tube. "Wake Tobin. Get to the chest."

The Kestrel's engine note changed as Bram gave her more steam, and Vesper felt the old boat gather herself, hull shuddering, paddle wheel biting deep. Too late. The launch came out of the dark on their starboard quarter, running lights blazing now, all pretense abandoned, and it hit them amidships with a crunch that threw Vesper against the wheel.

Wood screamed. The deck rolled. Somewhere below, Juno shouted, and then the sound that turned Vesper's blood to ice water: the chest hitting the deck planks hard enough to burst its hasp, and the slide of paper against wood as the Kestrel heeled toward the rail.

She got the wheel over hard to port, using the launch's own momentum against it, and felt the boats grind apart with a shriek of timber. The Kestrel righted herself. Below, Tobin's voice, ragged: "Got it—I've got it—" and then a splash, and Juno screaming his name.

Vesper lashed the wheel and ran.

* * *

The scene on the afterdeck was lantern-light and chaos. Tobin was sprawled against the rail, soaked to the waist, the chest clutched to his chest like a child. One of the deeds—a single sheet, ink already feathering where river water had touched it—lay on the deck, and beyond the rail, in the black water, Juno was swimming hard after a second sheet that skated away on the current like a living thing.

"Juno!" Vesper grabbed a boathook. "Back to the boat! Now!"

"I can reach it—"

"The river doesn't give things back! Come back!"

Juno's hand closed on the paper just as the current took her, sweeping her downstream faster than she could swim against it. Vesper saw the moment she understood—saw the girl's head turn toward the receding lights of the Kestrel—and then Juno went under, and didn't come up.

Vesper didn't think. She dropped the boathook, kicked off her boots, and dove.

The delta at night was a second world—cold, brown, blind. She swam by feel and faith, following the pull of the current, and found Juno by the flash of white paper still clenched in her fist. The girl was tangled in her own jacket, dragging herself down, and Vesper got an arm across her chest and hauled, kicking for the surface, for the lights, for anything.

They broke air together. The Kestrel was fifty yards upstream and falling behind; Wray's launch was circling, its searchlight swinging toward them like a slow yellow eye.

"There," Vesper gasped. "The willow snag. Kick."

They made the snag—an old drowned tree leaning out of the shallows—and clung to it while the searchlight swept past, close enough that Vesper could hear men's voices arguing about whether a body floating face-down was worth fishing out. Then the light moved on, hunting the chest, hunting the Kestrel, and Vesper pressed her forehead against the wet bark and laughed, or wept, or both.

"You're bleeding," Juno said. Her voice was small. Her left arm hung wrong at the shoulder, wrenched in the fall or the swim.

"So are you."

"Yeah." A shaky breath. "Captain. I got the deed."

Vesper looked at the paper crushed in the girl's fist, ink smeared but legible, the Meridian seal still proud in its corner. She thought of every mile of river between here and the last village, every lock where Wray's watchers waited, every hour ticking off the permits' life.

"Then we'd better get you home," she said.

* * *

Bram fished them out with the skiff a mile downstream, where the willows thinned and the bank offered footing. By lantern light in the engine room, he bound Juno's shoulder with a boat wrench and a length of canvas strap while she bit down on a leather belt and refused to cry out, and Vesper stood dripping on her own deck feeling something new kindle in her chest—not fear, not dread, but fury, clean and clarifying as struck flint.

For years she had survived by being no one. Small wake, small profile, small life. She had watched Ashe's dredges chew the river into submission and said nothing. She had watched Wray's warrants empty whole landings and steered wide. Invisibility had been her religion, and tonight it had nearly cost her crew her crew.

No more.

"Tobin," she said. "Where's Ashe's dredge?"

He blinked water from his eyes. "What?"

"The *Meridian Queen*. His big one. Where does she work this stretch?"

Tobin understood slowly, then all at once. "Vesper. She draws eight feet. You can't—"

"She's anchored mid-channel off Corvane Landing, isn't she? Dredging the cut so his barges can run the interior route once the permits lapse. Sitting fat and lit up like a carnival." Vesper wrung out her hair. "And we draw four."

Bram looked up from Juno's shoulder. "You want to ram a dredge."

"I want to strand one." She met his eyes. "Her anchors hold her mid-river. If her spuds come loose—if she drifts onto the bar at Corvane Point—she's stuck there through the permit deadline. Every barge she's meant to load sits idle. Ashe loses the interior route entirely."

"And Wray?" Tobin asked quietly.

"Wray will be aboard her, or near her, negotiating his master's business. Which means while everyone's watching the dredge—" she allowed herself the ghost of a smile— "the Kestrel runs the flooded cut at Sallow Bend, clean through to the last village."

Silence, except the tick of the cooling engine and Juno's careful breathing.

"That's suicide twice over," Bram said.

"It's a fight," Vesper said. "I'm done choosing the other thing."

* * *

They came at the dredge an hour before dawn, running dark, engine muffled to a mutter. The *Meridian Queen* sat exactly where Tobin said she would be—a mountain of iron and arc-light mid-channel, her crane arms folded, her wash pumps thundering sediment into the gray water. Barges rafted alongside, loaded and waiting. And moored at her flank, riding easy, Wray's launch.

Vesper stood at the wheel with Juno beside her, the girl's strapped shoulder making her stand crooked, her jaw set like someone chewing glass.

"Spuds are on the starboard side," Tobin reported from the bow, glasses to his eyes. "Anchors fore and aft. She'll swing if she comes loose—current's running two knots toward Corvane Point."

"Then we introduce her to it." Vesper eased the wheel over. "Bram. Everything she has."

The Kestrel surged. They crossed the last half-mile of black water in silence broken only by the wheel's churn, and Vesper aimed her boat's scarred bow at the gap between the dredge's stern anchor line and her port spud housing, at the taut cable that held three hundred tons of iron against the current.

"Brace," she said, and put the wheel hard over at the last moment, letting the Kestrel's momentum swing her stern across the cable like a blade.

The impact was nothing like the launch's ramming—no crunch, just a deep groan as the cable parted, and then the *Meridian Queen* began, majestically, horribly, to move. Arc-lights swung. Men poured from the deckhouse shouting. The stern swung wide, the current took her beam-on, and Vesper rang for full ahead as the dredge ground up onto Corvane Point's bar with a sound like a cathedral settling into its grave.

Behind them, Wray's launch was casting off, engines howling.

"They'll run us down before we clear the bend," Juno said.

"No," Vesper said. "They'll catch us. That's different."

* * *

Wray's launch caught them at the mouth of Sallow Cut, where the flooded rapids roared through a gap in the levee no wider than the Kestrel herself. The launch's bow gun—Vesper hadn't known Wray carried one—put two shots through the wheelhouse before Bram killed the lights and they plunged into the white water.

The Kestrel handled it the way she handled everything: badly, bravely, taking water. But the launch was bigger, heavier, built for open channels, and Wray knew it. He drove straight in after them.

Halfway through the cut, the Kestrel wedged between two submerged pilings and stopped, hull grinding, water pouring over the rails. And Wray's launch bore down with its searchlight full on them, and men with grappling hooks in the bow.

"We can't fight that," Tobin shouted over the roar.

"We don't fight it." Vesper pulled the chest from beneath the false floor, wrapped it in oilcloth, lashed it to his back with the same straps holding Juno's shoulder together. "We leave."

"What?"

"The rapids go through. We go through faster." She pointed at the churning slot between the pilings, downstream of the wreck they were becoming. "Swim it, ride it out below the falls, and walk to the village. It's four miles. Wray can't follow—he'd lose his launch in that water, and he knows it."

Tobin stared at her. "You've done this before."

"Once. I was younger and stupider." She cinched the last knot. "Stay flat, feet first, don't fight the water. The river takes what fights it. It carries what doesn't."

Juno was already at the rail, pale and grim, one-armed and furious. "I'm coming."

"No, you're not. You're staying with Bram and the Kestrel. Get her off these pilings if you can. If you can't—" Vesper gripped the girl's good shoulder. "Beach her. Walk to the village. We'll find you."

The launch was thirty yards out. Grappling lines coiled in the bow like snakes waking.

Vesper looked at Tobin. He looked back, and whatever passed between them needed no words—they were both gamblers now, betting everything on the same card.

"Together," he said.

"Together."

They went over the rail into the roaring dark, and the river took them.

* * *

The rapids tried to kill them twice. Once at the falls, where Vesper went under and came up coughing half the cut through her lungs; once in the tailrace, where Tobin's foot caught a snag and she had to dive to free him, the chest dragging them both toward the bottom until the strap tore half away and they surfaced together, gasping, clinging to a fence post driven into the shallows by some farmer forty years dead.

They crawled out on the gravel bar below the falls as the sky went gray with dawn. Behind them, Wray's launch sat wedged in the cut, holed on the same pilings, his men shouting uselessly at the water. Ahead, four miles of muddy road wound toward the last village, where the final deeds waited to be delivered, where the permits would lapse in less than a day.

Tobin lay on his back, chest heaving, oilcloth bundle still strapped to him, and started laughing—a wild, disbelieving sound.

"We made it," he said. "We actually—"

"Don't jinx it." Vesper sat up, wrung the river from her shirt, and looked east, where the sun was coming up over the flooded fields like a coin flipped into the air, still deciding which face to land on. Her whole body ached. Her boat was dying on the rocks behind her. Her crew was scattered along ten miles of river.

And she was smiling.

"Come on," she said, hauling Tobin to his feet. "We've got a race to finish."

They started walking.
