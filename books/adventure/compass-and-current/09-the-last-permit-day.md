## 9. The Last Permit Day

The Kestrel was dying, and she knew it.

Vesper felt it through her boots before Bram said a word—a new note in the engine's thrum, a shudder in the deck plates that hadn't been there yesterday. Water in the bilge rising faster than the pumps could argue with it. The hull had taken the rapids at Serpent's Teeth two nights ago and never quite forgiven her for it.

"She'll hold," Bram said, kneeling in the engine room with a lantern between his knees, his hands black to the wrist. "She'll hold until we don't need her anymore. That's all I'm promising."

"That's all I'm asking."

He looked up at her then, and in the lantern light his face was older than it had been a month ago. They were all older. Juno's arm was strapped across her chest where Wray's boarding hook had torn it open, and Tobin hadn't slept a full night since the fire on the water, and Vesper herself had stopped counting the bruises. "You know what you're asking," Bram said quietly. "The cut at Graywater. Nobody's run it flooded. Not once. Not ever."

"I know what the maps say."

"The maps are forty years old and written by men who drowned."

"Then they're due an update." She crouched beside him, put her hand flat on the hull plating, felt the tremor come up through the steel like a pulse. Somewhere below the waterline, a seam was weeping, and every mile would widen it. "Permits lapse at dawn, Bram. Ashe moved the date up. If the last deeds aren't in Elder Sefa's hands by first light, forty years of theft becomes legal on a magistrate's signature, and everything we've bled for turns to paper ash."

Bram wiped his hands slowly on a rag that was already beyond saving. "Juno votes yes. Tobin votes yes. So it doesn't matter what I vote."

"It matters to me."

"Then here's my vote: if we're going to kill her, we kill her doing something." He stood, joints cracking like kindling. "Get us to the mouth of the cut, Captain. I'll keep her heart beating till you get there."

* * *

Wray was waiting at the Graywater landing, because of course he was.

Vesper saw the launch from two miles out, its running lights doused but its silhouette unmistakable against the last gray band of night—low, wide-beamed, the enforcer's own vessel, anchored square across the mouth of the village landing with a floodlamp burning at its bow like a single hostile eye. Behind it, the lamps of Elder Sefa's village glimmered on the bluff, close enough to almost touch. Close enough to be worthless.

"He's not even hiding," Tobin said at her shoulder, binoculars raised. "He wants you to see him. He wants you to try the landing so he has an excuse."

"He doesn't need an excuse. He needs a signature, and he needs dawn." Vesper took the glasses. Through the lens she could pick out figures on the launch's deck—Wray's hired muscle, four of them, maybe five—and Wray himself at the rail, motionless, patient as a heron. Somewhere behind them both, upstream, Corvin Ashe's dredge sat stranded mid-river where she'd rammed it, rusting into the current, but Ashe himself had slipped away downstream days ago with lawyers and papers and the magistrate's bought pen. The dredge was lost. The war wasn't.

"There's another way in," Juno said. She'd come up despite the arm, her sling dark with river spray. "The old ferry cut. Graywater Narrows."

Tobin turned. "That's not a route. That's a rumor."

"That's my grandmother's route." Juno's voice was flat, and Vesper heard under it the town that had been drowned when the concessions came—the evicted streets, the burned wharf, the whole history Juno carried like ballast. "Before they closed it, the ferries ran it every day. It floods in monsoon, sure. But it floods *through*. The water goes in one end and comes out the other, and there's a channel down the middle if you read the current right. My grandmother read it right for thirty years."

"And the captains who died trying it?"

"They didn't have my grandmother." Juno looked at Vesper. "They didn't have you."

Vesper looked at the narrows. Even in the dark she could hear it—a low, continuous roar, the sound of the whole delta being squeezed through a gap barely wider than the Kestrel herself, white water flashing faintly where the floodlamp glow caught the spray. No captain had survived it flooded. That was the legend, and legends were usually just drownings with better stories attached.

But the permits lapsed at dawn. And Wray owned the landing. And the river had never once offered her a fair choice, only a choice.

"Bram," she called down the hatch. "How much does she have left?"

The engineer's voice floated up, dry as driftwood: "Enough for one miracle or two mistakes. Pick fast."

Vesper spun the wheel hard over. "Miracle," she said. "Hang on to something."

* * *

The Narrows took her the way a fist takes a fly.

The first hundred yards were manageable—fast water, standing waves, the banks closing in like a book shutting. Vesper drove the Kestrel straight down the tongue of the current, reading the water the way Juno coached from beside her, the girl shouting over the roar: *"Left of the boil! Left! Now hard right where it folds—"*

Then the channel bent, and the river stopped being water and became weather.

It hit them broadside, a wall of flood-swollen current slamming the hull, and the Kestrel slewed sideways with a groan Vesper felt in her teeth. The wheel fought her like something alive. A wave came over the bow entire, green and solid, tore the canvas off the forward hatch, and swept the deck clean of everything that wasn't tied down—including, for one endless second, Tobin, who went over the rail to his waist before Juno caught his collar one-handed and hauled him back, screaming words nobody would ever reconstruct.

Below, Bram kept the engine screaming. She could feel him down there feeding the old girl everything she had, coaxing revolutions out of a dying heart, and the Kestrel answered—answered her captain, answered her crew, answered forty years of rivers and cargo and stubbornness—by driving her nose back around into the current and holding it.

"Water's coming up!" Tobin bellowed, stamping the deck. "Fast!"

"Everything's coming up!" Vesper shouted back, and laughed, actually laughed, because the alternative was terror and terror steered badly. "Bram! Give me everything that's left!"

"You already took it!"

"Give me its ghost, then!"

The engine surged—one last impossible surge, the sound of a machine spending its final coin—and the Kestrel climbed the standing wave at the narrows' throat, hung there weightless for a heartbeat with spray flying off her rails like wings, and then dropped down the far side into the long, deep, merciful calm of the lower channel.

Behind them, the Narrows roared on, having failed to add them to its ledger.

Ahead, the bluff lamps of Elder Sefa's village swung into view from the upstream side—the side Wray's launch couldn't cover without abandoning the landing. Vesper brought the wheel around gentle, gentle, cradling the wounded boat toward shore.

"We made it," Tobin whispered. "We actually—"

The Kestrel lurched. Settled. Lurched again, worse, and this time the groan came up through the whole frame, and Bram appeared at the top of the hatch with water sheeting around his ankles.

"Seam's gone," he said simply. "Final seam. We have minutes, Captain. Maybe."

Vesper looked at the shore. At the village lamps. At the launch downstream, its floodlamp swinging toward them now, Wray's silhouette rigid at the rail as he understood, too late, which way the arithmetic had gone.

Then she looked at her boat—her home, her fortress, her invisibility made of iron and pine—and made the only choice left that mattered.

"Bram. Steer her onto the mudflat. Between the landing and the channel."

Bram stared at her. Then he understood, and something in his face broke and reformed into something steadier. "Aye, Captain." He touched the engine housing once, the way you'd touch a hand. "One last job, old girl."

The Kestrel came in slow and deliberate, listing, her pumps dead, her heart spent—and ran herself up onto the shoal at the mouth of the landing with a grinding shudder that Vesper felt in her bones like a farewell. There she settled, wedged fast, hull aground, decks tilted—but squarely, immovably, exactly between Wray's launch and the village stairs.

A blockade of her own.

"Everybody off," Vesper said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "Take the chest. Go."

* * *

They carried the deeds up the village stairs as the sky went from black to bruise to gold.

Elder Sefa met them at the top—an old woman with a walking stick and eyes that had watched forty years of lies arrive by water. Behind her, the whole village had turned out, and word was still spreading; people came down from the terraces, out of the fish sheds, carrying lamps against the fading dark. Tobin opened the chest on the landing platform with shaking hands, and Vesper watched him lay the deeds out one by one, each seal of the drowned Republic of Meridia catching the first true light of morning.

And then, impossibly, the crowd parted, and Magistrate Oyelaran walked through it.

Alone. Unarmed. In plain clothes, his warrant chain left behind somewhere, his face gray with the look of a man who had decided something expensive overnight. He stopped before the deeds and looked at them for a long moment.

"Ashe bought me," he said, loudly enough for the whole landing to hear. "Two weeks ago. I signed the acceleration order. I signed warrants. I told myself it was law." He lifted his eyes to Vesper, and there was no mercy in them, only accounting. "This morning I read the original fraud ledger your archivist sent ahead. Every page. I checked it against the concession registries myself, all night." He turned to the villagers. "At dawn, the concessions lapse. As of this sunrise, they lapse *void*—not renewed, not transferred, not purchasable. The land returns to its lawful heirs under the Meridian Restoration Act. Corvin Ashe holds nothing. His claims are dead paper." He paused. "I am prepared to swear to all of it, under oath, in any court that will have me."

The sound that came off the crowd was not a cheer, not at first—it was deeper than that, a low collective exhale, forty years leaving a thousand lungs at once. Then it became a cheer, and then it became singing, the old songs, the ones about the fraud, except now the verses had somewhere to land.

Downstream, Wray's launch idled at the edge of the shallows, blocked by a grounded riverboat, facing a defected magistrate and a village full of witnesses. Vesper watched the enforcer stand at his rail for a long minute, weighing whatever enforcers weigh. Then he killed his floodlamp, turned his bow downstream, and was gone into the morning mist—not defeated, perhaps, but unhired.

Vesper stayed on the stairs. She didn't go up to the platform. This part belonged to Tobin, and to Juno, and to the villages. She leaned on the rail of her grounded, gutted, magnificent ship and watched the sun clear the bluff, and felt the tears come without asking permission, and let them.

Bram came and stood beside her. Neither of them said anything for a while.

"She was a good boat," he said finally.

"She was the best boat." Vesper pushed off the rail. Below them, the tide was turning, and the Kestrel's hull gleamed wet and broken in the new light, salvage waiting to happen. "Get me a hammer, Bram. We're not letting the river have the bell."

* * *

By noon the whole delta knew. By evening, the songs had new verses.

Vesper stood on the mudflat as the light went long and copper, watching salvagers' lights bloom along the Kestrel's flank, listening to Juno and Tobin argue cheerfully about keel length in the way of people who had already decided they were staying. Somewhere downstream, a dredge rusted. Somewhere upriver, a man with no claims left was learning what the river did with men like him.

Her hands were empty. For the first time since she was nineteen years old, Vesper Iversen owned no boat, owed no debts, and was known by name in every village from the Narrows to the sea.

It felt, she discovered, exactly like freedom.

Then a boy came running down the beach with a message flimsy in his fist, salt-stained and urgent, and said, breathless: "Captain—they said to find the captain of the Kestrel. There's a ship standing in off the bar. Meridian colors. And they're asking for the woman who returned the deeds."

Vesper looked at the flimsy. Looked at the river.

"Well," she said. "Isn't that interesting."
