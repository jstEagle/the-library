## 10. New Keel, Old River

The bell came up on the third day of the salvage, tangled in a net of river grass and one very indignant catfish, and when it broke the surface the whole of Sallow Bend turned out on the mudbanks to watch.

Bram had rigged the winch off an old dredge piling, and Juno swam the last few feet down herself, because she said no one else's hands were small enough to work the shackle free. When she surfaced, gasping and grinning with the bell rope looped over her shoulder, the cheering started on the bank and didn't stop until she'd been hauled ashore and wrapped in two blankets and someone's grandmother's shawl.

Vesper stood at the waterline and let them look at her looking at the bell. That was the thing about being known now. People waited for her reactions like they meant something. Like they'd always meant something, and everyone had simply been too polite to say so back when she was nobody.

The Kestrel lay beached where Bram had run her aground that last dawn, half-buried in silt above the cut, her spine broken but her bones honest. The magistrate's assessor had come out, poked at the hull, and pronounced her a total loss with the satisfaction of a man reading someone else's obituary. Vesper had signed the paper without ceremony. Boats weren't people. Boats were promises, and promises could be made again.

But the bell was hers. Had been her father's before it was hers, and his before him, all the way back to a Meridian shipwright who'd cast it for a boat that never got built because the republic drowned first. There was a joke in there somewhere. Vesper hadn't found it yet, but Tobin swore it would come to her.

They hung the bell from a frame of salvaged keel timber outside the boathouse at Sallow Bend, and for now it rang only when the wind bothered to swing it — a low, cracked note that the village had already decided was good luck, though Visper suspected luck had nothing to do with anything the river did.

* * *

The new keel went down on a Tuesday, which meant nothing except that Tuesdays were when the fish market emptied early and half the village could come stand around and offer opinions.

She was smaller than the Kestrel. Forty feet against sixty, shallow-drafted, built for the interior channels that the big boats couldn't thread anymore — not since the flood had redrawn half the delta's map and left the old charts lying about their business like drunkards. Vesper had drawn the lines herself, working from memory and from the soundings Juno kept taking in a battered notebook, and every evening Tobin checked her figures against the Meridian archive copies he'd carried out of the drowned capital's records, wrapped in oilcloth, the way his grandfather had taught him.

"You're building her too narrow in the bow," Bram said, squinting at the lofted frame.

"I'm building her exactly as wide as she needs to be."

"She'll pitch in a crosswind."

"She'll turn in a crosswind. That's the point."

Bram grunted, which from Bram was a signature on a contract. He'd taken a room above the cooper's shop and hadn't mentioned leaving once. His tools hung on the boathouse wall in an order only he understood, and every morning he was down at the ways before Vesper, heating rivets, arguing with the tide.

Juno came and went between the boatyard and the villages upstream, carrying contracts now instead of contraband. The first fair agreements had gone out under the Kestrel's name — Vesper couldn't quite bring herself to retire it yet — and the rates were posted openly on the landing boards where anyone could read them: grain runs, mail, passengers, medicine. No more of the old arithmetic where a captain guessed what a village could bear and charged up to the line of breaking.

The elders at First Meridian had insisted on signing theirs in the old script, both languages side by side, and Tobin had read the Meridian aloud in a voice that shook only a little. Afterward an old woman named Sefa had pressed her palm flat against the paper and said, "Forty years. We sang it into our children so they wouldn't forget the shape of what was taken." She'd looked at Vesper then. "You brought back the shape."

Vesper had wanted to say something worthy. What she'd actually said was, "Your grain rates are on the board," and Sefa had laughed until she had to sit down, and somehow that had felt like the worthier answer anyway.

* * *

Wray was gone by then. Fled upriver three days after the permits lapsed, when the defected magistrate's affidavits reached the colonial office and it became clear that the enforcer's warrant had been bought paper from the start, issued against a chest whose existence Ashe had learned of through informants — through Tobin, once, long ago, and through others whose names were still surfacing like bodies after a flood.

Vesper had half hoped to see him go. Instead she heard about it secondhand: a hired launch at dawn, running lights dark, taking the high channel north with everything he owned stacked on deck. A man who had spent years making other people visible — marking them, listing them, hunting them — reduced to running dark himself.

"He'll land somewhere," Juno said, when the news came upriver. "Men like that always land somewhere."

"Let him," Vesper said. "Somewhere isn't here."

Ashe hadn't landed anywhere at all. His dredge still sat where the Kestrel had put her, mid-river off the old concession flats, settled onto a bar the flood had moved a mile east of where any chart said a bar should be. He'd tried to refloat her twice; the river had declined both invitations. Now the hulking iron shape listed gently in the current, rust blooming along her waterline in orange continents, her conveyor arm bent at an angle that looked almost apologetic. The villagers had stopped calling it Ashe's dredge. They called it the Monument.

Children swam out to it in summer. Vesper tried not to find this satisfying. She failed.

Ashe himself had retreated behind lawyers and the walls of his remaining concessions, but the concessions were dying — permits void, claims unenforceable, the whole architecture of paper he'd built on his grandfather's fraud dissolving in the courts the way the Republic of Meridia had dissolved in the water. Tobin tracked each ruling the way other men tracked weather, clipping notices and pinning them to the boathouse wall. The wall was getting crowded. Neither of them ever said what they'd do when there was nothing left to pin up.

* * *

On the night they drove the first futtock, Tobin brought out a bottle of plum brandy that had survived the flood inside the chest, sealed under the deeds, and nobody asked why an archivist would pack brandy with land titles. Some things you carried because they were worth carrying.

They drank it sitting on the keel timber itself, four of them in a row like sparrows on a rail, their boots hanging over the fresh-cut oak. The yard lamp threw their shadows long across the mud. Out past the landing, the river ran black and silver, doing what it always did, keeping what it kept.

"To the Kestrel," Bram said.

"To the Kestrel," they echoed, and drank.

Juno tipped her cup toward the dark water. "To the river, for spitting us all back out."

"That wasn't spit," Bram said. "That was the river deciding we were worth the trouble."

Tobin laughed, and then, quieter, turning his cup: "I keep thinking about my grandfather. He spent forty years copying those deeds, hiding them, sealing them, betting on a stranger finding a chest in a flood. He never met you. He just believed the river would hand them to somebody who'd do the right thing with them." He looked at Vesper. "He bet on a coin flip and won."

"It wasn't a coin flip," Vesper said. "It was a current. Currents go where they're going regardless. You just have to decide whether you're swimming with one or against it."

"Listen to her," Juno said. "Three months ago she wouldn't tell anyone her own birthday."

"My birthday is none of your business."

"See? Progress."

Vesper let them laugh. She even let herself smile, which was a currency she'd hoarded for years and was learning, slowly, to spend.

Later, when the others had gone and the yard was quiet, she sat alone on the keel with the last swallow of brandy and listened to the bell creak in the wind. Once, invisibility had felt like safety — a small boat, a false name on the manifests, cargo that appeared on no ledger anywhere. She had told herself the river rewarded those it couldn't see. But the river saw everything. It had seen the fraud and kept the receipts for forty years. It had seen the drowning and the evictions and the songs, and it had handed the proof to whoever happened to be holding the line that morning.

The river forgave nothing. It only remembered, and occasionally, returned.

What it kept, though — that was another matter. It kept its own kind. The ones who read the water instead of fighting it. The ones who pulled strangers out of floods without asking their names. The ones who came back, season after season, to the same bends and bars and broken landings, because coming back was the whole of the vow.

Vesper finished the brandy and set the cup down on the oak, and thought: *she'll need a name.* The new boat. Something true and not sentimental. She'd know it when the time came. Names arrived the way currents did — you couldn't force them, only be ready.

Behind her, the bell swung once in a gust off the water and rang its cracked, low note across the yard, and far upriver, where the channel narrowed into the flooded country the charts had given up on, something answered — or maybe it was only the wind, or maybe it was a boat running dark, coming home.
