## 1. The Wreck They Don't Name

The navy doesn't have a name for the wreck, which is how I know it's worth money. Everything the government bothers to name — ships, storms, wars, my father's inquest — gets named so it can be filed and forgotten. The things they leave blank are the things they're still afraid of.

I was forty feet down when I thought about that, which is a stupid depth for philosophy. Forty feet is where you start doing arithmetic with your air instead of your thoughts. But the cold off the escarpment coast has a way of making your brain wander while your hands stay busy, and my hands were busy: working the seal on a case wedged under a collapsed bulkhead of a ship that had never been here, according to every chart I owned.

The case was olive-drab, stenciled with numbers instead of words, and heavy in the way of things designed to survive being dropped out of aircraft. My gloves were old enough that I could feel its edges through them, sharp and certain. I clipped it to the lift line and gave it two tugs — *up* — and watched it rise through the green water toward the gray light, trailing bubbles like a thought escaping.

Then I hung there a moment longer, because the wreck deserved it.

She'd been a coastal survey vessel once; you could still read her bones. Twin screws, a hydrographic crane, the mounting plates where sensitive equipment had been unbolted before she went down — or after, depending on whose story you believed, and nobody official was telling stories. Whatever she'd been carrying had been important enough to remove and unimportant enough to leave forty sailors' worth of hull at the bottom of a bay nobody fished twice. The navy had "decommissioned" her, which in the local dialect meant *she sank and we'd rather not discuss it.*

My father used to say the sea keeps better records than any registry office. He said it the way he said most things — half joke, half sermon, all true — and then he went out one November and became a record himself.

Enough. I checked my gauge, found the number smaller than I liked, and started up.

* * *

The liaison was waiting on the quay when I surfaced, because liaisons always are. That's the first thing they teach you about this work: the client watches the water, not the diver. Divers are replaceable. Cargo isn't.

He stood at the end of the pier in a civilian coat that didn't quite fit his posture, the way military-issue civvies never do. Tall, fair going gray at the temples, hands clasped behind his back like a man posing for a portrait nobody had asked for. He'd introduced himself three weeks ago as Mr. Aldous, of the Maritime Registry, which was funny, because the Maritime Registry's actual office was a shed with a leaky roof staffed by a woman named Petra who mostly sold bait.

"Ms. Whitlock." His voice carried across the water without effort. "Clean run?"

"Clean enough." I hauled myself up the ladder, shed my mask, and tried to look like a woman who hadn't spent ninety minutes freezing in the dark. "Your case is on the line."

Two of his men — quiet men, deckhand-shaped but too clean — brought it aboard and set it in the crate they'd brought, packing it in foam with the tenderness other people reserve for infants. Mr. Aldous watched them do it, then watched me, and I did what I always did: I became furniture. I coiled my line. I checked my regulator. I looked like a woman thinking about nothing except whether her suit had a pinhole, because a woman thinking about nothing is a woman no one remembers.

This is the whole art of salvage work on the wrong side of the charts. Not bravery — anyone can be brave for ten minutes at depth. The art is being forgettable. You take the contract, you do the dive, you sign nothing, you ask nothing, and when the money clears you are a rumor with a boat. My father called it *diving quiet*. He learned it from a man who'd recovered things for people during the war and lived to be old by ensuring no one could ever testify to having met him.

"Anything else down there?" Aldous asked. Casual. Too casual. The question had a hook in it.

"Rust," I said. "Fish. A bulkhead that'll finish collapsing inside the year, if you were thinking of sending anyone else."

"I wasn't." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced, the kind that reaches the eyes because the eyes have been trained. He counted bills into a waterproof pouch — cash, always cash, because cash leaves fewer ripples — and set it on my winch drum. "Same terms next month?"

"Depends on the site."

"The same site."

"Then I'll want more." I said it flat, not greedy, just arithmetic. "That wreck's settling. Every dive costs more risk than the last, and risk is the only thing I actually sell."

Something flickered behind his eyes — reassessment, maybe, or irritation wearing reassessment's coat. He nodded slowly. "We'll discuss it."

"We just did," I said, and began stowing my gear, and after a moment he laughed — a short, genuine-sounding bark — and left with his crate and his quiet men, and the harbor took him back the way water takes a stone.

I waited until his launch cleared the breakwater before I opened the pouch.

Fourteen thousand. In used bills. For four hours of work.

It was too much. I want to be clear about that, because everything that came later grew out of that simple fact, the way a reef grows out of a single anchored thing. Salvage divers get paid in hundreds. Good ones, doing dangerous work, get paid in low thousands. Nobody pays fourteen thousand for rust and fish unless the rust and fish are a story they tell to cover something else. And when a man pays you too much, he isn't paying *you*. He's buying your silence in advance, and silence, unlike air, doesn't come with a gauge to tell you how much you've got left.

But I counted it anyway. Twice. Then I sat down on the winch drum in my dripping suit and counted it a third time, because that's what debt does to a person. It turns you into an accountant of moments.

* * *

The *Kestrel* — my father's boat, now mine in the way a scar is yours — sits at berth eleven in the harbor below the escarpment, third from the end, between a crabber that smells like betrayal and a pleasure yacht that hasn't left its mooring since the owner's divorce. She's forty years old, steel-hulled, ugly as an honest opinion, and she floats, which puts her ahead of most things in my life including, some mornings, me.

Her name is painted on the bow in letters my father repainted every spring, white over black, and underneath, small, in his own hand the last time he did it: *she dives deeper than she should.* People assumed it was about the boat. It wasn't. It was about him, and now it's about me, and neither of us ever learned.

Below decks she smells of diesel and old coffee and the particular salt-stink of neoprene that never fully dries. There's a chart table with a lamp that only works if you hold your mouth right, a bunk I keep meaning to fix the springs on, and a ledger.

The ledger lives in a drawer under the chart table, and I opened it that evening with the cash still warm in its pouch beside me, because this is the ritual, this is the liturgy: earnings on the left, debts on the right, and God or the tide or whoever keeps books for the drowning watching from above.

Here is what my father left me, itemized in his cramped hand and updated since in mine:

The *Kestrel* herself, mortgaged to Harrow & Sons Marine Credit, outstanding balance nine thousand two hundred. The diving gear, leased, four hundred a quarter. Fuel, food, the endless small vampirism of harbor fees. And beneath all of it, the great stone at the bottom of the cairn: the settlement. When the *Kestrel* went out that November with my father and a crew of two and came back with neither, the company that owned the cargo he'd been contracted to recover — a legitimate firm, a *named* firm, which should have told me something — settled with the families for a fraction of the loss and assigned the shortfall to the estate. To me. Thirty-one thousand, accruing interest at a rate I've stopped reading because reading it feels like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurts. It does. It always does.

Total, as of that evening: forty-six thousand, eight hundred and change.

Total earned from the navy's nameless wreck across five dives: sixty-one thousand.

You'd think the math would sing. For about a year it did sing — I paid down the mortgage, I bought real gloves, I ate vegetables that hadn't been pickled as a lifestyle choice. But the debts breed. Interest compounds the way barnacles do, silently and everywhere, and the settlement's shortfall regenerates like something cut. And the navy's work was drying up even as it paid better; Aldous's visits had gone monthly, then six weeks apart, and this last gap had stretched to nine. Wrecks like theirs don't need regular servicing. They need occasional cleaning up, and then they need to be forgotten, and I could feel myself sliding from asset toward loose end with every silent month.

Forty-six thousand against fourteen thousand a visit, and the visits stopping.

I closed the ledger and sat in the lamplight listening to the hull tick as it cooled, and I did the thing I do instead of crying, which is plan. Plans are cheaper than tears and you can act on them. I made three. One: find legitimate salvage, a real wreck with real cargo, and work the season honestly. Two: sell the *Kestrel*, pay down to something survivable, and go work someone else's deck until my hands forgot their own opinions. Three —

Three wasn't a plan yet. Three was just a feeling, a pressure behind the sternum, the sense of a door standing somewhere nearby that I hadn't found the handle to. I wrote *options?* in the margin of the ledger, which is the kind of thing that looks very foolish in retrospect, and put it away.

Outside, the tide was going out. I remember noticing that — noticing it the way you notice weather, idly, without knowing you're taking notes. The water in the harbor had dropped lower than I'd ever seen it, exposing a shelf of black rock along the seaward base of the escarpment that charts marked as permanently drowned. Gulls were walking where gulls don't walk.

I didn't think anything of it. That's the honest truth and I'll swear to it: I looked at the lowest tide in a generation and thought about fuel prices.

But the sea keeps records. And that night, sleeping badly in a bunk with springs like a trap, I dreamed of my father at the chart table, holding a lamp to his mouth to make it work, tracing a line on a chart with his finger and saying, in the tone he saved for sermons: *it's not north, Casper. Write that down. It's not north.*

I woke with the word *north* in my ears and the taste of salt in my mouth, and the tide, when I looked out at dawn, had come back in and covered everything, smooth and innocent as a made bed.

The pouch of cash was still on the chart table. Fourteen thousand, minus what the ledger demanded, leaving a margin so thin you could thread it through a needle. Enough to keep the wolves fed. Nowhere near enough to make them leave.

I put the kettle on. I checked my suit for pinholes. And I told myself, in the voice I use for lying to myself, which is unfortunately my most convincing voice, that the strange dive pay would come again, that the door I couldn't find wasn't real, that a woman who dives quiet stays safe.

The harbor disagreed. Halfway through my coffee, Marta Voss came down the quay past the *Kestrel* — old Marta, seventy-odd years of herring and widowhood in her shoulders — and stopped, and looked at the waterline on the escarpment's base, and looked at me, and said the sentence that undid the careful architecture of my ordinary life:

"You saw it, then. Last night. The cave."

I hadn't. But I was going to.
