## 2. Ledger of the Dead

The arithmetic of drowning is simple. You start with what you owe, subtract what you have, and whatever's left over is the depth of water above your head.

I did that arithmetic at my father's kitchen table, which was really my kitchen table now, though I still couldn't bring myself to move his chair. The table was scarred with ring stains from forty years of coffee mugs and one memorable burn from a chart he'd set down too close to the stove, and under the good light from the window it looked like what it was: the desk of a man who had died owing money to people who don't send polite letters.

Three ledgers. That was the whole estate. Not counting the boat.

The first ledger was the bank's. Formal, typeset, faintly apologetic in the way banks are when they're about to take everything you love. *Outstanding principal on vessel loan, M/V Kestrel: 61,400.* Plus interest accruing at a rate I could feel in my teeth. The Kestrel was my father's salvage boat — thirty-one feet of stubborn steel and older hydraulics, my office and my home and the last thing in the world that still smelled like him, which was diesel and rust and, underneath it, if you knew where to breathe, the particular salt-and-steel smell of a man who'd spent his life underwater.

The second ledger was handwritten, in my father's slanting script, and it was worse because it was honest. He'd kept accounts with half the harbor. Fuel advanced against future work. Engine parts bought on promise. Money borrowed from Marta Voss — two thousand, marked paid, then borrowed again, then marked paid again, a little dance of pride and friendship that had gone on for years. He'd owed people who never would have pressed him, which meant the debt wasn't just financial. It was a debt of face. Of standing. In a harbor town, that's the kind you can't discharge with a check.

The third ledger was the one that made me put my head down on the table for a while. No letterhead. Just figures in someone else's careful hand, delivered through intermediaries, for what my father had apparently borrowed in his last year. Numbers large enough that I understood, finally, why a careful man — and he was a careful man, a *paranoid* man, a man who triple-knotted his mooring lines — had taken the Kestrel out in weather no sane diver would look at.

He hadn't been reckless. He'd been cornered. There's a difference, and the difference is that recklessness kills you fast and cornered kills you slow, and my father had managed to do both at once.

* * *

Total obligations: something north of ninety thousand, once you counted everything, which I made myself do twice, because the first time I'd hoped I'd added wrong.

My assets were as follows. The Kestrel, worth maybe thirty if I sold her whole, more if I sold her in pieces, which felt like selling a body by the organ. My dive gear, my tools, a pickup truck with a transmission that made a sound like a dying whale. And the navy contract, folded in my jacket pocket like a live coal.

I took it out and read it again, because reading it was cheaper than thinking about it.

Recovery of specified equipment from a designated site. Salvage rights waived. Confidentiality — absolute, indefinite, enforceable. Payment: eighteen thousand, half on completion, half on "verification of condition," a phrase doing a lot of quiet work in that sentence. The liaison — a Lieutenant Rennick, whose card was heavier than most business cards, the kind of card that costs more than a fisherman makes in a week — had been very clear about the terms without ever once being clear about anything else.

Eighteen thousand. Against ninety. Even if I never ate, never fueled the boat, never replaced a single O-ring, I'd surface from this contract still forty fathoms down, and the next contract wouldn't come for months, and classified wrecks that pay too well are not a renewable resource. They're a lottery ticket you buy with your lungs.

And there was the other thing about the navy money, the thing I kept circling like a diver checking for entanglement hazards. Men like Rennick didn't pay eighteen thousand dollars for a sealed case unless the case was worth a hundred times that, or unless they were buying something besides the case. Discretion, maybe. Or silence. Or me — my knowledge of those waters, my willingness to go where they pointed and not ask what the compass of their interest was actually bearing toward.

I'd been diving that wreck for three days before Rennick approached me. Three days of the navy pretending the site didn't exist while their patrol boats idled just past the headland, close enough to watch, far enough to deny. Whatever was down there, they wanted it handled quietly, by someone expendable, with no paperwork and no questions.

I was perfect for the job. That was the humiliating part. I'd spent six years making myself invisible — no harbor bar tabs, no gossip, work logged under a name so unremarkable that dispatchers forgot it between calls. Careful invisibility, my father called it once, not quite approving. *A woman who hides is a woman who's found,* he said. I'd thought that was just him being dramatic. Now I wondered if he'd been describing himself.

* * *

The creditor's letter came at dusk, which felt deliberate even though I know it wasn't. Letters arrive when they arrive. But there was something theatrical about finding it pinned under the Kestrel's wheel, hand-delivered, cream-colored envelope, no stamp. Someone had walked down the dock, looked at my boat, and decided she was worth the walk.

I stood in the wheelhouse with the envelope unopened for a long moment. Outside, the water was going pewter-colored, and the escarpment stood up behind the town the way it always did — black rock, white icefields, indifferent as a verdict. Somewhere up in all that white was the glacier the old-timers talked about, the one that calved in summer with a sound like artillery. I'd lived in its shadow my whole life and never once thought about what it might be hiding. Mountains hide things. That's practically their job description.

I opened the letter.

It was brief. Creditors appreciate courtesy, but courtesy has limits. The note secured against the vessel M/V Kestrel falls due in thirty days. Failure to settle will result in seizure proceedings, which the writer — a Mr. Aldous Vane, a name like a minor villain in a novel my father would have hated — assured me he would pursue *with regret.*

Regret. I've been regretted before. It doesn't slow them down.

Thirty days. The navy contract paid on completion plus verification, which meant realistically three weeks of work, then however long their bureaucracy took to decide I'd earned the second half. Even in the best case, Vane's deadline and Rennick's payment schedule were two trains headed for the same crossing, and I was going to be standing on the tracks between them.

I sat down in my father's chair after all. I figured he wouldn't mind tonight.

Here's what nobody tells you about debt: it isn't the numbers. Numbers are just the shape of it. What it actually is, is smaller than that, day by day. It's choosing the cheap fuel. It's diving with a reg you should have serviced. It's smiling at Marta Voss across the fuel dock and knowing you're the reason her brother-in-law hasn't been repaid, and watching her pretend not to know, because kindness is its own kind of pressure. Debt shrinks you. Every month you get a little less person and a little more liability, until one morning you look in the mirror and see a balance sheet wearing your mother's eyes.

My father had refused to shrink. That was the tragedy of him, or maybe the point of him. When the debts got bad he didn't cut back; he went looking for one big score, one recovery that would square everything, and the sea — which forgives nothing and forgets nothing — had collected instead.

They said equipment failure. I'd read the incident report eleven times, sitting right here at this table. *Vessel lost with all hands during salvage operation; cause attributed to catastrophic mechanical failure; no irregularities noted.* Signed, Lieutenant J. Rennick, Naval Liaison Office. I'd been so deep in grief that the signature had just been a signature. A stranger's name on a stranger's mercy.

Now the same name was paying me too well to keep my mouth shut, and some cold, patient part of me had started asking questions I didn't want answered. Why had my father been working a site the navy didn't officially have? Who had hired him? Why did a routine mechanical failure require a lieutenant to sign off personally?

I didn't have answers. But I had a ledger, and a letter, and thirty days, and I had learned from my father's death the only lesson worth learning from it: cornered men make choices. I intended to make mine while I still had hands free.

* * *

Marta found me at the fuel dock the next morning, which surprised me, because I'd gone there specifically to avoid her. She was seventy-some years old and built like the pilings she'd grown up around, and she had the unnerving habit of appearing exactly where you least wanted an audience.

"You didn't sleep," she said. Not a question. Marta didn't traffic in questions; she dealt in conclusions.

"I slept fine."

"Your boat says otherwise. You moved her lines twice in the night. Nervous hands." She leaned on the rail beside me and looked out at the water like we were two women admiring the view, which neither of us was. "Vane's boy came by yesterday. Cream envelope."

Word traveled in this harbor faster than sound. Faster than shame, sometimes. "It's handled," I said.

"Nothing's handled. Handling is what people say when they haven't started." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were pale gray and absolutely without pity, which I've always appreciated about her. Pity would have finished me. "Your father borrowed from me four times. Paid every cent. You know why I kept lending?"

"Because he always paid."

"Because he always *came back.*" She tapped the rail with one blunt finger. "That's the collateral that matters in this harbor, girl. Not paper. Return. You come back, you pay, you come back again. Vane doesn't care if you come back. Vane cares about the boat."

"The boat's all I've got."

"No." She pushed off the rail and started away, then paused, and what she said next she said to the water rather than to me, which was how she delivered anything that mattered. "You've got your father's hands and his stubbornness and a pair of lungs the doctor calls pathological. The boat is just steel. Don't let them take the wrong things."

She left me there with the tide going out and my chest doing something complicated.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think yet, the thought waiting at the bottom like a body in dark water: the navy contract wasn't going to save me. Eighteen thousand against ninety, and Vane's thirty days, and Rennick's verification clause that could stretch into spring if he felt like it. The math didn't work. I'd known it since the kitchen table and I'd spent a day pretending otherwise, and pretending is just drowning with better posture.

So the question stopped being *how do I cover the debts* and became *what am I willing to do that I haven't done yet.*

I didn't have an answer. But I noticed, standing there, that my hands had gone still. Not calm — I want to be honest about that, it wasn't calm, it was the stillness of a needle finding its bearing. Some instrument in me had stopped swinging and settled on a heading, and I didn't yet know what lay along that line, only that I was going to follow it, and that for the first time since my father's funeral I wasn't tired.

I was broke, watched, and thirty days from losing everything. And I was, for the first time in six years, wide awake.

That evening the harbor master mentioned, in the idle way he mentions things, that the almanac was calling for an extraordinary low tide at week's end. Lowest in forty years, the old men were saying down at the co-op. Waterline dropping farther than anyone living had seen.

"Expose all kinds of stuff, a tide like that," he said, and shrugged, and went back to his paperwork.

I stood on the Kestrel's deck and looked at the escarpment, black against the last light, and felt the needle in my chest swing a degree further.
