## 3. Lowest Tide in Forty Years

The Voss woman came into my life the way most trouble does — carrying a crate of fish and an opinion.

I was hosing down the *Kestrel's* deck at the harbor steps, three days after the navy job, when her shadow fell across the gunwale. I looked up expecting another creditor or another fish broker with bad news about prices. Instead I got a woman somewhere north of seventy, built like a bollard, with hands that had clearly spent more years in cold water than I'd been alive.

"You're Whitlock's girl," she said. Not a question. Nobody in that harbor asked questions; they just delivered verdicts wrapped as statements.

"I'm nobody's girl," I said, because it was true and also because I was tired.

"Marta Voss." She set the crate on the quay — herring, silver-bright, still stiff with cold — and looked out at the water like she was checking whether it had behaved overnight. "Your father bought bait off me for eleven years. Never once haggled."

"He was a terrible businessman."

"So I heard." She turned those gray eyes on me, the color of the sea before weather. "Heard other things too. Heard you've been diving the escarpment side."

My spine did the thing spines do when you're trying to keep a secret and your body votes otherwise. "I dive where there's work."

"Mm." She let that sit between us like a stone dropped in still water. Then, apparently satisfied with whatever she'd read in my face, she nodded toward the headland — the great black shoulder of rock that split our coast from the navy's stretch of it, the one everyone called the Escarpment the way you'd call a mountain The Mountain, capital letters implied. "Tide's going out Thursday. Lowest in forty years. Almanac says so, and the almanac doesn't lie, it just makes you wish it would."

"Congratulations to the tide," I said, wringing out the hose.

"The old cave'll show." She said it lightly, the way you'd mention a neighbor's fence falling down. "Under the third point, past the kelp shelf. My grandmother saw it when she was a girl. Said the water pulled back like a curtain and there it was — a mouth in the rock, big enough to sail a skiff into." A pause, deliberate as a knot tied slowly. "Course, that was before they put the wreck down there. Before the navy decided that stretch of coast didn't exist anymore."

I kept my face doing nothing, which is a skill, and mine is well practiced. "I don't know anything about any wreck."

"No," Marta Voss agreed pleasantly. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of it." She picked up her crate, paused, and added, over her shoulder: "Thursday. Two in the afternoon, give or take. If a person were the curious sort."

Then she left, and I stood there holding a hose, watching the water lap the harbor wall at its ordinary, law-abiding height, and told myself very firmly that I was not the curious sort.

* * *

Here's the thing about curiosity: it's a debt too. It accrues. You can ignore the minimum payments for a while, but interest compounds, and eventually something comes calling.

I spent Wednesday being responsible instead. Responsible meant diving the mussel beds off the south shallows for a buyer who paid half what he should and on time, which in my current financial climate made him practically family. Responsible meant sitting at the *Kestrel's* little table with my father's ledger open under the lamp, running numbers I already knew by heart, hoping arithmetic had changed its mind overnight.

It hadn't. The ledger is a short book and a long grief. Dad kept accounts the way he did everything — honestly, badly, and too late. Engine overhaul, two seasons back. Coast guard fines for a mooring dispute he'd been too proud to walk away from. A loan from Harrow & Sons, Fisheries Credit, whose letterhead had recently begun arriving in red ink. And underneath all of it, the big one: the insurance company's clawback, the money they wanted returned because his last voyage had been ruled — and here was the word that lived in my chest like a splinter — *negligent*. Signed off by a naval review board. Cleared, closed, case file stamped and shelved while I was still planning his funeral.

Eleven thousand four hundred and sixty pounds, all told. Against which I had maybe nine hundred in the tin bolted under the bunk, plus whatever the mussels brought, plus the navy's payment — generous, yes, but spoken for the moment it landed, because Harrow & Sons had a direct debit arrangement with my account that amounted to a second, hungrier tide.

I closed the ledger. I made tea I couldn't taste. And I thought about the cave exactly not at all, which is to say I thought about it constantly, the way you don't think about a loose tooth.

Because here's what bothered me, and it wasn't legend or grandmother's tales. It was logistics. I'd dived that stretch of coast more times than I could count — paid dives, quiet ones, the kind where you surface without flags flying. I knew the third point like my own teeth. And I'd never seen so much as a shadow of a cave mouth, which meant either the legends were wrong, or the entrance sat deep enough below even spring lows that no living person had looked through it. Forty years since the last lowest tide. Forty years, and the navy's wreck sitting right offshore, and nobody asking why a classified salvage site happened to be anchored within swimming distance of a geological feature the locals had songs about.

Coincidence exists. I've just never been able to afford it.

* * *

Thursday dawned clear and cold, the kind of light that makes everything look honest. I went out on the water anyway, because a diver who stops working every time her own mind gets loud starves twice — once in the pocket and once in the skull. I ran the mussel lines until noon, hauled, sorted, and tried very hard to think about bivalves.

At half past one I told myself I was heading home. At quarter to two I was rounding the third point, engine at idle, telling myself this was a routine survey. Divers do surveys. Surveys are legitimate professional activities. The fact that I'd brought my good torch, both reels, and the spare mask suggested my subconscious had filed different paperwork.

The water was already leaving.

You have to understand what a record tide looks like from a boat, because it doesn't look like much at first — a few extra feet of wet stone, weed exposed and dripping, the usual. But then you get closer and the scale arrives. The kelp shelf, normally a dark smear a fathom down, stood up out of the sea like a reef deciding to become land. Barnacle lines I'd never seen ran along the point's base, pale bands of old high-water marks stacked like tree rings, forty years of ordinary tides suddenly revealed as a thin skin over something older. Gulls stalked ground they'd never walked, offended and delighted. The whole coastline seemed to exhale.

And there, past the kelp shelf, in the black flank of the third point —

A mouth.

Marta's grandmother hadn't exaggerated, or if she had, it was only in the direction of modesty. The opening was easily eight meters across, arched, its rim furred with dead barnacles and the white skeletons of mussels, descending inward on a smooth cobbled ramp that the retreating water had polished like a throat. It didn't look like a cave. Caves look accidental. This looked *built*, or at least argued for — the arch too regular, the approach too clean, as if the rock had been persuaded rather than eroded. Cold air moved out of it against the wind, steady, faintly mineral, the breath of some deep place that had been holding it a long time.

I cut the engine and drifted.

Self-preservation, at this point, made its usual presentation. It reminded me that classified wrecks don't happen near interesting caves by accident. It reminded me that the navy liaison who paid me too well had been very specific about dates this week, very specific about *not approaching the shoreward sites*. It reminded me that I owed eleven and a half thousand pounds to people who solved problems with paperwork and that disappearing into a hole in a military coastline was how problems got solved the other way. It reminded me, finally, of my father, who had died on water not thirty miles from where I floated, doing something the official record called routine.

The official record, I had learned, was a genre of fiction.

The tide was still going out. I could see wet stone inside the arch that hadn't been wet a minute ago, the sea backing off down the ramp like a servant withdrawing. In an hour, maybe less, the floor of the place would be bare. In six hours the water would come back and close the door for another forty years, and whatever the cave kept would go on keeping it, and I would go on owing eleven and a half thousand pounds to men who had never once in their lives stood in front of something genuinely unknown.

I thought about Marta Voss's face when she'd said *if a person were the curious sort.* She'd known. Old woman with a crate of herring had taken one look at me and known exactly which lever to pull.

I dropped anchor in the lee of the point, where the holding was kelp and gravel and honest. I checked my torch, my knife, my air — I wasn't diving, but habits are armor — and I sat in the cockpit for a long moment with my hand flat on the deck, on wood my father had varnished every spring, and I asked him silently what he'd do.

Which was stupid, because I knew what he'd do. He'd have gone in. He went in everywhere; it's what killed him, probably, the inability to leave a door unopened. The difference between us, I'd always believed, was that I could count.

I counted. One: the tide window, roughly five hours. Two: the navy patrol schedule, which I'd memorized without meaning to, next pass at four-fifteen, a fast boat on a loop that wouldn't come within a mile of the third point unless something flagged it. Three: the odds that a cave of local legend, on a coast the navy pretended didn't exist, connected to nothing worth knowing.

Those odds, I judged, were poor.

"Curious sort," I said aloud, to nobody, and it came out sounding like an accusation.

I stepped off the *Kestrel* onto the exposed shelf, boots finding grip on weed-slick stone, and started toward the arch. Behind me the harbor felt suddenly far away, and the ledger, and the red-inked letters, and the careful invisible life I'd built out of small jobs and a lowered chin. The cold breath of the cave reached me before I reached it, smelling of stone and deep salt and time, and beneath all of that — I'd swear this later, though I'd never say it — something else. Something faint and wrong and bright, like the memory of a signal.

The water pulled back another foot as I watched, polite as a curtain.

I went in.
