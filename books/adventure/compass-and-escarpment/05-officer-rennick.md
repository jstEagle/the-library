## 5. Officer Rennick

The navy sent a car for me. Not a boat — a car, which told me everything about how seriously they were taking this. A boat would have meant they thought of me as salvage. A car meant they thought of me as a problem.

It was a gray sedan with government plates and an interior that smelled of vinyl and institutional coffee, and it idled outside the Harbormaster's office at eight sharp on Tuesday morning while I stood in the doorway of Marta Voss's kitchen trying to decide whether I was going to be sick or merely die quietly.

"You don't have to go," Marta said. She was mending a net by the window, not looking up, her hands doing their old work without any input from the rest of her.

"Sure I do." I buttoned my one clean shirt to the throat. "Ignore them and they come back with more of them."

"They can't make you talk."

"No," I agreed. "But they can make everything else harder. Permits, moorings, fuel contracts. The navy doesn't need to arrest you, Marta. It just needs to remember your name wrong."

She snorted — a sound like a winch taking strain — and let it go. But when I crossed the yard she called after me: "Casper. You went into that cave."

I stopped. The morning fog was lifting off the water in rags, and somewhere out past the point a gull was making its argument about something.

"I heard you came back wet past your knees," she said, still not looking at me. "And that you didn't tell anyone what you found."

"Who says I found anything?"

"Mm." She pulled a knot tight. "The tide comes back tonight. Whatever's down there goes under again for another forty years. Remember that, girl. The sea is patient. Learn from it."

I got in the car.

* * *

They'd set up in the back room of the Coast Guard station, which had been cleared of everything except a table, two chairs, and a tape recorder that looked older than my father's boat. A man in civilian clothes — but civilian clothes worn the way officers wear them, pressed and correct, a jacket that had never once been used to wipe oil off its hands — stood when I entered.

"Casper Whitlock." He extended his hand. "Lieutenant Marcus Rennick. Thank you for coming in."

"Lieutenant." His grip was dry and calibrated, firm enough to signal respect, brief enough to signal he wasn't going to pretend we were friends. He was maybe forty-five, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been repurposed for other work. Gray at the temples, precise. Eyes the color of harbor water in February — technically blue, functionally cold.

I sat where he pointed. He sat opposite. The recorder sat between us like a small black headstone.

"This is informal," he said. "A conversation, not an interrogation. You're not under investigation."

"That's a relief. I'd hate to be under investigation before breakfast."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that a smile had been considered and rejected on grounds of efficiency. "You've been diving the site off the escarpment for eleven days now. Twelve recoveries. All logged?"

"All logged. Manifests signed, items sealed, receipts countersigned by your liaison, Commander—"

"Prewitt. Yes." He opened a folder. Of course there was a folder. There was always a folder; men like Rennick were born holding them. "Your work has been satisfactory. Thorough. Discreet." He said the last word with a small weight on it, the way you'd set down a coin to see if the table was level.

"I'm a professional."

"You are. That's actually why we're here." He turned the folder around so I could see a chart of the coast, the wreck site marked with a neat red X. "In the course of your dives, have you observed anything anomalous? Unusual currents, debris fields outside the expected scatter pattern, magnetic irregularities, anything at all that struck you as — off?"

Anomalies. The word hung there, official and empty, a door marked with nothing.

"Define off," I said.

He leaned back. "Anything that made you curious, Ms. Whitlock."

There it was. Curious. The exact word Marta had used about me, or would use, or had already used with someone who reported to someone who reported to him. I felt the shape of the trap without needing to see all of it: say yes and I become interesting; say no too fast and I become suspicious. The trick, as always, was to be boring at exactly the right speed.

"The current runs contrary to the tide tables on the east side of the escarpment," I said. "Has for years. Every diver here knows it. My father complained about it in his logs going back to before I was born. If that's an anomaly, you'll want to interview half the fishing fleet, and good luck getting them to say two words to you."

"Noted." He wrote something down. Actually wrote it, pen on paper, though the recorder was running. Men like Rennick liked paper because paper could be filed and forgotten, or filed and remembered, whichever served. "Magnetic readings?"

"My compass spins near the wreck. Iron hull, iron ore in the cliff, probably some unexploded ordnance from the war. I navigate by dead reckoning down there. Ask Prewitt — I flagged it in my first safety report."

"I've read your reports." He said it mildly, but I caught the undertow. *I've read your reports, Ms. Whitlock. I know how you write. I know what you leave out.* "You're careful. You document well. Your father trained you?"

"He did."

"Tell me about him."

And there it was — the real question wearing a polite coat. I kept my hands loose in my lap and my voice level, which took more effort than any dive I'd ever done.

"What's there to tell? Salvage man. Worked these waters thirty years. Died four years ago when his boat went down off Skerry Point in weather he shouldn't have gone out in. The board found operator error. End of story."

"Hardly end of story," Rennick said softly. "You're still paying for it."

I looked at him then, really looked, and he met my eyes with that flat harbor-water calm, and I understood that this was not a question at all. It was a hook, baited with my own life, offered gently to see if I'd swallow.

"My finances aren't relevant to anomalies near the wreck site, Lieutenant."

"Everything is relevant." He closed the folder. Folded his hands on top of it. "Here's the situation as I see it, Ms. Whitlock, and I'll be candid because I believe you respond better to candor than to comfort. Something was lost near this coast a long time ago. We are recovering it. The operation is sensitive, the equipment is classified, and recently certain indicators suggest that others may be aware of the site. So when I ask whether you've seen anything unusual, I'm not asking out of bureaucratic habit. I'm asking because if you have seen something and don't report it, you place yourself — and I say this with genuine respect — in a great deal of danger."

"Danger," I repeated. "From whom?"

"From people less patient than I am."

"And from you?"

His expression didn't change, which was itself an answer, beautifully delivered. "I'd prefer us to be allies."

"Then give me something to be allied about. What's down there?"

"You know I can't answer that."

"Then you know I can't either." I shrugged. "Lieutenant, I dive, I log, I seal, I sign. I've done it twelve times and I'll do it however many more times you pay me to. I don't ask questions because questions don't pay down debt. That's the whole of my politics."

He studied me for a long moment. Outside, through the salt-hazed window, a pair of fishermen were arguing about engine parts with the volume and tenderness of brothers. Somewhere under all of it, beneath the floorboards and the rock and forty fathoms of green water, the cave was waiting for tonight's tide to close over it like a mouth.

"One more thing," Rennick said, and now his voice changed — dropped a degree, went almost gentle, which is when I should have understood everything. "Your father's last voyage. The *Meridian*. You've read the incident report?"

The room went very quiet, the way rooms do when something inside you goes loud.

"I've read it."

"It was a thorough inquiry. Tragic accident. Weather beyond tolerances, cargo shifted, bilge pumps overwhelmed." He recited it like liturgy. "Signed off at every level."

"I know. I've read it forty times. I could recite it."

"Yes," he said. "I imagine you could."

And then I saw it. Not his face — I'd been looking at his face the whole time and it had told me nothing. I saw the signature block on page nine of that report, the one I'd traced with my finger so many times the ink had practically transferred to my skin. *M. J. Rennick, Lt., Board of Inquiry.* The junior officer whose findings had closed the case. The name at the bottom of the document that said my father had killed himself through carelessness, and that no one owed anyone anything, and that the sea keeps its own books.

Four years I'd carried that report like a stone in my chest. And its author had just bought me coffee I hadn't drunk and asked me about anomalies in a voice like warm milk.

"You signed it," I said.

Rennick regarded me without blinking. "I was on the board, yes."

"You signed the report that said my father was careless."

"I signed the findings of a full investigation, conducted according to—"

"He wasn't careless." My voice came out low and even, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "He checked that boat like a priest checks his conscience. He went out because the navy contracted him for a priority run and paid triple rate, and when he didn't come back, the same navy wrote a report blaming him instead of asking why a salvage man with thirty years of sense would push into weather like that. Triple rate, Lieutenant. Who orders a triple-rate run in a rising gale?"

The silence stretched. Rennick's hands stayed folded. If the name Whitlock meant anything to him beyond a line item, he gave no sign of it — and that absence was the loudest thing in the room.

"Investigations establish what can be established," he said finally. "I'm sorry for your loss. Truly."

"Are you."

"Yes." And the terrible thing, the thing I'd turn over for nights afterward, was that I believed he meant it — the way a surgeon means it when he tells you the operation failed. Sincere, procedural, and utterly without consequence. "Which is why I'd rather you hear this from me than from rumor. The recovery operation will conclude within the month. After that, the site will be formally closed. No further permits will be issued for that sector — commercial, private, or otherwise. Including yours."

"There's a contract."

"There was a contract. Circumstances have changed." He slid a fresh sheet across the table. "Final payment schedule, plus a closure clause. Standard language. Your lawyer can review it."

"I don't have a lawyer. Lawyers cost money, and money is the thing I don't have, as you so thoroughly researched."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not quite sympathy, not quite satisfaction. Recognition, maybe. One creature of hard arithmetic meeting another.

"Sign it or don't," he said. "But understand the shape of things, Ms. Whitlock. This coast is about to become very quiet. Quiet is easier for everyone. Especially for people with debts."

* * *

I walked back along the harbor road instead of waiting for the car, because I needed the wind and because I didn't trust my legs to behave in front of witnesses. The fog had burned off. The water lay flat and bright and treacherous as glass, and out past the point the escarpment stood up out of the sea with its glacier shoulder white against the sky, and I hated it, suddenly and completely, the way you can only hate a thing you also love.

*Quiet is easier for everyone.*

He knew. Maybe not everything — maybe not the cave, not yet — but he knew the shape of the hole in his map, and he was standing on it, smiling, offering me money to walk away.

I stopped at the rail by the fish sheds and took out the compass.

I'd wrapped it in a wool sock at the bottom of my tool bag, which is where I'd been keeping it for three days now, telling myself I was only holding it until I decided what to do, which is what thieves say, and cowards, and daughters of complicated fathers. The brass was cold. The needle swung, restless, and settled — as it always did, as it refused not to do — pointing away from north, inland, up, toward the ice.

My father's initials were scratched inside the case. I hadn't told Rennick that. I hadn't told anyone.

*Operator error,* the report said. *Weather beyond tolerances.*

Thirty years of sense, pushing into a gale, at triple rate. Unless it wasn't the gale he was running from. Unless it wasn't a cargo run at all. Unless a man who owned a compass that pointed at glaciers had spent his whole life being paid by the navy to keep his mouth shut, until one day the price of silence changed, and the sea — which is patient, which keeps its own books — collected.

The needle trembled against its glass and held its impossible bearing.

Tonight the tide would come back and close the cave for another forty years. Rennick wanted the coast quiet. Quill's letter sat in my cabin, offering a fortune for whatever the lowest tide revealed. And between those two forces, small and solvent-poor and freshly lied-to, I stood at the rail holding the one object on this coastline that couldn't be explained, filed, or buried.

I put it back in the sock. I put the sock in my bag. I zipped the bag shut, and my hands were steady, and that frightened me more than anything else that had happened all week.

Because I'd walked into that room intending to lie smoothly and walk out invisible, and I'd done both — and somewhere between the door and the rail I had stopped being a woman paying off a debt and started being a woman with a secret worth killing for.

The tide turned at 6:40. I intended to be in the water before it did.
